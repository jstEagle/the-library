## 1. The Mood of Sandbars

The Verrane River in low-water season was a liar with a beautiful face. It looked calm from the wheelhouse window, all that bronze light and glassy water, but Ulyana Marchetti knew better. Beneath the surface, sandbars migrated nightly like herds of something restless, and the channel the charts promised was often a suggestion at best, a rumor at worst.

She stood at the helm of the *Kestrel* with one hand on the wheel and the other on the throttle, reading the water the way other people read faces. A faint seam of darker current there — that was the channel's spine, hugging the far bank where the cutbank had collapsed two seasons ago and deepened the flow. A line of nervous ripples ahead and to starboard — that was the bar at Kilometer Sixty-Two, the one that had grown a new tail since the spring and hadn't bothered to update anyone about it.

She eased the throttle back a notch. The old diesel under her feet dropped its pitch from a growl to a grumble, and the *Kestrel* answered the way she always answered — reluctantly, complaining through the hull, but obedient to the last.

"Easy, old girl," Ulyana murmured. "We're not there yet."

Ten years on this river. Ten years of fuel docks and checkpoint stamps and cargo manifests written in three languages, of hauling diesel drums upriver in the dry months and timber and tin downriver when the rains came. Ten years of never staying anywhere longer than it took to unload, refuel, and cast off. The *Kestrel* was forty-one years old, had a collision scar on her port bow that Ulyana had patched herself with epoxy and stubbornness, and burned oil like a rich man burns money. She was, by any honest accounting, on borrowed time.

So was Ulyana. That was the arrangement. Neither of them asked the other to settle down.

The morning was already hot, the kind of heat that came up off the water and wrapped around your wrists like a living thing. Up ahead, the escarpment showed itself on the eastern horizon — that long blue-gray wall of stone that gave the whole district its name, rising out of the river plain like the spine of something buried. She'd been looking at that wall for a decade. It never got closer. It never needed to.

"Bar's moved," she said to the empty wheelhouse, marking it on her chart with a grease pencil. "Again."

The *Kestrel* carried no crew these days. She'd let the deckhand go in March, and before him the cook, and before the cook a whole shifting population of river men and women who came aboard with their duffels and their debts and left the same way. It was simpler alone. Cheaper. Nobody to ask questions, nobody to notice when she took a channel that wasn't on the charts, nobody aboard whose problems could become hers.

That was the rule, and she kept it the way she kept the channel: with constant, unglamorous attention.

*Never stop moving.* Her father had taught her the opposite rule — *the land is the only honest thing, Ulyana, buy land and the land will hold you* — and she had spent ten years proving him wrong, one sandbar at a time. The river didn't hold you. The river carried you past everything, and if you kept your bow pointed downstream of your problems, they'd never catch up.

Mostly they never caught up.

She smelled Port Sarelle before she saw it — diesel and drying fish and the green-rot smell of the market district, where produce went to die in the heat and the smell rose off the stalls like a confession. The port itself was a crescent of corrugated roofing and leaning pilings, a fuel dock, a customs shed with a flag that had faded to the color of weak tea, and a long line of moored barges waiting their turn like patients outside a dentist's office.

Ulyana hated the place. She hated every port, but Sarelle especially, because Sarelle had a customs officer named Dreville who liked to talk.

She brought the *Kestrel* in against the current, backing the throttle, letting the river do the work — you never fought the Verrane in low water, you negotiated with it. The hull kissed the fenders at the fuel dock with a sound like a held breath released, and she had the lines made fast before the dockhand had even finished strolling out, because she'd been making fast to docks since before this dockhand could read.

"Two hundred liters," she called down. "And don't top it off slow, I'm on a schedule."

"You're always on a schedule, Marchetti." The dockhand grinned up at her, missing two teeth and all of his ambition. "Where's the fire?"

Downstream, she thought. There's always a fire downstream.

The fueling took forty minutes. She spent them checking the bilge — dry, or dry enough, the old scar seeping its usual slow teaspoon an hour — and re-lashing cargo, and doing all the small maintenance of a boat that was held together by habit. Then she climbed the steps to the customs shed, manifest in hand, and found Dreville waiting for her with the particular stillness of a man who has been rehearsing a conversation.

Dreville was a small, neat man with a clerk's posture and a bureaucrat's talent for saying nothing at length. Today he didn't say nothing. Today he stamped her manifest without looking at it, set it down, and folded his hands.

"Quiet season," he said.

"It's low water. It's always quiet."

"Mm." He turned the stamp over in his fingers. "We had a visitor last week. From up-country. Well-dressed fellow. Not the usual mining crowd — too clean for the concession people, you know how they are." He glanced at her, quick and sideways. "He was asking after boats. Cargo schedules. Which captains ran the middle river regular."

"Half the river runs the middle regular."

"He was asking after one boat in particular." Dreville's voice dropped, not out of caution but out of the pleasure of being important. "He had a name he kept saying. Not Marchetti." The stamp turned over again. "The other name."

The wheelhouse was forty meters away and Ulyana's legs wanted to carry her there at a dead run. She kept her hands flat on the counter instead. She had learned a long time ago that stillness was a currency, and you paid with it only when you could afford the exchange.

"I don't have another name," she said.

"No," Dreville agreed, mildly. "Of course you don't." He stamped a second form she hadn't asked for and slid it across. "Transit waiver. The middle channel's flagged shallow at the Narrows, but between us, the flag's a year old and the bar's shifted east. You'll clear it fine if you hug the red piling." He looked up, and for one moment the bureaucrat's mask slipped and something almost human looked out. "I didn't tell the well-dressed fellow anything, Marchetti. I told him I'd have to check the records. Records take time."

"How much time did you buy me?"

"A week. Maybe two." He shrugged, and the mask came back down. "Fuel dock says you're paid up. I'd not linger."

She walked back down the steps without hurrying, because hurrying was information, and information traveled down a dock faster than water traveled downhill. The dockhand was finishing the fueling, coiling the hose. She paid him, tipped him enough to make him remember her generously and vaguely, and cast off.

Only when the *Kestrel* was a hundred meters off the dock, the port shrinking behind her in the heat shimmer, did she allow herself to think about it.

*The other name.*

Marchetti. The name sat in her chest like a stone she'd swallowed years ago and never passed. Land, timber, and money — the Marchetti concession holdings up-country, the family that had built a dynasty on escarpment granite and never once asked what it cost. Her father had signed her out of his life with the same fountain pen he used for contracts, and she had signed herself out of the name the same day, taking the river instead, taking the *Kestrel*, taking a surname she used on manifests the way other people used aliases.

And now someone clean and well-dressed was saying that name in port offices, asking after her boat.

She ran the possibilities the way she ran the Narrows — fast, eyes forward. Debt collectors: possible, but her debts were small and local and none of them knew the other name. Family lawyers: her father was dead three years, and the estate had been settled without her, which had been the point. The syndicate: the Verrane Mining and Concession Consortium, the beast her father had fed for thirty years, the beast that owned half the escarpment and wanted the other half. They didn't send well-dressed men to ask politely. They sent lawyers first and worse men later.

Unless they were looking for something they didn't yet have. Unless they were looking for a person.

She put it away. That was the discipline — you put the thing in its compartment and you ran the boat. The Narrows were coming up, and the red piling, and the bar that had shifted east, and the river did not care what name anyone was asking after. The river only cared about the next hundred meters, and so, for the next hour, did she.

She hugged the red piling. She threaded the Narrows with the throttle and the wheel and ten years of accumulated instinct, the *Kestrel* leaning through the bend like a horse that knew its rider, and came out the far side into open water with the escarpment still blue and far off to the east.

By late afternoon the light had gone gold and long, and she was running the middle river with the current helping, making good time toward the fueling dock at Kilometer Thirty-One, where she'd overnight — not overnight, she corrected herself, where she'd stop just long enough to take on water and be gone before dawn. Never a full night. Never a full day. The rule held.

The river was empty ahead of her, empty behind, just water and willow banks and the occasional fish eagle standing in the shallows like a disapproving clerk. She should have felt peace. This was the life she'd chosen, the life she'd built plank by plank out of the wreckage of the other one, and most days the motion itself was the comfort — the engine's heartbeat under her feet, the miles ticking past, everything left behind getting smaller.

Today the motion felt thinner. Today the wheelhouse felt small, and the name sat in her chest like a hook that had finally caught on something.

She was still turning it over when she saw the dock at Kilometer Thirty-One — and the figure stumbling along it toward her, waving both arms, moving wrong, lurching like a man whose legs had forgotten their instructions. Even at two hundred meters she could see he was soaked to the skin, and it hadn't rained.

Ulyana eased the throttle down and brought the *Kestrel* in against the dying light, because whatever else she was — runaway, liar, woman with a stone in her chest — she had never yet left a man on a dock to die in the dark.

The rule about never stopping, she told herself, had an exception clause. She'd just never needed it before.

She didn't know, easing the *Kestrel* against the pilings while the stranger collapsed to his knees on the planks, that the exception clause was about to cost her everything she had left.
