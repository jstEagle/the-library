## 2. A Dying Man's Bargain

The fueling dock at Serrano Bend was the kind of place Ulyana Marchetti liked best: ugly, efficient, and empty of anyone who might ask questions. A corrugated pump shed leaning into the current. A fuel barge moored alongside, its tanks sweating diesel in the afternoon heat. A dog asleep under a stack of drums, too old to bother barking.

She brought the *Kestrel* in against the current with two touches of the wheel and one of reverse, kissing the fenders so gently the dockhand didn't even look up from his magazine. Ten years of this river lived in her hands now. She could have docked her blindfolded by the sound of the water off the pilings.

"Two hundred liters," she said, hopping down to loop the stern line herself. "And whatever's in that cooler that isn't spoiled."

The dockhand — Ferro, she thought, or maybe it was the other one, they rotated through these stations like water through a lock — jerked his thumb at the shed. "Cooler's dead. Compressor went out Tuesday." He paused, and something shifted behind his eyes, the particular careful blankness of a man deciding whether to say a thing. "You're the Marchetti boat."

Ulyana kept her face level while her stomach dropped half an inch. "I'm the *Kestrel*. That's what matters on paper."

"Sure." He wound the hose off its reel. "Just — some fellow came through yesterday. Not syndicate colors, but he had their posture. Asking after cargo runs out of Verrane City. Asking which boats were owned by families, which by independents." He fed the nozzle into her tank and started the pump. "Said the name Marchetti like he was tasting it."

"And?"

"And I told him I don't read ownership registries for strangers." Ferro shrugged, elaborately casual. "Figured you'd want to know somebody was reading them anyway."

She wanted to know that, and she didn't, both at once. The name was a stone she'd carried so long she'd stopped feeling its weight until someone else touched it. "Appreciate it," she said, and paid him, and bought a case of warm beer instead of cold because the river didn't care about her comfort and neither, today, would she.

By the time the tanks were full the light had gone long and amber across the water, and that was when the man came stumbling down the dock road.

She saw him first as a problem: a figure weaving along the planks in the wrong gait, not drunk but failing, each step negotiated rather than taken. Then closer, and the problem acquired details — a man sixty-some years old, gray-bearded, dressed in surveyor's canvas gone black with river water, one boot missing entirely, the bare foot cut to ribbons. His skin had the waxy gray of fever. He was carrying himself upright through sheer architectural stubbornness, like a building whose foundations had already failed but whose walls hadn't been informed.

He walked past three other moored boats to reach hers. That was the first thing she noticed afterward, when she had time to notice things. Past the trawler, past the houseboat with its generator droning. Straight to the *Kestrel*.

"No passage," she said before he could speak. "No charity either. Dock's got a clinic two kilometers up the road."

"I know who you are," the man said.

His voice was wrecked, gravel dragged over gravel, but underneath it there was a spine of certainty that made her go still. "Everyone knows everyone out here. Doesn't get you aboard."

"You're Marchetti." He gripped the rail, and she saw his knuckles were split, the wounds crusted brown. "Ulyana Marchetti. Runs the *Kestrel* upriver every season, never stops longer than a night, never takes passengers, never files a flight plan she doesn't have to." A breath that shuddered going in. "I've watched your wake for six years, girl. You're the only captain on this river who treats the current like a contract."

"That's a lot of words to spend on getting refused."

"It's a résumé." And then his legs quit arguing with him, and he folded down onto her deck like wet canvas, and Ulyana learned something about herself in the next four seconds that she would think about later with some discomfort: she moved toward him before she'd decided to.

She caught him under the arms. He weighed less than a man his frame should weigh, the lightness of dehydration and sickness eating a body from inside. Up close he smelled of river mud and woodsmoke and the sweetish wrongness of an infection doing its work.

"Ferro!" she shouted. "Ferro, get over here!"

The dockhand ambled down, took one look, and shook his head with the fatalism of river people. "That'd be Elias Thorn. Prospector. Been working the Escarpment country alone since before you had that boat, I'd guess. Came down off the plateau maybe three days back, they say. On foot. In this heat." He crouched, peered at the man's ruined foot, and stood again quickly. "Clinic won't take him without papers. Papers are in Verrane City. Verrane City is nine hours downstream."

Everything about the situation argued for putting Thorn on the dock, covering him with a tarp against the sun, and casting off. It was the arithmetic she ran on everything: cost, risk, distance. Never stop moving. Stopping was how debts found you, how memories caught up, how names got attached to you again.

Elias Thorn opened his eyes. They were pale, almost colorless, and burning with a clarity that had nothing to do with health.

"Not the clinic," he said. "Not yet. I need thirty seconds of your time first, Captain, and then you can do whatever your conscience tells you. Which I'm guessing is nothing, which is fine. Nothing is honest, at least."

Ulyana looked at Ferro. Ferro looked at the sky, the dock, the dog — anywhere else — and withdrew with the wisdom of his profession.

She crouched so they were eye to eye. "Thirty seconds."

Thorn's hand went inside his coat, moving slowly, and every instinct she had tightened — but what came out wasn't a weapon. It was a bundle of oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, soft-edged and worn smooth by handling. He laid it on the deck between them without untying it.

"A concession claim," he said. "Unregistered. Surveyed proper, twenty-two years ago, by me, with instruments I calibrated myself. Four hundred hectares on the Escarpment plateau above the Kessel gorge. Copper, and under the copper, rare earths worth more than any copper strike in fifty years." His breath caught; he rode through it. "I filed the preliminary intent back then. Paid the fees. But the final registration requires physical filing of the full claim package at the district office, and I—" a dry laugh, "—I got greedy. Thought I'd come back with better assays, file once, file right. Twenty-two years I kept meaning to go back down. And now the permits lapse in thirty days, and I'm dying, and the mountain doesn't care about my intentions."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because in thirty days it becomes open ground, and there are men who've spent five years trying to make sure it becomes open ground *before* I can file it." His grip closed on her wrist, shockingly strong. "They burned my camp in April. Took my samples, my records, my second map. This one I sewed into my coat and carried out overland, and I'd have carried it to the district office myself except the river took my crossing at Marlowe Ford and nearly took the rest of me too."

"The syndicate," Ulyana said. It wasn't a question. Everyone on the Verrane knew the shape of the Consolidated Extraction Group even if they said *the syndicate* the way you said *weather* — as a force, not an institution.

"Corvo Halstead's people." Thorn said the name evenly, but his eyes flicked toward the dock road, and Ulyana understood he'd been checking over his shoulder since the plateau. "If they get this map, the claim dies and the ground goes into their filings with paperwork so clean you could eat off it. If nobody gets it, in thirty days it's a free-for-all and they'll win that too, because they always do. Unless—" he pushed the oilcloth bundle an inch toward her, "—it gets filed first. By someone fast enough and mean enough to run whatever's between here and the office."

"Find a lawyer."

"Lawyers can be bought. I checked. Mine sold me out in April." His mouth twisted. "Find a courier, then. Except couriers can be robbed, and this needs a judgment call at the end of it, not just legs. It needs someone who'll look at the registry clerk and know if the fix is in. Someone who reads water and men the same way." He coughed, deep and wet, and it took him a long moment to surface from it. "It needs you, Marchetti. I don't have thirty seconds left to explain why, so take it on faith from a dying man: I've done my homework, and you're the only name on my list."

"I'm not on anybody's list," Ulyana said. "That's the whole point of how I live."

"Then this is your lucky day, because I'm offering to put you on mine." He tried a smile; it came out crooked, half apology. "Here's the bargain. You carry that to the district office in Verrane City and file it under my name, Elias Thorn, before the permits lapse. Thirty days. That's all. You don't have to climb the plateau, don't have to defend the ground, don't have to want any part of what comes after. Just deliver it. Like cargo."

"Cargo doesn't have syndicate enforcers chasing it."

"This cargo does. I won't lie to you about that. Halstead will hear I came off the river, and he'll start pulling threads, and one of those threads leads to whoever I talked to last." Thorn's gaze held hers, steady despite the fever swimming behind it. "So I'm not asking you to carry a parcel. I'm asking you to swear. There's a difference, and you know it, and it's the difference that makes the delivery hold."

Ulyana sat back on her heels. The pump hose dripped somewhere behind her. Downriver the light was turning to brass, and the *Kestrel* rocked gently against the fenders, and she thought about the stone of her name, and the arithmetic, and the fact that she had moved toward this man before deciding to.

"I don't swear things," she said. "Swearing gives people a handle. I stay handle-free."

"Everybody swears something, girl. Most people just swear it quietly, to themselves, where nobody can hold them to it." Thorn's voice was fraying now, the certainty running down like a battery. "I watched you refuse a tow rope off a grounded barge in the dry season four years ago because the barge master had shorted a widow on her freight. Nobody asked you to. Nobody knew. You did it anyway, and you charged him double for the inconvenience, and I have thought about that for four years, because that is exactly the kind of mean, decent bastard who delivers a dying man's map."

Heat climbed her neck. She hadn't known anyone had seen that. She hadn't known anyone *cared*.

"What's in it for me?" she asked, because the real answer — *nothing, nothing is in it for you, walk away* — was a voice she'd stopped trusting around the time she'd started moving toward falling strangers.

"Ten percent of the claim, if you want it. Or cash, if you'd rather not own ground. Or nothing, if you'd rather do it for the pure pleasure of costing Corvo Halstead the prettiest thing his masters ever lusted after." Thorn sagged against her rail. "But I think you'll take the ten percent, eventually. I think you're tired, Marchetti, in a way that motion doesn't fix. I think you've been running from a name, and I'm handing you a reason to make one."

"Stop talking like you know me."

"I know boats," he said simply. "Yours is listing half a degree to port because you keep meaning to redistribute the ballast and keep choosing to run instead. Boats tell the truth about their captains. So do fevers, apparently." He laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing left him gray-lipped and shaking, and the thirty seconds were long past spent.

Ulyana looked at the oilcloth bundle on her deck. Small. Soft-edged. The kind of weight that seemed like nothing until you picked it up.

She should throw him off. She should throw him and the map and the whole dying-man's romance of it off her boat and cast off and be nine hours downstream by midnight with nothing attached to her but her own wake. That was the rule. That was the architecture of her entire life, load-bearing, tested by a decade of floods.

Instead she heard herself say, "Ferro! Get a stretcher or something with wheels. He's coming aboard."

Ferro appeared at the gangway with the expression of a man watching weather change. "Captain, you sure? That man's got trouble written all over him."

"He's got a map written all over him," Ulyana said. "Trouble's just the packaging."

They got Thorn into her bunk — the one berth she never offered anyone — and stripped the soaked coat off him, and she was peeling the sleeve clear when her fingers found the seam that didn't belong: a ridge of stitching, done in waxed thread, hiding something flat and stiff along the lining. She looked at Thorn. He was past talking now, eyes rolling with fever, but his hand crawled across the blanket and pressed once, twice, against her forearm. *Yes. Take it.*

She cut the stitches with her rigging knife and drew out a second bundle inside the first — no, not a second. The same map, she'd realize later, duplicated, because Elias Thorn had survived twenty-two years in syndicate country by never letting anything important exist in only one copy. Folded oilcloth, and inside that, oilskin, and inside that, paper: a hand-drawn chart dense with contour lines and instrument readings, annotated in a small fierce script, and beneath the chart a sheaf of assay reports, fee receipts stamped twenty-two years old, and the preliminary intent of claim, signed and witnessed.

Four hundred hectares. Thirty days. One signature between a dead man's work and the void.

Ulyana sat in the lamplight with the map in her lap and listened to Elias Thorn breathe — a wet, laborious sound, each breath a negotiation — and understood with perfect clarity that she had just become the kind of person who took aboard feverish strangers with priceless documents sewn into their coats. Her father would have laughed. Her father had built an empire on exactly this kind of arithmetic, the kind where you bet on people being desperate enough to be useful.

The difference, she told the lamp, the river, the sleeping man, was what you did after.

Outside, the last light drained off the water. Somewhere downriver, a barge horn sounded, long and low, and the dog under the fuel drums finally bothered to bark at nothing at all.

Ulyana folded the map away, checked her fuel gauge — full — and did the math she'd been avoiding: Verrane City, nine hours downstream, district office opening at eight. She could be there in two days' running, easy, if nothing went wrong.

Then Ferro knocked on her hull, twice, the way river people knock when they don't want to be heard saying what they're about to say.

"Captain," he said through the door. "There's a launch just come round the bend. Black hull, no markings, running slow like it's looking at boats." A pause. "It's slowing down."

* * *

Ulyana killed her lamp and stood in the dark of her own cabin, listening.

The launch's engine note hung out there in the dusk, unhurried, proprietary — the sound of a vessel that expected the river to move aside. It grew louder. It slowed further. Through her porthole she caught the sweep of a searchlight playing across the moored trawler, the houseboat, the empty stretch of dock.

It passed the *Kestrel* without stopping. But it slowed, she was almost sure, just slightly, just for a breath, the way a man slows his stride passing a window he means to come back to.

The sound faded downriver. Ulyana let out air she'd been holding and turned to the bunk, where Elias Thorn had surfaced from the fever just enough to fix those colorless eyes on her.

"Now you understand," he whispered. "Thirty days, Marchetti. Swear it."

She looked at the map. At the man. At the black water where the launch had gone.

"I swear," Ulyana Marchetti said, and hated how easily it came.
