## 3. What the Map Costs

Elias Thorn died the way he'd lived the last three days — badly, loudly, and without any regard for Ulyana's schedule.

She'd heard him go quiet sometime before first light, the wet rasp of his breathing finally smoothing into silence, and she'd told herself he was only sleeping. She told herself that while she made coffee in the galley, while she ran the engine checks on the Kestrel's ancient diesel, while she stood in the doorway of the aft cabin with two cups in her hands and watched the man's chest refuse to move.

She set the cups down on the deck. One of them slid and spilled. She didn't pick it up.

* * *

The river at dawn was gray silk, mist standing on the water in columns, and Ulyana sat on the deck beside a dead man and did the arithmetic she always did. One body. No port for forty kilometers. No doctor worth the name. A boat that couldn't afford to stop.

She'd known him for less than seventy-two hours. She'd known him long enough to watch him shake with fever, long enough to hear him talk about a daughter in the capital he hadn't seen in eleven years, long enough to swear a promise she already regretted.

"Damn you, Elias," she said quietly. It came out without heat. That was the worst part.

She covered his face with the tarp she used for engine parts and went to find the map.

* * *

He'd told her where it was at three in the morning, when the fever broke long enough for him to be lucid and terrified in equal measure. *Not in the bag. They'll look in the bag. It's sewn into the coat, left seam, under the lining — you have to cut it out, Marchetti, don't tear it, don't—* and then the coughing had taken him again, and she'd held a cup of water to his lips and told him to shut up and save his strength, and he'd gripped her wrist with fingers like bird bones and said, *You swore.*

She had. That was the trouble with promises. They didn't care whether you meant them at the time.

The coat was folded under his head as a pillow. She knelt, worked the left seam open with her smallest knife, and felt the stiffness of paper against her fingertips before she saw it. The lining gave. She drew out a folded sheet wrapped in oilcloth, and beneath the oilcloth, a hand-drawn map on survey paper so old the edges had gone the color of weak tea.

She spread it on the deck.

It was beautiful. That was her first thought, and it annoyed her. She'd expected a drunk's scrawl, a fantasy scratched out in charcoal. Instead: ink lines, precise and confident, a stretch of the upper Verrane she knew only as a rumor — the escarpment country, where the river cut through plateau rock and nobody with sense took a boat. Contour lines. Elevations in a careful architectural hand. A creek marked with a name she didn't recognize, and at the head of it, circled twice, a set of coordinates and three words in Thorn's tightening script:

*CONCESSION 44-B. UNREGISTERED.*

Below it, a date. Thirty days from the night he'd boarded. The day the old concession framework lapsed and every unfiled claim on the escarpment reverted to open bidding — which meant, she understood with a cold settling in her stomach, that whoever filed first with a valid survey won. No syndicate lawyers. No backroom. Just a clerk, a stamp, and a piece of paper in the right hands at the right time.

*Deliver it,* Thorn had said. *File it. Not sell it. File it. Swear it, Marchetti, or I'll haunt your bilge till the hull rots through.*

"You're already haunting my bilge," she told the tarp-covered body.

The map was worth money. She knew that the way she knew the river's depth by the color of the water — instantly, viscerally. There were men in Port Calloway who'd pay a year's cargo fees for a look at it, and men further upriver who'd pay in ways that didn't involve money. She could sell it. She could sell it and be gone before anyone knew she'd had it, and the promise to a dying stranger would dissolve like mist off the morning water, and she could keep moving, keep moving, keep—

She looked at the tarp. At the shape of a man who had trusted her with the only thing he had left.

"Damn you," she said again, and this time it had heat, and she folded the map along its old creases and put it inside her shirt, against her skin, where it felt less like paper and more like a coal.

* * *

They came at mid-morning.

She'd been expecting them since the fueling dock, if she was honest — had been half-expecting them since the moment she'd let Thorn aboard, because nothing that valuable traveled without a shadow. But knowing they were coming didn't make the sound of the outboard any easier to hear, that flat two-stroke buzz carrying upriver through the mist, and it didn't make the sight of them any easier to stomach when the skiff rounded the bend.

Three men. Two in plain work clothes with the specific emptiness of hired muscle. The third in a linen jacket that had no business on the Verrane, pressed and pale, with a folder under his arm and the smile of a man who'd never had to shout in his life.

"Morning," the pale man called, as the skiff came alongside. "Vessel inspection. Routine."

"Routine inspections don't come by skiff," Ulyana said. She stood at the rail with her arms crossed and her weight settled, the way she stood in weather. "They come with paperwork and a launch. Who are you?"

"Ostrow. Regional compliance, Meridian Consolidated." He said it smoothly, but she caught the half-beat before *compliance*, the way a card player catches a tell. "We're conducting a survey of unregistered claims along the upper corridor. Standard due diligence." His eyes moved past her, unhurried, cataloguing. The deck. The wheelhouse. The tarp.

The tarp.

"You've got a body under there, Captain," he said, and the smile didn't waver, which was somehow worse than if it had.

"I've got engine parts under there."

"May I?"

"No." She said it without inflection. "This is a working vessel, not a public dock. You want to inspect something, get a warrant and a government launch, and I'll be gone before you find either. Now get off my rail before you scratch the paint."

Ostrow studied her for a long moment. She let him. She'd spent ten years learning what men like him saw when they looked at her — a river rat, a cargo hauler, a woman with sun-cracked hands and no surname worth pronouncing — and she let him see exactly that. Let the map's weight press warm against her ribs and her face stay bored.

"Thorn boarded at the fueling dock two nights ago," Ostrow said. "Feverish. Half-drowned. A witness says a Kestrel took him on."

"A lot of boats use that dock."

"A lot of boats aren't captained by Ulyana Marchetti."

The name went through her like a fishhook. She kept her face still, but inside, something old and scarred turned over. *Marchetti.* She hadn't been called that on this river in a decade. She'd paid good money, in a manner of speaking, to stop being called that.

"Don't know the name," she said.

"Of course not." Ostrow stepped back into the skiff, unhurried, and adjusted his jacket. "Well. If a dying man should turn up on your deck, Captain — and I'm sure if he did, you'd do the humane thing and notify the authorities — we'd be very interested in his effects. Meridian rewards cooperation. Generously." He produced a card from his breast pocket and set it on her rail, weighting it with a coin. "And we're equally interested in people who aren't cooperative. It's remarkable, the paperwork problems a person can develop."

The skiff pulled away. Ulyana stood at the rail until the sound of the outboard faded, then picked up the card, read it — a name, an office in Port Calloway, nothing else — and dropped it over the side.

The coin she kept. She wasn't sentimental.

* * *

She buried Thorn at the next bend, in the strip of red clay and bamboo above the high-water mark, because the river took bodies and she couldn't stomach feeding him to the current that had already tried to kill him once. She dug with the spare shovel and sweated through her shirt and said no words over him because none of the words she had were honest. Then she sat on the bank for ten minutes with her hat over her eyes and let herself feel it — not grief exactly, she hadn't known him well enough for grief, but the specific weight of being the last person who knew a man's name.

Then she went back to the Kestrel and started the engine, because the rule was the rule. Never stop moving. Stopping was how they found you.

Except they'd already found her. That was the thing she kept circling back to as she took the boat out into the channel, hands easy on the wheel, mind running hard. Ostrow had her name — her real name, the name she'd buried with everything else — and he had the fueling dock, and he had a witness. He didn't have proof she had the map, but he didn't need proof. Men like Ostrow didn't need proof; they needed leverage, and *Marchetti* was leverage all by itself. There were people in the capital who would pay Meridian Consolidated a great deal to know where the estranged Marchetti daughter was spending her days.

Which meant the safe play — sell the map, run for the coast, disappear into the delta traffic — was no longer safe. The moment Ostrow's report went up the chain, she'd be a name on a list, and lists followed you to the coast. She'd spent ten years learning that the only thing you could outrun was the past of other people. Your own past traveled with you. It slept in your bilge.

So the arithmetic changed. If she was already implicated, then implication was a currency, and she might as well spend it on something.

She might as well file the claim.

The thought arrived fully formed and slightly terrifying, and she made herself look at it straight. File Thorn's claim. Thirty days. The escarpment. Monsoon season coming up the valley like a fist. A boat with a collision scar older than her last honest night's sleep. Against her: a syndicate, an enforcer she hadn't met yet but could already smell, and her own family name, which had a way of turning up like a bad debt.

For her: a map drawn by a man who'd spent thirty years learning that country. And the simple, ugly fact that she had nothing left to lose that she hadn't already lost.

"Fine," she said to the wheelhouse, to the river, to the tarp-shaped absence in the aft cabin. "Fine. But I'm keeping the boat."

* * *

The voice came from the aft deck at Kilometer Twelve.

"You're going the wrong way for the delta."

Ulyana's knife was in her hand before she'd finished turning. She'd checked that cabin twice. She'd checked it *three* times.

The woman standing by the stern rail was maybe thirty, brown-skinned, dark-haired, with the lean build of someone who climbed for a living and a canvas survey bag slung across her back like it had grown there. She had a cut on her cheekbone, crusted over, a day old. She held her hands up, palms out, and her eyes went to the knife without flinching.

"Nadia Reyes," she said. "Surveyor. And before you decide which side of me to put that — Elias Thorn was my mentor. I've been looking for him for two weeks."

Ulyana didn't lower the knife. "Thorn's dead."

Something moved through the woman's face — quick, controlled, but real, a crack in the composure and then the composure sealing over it. "I know," she said. "I saw the clay. Back at the bend. I've been following this boat since the fueling dock." She lowered her hands slowly. "I was going to come aboard last night. Then the skiff with the linen jacket showed up this morning, and I decided I'd rather introduce myself to you than to him."

"You stowed away on my boat."

"I hid on your boat. Stowing away implies I planned to stay hidden." She tilted her head toward the wheelhouse. "You're heading upriver. Toward the escarpment. That's Thorn's claim country, and that's where you'd be going if he gave you the map before he died." Her voice was steady, but Ulyana heard the wire under it, the effort of keeping it that way. "So. Did he?"

The map pressed against Ulyana's ribs, warm as a coal. Every instinct she had said *lie*, said *put her off at the next landing*, said *you don't know her, you don't owe her, you owe a dead man a filing and nothing else*. But instinct had also said the delta was safe this morning, and instinct was having a bad day.

"Who's hunting him?" Ulyana asked. "Really. Not the compliance-office version."

Nadia Reyes looked at her for a long moment, and whatever she saw in Ulyana's face made her decide something.

"Meridian Consolidated filed a competing claim on the escarpment corridor in March," she said. "Their permit is forged. I know it's forged because the survey data they submitted came out of Thorn's own notebooks — data he collected for a claim he never filed, data that was stolen from my office two years ago." Her jaw tightened. "Elias spent two years building the case against them and one night drowning in the Verrane with the proof sewn into his coat. And the man in the linen jacket — that's not compliance, Captain. That's Corvo Halstead's people. Halstead doesn't do compliance. He does endings."

Ulyana stood with the knife still in her hand and the engine humming under her feet and thought about a man in red clay above the high-water mark, and a name she'd spent a decade burying that had surfaced in one morning like a drowned body.

"You said Thorn was your mentor," she said.

"Twelve years. He taught me everything I know about reading rock."

"Then you know what's in his map better than I do." Ulyana finally lowered the knife, and nodded at the wheelhouse. "Get up here. You can read currents, you can tell me what's true about that permit, and you can start by explaining why a syndicate that already has a forged claim is still scared enough of a dead man's paper to send three skiffs after a cargo boat."

Nadia Reyes came forward along the deck, sure-footed as a cat, and something in Ulyana's chest — something that had been clenched since dawn — loosened by a single degree. Not trust. She wasn't a fool. But company, maybe. Company with its own reasons to burn the same house down.

"Because the forgery has a hole in it," Nadia said, reaching the wheelhouse. "And Thorn found the proof that fills it. And if that proof gets filed in the next thirty days, Meridian doesn't just lose the escarpment. They lose everything they've built on it." She looked out at the river running high and brown toward the plateau, and her voice dropped. "They know it too. That's why the skiffs. That's why him."

Ulyana put the wheel over and pointed the Kestrel's bow at the escarpment, a blue-gray wall at the edge of the world, and pushed the throttle forward until the old diesel settled into its working growl.

Thirty days. One map. One stranger with a cut on her face and a survey bag full of secrets she hadn't finished telling.

Behind them, far downstream, the two-stroke buzz of an outboard rose again over the water — and this time it wasn't alone.
