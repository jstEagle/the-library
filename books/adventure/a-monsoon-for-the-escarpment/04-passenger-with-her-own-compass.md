## 4. Passenger with Her Own Compass

The Kestrel ran the morning current with her usual stubborn grace, and Ulyana watched her passenger the way she'd watch a stranger handling her tools.

Nadia Reyes stood at the rail near the bow, one hand resting on the gunwale, reading the water. Not looking at it — *reading* it, the way Ulyana did, tracking the darker seams where deep channels ran, the pale braids of silt that meant sandbar, the way a fallen tree on the left bank bent the current into a long slow eddy. She didn't narrate what she saw. She just saw it.

"Sandbar ahead, starboard side," Nadia said, without turning around. "Maybe two hundred meters. The channel's going to pinch."

Ulyana checked the chart she had memorized years ago. The bar was there. It was always there this time of year. "I know."

"I figured you knew." Nadia turned then, and there was something in her face — not smugness, exactly. A kind of professional courtesy. *I'm not trying to take your boat from you. I'm showing you I can be worth my berth.*

Ulyana grunted and eased the wheel a degree to port. The Kestrel slid past the bar with a meter to spare, and neither of them said anything about it, which was its own kind of conversation.

Three days out from the fueling dock, and Ulyana still hadn't decided what to do with her. The woman had come aboard with Thorn's death still wet on all of them, claiming — plausibly, calmly, infuriatingly — that Elias Thorn had been her mentor for six years. That she'd taught him surveying notation, or he'd taught her; the story shifted in small details the way stories do when they're true but not whole. And when Ulyana asked why the syndicate's men had been asking after *her*, Nadia Reyes, at the same dock where they'd been asking after Thorn, Nadia had looked at her with those steady dark eyes and said:

"Because I used to work for them."

And then she'd gone below to sleep, as if that settled anything.

It settled nothing. Ulyana had spent the night turning it over. *Used to work for them* could mean a hundred things. It could mean she'd quit. It could mean she'd been fired for cause. It could mean she was still on their payroll and Corvo Halstead's people were hunting her the way you hunt a dog that's run off with something in its mouth.

So Ulyana watched her. And the more she watched, the less she understood, because everything Nadia did was either genuinely useful or genuinely unreadable, and often both.

She fixed the bilge pump's sticky intake valve without being asked, using a piece of gasket rubber Ulyana hadn't known she had. She read currents like a born riverwoman, though her hands were surveyor's hands, ink-stained and precise. She ate sparingly, slept lightly, and kept her gear packed in a single bag by the door as if she expected to be put ashore at any moment — or as if she wanted Ulyana to know she could leave at any moment, no hard feelings, no loose ends.

That last one bothered Ulyana most. People who kept their bags packed were people planning exits.

By midday they'd made good time, and Ulyana made her decision.

There was a place, forty kilometers upriver, where the official channel bent east around a long oxbow and the honest traffic went with it, dutiful and slow, past the customs post at Serano Bend. Smugglers and fuel-runners and people with reasons not to be counted took the western cut — a maze of flooded timber and dead channels the charts called the Verrane Backwater and the river people called, simply, the Cut. It saved six hours if you knew it. It cost you everything if you didn't.

Ulyana knew it. But she wasn't going to tell Nadia that.

Instead, approaching the fork, she said, "Customs at Serano Bend. They'll log us, ask questions, maybe radio ahead." She let the hesitation show, calculated down to the second. "Thorn's dead and we've got his map. You want to explain that to a customs officer?"

Nadia studied the water ahead, then studied her. "You have another route."

"Do I?"

"You grew up on this river. Everyone who runs cargo out here knows the Cut exists." A pause. "Whether you use it is your call. It's your boat."

"That's it? No argument?"

"What would I argue?" Nadia spread her hands. "You're asking me to trust you with an illegal shortcut. Fine. I trust you. Ask me something harder."

Ulyana held the wheel and felt the test tilting under her, not going the direction she'd planned. She'd expected evasion, or eagerness — the eager ones were the dangerous ones, too grateful, too quick to prove themselves. Instead she got this: level, unbothered, offering trust like it cost nothing.

Everything cost something. Ulyana had learned that before she learned to swim.

She took the Cut.

* * *

The Cut swallowed them in green shadow.

Flooded timber stood everywhere, gray trunks rising out of brown water, the drowned forest of some old logging operation gone back to the river decades ago. Channels braided and re-braided between the trunks, most of them lies. The water here moved slow and deep and silent, and it hid things — submerged fences, sunken barges, the occasional car body that locals had rolled off a bank for reasons nobody discussed.

Ulyana ran it at half throttle, standing, reading the surface the old way: the little dimples where current slid over a snag, the oily calm over deep water, the telltale V of debris caught on something just under. Nadia stood beside her at the rail, saying nothing, watching everything.

Halfway through, the channel narrowed between two great trunks leaning toward each other like conspirators, and beyond them the water split three ways.

Ulyana stopped the engine.

Silence dropped over the boat like a lid. Somewhere back in the trees a bird called, twice, and quit.

"Which one?" Ulyana asked.

Nadia looked at her. "You're testing me."

"I'm asking you."

"You're testing me." There was no anger in it. Almost admiration. "Fine." She stepped to the rail and studied the three channels. The left one looked best — wide, clean surface, obvious flow. The middle was choked with floating weed. The right was narrow and dark.

"The left is a trap," Nadia said. "Surface looks open because the current's feeding it from a side slough, not running through. See how the foam lines curve *into* it instead of along it? Dead end, probably a submerged fence across the mouth — I can see the posts if the light cooperates." She pointed right. "That one. It's narrow, but look at the drift — leaves moving through it steadily, same speed all the way down. Water's actually going somewhere."

Ulyana followed her finger. The posts were there, barely breaking the surface, furred with algae. She'd hit them herself, once, eleven years ago, and carried the scar on the Kestrel's hull ever since.

Nobody found those posts on their first pass. Nobody.

"Where did you learn to read water like that?" Ulyana asked quietly.

"Elias." Nadia's voice changed — went softer, rougher at the edges. "He said surveying is just learning to see what's actually there instead of what you expect. Rivers, land, people. Same skill." She turned to face Ulyana fully. "Take the right channel, Captain. And after we're through, ask me your real question. Because I can feel it sitting in your pocket like a knife."

Ulyana took the right channel. It was tight — she breathed in the trunks on both sides, close enough to touch — and opened after two hundred meters into a long straight reach of black water, and the Cut carried them through the rest of the drowned forest without another word between them.

At the far end, where the channel rejoined the main river and the afternoon opened up gold and wide, Ulyana finally asked it.

"Why does the syndicate want you?"

Nadia didn't answer right away. She watched the water. When she spoke, her voice was careful, each word set down like a stone in a crossing.

"Because I signed surveys for them. Concession assessments, boundary reports. And because two years ago I found something in the numbers that didn't add up, and I told them so, and they told me to correct my findings or lose my license." Her jaw tightened. "I corrected my findings."

"And now?"

"Now I keep my head down and freelance. Small jobs. Honest ones." She laughed, short and bitter. "Mostly honest."

"*Mostly* honest," Ulyana repeated.

"Captain." Nadia turned, and the late light caught her face, and Ulyana made the mistake of holding her gaze a beat too long. "Elias trusted you with his map. I'm trusting you with the fact that I don't trust myself to tell you everything yet. Not because I'm working against you. Because part of what I have to tell you would make you put me ashore, and I need to get where we're going first. Both of us need that. When we get there, you'll have all of it, every ugly piece, and you can decide what to do with me."

Ulyana looked at her for a long moment. The river ran gold around the hull. Somewhere behind them, thirty days ticked down toward nothing.

"You get until the Escarpment base," Ulyana said. "Then everything. Or you swim."

"Fair terms." Nadia almost smiled. "You drive a hard bargain, Marchetti."

The name landed like a slap. Ulyana's hands went white on the wheel.

"I never told you my name."

"No," Nadia agreed softly. "You didn't."

* * *

Nadia watched the captain's shoulders and understood, with the particular clarity of someone who has just made a serious mistake, exactly how much trouble she was in.

She hadn't meant to say it. It had slipped out the way truths slip out when you're tired and the light is beautiful and the person across from you has spent three days being more decent than they want you to notice. *Marchetti.* The name from Elias's files, from the syndicate's dossiers, from the family tree that connected a runaway cargo pilot to the mining fortune that had built half the concessions on this river — including, quite possibly, the fraudulent one Elias had died carrying proof of.

Ulyana Marchetti. Daughter of Aldo Marchetti. Sister of the attorney who signed the syndicate's papers. The woman whose family name was the key to everything, and who clearly wanted that name buried at the bottom of the Verrane with the rest of her history.

Nadia watched her knuckles, watched the rigid line of her back, and did the only thing she could think of.

"I'm sorry," she said. "That was cruel, and I did it on purpose, and I'm not even sure why."

Ulyana didn't turn. "Why *did* you?"

"Because you were winning." Nadia heard her own voice, flat and honest, and hated it and needed it. "You tested me all day — the Cut, the channels, the questions — and I passed, and passing felt good, and I wanted to hurt you a little for making me want your approval. That's the truth. It's not a good truth."

The engine hummed. The banks slid by. A fish jumped somewhere in the gold light, a bright brief arc, and was gone.

"My father sold concessions to that syndicate for twenty years," Ulyana said at last, to the river rather than to Nadia. "My brother draws up their paperwork. I haven't spoken to either of them in ten years, and I will burn that map before I let them use me to legitimize one more forged claim." She glanced sideways, finally, and her eyes were dry and furious and alive. "So whatever you're not telling me, Reyes, it better not be about my family."

"It's about your family," Nadia said quietly. "But not the way you think."

Ulyana killed the throttle and let the Kestrel drift, and the silence that came down over the boat was the silence of deep water, and Nadia met it without flinching, because Elias had taught her that too: you hold your ground when the current comes, or it takes you.

Behind them, far downstream, a single engine note carried against the wind — steady, patient, gaining.

Neither of them had to say whose boat it might be. Ulyana's hand found the throttle, and the Kestrel leaned forward into the dusk, running hard for the darkening north.
