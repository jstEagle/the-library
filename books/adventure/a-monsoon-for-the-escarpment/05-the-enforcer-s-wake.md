## 5. The Enforcer's Wake

The trading post at Sable Landing announced itself the way all good river towns did: by smell first. Diesel and drying fish and the sweet rot of overripe mangoes stacked in crates on the dock, then the sound — generators, a radio playing something tinny and half-loved, men arguing about fuel prices in three languages at once.

Ulyana brought the Kestrel in against the current with the ease of long habit, feathering the throttle so the old boat kissed the pilings instead of hitting them. Behind her, Nadia coiled line with hands that had clearly done it before but not recently, her rhythm a half-beat off, corrected each time she noticed Ulyana watching.

"Fuel, water, ice," Ulyana said, ticking it off. "Twenty minutes. We don't linger."

"You said that at the last place."

"And we left in twenty minutes."

"We left in forty because you stopped to argue with a man about spark plugs."

"He was wrong about spark plugs." She killed the engine. The silence after was enormous, filled immediately by the river slapping the hull and the post's generator drone. "There's a difference between lingering and being right."

Nadia's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "I'll get the ice."

Ulyana watched her go up the dock planks, tall and deliberate, surveyor's stride even on ground that shifted. Then she made herself stop watching and went to deal with the fuel man, because thinking about the way Nadia moved was a luxury she hadn't budgeted for.

The fuel man was Beto, gray-bearded, missing two fingers on his left hand from an outboard accident he told everyone about differently each year. He pumped diesel and talked, which was his nature, and Ulyana listened with the part of her mind that always listened, sorting talk for anything useful the way she sorted water for snags.

"—strangers up from the delta," Beto was saying. "Big boat. Not a working boat, you understand. Polished. Air conditioning, I heard. Who needs air conditioning on a river?"

"Tourists," Ulyana said.

Beto gave her a look over the nozzle. "Tourists don't ask questions like these ones ask. They asked me who runs cargo north this time of year. I said nobody sane." He paused, letting the pump gurgle. "They showed a picture. Woman, dark hair, scar through one eyebrow. And a boat. Green hull, white cabin, name of a bird."

The diesel smell suddenly seemed very loud.

"I told them I never saw such a boat," Beto said quietly, not looking at her. "But Beto is getting old, Ulyana. Old men forget things. You should maybe not be here when they remember."

She paid him more than the fuel cost. He took it without looking down, the way men do when they're ashamed to be bought and grateful anyway.

* * *

Nadia was coming back across the dock with a block of ice wrapped in sacking balanced on one shoulder when Ulyana saw them.

Three men at the far end of the landing, dressed in clothes too clean for the river. Not matching, but coordinated the way expensive things are coordinated without trying — linen shirts, good boots, sunglasses pushed up. One carried nothing. One carried a rifle casually, slung backward like a farmer carries a hoe. The third stood slightly ahead of the other two, and he was the one Ulyana's eyes kept returning to, because he was smiling.

He was a big man gone soft at the edges, or pretending to be — silver at the temples, a face built for boardrooms that had learned rivers. He moved like someone who had never once needed to hurry, because everything he wanted came to him or was brought to him, and if neither happened, he sent the two men behind him.

He was walking toward the Kestrel.

Ulyana calculated distances. Nadia, ten meters from the gangplank. The men, thirty meters from it. Her own position: at the rail, exposed, holding a fuel receipt like a weapon.

"Nadia," she called, mild as milk. "Ice goes in the cooler under the wheel. Not the forward locker — the seal's bad."

It was nonsense and Nadia knew it was nonsense, but she read the tone underneath, and her stride didn't change and her face didn't change, and she came up the plank and stepped aboard and put the ice away exactly where told.

Then the smiling man was at the foot of the gangplank, and he stopped there, which Ulyana noted. A man who stops at the edge of your territory before being invited understands territory.

"Good morning," he said. His accent was delta-coastal, educated over. "Is this vessel available for charter? Or am I intruding?"

"It's a cargo boat," Ulyana said. "We haul diesel drums and chickens. Charters are above our station."

"Everything worth having is above our station until someone offers enough." He climbed the plank anyway, unhurried, and the two men stayed below, flanking the mooring lines without touching them. It was choreography. It was very good choreography. "Corvo Halstead." He extended a hand.

Ulyana shook it. His grip was dry and precise, calibrated to be memorable without being threatening. Everything about him would be like that, she suspected. Calibrated.

"Captain Marchetti," she heard herself say, and hated the syllables even as they left her mouth — no. No, that wasn't what she'd meant to—

"Marchetti." Corvo Halstead tasted the name like wine. "Now, that's interesting."

"It's a common name."

"On the Verrane? It's a dynasty." He smiled wider. "May I come aboard properly, Captain? I have a business proposition, and my knees are too old for gangplanks."

Every instinct she owned said *cast off now*, with him standing on the plank, let him swim or fall, lose the ice, lose the fuel top-off, lose twenty minutes of advantage. But the two men below were already untying nothing, just standing by the lines, and Beto was watching from the pump with his remaining eight fingers very still, and if she ran, she confirmed everything, and if she ran, they'd simply follow, and a chase upriver with a half-full tank was arithmetic she'd lose.

Also — and this was the thought that frightened her most, later, when she examined it — some cold, curious cellar of her mind wanted to see how well she could lie.

"Mind the step," she said. "She leaks a little. Character."

* * *

He sat in her wheelhouse like he'd been born in it, hat off, hands folded, taking in the chart table, the coffee ring stains, the rosary of dried chilis Nadia had hung over the doorway for reasons she still hadn't explained. He looked at everything the way an appraiser looks.

"Tea?" Ulyana offered, because offering things made you the host, and hosts set terms.

"Generous of you. Yes."

She put the kettle on the spirit stove, buying seconds, and Corvo Halstead watched her do it with polite patience, and when she finally sat across from him with three enamel cups — Nadia leaning in the doorway, arms crossed, saying nothing — he opened like a man unwrapping a gift he already knew.

"I represent the Meridian Consolidated Concession Group," he said. "You may have heard of us. We hold mineral rights across most of the southern escarpment country."

"I've heard of you."

"Then you've also heard rumors, I'm sure. Rumors about an old prospector. Elias Thorn." He said the name gently, almost fondly. "A sad case. Brilliant man once. Lost his claim, lost his mind, lost his health, in roughly that order. Toward the end he was telling anyone who'd listen about a seam he'd found. Unregistered. Undocumented. A fairy story, really — but fairy stories make people do foolish things, Captain. They buy maps from dying men. They promise deliveries. They point leaky little boats at plateaus that will kill them."

The kettle began its low climb toward whistling. Ulyana reached over and lifted it off the flame, and the small domestic motion gave her face somewhere to be while she arranged herself.

"I met a Thorn once," she said. "Years ago. Sold me catfish at Kilometer Twelve. Smelled like a wet dog."

"That would have been his cousin. Also dead now, incidentally." Corvo accepted his tea. "The Elias Thorn I mean boarded a fuel dock four days ago, feverish, half-drowned, and according to witnesses spent his last hours in the company of a certain green-hulled cargo boat." He sipped. "He died the next morning. Terrible timing. Because Meridian has been searching for that man for eleven years, and now the only thing he ever had of value seems to have evaporated along with him. Unless, of course, it didn't."

"Sounds like a problem for Meridian," Ulyana said.

"It is. And I solve problems. That's my entire function, Captain — I'm the eraser at the end of the company's pencil." He smiled to show the metaphor was a joke. It wasn't. "So here is my proposition, plainly, because I find plainness saves everyone embarrassment. If Mr. Thorn gave anything to anyone aboard this vessel — papers, drawings, directions, a deathbed rambling — Meridian will purchase it. Generously. Enough to retire that beautiful rusting hull of yours and buy two boats that don't leak. Enough that no Marchetti ever has to run cargo again."

The name landed exactly where he'd aimed it. She felt it strike the old bruise and she felt her face do absolutely nothing, and that nothing was the smoothest thing she'd ever done.

"I don't know what you think a Marchetti is," she said, "but the ones I'm related to wouldn't cross the street to spit on me. Whatever dynasty you're shopping for, I got cut from the catalog."

"Mm." He studied her over the rim of the cup. "And the map?"

"What map?"

"The one you're not asking me about."

"There's no map I'm not asking you about. There's no map at all. There's a dead man I gave tea to because he was dying, and there's a fuel bill I'd like to get back to earning." She stood, gathered his cup with gentle finality. "If that's all, Mr. Halstead, I've got current to beat before noon."

He didn't move. For a long moment he simply looked at her, and she understood that whatever was happening behind those pleasant eyes was happening slowly and thoroughly, the way a flood fills a valley — no drama, just level after level going under.

"You lie well, Captain," he said at last, rising, settling his hat. "That's rare. Most people lie badly and it wastes both our mornings." At the wheelhouse door he paused, one hand on the frame, and turned back with the smile back in place. "A professional observation, nothing more. But I'll leave you with something free, since you won't sell. Meridian filed a competing claim on the escarpment concession last week. Properly stamped, properly witnessed, attorney of record one Marco Marchetti." He watched her the whole time he said it, hungry for the flicker, and she starved him — starved him so completely that something almost like respect crossed his face. "Family names open doors, Captain. Even cut-out-of-the-catalog ones. Do give my regards to whoever's holding Thorn's paper. Tell them the offer doesn't expire. Offers like mine don't."

He went down the gangplank without hurrying. The two men fell in behind him. At the end of the dock he stopped, spoke briefly to Beto, and patted the old man's shoulder in a way that made Ulyana's stomach drop — a pat that said *we'll talk about what you forgot*.

Then he was gone into the town's noise, and the wake of him stayed on the boat like oil on water.

* * *

Nadia spoke first. "Marco Marchetti. Your brother."

"My brother." Ulyana stood at the rail, watching the dock, watching Beto pretend to work. "He signed their paper."

"You knew he worked for them?"

"I knew he took their money. Signing is new." Her voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "Or maybe it isn't new. Maybe signing is just the part I finally get to see."

"Ulyana." Nadia came closer, low. "He offered to buy the map. That means he doesn't know where it is. That means we still have the lead on him."

"It means he suspects." She turned. "There's a difference, and the difference is that suspicion follows and knowledge ambushes. Right now we're followed. I'd like to keep it that way."

"So we cast off."

"So we cast off." She was already moving, throwing off the mooring lines, and the mechanical work steadied her the way it always did — hands busy, mind clear, the river waiting wide and brown and indifferent. Nadia took the bow line, and they worked without speaking, and the Kestrel slid off the pilings and swung her nose north.

It was only when they were a kilometer out, the trading post shrunk to smudge, that Ulyana let herself feel it — the whole shape of the last hour replaying in her chest. How easily the lies had come. *I met a Thorn once. Sold me catfish.* She hadn't hesitated. Hadn't sweated. Hadn't done any of the clumsy tells that honest people do when they break their own rules. She had lied to a dangerous man's face with the fluency of long practice, and beneath the fear there had been — God help her — pleasure. The clean click of it, like a well-made knot.

Her father lied like that. That was the whole inheritance, really; that was the family trade, before the land and the name. She'd sworn at nineteen, walking away down a gravel road with one bag, that she would rather be poor and true than rich and slippery, and she had mostly kept it, and keeping it had cost her everything except the boat.

And today it had taken her four minutes to spend that vow like small change, and she'd enjoyed the spending.

"You're quiet," Nadia said from the wheelhouse door.

"I'm driving."

"You're brooding and driving. You're talented."

Despite everything, Ulyana laughed — a short, surprised sound. "Go check the bilge. If we're taking on water, I want to know before the next ghost boards."

Nadia went. Ulyana held the wheel and watched the current slide past and told herself the feeling in her chest was only fear.

It was partly fear. That much was true.

Behind them, far downstream, a polished white boat with air conditioning rounded the bend and settled into their wake, patient as erosion, keeping a distance that was itself a message: *I know where you're going. Take your time.*

Ulyana opened the throttle anyway, and the Kestrel leaned north into the rising wind, and the first clouds of the early monsoon stood up on the horizon like a wall being built.
