## 6. Clouds Over the Concession

Nadia heard the weather before she understood it — a crackle in the Kestrel's radio that didn't belong to any voice, a static hiss like sand pouring through an hourglass. She was alone on the deck at first light, Ulyana below with the engine apart again, and the morning had come up the color of old pewter. Not the clean gray of high cloud. Something heavier, bruised at the edges, stacked in towers over the western escarpment.

The radio spat, resolved.

*"—advisory issued oh-four-hundred hours. Monsoon onset now forecast for the Verrane basin within seventy-two hours. All stations repeat: onset within seventy-two hours. This is three weeks ahead of seasonal norms. River traffic advised to seek moorage immediately—"* Static swallowed the rest.

Nadia stood very still, one hand on the rail, and let the words settle into her body the way cold water settles into boots. Seventy-two hours. Three weeks early. Every surveyor's calendar she'd memorized, every flood-stage chart Thorn had drilled into her over six years of fieldwork, all of it just became obsolete paper.

Behind her, below decks, a wrench rang off metal and Ulyana swore with the fluency of a woman who'd been swearing at that engine for a decade.

"Nadia." Not a question. Ulyana always knew where she was; Nadia had stopped finding that unnerving around day four and started finding it something else entirely, which was its own problem. "You hear that?"

"I heard it."

"Come tell me what I already know, then. Misery likes company."

Nadia climbed down into the engine bay, where the air smelled of diesel and hot iron and the particular sourness of bilge water that never fully left a boat this age. Ulyana lay half under the engine block, grease to her elbows, a rag knotted in her hair, and even like that — even filthy and folded into a space barely big enough for her shoulders — she managed to look like someone in command of her circumstances. It was, Nadia thought, the most attractive thing about her, and there was stiff competition.

"Monsoon's early," Nadia said. "Seventy-two hours, maybe less. The advisory put onset at the headwaters first."

Ulyana slid out from under the engine and sat up, forearms on her knees. For a long moment she said nothing, and Nadia watched her do the arithmetic — not the numbers, Nadia could do the numbers, but the *weight* of them, the way a pilot weighs a river she can't see yet.

"How much rise?" Ulyana asked finally.

"At the escarpment tributaries? Four meters, five if the catchment saturates. Main channel here, two and a half, three." Nadia crouched across from her, knees almost touching. "But it won't be gentle. Early monsoons come down angry. The ground's dry from low season — it won't absorb anything for the first week. First rain runs straight off."

"Faster water."

"Much faster. And debris. Whole trees coming down the gorge like battering rams."

Ulyana nodded slowly. "And checkpoints?"

"Closed. They close at stage-three flood, always have. Once they're closed—" Nadia spread her hands. "Nothing official moves. Nothing gets filed. Nothing gets inspected. The registry office upriver will lock its doors and everyone behind those doors is untouchable until the water drops."

"And our window closes with them." Ulyana wiped her hands on the rag, slow, methodical, buying time. "Twenty-six days left on Thorn's permits. If we don't beat the closures—"

"We don't file. We sit out the monsoon wherever we're moored when it hits, and Corvo sits too, but he only has to sit. He already knows where we'll be. We'd be a boat at anchor for four months with a map sewn into a cushion." Nadia heard her own voice go flat and clinical, the surveyor's voice, the one that reported facts without feelings because the feelings were too large to carry in the same breath. "He wouldn't even need to board us. He'd just wait for the water to drop and take it off our corpse."

Ulyana looked at her. In the dim of the engine bay her eyes were unreadable, which Nadia had learned meant the opposite — that everything was readable, all at once, and Ulyana was choosing which part to show.

"You've been thinking about this," Ulyana said. "Since before the radio."

"Since Kilometer Forty." Since the night they'd patched the split seam by lamplight, since Nadia had confessed the forged credentials and waited for judgment and received, instead, grace — quiet, unadorned, more disorienting than anger would have been. "Ulyana. The storm isn't just a threat. It's the only door we get."

*

They came up on deck together and the sky had thickened while they talked. The towers over the escarpment had merged into a single rampart, blue-black at the base, and the wind had swung around to the west with a wet mineral smell underneath it. The river ran low and brown-green beneath them, lazy as a sleeping animal, and Nadia found herself reading it the way she read all water now — not as it was, but as it would be. Where the sandbars would drown. Where the cutbanks would slump. Where the current would gather itself into ropes and start pulling.

Ulyana stood at the wheel without touching it, staring upstream.

"Say it," she said. "Whatever you've worked out. Say all of it."

So Nadia said it. She laid it out the way she'd once laid out traverse plans for Thorn, tapping points along the rail as if the river were a chart: "The flooded river is faster. Two, three times our current speed against the current — no. *With* it, going upriver we lose some, but the channel changes too. The narrows that are impassable at low water become runnable at flood, because the rocks that gut boats disappear under four meters of water. The Throat—" she saw Ulyana flinch at the name, and kept going anyway, "—the Throat is a death trap in dry season and a highway in flood. Locals run it every monsoon. You told me yourself your friend Dario ran cargo through it for nine seasons."

"Dario also drowned," Ulyana said.

"Dario drowned running it *late*, in the second week, when the debris is thickest and the water's at peak. Nobody runs it in the first forty-eight hours of rise. That's the window. The water's high enough to cover the teeth, not yet full of trees." Nadia turned to face her fully. "Corvo won't know that. Corvo's not a river man. He'll wait for the storm to pass because waiting is what enforcers do — they hold position, they contain risk. But we're not containing anything. We're on a clock. The storm that terrifies him is the thing that saves us."

Ulyana was quiet for so long that Nadia counted the drips off the awning. Eleven. Twelve.

"You're asking me to run a river I've spent ten years respecting," Ulyana said at last, "at triple flow, through a gorge with a graveyard's worth of submerged trees, in the first storm cells of an early monsoon. On a boat I patched with epoxy and prayer eight days ago."

"Yes."

"The seam at Kilometer Forty."

"Holds to stage four. I checked your patch work myself. It's ugly but it's sound."

"It's *ugly*," Ulyana repeated, and something cracked in her face — not a smile exactly, but the place where a smile lived, showing through. "You're telling me my boat is ugly and sound and you want to take her into the Throat."

"I'm telling you the alternative is losing everything Thorn died carrying. Yes. That's what I'm telling you."

*

Ulyana walked the boat after that. Nadia watched her do it — stem to stern, testing seams with her thumb, checking the pump, coiling lines that were already coiled, the ritual liturgy of a woman making peace with a decision her hands hadn't agreed to yet. Nadia stayed at the rail with the radio log open on her knee and pretended not to watch, and failed, and stopped pretending.

There was a version of this conversation that happened differently, Nadia knew. There was a version where she'd pitched the storm run out of desperation, voice shaking, and Ulyana had refused, and they'd moored somewhere safe and lost everything slowly. Nadia had lived that version in her head all night, which was why she'd pitched it instead out of the surveyor's calm, because dread was a current and you couldn't fight a current, you could only read it and set your sail.

Her whole life she'd treated fear as information. Thorn taught her that. *Fear tells you where the edge is,* he used to say, tapping her hand-drawn contour maps with a blunt finger. *Your job is to decide whether the edge is a wall or a doorway.* She'd thought, for years, that it was a wall — the syndicate, the forged credentials, the slow blackmail that had shrunk her career down to whatever jobs nobody respectable wanted. She'd run the edges her whole adult life, taking contracts at the margins, staying small enough to be invisible.

Then Elias Thorn had shown up half-drowned at a fueling dock with a map sewn into his coat, and every wall she'd mapped turned out to have a door in it, and the doors were all standing open at once, and the only price of walking through was terror.

The radio crackled again at midday. A second advisory, terser than the first: checkpoints on the middle Verrane closing within thirty-six hours. District offices closing with them. After closure, no filings accepted until stage readings dropped below three.

Nadia wrote the times down in the log with a pencil gone soft from humidity, and her hand was steady, and she was grateful for that small mechanical mercy.

Ulyana came back aft and stood beside her at the rail, close enough that their shoulders touched. Neither of them moved away.

"If we run it," Ulyana said, "and the seam lets go mid-gorge—"

"It won't."

"If it does."

Nadia made herself look at the water instead of at Ulyana, because looking at Ulyana while saying the next part would have cost more than she had budgeted. "Then we beach her, same as Kilometer Forty, and patch her in the rain, and keep going. Or we swim. I can swim. Can you?"

A breath of laughter from Ulyana, short and dark. "I grew up on this river. I could swim before I could write my name."

"There it is, then. Worst case, we lose the boat and keep the map. Best case—" Nadia finally turned, and let herself say the true thing, the thing under the navigation talk. "Best case, in nine days we're standing at a registry counter with a stamped claim, and Corvo Halstead is holding a worthless permit to a plateau he can't reach until the water drops, and by then it's ours. Documented. Inspected. Real. The storm doesn't just hide us, Ulyana. It *legitimizes* us. Everything filed before the closures stands. Everything after is his problem."

Ulyana stared upstream. The first cell had detached from the rampart now, a gray column leaning against the horizon, and beneath it the river lay flat and innocent as glass.

"My father used to say the monsoon was the river collecting its debts," she said quietly. "Everyone who took shortcuts in the dry season pays interest when the water comes." Her jaw tightened. "I haven't taken shortcuts. I've done everything slow and legal and careful for ten years, and it's kept me alive and kept me nothing. So fine." She turned, and the unreadable eyes were readable now, all of it, resolve laid open like a chart. "We ride it. First rise, we're moving. Before the checkpoints close, before the debris comes down, before Corvo figures out that his worst weather is our best road."

Nadia exhaled a breath she'd been holding since dawn.

"But," Ulyana added, and held up one greasy finger, "we provision today. Spare fuel, spare patches, spare everything. And we sleep in shifts starting tonight, because when the rise comes, we move within the hour, and I don't run rivers on a tired crew."

"Crew," Nadia repeated. "Is that what I am?"

"That's what you are." Ulyana said it lightly, but her hand found Nadia's shoulder on the way past — brief, warm, deliberate — and stayed a half-second longer than the word required. "Get the tarp off the forward hatch. If the sky's going to fall on my boat, it can fall on canvas first."

*

They worked through the afternoon while the light went strange and greenish and the air pressure dropped enough that Nadia's ears popped twice. They lashed the fuel drums, doubled the mooring lines, stowed everything that could become a projectile. Ulyana stripped, cleaned, and reassembled the raw-water intake with the economy of long habit, and Nadia ran soundings off the bow with the lead line, calling depths of a river that was about to stop being this river at all.

At dusk the first rain came — not the monsoon, not yet, just an outrider, fat warm drops that struck the deck like thrown gravel and raised a smell of dust and iron off the planks. It lasted eleven minutes and passed eastward, and in its wake the whole western sky had changed: lower, darker, organized. Committed.

Nadia stood at the rail drying the radio log with her shirttail and felt the dread arrive on schedule, right on cue, right where it always lived in her chest. She examined it the way she examined a contour line. *Fear tells you where the edge is.*

Except that for the first time in her life, the edge wasn't a wall. It was a doorway, and it was flooding open, and on the other side of it was a claim with both their names on it and a woman at the wheel who'd chosen the dangerous weight of staying.

Below, Ulyana called up: "Radio check. Anything new?"

Nadia keyed the set, scanned the band, and froze.

The advisory channel was repeating the closure schedule. But underneath it, faint, on a frequency the syndicate's patrol launches used — a frequency Nadia probably shouldn't have recognized, and did — a voice was reading coordinates. Slowly. Twice each.

She knew the numbers. She'd drawn them herself, two years ago, on a survey she'd never been paid for: the upper approach channels above the Throat.

Corvo wasn't waiting for the storm.

He was staging above it.

"Ulyana," Nadia said, and her steady surveyor's voice finally shook. "You need to hear this."
