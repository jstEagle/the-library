## 7. Leaking at Kilometer Forty

The river told her first.

Ulyana felt it in the wheel before she heard it — a softness in the helm response, the Kestrel answering a half-beat late, like a dancer who'd been drinking. Ten years of running this water had taught her to read the boat through her palms, and right now her palms were saying something was wrong below the waterline.

She throttled back and listened. Engine steady. Current steady, though it had thickened overnight with the first upstream runoff, gone from tea-colored to the brown of strong coffee. The sky to the southwest had been building all morning, towers of cloud stacked like cargo nobody had lashed down properly.

Then she heard it. Not a sound exactly. A rhythm under the engine's rhythm. A wet slap-slap-slap from the bilge that didn't belong.

"Nadia." She kept her voice level, the way you did when panic was contagious and there was only one other person aboard. "Get the flashlight. Check the bilge."

Nadia appeared in the wheelhouse doorway, hair tied back, surveyor's notebook still open in one hand. She'd been plotting their position by dead reckoning since the radio had started spitting static an hour ago. "How bad?"

"Bad enough that I'm asking instead of telling."

Nadia dropped through the hatch. Ulyana held the wheel and counted seconds the way she'd learned to count them during the Verrane flood of nine years ago, when a barge captain named Oskar had waited too long to check his bilge and they'd pulled his body out of the shallows two days later, tangled in reeds, his pockets full of unspent wages.

Thirty seconds. Forty-five.

"Ulyana." Nadia's voice came up the hatchway flattened and strange. "There's water over the stringers. It's coming fast."

* * *

The scar was an old one. Ulyana knew it the way you knew a scar on your own body — the Kestrel had taken a collision six years back, a sunken piling during high water that had opened a gash along the port chine just aft of midships. She'd welded it herself, badly, with borrowed equipment and no money for a proper yard job. The patch had held through five low-water seasons because low water forgave everything. But the river was rising now, weeks ahead of schedule, and rising water pressed where low water never did.

The split ran maybe forty centimeters, seam weld cracked open like a knuckle, and the river came through it in a steady pulsing rush that matched their speed through the water. Every kilometer they traveled, they drank the Verrane.

Kilometer forty. She'd remember that number later, the way you remembered the address of every bad thing.

"We beach," Ulyana said. "Now."

"The sandbar ahead—" Nadia started.

"I see it."

The bar sat on the inside of the bend, pale and new, the kind of deposit the river built and destroyed within a single season. Perfect. Sandbars were honest that way: they existed precisely long enough to be useful and not one day more. Ulyana brought the Kestrel in bow-first, easy, feathering the throttle until the hull kissed bottom with a grinding shudder that went up through the deck plates and into her teeth. She let the current push them the last meter so the boat settled into the sand rather than striking it, then killed the engine.

Silence, except for the bilge pump cycling, and the river sliding past, and somewhere far off a sound like a door closing — thunder, maybe, or the escarpment country clearing its throat.

Ulyana stood at the wheel a moment longer than she needed to. Her hands were shaking. She made fists until they stopped.

"You okay?" Nadia asked.

"Peachy." She grabbed the tool bag. "Get the tarp off the spare drum. We're going to need dry space over that seam if it rains before we're done."

* * *

Patching a hull on a sandbar was surgery in a gutter. The water in the bilge had to go before the epoxy would bite, so they bailed and pumped and bailed while the afternoon leaned toward evening and the clouds to the southwest organized themselves into something with intent. Ulyana stripped to her undershirt and worked lying on her side in the bilge, headlamp strapped on, grinding the old weld back to bright metal while rust flakes and river water ran down her forearm.

The Kestrel around her, exposed. That was the part nobody warned you about, owning a boat. Everyone thought the danger was the river. The real danger was this — the moment you opened your vessel up and saw how thin the skin was between you and all that brown water wanting in. Thirty years old, this hull. Older than her career, older than her exile, older than the promise she'd made herself at nineteen never to depend on anything that could be taken away.

And here she was, elbow-deep in its wound, depending on it absolutely.

"You're talking to her," Nadia said from above. Ulyana realized she'd been muttering.

"She listens better than most people."

"What are you saying?"

"That she's held together longer than she had any right to." Ulyana fed wire through the clamping plate. "Same as me."

Nadia crouched at the hatch edge, silhouetted against a sky going the color of a bruise. "You know, most people who run rivers the way you do either love boats or hate themselves. You've never struck me as someone who hates herself."

"You haven't known me a week."

"I've known you long enough to read a hull." Nadia passed down the resin tin. "You handle her like she's the last honest thing in your life."

Ulyana mixed epoxy by feel, hardener to base, counting under her breath. The smell of it cut through the bilge stink, sharp and chemical and somehow comforting — the smell of fixing things. "Careful, surveyor. That sounded almost personal."

"It was."

Ulyana looked up. Nadia's face was half shadow, half the orange glow of the work lamp, and she wasn't smiling, wasn't teasing, just looking at her with that steady attention she gave to contour lines and current seams — like Ulyana was terrain worth mapping accurately.

Ulyana broke the look first. Pressed the fiberglass cloth against the seam. Started laying glass.

* * *

They worked until full dark, by which time the patch was three layers deep and clamped, and the bilge pump was gaining ground for the first time in hours. Ulyana climbed out of the hull stiff as an old woman and stood on the sandbar in the wind, and that was when she really saw the sky.

The storm cells had come together while they worked. What had been scattered towers at noon was now a single wall of cloud across the entire southwestern horizon, black at the base, flickering internally like something alive trapped inside. The wind had swung around and picked up. It carried a smell she knew too well — wet earth, ozone, the green rot smell of the flooded forest coming awake.

"That's early," Nadia said beside her. "That's weeks early."

"The monsoon doesn't send invitations." Ulyana wiped her hands on her trousers. "It used to arrive like clockwork. Now it arrives whenever it wants. Climate's broken the calendar same as everything else."

"How long do we have?"

Ulyana judged the wall of cloud, the wind, the pressure sitting on her eardrums like a thumb. "If we're lucky, till tomorrow night. If we're not, till dawn." She turned to look at the Kestrel, beached and patched and listing slightly, her whole life sitting on a sandbar that wouldn't exist next month. "We can't outrun it to the next checkpoint. Not with a fresh patch. We ride it out here, or we find shelter downstream."

"There's nothing downstream but the gorge approaches."

"Which is why we're not moving tonight." She said it firmly enough that it became true. "A fresh patch needs cure time anyway. Twelve hours minimum before I trust it against real pressure. And that storm—"

She stopped. Because looking at the storm, looking at the boat, she felt something crack in her chest that had nothing to do with welding.

Ten years. Ten years of never stopping, of keeping the Kestrel moving because movement meant no debts collected, no questions answered, no roots for anyone to cut. The boat had been the rule and the reason and the armor, all three. As long as the Kestrel moved, Ulyana Marchetti — Ulyana Dunmore, Ulyana whatever-name-the-dock-papers said that week — was untouchable. A wake on the water. Nothing to grab hold of.

And now here she stood on a sandbar at kilometer forty with her armor holed and the sky falling, and the strange thing, the terrible thing, was that she wasn't alone. Nadia was already hauling the storm tarp out of the locker without being asked, rigging lines over the wheelhouse, working with the competence of someone who'd done rough camps in worse places. And Ulyana caught herself thinking: *I don't want to do the next storm without her.*

That was how armor failed. Not with a collision, not with a crack in the chine. With a thought like that, small and warm and fatal.

"Hey." Nadia's voice, close. "Where'd you go?"

"Nowhere." Ulyana took the line she handed over. "Help me get this last tie-down. Storm's going to hit harder than the river ever could."

* * *

They ate cold rice and tinned fish in the wheelhouse with the lamp turned low, listening to the wind climb through its registers. Ulyana checked the bilge twice an hour. The patch held. The pump cycled less and less often, and each time it stayed silent longer, something in her chest unclenched by a degree.

Near midnight Nadia fell asleep in the deck chair, notebook open on her knee, pencil still hooked in her fingers. Ulyana covered her with a blanket and stood at the window a long time, watching lightning walk the horizon in slow silent strokes, counting the seconds to thunder, doing the arithmetic every river pilot knew: distance, time, how much night remained.

Nine days on the permits. A hole in her hull mended with glass and stubbornness. A woman asleep behind her who knew her name — both names, actually, though Nadia didn't know she knew the second one yet.

The thunder arrived, closer now, and beneath it Ulyana heard the river change pitch. Rising. Faster than it should be. She put her hand flat against the window glass and felt the vibration of all that water gathering itself upstream, in the high country, on the escarpment, coming down toward them with the patience of something that had all the time in the world.

"Hold together," she told the boat quietly. "Both of us."

The first rain hit the roof like gravel, and far downstream, where the gorge began, the lightning lit up water standing in white walls — and something else, briefly, at the mouth of the narrows. A running light. Moving against the current.

Nobody moved against the current. Nobody except someone who didn't care what the river cost.

Ulyana woke Nadia with a hand on her shoulder. "Trouble," she said. "Get up. We're leaving."
