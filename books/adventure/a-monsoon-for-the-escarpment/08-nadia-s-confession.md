## 8. Nadia's Confession

The rain found them before the patching did.

Nadia heard it first as a sound she couldn't name — a hiss, a pressure, like the river drawing breath through its teeth — and then Ulyana looked up from the bilge pump and said, "That's it. That's the front edge. We've got maybe two hours of dark before it means anything."

Two hours. Nadia had learned to measure time in survey increments, in the clean arithmetic of chainage and elevation, but on the Kestrel time came in different units: how long a seam would hold, how long a lie would hold, how long before the woman at the pump asked the question Nadia had been dreading since Kilometer Forty.

They beached in the lee of a clay bank where the current slackened into an eddy, and Ulyana killed the engine with a tenderness Nadia hadn't expected from her. The boat settled into the shallows with a sigh, listing to port, and the silence that followed was enormous — the silence of a machine stopping after days of not stopping.

"Canvas," Ulyana said, already moving. "Aft locker. And the lamp. If we're doing this, we're doing it by light, because I'm not putting my hands inside a hull I can't see."

Nadia fetched both. The lamp was brass, old, its glass chimney smoked amber, and when Ulyana lit it the whole aft cabin turned the color of whiskey. Rain began to tick against the deck above them, tentative, testing.

Ulyana went over the side into thigh-deep water with a mallet between her teeth and a handful of wooden plugs in her pocket, and Nadia held the lamp high and watched her work. It was strange, seeing her there — this woman who kept herself at arm's length from everything, standing hip-deep in a rising river with her sleeves shoved up, pressing oakum into a wound in her own hull. The boat was her armor. Nadia had understood that within an hour of coming aboard. And armor doesn't crack often, but when it does, the person inside is more exposed than anyone who never wore any at all.

"Hand me the caulking iron," Ulyana said, mouth full of mallet handle.

Nadia passed it down. Their fingers touched in the transfer, wet and cold, and neither of them mentioned it, which was becoming its own language between them.

* * *

Inside, while the seam set, they waited. Waiting was worse than working. The rain thickened from ticking to drumming, and the river rose around the hull in a way Nadia could feel through the soles of her feet — a slow, patient lift, like something being carried.

Ulyana spread charts on the table and weighted their corners with tin cups, pretending to study a channel she already knew by heart. Nadia sat across from her and watched the lamplight move on her face and understood that the moment had arrived, that it had been arriving for two days, and that if she let it pass now it would not come again cheaply.

"You're going to ask," Nadia said.

Ulyana didn't look up. "I'm going to ask."

"Then ask."

Ulyana set down her pencil. She folded her hands on the chart, and Nadia saw her choose her words the way she chose channels — deliberately, testing depth before committing weight.

"Corvo Halstead doesn't chase maps," she said. "Maps are paper. He chases leverage. He boarded my boat polite as a customs officer and offered me money he knew I'd refuse, because the offer wasn't the point. The point was to see what I'd do when refused." She tilted her head. "He wants you too. He said your name like a man saying a debt he'd already collected once. So. What does the syndicate have on Nadia Reyes?"

The rain filled the pause. Nadia looked at the lamp, at the amber glass, at the small flame standing very straight inside it, and thought: *you can tell her the version that keeps you safe, or you can tell her the one that's true, and only one of those is worth anything on this river.*

"Elias Thorn wasn't just my mentor," she said. "He made me."

"I know. You said—"

"No. You don't know." Nadia's voice came out flatter than she intended, stripped of the surveyor's precision she hid behind. "Eight years ago I was a field assistant with two years of community college and a talent for reading terrain. Talent doesn't get you licensed. Licensing boards want degrees, transcripts, supervised hours — years of them. I had none of it. I had Elias, and Elias had a friend with a printing press and no scruples, and between them they manufactured a Nadia Reyes who had graduated, who had logged her hours, who existed on paper in every way that mattered."

Ulyana said nothing. The rain drummed. Somewhere forward, the patched seam creaked as the water tested it.

"The syndicate found out three years ago," Nadia went on. "Not because I slipped. Because Elias got sloppy — he used the same forger for a land claim he was pushing, and when the syndicate audited him they found my name in his files like a loose thread. They pulled it. They've been holding it ever since." She laughed, and it came out ugly. "Do you know what it's like, carrying a document that can unmake you? Every job, every certification, every map I've signed — all of it stands on forged ground. One letter from their attorneys and I'm not a surveyor anymore. I'm a criminal who falsified professional credentials. I lose my license, my standing, every claim I've ever verified gets reopened and thrown out. They didn't need to threaten me. They just needed me to know they could."

"And that's why they're hunting you now." Ulyana's voice was quiet. "Because Thorn died, and the thread came loose again."

"Because Thorn died holding proof of something bigger than me." Nadia pressed her palms flat against the table. "My forgery is a pebble. Whatever he found about their permits — that's the landslide. They'll bury both of us under it if they can. Corvo doesn't want to buy the map, Ulyana. He wants to buy everyone who's seen it. And people who can't be bought get handled another way."

Ulyana leaned back. The lamp threw her shadow long against the cabin wall, and for a moment she was silent in a way that Nadia recognized as dangerous — the silence of a woman running numbers.

"So let me make sure I have this straight," Ulyana said finally. "Every survey you've done. Every boundary line, every elevation, every stamp you've put on paper that someone staked money or a home or a life on. All of it —"

"Stands on forged ground. Yes. I said that."

"No." Ulyana shook her head slowly. "You said it like it condemns you. I'm asking whether it does."

Nadia blinked. "What?"

"The work." Ulyana tapped the table, once, hard. "Did you do the work? When you walked a line, did you walk it? When you measured, did you measure true?"

"Yes." It came out of Nadia like something torn. "God, yes. I over-checked everything. Every reading twice, every closure tripled, because I knew — I always knew — that if anyone ever looked closely at Nadia Reyes, the only thing that could save her was being better than the real thing. I never fudged a number in my life. I couldn't afford to."

The lamplight steadied. Outside, the rain went on hammering the deck, and the river lifted the hull another inch, patient as tide.

Ulyana studied her for a long moment. Then she stood, crossed the cabin, and took down a battered tin from the shelf above the chart table — tea, Nadia guessed, or coffee gone stale, something hoarded. She spooned some into two cups without asking and poured water from the kettle she'd set on the spirit stove earlier, and handed one across.

"My father bought his permits," Ulyana said. "Legally. Every signature real, every fee paid — with syndicate money, for claims he knew were salted, on land he knew wasn't his to sell. Everything true on the surface and rotten underneath." She wrapped her hands around her cup. "You forged your way in and then spent eight years making yourself honest. He signed his way clean and never once told the truth. If I have to choose which of those to hold against a person, it isn't going to be yours."

Nadia stared at her cup. Steam rose off it and bent in the draft from the door. She found, to her horror, that her eyes were burning, and she looked away toward the porthole, where the rain ran down the glass in braided ropes.

"I keep waiting for the part," she said, "where you throw me off at the next landing."

"That was the plan, was it? Get confessed, get marooned."

"It's usually how it goes."

"Well." Ulyana sat back down across from her, close enough now that the table barely separated them. "Here's what I know. Two nights ago I lied to Corvo Halstead's face and it felt like flying, and I've been sick about it since, because lying well is the family trade I swore off. So I'm not in a position to grade anyone's sins. What I am in a position to say is this: the syndicate holds your forgery over you because it's the only dirty thing about you. They hold my father's money over me because it's the dirtiest thing about *him*. Between us, Nadia, we might manage one clean person."

Something broke open in Nadia's chest — not grief exactly, though grief was in it, but the specific relief of a weight set down after so long being carried that the body has forgotten its own shape without it. She laughed, wet-eyed, and Ulyana pretended not to notice, which was its own kindness.

"There's more," Nadia said. "If we're doing this."

"Of course there's more."

"The credentials aren't the only copy of the proof. Elias kept duplicates. He was paranoid that way — duplicates of everything, sealed, dated, hidden. If his originals are on that map roll, his duplicates are somewhere he trusted. And there's exactly one place Elias Thorn trusted besides the river." She met Ulyana's eyes. "Me. Which means somewhere in my old survey caches, on a route we are already sailing, there is a sealed packet that proves the syndicate's escarpment permit was fraudulent from day one. Not just competing. Criminal. The kind of paper that doesn't just win a claim — it ends Corvo Halstead's career and burns the syndicate's charter with the district board."

Ulyana was quiet for a moment. Then she drank her tea, set down the cup, and said, "Then we'd better make sure this hull holds, because we're not stopping to patch again."

* * *

They worked through the dark hours in shifts. Nadia held the lamp while Ulyana re-caulked the worst seam by feel and fury, shouting measurements over the rain, and when Ulyana's hands cramped Nadia took the iron herself and drove oakum into the gap while Ulyana braced her shoulders against the hull and told her, grudgingly, that her angle was acceptable. Twice the river surged and shoved the boat against the clay bank and they froze, listening, waiting for the scar to split again. It held. Toward the smallest hour the rain eased from assault to insistence, and Nadia climbed out onto the foredeck to check the lashings and stayed there, soaked through, watching lightning walk the horizon in slow silent columns — the monsoon lining itself up like artillery.

Ulyana came out and stood beside her. Neither of them spoke. The storm front glowed faintly blue-green where the lightning lived inside it, and beneath it the river ran black and swollen, carrying branches, carrying the smell of drowned earth, carrying everything downstream whether it consented or not.

"When I was nineteen," Ulyana said at last, "I left home on a boat smaller than this one, and I told myself I'd never stop moving long enough for anyone to hand me anything I'd have to carry. Ten years. Do you know what I figured out tonight, knee-deep in my own bilge?"

"What?"

"That a leak you patch together holds better than a hull nobody's ever touched." She said it flatly, like a channel report, but her shoulder was warm against Nadia's, and she didn't move it away.

Nadia looked at her. Lightning flashed, and for half a second the river was silver and Ulyana's face was bare — no armor, no channel-choosing caution, just a tired woman looking at a storm and deciding, visibly, to sail into it anyway.

"First cells hit before dawn," Nadia said. "After that, the gorge runs change hourly. If we're taking the high water, we take it early."

"Then we don't sleep."

"We don't sleep," Nadia agreed.

They went below to wait for the sky to open, and it was there, folding charts by lamplight, that Nadia remembered the rest of it — the detail she'd held back even in confession, the last thing Elias had said to her, the night before he vanished upriver:

*"If it goes wrong, Reyes, find the Marchetti girl. Not for the map. For the duplicate. Tell her the escarpment was never theirs — tell her it was never ours either. Tell her to look at who signed the original lease."*

She hadn't understood it then. She watched Ulyana's pencil move across the chart, steady and sure, and understood it now, and understood also that saying it aloud would change everything — that it would put a name, a family name, into the middle of this fight where it could cut everyone.

The first thunder rolled down the valley, long and low, like something enormous turning over.

Nadia opened her mouth to speak — and the radio behind them crackled to life, a voice tight with static: *"—all stations, all stations, district flood warning, gorge checkpoints closed effective immediately, repeat, closed—"*

Ulyana's head came up. "Closed," she repeated softly. "Which means Corvo can't use the official route either."

"Or," Nadia said, "it means whatever he knows about where we're going, he now knows we have to go through the gorge to get there. And he's already moving."

Above them, the rain came back all at once, like a door slammed open, and the Kestrel strained at her lines against the rising water as if she already knew.
