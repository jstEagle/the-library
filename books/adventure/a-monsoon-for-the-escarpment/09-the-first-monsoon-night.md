## 9. The First Monsoon Night

The sky broke open an hour after dark, and it did not break like rain. It broke like a verdict.

Ulyana had been through forty monsoons on this river and she had never heard anything like the first clap of thunder that rolled down the Verrane valley — not a crack but a long tearing sound, as if the night had been stitched shut for years and someone had finally pulled the thread. The first drops hit the Kestrel's deck like thrown gravel. Then the river simply stood up.

"Tell me you're not thinking what I think you're thinking." Nadia stood in the wheelhouse doorway, one hand braced against the frame, hair already plastered to her face. Behind her, the cabin lamp swung in slow, sick arcs.

"I'm thinking we have nine days," Ulyana said, "and every hour we hide from this is an hour Corvo spends dry."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have."

She brought the throttle up a notch and felt the old engine answer with a cough, then a growl. The Kestrel had been built in a yard that no longer existed, by men whose names were lost even to the registry, and she handled flood water the way certain horses handle fire — badly at first, then with a fury that made you believe she'd been born for it. Ulyana had learned the boat's moods the way she'd learned nothing else in her life: completely, without reservation, without ever once being betrayed.

The gorge was eleven kilometers ahead. In low water it was a scenic stretch of cliffs and lazy channels where tourists paid good money to photograph hornbills. Tonight it would be a throat of brown violence, the whole river funneled between walls close enough to spit across, running maybe eight meters above its banks, carrying trees, fences, the roofs of houses, and God knew what else downstream at a speed that turned everything into ammunition.

Every sane pilot would be tied off somewhere, doubled lines, waiting out the front.

Ulyana eased the wheel over and pointed the bow at the lightning.

* * *

The first kilometer of the gorge taught her what the storm intended.

The water wasn't flowing anymore; it was *arriving*, wave after wave stacked behind each other like freight cars, and the Kestrel climbed each one and dropped off the far side with a slam that rattled Ulyana's teeth in her skull. Rain came sideways, so dense it erased the cliff walls ten meters away. She flew blind except for the lightning, which strobed the world white every few seconds — and in those frozen instants she read the river the way other people read print.

There: a whirlpool the size of a courtyard, spinning counterclockwise, hugging the left wall. Give it room.

There: a raft of debris — bamboo, a dead cow bloated and turning, half a canoe — riding the seam between two currents. Stay below it or above it, never in it.

There: white water boiling over something shallow. A new bar, deposited since last season, or an old rock wearing a fresh crown. The channel ran left of it, but only just.

"You're steering by the flashes." Nadia had wedged herself into the corner beside the chart table, both hands locked on the rail. Her voice was steady, which Ulyana noted with a strange, detached pride, as if the steadiness reflected well on her personally.

"Lightning doesn't lie. It just stutters."

"And if it stops?"

"Then I steer by memory and prayer, in that order."

Another flash. Another frozen photograph of hell. Ulyana adjusted five degrees to starboard, felt the current grab the stern and try to swing them broadside — the death grip, the one that put you sideways in a gorge and rolled you inside thirty seconds — and countered with more rudder than the manual said the boat could take. The Kestrel groaned but held.

The bilge alarm went off.

A short, ugly electronic bleat that Nadia had installed two seasons ago over Ulyana's objections. It meant water past the second strake. Not catastrophic. Not yet. But the old collision scar — the one they'd patched on the sandbar at Kilometer Forty with epoxy, canvas, and a prayer neither of them had voiced — was working loose in water like this. Of course it was. Everything loose came loose tonight.

"Pump's holding?" Ulyana shouted.

"Barely!" Nadia had already dropped into the engine compartment. Ulyana heard her wrestling the backup pump into position, cursing with a fluency that would have impressed the dockworkers at Port Sable. "There's a seam weeping aft of the patch! Slow, not fast!"

"Nothing on this river tonight is slow!"

She took the next set of waves at twenty degrees off their face, the only angle that let the hull speak to the water instead of arguing with it, and for ninety seconds there was nothing in the world but the wheel in her hands and the river's enormous breathing underneath her.

This was the part nobody understood. This was the part she could never explain to Marco, to her father, to anyone who had ever asked why she couldn't just come home, just settle down, just take what the family offered. Because here — here, in the worst water of her life, with a leaking boat and a stranger pumping bilge behind her and a syndicate enforcer somewhere upstream — here she was more fully alive than she had ever been in a drawing room. Fear and joy were the same animal. She'd known it since she was nineteen years old and had taken the Kestrel out alone for the first time. You didn't tame the animal. You rode it.

* * *

Kilometer six. The narrows.

The gorge pinched here to barely sixty meters, and the river, denied anywhere else to go, went vertical. Standing waves three meters high marched down the channel in ranks, and the noise was beyond noise — a continuous detonation, the sound of a mountain being argued apart. Ulyana throttled back to headway speed, because speed was useless now, worse than useless; in standing waves you didn't fight your way through, you *surfed*, you matched the water's rhythm and let it carry you, and any correction you made had to be small, patient, almost humble.

Flash.

The channel curved hard right around a spur of black rock, and beyond the spur —

Tree.

A whole tree, root ball first, some giant ripped from a bank kilometers upstream, its trunk as thick as the Kestrel's mast, lying just awash across eighty percent of the channel. Only a strip of churning green water showed on the far side, between the tree and the right-hand cliff, a strip maybe four meters wide, and the current was dragging them straight toward the trunk at a speed no engine in the world could beat.

Time did something strange. It thickened. Ulyana saw all of it in one lightning flash and then lived inside that flash while the darkness lasted: the trunk sliding closer, the root ball like a fist, the gap narrowing, Nadia's face appearing in the companionway, mouth open, no sound coming out.

There was exactly one maneuver for this. Exactly one. She'd used it twice in her life, both times alone, both times with nothing to lose, and it had a name among the river families that she'd always pretended not to hear: *the widow's turn*. You killed your way — you dropped the throttle to idle, threw the wheel hard against the current, and let the river itself pivot the boat, using the pressure differential between bow and stern to spin you a hundred and eighty degrees in roughly one boat-length. It was insane. It asked the hull to take a load it was never designed for. It asked the rudder to act as a brake, a lever, and a wing all at once. Done wrong, it capsized you instantly. Done right—

Done right, it was the closest thing to flying a boat could do.

If she tried to power through the gap, they'd hit the trunk at speed and the collision scar would open like a letter. If she tried to turn normally, the current would complete the turn for her — into the tree. The widow's turn was the only door out of the room, and the door was on fire.

"Nadia!" she roared. "Hold onto something that loves you!"

She pulled the throttle to idle. Slammed the wheel hard to port, into the current. And stepped on the gunwale toggle that released the drag plate — a steel fin she'd welded under the stern herself, years ago, for reasons she'd never been able to articulate until this exact moment.

The river took the boat.

For one full second the Kestrel went weightless, lifted on the wave crest, and Ulyana felt the whole hull begin to rotate beneath her feet, the stern swinging wide, the bow swinging in, the drag plate biting water and levering them around like a door on a hinge. The wheel fought her with a force that wrenched her shoulder. Something cracked — she'd find out later it was the wheel's wooden rim splitting along a grain line, and she'd keep the broken wheel forever, hang it on the cabin wall like a saint's bone.

They came around. Bow downstream. The tree filled the entire windshield, a black wall of bark and thrashing branches, close enough that spray off the root ball hit the glass.

"Now!" she screamed at the engine, and shoved the throttle to full.

The Kestrel leapt. The current, furious at being cheated, picked the stern up and flung them forward, and Ulyana threaded the bow through the gap between trunk and cliff with perhaps a meter of clearance on either side — she heard bark shriek down the starboard rail, a long tearing kiss that would leave a scar to match her own — and then they were through, into open water, into the dark, alive.

Nadia made a sound. Not a scream. Something lower, rawer, dragged up from underneath language.

Ulyana realized she was laughing.

It poured out of her before she could decide whether to allow it — great whooping gulps of laughter, wild and unlovely, the laughter of a person who has looked into the machinery of her own death and watched it jam. Her hands shook on the broken wheel. Her shoulder throbbed where the rudder torque had nearly dislocated it. And she laughed, and laughed, and found that under the terror, where fear should have been sitting, there was instead this — this fierce, blazing, unreasonable joy, bright as the lightning itself.

"We're alive," Nadia said. She had come up beside Ulyana without Ulyana noticing, and her hand closed over Ulyana's on the wheel — not taking it, just holding it, anchoring it. "You absolute maniac. We're alive."

"The widow's turn," Ulyana managed. "Only she knows how to do it. Only her."

"Don't ever do that again."

"Probably will."

"Don't—" Nadia started, and then the lightning flashed again and whatever she saw in Ulyana's face stopped her, because Ulyana knew what it must look like: drenched, bleeding a little from where the wheel rim had split her palm, grinning like a woman possessed. Nadia's expression shifted through anger and relief and arrived somewhere else entirely, somewhere warmer and more dangerous than either. "You're going to get us killed someday."

"Not tonight." Ulyana turned her hand over and gripped Nadia's properly. "Tonight I'm too good."

* * *

They cleared the gorge mouth at 0300, if the clock in the wheelhouse could be believed, and the river opened out below the escarpment foothills into a drowned plain. The storm had spent its first fury and settled into a long, grinding downpour, the kind that could go for days. Ulyana found a flooded oxbow off the main channel — deep water, high cutbanks, sheltered from the worst wind — and nosed the Kestrel in against the bank, double-lining her to two ironwoods that groaned but held.

The bilge was manageable. The pump gained ground slowly, gallon by gallon, while the rain drummed the cabin roof like a thousand impatient fingers. Nadia brewed tea on the gimbaled stove, and they drank it in the wheelhouse wrapped in blankets, watching the lightning walk the ridgelines of the escarpment far to the east, silent now, the thunder arriving late and softened by distance.

Ulyana's palm had stopped bleeding. Nadia had bandaged it without asking, her fingers quick and impersonal and somehow not impersonal at all.

"The drag plate," Nadia said finally. "You welded that yourself?"

"Six years ago. Couldn't have told you why."

"You could now."

Ulyana looked at her. The lamp was low. The rain made a privacy of the whole world. "I could tell you a lot of things right now," she said, "that would be easier to say than what I'm actually thinking."

"So say the harder thing."

"The harder thing can wait until we're not leaking." She smiled, tired and true. "But it's waiting. I'll give it that."

Nadia held her gaze a moment longer, then looked away toward the black eastern horizon, and Ulyana saw her shoulders change — saw something settle in them, some decision arriving quietly, the way decisions did when they mattered most.

"There's a checkpoint at Sorrel Bend," Nadia said. "Twenty kilometers up. Or there was."

"Was?"

"In flood like this, the government pulls the crews out. Locks the gatehouse and leaves." She paused. "But the syndicate doesn't need a government crew to staff a checkpoint. They just need men with guns and a reason to stop boats."

Ulyana followed her eyes to the horizon, where the lightning was still stitching its bright, stuttering map of the country ahead. Somewhere up there, past Sorrel Bend, past the flooded flats, sat the district registry office and a clock ticking down from nine days. And somewhere between here and there, dry under a tarpaulin or warm in a seized guard post, Corvo Halstead was waiting.

"He knows the flood closes the checkpoints," Ulyana said slowly. "So he won't bother watching the river."

"No. He'll watch the road."

"Then we don't take the road." Ulyana drained her tea and stood, and the joy was still there under her ribs, banked now, burning steady. "Get some sleep. At first light we're going to find out what the Kestrel thinks of Sorrel Bend."

Outside, the rain kept falling, and the river kept rising, and the escarpment waited in the dark like a promise neither of them had dared read aloud yet.
