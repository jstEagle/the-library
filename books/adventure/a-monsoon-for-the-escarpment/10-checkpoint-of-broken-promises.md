## 10. Checkpoint of Broken Promises

The checkpoint announced itself as a smell before it became a sight: diesel, wet canvas, and the particular sourness of too many men living under tarps in the rain. Ulyana smelled it from two kilometers out, nose into the wind, and cut the Kestrel's throttle to a crawl.

"Checkpoint's flooded," she said. "Look."

Nadia came up beside her at the rail, hair plastered flat by the drizzle that had been falling since dawn. Ahead, where the river narrowed between the old customs pilings, the water had swallowed the landing entirely. The checkpoint buildings — a corrugated office, a fuel shed, a flagpole with nothing flying — stood in brown water to their eaves. A patrol skiff sat half-sunk against the shed roof, and a line of stranded travelers picked their way along the levee crown with their belongings on their heads like a procession of herons.

"Can we run it?" Nadia asked. "The channel looks open past the pilings."

"The channel's open. The paperwork isn't." Ulyana chewed the inside of her cheek. "Flooded checkpoints mean the registry relay is down. It also means when they reopen, there'll be a man with a ledger and a grudge checking every hull that passed while he was eating lunch. I don't run checkpoints blind. Not with what we're carrying."

"So we go ashore."

"So we go ashore." She swung the wheel over. "There's a town on the high bank, Serrado Landing. Half the river families moor there during flood season anyway. We'll tie up, dry out, and find out what the water's doing before we decide anything else."

She didn't say the other thing she was thinking: that she wanted eyes on the registry board at the landing. Every river town had one — a public notice board where claims, liens, and permit filings got posted for anyone with a reason to read them. Thirty days ago she'd have motored past without a glance. Now the map rode sewn into the lining of her jacket, and every posted notice felt like a hand reaching for her collar.

The Kestrel nosed up to the town dock just after noon, riding low on bilge water they hadn't fully beaten back since Kilometer Forty. The patch was holding. Barely. Ulyana made fast with hands that knew the work without her attention, which was good, because her attention was already climbing the muddy stair to the high street, scanning for the notice board outside the ferry agent's office.

It took her four minutes to find it. It took her another full minute to understand what she was looking at.

* * *

The board was a sheet of weathered plywood behind chicken wire, layered with years of postings gone gray and soft. Most of it was the usual sediment — lost dogs, fuel prices, a prayer request for old Marta's hip. But pinned dead center, fresh, the paper still white at its edges, was a Notice of Competing Claim.

Ulyana read it twice. Then a third time, because the words refused to arrange themselves into sense.

**NOTICE OF INTENTION TO FILE CONCESSION CLAIM No. 4471-C**
*Verrane Escarpment Plateau, Upper Concession District*
Filed on behalf of Meridian Consolidated Mining Syndicate, per original survey authorization dated 14 March, eleven years prior.
Surveyor of record: R. Calloway.
Attorney of record: **M. Marchetti.**

The name sat on the page like a hook through her palm.

Marco.

Her brother's signature was reproduced beneath the printed block — she'd have known it anywhere, that careful architectural hand he'd practiced since they were children, letters built like bridges, everything load-bearing. He used to sign her birthday cards with it. *To my sister, who will outrun the river someday.* She'd been nine. She'd kept the card until the ink faded and then kept it longer.

"He signed it," she said aloud, though Nadia had only just caught up, breathing hard from the stairs.

"What?" Nadia crowded in, rain dripping off her chin onto the plywood. Her eyes went down the notice, fast, professional, and stopped at the same place. "Marchetti. That's—"

"That's my family name. Yes." Ulyana's voice came out level, which surprised her. Underneath the levelness something was tearing loose, some old mooring line parting strand by strand. "My brother is the syndicate's attorney of record."

Nadia said nothing for a moment. Rain ticked on the tin awning overhead. Down on the dock someone shouted about a fouled line, and the sound seemed to come from very far away.

"Read the rest," Nadia said quietly. "The surveyor of record. R. Calloway."

"I see it."

"Do you know what that means? Calloway wasn't a surveyor. Calloway was Meridian's land man — I met him once, in Thorn's office, when I was still green. He couldn't read a contour map if you paid him. Which means the original survey authorization they're citing—" She tapped the paper. "—is forged. Or bought. Either way, Thorn was right. Their claim to the escarpment has been rotten from the start, and this notice is them trying to make it look legitimate before anyone checks."

"They're not trying," Ulyana said. "They've done it. This isn't an intention to file, whatever the heading says. This is a filing. It's been stamped." And it had — the district registrar's ink mark sat fat and blue near the bottom, dated six days ago. Six days. While she'd been patching seams at Kilometer Forty and running smuggler channels and learning to trust a woman with borrowed credentials, Marco had been feeding paper into the machine that would bury Elias Thorn's last wish under a mountain of legitimate-looking lies.

She thought about the night she'd left home. Nineteen years old, one bag, her father's voice following her down the drive: *You'll come back when you understand what family costs.* She'd understood, eventually. Family cost exactly what she'd been unwilling to pay — her name on documents she hadn't read, her silence about things she hadn't agreed to. So she'd traded Marchetti for Marchetti-less, taken her mother's maiden name onto the boat papers, and told herself the river was enough family for anyone.

Ten years. Ten years of never stopping moving, and the whole time her brother had been standing still, signing things.

"Hey." Nadia's hand landed on her shoulder, light, tentative. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing."

"You're doing the thing where your jaw goes rigid and you look like you're going to put your fist through the board."

"I'm considering it."

"Don't. The board didn't do anything wrong." Nadia stepped closer, lowering her voice. "Listen to me. This changes the math. If they've filed a competing claim with a real stamp on it, we're not racing Corvo anymore. We're racing the record itself. Whoever's paper reaches the district office first, cleanest, wins. Everything else is noise."

Ulyana stared at the signature. Bridges, load-bearing. She remembered being twelve and watching Marco study for his exams, how he'd explained consideration and contract to her using sweets from the kitchen jar — *you promise me the blue ones, I promise you the red ones, and if either of us breaks it, the whole jar goes to Father.* She'd broken it every time. He'd never broken it once. Even then, even at ten years old, Marco had understood that promises were instruments, and instruments were meant to be played by whoever held them.

And now he was holding hers.

* * *

They found rooms above the ferry agent's office because it was the only lodging with a roof that didn't sing in the wind, and because the agent, a wide woman named Odile who'd once hauled cargo for Ulyana's old employer, asked no questions beyond whether they wanted the fish stew or the fish stew. They ate the stew. Outside, the river climbed the dock pilings another handspan, and the clouds coming down the valley had organized themselves into something with intent.

Ulyana couldn't sit still. She paced the narrow room, jacket off, map transferred to the oilskin pouch she now wore across her body like a bandolier, and tried to think the problem instead of the man. The problem was tractable. The man was not.

"Say it," Nadia said from the cot, where she sat cross-legged mending a tear in her pack with dental floss and a bent needle. "Whatever's circling. Say it out loud. It helps."

"It doesn't help."

"It helped me, the night I told you about my credentials."

"That was different. What you confessed was yours. This—" Ulyana stopped at the window. Below, two boys were bailing out a dugout that had shipped water at its mooring, working in perfect unison without speaking, the way siblings did. She looked away. "This is mine. Being handed to me in installments."

"Then give me the installment."

She almost didn't. But the room was small and the rain was loud and Nadia had a way of waiting that made silence feel more expensive than speech.

"My father sold land rights to Meridian when I was seventeen," Ulyana said. "Our land. Well — land we lived on, land my grandfather cleared. Father owned the paper, so Father made the sale, and the sale gutted three families downstream who'd farmed that floodplain for sixty years on a handshake. When they came to our door, angry, desperate, my father hid behind lawyers. Behind Marco, eventually. Marco was in law school by then. Father was so proud. 'My son will protect us,' he said. What he meant was: my son will protect me from having to be decent."

"And you?"

"I told my father I'd testify about the handshake agreements if it ever went to court. He disowned me by telegram. Eleven words. Cheapest thing he ever bought." She turned from the window. "Marco wrote to me after. Once a year, sometimes twice. Letters I never answered. In the early ones he defended Father. In the later ones he just... reported. Marriages, deaths, the price of land. As if he were keeping a file open in case I ever wanted to resume the relationship. As if family were a claim you could keep alive by paying the annual fee."

"Did you keep the letters?"

The question landed harder than it should have. Ulyana looked at her, and Nadia had the grace to look slightly apologetic.

"A locked box on the Kestrel," Ulyana admitted. "Under the floor plates. With the spare impeller and my mother's ring."

Nadia nodded slowly, saying nothing, which was somehow worse than any observation she could have made.

"The point," Ulyana said, "is that I left all of it. The name, the money, the land, the man. I built a life where the only paper that mattered was my cargo manifest. And now my brother's signature is on the document trying to steal the one honest thing a dying man ever gave me, and I can't decide if I'm furious or—" She stopped. Started again. "Or heartbroken. Which is worse. Fury I know how to use."

Nadia set down the needle and floss. "You know it might not be what it looks like."

"It's exactly what it looks like. It's his signature."

"Signatures get used, Ulyana. You told me yourself your father hid behind lawyers. Maybe Marco's being used the same way. Maybe he signed a routine representation agreement years ago and Meridian is invoking it."

"Then he'll renounce it. Publicly. Today, if he has a spine."

"And if he doesn't?"

"Then he's Father." The words came out flat and final, a hatch closing. Ulyana heard herself and hated the sound. Ten years of running from that man and here she was, becoming him in miniature — drawing the border, cutting off the straggler, choosing certainty over the unbearable possibility that people were more than their worst signatures.

Nadia let the quiet sit for a moment. Then: "When we were patching the Kestrel, you gave me grace I hadn't earned. Forged credentials, syndicate leverage, all of it. You said loyalty and truth weren't the same thing, but they could be, if you chose both." She stood, crossed the small room, and took Ulyana's face in her hands — rain-cold fingers, steady eyes. "Choose both here. Hate what he signed. Find out why he signed it. In that order."

Ulyana closed her eyes. Beneath her sternum, the oldest wound — the one shaped like a dining room table with an empty chair — cracked open another centimeter, and to her surprise, what leaked out wasn't blood. It was something more like grief, and grief, unlike fury, could be carried.

"All right," she said. "Both."

Outside, the rain thickened. Somewhere downriver, thunder spoke in the register of heavy machinery.

* * *

The answer came at dusk, and it came the way answers always came on the river: carried by water, wearing a stranger's face.

A boy from Odile's kitchen knocked on their door and said there was a gentleman below asking after the captain of the Kestrel. Ulyana went down with her hand nowhere near a weapon, because weapons were for people who hadn't learned patience, and found a courier standing under the office awning, soaked, holding a sealed envelope out at arm's length like something dead.

"No reply required," the courier said. "Paid in full, both directions."

The envelope was heavy stock, syndicate watermark, addressed in type. Inside was a single sheet, and on the sheet, in that careful architectural hand she'd have known blindfolded, was a letter.

*Yuli.*

Nobody had called her that in ten years. She read standing in the lamplight while Nadia watched from the stairs.

*I won't insult you by pretending the signature on the escarpment filing is a surprise. I signed it. I want you to know what you'll conclude anyway: I signed it before I knew it was yours. Meridian retains me on a general basis; the escarpment matter came across my desk as a routine adverse-claim defense. By the time I understood what ground we were fighting over, withdrawal would have breached my retainer and exposed me to liability — and, frankly, exposed you, because the first thing a withdrawing attorney must do is identify the adverse party. My withdrawal would have handed them your name on a plate.*

*So I stayed on. Badly. Slowly. Every filing I've drafted has been late, incomplete, or technically defective, and every delay has been billed to them as diligence. I have bought you eleven days so far. I may be able to buy a few more. I may not. Corvo Halstead does not believe in my diligence, and he has begun checking my work himself.*

*I am not asking forgiveness. I am telling you the clock is shorter than you think, and that the permits lapse in nine days, and that as of yesterday Meridian knows your route. There is a man at the flooded checkpoint who takes Corvo's money and calls himself a ferry pilot. Trust the water, not the pilots.*

*Father is dead, Yuli. You know that. What you may not know is that at the end he asked for you, and I lied and told him you'd written. I would lie again. Some inheritances are debts. This one, I'm finally trying to pay.*

*M.*

Ulyana read it three times. The lamp hissed. The river climbed.

"Well?" Nadia said softly.

"He says he's been sabotaging them from inside." She folded the letter along its creases, precisely, the way Marco folded everything. "He says he's buying us time."

"Do you believe him?"

The honest answer was: she wanted to, which was the most dangerous condition a Marchetti could suffer. She thought of the notice board, the blue stamp, the bridge-built letters. She thought of her father asking for her at the end, and Marco lying to cover the absence she herself had carved. Somewhere in that tangle was a truth, and somewhere in it was a trap, and ten years on the river had taught her that the two usually ran together in the same channel, and that the pilot's job was not to choose but to read the water.

"Nine days," she said. "He says nine days, and Meridian knows our route, and there's a bought man at this checkpoint." She looked up at the stairs, at Nadia's face half in shadow. "Which means we cast off tonight, ahead of the storm, and we stop believing that any delay is safe. Whatever Marco is — saboteur or trap — the answer's the same. We move."

Nadia nodded once. "I'll wake Odile about provisions."

"Nadia." Ulyana held up the letter. "If this is true — if he's been bleeding for us in a room full of wolves — then I owe him something I don't know how to pay. And if it's false, then the man who taught me what a promise was has finally broken one, and the whole jar goes to Father." She pushed the letter deep into the oilskin pouch, against the map, where the two papers touched like opposite poles. "Either way, I'm done letting other people hold my family's pen."

Thunder rolled down the valley, closer now, and the first hard rain began to fall on the tin roofs of Serrado Landing like applause before a verdict.
