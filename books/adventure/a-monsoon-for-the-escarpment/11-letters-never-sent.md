## 11. Letters Never Sent

The rain came down like a verdict.

Ulyana felt it before she heard it — a change in the air pressure that made her fillings ache and the Kestrel's old hull tick in a language she'd learned to read the way other people read faces. By the time the first fat drops hit the wheelhouse glass, she'd already cut the engine and let the current carry them sideways into the lee of the Ponta Verde fuel dock, where the river widened and the town huddled under tin roofs like something trying not to be noticed.

"Storm cell's parked," Nadia said, coming up the ladder with the radio log in her hand. Her hair was plastered to her forehead and she hadn't bothered fixing it, which Ulyana was learning was a thing Nadia did — abandoned vanity the moment it became inefficient. "Station at Cachoeira says it's not moving for two days, maybe three. Flash flooding on all the feeder creeks. They've closed the upper channel."

"Closed it to idiots," Ulyana said. "We'd be fine."

"You'd be fine. I'd be a statistic." Nadia dropped onto the bench beside the helm and peeled off her wet jacket. "Even you can't run water you can't see."

"Watch me."

"I have been. For two weeks." Nadia said it lightly, but something under it wasn't light, and Ulyana looked away first, out at the rain hammering the dock, the fuel pumps lashed down under tarps, the town's single street dissolving into a brown ribbon. "Two days," Nadia said. "Three. We're stuck, Ulyana."

Stuck. The word landed in her chest like a fishhook. Ten years she'd built her whole life around never being stuck — never moored longer than a fuel stop, never committed to a berth, a lease, a face that would expect her back. Motion was the trick. Motion was how you outran a thing. She'd never let herself think too hard about what the thing was, because thinking was the first step toward stopping, and stopping was where the water got in.

"Then we're stuck," she said, and heard how flat her voice came out.

Nadia heard it too. Ulyana could tell by the way she went quiet, the way she did — not pushing, just present, a surveyor's patience with terrain that hadn't decided what it was yet.

They ate canned beans and fried plantain Nadia bought from a woman on the dock who charged triple in storm weather and was worth every cruzeiro. The Kestrel rocked at her lines. Rain drummed the wheelhouse roof like a thousand small insistent hands. Somewhere upriver, Corvo Halstead was presumably doing something patient and terrible, and Marco was presumably doing something Marco-shaped, and the permits were bleeding out days like a man bleeding out hours, and none of it could be touched from inside this weather.

Ulyana found herself, after the dishes, not going to her bunk.

She went to the chart table instead, where she kept a tin box she hadn't opened in — she did the math and didn't like the number. Nadia was in the forward cabin reading by lamplight, and the boat was small enough that not-going-to-your-bunk was a visible act, a choice with an audience.

"What's in the box?" Nadia asked, without looking up.

"Nothing."

"You've been looking at it for ten minutes. That's a long time to look at nothing."

Ulyana opened it.

Inside: a bundle of letters, tied with waxed twine, gone soft at the edges from river damp that had gotten into the tin despite her best efforts. Eleven of them. She knew the count without counting. She'd written them the first year, when the wound was fresh enough to talk to, and then she'd written none for nine years, and she had never mailed a single one, because mailing one would have been a door, and doors swing both ways.

"Letters," she said.

"I can see that." Nadia set down her book. "To who?"

Ulyana sat with it. The rain filled the silence, generous, giving her somewhere to hide if she wanted to. She found she didn't want to. That was the strange thing — two weeks of this woman reading currents and reading her, and the wanting-not-to-hide had crept up on her like high water, quiet and total.

"My father," she said. "The first year. After."

Nadia turned on the bench to face her fully, lamp between them, and waited. She was good at waiting. It should have been irritating. It was, instead, the only reason Ulyana kept talking.

* * *

"My family name is Marchetti," Ulyana said.

She watched it land. Nadia was too controlled to react much, but her eyes did a thing — a quick recalibration, like a compass needle finding a new north. Everyone on this river knew the Marchetti name. The concession lands, the trading houses, the old money that had gotten old by being ruthless when it counted.

"Marchetti," Nadia repeated. "As in the Marchetti concession. As in the family the syndicate's been trying to buy out for twenty years."

"As in my father sold them the river rights to our own land when I was nineteen, and called it stewardship." Ulyana turned one of the letters over in her hands without opening it. She knew them by heart anyway. "My mother was dead two years by then. It was me and my brother Marco and my father, and my father had this idea that the family survived by becoming useful to people stronger than us. That you survive the flood by becoming part of the water." She laughed, short and ugly. "I thought we were the ones who stood on the high ground."

"What did he do exactly?"

"Signed a servitude. Gave the syndicate riparian access across Marchetti land — our land, land my grandfather cleared — so their barges could run ore down to the processing works. And when I stood up at the table and said no, he had me removed from the room. Physically. By men I'd known since I was a child." The memory still had teeth. She let it bite. "And then he had the lawyers draw up papers disinheriting me. Not in anger. That's the part I've never been able to make anyone understand. Not in anger. In *procedure*. Like I was a clause that had to be struck for the document to be valid."

Nadia said nothing for a moment. The lamp flickered. Outside, the rain kept up its work on the roof, the river, the world.

"And Marco?" Nadia asked.

"Marco stayed. Marco always stays. He's the one who believes you fix a thing by standing inside it and absorbing the damage personally." She shook her head. "He sent me letters too, the first few years. I sent them back unopened. It seemed fair. He kept the name; I kept the silence."

"And the letters you wrote him—"

"To my father. I told you." She looked down at the bundle. "I never sent them. I wrote one every month for eleven months, and every month I wrote the truth into it, and every month I put it in this tin instead of the mail sack, because the truth was — the truth was that I missed him. That's all. Eleven letters and the whole content of them is: I miss you, and you were wrong, and I miss you, and you were wrong. Eleven rounds of the same fight, fought against a man who wasn't in the room."

"Is he—"

"Dead. Four years now." She said it the way she reported river depths: flat, factual, useful. "I found out from a fuel dock gossip in Verrane City. Four years, and I heard it from a man who was mostly telling me about his hemorrhoids." She laughed, and it came out wrong, and Nadia reached over and put her hand on Ulyana's forearm, and Ulyana let it stay there.

"I never went to the grave," she said. "I know where it is. Santo Amaro, the family plot, on the land that isn't mine anymore. I've run past that stretch of bank two hundred times. Two hundred times I've looked at that hill and kept the wheel steady and gone on by." Her voice did something she hated, cracked on *gone on by*, and she let it, because Nadia was watching and there was no point performing competence at this hour, in this rain, with this woman. "That's the trick, you understand. That's the whole trick. You keep the wheel steady and you go on by, and if you do it enough years, going on by is just what you are. A woman made of wake. Nothing behind you but disturbed water."

"Ulyana."

"I'm not finished."

"No," Nadia said. "I know. Go on."

* * *

"The thing I've never told anyone," Ulyana said, "is that he was right about half of it."

Nadia's hand was still on her arm. Ulyana looked at it rather than at her face.

"He was right that the syndicate would have taken the river rights one way or another. They'd have taken them through the courts, through the permits office, through some bought clerk — and if all that failed, they'd have taken them the way they're trying to take Thorn's claim now. Corvo Halstead is not a new method. He's an old one. My father saw the flood coming and he made the only deal that kept the family standing, and the cost was me, and he paid it." She swallowed. "He paid it *choosing it*. That's what I can't forgive. Not the deal. The choosing. He sat at that table and he looked at his daughter and he calculated, and I came out on the wrong side of the arithmetic, and he signed anyway, in his good pen, the one he used for church."

"And the other half?" Nadia asked quietly. "The half he was wrong about?"

Ulyana was silent a long time. The Kestrel creaked at her lines, that familiar old-bone sound, and the rain went on, and somewhere in the town a dog was losing an argument with the weather.

"The other half is that I thought walking away would cost me nothing," Ulyana said. "That's the half. I thought I was the kind of person who didn't need — a name, land, a family plot, a father. I built a whole life on not needing it. Ten years of never stopping, because as long as I was moving, I never had to find out whether the life I'd built had a floor under it." She finally looked up. "And then a dying man sewed a map into his coat and asked me for one promise, and I said yes, and I don't even know why. Except I think — I think I know why. I think some part of me has been running cargo up this river for a decade trying to deliver something, and it was never ore, and it was never fuel, and it was never any of it. It was me. Undelivered. Eleven letters in a tin."

The lamp hissed. Nadia didn't move.

"Thorn asked me to deliver his claim before the permits lapse," Ulyana said. "And I said yes to a stranger in thirty seconds what I couldn't do for my own father in ten years. Finish the thing. Stand still long enough to finish the thing." She exhaled. "So that's what the Marchetti name is. It's a thing I refused, that refused me, that I've been carrying anyway, sewn into my coat like a dead man's map. And now the syndicate is filing forged claims and my brother is signing their papers, and the name I threw away is being used as a weapon against the one promise I ever kept."

Nadia was quiet for a while. When she spoke, her voice was low and even, the voice she used for reading water.

"You know what I did when Thorn forged my credentials?" she said. "I told myself the same story you just told. I told myself I was the kind of person who didn't need to be legitimate. That the work was the thing, not the paper." She shook her head slowly. "It took me nine years to admit the paper was the thing. The paper is the thing that lets you stand on the ground and say *this is mine, and I am real, and you have to deal with me.*" She squeezed Ulyana's arm. "You've been real this whole time. You just never filed it."

Ulyana laughed — a real one this time, wet and surprised out of her.

"That's the worst survey metaphor I've ever heard."

"I'm a fraud, remember. My metaphors are all forged."

And Ulyana laughed again, harder, until it turned into something else, and Nadia didn't pretend not to notice, just moved closer on the bench and let Ulyana's forehead come down against her shoulder, and held her while the rain worked the roof and the river worked the hull and ten years of going on by finally, finally stopped.

* * *

Later — much later, the lamp burned low, the storm grumbling off toward Cachoeira — Ulyana untied the waxed twine and took out the eleventh letter, the last one, the one she'd never had the courage to reread. She read it aloud, because it seemed suddenly important that it exist outside the tin, outside her, in the air of the boat where another person could hear it.

*Papa. You were wrong. I want you to know I know you were wrong, and I want you to know that I also know you did what you thought was the only thing, and I hate that I know that. If you ever read this: I kept the river. Not the land, not the name — the river. Every day, the whole length of it. You sold the rights. I never did. Yours, Ulyana.*

Nadia listened all the way through without moving. Then she said, "You should mail it."

"He's dead."

"So file it," Nadia said. "At the grave. At Santo Amaro, when this is done. Read it to the hill. You've gone by two hundred times." Her eyes were steady in the lamplight. "Go *to*, once."

Ulyana looked at her — this woman who'd been aboard two weeks, who read water like scripture and lied about nothing except the one thing that had nearly drowned her — and felt the strange vertigo of a life changing course at speed.

"We're still stuck here two days," she said.

"Yes."

"And Corvo's still upriver."

"Almost certainly."

Ulyana reached for the chart. And that was when the knock came — three slow raps against the wheelhouse door, out of the rain, at an hour when no honest person was on the water.

Nadia went still. Ulyana's hand found the chart table's edge.

"Who delivers mail in a flood?" Nadia whispered.

"Nobody," Ulyana said, and moved toward the door. "That's what worries me."
