## 12. Marco at the Landing

Marco had rehearsed the speech for three days, and it died in his throat the moment he saw her boat coming downriver.

He stood on the landing at Porto Serrado with his hands in his coat pockets and rain starting to spit out of a sky the color of wet slate. The Kestrel came around the bend at half speed, riding low, her paint gone chalky from ten years of sun she'd never once been moored long enough to suffer properly. He knew that hull better than anyone alive except the woman driving it. He'd helped their father caulk those seams when Ulyana was still small enough to fit inside the bilge.

*She's thinner,* was his first thought, which was a coward's thought, so he buried it under the second one: *Corvo's people are two hours behind me, maybe less.*

The Kestrel swung toward the dock with the unhurried precision of a boat that had never needed to hurry anywhere but away. Ulyana stood at the wheel in an oilskin slicker, hair plastered dark against her cheek, and beside her — Marco's stomach tightened — stood a woman he didn't know, compact and watchful, with surveyor's hands and eyes that catalogued him before he'd finished raising one hand.

*So the rumors were true. Thorn's map, and someone who can read it.*

"Marco." Ulyana didn't kill the engine entirely; she let it idle, holding the boat a meter off the pilings, close enough to talk and far enough to leave. Of course. Even now. "You're a long way from your office."

"I'm a long way from a lot of things." He kept his voice level. Rain ticked against the tin roof of the fish shed behind him. "Can I come aboard?"

"No."

It wasn't cruel. That was the worst of it. It was simply the truth, delivered the way she delivered everything — without decoration, without the ten years of letters neither of them had sent stacked underneath it.

"Nadia," Ulyana said, and the other woman stepped forward to the rail. "This is my brother."

*Brother.* Nadia looked at him the way you look at a bridge you've been told is sound and don't quite believe. "You're the attorney who filed the competing claim."

Marco felt the words land like a hook set behind his ribs. So she knew. Which meant Ulyana knew, which meant the checkpoint clerk at Broken Promises had talked, or worse — Corvo had wanted them to know. "I filed what I was paid to file. I've come to tell you it doesn't matter anymore, because none of it will matter in nine days."

Ulyana's jaw tightened. "The permits lapse in thirty."

"They lapse in nine." He let that sit. "Thorn's filing window was backdated against the original concession grant. Corvo found the discrepancy last week. He's not chasing you anymore, Lena. He's *timing* you. He knows exactly when the clock runs out, and he knows every checkpoint between here and the district office, because I drew the route for him."

The silence that followed had weight. Rain thickened against the river, dimpling the brown water into static.

"You drew it," Ulyana said quietly.

"I did my job."

"Your job." She almost laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "Papà used to say that too. Right up until the day he signed our name over to men like Halstead."

"That's not why I'm here."

"Then say why you're here, Marco, because I have a boat leaking at kilometer forty and a storm coming and no time for family."

He made himself hold her gaze. This was the part he'd rehearsed, and it came out anyway sounding like a stranger: "Give me the map. File nothing. Walk away from the claim, and I'll get Corvo to drop the warrant on your passenger. Her credentials are forged — you know that by now, don't you? He owns her. One word from the syndicate and she loses her license, her standing, everything. You can save her. All you have to do is give up a piece of paper for a dead man's dream."

Nadia went very still at the rail. Ulyana didn't look at her, but Marco saw her hand find the other woman's arm — not restraining, anchoring.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then in nine days the permits expire, the syndicate files its own survey — mine, this time, real coordinates, real markers — and the escarpment belongs to Verrane Consolidated forever. And Corvo stops being polite." He paused. "You've seen him be polite. You know what comes after."

Thunder muttered somewhere upriver, under the horizon, patient as bookkeeping.

"I came to warn you," Marco said. "That's the truth. And I came to take the map. That's also the truth. I stopped being able to separate the two a long time ago, and I'm sorry, Lena. I'm sorry in the way that doesn't help anybody."

Ulyana studied him for a long moment, and Marco understood, suddenly and completely, that she was saying goodbye to something — some last version of him she'd kept folded away through ten years of river, the brother who'd taught her to read current marks, who'd cried at their mother's funeral and then taken the syndicate's retainer six months later. She was looking at him the way you look at a sandbar that has shifted since the last chart.

"The answer's no," she said.

"Lena—"

"It was always going to be no. You knew that when you got on the train. You just needed to be able to tell Corvo you tried." She eased the throttle forward a fraction, the Kestrel breathing against the current. "Go home, Marco. Whatever's left of it."

"There won't be a home," he said. "Not for either of us, after this."

"Then we'll finally have something in common."

She turned the wheel. The Kestrel slid away from the landing, stern swinging, and Nadia Reyes watched him over her shoulder until the rain took them both.

Marco stood on the dock a long time after the engine note faded, water running off his chin that might have been rain. Then he took out his radio, and did not call Corvo, and hated himself for the delay, and called him anyway.

* * *

Nine days.

Ulyana ran the number the way she ran rapids — over and over, looking for the line through it.

Nine days to cover four hundred kilometers of rising river, past checkpoints Corvo owned, with a leaking hull and a monsoon arriving early and a brother who'd just shown her exactly how much of him was left worth grieving. She'd expected the ultimatum. What she hadn't expected was how much it would hurt to be right.

"He hesitated," Nadia said. She was at the chart table, drying the day's fixings, not looking up. "Before he radioed. I saw him standing there. Ten seconds, maybe more."

"Ten seconds isn't enough to build a man out of."

"It's a start."

Ulyana said nothing. Ahead, the river bent north into country she'd only run in dry season, and the clouds stacking on the horizon had stopped pretending to be anything but what they were. She checked the bilge — two inches, steady, the patch holding — and put the Kestrel's nose into the darkening water.

Behind them, far back, a launch light blinked once through the rain. Watching. Following. Not closing yet.

Corvo wasn't chasing anymore. He was herding.

And somewhere ahead, in nine days or less, there was a registry office and a stamp and a dead man's promise — and only one channel left that could beat the clock.
