## 13. Running the Flooded Narrows

Nadia had charted rivers on three continents, and she had never once drawn a line like the one Ulyana was about to steer.

The Throat didn't appear on any official survey. It appeared in the margins of old prospectors' maps as a warning — *avoid at high water* — and in the memory of river families as a rumor of a shortcut that ate boats whole. On paper it was a two-kilometer channel where the escarpment's runoff funneled through a gap barely wider than the Kestrel herself, dropping nine meters over its length, which in flood-season numbers meant water moving faster than any engine could fight.

On Nadia's instruments, it was a corridor of chaos: standing waves, reversal currents, and one submerged rock shelf that the locals called the Grinder because of what it did to hulls.

"You're sure about this," she said, not for the first time.

Ulyana stood at the wheel with her hands loose and easy, which was how Nadia knew she was terrified. "Corvo's launch is forty minutes behind us if he took the main channel when I think he did. The registry office closes at six. The Throat saves us three hours." She glanced over. "You want to do the math again?"

"I've done the math. The math says we die."

"The math doesn't know this river."

Nadia looked out through the rain-lashed windshield. Ahead, the channel narrowed between walls of black basalt slick with spray, and the sound coming up from it was not the sound of water. It was the sound of something enormous breathing.

"Okay," she said, and heard her own voice go flat and professional, the voice she used when the ground was giving way under a survey team. "Then let me do my job."

She spread the charts she'd redrawn by hand over the chart table, weighted with coffee mugs, and called out readings while Ulyana worked the wheel. Depth sounder. Current speed off the GPS drift. The angle of the foam lines visible through the rain, which told you more than any instrument about where the river actually wanted to take you.

"Entry current's running eleven knots," Nadia said. "Kestrel makes eight, maybe nine with the flood assist. You can't power against it. You have to *angle*."

"I know how to read a rapid, Reyes."

"Then you know the exit is the problem. The channel dog-legs left at the bottom, and everything the river throws down gets slammed into the right wall first. That's where the Grinder is. We come out of the drop too fast, we can't make the turn, and we hit stone going twenty knots."

Ulyana was quiet for a moment. Rain drummed the cabin roof like applause from an unfriendly crowd.

"So we don't come out of the drop too fast," she said. "We come out slow."

"With no engine? How?"

Ulyana turned and looked at her, and there was something in her face Nadia hadn't seen before — not the restless competence, not the armored irony. Something rawer. She was asking permission without asking.

"There's a trick," Ulyana said. "Old smugglers' move. You kill the engine before the drop and drift the worst of it dead-stick, stern-first, using the rudder to steer. Boat rides the water instead of fighting it. Slower entry, slower exit, enough control to make the turn." She paused. "I've done it twice. Both times on purpose. Once it worked."

"And the other time?"

"I'm still here."

Nadia stared at her. Then at the black gap in the rock ahead, where the Verrane poured itself through the escarpment like water through a funnel. Then back at Ulyana.

"You're asking me to trust you with my life on a maneuver you've successfully completed once."

"I'm telling you what I'm going to do. Trust is your department." But her hands had tightened on the wheel, just slightly, and Nadia understood that this was the real question underneath all of it — the one Ulyana Marchetti had been avoiding since a dying man sewed a map into his coat. Whether anyone could be trusted with her. Whether she could stand being trusted.

Nadia came forward and put her hand over Ulyana's on the wheel.

"Kill the engine at the white cross-marker," she said. "I'll call the drift. You steer. And Ulyana—" she waited until those dark eyes met hers, "—once it works, it works every time. That's how statistics work when you survive them."

Something cracked at the corner of Ulyana's mouth. Not quite a smile. Close enough.

"Get your life jacket on, surveyor."

* * *

The white cross-marker was a strip of faded paint on the left-hand rock face, put there by someone decades ago who had also meant to run the Throat, and Nadia tried not to think about whether they'd survived. The roar filled the boat now, vibrating up through the deck plates into her teeth. Spray came over the bow in sheets. The Kestrel shuddered as the current got its teeth into the hull and began pulling them toward the gap.

"Marker," Nadia said.

Ulyana killed the engine.

The silence lasted half a second — the strange, ringing quiet of a boat suddenly powerless — and then the river took them.

The first drop hit like a hand slamming them down. Nadia grabbed the chart table as the stern lifted and the world tilted thirty degrees, water climbing the windows, and then they were *in* it, drifting stern-first down a chute of brown violence with nothing between them and the rocks but a rudder and ten years of muscle memory.

"Stern's swinging right!" Nadia shouted over the roar. "Current's pushing us toward—"

"I see it!" Ulyana had the wheel hard over, boots braced wide, whole body leaning into the turn like she could push the boat with her spine. The Kestrel yawed, straightened, yawed again. Through the aft window Nadia watched the canyon walls scroll past close enough to touch, wet stone streaked with foam, and the standing waves behind them marching down the throat like a collapsing staircase.

"Second drop in fifty meters!" she called, reading the water ahead — reading it the way Thorn had taught her, the way no satellite survey ever could. "It kicks left! Whatever you do, don't let her broach!"

"Don't let her *what*—"

The second drop took them.

This one was worse. The boat went weightless for a full second — Nadia's stomach floated up into her ribs — and then slammed down into the trough so hard the bilge alarm shrieked and something below decks crashed loose. Water buried the windows entirely. For one endless moment they were inside the river, blind, enclosed in churning green-brown light, and Nadia thought with perfect clarity: *this is how it ends, drowned in a smuggler's channel chasing a piece of paper.*

Then the Kestrel surfaced, wallowing, and Ulyana was already hauling the wheel around, screaming wordless instructions at the boat itself, and Nadia scrambled to the aft window.

The Grinder was ahead. The rock shelf broke the surface in one place — a black fang streaming white — but its true mass ran underwater across the entire right side of the channel, and the exit turn was *now*, immediately, or the current would pin them against it and fold the hull like paper.

"Hard left! Left, left, LEFT—"

"I've got it!"

Ulyana threw her whole weight onto the wheel. The rudder bit. The Kestrel, drifting helpless at fourteen knots, began — slowly, agonizingly, a degree at a time — to swing. The black fang slid closer. Closer. Nadia could see the individual bubbles in the foam peeling off it, could see the dark seam of the underwater shelf extending toward them like a reef of teeth.

Ten meters. Five.

The stern cleared the fang by less than the length of an oar. The keel kissed the shelf — Nadia felt it through the deck, a long grinding shudder, the sound of fiberglass arguing with granite — and then the current released them sideways into the lower channel and the Kestrel swung her nose around and shot out of the Throat into the relative calm of the flooded main stem beyond.

They drifted. The rain fell. Somewhere behind them the roar of the narrows continued, indifferent.

Ulyana slumped over the wheel, chest heaving. Nadia realized she was gripping the chart table hard enough to leave marks in the wood.

"Bilge pump," Ulyana gasped. "Check the—"

"On it." Nadia clattered below, found the pump running, found fifteen centimeters of water sloshing in the bilge and falling — falling, thank God — and something had come loose in the galley that turned out to be the entire contents of a storage locker. She secured it, checked the patch they'd laid on the Kilometer Forty scar, found it holding, and climbed back into the cockpit soaked to the skin and shaking.

Ulyana hadn't moved. She was staring at the wheel with an expression Nadia couldn't read.

"Nadia." Her voice was rough. "Restart the engine."

Nadia found the starter, pumped the fuel, turned the key. The old diesel coughed, caught, coughed again, and settled into its familiar ragged idle. The sound of it nearly made her laugh.

"It held," she said. "Through all that. It held."

Ulyana nodded slowly. Then she reached out and took Nadia's wrist — not pulling, just holding, her thumb against the pulse point, as if checking that Nadia was real.

"You called the drops," she said. "Dead accurate. Both of them."

"Somebody taught me well." She hadn't meant to say it. Thorn's name hung unspoken between them, and Ulyana's grip tightened fractionally.

"He'd have been proud," Ulyana said quietly. "Of both of us, probably. He had terrible judgment about people." A pause. "Lucky for us."

* * *

They made the bend above Serrano Landing by four o'clock, running with the flood now, the whole Verrane become a brown conveyor belt carrying them toward the district office at a speed the Kestrel had never managed under her own power. Nadia took the watch while Ulyana slept for ninety minutes in the pilot chair, truly asleep for the first time since Marco's ultimatum, her face slack and younger.

Nadia watched the river and did the arithmetic she couldn't stop doing. Registry office closed at six. Corvo somewhere behind them — unless he wasn't. Unless he'd guessed the Throat, or taken the high road to the office itself, or simply decided that burning the Kestrel at anchor required less effort than catching her. Nine days on the permits when Marco delivered his ultimatum; seven now. Hours, really, once you counted filing queues and clerks who took lunch breaks during monsoons.

She thought about what Ulyana had done in the Throat. Not the maneuver — though God, the maneuver — but the moment before it, when she'd said *trust is your department* and waited, visibly, for the answer. Ten years of never stopping moving, and she'd handed a stranger the wheel of her survival.

Not a stranger anymore, Nadia thought. Not for a long time now.

Ahead, the river widened toward the last reach before the district town, and Nadia raised the binoculars out of habit and swept the far bank.

She stopped sweeping.

A launch sat at the government dock upstream of the registry office. White hull, twin outriggers, a radio mast with a whip antenna bent from hard use. She knew that boat. She'd seen it tied up at three trading posts, always polite, always waiting.

"Ulyana." Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Wake up. You need to see this."

Ulyana surfaced instantly, no grogginess, eyes sharp. Nadia handed her the binoculars and watched her find the launch, watched her shoulders set.

"That's Corvo's tender," Ulyana said. "He beat us here." She lowered the glasses. "But look at the dock. Look at what *isn't* there."

Nadia looked. No Corvo on the dock. No men at the registry office door. Just the empty rain-slicked boards and the clerk's window glowing yellow against the grey afternoon.

"He's not blocking the door," Nadia said slowly. "He's waiting for us to come to him. Or—" she stopped, the cold arriving all at once, "—or that's the tender, and Corvo himself is already inside the office, talking to the clerk, filing something of his own."

Ulyana was already pushing the throttle forward, the Kestrel surging against the flood.

"Then we'd better hope," she said, "that whatever he's filing takes longer than the last two kilometers of this river."

The rain thickened. The district town's lights swam up out of the murk, and somewhere among them, a clock neither of them could see was running down.
