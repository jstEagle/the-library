## 14. Strangers Who Become Crew

The Kestrel came around the last bend of the Throat's outflow at dusk, listing two degrees to port and sounding like a kettle left too long on the fire — a rattle in the engine mounts that Ulyana had been listening to for six hours the way you listen to a cough in a child's chest, waiting to know whether it would pass or become something worse.

It was not going to pass.

"Smoke," Nadia said from the bow, one hand on the rail, the other shading her eyes against the low gold light. "Left bank. Two chimneys."

Ulyana looked. The settlement sat where it always sat, wedged between the river and the first rise of the escarpment foothills: a dozen houses on stilts, drying racks hung with fish, nets mended on porches by women who watched the water the way other people watched television. Sarno's Landing. Population, depending on the season and the census-taker's patience, somewhere between forty and nobody.

She hadn't planned to stop here. Stopping was against her religion. But the engine rattle said otherwise, and so did the bilge pump, which had been cycling every four minutes instead of every ten since they'd cleared the Narrows, and so did the simple arithmetic that a dead engine in monsoon flood meant a dead boat and possibly dead crew.

"You know these people," Nadia said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I owe them," Ulyana said. "Or they owe me. After ten years on this river the ledger gets complicated."

* * *

The woman who met them at the landing was small, gray-haired, and built like something the river had tried to drown several times and failed. Amara Sarno. She took one look at the Kestrel's list, one look at Ulyana's face, and started giving orders before Ulyana's boots touched the dock.

"Tomas! Get the blocks under her hull before she sits wrong. Riva — boil water. You." This to Ulyana, with the flat authority of a woman who had pulled three children and one husband out of floodwater over the years. "When did you last sleep?"

"I don't—"

"That's what I thought. There's rice on. Eat first, argue after."

Ulyana opened her mouth to explain that she didn't need feeding, that she needed a machine shop and twelve hours and maybe a miracle, and found that Amara had already turned away to shout something about a gasket kit, and that arguing with her would be like arguing with the current.

So she ate.

That was the thing about Sarno's Landing that always undid her a little. Nobody asked questions first. The questions came later, if at all, and meanwhile there was rice with dried fish and chili oil, and someone's grandmother pressed a cup of tea into Nadia's hands and studied her face with frank, unembarrassed curiosity until Nadia laughed, and then the grandmother laughed, and that was apparently the whole negotiation.

"She fits right in," Amara observed, sitting down across from Ulyana with her own bowl. Her eyes went to the window, where Nadia had drifted over to help Riva's daughter untangle a net, hands moving with the quick competence of someone who'd grown up around boats even if she claimed deserts and survey grids. "New crew?"

"Passenger."

"Mm." Amara ate a mouthful of rice. "Your hull's scarred along the port strake. Old damage, reopened. You ran the Throat."

"The river ran us. We mostly survived it."

"And the men asking after you at Verrane Junction — they'll come here next?"

Ulyana set down her spoon. "Who told you about men at the Junction?"

"A fuel barge came through at noon. Its captain drinks my husband's rum and tells him everything. A launch flying syndicate colors, asking river families about a boat named Kestrel, a woman named Marchetti." Amara said the name without ceremony, without weight, the way you'd say any name, and Ulyana felt the old flinch anyway — the reflex of a decade of keeping that word locked in a box at the bottom of herself. "They offered money. My husband told them we hadn't seen you since the dry season. Which was true when he said it."

"And now?"

"Now you're not here. You're a rumor going the other way." Amara refilled her bowl without asking. "Tomas can quiet your engine rattle. He rebuilt the Kestrel's injector pump three years ago; he knows her temper. But she needs the night, and you need sleep, and in the morning—" she shrugged, a gesture that contained the entire philosophy of the river, "—the water will tell you what it wants."

* * *

They worked through the evening by lamplight and generator hum.

Ulyana helped Tomas pull the inspection plates while Nadia held lights and handed tools, and the three of them fell into a rhythm that felt older than any of them — the wordless economy of people fixing a boat, where a grunt means *tighter* and a tap on the housing means *there*. The rattle turned out to be a sheared mount bolt and a cracked bracket, the kind of failure that would have left them drifting engineless somewhere above the checkpoint, and when Tomas held up the broken bolt and raised his eyebrows, Ulyana felt the full delayed horror of how close it had been.

"Floodwater debris," Tomas said. "You hit something in the Narrows."

"A tree. Submerged. We clipped it in the dark."

"In the dark." He shook his head with something that wasn't quite disapproval and wasn't quite admiration. "Your mother ran boats like that. All nerve, no margin."

"My mother never ran anything but a kitchen," Ulyana said, before she could stop herself.

Tomas glanced up, caught something in her face, and let it go. That was another thing about these people. They knew when a door was closed, and they didn't knock. But as he turned back to the engine he said, quietly, "Amara still keeps the photograph. From the flood year. You, age nineteen, hauling her grandkids off the roof of the church."

"I remember the roof."

"We remember you." He tightened the new bolt with a final quarter-turn. "River doesn't forget who showed up. That's all I'm saying."

* * *

Past midnight, the work done and the generator shut down, Ulyana couldn't sleep.

She climbed to the Kestrel's wheelhouse with a blanket and sat in the pilot's chair, watching the settlement bank itself against the dark. Lamps going out one by one. A dog making its rounds. The river running high and heavy past the pilings, carrying the monsoon's first real load of silt, smelling of wet earth and far-off mountains.

Nadia found her an hour later, or maybe just came looking because her own bunk felt too empty. She climbed in without asking, folded herself onto the bench seat across the wheelhouse, and for a while neither of them spoke. It was the kind of silence Ulyana usually fled from. Tonight she let it sit.

"Amara's granddaughter asked me today if I was staying," Nadia said finally.

"What did you tell her?"

"I said we had nine days and a registry office." Nadia's voice was dry, but something underneath it wasn't. "She looked at me like I'd answered a different question. Maybe I had."

Ulyana turned that over. Outside, a fish jumped — the big splash-and-slap of a catfish hunting baitfish in the flooded shallows.

"When I was nineteen," she heard herself say, "I pulled Amara's grandchildren off a church roof during the flood. Four kids and an old man. I didn't think about it. The water was rising and their parents were downstream and somebody had to climb." She wrapped the blanket tighter. "I didn't do it to be owed. But they've owed me fifteen years, and I've spent fifteen years pretending that doesn't mean anything, because meaning things is how you get tied down."

"And tonight?"

"Tonight I'm eating their rice and sleeping under their lamps and tomorrow their men will lie to armed strangers for me, and none of them will ever ask me for anything except that I keep showing up." She stared out at the black water. "I built a whole life on the idea that moving was safety. That if I never stopped anywhere long enough, nothing could take root and nothing could be taken. And it turns out I stopped taking root everywhere I went whether I meant to or not. Every sandbar, every landing, every kid I hauled off a roof. The roots grew anyway. I just refused to look down."

Nadia was quiet for a moment. Then: "That's the most words you've said about yourself since I met you."

"Don't get used to it."

"Too late." But she said it softly, and then she crossed the wheelhouse and settled into the pilot's chair beside Ulyana's, close enough that their shoulders touched, and neither of them moved away. "For what it's worth — the granddaughter asked if I was staying. I didn't correct her grammar."

Ulyana huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Nine days," she said.

"Nine days," Nadia agreed. "But after."

*After.* Ulyana let the word sit there, dangerous and warm, and didn't throw it overboard.

* * *

Dawn brought trouble, because dawn usually did.

Ulyana was awake before the light, checking the bilge — dry, gloriously dry, Tomas's patch holding — when Riva's boy came pelting down the dock path, barefoot, breathless.

"Boat on the water," he gasped. "Gray launch. Syndicate colors. Asking at the fish racks — they're coming here."

Ulyana was already moving. "How many?"

"Three men. One talks fancy. The others carry like soldiers."

Corvo's scouts. Of course. The Throat was illegal but not invisible, and a gray launch burning fuel upriver would have been logged at two checkpoints. She woke Nadia with a hand on her shoulder and found Amara already standing in her doorway, dressed, unhurried, as if she'd been expecting the morning for years.

"How long do I have?" Ulyana asked.

"Long enough. Go stand behind my smokehouse and be a stranger." Amara's eyes were steady. "You've never been here. We've never seen you. My husband will handle the talking."

"Amara—"

"He owes you four grandchildren alive. Let him pay some interest." She was already walking toward the dock, tying her apron, becoming an old woman with fish to dry and nothing else in the world on her mind. It was, Ulyana thought, the finest piece of acting she'd ever witnessed, and she'd once watched Corvo Halstead lie to a room full of federal inspectors.

From the shadow of the smokehouse, between hanging strips of catfish, Ulyana watched the gray launch tie up. Three men stepped ashore. The talker was soft-bodied and precise, with the pleasant emptiness of a man hired for his manners. He showed papers to Amara's husband. He smiled. He described a boat, a woman, a map. He mentioned money, then consequences, in that order, which told Ulyana everything about his employer's priorities.

Old Sarno scratched his belly. Spat thoughtfully into the water. Shrugged with the magnificent vagueness of a man whose memory had been eroded by decades of rum and sun.

"Had a Kestrel through, maybe? Dry season? Boats come, boats go. You want fish? Good fish today."

The talker persisted. Sarno persisted harder. The soldiers looked bored. And twenty minutes later — twenty minutes in which Ulyana stood among the smoking fish with her heart going like the Kestrel's repaired engine and Nadia's hand finding hers in the dark and gripping hard — the launch cast off and headed upriver, toward the checkpoint, toward the wrong end of the river entirely.

Sarno had sent them north. Told them, with a straight face and a drunkard's ramble, that he'd seen the Kestrel running for the upper junction two days back. It would cost Corvo half a day to discover the lie, and by then—

By then the tide of the whole affair would have turned, one way or the other.

* * *

They cast off mid-morning, the Kestrel riding level and quiet for the first time in days, the engine purring instead of rattling. Amara stood on the dock with a parcel of food wrapped in banana leaf and an expression that permitted no refusal.

"You'll take the eastern channel past Kilometer Sixty," she said. "The flood's opened it early this year. It saves you six hours and the checkpoint at the bend."

"That channel hasn't been surveyed since the last flood," Nadia protested. "There could be—"

"There could be anything. There always could." Amara pushed the parcel into Nadia's arms. "That's why you take a Sarno at the tiller for the first stretch. Riva's boy knows the marks. He'll put you off at the split."

Ulyana looked at the old woman for a long moment. Fifteen years of coming and going, of taking favors and repaying them in kind, of keeping every transaction clean and deniable and safe — and it had never once been safe, and it had never once been only a transaction, and she had known that, somewhere below the waterline, the way you know a hull has taken water.

"Thank you," she said. It was inadequate. It was all there was.

Amara waved it off. Then, quieter, gripping Ulyana's wrist with a strength that belied her size: "Whatever's chasing you — finish it. Come back a free woman. Or don't come back at all, but go down fighting, because the river remembers who showed up, and so do I."

The Kestrel pulled away from the dock with Riva's boy at the tiller calling depth-marks in the local dialect, Nadia beside him with the chart open, Ulyana at the wheel with the morning sun on her face and the escarpment rising ahead of them, blue and enormous, closer than it had ever been.

Behind her lay a landing full of people who had lied for her without being asked. Ahead lay nine days, a registry office, a brother, and a man named Corvo who now knew he'd been pointed the wrong way.

Ulyana realized she was smiling. It felt strange on her face, like a rope knot tied in a material that didn't hold knots.

"Nadia," she called forward. "When this is done — when the claim's filed and Corvo's finished — how do you feel about learning a river?"

Nadia turned, hair whipping in the wind, and grinned. "Depends. Does the job come with a boat?"

"It comes with a leaky old Kestrel, a family of liars on a mudbank, and more debts than either of us can count."

"Sounds terrible," Nadia said. "I'm in."

Ahead, the eastern channel opened between the reeds — narrow, unmarked, and running fast with the flood — and Riva's boy shouted the first mark, and Ulyana swung the wheel and drove the Kestrel into the unknown water at full throttle.
