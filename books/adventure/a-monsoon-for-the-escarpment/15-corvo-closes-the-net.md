## 15. Corvo Closes the Net

Nadia heard the outboard before she saw the boat — a long, flat drone upstream of the mooring, the kind of engine note that didn't belong to anyone who lived on this stretch of river. She was on the Kestrel's aft deck with a mug of tea going cold in her hands, watching mist burn off the water, and by the time the sound resolved into a gray-hulled patrol launch running registry colors it had already slowed to a crawl.

Two men aboard. Neither was Corvo Halstead. That was somehow worse. Corvo came himself when he wanted something; he sent other people when he wanted you to know he'd stopped asking.

The launch didn't stop. It ran past at that insolent half-throttle, close enough that its wake rocked the Kestrel against her lines, and one of the men lifted an arm — not a wave, exactly. A gesture toward the shore road, where it climbed away from the landing toward the district seat and the registry office nine kilometers inland.

Then they were gone around the bend, and the river went back to pretending nothing had happened.

Ulyana came up from the engine compartment wiping grease on a rag, and Nadia watched her read the whole scene from the wake still slapping the hull. Ten days on the river together and Nadia could do that now — read Ulyana Marchetti the way she read contour lines, small displacements meaning large things underneath.

"Registry colors," Nadia said.

"I saw." Ulyana looked up the bank, where the road vanished into wet green. "Road's the only way to file in person. Nine klicks, one road, no cover once you're past the tree line."

"And now there's a launch telling us they know that too."

Ulyana's jaw did the thing it did when she was refusing to say something obvious. "We knew this was coming. We beat him through the Throat. We're ahead."

"We're ahead," Nadia agreed. "He's ahead of *ahead*."

* * *

The message came an hour later, and it came politely, which told Nadia everything about how much Corvo thought he needed force.

A boy of maybe fourteen paddled a dugout out to the mooring, held up an envelope in a plastic sleeve like an offering, and would not come closer no matter how Ulyana coaxed. He left the envelope floating in a tin bowl and paddled backward the whole way to shore, eyes on the Kestrel the entire time, as if the boat might lunge.

Ulyana fished it in with a boathook. The envelope was heavy stock, dry despite the humidity, expensive. Inside, a single card, typed:

*Mrs. Marchetti —*

*The registry road is closed as of this morning for flood assessment. I regret the inconvenience. Alternative arrangements can be made.*

*You have until tomorrow's sundown to deliver what Elias Thorn carried off my employer's property. Failure to comply will be met with consequences beginning with the vessel currently at anchor and proceeding from there.*

*I want to emphasize that this is a business matter. Nothing about it needs to be difficult.*

*C.H.*

Nadia read it twice over Ulyana's shoulder. The paper trembled slightly, and it took her a moment to realize the trembling was hers, transmitted through her grip on Ulyana's arm.

"He knows your name," Nadia said. "Your real name. Not the boat name, not the cargo name."

"He's known it since the trading post. Maybe since before Thorn ever found me." Ulyana set the card down on the chart table with exaggerated care, the way you put down something venomous. "Burn the Kestrel at anchor. At *anchor*, Nadia. He wants me to watch it happen from shore."

"Then we don't give him the chance to make it theater."

"No?" Ulyana turned, and there was something raw under the anger. "What's the alternative? Hand him the map and let his lawyers bury Thorn's claim under three layers of forged paper? File by carrier pigeon? Swim the road closure?"

Nadia didn't answer right away. She walked to the stern windows instead, looking downriver toward the district seat, invisible beyond the bend but present in her mind as a grid she'd memorized years ago: the registry office with its green shutters, the radio mast behind it, the cluster of government buildings on the rise. And two streets over, the surveyor's cooperative — a low building with a rusted antenna and a man named Tomas Ferreira who owed her more than money.

She'd been turning something over since the Throat. Since the flooded narrows, when Ulyana had handed her the wheel of a dying boat in white water and said *your call* without a flicker of doubt. Courage wasn't something Nadia had ever felt she owned outright. But borrowed courage spent just as well.

"There's a relay," she said.

Ulyana looked at her.

"The registry accepts filings by verified radio transmission during flood closures. Emergency provision — it's in the statute, section eleven, they wrote it after the '09 season drowned the whole road network for six weeks. A licensed surveyor transmits the claim coordinates, marker descriptions, and supporting documentation to the registry clerk, who logs the timestamp. The physical originals follow within thirty days." Nadia tapped the card. "Corvo closed the road because he thinks the road is the only door. It isn't. There's a window, and it has an antenna."

"Can you transmit?"

"Not from here. But Tomas Ferreira runs the cooperative two streets from the registry office. He'll do it. He owes me." She said it steadily and hoped Ulyana wouldn't hear the seam in it — that Tomas owed her partly because she'd covered for him on a boundary dispute that had gone badly, partly because he'd been in love with her for four years and she'd never once encouraged or discouraged it, because his goodwill was useful. Everyone's ledger had ugly entries. Hers were just better organized than most.

"And Corvo hasn't cut the radio?"

"He can't. Cutting the registry's emergency relay means cutting the district's flood coordination, medical traffic, everything. Even Halstead can't do that without making it official, and official means inspectors, and inspectors are the one thing the syndicate can't survive right now. Their permits are forged, Ulyana. They need this quiet. Quiet is our weapon."

Ulyana was quiet herself for a long moment, staring at the card. Then: "So we transmit the claim. Fine. Why does your face look like that?"

"Like what?"

"Like there's a second half of the plan you haven't said out loud yet."

Because there was. Nadia made herself turn from the window and face it. "Because if we file by relay and run, Corvo doesn't stop. He burns the Kestrel anyway out of spite, or he follows the signal, or he spends the next year making both our lives unlivable while the claim sits in litigation. A filed claim is a shield, but shields work best when the enemy is committed somewhere else." She took a breath. "So we commit him somewhere else. We give him a map."

* * *

They argued about it through the afternoon, which meant Ulyana argued and Nadia held position, and somewhere in the middle of it the argument stopped being about tactics and became about something else entirely.

"A forgery." Ulyana stood at the chart table with her palms flat on it, as if holding the boat steady against a current that wasn't there. "You're proposing I hand that man a fake map and smile at him."

"I'm proposing we buy forty-eight hours with paper. He'll take the decoy, run his people to coordinates we invent, and by the time he figures out the plateau has no markers where his map says they should be, the real claim is stamped, logged, and public record. Public record, Ulyana. After that, killing us costs him more than leaving us alive."

"You don't know that men like him calculate that way—"

"Yes I do." It came out harder than Nadia intended, and she let it stay hard. "I've worked for outfits like his. Smaller, meaner. Men like Corvo don't burn bridges while they're still standing on them. He's ruthless, not stupid. If the claim is filed and legitimate, destroying us creates witnesses, inquiries, headlines. What he'll actually do is try to buy us, and when that fails, he'll vanish and lick his wounds. I've seen it."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I'm wrong standing next to you, which is where I'd be anyway."

That landed. Nadia watched it land — saw Ulyana's shoulders drop half an inch, saw the argument go out of her the way a fire goes out of wet wood, not defeated so much as saturated.

"You trust me with this," Ulyana said quietly. It wasn't quite a question.

"I trusted you with my life in the Throat. This is easier. This is just paper."

"It's never just paper. Paper is the whole reason either of us is in this mess." Ulyana laughed, short and bitter, then rubbed her face with both hands. When she lowered them she looked older and younger at once. "Fine. Your gambit. But I get to build the decoy. I've spent ten years reading this river and I know what a fake concession map needs to look convincing — the fuel caches, the portage notes, the little coffee stains of authenticity. If we're going to lie to that man, we're going to lie beautifully."

Something loosened in Nadia's chest that she hadn't known was tight. "Beautifully," she agreed.

* * *

They worked through the evening by lamplight while rain started again on the cabin roof, soft and patient, the monsoon settling in for the long haul.

Nadia drafted the legal skeleton: claim description, coordinate block, the statutory language Tomas would need to read aloud for the transcript. Her hands were steadier than she expected. Somewhere between the Throat and tonight, the fear had changed shape — it was no longer the fear of being caught, which had ridden her for six years like a rider on her back. It was the fear of failing something that mattered, which was heavier but cleaner, and which she could carry.

Ulyana built the decoy. She aged the paper with damp tea and dried it over the stove. She drew with Thorn's own style copied faithfully — the cramped elevation notes, the little hachure marks for scree slopes — using the real map only as reference, never letting ink touch it. Twice she caught Nadia watching her and said, "Don't look at me like that. I'm committing a crime, not performing surgery."

"You're good at it," Nadia said. "That's what worries me."

Ulyana's pen paused. "Yeah," she said, softer. "Me too."

Near midnight, Nadia tested the radio band and picked up fragments of the district flood net — water levels, road closures, a clipped voice listing gauge readings like a litany. Kilometer Forty was at 6.2 meters and climbing. The Throat was officially closed, which meant officially impassable, which meant every boat that mattered was ignoring it.

And then, threaded through the routine traffic, a voice she recognized: dry, unhurried, reading coordinates for a bridge inspection. Tomas Ferreira, alive and on the air, two streets from the registry office, exactly where the world had last left him.

Nadia keyed the mic twice — a double click, no words, nothing that would mean anything to anyone else.

Thirty seconds passed. Rain on the roof. Ulyana watching her across the lamplit chart table, the decoy map drying beside the real one, twins except for the truth.

Then the speaker clicked twice back.

Nadia exhaled a breath she felt she'd been holding since the trading post. "He's listening. Tomorrow, sundown minus two hours, we go ashore at the fish market landing, walk two streets, and file a claim that ends this."

"And Corvo?"

"Corvo gets his meeting first. You deliver the decoy at midday, exactly where he asked, exactly as frightened as he expects you to be. Give him his victory. Let him savor it." Nadia reached across the table and turned the real map face-down, hiding Thorn's dead careful handwriting. "By the time he learns what his prize is worth, it'll be worth nothing."

Ulyana studied her for a long moment in the yellow light. "When did you become the dangerous one?"

"I was always the dangerous one. I just used to be dangerous on their side of the ledger." Nadia smiled, and found it didn't feel borrowed anymore. "Get some sleep, Mrs. Marchetti. Tomorrow we spend everything."

Outside, the rain thickened, and downriver, unseen, the lights of the district town burned through the weather — and among them, Nadia knew, a man was already planning a bonfire at the water's edge, never suspecting that by nightfall the only thing left to burn would be his own case.
