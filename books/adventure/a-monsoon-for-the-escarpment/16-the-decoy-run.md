## 16. The Decoy Run

Nadia drew the forgery by lamplight in the Kestrel's cabin while the river families' children slept three decks of driftwood away, and Ulyana watched her do it and felt something in her chest turn over like an engine catching on the third try.

"You're good at this," Ulyana said.

"I'm a surveyor," Nadia said, not looking up. "I've been forging my own legitimacy for six years. This is just more paper." Her pen moved in small, certain strokes — contour lines, a benchmark cairn, the distinctive double-notch Thorn had used to mark water sources. She'd studied the real map for two hours before Ulyana had wrapped it in oilcloth and sewn it back into the lining of her own jacket, and now she was reproducing it from memory with only one deliberate difference: the coordinates.

The fake claim sat eleven kilometers northeast of the true one, up a box canyon that flooded to chest height in monsoon season and drained through a chute of broken limestone. Anyone who went there in the next week would find nothing but white water and a long walk back.

"Make the elevation readings plausible," Ulyana said. "Corvo will have a geologist."

"He has a geologist who's never seen Thorn's hand." Nadia tapped the page. "Thorn pressed too hard on his downstrokes. Left-handed, arthritic. I'm copying that." She did — the lines came out slightly gouged, the ink pooled at every turn. "And he always dated in the old registry format. Month-day-year, not day-month. Everyone under forty uses the new format. Corvo's people won't notice because they're the ones who use the new format."

Ulyana looked at her for a long moment. "How many times have you thought about getting caught?"

"Every day since I was twenty-three." Nadia set down the pen and flexed her fingers. "It teaches you to think like the person hunting you. It's not a skill I'm proud of."

"It's a skill I'm grateful for."

"Don't be grateful yet." Nadia blew across the ink. "We still have to sell it. Paper doesn't convince Corvo Halstead. You do."

* * *

They chose the trading post at Serrano Bend because it had one road in, one road out, and a veranda where a man could sit with a drink and look like he owned the weather. Corvo had left word at three posts along the middle river: *Ulyana Marchetti, the Kestrel, terms available.* The word *terms* was doing polite work. Everyone on the river knew what happened to boats that didn't come to terms.

Ulyana dressed for it the way she dressed for customs inspections — clean shirt, hair braided tight, the Kestrel's deck swabbed so the boat itself looked like a woman with nothing to hide. Nadia stayed below with the engine hatch open and grease on her hands, playing mechanic, close enough to hear everything through the deck plates.

Corvo arrived at dusk in a gray launch with twin diesels and no name painted on the transom. He came up the gangway alone, which was its own kind of message — he didn't need the four men who stayed with the boat.

"Captain Marchetti." He took the chair she offered without being asked, settling into it like a man testing furniture he intended to buy. He was younger than his reputation, or maybe the reputation was just what the river needed him to be: broad-shouldered, silver at the temples, hands folded, eyes that catalogued everything on deck including the fresh weld scar near the bow where the collision patch had been. "You've had repairs."

"River does that to a hull."

"The river does a great many things." He smiled without warmth. "I understand you've been spending time with the Ferreira family downstream. Good people. Very loyal to you, apparently. My scouts found that loyalty inconvenient."

"They're river people. They're loyal to whoever fixes their pump."

"Mm." He let the silence stretch. Ulyana poured two glasses of the cheap cane spirit she kept for officials and pushed one across the table. He didn't touch it. "Let me save us both the evening, Captain. Elias Thorn boarded your boat at Kilometer Twelve fueling dock on the ninth. He died aboard it on the tenth. He carried something, and you have it, and I am authorized to make you rich enough that you never run cargo again."

"And if I say I don't have anything?"

"Then I'm authorized to make other arrangements, and you'll spend the rest of your life checking anchor lines." He said it the way other men discussed freight rates. "But I don't think we'll need that conversation. I think you're a practical woman. Everything I've heard says so. Ten years on this river, no debts unpaid, no port skipped, no promises broken. You have a reputation for keeping your word."

"That's funny," Ulyana said, "coming from a man whose permits are forged."

Something flickered behind his eyes — quick, professional, gone. "I have no idea what you mean."

"No," Ulyana agreed. "You wouldn't."

She let the silence work. This was the part she'd rehearsed on the run upriver, lying awake in the berth while Nadia slept, running the scene like a rapid over and over until she knew every rock. Corvo wanted the map. A woman who denied having it was a problem. A woman who bargained was a partner. And a woman who was afraid — a woman whose fear showed at exactly the right moments — was a customer.

She let her hands shake. Just slightly. Just enough.

"Look," she said, and made her voice roughen. "Thorn was dying when he came aboard. He gave me papers. I didn't ask what they were — I thought they were debt notes, honestly, the way he talked about owing people. When I opened them—" She stopped. Drank. Let the pause do its work. "When I opened them, I understood why men like you were asking after him."

Corvo leaned forward a centimeter. "Go on."

"There's a claim. Escarpment plateau, north fork drainage. Thorn surveyed it himself, years ago, before your company's concession filings closed the area. He kept it hidden because—" she laughed, short and bitter, "—because he knew what would happen if anyone found out. What is happening right now, on this veranda."

"And the map?"

"It's mine now. He signed it over. Witnessed by the dock master at Kilometer Twelve, if you want to check." That much was even true; the old dock master had scrawled his name on the transfer for the price of a bottle, and by now he'd have forgotten what he'd signed. "I'll sell it. But not for a promise of riches from a syndicate that forges its own permits. Cash. Certified draft, drawn on the Verrane Mercantile Bank, payable before I hand over anything. And a bill of sale that says the transaction is complete, all claims settled, no further interest in me, my boat, or my passengers."

"Passengers." His gaze drifted toward the engine hatch. "The surveyor."

"She's crew."

"She's a woman with forged credentials whom my company has been generous enough not to prosecute. Generosity has limits, Captain."

"Her papers are between her and the licensing board. Not you." Ulyana held his eyes and hated how steady hers were. "Cash and the bill of sale, Mr. Halstead. Or I take my chances with the river, and you can explain to your board why the claim lapsed unclaimed."

He studied her for a long moment, and Ulyana understood that he was weighing her the way she weighed a channel in flood — depth against current, risk against reward. She watched him decide that greed outweighed suspicion. It was the same arithmetic her father had done, once, choosing syndicate money over his daughter, and she felt the recognition land in her stomach like bilgewater.

"Tomorrow morning," Corvo said. "The bank opens at nine. I'll have the draft by ten. Where do we exchange?"

"Here. On my deck. Then you leave, and I never see you again."

"Captain Marchetti," he said, rising, extending his hand, "you drive a hard bargain. Your father would have been proud of you."

She shook his hand. Her skin crawled where his had been, and she smiled, and the smile felt like something dead being puppeted upright.

* * *

The certified draft arrived at ten sharp, delivered by one of Corvo's men with the launch idling offshore. Ulyana checked the serial numbers against the bank's published list — Nadia had insisted, and Nadia was right — and then she brought out the map.

She'd wrapped it in waxed canvas, tied with tarred twine, handled it with the reverence due a dying man's last wish. Corvo received it the same way, and for a moment — just a moment — she saw something almost human cross his face. Relief, maybe. Or the particular hunger of a man who believed the thing he'd killed for was finally in his hands.

Because Thorn hadn't drowned, whatever story Corvo told himself. Ulyana had done the arithmetic weeks ago: the fever, the timing, the way Corvo's questions had started before Thorn ever reached her boat. Somewhere behind this polite man and his gray launch was the reason Elias Thorn had been running through monsoon country with a hole in his lungs.

"Coordinates check out?" she asked.

"My geologist will verify them." He tucked the package inside his coat. "If they're false, Captain—"

"If they're false, you know where to find me. I've spent ten years being findable. It's the one thing I'm good at besides driving this boat."

He smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, and that was worse. "Pleasure doing business."

The gray launch turned downstream toward the escarpment country, twin diesels chewing the brown water into foam, and Ulyana stood on her deck and watched it shrink until it was a smudge, until it was nothing, until the river closed over the place where it had been.

Nadia came up the companionway wiping her hands on a rag. "Well?"

"He bought it."

Nadia exhaled. "Then we have maybe four days before his geologist finds a box canyon full of whitewater and he figures out—"

"Three. Maybe less, if he flies a spotter plane." Ulyana turned away from the empty river. "We run tonight. High water, night navigation, straight up the Throat."

"Ulyana." Nadia caught her arm. "Hey. We did it. Look at me — we *did* it."

"We did it." The words came out flat as a sounding line hitting mud.

* * *

They cast off at slack tide under a sky the color of wet slate, and Ulyana took the wheel herself because she didn't trust her hands to do anything else. The Kestrel climbed the darkening river at full throttle, and the engine noise filled the cabin, and still Ulyana could hear the sound the waxed canvas had made when Corvo's coat closed around it. A soft crinkle. Almost like paper. Exactly like paper.

Elias Thorn had traded her his life's work for a promise, and she had kept the promise — the real map was sewn against her ribs right now, riding above her heartbeat like a second heart. She had kept the promise. So why did it feel like breaking one?

*Because you lied well,* she thought, steering through the first sheets of rain. *Because you sat on that veranda and performed fear and greed and desperation like a woman born to it, and you enjoyed it.*

That was the truth underneath, the one she couldn't scrub off with river water. There had been a moment — Corvo leaning forward, hanging on her invented details, believing her — when something in her had lit up like the running lights. Power. The pure animal pleasure of controlling what another man believed. She had seen her father in that moment, or rather she had seen herself wearing her father's face, and the resemblance had not ended when she walked out of the Marchetti house at nineteen with one bag and no name. Blood kept its accounts. She'd just moved the ledger to a different boat.

"Talk to me," Nadia said from the chart table, not looking up from the compass. "You've been quiet for an hour, and you're holding the wheel like it insulted you."

"I sold a dead man's ghost today."

"You sold a lie to a man who kills people. Those aren't the same thing."

"Aren't they?" Ulyana adjusted course around a half-submerged snag, movements automatic. "My father used to say the difference between a businessman and a criminal was paperwork. I swore I'd never be either. Today I was both."

Nadia set down the pencil. She crossed the pitching cabin and stood beside Ulyana at the wheel, one hand braced on the overhead, and looked out at the rain-hammered dark instead of at her.

"When I was twenty-three," Nadia said, "Thorn forged my credentials because the alternative was watching a mining company strip a watershed that fed four villages. I've told myself for six years that the forgery was the moral act. Some days I believe it." A wave slapped the hull; neither of them flinched. "Some days I know I just wanted to matter, and I let him give me a shortcut to mattering. Both things are true. They're both going to stay true. That's what doing the right thing badly looks like, Ulyana. It looks like this. It feels like this."

"And if I liked it?"

"Then you're honest with yourself, which is more than Corvo Halstead will ever be." Nadia's shoulder pressed against hers, brief and warm. "You gave a lie to a killer to protect a truth. He'd have burned the Kestrel at anchor otherwise, and you know it. Save the self-flagellation for after we file. If there's time left over, I'll help."

Ulyana laughed despite herself — a short, cracked sound the wind took immediately. Ahead, through the ragged curtain of rain, lightning stitched the horizon above the escarpment, and beneath the thunder she could hear it: the Throat, kilometers upstream, already roaring.

"Corvo's geologist will find that canyon in three days," she said. "Four if the storm grounds his planes."

"Then we file in two."

"Two days means running the Throat at peak flood." Ulyana looked at the black water, the white seams of rapids showing even in the dark, the river risen a meter since morning and climbing. "Nadia, I need you to hear what I'm about to say and not flinch. If the Kestrel broaches in the Throat at this level, we don't swim out. Nobody swims out of the Throat in flood. The river takes the boat, the map, and both of us, and in ten years some ferry pilot points at a bend and tells passengers a ghost story."

Nadia was quiet for a moment. Rain drummed the cabin roof like impatient fingers.

"Elias ran that water twice," she said finally. "He told me the second time that the river doesn't kill pilots. It kills hesitation." She met Ulyana's eyes. "Are you hesitating?"

Ulyana gripped the wheel and felt the answer move through her arms before it reached her mouth — the old restless certainty, the thing that had kept her moving for ten years, repurposed at last toward something worth arriving at.

"No," she said, and pushed the throttle forward. "Hold onto something. We're going to beat him to the registry if I have to carry this boat up the escarpment on my back."

Ahead, the lightning flashed again, and in its white instant Ulyana saw the entrance to the Throat — a black seam in the escarpment wall vomiting spray — and saw, moored in the lee just below it, a gray launch with twin diesels and no name on the transom.

Corvo hadn't gone to the decoy coordinates after all.

He was waiting at the narrows, and he was between them and everything that mattered.
