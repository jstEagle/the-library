## 17. Escarpment Rising

The rain came at them sideways now, the way it had for two days, and Nadia had stopped thinking of it as weather. It was a condition. A fact of the world, like gravity or the weight of the map case strapped against her ribs. The Kestrel's wipers had died outside Kilometer Forty and Ulyana piloted with her head out the side window half the time, rain hammering her face, reading the water by instinct and fury.

"There." Ulyana's voice carried over the engine noise without effort. It always did. "See the notch?"

Nadia saw it. On the eastern bank, where the Verrane bent hard around a shoulder of black rock, the escarpment rose out of the flood like the spine of something drowned and rising. The monsoon had turned the base of it into a churning brown lake, but the rock itself climbed clean and vertical for three hundred meters before disappearing into cloud. Somewhere up there — four hundred meters, maybe five, along the basalt shelf Thorn's map described — were the claim markers. Iron posts driven in 1961 by a survey crew that had never filed their findings.

And somewhere in the seam between the rocks, if Elias Thorn hadn't lied to her too, was the proof that everything the syndicate had built on this river was rotten from the first stone.

"Can we get ashore?" she asked.

Ulyana cut the throttle and let the Kestrel drift, studying the bank with the flat assessment Nadia had learned to read over three weeks of river. Three weeks since the fueling dock. Three weeks since a feverish old man had pressed a folded map into Ulyana's hands and made her swear on something Ulyana wouldn't name.

"The eddy behind that spur," Ulyana said finally. "If I come in bow-first and you jump with the line, I can hold her against the current long enough to tie off. After that she's at the mercy of whatever the river does overnight."

"And if the river wins?"

Ulyana's mouth twitched. Not quite a smile. "Then we walk home."

* * *

They jumped at the spur and the river nearly took the line off the cleat in the first minute. Nadia hauled until her shoulders screamed while Ulyana feathered the engine, and then the Kestrel swung into the lee of the rock and went still, groaning, riding her anchor lines like an animal settling into a den. Nadia stood waist-deep in floodwater on a shelf of submerged basalt and felt the whole escarpment humming through her boots — not sound exactly, but pressure. The mountain taking the river's hit and giving nothing back.

Thorn had loved this place. She understood that now. He'd talked about it in his last lucid hour, back when he was still trying to convince them both: *the rock doesn't negotiate, girl. That's why I trusted it more than people.*

She'd thought it was fever talk. Standing here, she wasn't sure.

Ulyana killed the engine and the silence was enormous — just rain and water and the creak of strained lines. She came over the gunwale with the pack, the pry bar, and the expression she wore when she'd already decided something and was waiting for the world to catch up.

"How high?"

Nadia checked the map one more time, though she'd memorized it days ago. Thorn's hand-drawn contours, the elevation notes in his cramped engineer's script. "The markers are on the second shelf. Maybe an hour's climb in this. The cache—" she hesitated. "He said the cache is higher. Near the old survey camp."

"Cache."

"He didn't tell you?"

"He told me to deliver a map and file a claim. He was rationing information like it was morphine." Ulyana shouldered the pack. "What's in the cache, Nadia?"

And there it was — the question Nadia had been circling for eleven days, through smuggler channels and flooded narrows and one night of lamplight confessions that had cost her more than she'd expected to pay. Ulyana had given her grace after the confession about the forged credentials. Grace, and no further questions. But they were standing at the bottom of the mountain now, and the truth was going to be dragged out of this rock one way or another.

"Elias didn't just find markers," Nadia said. "He found the original survey records. The 1961 crew's field books. And he found the syndicate's founding permit — the concession charter that gives them title to half this river basin." She pulled the waterproof sleeve from inside her jacket, the photocopy she'd been carrying since Port Amara, since before any of this. "I ran the registry numbers six months ago. The charter references a mineral survey that was never conducted. The signature of the government geologist who supposedly certified it — that man retired in 1959. Two years before the permit date."

Ulyana took the page and read it in the rain, holding it under her jacket to keep it dry, her eyes moving fast. When she looked up, the rain was running off her jaw and her face had gone very still.

"They've been mining under a forged permit for sixty years."

"Every lease downstream. Every royalty agreement. Every claim they've bullied small operators off of." Nadia heard her own voice go flat and hard, the voice she used for data. "It's all built on paper that was never legal. Elias knew. He tried to take it to the district office twenty years ago and got run off the river for it. So he waited. He kept the proof and he waited for someone with a boat and a reason."

"A dying man's revenge," Ulyana said quietly.

"A dying man's *correction*." Nadia took the page back. "There's a difference."

"Is there?" Ulyana looked up at the escarpment, veils of water sheeting off its face. "Come on. Let's go meet the truth where it lives."

* * *

The climb was worse than the map promised.

The basalt held handholds the way basalt does — grudgingly, in cracks that wanted fingers broken — and the rain turned every surface to grease. They roped together with the Kestrel's spare anchor line because it was what they had, forty meters of braided nylon that Ulyana insisted on testing twice before trusting it with either of their weights. Nadia led the first pitch because she'd done this work for twelve years and Ulyana hadn't, and felt the strange reversal of their usual geometry: for once she was the competent one, calling holds, setting anchors, listening to Ulyana breathe hard behind her and say nothing except *good* and *left* and *again.*

The second shelf appeared out of the rain like a promise kept. A horizontal seam in the cliff face, wide enough to walk, running east toward a stand of stunted figs whose roots split the rock. And there, half-swallowed by lichen and sixty years of weather, stood the iron post.

Nadia reached it first. Her hands were shaking — cold, adrenaline, something else — as she scraped the lichen away with her knife.

The stamp was still legible. *Verrane Concession Survey — Party 4 — 12 June 1961.* Below it, the registration number, and below that, punched deep into the iron, the coordinates that matched Thorn's map to the meter.

"It's real," she said, and her voice cracked on the second word. "Ulyana. It's *real.*"

Ulyana crouched beside her, rain streaming off her hood, and put her palm flat against the post the way you'd check a horse's flank. "Sixty years," she said. "Standing here the whole time while they told everyone the plateau was empty."

"There were supposed to be four posts. Party Four set four." Nadia was already moving east along the shelf, counting paces, checking Thorn's contour notes against the actual rock. "Second one should be—"

She found it forty minutes later, and it told her something the first one hadn't.

The second post had been pulled. Not toppled — *pulled*, the socket chiseled wider and the iron dragged out, then jammed back crooked and shallow, a botched erasure. Someone had come up here, decades ago, and tried to make the survey disappear. And someone else — Thorn, almost certainly, sometime in the years when the river believed him dead — had hammered it back in, badly, and left it standing anyway. Defiance in iron.

"He knew they'd come for it," Nadia said. She knelt in the mud beside the crooked post, rain drumming her shoulders. "That's why he moved the field books. Why the cache is higher up. He knew the syndicate would send people to destroy anything on the plateau itself, so he kept the real evidence separate. The markers prove the survey happened. The field books prove what it found. And the permit copy proves what they did about it."

Ulyana stood looking down at her, and in the grey light her face had changed. Nadia had watched her lie beautifully to Corvo Halstead, watched her run a gorge blind in lightning, watched her hold a leaking boat together with willpower and bilge paint. She'd thought she'd seen every version of Ulyana Marchetti.

This one was new. This was Ulyana understanding, all at once, the size of what they were carrying.

"My father signed leases for these people," Ulyana said. "When I was nineteen. He sat me down at the kitchen table and explained that the Marchetti land was worth ten times more leased to the consortium than farmed, and that anyone who couldn't see that was a child." She laughed, short and airless. "I thought he was greedy. I've spent fifteen years thinking my father was just greedy."

"You think he didn't know?"

"I think—" Ulyana stopped. Rain filled the pause. "I think he might have been lied to too. Or I think he knew and told himself the paper must be fine, because the alternative was admitting he'd sold our name to thieves. I don't know which is worse." She shook herself, physically, like a dog shedding water. "The cache. Show me how far."

"Another two hundred meters of climbing. The old survey camp should be under an overhang near the third marker position — Thorn said the tarp was still intact in '09."

"Then we climb. And Nadia—" Ulyana caught her arm above the elbow, grip firm through wet canvas. "Whatever's up there, we photograph everything before we move anything. Two copies of every image. One stays on me, one goes in the bilge compartment welded under the floorboards. If Corvo takes us, he gets the decoys and nothing else."

"When did you become the careful one?"

"Somewhere around the third time this boat nearly sank." But Ulyana's hand stayed on her arm a moment longer than the sentence needed, and Nadia let it.

* * *

The survey camp was exactly where a dead man's memory said it would be.

Under the overhang, sheltered from sixty years of rain, a rotting canvas tarp covered a low stack of oilcloth-wrapped bundles, and beneath the bundles, in a ammunition box gone brown with rust, lay the field books.

Nadia opened the first one with hands that would not stay steady, and the past spoke in pencil.

*Party 4, day 11. Struck the mother lode of disappointment — no alluvial gold worth the name on the plateau proper. BUT. Basalt formation on the east shelf carries copper sulfides at concentrations I have not seen in thirty years of this work. Estimated tonnage exceeds anything registered in this district. Recommend immediate filing.*

Day 14. *Party chief received visitor from the Consortium. Private conversation. Chief has gone quiet. Says we will "reassess" before filing.*

Day 15. *Chief says the survey is concluded and the results are negative. My own readings say otherwise. I have copied the field notes. If anything happens to these pages, the copies exist elsewhere.*

Day 16. *We are going down the river tomorrow. The chief is drinking. The Consortium man asked me, smiling, whether I valued my family. I said yes. He said, good, then the plateau is empty and always was.*

Nadia sat back on her heels. The rain roared beyond the overhang and she barely heard it. Sixty years ago a surveyor — some other surveyor, some earlier version of herself with better handwriting and worse luck — had sat in this exact spot and understood that the truth was worth more than his safety, and had hidden it in iron and oilcloth and trusted the mountain to keep it.

"Elias found these in '09," she said. Her voice sounded far away. "He told me once he'd spent his whole life prospecting for something worth finding, and what he found was paper. He said it like a joke. It wasn't."

Ulyana had taken the permit copy from her jacket and laid it beside the field book, the forged signature and the dead surveyor's honest numbers facing each other across sixty years. She was quiet for a long time.

"This ends them," she said finally. "You understand that. Not the claim — the claim is money, and money they can survive. This—" she touched the field book "—this is the charter. Every permit they've ever filed descends from the 1961 concession. If the root is forged, the whole tree comes down. Leases void. Royalties clawed back. Half the river basin reverts to unclaimed status." She looked up, and there was something in her face that frightened Nadia a little — not doubt, not fear. Appetite. "My father's leases. Void."

"Is that justice," Nadia asked carefully, "or is that revenge?"

Ulyana was silent. Then, slowly, she began wrapping the field books in fresh oilcloth from the pack, her movements precise, unhurried.

"Both," she said. "For the first time in my life, both at once. That's what Elias was offering, I think. Not a choice between the safe life and the right one. Just both, at the same time, if you're willing to carry the weight." She cinched the wrap and handed the bundle to Nadia. "Carry this. I'll carry the markers' photos. And Nadia — when we get to the district office, when this goes on the record — your name goes on it too. Your credentials may have started forged, but this work is real. Twelve years real. Nobody erases that. Not the syndicate, not the registry, not you."

Nadia wanted to answer and found she couldn't. She nodded instead, and tucked the bundle inside her jacket, against her ribs, next to where the map had ridden for three weeks. Lighter now. Or heavier. She genuinely could not tell.

* * *

They were halfway down the second pitch when Nadia saw it — a flicker through the rain on the river below, movement where movement shouldn't be.

She froze on the rope. "Ulyana."

Ulyana swung in beside her, followed her gaze down.

At the mouth of the eddy, where the Kestrel rode her lines in the lee of the spur, a black-hulled launch was sliding upstream against the flood with the arrogant ease of twin two-hundred-horse engines. It didn't slow. It didn't need to. A figure stood at the rail with binoculars raised, sweeping the cliff face, patient as weather.

Corvo Halstead hadn't followed them up the Throat after all. He'd simply known where the map ended — because every map ends somewhere — and had come to wait at the last page.

"He can't see the overhang from the water," Nadia breathed. "The camp's invisible from below. If we stay here—"

"He'll wait us out. He knows we have to come down eventually, and he knows the Kestrel can't outrun that launch in flood water." Ulyana's voice was calm, which meant she was already three moves ahead and didn't like any of them. "But he doesn't know what we found. And he doesn't know about the radio relay schedule."

"The relay. Tonight's window is at nine."

"Nine o'clock." Ulyana grinned, and it was the grin from the gorge, terror transmuted, and Nadia fell a little further in love with it despite everything. "So we sit in a dead man's camp, in the rain, above a syndicate enforcer, and we transmit sixty years of fraud to a district office that might not even be listening. And then—" she glanced down at the black launch, at the patient figure with the binoculars, "—then we figure out how to get off this mountain alive."

Above them, the escarpment vanished into storm cloud. Below them, Corvo Halstead dropped his binoculars and, unhurried, began to anchor his launch for the night.

Nadia settled back against the basalt, the field books hard against her heart, and started counting minutes to nine.
