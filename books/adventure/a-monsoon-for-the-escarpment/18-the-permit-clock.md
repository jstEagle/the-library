## 18. The Permit Clock

The radio died at 11:47 in the morning, mid-word.

Ulyana knew it was dead the way she knew weather — not by the instrument but by the silence that followed it. Nadia had been dictating the claim coordinates to Devi Okafor's relay station in Serrado for eleven minutes, spelling out the surveyor's registration number twice because the static kept eating the vowels, and then the static swallowed everything, and Nadia's voice kept going for another full sentence before she understood that no one was on the other end anymore.

"Devi? Devi, confirm receipt. Devi—"

Ulyana stood at the Kestrel's rail with the rain running off her jaw and watched Nadia's shoulders change. She'd seen that shape before, on other people, in other years. It was the shape a body makes when it understands that the thing it was holding has already fallen.

"Antenna's fine," Nadia said, though she was already climbing to check it. "Power's fine. It's the relay. Devi's station must have gone down."

"Or been made to go down." Ulyana didn't raise her voice. There was no one to raise it to. The river around them was a brown fury, the escarpment a gray wall to the east, and the rain came down in ropes. "Corvo found the decoy."

Nadia came down the ladder slowly, one rung at a time, like a woman descending into her own bad news. "The coordinates were good. I built them good. Real tributary, real bench land, plausible markers. It would take him a day to know."

"He's had a day." Ulyana checked her watch. "He's had a day and a half, and Corvo Halstead doesn't get fooled for long. He'll have put a man on Devi's station the moment he smelled the forgery. Maybe he didn't even burn it. Maybe he just listens now. Either way, nothing we say into that box reaches a registry clerk."

Nadia stood in the cockpit, dripping, the claim papers wrapped in oilcloth pressed against her chest like a child. "The radio relay was the whole plan."

"The whole plan was mine," Ulyana said. "The decoy was yours. So we split the blame evenly and move on." She heard herself say it and was surprised at how steady it came out, because underneath the steadiness her heart was doing something ugly and fast. Eleven years on this river had taught her every kind of corner, and this was the worst kind: the one with a clock in it.

She did the arithmetic out loud, because saying numbers made them real and real things could be fought.

"Permits lapse at close of business tomorrow. Not midnight — close of business, four o'clock, because the district office keeps banker's hours even at the end of the world. The office is at Porto Verde. That's sixty kilometers upriver from here, and the river is running at flood stage, which means against us the whole way." She let that sit. "The Kestrel makes eight kilometers an hour against this current if I ask everything of her, and I'm going to ask everything of her."

"Eight hours of running," Nadia said.

"Eight hours if nothing breaks. Nothing has broken yet today, which by my accounting means we're overdue." Ulyana looked upriver, where the gorge narrowed into the gray distance and the rain turned the world into a watercolor nobody had finished. "And Porto Verde sits past the Narrows. And the Narrows are where Corvo will be."

* * *

They took stock the way you take stock in a flood — quickly, and without lying to yourself.

Fuel for the run, barely. The pump patched at Kilometer Forty was holding but singing a thin note Ulyana didn't like. The bilge was dry, which was the one mercy. The Kestrel had carried them through the Throat engineless and out the other side, and Ulyana had privately promised the old boat that if she got them through this too, Ulyana would give her a full season at anchor and never call her a workhorse again. Boats heard promises. She believed that the way she believed in the river — without proof, and completely.

Nadia spread the chart on the chart table and weighted the corners with mugs. Her hands were steady. Ulyana had learned that about her in the past three weeks: Nadia's hands went steady precisely when things were worst, as if fear and precision were the same muscle.

"Options," Nadia said. "One: we wait out Corvo. He can't blockade the Narrows forever."

"He doesn't have to. He has to blockade them until four o'clock tomorrow." Ulyana traced the river with one finger. "Time is on his side. Time is the only thing on his side."

"Two: we go around. There's the overland track from Kilometer Fifty-One to the plateau road—"

"Two days on foot in this rain, carrying papers we can't let get wet, through country where his scouts are already walking. No."

"Three." Nadia's finger stopped on the chart, on the stretch of river just below Porto Verde where the channel split around a long island and the survey lines went thin and uncertain. "We go through. Straight through the Narrows, past whatever he's got anchored there, and we don't stop."

Ulyana looked at the chart for a long time. The Narrows in flood were a different animal than the Narrows on paper — a kilometer and a half of compressed water, standing waves, and the wreck of the old ferry Amália lying on the north side where she'd gone down thirty years ago, her superstructure just under the surface at high water, a steel reef waiting politely for anyone in a hurry.

"He'll have a launch athwart the channel," Ulyana said. "Maybe two. He'll have men with rifles, and he'll have the law on his side, or at least the appearance of it — he'll claim we're carrying stolen survey documents, and half these checkpoint boys are on the syndicate's payroll, so the claim will work."

"Then we don't stop for the claim."

"You're proposing we ram a federal checkpoint."

"I'm proposing," Nadia said, and now her voice changed, dropped into the register Ulyana had heard only once before, on the night in the Throat when the engine flooded and Nadia had taken the wheel without being asked, "that in eleven hours one of two things is true. Either we're at the district office, or we're not. And if we're not, everything Thorn bled for, everything the river families lied to Corvo's scouts for, everything you've spent three weeks being hunted for — it all expires at four o'clock like a ticket. I didn't come this far to let it expire politely."

Ulyana looked at her. Rain came through the cockpit opening and spattered the chart, and Nadia didn't move to shield it, and Ulyana thought: this is the woman I would have chosen in any weather. The thought arrived without ceremony, the way true things do, and she set it aside because there was no time to hold it properly. Later. If there was a later, she would hold it.

"There's a fourth option," Ulyana said. "We run the Narrows at night. Before dawn. Corvo's launches won't expect traffic in the dark in flood — no one runs the Narrows in the dark in flood. We come through at four in the morning, we have twelve hours of margin, and we're at the office steps when the clerk unlocks the door."

Nadia stared at the chart, then at her. "Can you do that? Navigate it blind?"

"Not blind." Ulyana felt the old certainty rise in her, the one that lived below fear, below memory, in the place where she was nothing but a woman and a wheel and moving water. "By lightning. The storm cells are stacked up over the plateau all night — the forecast said so before the radio went. Every flash, I get a picture. Flash to flash, I steer between the pictures. It's how I ran the gorge the first monsoon night, except the Narrows are worse and the margin is thinner and I'll be doing it against the clock instead of with it."

"And if the lightning stops?"

"Then I steer by the sound of the Amália's wreck breaking the water, and after that I steer by God, and I've never met Him." She rolled the chart closed. "Get the papers ready. Triple-wrapped. If we go in the water, you hold the papers and I hold you, and we swim for the north bank, and you walk to Porto Verde if you have to crawl."

Nadia was quiet a moment. "You'd give me the papers? Not keep them yourself?"

"I can swim. You can't, not really — I've seen you in the water at the sandbar, you swim like a man fighting a debt. The papers go with the person most likely to reach the office." Ulyana said it flatly, practically, and watched Nadia understand what was underneath it, which was: I have decided which of us the river gets if it comes to choosing. Nadia's jaw tightened and she looked away, out at the rain, and said nothing, and the nothing was an argument Ulyana declined to have.

* * *

They ran through the afternoon at eight kilometers an hour against the current, the Kestrel laboring, the pump singing its thin note, the rain never once slackening. Ulyana took the wheel and did not give it up. There was a discipline to it — hands on the spokes, eyes reading the water the way other people read faces — and the discipline kept the other things at bay: Corvo's face across the trading post table, politely lethal; her father's signature on the syndicate's forged filing; Marco's ultimatum hanging in the wet air of the landing like a smell that wouldn't wash out.

*You have until the permits lapse. Bring the map to me and there's still a place for you in this family.*

There was no place in that family. There never had been. What there was, was a boat, and a woman in the cockpit who had confessed her worst sin by lamplight and been met with grace instead of judgment, and a dead prospector's promise sewn into an oilcloth bundle, and sixty kilometers of angry water between her and a stamp on a piece of paper that would mean, for the first time in eleven years, that stopping was survivable.

She caught herself thinking in those terms and was startled. Roots. She was thinking in roots. The word had been a curse in her mouth for a decade — Marchetti land, Marchetti name, Marchetti weight — and somewhere between the sandbar and the storm it had turned into a thing she wanted. Not the inheritance. The anchoring. The right to be somewhere when the rain ended.

"Hour mark," Nadia said beside her. "Forty-eight kilometers to the Narrows at this speed. We'll make the staging bend by two in the morning."

"Then we drift until four. I want the last approach at idle — no engine noise carrying downstream to his pickets."

"You've done this before. Waiting in the dark above a checkpoint."

"Once." The memory came up cold. "Not this river. Another life. I waited above a fuel dock in Chiado for six hours while a man I owed money to walked the bank with a lantern, and when his light went out I came down the channel with the engine at a whisper, and I never stopped moving again for eleven years." She adjusted the wheel a degree, chasing the deeper water on the outside of the bend. "Waiting is the worst part. Running is easy. Running is just the river and me."

Nadia put her hand on Ulyana's shoulder — briefly, and then not briefly, and left it there. "After tomorrow," she said, "you can stop moving."

"After tomorrow," Ulyana said, "I'll believe you."

* * *

The lightning began at midnight, far off over the escarpment, silent sheets that lit the plateau's edge like a photographer's flashbulb — the whole gray wall of the world appearing and disappearing, appearing and disappearing, as if reality itself couldn't decide whether to hold.

Ulyana killed the running lights at the staging bend and let the Kestrel drift. The current took them down toward the Narrows at the river's own pace, patient, enormous, and in the cockpit they waited the way you wait before a surgery — with everything already said and nothing left to say.

At ten minutes to four, the lightning came closer and began to speak.

Each flash gave Ulyana the Narrows in slices. The channel, white-toothed, standing waves to the height of a man. The north bank: black rock, and against it, just showing above the flood, the rusted shoulder of the Amália's hull. The south bank: a checkpoint shack with its lamp burning — and below it, athwart the lower channel, the long low shape of a syndicate launch, riding at anchor with a searchlight hooded, waiting for daylight the way Corvo did everything. Waiting. Certain. Patient as money.

And fifty meters behind it, a second boat. Smaller. A skiff with two men and a lantern, picketing the upper approach.

"Both exits covered," Nadia breathed.

"Both exits covered in daylight thinking," Ulyana said. "They're anchored for a boat that comes to them. We're not going to come to them." She felt the clarity come down over her like the rain itself — cold, total, washing everything else away. This was the thing she had never been able to explain to Marco, to her father, to anyone who had ever asked her why she couldn't just settle: that in moments like this one she was more herself than she was anywhere else on earth. Cornered, clocked, and clear. "Flash to flash, Nadia. Count with me."

The lightning walked down the sky. One — the Amália's shoulder, a black seam of wreck. Two — the standing wave train, a staircase of white falling upstream. Three — the launch, motionless, its searchlight hood dark. Four —

Four showed her the gap. Between the wreck's downstream shoulder and the first of the standing waves there was a lane of broken but navigable water, maybe twelve meters wide, that no daylight pilot would take because no daylight pilot could see it, and it ran the whole length of the Narrows like a seam in cloth, threading between the reef and the rapids, past the anchored launch on its blind side, out into the wide water below.

"North of the launch," Ulyana said. "Between the wreck and the waves. Hold the papers."

"Ulyana—"

"Hold the papers." She put both hands on the wheel. "Engine to full. Now."

Nadia threw the throttle open and the Kestrel leapt, and the Narrows took them.

The sound was the whole world. Ulyana steered by lightning — a flash and the wreck's shoulder looming black on her left, a flash and the wave-train roaring on her right, and between them the Kestrel climbing and slamming, climbing and slamming, the patched pump screaming its thin note, bilge water suddenly at her ankles and rising. A flash showed the launch fifty meters downstream and dead ahead, and she bore left, hard, into the seam, close enough to the wreck that she heard the flood tear at the old ferry's rusted plates with a sound like a crowd hissing.

A searchlight blinked on behind them — Corvo's launch, waking — and a voice came across the water with a loudhailer, distorted by wind and rain into something barely human, and Ulyana did not listen to it, because listening was a luxury and the clock was not.

"Flash," Nadia counted, and the lightning gave them the lower channel, open, empty, the far bank a gray promise.

The Kestrel shot the gap between the Amália's grave and the standing waves, scraped something that shuddered the whole hull, and came out the downstream end of the Narrows into the wide brown flood with the searchlight swinging wild behind them and the district office of Porto Verde — twelve kilometers, one hour, three hours of margin — waiting upriver in the rain.

Then the engine coughed.

Ulyana's blood went to ice water. The cough came again — a stutter, a gasp, the sound the Kestrel had made once before at the worst moment of the Throat — and she was already moving, already shouting to Nadia to check the fuel line, because she could hear what it was: water in the fuel, or the pickup sucking air, or the bilge that had risen past her ankles finding its way somewhere it should never be.

The engine caught. Held. Caught again.

And downstream, behind them, the searchlight steadied, and the syndicate launch's engines started — two of them, deep and angry, coming up to full.

Ulyana looked at the gauge. Looked at the rain. Looked at the twelve kilometers of flood between her and the only stamp in the world that mattered.

"Come on, old girl," she said to the wheel, to the hull, to the eleven years. "One more lie to tell the river."

The Kestrel answered — a shudder, a surge, and then, grudgingly, eight kilometers an hour against the current, with Corvo Halstead's launch coming up the Narrows behind them like the end of the world.
