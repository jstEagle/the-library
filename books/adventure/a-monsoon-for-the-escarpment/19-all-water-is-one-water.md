## 19. All Water Is One Water

The rain came down like the sky had finally given up pretending to be merciful.

Ulyana stood at the wheel of the Kestrel with both hands locked on the spokes, watching Corvo Halstead's launch cut across the flooded channel ahead of them, its running lights burning through the gray curtain like two small, arrogant suns. Behind it, a second boat — a hired gunboat out of Porto Serrado, the kind with a mounted winch and men who didn't ask questions — held position at the narrows where the river squeezed between drowned cliffs and ran white.

They'd known. Of course they'd known. The radio relay had failed mid-transmission an hour ago, and somewhere in that silence, Corvo had done his arithmetic. There was one road to the district office now, and he was standing on it.

"He's signaling," Nadia shouted over the storm. She was braced against the chart table, soaked through her oilskin, hair plastered flat. "He wants us to heave to."

"Of course he does." Ulyana's voice came out flat and calm, which surprised her, because inside she was anything but calm. Inside she was ten years old again, watching her father sign papers that sold their name, watching him choose money over blood, learning the lesson she'd built her whole life on: *never stop moving, never let anyone hold something of yours.* "Signal back that we're considering our options."

"We don't have options."

"We have one." Ulyana looked at the water.

That was the thing about the Verrane in peak flood that no chart could teach you. Every mapmaker drew the channels as they were in low water — neat blue lines between tidy banks. But in monsoon, all those lines dissolved. The sandbars vanished. The smuggler cuts, the dead arms, the oxbows — everything became one single, roaring, brown animal, and the river stopped being a path and became a fact. You couldn't fight it. You couldn't out-think it. All you could do was read it, and commit, and pray you'd read it right.

All water is one water. Her father used to say that, standing at the rail of his own boat a lifetime ago, before the syndicate's money reached up the river and pulled him under. She'd hated the phrase for a decade. It sounded like surrender.

Standing here now, with Corvo's launch ahead and the district office forty kilometers upriver and nine hours left on a permit clock, it sounded like permission.

"Nadia." She kept her eyes on the water. "The Throat spat us out two hundred meters behind him. His launch draws what — a meter and a half?"

"At least. Loaded, more."

"And mine draws ninety centimeters empty." Ulyana felt the old grin start, the one she usually kept for bad decisions. "And I know where the gravel bar sits off the north point, because I've run this stretch every low-water season for ten years and memorized every rock that ever showed its teeth. Right now that bar is under maybe sixty centimeters of flood. He can't cross it. I can."

Nadia stared at her. Rain hammered the wheelhouse roof. "You want to go over the bar."

"I want to go over the bar, come down inside his line, and put my bow through his hull before he understands what's happening."

"That's not navigation. That's assault with a riverboat."

"It's both. I'm a multitasker."

But Nadia wasn't laughing. She grabbed Ulyana's sleeve, turned her from the window. Up close her eyes were fierce and terrified and absolutely certain. "Corvo will shoot. He burned a man's boat at anchor last season — everyone knows it, nobody can prove it. If you do this, there's no taking it back."

"There's no taking any of it back," Ulyana said. "Not the map. Not Thorn dying in my bunk. Not ten years of running. The only question left is whether I arrive at that office as a woman who hid, or a woman who delivered." She covered Nadia's hand with her own, brief and hard. "Get below. Strap the documents to your chest. If we take water, you swim for the north bank with the claim papers and you don't look back."

"And if you go down?"

"Then you file it yourself and tell the story however makes me sound best."

Something cracked in Nadia's face — half laugh, half sob — and then she kissed Ulyana once, hard, tasting of rain and fear, and went below without another word. That was the thing about crew, Ulyana thought. Real crew didn't waste breath on goodbyes.

She throttled up.

* * *

The Kestrel answered like she always did — reluctantly, then completely. Forty years of patched steel and a rebuilt engine that Ulyana knew better than her own heartbeat. The bow lifted into the flood, dug in, and held.

Corvo saw her coming. Of course he did. His launch swung its searchlight across the water and found her, and the loudspeaker crackled through the rain: *"Kestrel, heave to or be fired upon. This is your final warning."*

Ulyana keyed her own mic, mostly because she wanted him to hear her voice when she refused. "Corvo Halstead. This is Ulyana Marchetti."

The name landed. Even through the static she heard the pause on his end — the small hitch of a man recalculating. Nobody had called her by that name on this river in ten years. She'd paid good money and bad decisions to bury it.

"You're carrying stolen property," Corvo's voice said, smooth as bilge oil. "Come alongside and we'll sort this out civilly. I have no quarrel with you personally."

"You burned a fisherman's boat at Angra dos Reis because his cousin owed your syndicate money," Ulyana said. "You have a quarrel with anyone who won't kneel. And you're standing on forged permits, and in about eight hours everyone's going to know it."

"Last warning."

"Here's mine." She spun the wheel hard to starboard and drove the Kestrel straight at the drowned gravel bar off the north point — the one no loaded launch could cross, the one her keel would kiss and clear by a hand's width if the flood was what she remembered and God was paying attention.

The first shot hit the wheelhouse a second later.

Glass exploded inward across the chart table. Ulyana ducked below the sightline, steering by the compass and by feel, by twenty years of knowing how a hull talks to the water. A second round punched through the port side. Somewhere below, Nadia was shouting something Ulyana couldn't hear over the engine and the rain and the roar of the bar coming up beneath them.

Then the Kestrel hit the shoal.

It wasn't a grounding — it was a graze, a long shrieking scrape along the keel as the boat climbed the drowned bar, listed hard to starboard, hung there for one endless heartbeat with the river trying to roll her —

— and slid off the far side into deep water, wallowing, shipping green water over the rails, but *moving.*

Inside the line. Below Corvo's guns. Two hundred meters of open brown flood between her bow and his stern.

Ulyana came up from behind the console, slammed the throttle to full, and aimed the Kestrel at the launch like a woman throwing a spear.

* * *

Later, she would remember it in fragments, the way you remember a fever.

The launch turning — too slow, too heavy, its captain realizing too late that the crazy riverwoman wasn't veering off. The gunner on the foredeck firing wild, rounds skipping off the water. Nadia bursting up from below with the document satchel strapped across her chest, taking the wheel beside Ulyana without being asked, without a word, their shoulders pressed together like they were one person operating one machine.

"Hold," Ulyana said. "Hold — *now.*"

Nadia hauled the wheel over. The Kestrel swung her bow across the current, using the flood itself as a weapon, letting thirty thousand cubic meters per second of monsoon river shove her sideways into the launch's flank the way a wrestler uses an opponent's weight against him.

The impact was enormous.

Steel met steel with a sound like a cathedral bell falling down a mountainside. The launch heeled, took water over its gunwale, its searchlight whipping wildly across the sky. Men were shouting. The gunner went into the river and came up thrashing, and Ulyana — even now, even like this — threw the Kestrel's ring buoy toward him before she straightened out, because some rules you keep or you stop being who you are.

And on the launch's aft deck, holding the rail with white knuckles, stood Marco.

Her brother. Soaked, hollow-eyed, wearing a syndicate windbreaker like a costume that no longer fit. Their eyes met across twenty meters of chaos and rain, and Ulyana saw the exact moment he understood what he was looking at: not the runaway sister from the family legend, but a woman who had just taken a gunshot-punctured boat over a drowned bar to deliver a dead man's promise — while the people he'd tied himself to shot at her for it.

Corvo appeared beside him, dragging a pistol from his coat, leveling it at the Kestrel's wheelhouse — at Nadia, at Ulyana, at whatever moved.

Marco grabbed his arm.

She saw it happen. Saw her brother — the boy who'd taught her to skip stones, the man who'd signed the forged filing, the stranger who'd handed her an ultimatum in a river town — physically wrench the enforcer's aim skyward so the shot went high and wide into the rain. Saw Corvo turn on him with fury on his face. Saw Marco say something she couldn't hear, jaw set, hands still on the gun, choosing — finally, stupidly, beautifully — a side.

"Go!" Marco screamed across the water. "GO, ULYANA!"

She went.

The Kestrel surged past the listing launch, past the shouting men, past her brother standing in the rain with his whole life collapsing around him and his sister's wake washing over the deck. The narrows opened ahead — white water, drowned trees, the district office landing somewhere beyond it in the rain like a promise someone had actually intended to keep.

Behind them, Corvo's launch sat low in the water, engine flooding, radio crackling threats that the river was already drowning out.

Ahead of them, the clock was still running.

"How long?" Nadia shouted, hauling the satchel higher on her shoulder.

Ulyana checked the waterlogged watch on her wrist, then laughed — a wild, unhinged sound she didn't recognize as her own.

"Six hours to the office. Permits lapse at midnight." She fed the throttle everything the old engine had left. "We're going to make it by twenty minutes."

"And if the engine quits?"

Ulyana looked at the flooded river, at the drowned world where every channel had become every other channel, where the maps had failed and only faith remained.

"Then we drift," she said, "and we swim, and we crawl up the steps of that office dripping, and we file anyway. All water is one water, Nadia. It all flows the same direction eventually."

The Kestrel plunged into the narrows, riding the flood toward the last hours of a dead man's promise — and somewhere behind them, in the rain, her brother raised his hand in a salute or a farewell or a beginning, and did not lower it until the storm took them from his sight.
