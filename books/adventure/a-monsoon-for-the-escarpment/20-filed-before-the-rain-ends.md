## 20. Filed Before the Rain Ends

The district office of Verrane Bend sat on a bluff above the river like a man who had never once gotten his boots wet — a squat stone building with a tin roof and a flagpole, dry and smug while half the territory drowned below it.

Ulyana saw it through the windshield of the Kestrel's wheelhouse and felt something crack open in her chest that wasn't pain, exactly. It was closer to the moment a fever breaks.

"Dock's gone," Nadia said, binoculars raised. The landing that should have stretched out from the base of the bluff was underwater, its pilings showing only as two broken teeth in brown floodwater. "We can't tie off."

"We don't need to tie off." Ulyana brought the boat around into the current, throttling back until they were barely holding position against the flood. "I need you to take the wheel."

Nadia lowered the binoculars slowly. "You're going to swim."

"I'm going to wade. There's a shelf under the bluff — I've landed there before, years ago, when the water was high but not *this* high." She was already pulling off her boots, already stuffing the oilskin packet inside her jacket, against her skin, where no water short of drowning would take it. "The claim markers, Thorn's proof, everything Corvo wants to burn — it's all copies now. This is the original filing packet. It doesn't leave my body."

"And if the current takes your body?"

"Then it takes the packet too, and you get to tell Marco he was right about me being dramatic." Ulyana looked at her — at this woman she'd known for less than a month, who had bled beside her in the Throat, who had held the bilge pump for six hours without complaint, whose hands were shaking now and not from cold. "Nadia. Take the wheel."

Nadia took the wheel.

---

The water took her to the waist before her feet found bottom, and then it took her again, and for one long rolling moment Ulyana Marchetti was nothing but a leaf in a river's fist — spun, pressed down, shown briefly the green murk of her own possible ending. Then her boot heel struck the submerged shelf and she lunged forward, arms windmilling, and caught the root system of an old fig tree growing sideways out of the bluff face.

She climbed. It took everything. Her palms tore on bark; her left knee, which had been complaining since the blockade run, finally screamed loud enough to be heard over the rain. Halfway up the muddy slope she stopped, forehead against the wet earth, and laughed — a single ugly bark of sound that the storm swallowed whole.

Then she kept climbing.

---

The clerk's name was Odile Fenn, and she was sixty if she was a day, and when Ulyana burst through her door trailing water and blood and river mud across a floor that had clearly never seen either, Odile Fenn did not scream, did not reach for a weapon, did not even look up from her ledger for a full five seconds.

"You're bleeding on my linoleum," she said.

"I'm filing a concession claim."

"The registry road is closed."

"The registry road," Ulyana said, dripping, "is a rumor. I'm standing here. File it."

Odile Fenn looked up then. She had the kind of face that had spent forty years watching desperate people come through doors, and most of them liars, and she weighed Ulyana the way she might weigh a suspect coin. Outside, thunder rolled down the valley like furniture being dragged across heaven.

"Name," she said.

"Ulyana Marchetti." Saying it aloud still cost something. It always would. But it came easier than it had a month ago, easier than it had even yesterday, and she noticed that the way you notice a wound has stopped bleeding. "Filing on behalf of Elias Thorn, deceased, concession holder of record, Escarpment plateau, parcel seven-oh-nine. Permits lapse today." She set the oilskin packet on the counter and began unwrapping it with fingers that wouldn't quite obey. "Original survey. Marker verification. Thorn's affidavit regarding the syndicate's founding permit — which, for the record, is forged, and I have the documentary proof of that too, because apparently I'm thorough now."

"Filing fee—"

"Take it out of what's left of my dignity." Ulyana put a sodden fold of bills on the counter. Somewhere behind them, down the flooded road, an engine sound rose and fell — big engine, shallow draft, the kind of sound that meant Corvo Halstead had discovered his coordinates were fiction and had come back upriver in a mood. Ulyana didn't turn around. Turning around was how you lost races. "How long?"

Odile Fenn opened the packet. And then, for the first time, something moved behind her eyes — the small involuntary widening of a woman who had processed forty years of paperwork and knew, instantly and completely, when a document was real. Her finger traced the survey lines. Her lips moved over Thorn's cramped annotations. When she reached the affidavit, she went very still.

"This is—" she began.

"Yes," Ulyana said.

"—going to cause a great deal of trouble."

"It already has. It killed a good man, it nearly killed me twice, and it made my brother choose a side after twenty years of sitting on fences. Trouble is the least it owes anybody." The engine sound was louder now. Through the rain-streaked window Ulyana could see the bend of the flooded road below, and a shape moving along it — men, three of them, moving fast, and behind them the grey hull of Corvo's launch nosing through what used to be someone's orchard. "Madam Clerk. How long?"

Odile Fenn pulled a stamp from a drawer as old as the building. She checked a wall clock. She checked it again, and Ulyana watched her do the arithmetic, and watched the arithmetic come out close enough to make the clerk's jaw tighten.

"Fourteen minutes," Odile Fenn said. "The permits lapse at noon. It is currently eleven forty-six." She squared the documents, aligned their edges with a precision that bordered on ceremony, and looked up. "Sign here. Initial here. And here. And if any signature smudges because your hands are soaked through, Marchetti, I will personally throw you back in the river."

Ulyana signed. Her hand shook so badly the M came out looking like a seagull, but it was legible, and legible was all the law required. She initialed. She signed again. The stamp rose, hung for a heartbeat — Odile Fenn met her eyes over the top of it, and something passed between them that had nothing to do with ink —

— and came down.

The sound it made was very small. A thump of rubber on paper, muffled by the drumming of the rain on the tin roof. Ulyana had imagined this moment, in the rare hours she allowed herself imagination, as something operatic: trumpets, or at least thunder timed for effect. Instead it was a thump. A clerk's stamp. The same sound that registered births and deaths and dog licenses in a hundred dusty offices across the territory.

It was the loudest thing Ulyana had ever heard.

"Filed," Odile Fenn said. "Concession seven-oh-nine, Escarpment plateau, held in trust pending probate of the Thorn estate, claimant of record Ulyana Marchetti." She blotted the page, closed the ledger, and locked it in a cabinet with a key she wore on a chain around her neck. "Congratulations. You own a mountain."

The door banged open.

---

Corvo Halstead stood in the doorway with rain streaming off him and two syndicate men at his shoulders, and for a moment nobody moved. Water pooled around his boots. His coat was dark with it. He looked — Ulyana thought with a strange, detached clarity — tired. Not defeated; she wasn't fool enough to think a man like Corvo ever looked defeated. But tired, the way a machine looks tired, running hot past every tolerance its engineers built into it.

"Marchetti." His voice was pleasant. It was always pleasant. That was the horror of him. "You're making a habit of disappointing me."

"You're making a habit of showing up late." Ulyana stepped between him and the cabinet, though she knew the gesture was theater — the documents were filed, witnessed, stamped, and locked behind a clerk who had been refusing bribes since before Corvo learned to shave. "Parcel seven-oh-nine is on record. Your permits are void. Your founding claim was forged — Thorn proved it, and now the district office has the proof. You're not an enforcer anymore, Corvo. You're a defendant."

Something flickered across his face — fast, quickly mastered, but real. He glanced at Odile Fenn. "Clerk. That filing was made under duress. The claimant is a fugitive. I'm authorized to—"

"You're authorized," Odile Fenn said, without looking up from relighting a pipe that had gone out sometime during the previous administration, "to stand where you are, because the district constable is four minutes out and I telegraphed him an hour ago, when the Marchetti girl's brother walked in here soaking wet and asked me to witness his testimony."

Ulyana turned.

Marco stood just inside the door, off to the side, where a man stands when he isn't sure he'll be welcome. He was drenched. There was a cut above his eyebrow, crusted and fresh, and his hands — his careful, manicured, syndicate-salaried hands — were wrapped in somebody's field bandage. He looked at her the way he'd looked at her across the dinner table the night their father announced the arrangement, the night she'd walked out: like a man reading a verdict he'd helped write.

"You told them," Ulyana said. "About the forged permits. About Corvo."

"I told them everything." Marco's voice was steady, but his eyes weren't. "I've been telling them since the narrows. I signed those documents, Uli. I watched them forge Thorn's seal two years ago and I told myself it was administrative. Paperwork. Everyone does it." He swallowed. "Everyone doesn't do it. That's the whole point of laws, I think. I'm late understanding that."

Behind Corvo, down the flooded road, a whistle blew — two notes, then three, the constable's signal, and Corvo's men shifted like dogs hearing a distant pack. Corvo himself didn't move. He looked at Ulyana for a long moment, and whatever he found in her face seemed to settle some private account.

"For what it's worth," he said, almost gently, "the map was beautiful work. Thorn always was a craftsman." He raised his hands, palms out, as the constable's men came through the door behind him — four of them, rifles slung, breathing hard from the climb. "Gentlemen. I assume there's paperwork."

"There's always paperwork," said the constable, who was Odile Fenn's nephew and had her exact jaw. "Turn around."

They took him out into the rain. Corvo went quietly, which was somehow worse than if he'd fought. At the bottom of the steps he paused, looked back once at the Kestrel riding her anchor in the flood below the bluff, and shook his head — not in anger, Ulyana thought. In something like professional respect. Then the constable's launch took him away downriver, toward the capital, toward tribunals, toward the slow grinding end of a career built on other people's signatures.

---

Afterward there was a strange, weightless interval — the constable taking statements, Odile Fenn making tea on a spirit stove because the power was out, Marco hovering near the door like a man waiting to be told whether the building was on fire. Nadia arrived twenty minutes after the arrest, having run the Kestrel in close enough to wade ashore, and came through the door soaked and furious and searching the room with her eyes until she found Ulyana, and then she simply stopped, and the fury drained out of her face and left something younger underneath.

"Well?" she said.

"Filed." Ulyana held up her ink-stained hand. "Fourteen minutes to spare. The clerk threatened to throw me in the river if I smudged."

"She'd have done it, too," Marco said. "Aunt Odile's thrown bigger men in smaller rivers."

Nadia crossed the room and took Ulyana's hand — the ink-stained one — and held it, and didn't say anything, and didn't need to. Around them the constable's men wrung out their hats, the tea kettle whistled, the rain kept up its endless static against the tin roof, and Ulyana stood in the middle of a government office holding a dead prospector's dream in a clerk's ledger, and felt absolutely nothing like she'd expected to feel.

She'd expected triumph. What she got was lighter and stranger: disbelief, mostly. The persistent sense that the floor was missing. Ten years of motion, ten years of *never stop, never moor, never own anything they can take* — and it had ended here, in fourteen minutes, with a thump of rubber on paper.

"Miss Marchetti." Odile Fenn appeared at her elbow with a chipped enamel cup. "Your copy of the filing. Keep it dry." A pause. "And for what it's worth — I knew Elias Thorn. Bought survey stakes off him thirty years ago. He'd have liked knowing it ended with a Marchetti doing the honest thing."

"He'd have liked knowing it ended at all," Ulyana said, and took the cup, and drank tea that tasted like rust and victory.

---

Outside, eventually, the rain softened. Not stopped — monsoons don't stop, they breathe — but softened, from assault to conversation, and Ulyana walked out onto the bluff alone while Nadia slept in a chair and Marco made phone calls on the constable's ancient radio telephone, his voice low and exhausted and, for the first time she could remember, unburdened.

Below her, the Verrane ran high and brown and unstoppable, carrying whole trees, whole roofs, whole lives downstream toward a delta none of them would see. The Kestrel rode her anchor line, listing slightly, patched in six places, scarred in a dozen more, the ugliest and most beautiful thing on the water.

Ulyana stood in the rain and unfolded the clerk's certified copy, holding it under her jacket when the wind gusted, reading it again though she knew every word. *Claimant of record: Ulyana Marchetti.* Roots. Paper roots, thin as onionskin, but roots — the first she'd put down since she was nineteen years old and walking away from a house full of money and lies.

Her father had died owning half a river valley and loving none of it. She owned a mountain she'd never seen in daylight, and she was already, absurdly, planning where to plant things.

Footsteps behind her. She didn't turn. She knew the stride — she'd have known it blind, in a crowd, underwater.

"Nadia's asleep," Marco said, coming to stand beside her. Rain dripped off the eaves between them. "And I should go. There's testimony to finish, depositions, the tribunal will want—"

"Marco."

He stopped.

"Come to the escarpment," Ulyana said, still watching the river. "When it's over. When they've finished with Corvo and the permits are confirmed and the land is actually ours — mine and Nadia's. Come see it. Not to apologize. Apologies are cheap and you've never been cheap. Just come see it." She finally turned her head. "I'm not ready to forgive you. I might not be for a long time. But you testified against the syndicate knowing it would end you financially, and probably socially, and you did it anyway. So there's a version of my brother in there somewhere, and I'd like to meet him. Eventually. On my schedule."

Marco looked at her for a long time. Rain ran down his face, or maybe not all of it was rain.

"On your schedule," he agreed hoarsely. "Uli — the letter. Dad's letter. You found it?"

"I found it."

"Read it yet?"

"Twice." She folded the certified copy carefully, tucked it inside her jacket, over her heart, where the map had ridden through the Throat. "I'm going to visit him tomorrow. First time." She looked out at the brown water, at the drowned world, at the boat that had carried her through all of it. "There's something I have to say to him. And something I have to say to the name."

Below them, far downriver, the clouds were breaking — just a seam, just a knife-cut of gold light along the western escarpment, where a mountain waited with her name newly written on it.

Ulyana Marchetti stood in the rain a while longer, letting it wash the blood and mud and ink from her hands, and for the first time in ten years, she was in no hurry to be anywhere else.
