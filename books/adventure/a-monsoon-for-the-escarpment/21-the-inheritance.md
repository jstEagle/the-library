## 21. The Inheritance

The rain had thinned to a fine gray mist by the time the Kestrel nosed into the Marchetti landing, and the river had gone quiet in that way it did after a monsoon peak — swollen, sated, almost apologetic. Ulyana brought the boat in slow, hands light on the wheel, and did not let herself think about the fact that she had not tied up at this dock in eleven years.

The pilings were still hers, in a way. Her grandfather had cut them from ironbark, and ironbark did not care about disownings. They stood in the brown water the same as they always had, furred with moss, patient. She looped a line around the second one and killed the engine and the silence that followed was so complete she could hear the mist ticking against the cabin roof.

"You don't have to do this today," Nadia said from the doorway.

"I know."

"You could do it next week. Next month. You just filed a claim, you just outran a syndicate enforcer, you're allowed to sit down."

Ulyana looked at her. Nadia was still wearing the surveyor's jacket, still streaked with escarpment clay, and there was a bruise coming up yellow-green along her jaw from where she'd caught a stanchion in the Narrows. Eleven days ago Ulyana had not trusted her with the wheel. Now the thought of doing this without her felt like running a rapid without a chart.

"Sit down later," Ulyana said. "Come on."

* * *

The Marchetti cemetery sat on the shoulder of the hill above the landing, behind a low wall her great-grandfather had built from river stone, and the path up was exactly as she remembered it: too steep, badly graded, and lined with the same stubborn hibiscus her mother had planted the year Ulyana was born. They were blooming. Nobody had pruned them in a decade and they had decided, in the absence of discipline, to flourish.

She stopped at the gate.

Marco was already there.

He stood at the far end of the rows, hat in his hands, rain beading on his shoulders, and he had clearly been standing there a long time — long enough for the ground around his heels to go soft and dark. He heard the gate and turned, and for a moment neither of them moved. The last time she had seen him, he had been sliding an ultimatum across a table in a river town, and she had been sliding it back, and Corvo Halstead's men had been watching from the door.

"You came," he said.

"You look surprised."

"I've been surprised a lot lately." He gestured vaguely at the whole of the last two weeks — the blockade, the testimony, the arrest, the district office with its stamp coming down on paper like a gavel. "Mostly at myself."

Ulyana walked up the row. She did not hurry. She made him stand there in the mist while she came, and she knew she was doing it, and she let herself do it, because there were debts in this family and someone had to keep the ledger.

Their father's grave was the newest stone by a decade. GRANATO MARCHETTI. The dates. Nothing else. No epitaph, because Marco had ordered the stone before the estate was settled and the estate had been eaten by the syndicate's lawyers, and by the time there was money again there was no one left who wanted to write words.

Ulyana stood over it. She had rehearsed this — on the river, at night, in the eleven days since the clerk's stamp — and every version had been different. Rage. Grief. A speech. Silence. What arrived, actually, standing in the wet grass, was none of them. It was smaller than that. It was the memory of being nine years old and standing on her father's boots while he steered the old Marchetti barge, his hands over hers on the wheel, both of them laughing at the wake.

"He taught me to pilot," she said. Her voice came out rough. "Everyone thinks I learned it on the Verrane, running cargo. I didn't. I learned it right here, standing on his boots."

"I know," Marco said. "I was on the deck. I was jealous."

"You were twelve. You were jealous of everything."

"I was jealous of that most." Marco turned his hat in his hands. "Ulyana. I need to say some things and I need you to let me finish them, because I've been practicing them for eleven days and if you interrupt me I'll lose them."

She said nothing. Nadia, at the gate, had the good sense to stay there.

"I signed the syndicate's filing," Marco said. "I knew the permits were forged. Not suspected — knew. I'd seen the originals in the estate files when I was settling Dad's affairs, and I knew the dates didn't match, and I signed anyway because they were paying the debts Dad left and because I told myself it was just paper, it was just a claim on land nobody had walked in forty years, it was just —" He stopped. Started again. "And then Corvo put a gun on the dock at the blockade and you came through it anyway, and I watched him, and I understood that it was never just paper. It was you. It had always been you. He was going to burn your boat with you in it, and I had signed the document that put him there."

"Marco —"

"Please. Let me finish." His voice cracked and he steadied it. "I testified. That's not forgiveness, I know that. That's just arithmetic — I broke it, I said so. I'm not asking you to come home. I'm not asking you to be my sister again on my schedule. I'm asking for the chance to earn it back at whatever speed you decide. That's all. That's the whole of it."

The mist drifted through the hibiscus. Down at the landing, the Kestrel sat at her mooring with her patched seams and her scarred hull, and Ulyana thought about how many times she had patched that boat and how she had never once pretended the patch was the original plate. You fixed what was broken. You didn't pretend it had never broken. You ran it and you watched the seam and you trusted it again slowly, in fair water first, and then in worse.

"Here's what I can give you today," she said. "I can give you the truth, which is that I have hated you for eleven years and it has been the heaviest cargo I ever carried, and I am so tired, Marco. I'm tired of carrying it."

His eyes went bright.

"And I can give you time," she went on. "Not absolution. Absolution isn't mine to give anyway — some of what you broke, you broke against Dad, and against the river families, and against Nadia, and they get their say too. But time. Come to the landing in a month. Bring nothing. We'll drink coffee and I'll show you the escarpment survey and we'll see how it sits."

"That's —" He swallowed. "That's more than I hoped for."

"It's less than you want."

"It's more than I deserve." He put his hat on, took it off again, put it back on, and then — awkwardly, arms half-raised, asking permission with his whole body — he stepped toward her.

Ulyana let him hug her. It was brief and stiff and his coat was cold with rain, and she found that her eyes were burning, and that none of the eleven years had gone anywhere, and that some of the weight had gone anyway. Both things were true. She was learning, at forty-one, that both things could be true.

"I'll be here in a month," Marco said into her shoulder. "I'll bring coffee."

"Bring the good beans. Not the dock blend."

"The dock blend is fine."

"The dock blend is a war crime, Marco."

He laughed — wet, cracked, real — and it was their father's laugh, exactly, and Ulyana had to look at the grave to keep her composure. Then he nodded to Nadia at the gate, a solemn little bow that meant *thank you for what you did for her*, and went down the hill, and his footsteps faded into the mist, and he was gone.

* * *

Nadia came up the rows slowly, giving the grave the same respect she gave everything — unhurried attention, no assumptions.

"Thorn's map led here," she said. "You know that. The concession boundaries — the old survey pins he used as reference — half of them are Marchetti pins. Your grandfather surveyed this whole escarpment in the fifties."

"I know."

"Thorn wasn't a stranger who stumbled onto a lost claim. He was your father's partner, once. Before the falling out." Nadia crouched at the grave's foot, where the ground was disturbed — freshly, within the last week. "Someone's been digging here."

"I know that too." Ulyana knelt. "Marco told me, after the testimony. Dad was buried with his survey case. Marco sold the case to the syndicate's lawyers with the rest of the estate. But Dad —" she smiled, and it hurt, "— Dad never trusted lawyers with anything that mattered. There's a false bottom."

She dug with her hands, in the wet earth, the way you'd dig a clam, and in four minutes she had it: a oilcloth-wrapped bundle, flat and rectangular, sealed with the wax she recognized from a thousand childhood letters — her father sealed everything, bills, grocery lists, like every document was a treaty.

The oilcloth held two things. The first was the original concession permit, the true one, dated thirty years back, with her grandfather's survey seal and her father's signature — the document that made the syndicate's filing not just forged but *moot*, the root title, the thing Thorn had spent his last decade trying to get back to. The second was a letter. Unsent. Addressed, in her father's careful hand: *To my daughter, if she ever comes back up the hill.*

Ulyana sat down in the wet grass and opened it.

*Ulyana.*

*If you're reading this, then either I finally found the nerve to send it, or you came home over my grave, which — knowing you — is more likely.*

*Your brother will have told you I chose the syndicate's money over you. That's what it looked like from the kitchen, the night we fought, and I let him believe it because the truth was worse. The truth is I chose it over myself. The concession was failing. The syndicate offered to carry the debts, and I knew what they were, and I signed, and I told myself I'd buy it back in five years. Then ten. Then your mother died and I stopped telling myself anything. A man can drown in a river without ever touching the water, Ulyana. I drowned at my own desk.*

*When you left, I was proud of you and I could not say it, because saying it would have meant saying what I'd become, and I wasn't brave enough. You were. You were always the brave one. You took nothing and you went downriver and you built a life with your two hands, and every year I read the cargo registries to find your boat's name, and every year I found it, and every year I was proud.*

*The escarpment claim is yours. It was always meant to be yours — you're the one who loved the river, not the ledgers. Thorn has the reference map. If he ever finds you, take it. Don't be proud. Be brave. They're not the same thing, and I only learned that too late.*

*Come up the hill sometime. The hibiscus misses pruning.*

*Your father,*

*G. Marchetti*

Ulyana read it twice. The second time, her vision blurred so badly she had to hold the page at arm's length, and Nadia crouched beside her and said nothing, which was exactly right, and put one hand flat between Ulyana's shoulder blades, which was also exactly right.

"He was proud of me," Ulyana said finally. "Eleven years I thought —"

"He wrote it down. That counts."

"He never sent it."

"He sealed it and buried it with himself and trusted you to come dig." Nadia's mouth curved. "That's not nothing. That's a man who knew exactly who his daughter was."

Ulyana laughed, or sobbed, or did whatever that thing was that sits between them, and folded the letter along its old creases, and put it inside her jacket, over her heart, where the map had ridden for three hundred kilometers of floodwater.

* * *

They stayed on the hill until the mist burned off, which took hours, because neither of them suggested leaving. Ulyana told Nadia stories — the boots, the barge, the time her father had fined her a week's wages for running a sandbar at night and then secretly paid the wages back through Marco, badly disguised, because he could not bear for her to actually go without. Nadia told her about Thorn in his good years, before the fever and the maps: how he'd taught her to read a contour line, how he'd said *the land keeps honest books, it's only people who cook them*.

"He'd have liked you," Ulyana said.

"He'd have hated me. I cook the books professionally." Nadia paused. "Honest ones, though. Now."

"Now," Ulyana agreed.

By late afternoon the sun came through, hard and clean the way it did after monsoon, and the whole valley steamed gold, and Ulyana stood at the wall looking down at the landing, at the Kestrel, at the river running high and brown and endless toward the delta.

"I'm taking the name back," she said.

Nadia turned.

"Marchetti. The registry has the claim filed under *U. Dunmore*, because that's the name on my master's license, and it's a fine name and I earned it. But the concession — the root title — is Marchetti ground, surveyed by Marchetti hands, and my father died thinking I'd never want it." She looked at the grave. "I don't want it the way he left it. I don't want the debts or the syndicate's shadow or the desk he drowned at. But the name — the name I'll take. On my terms. Both names. Dunmore on the hull, Marchetti on the claim, and anyone who doesn't like the arithmetic can swim."

"You're going to amend the filing."

"Tomorrow morning. District office, first light, before Corvo's lawyers think of it." She glanced sideways. "It'll list two claim-holders. I told the clerk there'd be an amendment."

Nadia went very still. "Ulyana."

"You surveyed it. You climbed it. You read the fraudulent permit for what it was when I was still trying to outrun the whole question. And you're the only person alive who can say the boundary markers are honest, because you're the one who's going to keep them that way." Ulyana faced her fully. "I spent a decade learning that nothing on this river survives alone. Not boats, not claims, not people. So. Nadia Reyes, Marchetti claim-holder. Does it sit right?"

The mist had gone out of Nadia's eyes and left something bright there, and she looked — for the first time since Ulyana had fished her out of a fueling dock — like someone entirely unafraid.

"It sits right," Nadia said. "It sits so right it's going to take me a while to stop grinning, so give me a minute."

"Take two. We're not going anywhere."

And that was the strange, new, terrifying truth of it — they weren't. The Kestrel sat moored below, and the claim sat filed on the hill, and the name sat back where it belonged, and Ulyana Marchetti Dunmore stood in her father's cemetery in the last gold light of the monsoon's end and felt, unmistakably, like a woman who had dropped anchor.

Then Nadia's radio crackled.

They both froze. It had been silent for two days — the relay net down for maintenance, the storm season winding down, the whole river finally quiet. The voice that came through was the district clerk's, brisk and apologetic, and Ulyana caught only half of it before the static took the rest, but the half she caught was enough:

"—repeat, all claim-holders on the Escarpment concession, be advised — a counter-filing was lodged this morning under the old Halstead authority — the syndicate's counsel is asserting the Corvo arrest doesn't void their standing — hearing set for Thursday —"

Ulyana looked at Nadia. Nadia looked at the radio.

"Thursday," Ulyana said. "They never learn."

She started down the hill at a walk, then a stride, then a run, the letter over her heart and the river roaring gold below, and behind her Nadia was laughing and running too.
