## 22. A Boat Worth Anchoring

The dry season came back the way it always did — not all at once, but in increments you could read on the river. The water dropped a hand's width a day, then two. Sandbars surfaced like old debts being called in. The current slowed from something that roared to something that merely insisted.

Ulyana stood at the Kestrel's rail and watched the escarpment do what it had done for ten thousand years: hold still.

She'd spent a decade learning every way a river could lie. Where the channel shifted overnight. Where the chart said deep and the sounder said gravel. Where a green stretch of water hid a submerged tree with enough reach to gut a hull. She'd trusted none of it, and that distrust had kept her alive, kept her moving, kept her whole — or whatever passed for whole when your entire strategy was never standing in one place long enough to be found.

Now she was moored. Properly moored, with bow lines and stern lines and a spring line she'd tied herself, double-checked twice, and then left alone. The Kestrel sat against the pilings at Verrane Landing like a dog that had finally been allowed off the leash and chosen, of all things, to stay.

Three months since the district office. Three months since the clerk's stamp came down on the filing with a sound so small she'd almost missed it under the rain hammering the tin roof. Three months since Corvo Halstead was led away in cuffs he'd probably have picked himself if anyone gave him a minute alone with them, and Marco stood in the downpour looking at her like a man who'd handed over his whole life's savings and wasn't sure it would be accepted.

She'd given him time instead of absolution. He'd taken it the way Marchettis took most things — as a debt to be repaid on his own schedule. He wrote now, once a week, short letters full of survey data and legal updates and almost nothing personal, except always one line at the end that was trying to be something more. *The claim boundary markers are confirmed at all four corners.* *Mother's roses need pruning; I've hired someone.* *I hope the boat is well.*

*The boat is well.* As if the Kestrel were a mutual acquaintance they were both too proud to ask after directly.

Ulyana lit the stove and put water on for coffee and did not check the mooring lines again. That was the discipline now. Not running — staying. It turned out staying had its own skill set, and she was bad at it, and getting better.

* * *

The claim papers hung framed above the chart table, where a mirror used to be. Nadia had hung them there herself, with a level and four screws and an argument about sightlines that Ulyana had pretended to lose. Two names on the concession. *Marchetti-Reyes.* She'd fought the order of it for a week before giving up, because Nadia pointed out that alphabetically it made no difference and emotionally it made all the difference, and Ulyana had no counterargument for that.

Her name. On paper. In ink that wouldn't run unless the whole registry burned.

For ten years she'd been Ulyana, just Ulyana, the woman off the cargo boat who paid cash and asked no questions and answered to nothing but the river. The name Marchetti had been a thing she'd amputated — land, money, a father who'd signed her out of the family the same year he'd signed in with the syndicate. She'd told herself the name cost more than it paid. She'd believed it right up until the night she'd stood in the rain holding a stamped claim and realized the name had never been the problem. What you did with it was the problem. Who held it beside you.

Nadia came up from the engine compartment smelling of diesel and grease, wiping her hands on a rag that had started life white sometime last season.

"Injector pump's good," she said. "Third time's the charm."

"You said that the first two times."

"The first two times I was optimistic. This time I'm experienced." She dropped onto the settee across from the chart table and looked at the framed claim the way she looked at everything — like it was a problem she intended to solve or die solving. "Survey team comes Thursday. The escarpment crew. Real credentials this time, mine included, which is its own kind of strange."

"Strange how?"

"Strange like wearing shoes that fit." She flexed her bare feet against the deck grating, a habit Ulyana had catalogued somewhere between the flooded narrows and the first monsoon night and never mentioned aloud, because some things you kept. "Ten years I've been surveying with forged papers, waiting for someone to pull the thread. Now the papers are real and I keep checking my pockets for the weight of the fake ones anyway."

Ulyana knew that feeling. She carried it too — the phantom weight of the map sewn into Thorn's coat, the habit of casting off before dawn, the reflexive glance over her shoulder at every dock. You could change your life in a single reckless act of faith. Changing what your body expected took longer.

"Thursday," Ulyana said. "Then we stake the boundaries properly. Then—"

"Then we decide what we're keeping." Nadia said it gently, which was how she delivered all her hardest observations. "The syndicate's equipment is still up there. Their core samples are actually useful. But their road cuts through the watershed, and if we honor Thorn's claim the way he meant it—"

"We honor it the way he meant it." Elias Thorn had traded a dying man's last possession for a promise, and the promise had never been *get rich*. It had been *deliver it*, and delivering it meant the claim worked the way he'd drawn it: modest extraction, honest surveys, the escarpment left mostly an escarpment. "I didn't outrun an enforcer through peak flood to become my father."

"No," Nadia agreed. "You became something else. We're still figuring out what."

* * *

Thursday brought the survey team and, unannounced, Marco.

He came down the landing ramp carrying a document case and wearing the expression of a man arriving at a border crossing unsure of his visa. Ulyana watched him from the wheelhouse window and made herself keep working — coiling a line that didn't need coiling — until he reached the gangway and stopped, because there was nowhere else to stop.

"Ulyana."

"Marco." She let the silence sit. He'd earned some silences.

"I brought the final permit confirmations. And—" he opened the case, hesitated, "—something else."

He handed her an envelope, soft-edged, yellowed at the creases. Her father's handwriting on the front. Her name, spelled correctly, which somehow hurt worse than if he'd gotten it wrong.

"He wrote it the winter before he died," Marco said. "I found it going through his files for the testimony. I should have given it to you at the grave. I couldn't. I'm giving it to you now because the surveyor said you'd be here, and because I've run out of ways to be a coward about it."

Ulyana turned the envelope over. The seal had been broken years ago — Marco had read it, or tried to, and hadn't been able to finish, or hadn't been able to start. She didn't ask which.

"Stay for coffee," she heard herself say. "You can watch the surveyors argue with Nadia about datum points. It's better than theater."

Something moved through his face — relief, grief, gratitude, all the weather of a man who'd spent a year learning what his father's choices had cost. "I'd like that."

They drank coffee on the aft deck while Nadia and the lead surveyor argued cheerfully about benchmarks, and Marco told her, haltingly, about the trial preparations, and about their mother's grave, which he tended, and about the house on the hill that the family had lost to the syndicate's creditors and that the courts might return now that the permits were proven fraudulent. Ulyana listened. She did not forgive him yet. But she let him sit on her deck, on her boat, in her life's new harbor, and understood that this too was a kind of answer.

After he left, she waited until dark, and then she opened the letter at the chart table with the lamp turned low.

*My daughter. If you are reading this, then either you came home or someone finally forced my hand, and knowing you, it will be the second. I signed the papers because I thought I was saving the family. I understand now — I think I understood even then — that I was choosing the easier cowardice. You chose the harder one, which was leaving, and you were right, and I have hated you a little for it every day because you were right when I needed you to be wrong.*

*I don't ask forgiveness. I ask only this: the name was never mine to give or take. It was always yours. Spend it better than I did.*

There was more, but Ulyana read those two paragraphs four times and then folded the letter along its original creases and put it in the drawer beneath the chart table, next to the spare fuses and the flares, because that was where you kept the things that mattered in an emergency.

* * *

The mooring lines creaked at night. That was the thing nobody warned you about — that staying had sounds. Running had engine noise and water noise and the clean white noise of constant motion, and you could lose yourself in it. Staying had creaking lines and settling hulls and the particular silence of a boat at rest, which was not silent at all, and in which every feeling you'd outrun for a decade came ashore one by one and asked to be let in.

Some nights the feelings won. Some nights she lay awake cataloguing everything she'd lost: ten years, a family, a version of herself that never had to learn what her father's signature looked like on a forged permit. Some nights she got up and checked the lines, not because they needed checking, but because her hands wanted work.

But more nights now, the other thing happened. Nadia would come up from below with two mugs and no announcement, and they'd sit in the wheelhouse while the river went silver and then black, and talk about nothing — injector pumps, datum points, whether the survey cook made better bread than the bakery at the landing — and the silence between the words would be the good kind, the harbor kind, and Ulyana would think: *this is what I was running toward without knowing it.*

One evening in late dry season, Nadia stood at the rail watching the sunset burn the escarpment orange and said, without turning around:

"You haven't cast off early once. Not once since we filed. I keep waiting for it."

"So do I."

"And?"

Ulyana considered it honestly, the way she considered currents. "It turns out stopping only costs more than moving if you're stopping in the wrong place."

Nadia was quiet a moment. Then: "Is this the right place?"

Ulyana looked at the mooring lines, taut and trustworthy. At the framed claim on the wall behind her. At the woman at the rail who had walked into her life as a stowaway with her own compass and stayed as something the language didn't quite have a word for yet, though Ulyana was working on it.

"Yeah," she said. "This is the right place."

Below them, the Kestrel settled against her lines with a sound like a sigh, and held.
