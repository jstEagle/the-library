## 1. The Sandbar's Mood

The river told you things before it showed you, and Oscar Fairweather had spent fifteen years learning to listen.

He stood at the wheel of the Marigold with his boots planted wide, one hand resting on the brass rail the way another man might rest a hand on a horse's neck, and he watched the water ahead of them change its mind. That was the thing about the Serevan in the dry season—it couldn't decide what it wanted to be. Upstream of the crossing it ran deep and green and confident, sliding between clay banks with the smugness of a river that had never been argued with. But here, where the valley widened and the current spread itself thin over a graveled shelf, the water went shallow and quick and dishonest, braiding itself into channels that shifted with every flood and never quite shifted back.

The crossing was called Widow's Teeth on the charts. On the river, everyone just called it the Crossing, because there was only one that mattered, and because naming it twice felt like tempting it.

"Sandbar's moved," Oscar said.

Dario Voss, his first mate, looked up from the cargo manifest he'd been pretending to study for the last hour. "You can't know that."

"I can know that." Oscar tilted his chin at the water thirty yards ahead, where the surface lay flat as poured tin. "See how it's gone glassy there? Smooth water over shallow ground. The bar's pushed maybe forty feet east since we came through in the spring. Flood did it." He turned the wheel two spokes to port, easy, unhurried, the way a man adjusts his hat rather than the course of a loaded boat. "We'll take the left channel."

"The left channel's narrower."

"The left channel's deeper." He smiled without looking at Dario. "Narrow and deep beats wide and dry every day of the week, my friend."

Dario grunted and went back to his manifest, which was the way Dario answered most things he didn't want to argue about out loud. Oscar let him. A first mate who agreed with everything you said was worse than useless; a first mate who disagreed with everything was exhausting. Dario lived in the productive middle, and besides, Oscar needed him too much to waste energy winning arguments he'd already won in his head.

Behind him, the deck of the Marigold carried sixty tons of freight—bales of dyed cotton, crates of machine parts bound for the mill at Corven's Landing, a consignment of salted fish that smelled like a crime scene and which Oscar had accepted mostly to spite the shipping agent who'd tried to lowball him. The boat sat heavy in the water. Too heavy, honestly, for a crossing this late in the season, but the season was money, and money was wages, and wages were the reason half his crew currently hated him.

He could hear them back there now. Not the words—the wind took most of those—but the tone. The particular aggrieved music of men who hadn't been paid on time.

* * *

They came up to the wheelhouse an hour later, when the Crossing was behind them and the river had gone wide and brown and forgiving again. There were four of them: Pell and Anselm, who worked the winch and the lines; old Teodor, who'd been running decks on the Serevan since before Oscar owned a boat, or any boat had owned him; and young Rudi, who was nineteen and strong and had not yet learned that strength was the cheapest thing a man could sell on a river.

Pell did the talking, because Pell always did the talking. He was a big man gone soft around the middle, with a beard he trimmed like he was proud of it and hands like slabs of river stone.

"Captain," Pell said. "About the wages."

"What about them?" Oscar knew exactly what about them. He kept his voice light anyway, pleasant, the voice of a man with all the time in the world.

"They're late."

"They're not late. They're delayed. Late implies I've forgotten. I haven't forgotten." He leaned back against the rail and crossed his arms, and gave them the smile—the one that had gotten him out of more trouble than any amount of honest work ever had. "Gentlemen. Do you see this river?"

They looked at the river. It was hard not to; it was everywhere.

"This river," Oscar said, "is going to drop another foot before the monsoon comes, and when it does, every boat on the Serevan is going to be fighting over the same shrinking water like dogs over one bone. But us—we're going to be the ones holding the bone. Because right now, while every other captain is sitting safe in port complaining about the draft, we are moving freight. Moving freight means getting paid. Getting paid means—" he spread his hands, presenting it to them like a gift, "—wages. Better ones than Kessler's boys get. Ask anyone who's worked both decks."

"You said that last month," Rudi said.

"And was I wrong last month?"

"You were late last month."

Oscar laughed, and it was a good laugh, warm and easy, and he saw some of the tension go out of their shoulders because that was what the laugh was for. It was a tool, same as the wheel, same as the depth pole leaning against the rail. He'd learned long ago that charm was cheaper than cash and almost as effective, if you applied it thick enough and often enough.

"Here's what I'll do," he said. "We make Corven's Landing by tomorrow night, I offload the fish—which, Rudi, I promise you, is the last fish this boat carries until the ice comes—and I settle everyone up. Full arrears plus a bonus for the Crossing. Nobody else on this river pays a bonus for running Widow's Teeth."

"There wasn't anything to run," Anselm muttered. "Water was high enough."

"It's never nothing," Oscar said, and meant it more than they knew. "That's the lesson of this river. It looks calm right up until it isn't. You pay attention every single time, or the one time you don't is the time it takes your boat out from under you."

Teodor spat over the rail, unhurried, ceremonial. "Fairweather boats sink," he said. It wasn't cruelty. It was just the old man's way of saying what everyone thought and no one said: that the name on the stern was a joke the whole river was in on. *Fairweather.* And one of them, the famous one, the Halcyon, sitting at the bottom of the drowned reach these fifteen years with the family fortune lashed to her deck.

Oscar's smile stayed exactly where it was. Fifteen years of practice held it there.

"Well," he said cheerfully, "then it's lucky we're careful, isn't it?"

* * *

He sent them forward and stood alone at the wheel while the afternoon came down gold across the water, and he thought about the word *careful*, and about how little it had to do with why he never stopped moving.

Other captains had ports. Home water, a mooring they paid for by the year, a woman or a dog or a chair with their shape worn into it. Oscar had the Marigold and a rule: one night, never two. Dock, unload, load, settle what debts could be settled, and be gone with the morning current. Fifteen years of one-night stands with every town on three hundred miles of river, and people had stopped asking why. They just called him the Fairweather man, said it with a shrug, as if restlessness were something you inherited along with the name.

Maybe it was. His father had been the opposite—a man who put down roots so deep they drowned. The Halcyon had been more than a boat to Elias Fairweather; it had been a house, a business, a flag, a promise. The river concession, the permits, the contracts, the standing—all of it tied to that hull like a keel. And then one night in the flooded reach, in weather nobody could afterwards explain, she'd gone down with all of it aboard, and Oscar—who should have been on her, who'd begged off at the last minute to chase a girl in Corven's Landing—had woken up the next morning with nothing but a name that made dockhands smirk and a silence in his chest he'd been outrunning ever since.

Because that was the truth under the charm, the ballast he never talked about: he didn't move because he loved the river. He moved because stopping meant thinking, and thinking meant that night, and that night meant his father's face the last time he'd seen it—Elias Fairweather standing on the Halcyon's deck, raising one hand, casual as anything, the way a man waves when he fully expects to wave again tomorrow.

Oscar adjusted the wheel a degree. The current here set hard against the outside bank, trying to shove them into the willows, and he let it push just enough to feel it before easing off, the way you'd lean into a stubborn animal to remind it who was steering.

He read water better than people. He knew that about himself. Give him a stretch of river he'd never seen and an hour of daylight and he'd tell you where the channel lay, where the snags were buried, where the current would turn treacherous when the rain came. But sit him across a table from a man and ask him what the man was feeling, and Oscar was guessing. Ask him what *he* was feeling, and he'd offer you a smile and a story and change the subject before you noticed he'd answered nothing.

It worked. It had worked for fifteen years. Competence was armor, and he wore it well, and the river rewarded it daily, and as long as the Marigold kept moving there was nothing in the world he had to stop and face.

Ahead, the bend at Halfway Light opened up, and beyond it the smoke of Corven's Landing lay faint against the far shore, a gray smudge like a pencil mark someone had forgotten to erase. Tomorrow night, that smudge would become a dock, a tavern, a ledger full of numbers he owed and numbers owed to him. Tonight there was still water between him and it, and water was the only place he'd ever felt honest.

"Left channel's paying off," Dario said, coming up beside him with two cups of coffee, handing one over. It was terrible coffee, boiled too long, tasting of the pot more than the bean. Oscar drank it anyway.

"Told you."

"You told me." Dario drank, watching the banks slide past. Then, casually, in the tone of a man mentioning weather: "Port official flagged me at the last landing. Wanted to talk to you specifically. Something about paperwork."

"Paperwork." Oscar's stomach did something small and involuntary, the way it always did at that word. "What kind of paperwork?"

"Didn't say. Said it was about a name." Dario glanced sideways at him. "Said it was about *your* name."

The river ran on, brown and patient, carrying them toward the smudge of smoke, and Oscar Fairweather—captain, pilot, reader of currents, dodger of every stillness the world had ever offered him—found, for the first time in a long while, that he had absolutely nothing charming to say.
