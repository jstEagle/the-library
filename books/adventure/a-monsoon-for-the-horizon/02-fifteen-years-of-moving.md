## 2. Fifteen Years of Moving

The harbor master's office at Vellsmoor smelled of wet paper and old tobacco, and Oscar Fairweather had been inside it for exactly four minutes when the man behind the desk said his father's name.

"Fairweather." The official—Tobin, according to the brass plate on his desk, though the plate was tarnished enough to read as *Fobin*—ran a finger down a ledger thick as a Bible. "That name's in here. Fairweather. Concession holder, upper Serevan reach. You any relation?"

Oscar kept his face doing nothing at all. He'd had fifteen years of practice at that particular trick, and he was better at it than he was at reading water, which was saying something.

"Distant," he said. "Common name upriver."

"Mm." Tobin licked his thumb and turned a page. Outside the office window, the Marigold sat at her mooring with her hold half unloaded, and Dario was down there supervising the stevedores with the particular aggression of a man who believed everyone on the dock was stealing from him personally. Which, in fairness, they usually were. "Says here the concession permits lapse in ninety days. End of monsoon season. After that, salvage rights revert to the crown, and the whole reach gets re-surveyed for auction."

"Ninety days," Oscar said.

"That's what I said." Tobin looked up over his spectacles. He had the eyes of a man who spent his life looking at paperwork and had long ago stopped seeing anything else, but there was something underneath that, something sharper, and Oscar didn't like it. "You know, you've got his look about you. The elder Fairweather. I processed his manifests, back when. Big man, big voice. Used to bring his boy into this very office, sit him right there on that crate while the papers got stamped."

"I wouldn't know."

"No," Tobin said mildly. "I expect you wouldn't."

* * *

Fifteen years ago, give or take a season, there had been a night of rain like the sky coming apart, and a boy of nineteen standing on the mudbank at Kessel's Landing watching torchlight play across black water, waiting for a boat that wasn't coming up.

He remembered the sound first. Not the crash—that had been miles downstream, around the bend where the river narrowed and the current ran mean—but the sound after, the silence, the whole crowd of them on the bank listening to a river that had swallowed something enormous and was pretending it hadn't. His mother's hand on his shoulder, gripping hard enough to leave bruises he didn't find for two days because he couldn't feel anything below the neck.

They never found the hull. That was the thing nobody said out loud. The Halcyon went down in the drowned reach with eleven souls aboard and the river gave back three of them and kept the rest, and it kept the boat too, folded it into silt somewhere in that flooded valley like a fist closing around a coin.

And the concession—the family's concession, the permits, the manifest, the deed, the whole paper architecture of a life built on the water—went down with it. His father had been carrying everything aboard that night. Every document. Every stamp and seal. Oscar had learned later that this was against every rule of river trade, that you kept your papers ashore in a strongbox, that no captain worth his salt carried his whole livelihood on his own deck through a monsoon squall, and he had asked his mother why, why would he do that, and she had looked at him with an expression he'd never been able to read and said, *Because he trusted the river more than the banks.*

Oscar had decided, at nineteen, that he trusted neither.

* * *

"So," Tobin said, and the word came back into the present like a hook through the cheek. "Ninety days. You'll want to notify any interested parties, I suppose. Family of the original holder. Blood heirs have certain rights of recovery under the old statute, before it all goes crown-side."

"There are no heirs."

"You'd know better than me." Tobin closed the ledger with a soft, terrible finality, and then he didn't move his hands off it, and Oscar understood that the conversation had just changed shape. "It's a funny thing, though. Fifteen years that entry's sat in this book, and in all that time not one soul has come asking after it. And now, this season, I get a letter from a surveyor's office in the capital requesting copies of the concession records. And now you walk in wearing a Fairweather jaw and telling me you're nobody."

Oscar took out his purse.

He did it without hurry, without ceremony, the way he did everything that mattered. He counted out coins onto the scarred wood between them—not so many that it looked like desperation, not so few that it looked like insult—and watched Tobin look at them the way a man looks at a door he's been meaning to walk through for years.

"What name would you like to forget?" Tobin asked.

"All of them," Oscar said. "The entry. The letter request. The conversation we just had. As far as this office is concerned, I'm cargo master on the Marigold, out of nowhere in particular, and I came in to ask about mooring fees, which I'll now pay double for the inconvenience."

Tobin swept the coins into a drawer with the smoothness of long habit. "Mooring fees it is. Terrible weather coming, they say. Early monsoon this year, the pilots are all grumbling." He paused, and the sharpness surfaced again, briefly, like a pike under still water. "You know, son, money buys forgetting, but it doesn't buy much else. Ninety days is ninety days whether I write it down or not. Clocks don't take bribes."

"Clocks," Oscar said, standing, "are the only honest things left on this river."

* * *

Outside, the light had gone the color of pewter, and the wind had shifted, and every pilot on the Vellsmoor waterfront was looking at the sky with the expression of men who had been lied to by a calendar. The monsoon was supposed to be six weeks out. The clouds stacking up over the western hills said otherwise.

Dario met him at the gangplank, arms crossed, jaw set at its usual angle of permanent grievance. "Stevedores shorted us two crates of dried cod. I made them open every bale on the dock. We're going to be late leaving."

"We're not leaving tonight."

"We're not—" Dario's voice cracked upward. "Oscar. We've been in port nine hours. Nine. The crew's already asking when we sail, and when I tell them tomorrow instead of tonight, they're going to start asking other questions. Like why their captain can't stand still in one place longer than it takes to resupply. Like why we never take the contracts that pay best because they run through the upper reach. Like why—"

"Dario."

"—like why," Dario went on, because Dario had never once in eight years stopped talking when told to, "we're always moving. Always. Fifteen years you've run this boat and I've been with you for eight of them and I could count on one hand the nights we've spent twice in the same berth. There's a word for a man who won't stop running, and it isn't *captain*."

Oscar looked past him at the Marigold. She was forty feet of patched teak and stubbornness, narrow-beamed for the shallows, shallow-drafted, ugly as a mudskipper and twice as durable. He'd bought her with the last of his mother's money seven years after the Halcyon went down, and he had run her up and down this river ever since, and in all that time he had never once taken her above Kessel's Landing. Not once. The crew thought it was shoal water. Dario thought it was superstition. Both were true, and neither was the truth.

"The cod," Oscar said. "Did we get it or not?"

"We got it. Plus damages." Dario spat over the rail. "You're not hearing me, Captain."

"I hear you fine. I just don't like the song." Oscar stepped past him onto the deck, and the familiar give of the planks under his boots settled something in his chest the way it always did, the way solid ground never had. "We sail at first light. Northbound as far as Tarrow Bend, then west along the low country. Grain contract. Good money, easy water."

"Easy water," Dario repeated, flat. "Right. Because God forbid we go anywhere the current argues back."

Oscar almost smiled. Almost. It was an old dance between them, worn smooth as river stone, and usually it worked, and usually Dario's complaints were just the shape his loyalty took when it had nowhere else to go. But tonight there was something under it, something Oscar had been noticing for weeks now—a new tightness around the man's eyes, a new care with the wage book, a new way of counting the crew's shares twice. Debt did that to a man. Oscar had seen it before. He knew the signs the way he knew the signs of a shifting sandbar: the surface looks the same, but the bottom has moved.

He filed it away. He filed most things away. That was the whole architecture of his life—a series of locked compartments, each one sealed, each one holding something he'd decided not to look at yet.

* * *

That night he didn't sleep. He rarely slept in port; ports were where the past caught up with you, because ports were where you stopped.

He sat in the stern cabin with a lamp turned low and the manifest of the grain contract unrolled on his desk, and he read the same line of figures eleven times without absorbing one of them, because Tobin's voice kept running under it like groundwater. *Blood heirs have certain rights of recovery.* *Ninety days.* *Clocks don't take bribes.*

Ninety days.

He tried to make himself feel nothing about it and discovered, with a kind of cold professional interest, that he couldn't. For fifteen years he had built a life designed to require no feeling whatsoever about the Halcyon or the concession or the drowned reach or the man at the wheel of it. He had engineered his days so that there was never a quiet hour large enough for grief to land in. Moving was the trick. Motion was the whole of it. A boat underway has no time to think; a boat at anchor thinks all night.

And now some clerk in a tarnished-brass office had put a number on the thing he'd been outrunning, and the number was ninety, and numbers had a way of arriving whether you paid them or not.

Because here was the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since he was nineteen years old: if the permits lapsed, the crown would survey the reach. The crown's divers would find the hull. The crown's clerks would raise whatever the river had kept, and they would do it with lanterns and ledgers and witnesses, publicly, in daylight, and everything his father had gone down with—all of it, the papers, the seal, whatever truth was folded in the silt with the dead—would come up into the air whether Oscar Fairweather wanted it or not.

His money had bought Tobin's silence. It hadn't bought a single day.

He blew out the lamp and sat in the dark and listened to the harbor sounds: halyards ticking against masts, a drunk singing two streets over, the small endless conversation of water against hull. Somewhere north of here, upstream, past Kessel's Landing, past the bend where the current ran mean, the Halcyon lay in her grave of silt with her hands full of paper, and for fifteen years he had arranged his entire existence around the principle that she could stay there forever.

Ninety days said otherwise.

*First light,* he told himself. *Grain contract. South and west, away from all of it.* And the plan was good, the plan was always good, the plan had worked for fifteen years—

Which was when someone knocked on the Marigold's hull, three slow raps from the dark water below, and a woman's voice carried up through the night, clear and unhurried:

"Captain Fairweather. We should talk about your father's boat."

* * *

He came up on deck with the lamp relit and his hand nowhere near the knife he wanted it near, because whoever stood on his gangplank in the dark already knew his name, which meant the knife was beside the point.

She stood at the foot of the plank with the harbor lights behind her, and even before she stepped into the glow he knew who she was, because there was only one person on this river with that walk—that unhurried, proprietary stride of someone who considered every dock she stood on to be temporarily hers. Maren Kessler. Older by fifteen years, harder by considerably more, but he'd have known her anywhere, the way you know a scar.

"Hello, Oscar," she said. "You look terrible. All that moving around—it ages a man."

"Maren." He kept his voice level, bored, a man interrupted over nothing. "I don't do business after dark."

"Good thing I'm not here for business." She smiled, and it was the same smile from thirty years ago, when they were children racing coracles in the shallows at Kessel's Landing and she cheated every single time. "I'm here to offer you a job."
