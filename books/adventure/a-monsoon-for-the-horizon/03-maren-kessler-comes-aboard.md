## 3. Maren Kessler Comes Aboard

The tavern at Vell's Landing was called the Drowned Rooster, which Oscar had always taken as an honest warning rather than a name. It sat on pilings over the river's edge, tilted two degrees downstream like a drunk leaning toward his next mistake, and it smelled of tar, fried fish, and the particular despair of men who had been waiting for cargo that wasn't coming.

Oscar came in out of the heat just past noon, wanting exactly three things: a cold beer, a plate of whatever the cook hadn't ruined yet, and ninety minutes in which nobody needed him to make a decision. He got none of them.

He'd barely reached the bar when he saw her.

Maren Kessler sat alone at the corner table by the window—the table with the best view of the water, because of course she'd taken it—dressed in traveling linen too clean for the Landing and drinking nothing. A leather folio lay squared on the table before her, corners aligned to the wood grain, and beside it her hat, black, with a band the color of dried blood. She was watching the river the way other people watched enemies.

Fifteen years, and she still made his spine itch like a wool shirt.

He considered walking back out. He genuinely considered it, hand already on the door's rail, weighing the dignity of retreat against the certainty that she'd simply follow him to the ends of the Serevan, because that was what Kesslers did—they arrived where you were going before you decided to go there.

"Fairweather." She didn't turn around. "Sit down. The beer here is terrible but it's cold, and I'm buying."

"You're buying," Oscar said. "That's how I know something terrible is about to happen."

He sat down anyway. That was the thing about Maren Kessler: she never had to force you anywhere. She just arranged the world so that sitting down was the least stupid option available, and let you do the rest yourself.

* * *

They'd grown up on the same stretch of river, forty miles apart and a whole world between. The Fairweathers ran freight and passengers out of Corren's Bend; the Kesslers ran everything else—towing, dredging, insurance, the trading posts, half the warehouses along the middle reach. His father used to say the Fairweather concession was honest work and the Kessler fortune was honest work's tax collector. Aldous Kessler had said worse about Fairweathers, though never to their faces. There had been a lawsuit once, over a collision at the Split Rock narrows, and after the lawsuit there had been silence, and after the silence there had been the night the Halcyon went down, and after that night Oscar had stopped counting the years by anything except distance traveled.

Maren was five years younger than him and had been outmaneuvering him since she was twelve, when she'd bought up all the ice at the Corren's Bend regatta and sold it back to the vendors at triple price during the finals. He'd hated her for it. He'd also, privately, admired her, and he'd spent fifteen years making sure neither of those facts ever showed on his face.

She looked good. That annoyed him. Silver coming in at her temples now, worn like punctuation; hands quiet on the table; eyes the flat gray-green of deep water over rock. She had her father's stillness and, thank God, not yet his smile.

"You're a long way from Kessler House," Oscar said when the beer arrived. It was terrible. It was cold. He drank it anyway.

"I'm exactly where I need to be." She slid the folio across the table without opening it. "And so are you, as it happens. Which is why I'm here."

"I don't read contracts before lunch."

"It's not a contract yet. It's an offer. Contracts come after you stop being stupid." She nodded at the folio. "Open it."

He didn't touch it. "Whatever it is, the answer's no. Let's save ourselves the afternoon."

"The answer's no," Maren repeated, tasting it. "You know, most people would at least look first. Curiosity. Self-preservation. Something."

"I've known you twenty-five years, Maren. Looking first is how you get people. You put something shiny on the table and wait for them to reach, and then you own the hand that reached." He leaned back, chair creaking. "I don't reach anymore."

Something moved behind her eyes—not hurt, he was almost sure, more like recalculation. She picked up her glass, found it empty, and set it down again with a small precise click.

"Your father's concession permits expire in eighty-nine days," she said.

The words landed somewhere under his ribs, cold and specific. He kept his face still. He'd paid a port official in Corren's Bend a month ago—a fat envelope, a forgettable conversation—to make sure the name Fairweather stayed off every ledger and notice board between the delta and the highlands. Apparently the money had bought less forgetting than advertised. Or she'd never needed the ledgers at all. She'd have known the dates by heart. Her family had helped write the law.

"That's crown business," he said. "Not mine."

"It becomes yours the day the permits lapse. Salvage rights revert to the crown, the concession reverts to open claim, and every hull, manifest, and deed registered to Fairweather shipping becomes public property." She turned the folio ninety degrees, aligning it with the table edge again. A tell. She did it when she was about to spend money. "Including the Halcyon."

The tavern noise seemed to drop away—the clink of glasses, the argument at the dartboard, the cook shouting at someone—all of it receding behind a thin ringing in his ears. He took another drink to cover it. The beer tasted like nothing now.

"The Halcyon is at the bottom of the drowned reach," he said. "Under sixty feet of floodwater. Nobody's salvaging anything from down there except silt and old rope."

"Not at current levels. But the government survey last spring put the wreck at eleven fathoms in the dry season channel, and the new dredging schedule means the reach gets charted within two years regardless. Wrecks get raised, Fairweather. Slowly or quickly, honestly or otherwise, that hull comes up. The only question is who's standing on the deck when it does." She finally looked at him straight on. "I want it to be both of us."

* * *

He laughed. He couldn't help it—one short, ugly bark of a laugh that turned a few heads at the bar.

"Both of us. You and me. Partners." He shook his head. "You must think I've been drinking since dawn."

"I think you've been running since you were nineteen, and running men are easy to find and hard to keep. I'm offering you the one job in the world you can't run from, because it's the one job that runs toward you." She opened the folio at last and turned it to face him. Clean typescript, crown watermark, her signature already dry at the bottom. "Recover the Halcyon's hull and manifest before the permits expire. Full salvage crew, my equipment, my divers, your knowledge of the reach—you're the only living pilot who's run that channel above flood stage, and we both know it. We split the recovered value fifty-fifty. The concession rights stay with the crown's process, untouched. I'm not asking you to fight the law, Oscar. I'm asking you to beat it to the wreck."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I salvage it myself, and the value of the Halcyon goes entirely to Kessler Shipping, and you go back to hauling palm oil and complaining about wages." She said it without malice, which somehow made it worse. "I'd rather not. Fifty-fifty is generous and you know it's generous. Your boat leaks, your crew is underpaid, and your engine room sounds like a dying animal. I've heard the Marigold come into port. So has everyone within a mile of the water."

"She's got character."

"She's got rot. Character is what we call rot when we're fond of it." Maren folded her hands. "Say yes, Oscar. Not for me. For the boat you're so fond of, and the crew you pretend isn't yours, and the father you've spent fifteen years pretending doesn't live at the bottom of that valley."

The last sentence hit harder than she could have known—or maybe she knew exactly, which was the whole problem with Maren Kessler. He set the beer down carefully, because his hand wanted to do something else with it.

"My father," Oscar said, "is dead. The Halcyon is dead. Whatever's left of either of them can stay where it is until the river decides otherwise. I haul cargo, Maren. That's the whole of my ambition, and it's served me fine."

"Fifteen years of fine." She smiled then, thin and sharp, and it was Aldous Kessler's smile after all, and something in Oscar's chest closed like a fist. "One more thing you should know before you finish saying no."

"Don't."

"Hireling rates on the Marigold went up this morning," she said. "Double wage, hazard bonus, paid on signing. I signed six of your crew at the trading post yesterday evening. Seven, if you count your engineer, and you should, because he brought the engine specifications."

The tavern came back all at once—the noise, the heat, the smell of fish—and Oscar felt the floor tilt very slightly, the way it did when a sandbar shifted under a hull you'd trusted.

* * *

He made himself finish the beer. It took a while. Maren waited with the patience of a woman watching a lock fill.

Six of seven. He ran the names through his head like a man checking a wound for splinters. Brannic at the wheel when he wasn't. The Torres brothers on deck. Old Samm in the rigging, who'd sworn on his mother's grave he'd never work for a Kessler. And Dario. Dario, who'd been with him nine years, who knew the Marigold better than Oscar did in the dark, who owed money—Oscar had heard the whispers at the last three ports, seen the way Dario flinched at letters from downstream—and who had gone ashore at the trading post yesterday evening and come back late, cheerful, with a new knife and no explanation.

Seven, if you count your engineer. She'd counted. Of course she'd counted.

"You didn't hire them to take the job," Oscar said slowly. "You couldn't. They can't sign for salvage work without the captain's consent—it's in their articles. You hired them so they'd *want* to."

"I hired them so they'd have a reason to look at you when you refuse," Maren said, "and ask themselves why they're eating rice and beans on a leaking tub while double wage sits on a table in front of them. Loyalty is a wonderful thing, Oscar. It's also the first casualty of arithmetic." She stood, unhurried, and settled her hat on her head. "The offer stands for ten days. After that I contract a crew out of the delta and you can watch from the bank."

"And if I tear that paper up?"

"Then I'll print another. I print well." She paused at the edge of the table, and for a moment—just a moment—the rival fell away and he saw the girl who'd sold ice at the regatta, sharp-eyed and hungry, looking at him across a gap that had never once gotten narrower. "For what it's worth, I hope you say yes. Not because I need you. Because you're the best pilot on this river and it's a waste, watching you run from the one place you actually belong."

She left coins on the table, exact change plus a tip generous enough to be insulting, and walked out into the white heat of the Landing without looking back.

Oscar sat alone with the folio, the terrible beer, and the sound of the river going about its business below the pilings—patient, indifferent, rising.

He didn't touch the contract. He stared at it for a long time instead, at the crown watermark and the dry ink of her signature, and thought about eighty-nine days, and eleven fathoms, and a hull wedged in silt with his whole life folded inside it like a letter nobody had opened.

Then he heard boots on the gangway, quick and familiar, and Dario came through the door with his hat in his hands and a smile that didn't reach anywhere near his eyes.

"Captain," Dario said. "Got a minute? There's something the crew wants to talk to you about."
