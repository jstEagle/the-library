## 4. A Crew That Sings Two Songs

Dario Voss counted money the way other men counted sheep—not to fall asleep, but to stay awake to what he owed.

Two hundred and forty crowns. That was the number that lived behind his eyes now, the number Salim the factor had said with a smile that showed all his teeth and meant none of them. Two hundred forty, plus interest that compounded like floodwater, plus the understanding—unspoken but load-bearing—that a man who couldn't pay didn't get to keep his fingers. Dario had done the arithmetic on the deck of the *Marigold* a dozen times that week, scratching it into the condensation on his cup, wiping it away before anyone could see. It never came out smaller.

So when Maren Kessler's offer came down the gangplank like weather, Dario was already halfway to yes.

* * *

The crew gathered in the fo'c'sle after supper, which was how all the real business of the *Marigold* got done. Oscar held court at the tavern or the wheel; the fo'c'sle belonged to the men. Lamplight swung with the river's motion, throwing shadows across six faces and one empty bunk where Fenner used to sleep before his knee went bad in Serevan Port.

Petey Okafor spoke first, because Petey always spoke first. "Half pay up front," he said, turning his tin cup in his hands. "That's what the woman's clerk told me. Half up front, half on delivery of the hull."

"Delivery of the hull." Rostam snorted from his hammock, where he lay with his boots on like a man who'd stopped caring about etiquette somewhere around his third wreck. "You heard what she wants. The *Halcyon*. Fairweather's father's boat. Off the bottom of the drowned reach, before the permits die."

"She's not asking us to raise her whole," Petey said. "Just strip her. Hull planking, fittings, whatever's salvageable. The metal alone—"

"The metal alone is worth more than we make in two seasons," Rostam finished for him. "Which is exactly why I don't trust it. Nobody pays that kind of money for scrap."

"It's not scrap if it's got a name on it," said old Tam, who had fished the Serevan longer than any of them had been alive and treated every conversation as an excuse to say so. "Kessler money. Kessler boats. My father hauled freight for a Kessler when I was a boy. They pay fair and they collect hard, and they remember every face that ever crossed them. You want that memory pointed at you?"

"I want my wages pointed at me," Petey said. "We're three months behind. Three months, Tam. Oscar keeps promising the next run will cover it, and the next run covers fuel and food and half a wage, and here we are again."

Nobody argued, because nobody could. Dario sat by the lamp and let them talk, saying nothing, doing the arithmetic again. Half of Kessler's advance would clear Salim. Not all of it—the interest ate like rot—but enough to buy time, and time was the only currency Dario had ever been good at stretching.

"You're quiet, Dario," Rostam said. "You always have an opinion. Usually too many."

He weighed it. A first mate who pushed too hard looked bought; a first mate who stayed silent looked like he didn't care whether the crew ate. He chose the middle channel, as he'd learned to choose every channel since the debt took root in him.

"I'm thinking about the season," he said. "Low water's coming early this year. Ask Tam."

Tam nodded slowly, pleased to be consulted. "Early. River'll drop out from under us by mid-season. Shallow runs, no cargo, no cargo, no wages."

"And monsoon after that," Dario went on. "If we take a contract, it has to be one that pays through the dead water. Kessler's does. Oscar's pride doesn't." He let that sit. "I love the captain. You all know I do. Fifteen years I've stood beside him. But love doesn't recaulk a hull, and ours is leaking now—we all heard it in the hold last night. We need the work. She's offering the work. That's the whole of it."

"Except it isn't," Rostam said, "because the work is pulling up a grave."

The lamplight swung. For a moment nobody spoke, and Dario understood that Rostam had put his finger on the thing everyone else had been circling: the *Halcyon* wasn't a wreck to these men. It was a story. The boat that sank with old Fairweather aboard, the night the sky came down and the river swallowed its own. Every one of them had signed onto the *Marigold* knowing they served under the son of that story, and some part of their loyalty had always been built on not asking him to look at it directly.

"Graves don't pay interest," Dario said quietly.

It was the truest thing he'd said in months, and it landed harder than he intended. Petey looked at him sharply. Tam closed his eyes.

* * *

They brought it to Oscar at dawn, which was cowardice dressed as procedure, because dawn was when the captain was most human—coffee in hand, squinting at the current, not yet fully armored in charm.

Dario did the talking, because that was the job. He laid it out the way he'd rehearsed: the crew's back wages, the leaking hold, the low water coming, the monsoon after. He kept Kessler's name small in the telling and the numbers large, and he watched Oscar's face do what it always did—go pleasant and distant, like a man reading water while pretending to listen.

"You've all talked this through," Oscar said.

"We have."

"And you want me to sign us onto a Kessler contract."

"I want us fed through the season," Dario said. "The contract happens to come with her name on it."

Oscar drank his coffee. Downriver a barge blew its horn, long and low, and the sound seemed to go through him like a current. When he turned, he was smiling, and Dario had known him long enough to know the smile was the danger sign, not the safety one.

"Fifteen years ago," Oscar said, "Maren Kessler stood on a dock in Serevan Port and told me my father's boat sank because Fairweathers cut corners. In front of thirty people. She was seventeen and she believed it, and I've spent fifteen years making sure I never had to stand still long enough for her to say it twice." He set the cup down. "Now she wants to hire my boat to dig up my father. And my first mate thinks I should say yes."

"My first mate thinks the crew should eat," Dario said. "You can hate me for the difference."

Something moved behind Oscar's eyes—hurt, maybe, or the recognition of hurt—and then it was gone, smoothed over like water closing above a stone. He looked past Dario to the others gathered at the rail, six men trying very hard to look like they weren't listening.

"Conditions," Oscar said.

Dario felt the crew exhale around him.

"One," Oscar said, raising a finger. "We salvage the hull and manifest. Nothing else comes off that wreck without my say. Two—" the second finger, "—Kessler's people board nothing. Her clerks can read documents on our deck, in daylight, with me standing there. Three." He paused, and his voice changed, dropped into something older and flatter. "No one touches the captain's cabin. Not her divers, not her clerks, not one of you. That's mine. If anything of my father's comes off that boat, I carry it myself, or it stays down."

"That's not standard salvage terms," Dario said. "She'll balk."

"Then she can balk from her own deck." Oscar picked up his coffee again, and just like that he was the charming captain once more, light on his feet, the conversation already over in his mind. "Draw up the articles, Dario. Your handwriting's prettier than mine. And somebody find out why the port bilge pump sounds like a dying cat—I want it fixed before we take on contract work."

He walked aft toward the wheel, whistling something tuneless, and the crew dispersed with the loose energy of men who'd gotten what they wanted and didn't want to examine the price.

Dario stayed at the rail a moment, watching the river slide by, brown and patient and full of everything it had taken. He should have felt relieved. The contract meant wages; wages meant Salim off his back, at least until the next rotation of hell. Instead he felt the crack he'd heard in his own voice that morning in the fo'c'sle—*graves don't pay interest*—and wondered when exactly he'd become a man who said things like that out loud.

There was a knock against his boot. Petey, crouched, tying off a line that didn't need tying.

"That condition," Petey said, low. "About the captain's cabin. You see it too, right? There's something down there he doesn't want found. Something worth more than planking."

"Or something worth more than money," Dario said. "To him."

"To him," Petey agreed. "Question is what it's worth to her."

Dario didn't answer. He watched the water, and the water gave nothing back, and somewhere downstream a Kessler clerk was already drafting articles with fine print fine enough to hang a man by.

* * *

That evening, at the midriver trading post called Halfway House, while the *Marigold* took on fuel and the crew drank their advance rumors into shape, a man in a good coat found Dario at the end of the pier.

He'd seen him before—Kessler's agent, the quiet one, the one who watched instead of talked. Now the man talked.

"Ms. Kessler respects the captain's conditions," he said. "All of them. She's a reasonable woman." He let a silence open, the way experienced men do, and let Dario step into it. "Of course, conditions only matter if the salvage happens in time. Permits expire at monsoon's end. If the work ran long—if the river ran high, if equipment failed, if a leak proved stubborn—well. Then the permits lapse, the concession reverts to the crown, and Ms. Kessler's company holds prior claim on everything in that reach. Everyone gets paid their contracted wages regardless. No one's fault. Just weather."

Dario's mouth was dry. "You're asking me to slow us down."

"I'm asking you to be realistic about the river," the agent said, and pressed an envelope into Dario's hand. It was heavy. It was so heavy. "For your discretion. There'll be another when the permits lapse."

Inside the envelope, Dario knew without counting, was most of two hundred and forty crowns.

He thought of Oscar at dawn, whistling, setting conditions like a man who still believed conditions could protect anything. He thought of the fo'c'sle lamp swinging, six faces, one empty bunk. He thought of Salim's smile.

The river went on sliding past, brown and patient, taking everything.

Dario closed his hand around the envelope and did not give it back.
