## 5. The Stowaway with Ink-Stained Fingers

Three days in a cargo hold teaches you things about yourself. Ilsa Reyes learned that she could sleep sitting up with her spine against a crate of dried fish, that hunger stopped being a feeling and became a kind of weather, and that the Marigold's second mate had a habit of counting crates aloud when he thought no one was listening—which was a problem, because she had made one of them disappear.

Not disappear, exactly. She had become it.

The trick was simpler than it sounded. The hold's manifest listed forty-one crates of salted fish bound for the trading post at Kiln Bend. She had watched the loading from the shadows of the fish market in Puerto Sombra, counted along with the stevedores, and then—when the last crate went down the ramp and every man's attention turned to the securing chains—she had stepped into the gap where a forty-second crate would have been, folded herself between the hull plating and the stacked cargo, and let the dark close over her like water.

She'd done harder things. She'd crossed the Cordillera Negra on foot with a theodolite strapped to her back. She'd spent six weeks alone on a glacier taking elevation readings while her fingers turned the color of candle wax. But those hardships had come with purpose visible at both ends—a summit reached, a chart completed. This was different. This was three days of waiting to be found, and not knowing what would happen when she was.

Her commission papers said she was authorized. Her commission papers were also signed by someone who had lied to her about what the authorization was for, so she kept them folded small against her ribs and told herself the two facts canceled out.

Above her, through the deck planking, she heard the river. That was the thing about stowing away on a working boat: you learned its body by ear. The diesel throb under everything, steady as a pulse. The slap of water against the hull, changing pitch as the current changed. The creak of cargo shifting in its lashings. And voices—the crew's voices, worn smooth by repetition, arguing about wages, about a woman named Consuelo in Puerto Sombra, about whether the captain was a genius or a lunatic or both.

The captain. Oscar Fairweather.

She had seen him exactly once before this voyage, three years ago, across a survey office in the capital, and he had been arguing with a clerk about tide tables with such ferocious precision that she'd remembered his voice before his face. Then she'd seen the name on the file she was pulling: FAIRWEATHER, O.—licensed pilot, upper Serevan reach—and felt something turn over in her chest, because everyone who worked the river knew that name, and everyone who worked the river knew to say it quietly.

Now she listened to that voice through four inches of pine decking as it gave orders in the flat, unhurried tone of a man who had never once needed to raise it, and she tried to reconcile it with the boy in the old photographs—the one standing beside his father on the deck of the Halcyon, squinting into a sun that hadn't yet set on anything.

* * *

They found her on the fourth morning, off Kiln Bend, in the gray hour before the light got honest.

Ilsa heard the hatch open above and boots on the ladder, and she made her decision the way she made all her decisions: quickly, and then without looking back. When the lantern swung into the hold, she was already standing, hands visible, ink-stained fingers spread wide.

The second mate—Rojas, she'd learned his name through the planking; he talked in his sleep too—went for the knife at his belt and shouted up the ladder, and within a minute the whole boat seemed to pour down into the hold: three crewmen, the first mate Dario with his jaw set like a man arriving at a fight he'd been promised, and finally, last, the captain himself, ducking under the low beams with a lantern held high.

Oscar Fairweather looked at her the way she imagined he looked at water: reading it, measuring it, finding the sandbar underneath.

"Well," Ilsa said, because silence was a currency she couldn't afford just now. "Before anyone throws me overboard, I should mention I can swim, so that threat carries less weight than you might hope."

Nobody laughed. Dario stepped forward. "We're putting ashore at Kiln Bend. You'll walk to the constable from there."

"She'll do nothing of the sort," Fairweather said.

It wasn't kindness in his voice. It was arithmetic. She watched him do it: a stowaway put ashore was a story told in taverns, and stories about the Marigold carried names in them, and names were the one cargo he refused to haul. She'd bet her remaining coin he was thinking exactly that.

"Who are you," he said. Not a question. A demand wearing a question's clothes.

"Ilsa Reyes." She reached slowly—keeping every movement legible—and drew the folded papers from inside her jacket. "Cartographer. Licensed, third class, hydrographic specialty."

Dario took the papers first, scanned them, and handed them up to the captain with an expression that suggested he'd hoped for something easier, like a smuggler.

Fairweather read. The lantern light moved on the page. Ilsa used the time to look at him properly for the first time: taller than the photographs suggested, leaner, with the particular stillness of men who work moving water. His eyes were the color of the river in dry season—brown going green where the light caught them—and they did not stay anywhere long. They touched her face, her hands, the papers, the crates around her, cataloguing, and moved on.

"You're commissioned," he said. "By whom?"

"The Bureau of Hydrography."

"That's the seal of the bureau." He tapped the paper. "This isn't your commission. This is a letter of introduction. Where's the actual contract?"

She had prepared for this. She had prepared for it badly, because the truth was a shape she couldn't show him whole. "A private party," she said. "Engaged through the bureau as intermediary. It's standard practice for—"

"Who."

The word came out flat as a sounding line hitting bottom. Around them the crew had gone very quiet, the way crews go quiet when they smell money or trouble, and Ilsa suspected that on this boat the two smells were related.

"A salvage interest," she said.

"Kessler."

He said the name the way other men spat. And there it was—the turn in the current she'd been dreading since Puerto Sombra. Because she had known, hadn't she? Somewhere beneath the professional language of her contract, beneath the phrase *drowned reach, sector nine, prior survey unavailable*, she had known whose drowned reach it was, and whose survey was unavailable, and why. She had told herself the knowledge didn't matter. A chart was a chart. Water didn't care who paid for the map.

But the man holding her papers cared, and the water was his history, and suddenly none of her clean professional distances would hold.

"Yes," she said. "Maren Kessler."

* * *

They brought her up to the deck, because Fairweather wouldn't conduct business in his own hold—"Nothing happens below decks that I don't order below decks," he told Rojas, and Rojas nodded like this was scripture—and there, in the early light with Kiln Bend's smoke smudging the eastern sky, the negotiation happened.

It was not much of a negotiation. It was more like watching a man take apart a lock with his thumbs.

"You'll be put ashore," Dario said again. "There's a constable at Kiln Bend. Stowing away is—"

"Is a fine and a lecture, and we both know it." Fairweather hadn't raised his voice once. "What I want to know is what Kessler thinks my boat is doing in sector nine."

Ilsa weighed her options and found them all thin. "Charting. The commission is for a full hydrographic survey of the drowned reach. Soundings, current measurements, hazard marking. The permit registry shows the concession lapses in eighty-seven days, and after that the crown will want the channel documented before it opens salvage to general bid."

"And she hired a cartographer instead of divers."

"Divers cost more than cartographers."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile; more like the memory of one, filed away somewhere deep. "And the survey you're carrying. The old one. Sector nine was surveyed before. Twenty years ago. I've seen the reference in the bureau index."

Here it was. Here was the reef under the surface, and she could either run aground on it honestly or lie and sink slower.

She took out the map.

She had carried it against her back, rolled in oilcloth, the whole journey, and unrolling it on the deck of the Marigold felt like undressing in public. It was a beautiful thing—she allowed herself that much pride even now—hand-inked on linen-backed paper, the drowned reach rendered in the old style, with soundings written in a fine copperplate hand and depth contours shaded in washes of blue-gray like weather coming in. And in the lower right corner, stamped in faded wax-red:

**FAIRWEATHER RIVER CONCESSION — SURVEY OF RECORD — J. FAIRWEATHER, MASTER**

The deck went silent. Even the engine seemed to drop its voice.

Fairweather looked at the map for a long time. Longer than he'd looked at anything else since she'd come aboard. His father's name sat there in the corner, twenty years old, and Ilsa watched a man try to have no expression and fail by degrees.

"Where did you get this?" he said finally, and his voice had changed—it was quieter, and quieter was worse.

"Bureau archive. It's the survey of record for the reach. My commission required me to verify it against current conditions." She hesitated. "It's marked *restricted*. I was given a copy authorization. I've since come to doubt the authorization was... regular."

"You mean Kessler stole it."

"I mean I don't know how she got it, and I've been telling myself that's not my problem." She met his eyes and held them, which took more effort than the glacier had. "I'm telling you now that it is my problem. That's why I'm showing you. I could have waited until we reached the reach. I chose not to."

"Why?"

"Because you'd find out anyway. Men like you always find out. And because—" She stopped. The true answer was *because I've spent three days listening to you through the floorboards and I already trust you more than the person paying me*, and that answer was dangerous, so she built a smaller one. "Because a chart is only worth what's true on it. I don't put my name on work built on stolen ground."

Dario shifted his weight. "Captain, whatever she is, she's Kessler's. Anything she's told us could be—"

"I know what she is, Dario." Fairweather rolled the map closed with careful hands, the way you handle something that might still be alive. He looked at Ilsa again, and this time the look stayed. "You understand I can't let you off at Kiln Bend now. You've seen the map. You know our heading. If Kessler sent you, sending you back with that knowledge is worse than keeping you."

"So I'm a prisoner."

"You're ballast." He handed the map back to her, and their fingers almost touched on the oilcloth, and she noticed he made sure they didn't. "You eat when the crew eats, you work when the crew works, and you stay out of the wheelhouse. If we reach the drowned reach and your charts are straight, I'll pay your passage onward myself. If they're not—"

"If they're not, you'll know I lied about the only thing I have that's worth anything."

He studied her a moment longer. "Ink-stained fingers," he said, almost to himself. "You've been drawing since before dawn. In the dark. In my hold."

"Soundings from memory. The reach, from the old survey. I wanted it fresh in my hand before I saw the real water." She shrugged. "Habit."

"Habit," he repeated, and there was that almost-smile again, briefer this time, gone before it landed. "Get her a hammock, Rojas. And somebody give her something to eat before she draws us."

* * *

That evening, anchored off Kiln Bend waiting for the customs skiff, Ilsa sat on the foredeck with her supper—a bowl of rice and fish that tasted like forgiveness—and drew.

She couldn't help it. The river here bent wide and slow around a bluff crowned with kapok trees, and the evening light came down the channel in long gold bars, and the far bank showed a shoal line she could read from fifty yards: the water going from brown to olive to a pale glassy green where the bottom shelved up. Any cartographer alive would want that transition on paper. She worked fast, pencil first, ink after, and she was so absorbed that she didn't notice Fairweather until his shadow fell across her page.

She froze. He crouched, unhurried, and looked at the drawing for a long moment.

"You've got the shoal wrong," he said.

"It's right. The color change starts at the third marker post."

"The color change lies. There's a scour hole under the glassy patch—spring flood cut it in '09 and it never filled. Looks shallow, runs eleven feet." He tapped the page with one finger, not quite touching the ink. "Your old survey has it wrong too. My father charted it before the flood."

Ilsa looked at the drawing, then at the water, then at him. "You can see that? From here?"

"I can see it the way you can see that your ink's going to feather if you don't blot it. Fifteen years on this river." He stood, and for a moment he looked out at the channel with an expression she couldn't file under any of her categories—not nostalgia exactly, and not grief, but some sediment of both. "Fix the shoal. Eleven feet, center of the glassy patch, trending shallower to the north bank."

He walked aft toward the wheelhouse. At the door he paused, half-turned, and she had the distinct sense that he was watching her the way he'd watched her all day—too closely, a man checking a knot he didn't tie himself.

"Miss Reyes. Tomorrow we pass the mouth of the Verdugo. After that, everything on your map is personal. Make sure your work is as good as your hiding."

Then he was gone, and Ilsa sat with her cold rice and her corrected shoal and the strange new fact of the evening: that being seen through, thoroughly and without cruelty, felt less like exposure than like arrival.

Below her feet, the Marigold's engine idled in the dark, patient as a held breath, and eighty-six days ticked somewhere downstream, running toward them faster than the current.
