## 6. Upstream into the Clouds

The river changed its mind a mile past the trading post, and Oscar Fairweather felt it through the soles of his boots before he saw it anywhere.

That was the thing about the Serevan nobody who hadn't spent fifteen years on her could understand. She didn't warn you with signs. She warned you with weight—the way the current leaned against the hull, the way the wheel went heavy in your hands like something sulking. The Marigold had been climbing all morning through water gone the color of strong tea, and now, rounding the bluff where the old mission used to stand before the flood took it, Oscar felt the river gather itself beneath him and decide to be difficult.

"Shoal off the starboard bank," he called down to Dario at the bow line. "Swing wide. Wide as you like—there's nothing to hit on the left but water."

Dario shaded his eyes and squinted upstream. "I don't see anything."

"You're not supposed to see it. You're supposed to feel sorry for whatever boat finds it first." Oscar eased the throttle back a notch, watching the wake fold in on itself behind them. "The Serevan drops her silt right there when she slows. There's a bar under that flat water growing every day, and one morning it'll be an island, and some government man will put it on a chart, and by then it'll have moved again."

He heard Dario mutter something to the deckhands—something with the word *wages* in it—and let it go. The grumbling had been constant since Kessler's contract came aboard, two songs from one crew, and Oscar had decided his only play was to out-sing both of them. Work them hard, pay them on time, and never once let them see him check the sky.

Except the sky was checking itself.

He'd noticed it at dawn: clouds stacking up over the western ranges a month ahead of any monsoon worth its name, tall and blue-gray and organized, like an army arriving early for a war. The old men at the landing stages said the season was turning strange. The river said it too, running higher than it should for this time of year, colder, carrying mountain snowmelt down out of country that should still be holding it.

Oscar didn't believe in omens. He believed in draft marks and barometric glass and the specific sound a hull makes when it's tired. But he looked at those clouds and felt something older than belief stir in his chest, some animal recognition that said *go now, go fast, get there before the water does.*

"Give me everything she's got," he told the engine room, and the Marigold dug in.

* * *

She found the stowaway an hour later, or rather the stowaway found usefulness, which amounted to the same thing.

Ilsa had been aboard three days now, officially tolerated and unofficially watched. She'd claimed a corner of the cargo deck near the wheelhouse, spread her charts out on an upturned crate, and made herself quiet in the way of people who are used to being ignored. Oscar had half expected her to be useless—a mapmaker, a drawer of lines on paper, someone whose rivers were ink and whose floods were hypothetical.

Then, coming up the long reach below the mission bluffs, she stood at the rail, tilted her head the way she did when she was reading something, and said, "You're going to want to come left. Sooner than you think."

Oscar kept his eyes on the channel. "Is that so."

"The current's folding. Can't you see it? Off the point there—the surface is moving one direction and everything under it is moving another." She pointed with a pencil, not her finger. Pencil, he'd noticed. Always the pencil, stained dark at the grip where her fingers held it. "There's a scour hole below that bend. The deep channel runs along the far bank until the bend, then cuts back hard. If you hug the near side like you're doing, you'll drop your stern into the hole and the current will swing your nose into the bank before you can correct."

He almost told her she was wrong. The words were actually in his mouth—he could feel their shape, fifteen years of certainty behind them. Instead he looked. Really looked, the way he looked at water when he was alone and honest about it.

The surface off the point did fold. A faint seam, a wrinkle in the current where two minds disagreed. He'd seen it a thousand times and read it a thousand times, but he'd never once thought of it as *folding*, and the fact that a stowaway cartographer had named it faster than he had—

He swung left. Early. The Marigold swept around the bend wide and clean, and the scour hole yawned away off the starboard quarter, hungry and empty.

Dario glanced back at the wheelhouse with an expression Oscar chose not to interpret.

"Well," Ilsa said. She didn't smile, exactly. It was more the absence of a frown, which on her face was practically a celebration. "You do listen."

"Don't make a habit of being right where I can hear it."

"Where would you prefer I be right?"

"Someplace downstream."

But he was fighting a grin, and she knew it, and the worst part was that she let him know she knew it, and just like that the whole arithmetic of the voyage shifted. Three days ago she'd been a problem to be managed. Now she was standing at his shoulder reading his river, and he was letting her, and neither of them had agreed to any of it.

* * *

They made the confluence by midafternoon, where the Serevan met her gray tributary and the water went from tea to slate. The clouds had come down off the ranges while they steamed, filling the valley ahead of them like smoke poured slow. The light went bronze and strange. Birds moved low and purposeful, all heading the same direction, none of them asking permission.

Ilsa unrolled a chart across the binnacle and weighted the corners with brass fittings she'd borrowed without asking. "The drowned reach starts above the second gorge," she said. "Old survey has the valley floor at forty feet below the normal highwater line. When they dammed nothing and drowned everything—" she caught his look and adjusted, "—when the flood took it, the maps stopped being maps and started being guesses. That's why they hired me. Nobody's charted it since."

"They?"

Her pencil paused. Just a fraction. "The commission came through a survey office. You know how these things chain together."

He did know how things chained together, and he also knew what a pause that size cost, and he filed both facts away in the same drawer where he kept Dario's new boots and the second engineer's sudden good mood. The Marigold was carrying more secrets than cargo this trip. Fine. Secrets floated, mostly. It was hulls that sank.

"What's actually down there?" he asked instead. "Besides my father's boat."

"Silt, mostly. Snags. The tops of trees that have been standing dead in the water for fifteen years." She traced the reach with her pencil, and he watched her draw the drowned valley from memory of documents, the contours climbing the page like ribs. "And the Halcyon, if the old reports are right, wedged somewhere against the church foundation. Deep enough that the salvage crews gave up. Shallow enough that a determined crew with good divers and a fair wind might—"

"Might."

"Might." She looked up at him. Her eyes were the color of the slate water, and just as unwilling to show you the bottom. "You don't talk about him. Your father. I've noticed."

"There's not much to say. He had a boat. The boat sank. I got a different boat."

"That's not 'not much.' That's nothing, dressed up." She rolled the chart closed before he could answer, which was probably mercy, though it didn't feel like it. "You'll talk when the water's over the wheelhouse. Everyone does."

"I've been over the wheelhouse twice. Didn't say a word either time."

"No," she said, gathering her charts. "You steered. That's your version of talking. I've been watching."

She walked aft, and Oscar stood at the wheel feeling like a man who'd been read like a chart himself—soundings taken, hazards marked, channels noted—and couldn't decide whether he'd been insulted or seen. Both, probably. With her it was going to be both, always.

* * *

The first leak announced itself at dusk, quietly, the way bad news prefers.

The second engineer came up wiping his hands on a rag that had given up its original color years ago. "Bilge's taking water, Captain. Slow. Pump handles it easy, but it wasn't taking any yesterday."

"How slow?"

"An inch in four hours, maybe less. Might be a seam weeping with the river running cold and high. Strain on the planking."

Might be. Oscar went down and looked himself. Knelt in the bilge with a lamp, the way his father had taught him before teaching became a word for the past, and ran his thumb along the seams. Most of them wept a little—that was a boat, not a bucket—but one joint forward of the engine bed was damp in a way that bothered him. Not weeping. Wicking. And when he pressed his thumbnail to the caulking, a thread of oakum came away too clean, too whole, like it hadn't failed so much as been persuaded.

He sat in the dark of the hold for a long moment, lamp guttering, listening to the river press against the planks a hand's width from his face.

Cold water, high strain, tired seams—all true. All plausible. And the cut was clean, and clean was a word that belonged to knives, not rivers.

He climbed back into the evening and said nothing. Told the engineer to pump on a schedule and log the inches. Watched Dario laughing with the deckhands over supper, watched Ilsa working by lamplight over her charts, watched everyone, catalogued everyone, and hated how natural the watching was starting to feel.

Above the gorge the clouds had finished assembling. They lay across the whole upper valley now, black-bottomed and patient, and the first wind came down off them smelling of iron and distance. The Marigold steamed into the teeth of it with her lamps lit early and an inch of river in her belly, and Oscar Fairweather stood his watch and told himself the tightness in his chest was just weather coming.

Behind them, far downstream, lightning stitched the horizon to the hills. Ahead, the drowned reach waited with everything it had kept.

At midnight, the rain arrived.
