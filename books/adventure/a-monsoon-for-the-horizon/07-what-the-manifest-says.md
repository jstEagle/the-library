## 7. What the Manifest Says

The manifest fragment lived in Ilsa's chart case, and the chart case slept under her pillow, and she had not told Oscar Fairweather any of this.

She told herself it was prudence. A cartographer protected her instruments the way a surgeon protected his blades—there was nothing sinister in it. The fragment was paper, and paper on a river boat was a perishable thing: rain, damp, spilled coffee, Dario's greasy hands. It needed guarding. That was all.

It had been three days since they'd pulled the waterlogged tin box off the trading post dock, three days since the postmaster's clerk had handed it over with the bored ceremony of a man surrendering something he'd forgotten he held. *Salvage records, Halcyon, registered loss fifteen years past. Sign here.* Oscar had signed with a flourish that fooled no one, and then he'd carried the tin to the bow of the Marigold and stood there in the wind for a long time before bringing it below without opening it.

He hadn't opened it yet. She knew because she'd watched him not open it—watched him set it on the shelf above his bunk, square it twice, and leave it there like a man leaving a dog he didn't trust.

Ilsa opened it on the fourth night, when the Marigold lay moored at a fueling station and the crew had gone ashore to spend wages they grumbled about earning.

* * *

The tin smelled of rust and old river. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, lay what remained of the Halcyon's cargo manifest—a single sheet, torn across the middle, the lower third missing entirely. Water had gotten to it years ago; the ink had bloomed into gray flowers along every line. But manifests were written by clerks, and clerks wrote large, and enough survived.

Ilsa spread it flat on her bunk under the lamp and read it the way she read everything: top to bottom, left to right, twice.

Bales of cotton. Crates of machine parts, consigned to an upriver mill that had itself been dead a decade. Salted fish, quantities absurd. And then, in the special declarations column—the column most captains left blank or filled with lies—the clerk's hand turned careful, formal, almost reverent:

*One (1) instrument of conveyance, sealed, being the original grant and deed of concession, Fairweather Reach, executed under crown authority, to be delivered to the Office of Concessions upon demand.*

Ilsa read it four times.

The original deed. Not a copy, not a registration duplicate—the *original*, the paper from which every other claim on the reach descended, riding in the captain's cabin of the Halcyon the night she went down. Which meant it had gone down with her. Which meant it lay now at the bottom of the drowned reach, sealed in whatever the river had left of the hull—or dissolved into silt, or eaten by fifteen years of water, or—

Or recoverable. Salvageable. Worth more than the hull, worth more than the manifest, worth more than every bale of rotted cotton listed above it. Because a concession permit was one thing—an annual permission, renewable, revocable—but a deed was blood and bone. A deed was ownership. And if the permit law honored a recovered original, then whoever raised that paper from the wreck would own the Fairweather Reach outright.

Her commission said otherwise. Her commission, signed in Kessler's name by an agent whose smile she'd trusted too easily, instructed her to chart the drowned reach, mark depths and hazards, note the wreck's position and condition—and to report, through the agent, anything "pertaining to salvage value." Anything pertaining to salvage value.

A deed was salvage value beyond reckoning.

Ilsa sat very still on her bunk and listened to the Marigold breathe around her—the tick of cooling metal, the lap of water against the hull, somewhere above the soft argument of two crewmen sharing a cigarette. She thought about how Oscar had squared the tin on the shelf twice and walked away. She thought about the way he read water, the way he'd let her stay aboard after finding her stowed among the crates, watching her too closely ever since but letting her stay anyway, and how he still didn't know why she was really here, not fully, not the whole shape of it.

She thought about the fact that she had known, when she took Kessler's money, exactly what kind of woman Maren Kessler was. Everyone on the river knew. What she had told herself then—that it was just survey work, just charts, that knowledge was neutral—felt thinner every day, like caulking gone past its season.

"You could tell him," she said aloud, to the empty cabin, and the words sounded small and obvious and true, and she did not move.

Because there was the other clause. Buried in the schedule of deliverables, in language so dry you could sail right past it: all charts, notes, and findings produced under commission became the property of the commissioning party upon delivery. If she told Oscar, she wasn't just sharing information. She was handing Kessler the location of a deed, dressed up as honesty. Every word she gave him, he might carry straight into the trap. Unless she broke the commission entirely—burned it, repudiated it, ate the penalty and the lawsuit and the blacklisting that would follow, the end of any career on this river or any other.

She was twenty-six years old and her whole profession fit inside one leather case, and the case was under her pillow, and she did not move.

Instead she did the thing cartographers do when they cannot act: she recorded. She took out her field book and copied the declaration line in her own neat hand, letter by letter, preserving the clerk's spelling, the bloom of the ink, even the tear line where the page ended. Then she drew a small map of the Halcyon's last known position from memory—the coordinates she'd already begun plotting from old surveys and Oscar's reluctant, grudging answers—and marked the captain's cabin with a single careful X.

Then she looked at what she'd made and understood that she had just done the worst thing of all. Not lying. Not stealing. Just—keeping. Becoming a person who kept things, who filed truths away in waterproof cases, who drew maps of secrets. Her father had been a customs clerk in a port town full of smugglers, and he used to say the most dangerous people on any waterfront were never the thieves; they were the ones paid to know things and say nothing. She had sworn, at nineteen, never to become one of them.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the river moved in the dark, patient as arithmetic.

* * *

Morning found her on deck early, ink-stained fingers wrapped around coffee she didn't want, watching Oscar come up from below.

He looked like he always looked at the start of a day—like a man assembling himself from parts, charm first, then competence, then the wall. But today he paused at the companionway and glanced back down toward his cabin, toward the shelf, toward the tin, and Ilsa saw something cross his face that she recognized from her own mirror: the look of a person deciding, again, not to open the door.

"Fair weather," he said, nodding at the sky, which was lying about itself—high pale blue with a bruise of cloud stacked far upriver where the drowned reach waited.

"For now," she said.

"You sleep all right? You've got the look of a woman who's been arguing with someone."

"Myself," she said. "I lost."

He smiled, and it reached his eyes more than usual, and that was somehow worse. "Breakfast is whatever Dario didn't burn. Come argue with me about currents instead. We've got the narrows after noon and I want your eyes on them."

Your eyes. As if she were part of the ship's company now. As if he'd decided it without consulting either of them.

They stood at the wheel through the morning, and it was the closest thing to happiness she'd felt in months—his hands light on the spokes, reading the water aloud like scripture, her calling depths from the lead line, their voices falling into a rhythm neither of them acknowledged. Once, passing a slack stretch between bars, he said, "You know what my father used to say about charts?" and stopped himself, jaw tightening, and steered around the sentence like a snag. She let him. She was getting good at letting him.

That was the trouble. That was exactly the trouble. Every hour aboard made the secret heavier and the telling harder, and harder, geometrically, because now it wasn't just a fact she was withholding—it was a fact entangled with a man's hands on a wheel and a joke half-finished and the specific way he'd started saying her name like it belonged on a crew list.

At noon they ran the narrows, and she forgot everything for forty minutes except water depth and rock shadow and the sound of his voice going low and certain, and afterward the crew cheered the Marigold herself, and Oscar laughed—a real laugh, unarmored, gone in a second—and Ilsa stood at the rail and felt her duty and her heart pull in opposite directions like a mooring line in a current.

* * *

That night she climbed to the foredeck with her field book, ostensibly to check star positions, actually to be alone, and found Dario there instead, smoking, looking downstream.

"The boss asleep?" he asked.

"Probably."

"He doesn't sleep much." Dario flicked ash over the rail. "You noticed that. Fifteen years I've sailed with him and I've seen him sleep maybe four nights out of ten. Man treats rest like a debt." He studied her sideways. "You're writing a lot in that book of yours."

"I'm a cartographer."

"Mm." He drew on the cigarette. "Kessler pays well, I hear. For knowing things."

The night went cold all at once. "I don't know what you mean."

"No," Dario agreed pleasantly. "Nobody ever does." He dropped the butt, ground it under his heel, and paused at the hatch. "For what it's worth—I hope whatever's in that book keeps everybody safe. That's all any of us can ask of paper."

Then he was gone, and Ilsa stood alone under the indifferent stars with her field book against her chest like a shield, understanding that she had just been measured by a man who was himself measuring something for someone else, and that the river was full of people keeping secrets, and that hers was the only one that mattered, because hers was the only one that could make someone rich or ruinous overnight.

Below her, through the deck planks, she could picture the tin on its shelf, squared twice, unopened. The deed sleeping in the drowned reach. The truth sitting in her hands like ballast she couldn't jettison and couldn't lift.

Tomorrow, she told herself. Tomorrow I'll decide.

But she was a cartographer, and she knew better than anyone that *tomorrow* was just a place you marked on a chart—easy to draw, hard to reach—and far upriver, where the clouds had been stacking themselves for days, the first thunder of the monsoon rolled down the valley like something clearing its throat.
