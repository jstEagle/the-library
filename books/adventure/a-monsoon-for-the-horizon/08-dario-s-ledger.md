## 8. Dario's Ledger

Dario Voss kept two ledgers. One was the Marigold's official book, kept in fair hand, every wage and ration entered in columns so straight you could sight a rifle down them. The other was a small red notebook he kept zipped inside his coat, against his ribs, where no one would ever think to look.

The first ledger said he was owed four months' back pay.

The second said he owed eleven hundred crowns to a man named Ferro Lask, who collected debts the way the river collected driftwood—slowly, patiently, and always in the end.

He was thinking about both ledgers when the Marigold tied off at Halfway Post, the midriver trading station that was less a station than a rumor of one: a stilted warehouse, a fuel pump that worked on Tuesdays, a tavern built from three shipwrecks and a lie. The rain had let up that morning, and the whole crew came ashore smelling of bilge and wanting beer. Oscar gave them two hours and a warning—*back before the light goes*—and stayed aboard himself, which was his way. The man could read water better than his own crew, and he knew it, and somehow that made it worse.

Dario bought his crew their beer because that was what a first mate did, and because buying goodwill was cheaper than earning it. Then he said he had stores to check and walked out into the grey afternoon alone.

The agent was already there.

Dario had spotted him two days earlier, at the last fuel stop—a soft-bellied man in a good coat that didn't belong anywhere on the Serevan, drinking tea like it was a chore he'd been assigned. He'd introduced himself then as Pell, an inspector of hull registrations, and Dario had smiled and lied and walked away with his heart going like a pump in a flooding hold. Nobody inspected hull registrations on the upper Serevan. Nobody wore a coat like that within a hundred miles of it.

Now Pell was sitting at the corner table of the tavern's back room, the one with the warped floor and the window that looked out on the fuel dock, and there were two cups of tea on the table and a leather folio lying flat beside them, and Dario understood that he had already decided to sit down before his feet had agreed to it.

"You're early," Pell said.

"You're not an inspector."

"No." Pell didn't apologize. He poured the second cup and slid it across. "Sit down, Mr. Voss. I'll only take ten minutes, and you can hate me the rest of your life. Plenty of men have."

Dario sat. The tea was real tea, not the sawdust they sold upriver, and that alone made him angry. He wrapped his hands around the cup anyway. His fingers had a tremor in them he hadn't earned honestly; it came from Ferro Lask's last visit, and the crowbar, and the two fingers on his left hand that still ached in cold weather.

"Eleven hundred crowns," Pell said.

Dario's head came up so fast his neck cracked.

"Eleven hundred crowns owed to Ferro Lask, of the Lower Basin, plus interest compounding since the spring. Two fingers broken in March as a gesture of good faith. A deadline of the first of the dry season for the balance." Pell recited it the way Dario entered wages in the fair book—flat, precise, no drama. "I'm not with Lask. I want you to understand that. I'm not here to break anything. I'm here because I did my research, and you, Mr. Voss, are the most reasonable man on the Marigold."

"That's not the compliment you think it is."

"It's the only one that matters." Pell opened the folio. Inside were papers, and Dario made himself look at none of them. "You've been first mate on that boat six years. You've kept her running on charm and baling wire while your captain spends the crew's wages on fuel to keep moving, because God forbid Oscar Fairweather should stop anywhere long enough to be found. Am I wrong?"

"You don't know him."

"I know the file." Pell turned a page. "I know the concession permits expire in eighty-one days. I know that when they lapse, salvage rights to the Halcyon revert to the crown, and the wreck sits in the drowned reach until the silt takes it for good. I know that Maren Kessler has spent a fortune positioning herself to be the one who recovers it. And I know that the only thing standing between her and that fortune is a leaky riverboat and a captain who's been running from his own name for fifteen years."

Dario drank the tea. It was excellent and he hated it. "You've got the wrong man. I push a broom and argue with the cook. I don't make decisions on that boat."

"Of course you don't." Pell smiled, and it was a kind smile, which was the worst thing about it. "You just keep the ledger. And a man who keeps the ledger knows exactly how many days of coal are in the bunker, how many days of rice in the hold, and how long a crew will work for back pay before they start doing arithmetic of their own. You know things, Mr. Voss. I'm not asking you to decide anything. I'm asking you to know things at me."

* * *

The rain started again while they talked, a fine grey sift against the window, and the light in the back room went from afternoon to evening without anyone noticing.

Pell laid it out the way a man lays out cards he's already counted. Kessler didn't want the Marigold sunk. She didn't want anyone hurt—*and here Dario laughed, one short bark, and Pell waited it out with the patience of a man being paid by the hour*. She wanted the salvage delayed. Delayed past the expiry of the permits, when the crown's claim would swallow the wreck whole and Kessler's lawyers, who had been chewing on that particular clause for two years, would find a way to make the crown's claim become her claim. All of which required only one thing: that the Marigold arrive at the drowned reach too late.

"You want me to lose us time," Dario said. "That's it. That's the whole job."

"I want you to lose a day here. A day there. A bearing that's a degree off. A fuel line that clogs. A crew that grumbles a little louder than usual." Pell shrugged. "Sabotage is for amateurs and men with nothing to lose. You have a great deal to lose. That's why I'm talking to you and not the cook."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you say no." Pell closed the folio, and for a moment Dario let himself believe it would be that simple, that he could stand up and walk back to the boat and be the man he told himself he was at two in the morning. "And you go back to a boat that's four months behind on wages, with a hold that leaks and a captain who won't stop long enough to fix her properly, and in sixty days Ferro Lask comes looking for eleven hundred crowns, and I promise you, Mr. Voss, Lask's arithmetic is not as patient as mine."

Dario looked at his hands around the cup. The two bad fingers wouldn't curl all the way.

"How much," he said, and heard his own voice the way you hear a stranger's, flat and small, from across the room.

* * *

The number was five hundred crowns, half now, half on expiry, and it was not enough. That was the obscene part. It was not nearly enough for what it was. Eleven hundred owed, five hundred paid, and the difference between them was the width of a man's spine, and Dario could feel exactly how wide his was, lying there on the warped floorboards of the back room of a tavern built out of shipwrecks.

Pell counted the notes onto the table in small neat stacks, and Dario watched his own hand reach out and take them, and it was like watching it happen to somebody else—some poorer, hungrier version of himself who had been waiting in his skin all along for the excuse.

"You'll want to count it," Pell said.

"I'll count it."

"And Mr. Voss—" Pell paused, folding the folio away, and for the first time he looked almost human, almost sorry. "The captain. Fairweather. He trusts you. You can see it, the way he hands you the boat when he goes ashore, like he's handing you the only thing he owns. I'd think about that, if I were you. Not to stop you. Just so you know what the thing costs. Men who don't know what a thing costs always pay twice."

"Get out of here," Dario said, "before I do something we both regret."

Pell left the way he'd come, through the rain, unhurried, a soft man in a good coat, and Dario sat alone in the back room with five hundred crowns in his coat and the taste of good tea going sour in his mouth, and he did not count the money. He sat there until the light went, and then he counted it, twice, because his hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

* * *

He walked back to the dock in the dark with the rain coming down harder, and the Marigold sat at her mooring with one lantern lit in the wheelhouse, and Dario stood on the fuel dock for a long time just looking at her.

Six years. He'd come aboard six years ago with nothing but a first mate's ticket and a talent for machinery, and Oscar had taken one look at his references and said, *references are stories people tell about you; can you keep her pumps running?* And Dario had kept her pumps running through two monsoons and a grounding and the winter the cook knifed a passenger over cards. He'd stood four-hour watches in floodwater. He'd gone into the bilge with a lamp in his teeth to find a crack by feel because the lamp kept going out. Oscar Fairweather trusted him with the boat, and the boat was the only thing the man had, and Dario had just sold pieces of her schedule to the woman who wanted her captain's inheritance.

He took out the red notebook, right there in the rain, and opened it to the last page. The columns were there as always—*Lask, 1100; interest, 84; flour, 12; boots, 6*—and beneath them, in the fair hand he used for the official book, he wrote: *Received, 500. On account.*

On account. As if it were wages. As if it were owed to him.

He thought about throwing it in the river. The notebook, the money, all of it—there were men who would have, and he could name them, and every one of them was asleep below decks of the Marigold at that moment, trusting him to keep the watch. He thought about going to Oscar's cabin and putting the whole thing on the chart table: *here's what I am, here's what I did, do what you like.* He even got as far as the gangplank. He stood on it with the rain hammering the planks and the lantern light wobbling in the wheelhouse window, and he saw, as clearly as if it were already written in the fair book, what happened next: Oscar's face going still, the way it did, the way it went when the port official had said the name *Fairweather* out loud at the last big station. The quiet. The boat getting underway an hour later with one man fewer, and Dario standing on some dock watching the Marigold go, holding five hundred crowns and a first mate's ticket that no one upriver would ever honor again.

He went below instead. He dried off. He zipped the red notebook back against his ribs, where it lay like a second heart, a worse one.

* * *

The crew came back aboard by ones and twos, wet and loud, and Dario made the rounds the way he always did—checking lines, checking the bilge, saying the small things that kept a boat afloat that never appeared in any ledger. The cook asked if there'd been news at the post. Dario said no. The boy asked if they'd make the drowned reach before the monsoon. Dario said ask the captain.

Oscar was in the wheelhouse when Dario came up to report. He stood at the window with his back half-turned, looking downriver, toward the way they'd come, and for a second Dario had the wild, crawling feeling that the man knew—that he could smell the money through the coat, the way he could smell a change in the current before the water showed it.

"Stores?" Oscar said.

"Got most of it. Rice, oil, a drum of pitch." Dario made his voice do what it always did, easy and level, the voice of a man with nothing against his ribs. "Fuel pump's working. Tuesday, like the sign says."

Oscar nodded. He didn't turn around. Out on the black water the rain was easing, and far downriver, so far it might have been imagination, there was a smudge of light where the post's lamps couldn't reach—something moving, something with a wake.

"We go up at first light," Oscar said. "The drowned reach. I want to be on the wreck before the river finishes rising."

Dario's hand, resting on the doorframe, went cold. "First light," he said. "Aye."

Oscar turned then, and looked at him, and smiled the tired smile he wore like the wheelhouse wore its paint—thinned, but holding. "You all right, Dario? You look like a man doing arithmetic."

"Just working out the coal," Dario said.

And that was true, God help him, that was the truest thing he'd said all day: he was working out the coal, and the rice, and the days, and how many of each it would take to make the Marigold arrive at the drowned reach just a little too late—and the red notebook against his ribs was heavier than any cargo he'd ever loaded, and first light was coming, and he had not yet decided what he would do when it came.
