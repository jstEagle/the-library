## 9. The Leaking Hold

The squall came down the valley like something with a grudge.

Oscar felt it before he saw it—a pressure change in his ears, a flattening of the water ahead, the birds going quiet in the drowned trees that lined the upper reach. Fifteen years on the Serevan had taught him to read weather the way other men read faces, and this face was ugly.

"Dario! Double-lash the deck cargo!"

Dario moved without argument, which was itself a small miracle these days. Since Kessler's contract had been signed—since Oscar had signed it, God help him—the Marigold had run on a current of tension thicker than anything the river offered. The crew worked. The crew ate. The crew sang their two songs, one for the captain and one when they thought he wasn't listening. But nobody laughed anymore, and Dario hadn't met his eyes straight-on in a week.

The first rain hit like flung gravel. Then the sky opened all the way and the world went white with water.

* * *

They ran the squall at anchor, snubbed to a stand of ironwood trees on the eastern bank, because running it was suicide and everyone aboard knew it. The Marigold bucked at her lines like a horse that wanted to bolt anyway. Oscar stood at the wheel out of habit more than necessity, one hand on the brass, watching the river climb.

It was Ilsa who heard it first.

She'd been below with her charts, pinned down by the weather, and she came up the companionway with her hair plastered to her face and an expression he'd learn to dread.

"There's water in the hold," she shouted over the rain. "A lot of it."

"How much is a lot?"

"Enough that my crate of ink is floating."

Oscar's stomach dropped through the deck plates. He handed the wheel to Tam, the oldest man aboard, and went down the ladder three rungs at a time.

The hold told the story before his lantern did. Water sloshed across the bilge boards, black and fast-moving, already ankle-deep and rising. His cargo—cotton bales, salt pork, the tools they'd need for the salvage—sat in a spreading tide. And from forward, near the bow where the hull curved up toward the stem, he could hear it: the sound of the river coming home.

He waded forward, found the pump, and threw himself onto the handle.

"DARIO!" His voice cracked against the storm. "ALL HANDS! PUMPS!"

* * *

They pumped through the night.

There were two pumps aboard the Marigold, both of them older than most of the crew, and they took turns on the handles in pairs, two hours on, two hours off, while the rain hammered the deck above like a fist demanding entry. The rhythm of it got into everything—the groan of the handles, the gurgle of the suction hoses, the splash and slap of water finding its level. By midnight the bilge was winning. By two in the morning, barely. By four, they'd stopped losing ground, which on a leaking boat in a monsoon squall counts as victory.

Oscar didn't leave the forward pump. He sent men to rest and stayed himself, arms burning, shirt soaked through with sweat under the rain that blew down the hatch. Tam tried to relieve him twice and got nothing but a shake of the head.

At some gray hour before dawn, Ilsa appeared beside him with a tin cup of coffee gone cold and put it in his hand without asking.

"You'll fall over," she said.

"I'll fall over after we're dry."

"You're not dry. You're just less wet than the boat." She took the pump handle opposite him without being asked, matching his stroke, and for a while there was only the work. Her hands were blistered. She didn't complain about them, and he noticed that, filed it away. A stowaway cartographer who pumped without complaint was either very brave or very guilty, and he wasn't yet sure which.

"The leak," she said, between strokes. "Did you find it?"

"Forward, port side. Where the garboard strake meets the stem post." He wiped his face with his shoulder. "I'll know more when there's light."

"What does that mean?"

"It means I want to look at it properly."

She studied him across the pump. "You think it's not what it looks like."

"I think," Oscar said, "that I've sprung leaks before. Dozens. Rivers do that. They find your weak points and they lean on them." He set the coffee down on a crate. "This isn't that."

* * *

Dawn came the color of dishwater, the squall blown out to nothing, the river lying flat and brown and innocent as a churchgoer. Oscar waited until the watch changed, until the deck was quiet, until only Tam was awake at the wheel—and then he went over the side.

The water was warm as bathwater and opaque as tea. He dove with a knife between his teeth and a hammer tucked in his belt, feeling his way down the hull by touch, counting planks the way a man counts stairs in the dark. At the bow, port side, low near the turn of the bilge, his fingers found the wound.

Caulking had been driven out of a seam three feet long. Oakum hung from it in pale shreds like something disemboweled.

He hung there in the murk, breathing around the knife, and made himself look closer. Because here was the thing about leaks: rivers tore caulking out ragged. Working seams, frost-heaved planks, a grounding on a sandbar—any of those left a rough edge, fibers pulled and splintered, damage that looked like damage.

This looked like surgery.

He ran his thumb along the seam. The remaining caulking sat proud and even on either side of the gap, undisturbed, exactly as it should be—except for the three feet where it had been driven *out*, cleanly, with a tool. A caulking iron, or the back of a heavy blade. One pass, maybe two. Ten seconds of work for someone with access to the forward hold and a reason to be there.

Ten seconds of work that nearly sank them.

Oscar surfaced slowly, treading water beside the hull, and let the river hold him while he thought. Above him the Marigold rolled gently at her lines, patched with a canvas apron and a bucket of pitch he'd slapped on from inside, holding—for now. Holding the way a man holds a door shut when he knows the lock's been broken.

Someone had done this. Someone aboard.

* * *

He didn't say anything. That was the discipline of it, the part that cost him. Every instinct he had screamed to line them up on deck—Dario, Tam, young Pell, the Voss brothers, all seven of them plus the woman who'd appeared out of his own cargo like a bad omen—and ask the question point-blank. Watch the faces. Guilt has a smell; fifteen years of hiring and firing and reading crews had taught him that much about people, even if he'd never learned much else.

But a captain who accuses without proof gets a crew that closes ranks against *him*. And if he named the wrong man, the right man would smile behind his back and drive the next seam out somewhere worse. Somewhere midriver. Somewhere at night.

So Oscar said nothing. He climbed aboard, wrung out his shirt, and called for breakfast like a man without a care in the world.

And started watching.

* * *

Watching, it turned out, was its own kind of exhausting.

He watched Dario count the pay envelope twice at breakfast, quick and furtive, and wondered—not for the first time—where a first mate drawing fair wages found money for the debts the trading-post brokers kept mentioning in that casual way brokers have. He watched Tam spit over the leeward rail and mutter at the clouds, ordinary as bread. He watched the Voss brothers arm-wrestle on the cargo hatch and laugh, and decided the laughter was real, or real enough.

He watched Ilsa sit cross-legged on the foredeck with her charts weighted down with pebbles, re-inking the pages that had gotten wet, and caught himself watching her too long, too often, and hated how easy it was. She'd been useful on the run upstream—read the currents better than his pilot books, spotted the false channel above Widow's Bend that would have put them on a mudbank. Useful wasn't trustworthy. Useful was exactly what a clever saboteur would make herself.

That thought arrived unbidden and sat down in his chest like a stone. He didn't like it. He liked even less that he couldn't make himself pick it up and throw it out.

At noon he made his rounds of the hull again, diving with a mallet and fresh oakum, driving new caulking into the seam with his own hands so no one else's touched it. The work steadied him. Wood and fiber and pitch didn't lie. If only people were built the same way.

"Bad plank?" Tam asked when he came back over the rail.

"Frost-heaved strake. Old damage." The lie came smooth, and Tam nodded and went back to the wheel, and Oscar felt the lie settle into him like ballast. He was keeping things from his crew now. Keeping things was how this whole rot started—he knew that better than anyone, knew it from the inside of his own history—but knowing changed nothing.

Paranoia, he was discovering, wasn't loud. It was quiet. It was the way you started eating last, so you could see who reached for the knife block. It was noticing that Dario's boots were dry at dawn when everyone else's were soaked. It was lying awake in the night listening to the hull creak and trying to match each sound to a sleeping body, wondering which breath belonged to the man with the caulking iron.

* * *

On the third evening after the leak, the river gave them a gift: a long pool of slack water below the ruins of an old customs station, good holding, good wood nearby, and no neighbors for twenty miles. They anchored early. Oscar ordered double watches anyway, and saw Dario's jaw tighten at it, and pretended not to see.

After supper he climbed to the roof of the pilothouse with the excuse of checking the weather, which was clear and starry and required no checking whatsoever. From up there he could see the whole deck laid out below him like a hand of cards.

Ilsa came up the ladder ten minutes later. She didn't ask permission; she never did. She settled against the pilothouse wall an arm's length away and looked at the stars like she was reading them.

"You haven't slept," she said.

"I sleep."

"You sleep like a cat in a dog yard. Everyone's noticed."

"Everyone should worry less about my sleeping and more about their pumping."

"That's the thing." She turned to look at him, and the starlight caught the ink stain on her fingers, the one that never seemed to wash fully away. "Pumps don't fail on their own, Oscar. I grew up around boats. My father ran a survey launch on Lake Veren. When a seam lets go clean, like that—" she tapped the deck beneath them, right over the patched strake— "somebody helped it."

His blood went cold and then hot. "You looked at it."

"I looked at the oakum you pulled. It was hanging in the scrap bin. Fibers cut, not torn." She held his gaze, unflinching. "I'm telling you because I thought maybe you'd figured it out and were doing something about it. Are you?"

"And if I have? What would you do about it, Ilsa? You've been aboard three weeks. You showed up in my hold with a map that has my family's seal on it, commissioned by nobody you'll name. Why shouldn't I start with you?"

Something flickered across her face—hurt, quickly shut away, but not quickly enough. He watched her decide not to answer the question, and watched her decide to answer a different one instead.

"Because whoever did this wants us sunk," she said quietly. "Not delayed. Sunk, up here, where the reach drowns everything and the crocodiles handle the paperwork. And I don't know why anyone would want that unless they don't want us reaching the Halcyon at all." She paused. "Which means whatever's down there matters more than any of us knows. Including you."

Below them, a lantern moved along the deck. Dario, making his rounds—or saying he was making his rounds. Oscar tracked the light until it disappeared into the fo'c'sle, and the silence stretched between him and Ilsa like a third person neither of them would acknowledge.

"Keep your eyes open," he said finally. "And Ilsa—if you're holding something back, whatever your commission is, whoever paid for it—now's the time. Because the next seam they cut might be one we can't patch."

She looked at him for a long moment, and he could see the war behind her eyes, duty pulling one way and something newer pulling the other.

"Ask me again tomorrow," she said, and went down the ladder.

Oscar stayed on the pilothouse roof until the moon set, watching the dark water slide past the anchor chain, listening to the hull breathe. Somewhere below decks, seven men and one woman slept, and one of them had a caulking iron hidden somewhere, waiting for the next squall.

He just had to figure out which one before the river did its worst.

And then, near midnight, he saw it: a faint light downstream, past the point, where no light should be. Two flashes. A pause. Two more.

Signal lamps. Somebody on the bank, talking to somebody downstream.

Oscar's hand closed slow around the rail until his knuckles went white, and the paranoia in his chest stopped whispering and started naming names.
