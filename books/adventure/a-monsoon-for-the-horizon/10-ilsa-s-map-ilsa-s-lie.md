## 10. Ilsa's Map, Ilsa's Lie

He hadn't meant to find it. That was the thing he would tell himself later, turning it over like a coin he couldn't stop flipping: he hadn't been looking. The river had simply run him there.

They had anchored in the lee of a clay bluff two days out from the drowned reach, waiting out a squall that came down the valley sideways and turned the Marigold's rigging into a chorus of complaints. The rain had stopped near dusk, and Oscar had come below to check the bilge readings—habit now, obsession maybe, the leak in the forward hold living in his head like a splinter he couldn't dig out. The lamp was lit in the aft cabin where the charts were kept. It shouldn't have been. He'd blown it out himself at noon.

He stopped in the doorway.

Ilsa sat at the chart table with her back to him, bent over the wood like a woman praying, her hair pinned up with what looked like a pencil, her sleeves rolled past her elbows. She had spread her own charts across the table—the drowned reach, the Serevan above the falls, the whole broken spine of the river they'd been climbing for a week—and she was drawing. Not surveying. Not correcting. Drawing, in a fine black hand, the same mark over and over again in the corner of each fresh sheet, and Oscar knew that mark the way he knew his own heartbeat.

A river coiled through a crescent moon, with three stars set above it.

The Fairweather seal.

His father had stamped it on every manifest, every bill of lading, every chart the Halcyon ever carried. Oscar had seen it burned into crates, inked onto ledgers, branded into the doorframe of the family office in Serevan before the office stopped being theirs. He had spent fifteen years making sure no one put that mark on anything new. And here was a woman he had let aboard his boat, a stowaway he had pardoned with nothing more than a warning, pressing it into wet ink like it belonged to her.

The bilge readings forgotten. The leak forgotten. The whole careful architecture of his calm gone quiet.

"Ilsa."

She didn't startle, which was its own kind of answer. She finished her stroke—steady, unhurried—set down the pen, and turned in the chair. Her fingers were black to the second knuckle.

"Captain." Her voice was even. "You're dripping on the deck."

"Whose commission are you working?"

The question came out flat, and he was glad of it. Flat was better than the other thing underneath it.

She looked at him for a long moment. He watched her decide something—watched the calculation move behind her eyes and then, strangely, watched her set it down, like cargo too heavy to keep hauling.

"Sit down, Oscar."

"Don't tell me to sit down on my own boat."

"Then stand." She turned back to the table and began, methodically, stacking the charts she'd been working on. "But you'll want to sit."

* * *

She told it plainly, which he hated, because he had built the whole scene in his head with her caught and cornered and clever, and instead she gave him the truth like a receipt.

The commission had come to her in Serevan, eight months ago. A firm name she'd never heard before—a broker's name, she understood now, a name with a woman behind it. The work was straightforward: chart the drowned reach above the falls. The government survey was twenty years old and useless; the flood had redrawn the channels; someone wanted current soundings before the salvage window closed. Good money. Better than she'd made in three years of port charts and harbor plans.

"I asked who the client was," she said. "They told me it was a crown survey. I asked twice more and they stopped answering my letters, but by then I'd taken the advance, and an advance is a leash, Oscar. You know how it works. You've held the other end of enough of them."

"I've never held a leash on a liar."

"No," she agreed. "You've held one on a crew that sings two songs, and you pretend not to hear the second verse. That's not so different."

He crossed the cabin in three steps and put his hand flat on the charts she'd stacked, pinning them to the table. "Show me what you've been drawing. All of it. Every sheet."

She showed him.

It was worse than the seal. The seal he could almost forgive—a habit, a signature, cartographers copied marks the way sailors copied knots. But underneath the survey work, on the backs of the fresh charts, in her small precise hand, were notes. Soundings of the reach, yes, but also: the old Fairweather mooring, its pilings still standing. The likely drift path of a sunken hull, calculated from the flood year's current. And in one corner, boxed and underlined, a set of figures that could only be one thing.

"The Halcyon's last known position," she said quietly, before he could ask. "From the wreck reports. I reconstructed it."

"You reconstructed it for her."

"I reconstructed it for a crown survey I believed in." She held his eyes. "I didn't know what it was for. I want you to believe that, and I know you have no reason to. But I didn't know. I found out who Maren Kessler was the night I stowed away on your boat—found out because I heard her agent talking in the tavern, and I understood that everything I'd drawn for eight months was going to be used to take something from you."

"So you came aboard to finish the job anyway."

"I came aboard because the only way to un-draw a map is to know where it leads." Her voice didn't rise. "And because—" she stopped. Started again. "Because I'd read everything about the Fairweather concession. The permits, the reach, the wreck. It's the most interesting stretch of river on this whole waterway and nobody has charted it properly in twenty years. I'm a cartographer, Oscar. You found me hiding in your hold with ink on my hands. What did you think I was running from? A bad review?"

He wanted to hit something. The table, the bulkhead, the lamp. Instead he stood there with his hand still pinning her charts, feeling the rain start up again on the deck above, soft at first, then steady, the monsoon clearing its throat.

"You let me sail you toward the reach," he said. "You read my currents for me. You sat at my table."

"Yes."

"You watched me worry about that leak and you said nothing."

"I said plenty about your leak. You didn't listen. Your first mate was cutting the caulking and you thought it was the river." She said it without cruelty, and somehow that was worse. "I didn't tell you about Kessler because the moment I did, you'd put me off at the next landing, and then the only person on this river who knows what she's actually buying would be gone. I chose wrong. I know I chose wrong. I'm telling you now because you found the charts, and because I was going to tell you tomorrow, and because—" her jaw tightened— "because I'm tired of carrying it."

He lifted his hand off the charts. Picked up the top sheet. The drowned reach, beautifully done, the channels rendered in her fine sure lines, the Fairweather seal drying in the corner like a signature on a confession.

It was, he thought, a good map. It was the best map of that reach he had ever seen, better than his father's, better than the crown's, and she had made it with a leash around her neck and a lie in her pocket, and she had slept two bunks away from him the whole time.

"You know what this mark means," he said.

"I know what you think it means."

"What it means," he said, "is that my father stamped it on everything he owned, and everything he owned went to the bottom of the river, and I have spent fifteen years making sure it never touched anything again. And you sat in my cabin and put it on paper like it was yours to use."

"Oscar—"

He gathered the charts. All of them. Her drowned reach, her soundings, her eight months of work, the notes on the backs, the seal in every corner. She didn't move to stop him. She watched him with an expression he couldn't read and didn't want to—too steady, too sad, like she'd already run this scene and knew how it ended.

He went up the companionway into the rain.

* * *

The river took the charts in pieces. He threw the first sheet flat and it slapped the black water, floated a heartbeat with the ink already bleeding, then the current folded it under. The second and third he tore in half first. By the fourth he wasn't tearing them, just throwing, arm after arm, the rain running down his face, the river swallowing each one without comment, because that was what the Serevan did—it took things and gave nothing back and kept moving, and he had learned everything he knew from it.

Ilsa came up the companionway and stood at the rail while he threw the last of them. She didn't shout. She didn't plead. The rain plastered her shirt to her shoulders and she just stood there, watching her eight months go into the current, and when the last sheet was gone she said, over the noise of the water:

"Are you finished?"

He turned on her. "You have some nerve—"

"I have a great deal of nerve, yes, it's why I'm good at my work." She pushed her wet hair out of her face. "Now listen to me, because I'm only going to say this once and then you can put me off at the bluff landing and I'll walk to Serevan in the rain and we'll both be rid of each other."

"Nothing you say—"

"The charts are gone. Fine. I don't need them." She tapped her own temple. "Eight months, Oscar. Every sounding, every channel, every figure. It's in here. You can throw the paper in the river every night for the rest of your life and it won't change that. The only question—the only one—is whether the next thing I draw goes to Maren Kessler or to you."

The rain hammered the deck. Somewhere below, the bilge pump cycled on, and he heard it the way you hear your own name across a crowded room.

"Kessler paid you," he said.

"Kessler's broker paid me, for a lie. You've fed me, sheltered me, and paid me nothing, and I'd still rather draw for you." She said it simply, no music in it, and that was what made it land. "I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to be smarter than your pride. You need the drowned reach charted before the permits expire. I'm the only person on this river who can do it in time. Throw me off if you want. But throw me off because of what I did, not because of what you're afraid I'll do."

"And what am I afraid of?"

"That I'm the first person in fifteen years who's seen the whole map of you," she said, "and you'd rather sink than be read."

He had no answer for that. The river had an answer for everything and he had none for that.

"Get below," he said finally. "You're dripping on my deck."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, not quite anything. She went down the companionway. He stayed at the rail a long time after, watching the current carry the last ghost of ink out toward midstream, and he made himself say the true thing, silently, to the rain, because he'd learned that from the river too: she was right. About the leak. About the caulking. About him.

He went below, and the lamp was still lit at the chart table, and she was sitting where he'd found her, with a blank sheet of paper in front of her and the pen in her ink-black fingers, waiting.

She hadn't drawn anything. She was waiting for him to say whether she could.

Oscar stood in the doorway a long moment. Then he crossed the cabin, took the pen from her hand, and drew, in a rough and graceless line, the coiled river and the crescent moon and the three stars.

"From memory," he said. "That's the only way it gets drawn again. From memory, and by me."

She looked at the mark, then at him, and nodded once.

Above them, the rain thickened into something with intent, and far downstream, at the mouth of the drowned reach, a steamer's lights would be coming on that neither of them could see yet.
