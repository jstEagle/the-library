## 11. The Drowned Reach

The river stopped being a river an hour before we arrived.

Ilsa noticed it first in the charts, then in the water itself. The current slackened, spread, went glassy and brown and patient, and the banks fell away on both sides until the Marigold was no longer threading a channel but crossing something vast and shallow and drowned. Trees stood to their shoulders in the flood, their crowns still green, their trunks furred with a season's worth of silt. A church roof broke the surface a half-mile downstream, its bell tower tilted like a man leaning against a wall he no longer trusted.

"The Serevan Dam backed up forty years ago," Oscar said from the wheel. He said it flatly, the way other men recited cargo weights. "The government flooded the valley to feed the lowland mills. Three villages went under. The maps Ilsa drew were the last ones made of dry land."

"I didn't draw them," Ilsa said. "I copied them. The surveyor who came through never charted the deep water past the second bluff. Nobody has. That's why you need me."

"That's why Kessler needs you," Dario muttered at the rail.

Nobody answered him. The rain had started an hour ago—not monsoon proper, not yet, but a gray insistent drizzle that beaded on the brass rails and made the whole world smell like wet iron and rotting leaves. Above them the clouds had come down off the mountains early this year, low and bruise-colored, stacked in ranks that seemed less like weather than like siege equipment.

Ilsa stood at the bow with her chart case under her oilskin and felt the valley take hold of her imagination the way good blank space always did. Every cartographer knew the feeling: here was territory no one had written down, and everything she put on paper would become the truth other people navigated by. It was power. It was also a debt she hadn't finished paying, and she thought of Kessler's commission folded in her cabin—*chart the reach, mark the hull, note all approaches*—and pushed the guilt down where it lived these days, next to the manifest fragment and everything else she wasn't saying.

"Sound," Oscar said.

Dario swung the lead line out over the bow. The weight sang briefly, dropped, and the line hissed through his palms.

"Mark—twelve!" he called.

Oscar grunted. "Twelve feet over what used to be somebody's front yard." He turned the wheel a spoke. "Keep sounding. The old channel runs along the eastern bluff. If my father's charts are worth anything—and they were worth more than mine—the Halcyon sits somewhere in that channel, buried to her gunwales."

"You've never come looking before?" Ilsa asked.

She regretted it as soon as she said it. Oscar's jaw did the thing it did when a question got too close to bone—a small tightening, a muscle jumping once below the ear.

"Fifteen years ago I swore I'd never pilot anything up this reach again," he said. "Now look at me."

He said nothing else, and the Marigold moved on into the drowned country, her engine knocking patiently, her patched hull groaning at every swell.

* * *

They anchored at dusk in the lee of the second bluff, where the drowned forest thinned and the water deepened toward the old channel. Oscar would not go closer in the dark. "Wreck water eats boats," was all he said, and no one argued, because everyone had seen the way dead trees stood just under the surface like spears, waiting for a hull to drift across them.

The rain thickened after sundown. It came down in ropes now, drumming the deckhouse, filling the river with sound until conversation meant shouting into someone's ear. They ate salt pork and hard bread in the galley, and Ilsa watched Oscar eat without tasting anything, his eyes going again and again to the black water beyond the porthole, as if he expected the valley to give something back.

Divers couldn't work at night. Everyone knew that. But Oscar went out on deck anyway at midnight, and Ilsa, unable to sleep, followed him.

He stood at the stern rail with a lantern unlit in his hand, watching rain stitch silver lines down through the dark.

"My father used to say the river keeps what it's given," he said, without turning. He'd known she was there; she was beginning to understand that he always knew. "Cargo, boats, men. He said it like it was a fair bargain. Like the river was honest."

"And you don't think it is?"

"I think it doesn't care either way." He finally lit the lantern, and its small yellow circle made them both look more tired than they were. "Honest things care. The river just takes. My mother told me the night the Halcyon went down, the water was so calm you could see the stars in it. No storm. No warning. Just a boat that didn't come home."

Ilsa wanted to tell him then. The manifest fragment sat thirty feet away in her chart case, and the words on it sat heavier: *one (1) sealed strongbox, ship's papers and deed, captain's custody.* She had known for six days. She had watched him steer toward this valley for six days knowing he was sailing toward his own inheritance and calling it salvage.

Instead she said, "Tomorrow we'll find her."

"Yes," Oscar said. "Tomorrow we'll find her." And there was something in his voice she couldn't name—dread or hunger or both wearing each other's clothes.

* * *

Morning came the color of pewter, and the monsoon arrived with it.

Not the drizzle of yesterday but the true thing, the vertical wall of warm rain that turned the river to boiling mercury and the sky to a single featureless ceiling. The crew worked in oilskins that streamed water even as it fell on them. Sound carried strangely—Ilsa heard the splash of the lead line as if it were inside her own skull.

They took soundings all morning, working a grid upstream from the bluff, and it was Ilsa's chart that found her.

"Here." She laid her pencil on the paper, on a faint irregularity she'd noted between two soundings—an anomaly in the bottom contour, a long shadow too straight to be geology. "Between these marks. Something long and narrow, lying northeast-southwest. That's not a tree. Trees fall any which way. This is a keel line."

Oscar studied the chart for a long moment. Then he looked up at the drowned valley—at the rain, at the brown water, at the tilted church tower miles downstream—and Ilsa saw fifteen years fall off him and settle back on all at once.

"Drop the marker buoy," he said quietly.

The buoy went in at noon. By one o'clock, two of the crew—Ferran and the boy Tomas, who had the best lungs—were in the water in canvas diving suits, air pumping down to them through hoses from the deck, visibility below measured in inches of silt-cloud.

What came back up came back in fragments, shouted reports relayed through the tender, each one landing on the deck like a stone:

*"Touching bottom—mud, soft—"*

*"Found planking! Iron-fastened, big frames—this is a ship, this is a big ship—"*

*"Name board! Painted letters—H-A-L—"*

The deck went silent except for the rain. Even the engine seemed to hold its breath. Oscar stood at the rail gripping it so hard his knuckles went white, and Ilsa watched him watch the water, and understood that for him the Halcyon had been an abstraction for fifteen years—a name, a wound, a reason to keep moving—and now she was a set of coordinates, a place, a thing with a name board a boy could touch.

By late afternoon the divers had mapped the wreck by feel: hull intact but buried to the main deck, wedged nose-down in the old channel's silt, held fast by fifteen years of accumulated mud and one fallen tree that lay across her stern like a bar across a door. Salvageable, Ferran reported. Barely. If the river didn't shift her deeper first.

And then, near dusk, Tomas came up fast, half-panicked, coughing river water around his mouthpiece, and when they got the helmet off him he could barely get the words out.

"There's a box," he gasped. "In the hold, aft, behind a collapsed bulkhead. Iron. Big as a sea chest. It's welded shut—no, not welded—seized, fused, like it's been shut since the day she sank. We couldn't even find a seam. And the captain's marked it, sir—" the boy looked at Oscar, and his voice dropped, "—there's paint on it. Fairweather blue. Same as your funnel."

No one spoke. The rain hammered the deck. Downstream the drowned church leaned in the failing light, and somewhere beneath forty feet of brown water, a box with a dead man's colors painted on it waited in the dark with its seam fused shut, holding whatever Elias Fairweather had thought worth sealing before the end.

Oscar turned away from the rail. For a moment Ilsa thought he wouldn't speak at all.

"Rig the lifting gear at first light," he said finally. His voice was steady. His hands were not. "And double the anchor watches tonight. The river's rising."

It was. Ilsa could see it—the water climbing the bluff in slow gray inches, the current tightening, the whole drowned valley gathering itself for the season ahead. Somewhere downstream, she knew, Maren Kessler was counting days on a permit calendar. Somewhere in her own cabin, the manifest fragment waited in the dark with its secret about the strongbox, and every hour she kept it, the secret got heavier, the way ballast does.

That night she stood a watch alone under the awning, chart on her knees, pencil idle, listening to the rain and the creak of the anchors and the river rising inch by inch around them. The Halcyon lay forty feet down, wedged in the mud of the old channel, her name board in a boy's hands, her strongbox painted in a color Ilsa now recognized everywhere—in the Marigold's funnel, in the trim of the wheelhouse, in the faded ribbon Oscar still tied around his pocket watch.

The past had surfaced. Literally, completely, and three days ahead of schedule.

And tomorrow, Ilsa knew, watching the black water climb, someone was going to ask her what else the manifest said—and she would have to decide, finally, whether she served the woman who paid her or the man whose father had painted that box blue.
