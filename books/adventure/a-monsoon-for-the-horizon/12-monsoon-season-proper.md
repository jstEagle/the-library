## 12. Monsoon Season Proper

The rain came on the third morning like a debt collector—no warning, no negotiation, just the whole sky opening at once over the drowned reach.

Oscar stood under the Marigold's awning and watched it fall. Not rain as he knew it, not the polite afternoon showers that moved cargo up and down the Serevan all summer. This was monsoon proper, the kind the old pilots called *the long water*, when the river stopped being a road and remembered it was an animal.

"Barometer's falling," Ilsa said beside him, her charts weighted down with brass fittings against the wind. "Falling fast."

"Barometers always fall in a storm. That's how they know they're barometers."

She didn't laugh. She'd stopped laughing at his jokes two days ago, somewhere around the time the first squall knocked the survey buoy loose and they'd spent six hours chasing it downstream. Now she just looked at him with those ink-stained fingers curled around her pencil, waiting for him to be useful instead of charming.

"The wreck," she said. "If the river rises another three meters—"

"I know what happens."

He did know. The Halcyon lay wedged in a silt shelf forty feet down, held there by fifteen years of sediment packing around her hull like concrete setting. But a monsoon flood didn't erode. It *scoured*. He'd seen it happen to other wrecks—the river lifting them like toys, rolling them, burying them so deep no diver alive could reach. Everything his father had left, everything the salvage was worth, sitting in a valley that was about to become a canyon of moving water.

"Pump stations," he called out. "Double shifts. And get that storm anchor off the bow and onto the upstream line—we hold position or we lose the site."

Dario appeared at his elbow, rain streaming off his jaw. "Captain, the crew's asking about rations."

"We've got rations."

"We've got eleven days of rice if we feed everyone half portions. Less if the divers keep burning through calories." Dario's voice was flat, careful, the voice of a man reading numbers he didn't like. "And the Marigold's taking water again. Slow, but she's taking it."

Of course she was. The caulking he'd rechecked twice, the seams he'd personally run his thumb along—they were weeping anyway, as if the boat itself had decided to grieve with him.

"Then we pump," Oscar said. "And we eat less. Put it to the crew however you like."

Dario looked at him a moment too long before turning away. Oscar told himself it was nothing. He was getting very good at telling himself things were nothing.

* * *

By the second day of rain, the world had shrunk to the size of the deck.

The drowned reach disappeared. The cliffs on either bank dissolved into gray curtains; the far shore might have been another country, another century. Water came down so hard it bounced off the planking, and the sound of it made conversation impossible—you shouted into the storm and watched your words drown before they crossed four feet of air.

They ran pumps in rotation. They ate cold rice under canvas. They huddled around the one dry lantern in the galley and played cards with a deck gone soft as bread, and Oscar watched the tempers start to fray like old rope.

Pell, the youngest hand, picked a fight over nothing—a card, a look, nothing—and Dario broke it up with a shove that was harder than it needed to be. Old Sefa sat by the hatch staring at the water and muttering prayers to whatever river god had let him live this long. The divers, Voss and Tam, slept in their gear because stripping wet suits in the cold took more energy than sleeping in them, and woke up already exhausted.

Ilsa worked. That was the strange thing about her—while the rest of them decayed, she sharpened. She redrew the reach from memory, marked the wreck's position against shifting reference points, calculated scour rates with figures she muttered like incantations. When Oscar brought her tea she didn't ask for, she looked up at him with eyes that hadn't slept enough and said:

"Two days. Maybe three. After that the silt shelf goes and the Halcyon rolls into the channel."

"And then?"

"And then your father's boat becomes part of the riverbed, and nobody's grandfather ever finds her again."

*Your father's boat.* She still said it carefully, watching him each time, testing whether the words would cut. He appreciated that about her, or resented it—he hadn't decided which.

"We dive between squalls," he said. "Voss says the vis is bad but workable. We keep pulling up whatever we can pull."

"You're diving for scrap," she said quietly. "You know that. The hull's worth nothing broken apart in pieces. What you're really after is in the cabin, and the cabin's buried under the collapsed deck, and every hour of this rain buries it deeper."

He set the tea down in front of her and didn't answer, because the answer was: *I know.* He'd known since the day they found the hull wedged in the silt like a body in a shallow grave. The manifest fragment Ilsa carried spoke of cargo, of value—but Oscar wasn't diving for value. He was diving for a wheelhouse, for brass fittings his father's hands had worn smooth, for something he couldn't name and wouldn't examine.

"Get some sleep," he said instead.

"Soon," she lied, and went back to her charts.

* * *

On the fourth night, the river rose past the flood mark his father had painted on the cliffside twenty years ago.

Oscar saw it by lantern light during a break in the rain—a faded stripe of white paint on black stone, the high-water mark the elder Fairweather had brushed on with his own hands, laughing, saying *above this line we don't build, boy, above this line belongs to the river.* The current now slid a full arm's length above it, dark and muscular, carrying whole trees past the anchorage like matchsticks.

He stood at the rail a long time, watching the debris run. Somewhere upstream, villages were losing roofs. Somewhere upstream, the concession offices he'd spent fifteen years avoiding were probably sandbagging their doorways, clerks stacking permits in oilskin while the water climbed their walls.

Ninety days, the port official had told him, back when ninety days felt like a fortune. The monsoon had eaten most of them. What remained fit inside a week.

"You should sleep."

Ilsa again. She moved quiet as ink. She stood beside him at the rail, close enough that their shoulders touched, neither of them acknowledging it.

"You first," he said.

"I don't sleep well on boats."

"Nobody sleeps well on boats. We just get good at lying still."

She almost smiled. In the lantern light, with the rain hammering the awning overhead, it was the closest thing to warmth left in the world, and he found himself holding onto it like a man holding onto a rail.

"Oscar." Her voice dropped. "There's something I need to tell you about the manifest."

Whatever it was, he never heard it. A shout went up from the bow—Sefa's voice, cracked with alarm—and both of them turned to see the upstream anchor line snap taut, then shudder, then begin to *creep*, the winch drum turning slowly as the river pulled the Marigold toward the channel where the wreck lay waiting below like a mouth.

"All hands!" Oscar roared, and the siege began in earnest.

* * *

They fought the river for six hours.

It was brute work—every soul aboard hauling on the spare lines, rigging a second anchor off the stern, feeding chain to the winch while the drum groaned and the deck tilted and the rain came down like judgment. Twice the stern anchor dragged, and twice they reset it, men sobbing with effort in the dark, Voss screaming soundings into the night while Ilsa read the current by feel alone, calling corrections that Oscar passed down the line without understanding how she knew them.

Toward dawn, the river relented—or grew bored. The lines held. The Marigold rode her anchors, battered, leaking worse than ever, but alive.

They collapsed on the deck like the dead. Oscar sat with his back against the mainmast, watching the gray light come up through the rain, and counted his crew the way a gambler counts chips. Pell, hollow-eyed. Sefa, praying again. Voss and Tam, asleep sitting up. Dario—

Dario was standing at the stern rail, looking downstream.

Oscar watched him for a while. Watched him take something from inside his coat. Watched him hold it up—a signal lamp, shuttered, its beam pointed downriver into the murk—and flash it. Three short. One long. Three short.

Then closed the shutter and put it away, and turned back to the deck wearing the face of a tired first mate, and met Oscar's eyes across forty feet of ruined morning.

Neither of them moved. Neither of them spoke. The rain filled the silence between them like water filling a hull.

*Three short. One long. Three short.* Oscar didn't know the code. But he knew a man signaling downstream when he saw one, and he knew exactly who was moored downstream, riding out the same monsoon with a steamer full of divers and patience.

He thought about confronting him. He thought about putting Dario off the boat at the next trading post, if any trading post still existed above the flood line. He thought about the contract Maren Kessler had pressed into his hands in that riverside tavern, and the fine print he'd never read, and the crew she'd bought out from under him before he'd even said no.

Instead he did nothing. Because the river was rising, and the silt shelf was failing, and in two days—maybe three—the Halcyon would roll into the channel and be gone forever, and he could not afford to fight a war on two fronts. Not yet.

So he held Dario's gaze across the deck, and let him see that he'd seen, and said nothing at all.

Dario's face went gray. He understood. And he turned away and went to check the pumps like a good first mate, and the Marigold leaked, and the rain fell, and somewhere below them in the drowning dark his father's boat shifted in the silt—Oscar felt it through the hull that night, a deep subsonic groan, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Two days. Maybe three.

He stood his watch alone, listening to the river eat the world, and waited to see which would fail first: the wreck, the boat, or the man he'd trusted longest.
