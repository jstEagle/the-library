## 13. The Captain's Cabin

The divers went down at first light, into water the color of steeped tea.

Oscar stood at the Marigold's rail and watched the bubbles rise in a broken line, marking the path of Tam and the boy, Renn, as they followed the guide rope down to where the Halcyon lay wedged in the silt of the drowned reach. Forty feet down, maybe forty-five. The flood had swallowed this valley whole a generation ago—orchards, ferry landings, a church with its steeple still poking above the surface like a drowning man's finger—and somewhere in that cold brown dark was the hull his father had commanded for twenty years.

He had not slept. None of them had, not really. The rain came in sheets now, monsoon proper, drumming on the deckhouse roof so constantly that silence itself had become strange, a thing you had to remember how to hear. The river rose a hand's width every day. You could see it climbing the pilings of the old orchard fence that broke the surface downstream, could see it taking back ground it had given up when they'd dammed the upper valley and drowned everything below the falls.

"Tam says the cabin's breached aft," Ilsa said, coming to stand beside him. She had her oilskin hood up and her hands buried in her sleeves against the damp, but she didn't hunch from the weather the way the crew did. She leaned into it, like she was listening to something in it. "There's a gap where the stern door tore loose. They can get in without cutting."

Oscar nodded. He'd heard Tam report it himself, ten minutes ago, shouting up through the rain while Renn shivered beside him and the air hose hissed. He'd heard it and he hadn't moved, and Ilsa had watched him not move, and neither of them had said anything about it.

"They'll start with the strongbox," he said.

"The strongbox," she agreed. "That's what we came for."

It wasn't, though. Not entirely. And they both knew it, and the knowing sat between them like a third person at the rail.

* * *

They brought up the first things by midmorning: a ship's bell crusted green, a lantern whose glass had gone opaque with silt, a crate of preserved fruit whose labels had dissolved into pale ghosts. The crew handled these objects with a strange gentleness, passing them hand to hand, setting them on canvas spread across the deck. Fifteen years underwater and the labels still legible enough to read *Serevan Orchards, Est. 1841*. Somebody's whole life in a crate of rotting peaches.

Dario worked the winch without speaking. He'd been quiet since the mutiny—or since Ilsa had talked them all down from the mutiny, which amounted to the same thing—and he worked now with a doggedness that looked, to Oscar's eye, a great deal like penance. Good. Let him haul his way back to trust one crank at a time.

"Captain." Tam's voice came up through the speaking tube, distorted by the hose and the rain and the depth. "We're at the cabin door. It's—it's open. We're going in."

Oscar took the tube himself. "Report every few minutes. Don't touch anything with brass fittings—the metal goes soft down there. Anything you disturb, mark it."

"Aye, Captain." A pause, long enough that Oscar could hear the hiss of air and the tick of the pump. Then, quieter, almost apologetic: "There's a wheel, Captain. Forward bulkhead. Still mounted."

Still mounted. After fifteen years. After the boat had gone down bow-first into the flood channel and slammed into whatever she'd hit and settled into the mud like a tired animal lying down.

"I know," Oscar said. "I put my hands on that wheel before I could walk."

Another pause. "There's someone at it, Captain."

The rain filled the silence. The pump ticked. Somewhere downstream the river groaned against the orchard fence, patient as arithmetic.

"Say that again," Oscar said.

"There's a man at the wheel. Skeleton. Hands on the spokes. He's been there the whole time."

* * *

He told himself he wouldn't go down.

He told it to himself the way you tell yourself anything you already know is a lie: firmly, repeatedly, with increasing desperation. There was no reason for him to descend. Tam and Renn were competent divers; the salvage was proceeding; the strongbox welded shut in the forward hold was the prize, and the deed—if the deed existed, if half the stories were true—was in the strongbox, not at the wheel. His job was topside. Command meant staying above the waterline. Command meant exactly this: standing dry at the rail while other men did the wet work.

But his hands were already checking the straps of the diving rig, and Dario was tightening them without being asked, and Ilsa was holding out the weighted boots with an expression he refused to look at directly because he knew what he'd find in it—not pity, worse, understanding.

"Forty feet," Dario said. "Cold as a tax collector's heart down there. You feel the pressure in your teeth. When the light goes, don't panic—the lamp catches the silt and it looks like the whole world's turned to smoke. It clears. Just wait and it clears."

"You've been down twice," Oscar said.

"Twice is enough to know that much." Dario cinched the last strap and then, unexpectedly, gripped Oscar's forearm. His hands were shaking. So were Oscar's, though he'd have cut off a finger before admitting it. "Fifteen years, Captain. Whatever's down there, it kept. It'll keep another hour if you need it."

"It won't," Oscar said. "Nothing keeps. That's the whole lesson of the river."

He went over the side.

* * *

The water took him the way night takes a room—one moment everything, the next nothing, just the roar of his own breath through the helmet and the slow drop along the guide rope, hand over hand, into the brown dark.

At thirty feet the cold found him. At forty his boots touched silt and the lamp caught the murk and the world did turn to smoke, exactly as Dario had promised, a swirling gray-brown nothing in every direction. He stood still. He waited. He counted his breaths because counting was something to do, and at eleven the current shifted and the smoke thinned, and the Halcyon appeared around him like a cathedral assembling itself out of fog.

She was bigger than he remembered. Everything was bigger than he remembered; he'd been nine years old the last time he'd stood on her deck. But even allowing for the memory of a child, she was big—a river steamer of the old school, twin stacks folded flat by the collapse of her upperworks, paddle housing split open like a ribcage, railings furred with fifteen years of silt-grown weed that waved in the current with a horrible gentleness. Fish hung motionless in the doorways of what had been cabins. A school of small silver things poured out of the pilot house window as his lamp swept past, scattering like startled thoughts.

He had dreamed this boat for fifteen years and in the dreams she was always burning or always sinking or always just ahead of him in the fog, and none of the dreams had prepared him for how *quiet* she was. Not wrecked. Resting.

The guide rope ran aft along her deck toward the breached stern door. Tam and Renn had marked the route with knotted cord, and he followed it, boots sinking ankle-deep in silt with every step, past the collapsed galley, past a steward's pantry where plates still stood stacked on shelves as if waiting for breakfast service. His lamp caught a child's shoe in the corner of a corridor and he made himself keep walking.

The captain's cabin was aft, port side. Of course it was. He'd known that before he knew his own name; he'd been born in the bunk above that desk.

The door hung open on torn hinges, and inside, the lamplight found the room almost intact. A desk bolted to the floor, drawers swollen shut. A chart case, glass cracked, charts inside gone to pulp. A cot frame. A shelf of books fused into a single brick of paper. And forward, through the open bulkhead door where the cabin connected to the wheelhouse—

The wheel.

Brass, still brass under the silt, glowing dully in the lamplight like something alive. Eight spokes. His father's initials engraved in the hub, *E.F.*, done by some dockside engraver in the year Oscar was born.

And the man at it.

Oscar stopped in the doorway of the wheelhouse and could not make his legs carry him further.

The skeleton sat upright in the remains of the chair, held together by clothing and by the wheel itself, hands resting on two of the spokes—not gripping, not clawing, just *resting*, the way a man rests his hands on a wheel when the river is calm and there is nowhere in particular to be. The uniform coat had mostly gone, but the buttons remained, scattered down the front of the ribs like a row of small moons. The skull inclined slightly forward, chin toward chest, in the posture of a man reading instruments, or praying, or simply watching the water come.

He had stayed at the wheel.

Everyone had always said the elder Fairweather had gone down with his boat, but going down with a boat was a phrase, a figure, a thing men said about honor. This was not a figure. This was a man who had felt the Halcyon strike, who had felt her begin to fill, who had perhaps sent his crew over the side into the flood channel and then turned back to his wheel and put his hands on the brass and *stayed*, because the Halcyon was his charge and a captain does not abandon his post, and because—Oscar understood this suddenly, completely, with a clarity that hurt worse than the cold—because there was no one left to steer for. Because if the boat was going to the bottom, then the bottom was where a Fairweather belonged.

"Fifteen years," Oscar said aloud, and his voice boomed stupidly in the helmet, huge and alone. "You absolute stubborn old bastard. Fifteen years."

His eyes were burning and it wasn't the silt.

* * *

He didn't remember crossing the wheelhouse. One moment he was in the doorway; the next his gloved hands were on the wheel, over the places where his father's finger bones rested, and he was standing beside the chair like a boy called to the helm for his first lesson. *Steady as she goes, Oscar. Feel it? That's the river talking. Your job is just to listen better than she talks.*

He had forgotten the sound of his father's voice. He hadn't—he'd have sworn on anything holy that he hadn't—but here it was, whole, unbroken, saying words he hadn't thought of in decades, and it undid him more thoroughly than the bones did. Grief, he discovered, was not a thing you finished. It was cargo. It rode low in the hold and you learned to sail around its weight, and you could do that for fifteen years, and then a lamp would catch a wheel in the dark and the whole shifting mass of it would come loose and slide, and you'd be bailing again, alone, forty feet under.

He stood there until his breathing steadied. The fish came back, silver threads moving through the lamplight. Somewhere above, muffled by forty feet of tea-colored water, the rain went on falling.

It was Renn who found him—the boy's lamp appearing in the corridor behind, the kid freezing when he saw the scene, seeing the captain and the dead man and the wheel and understanding, in the wordless way of the young, that he had walked in on something that was not salvage.

"Captain," Renn said softly. "There's—sir, there's a packet. On the desk. In the cabin. Oilskin, wrapped and tied. It's clean, sir. However it was stored, it kept dry."

Oscar turned. Through the bulkhead doorway, on the desk bolted to the floor, sat a package roughly the size of a book, wrapped in oiled canvas and bound with tarred cord, sitting atop the rotted remains of everything around it like the one true thing in a room of lies. Someone had wrapped it well. Someone had meant it to last.

His father had known the boat was going down. Had known it in time to wrap a letter in oilskin and leave it on the desk, and then had walked forward to the wheel and taken up his post.

*For whoever comes looking,* the wrapping seemed to say. *For whoever finally stops running.*

"Leave it," Oscar heard himself say. "Don't touch it. Mark the position and go up. Both of you. Now."

"And you, Captain?"

"I'm right behind you."

He was not right behind them. He stood in the wheelhouse of the Halcyon for a long time after their lamps had dwindled up the guide rope, his hands hovering over the oilskin packet on the desk, close enough to touch, close enough to take. All he had to do was pick it up. Carry it topside. Cut the cord. Fifteen years of not-knowing, ended in the time it took to read a page.

His fingers actually closed on it. He felt the oilskin, slick and intact, felt the stiff shape of paper within.

And he let go.

Because once he read it, it would be real. Whatever his father had to say—confession, apology, accusation, absolution—once the cord was cut, there would be no version of Oscar Fairweather that had never read it. He had built a whole life on the far side of that letter. Fifteen years of moving, of never docking twice, of reading water instead of people—all of it mortared together with the not-knowing, and he was suddenly, terribly certain that the letter was load-bearing. That pulling it free might bring the whole structure down.

Or save it. He couldn't know which. That was the nature of letters.

He went up the rope into the gray light and the endless rain, empty-handed, and when Dario asked—"Well? What's down there?"—he heard himself answer in a voice that sounded borrowed:

"Wreckage. Silt. The strongbox is our prize; the rest is grave goods." He pulled off the helmet, and the rain hit his face and he let it, let it run cold down his skin, washing away whatever evidence of weeping might have survived the dive. "We've got maybe three days before the permits lapse and the river shifts her deeper. Everyone sleeps in shifts. Tomorrow we crack that box."

Ilsa was watching him from beneath her hood. She'd dried his spare clothes by the stove, he noticed; they hung there steaming, and beside them, laid out with a care that made his chest ache, was a towel and a tin cup of the terrible black coffee the crew drank, waiting for him.

"You found him," she said quietly. Not a question.

Oscar took the coffee. His hands were steady, which surprised him.

"He's still at the wheel," he said. "Hands on the brass. Fifteen years, and he never once let go."

Ilsa said nothing. She didn't offer comfort, didn't ask what else he'd found, didn't glance toward the winch where the oilskin packet would soon be coming up whether he willed it or not—for of course it would; Renn had marked it, Tam had seen it, and salvage was salvage, and by tomorrow morning half the crew would know the captain's father had left a letter, and the day after, Kessler's spies would know it too.

That was the thought that finally moved him, more than grief, more than duty: Maren Kessler's steamer was out there somewhere beyond the rain, and secrets surfaced on this river faster than wrecks did.

"We bring the packet up tonight," Oscar said, setting down the cup. "Before anyone else knows it exists. And Ilsa—I want your eyes on it first. If there's a name on that wrapping that isn't mine, I need to know before I cut the cord."

Her hood tilted. "Why mine?"

"Because everyone else on this boat has already sold something to somebody," Oscar said. "You threw your charts overboard and stayed anyway. That's the only currency I trust anymore."

Out on the black water, lightning stitched the horizon, and the thunder came rolling up the drowned valley like something enormous turning over in its sleep.
