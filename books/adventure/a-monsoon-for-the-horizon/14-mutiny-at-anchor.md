## 14. Mutiny at Anchor

The Marigold had been listing to port for two days, and by the third morning she was leaning like a drunk against a lamppost, her rail kissing the brown water every time the river breathed.

Dario Voss stood on the tilting deck and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the money changed hands in the trading post's back room. Rations for eleven men: nine days, maybe ten if they stretched the rice with river fish. Pumping shifts around the clock: four men at a time, arms and backs already worn to rope. The leak: held at bay but never beaten, a slow patient enemy that gained ground every time the squalls came through. And downstream, three days' hard run, Kessler's agent waiting with more money than Dario had ever seen in one place, and a deadline that was eating the calendar alive—permits expiring, salvage reverting to the crown, everything they'd bled for turning to nothing if they didn't get off this reach before the monsoon swallowed it whole.

The math was simple. The math said go.

The math also said that going meant abandoning Oscar Fairweather on a flooded reach with a dying boat, and Dario had been running from that particular sum for two days now, the way a man runs from a bill collector.

He found half the crew in the galley, which was now the low side of the galley, cooking oil sliding across the tilted stove top and pooling against the bulkhead. Ruiz was there, and the Okonkwo brothers, and young Tam with his burned hands wrapped in rags. They went quiet when Dario came through the door, and the silence told him everything. They'd already done his arithmetic. They were just waiting for someone to say it out loud.

"The longboat," Dario said. "She'll hold eight. We run downstream tonight, ahead of the next blow. We come back with a steamer, a proper salvage crew, pumps that actually work."

"And the captain?" Ruiz asked. Not hostile. Worse than hostile—genuine.

"Comes with us or stays, his choice." Dario heard how it sounded even as he said it, and hated himself for the way he'd softened it. "But we can't pump this boat forever. You all know it. I know it. He knows it too—he just won't say it, because saying it means admitting the Halcyon beat him twice."

Tam looked down at his bandaged hands. "He pulled me out of the engine room when the coupling let go. Carried me up two ladders with water at his waist."

"He'd carry any one of you out," Dario said. "That doesn't change what the river's about to do to us. Monsoon season proper starts any day now. When it does, this reach becomes a lake with a current, and the Marigold goes to the bottom next to the Halcyon, and we go with her unless we're gone first."

Nobody argued. That was the worst part. In fifteen years of running this river, Dario had watched crews argue about everything—wages, watch rotations, whose turn it was to gut the fish—and Oscar had always won those arguments without raising his voice, just by being the man who read water better than anyone alive. But nobody argued now, because there was nothing to argue. The numbers were the numbers.

"We move tonight," Dario said. "After the evening pump shift, while he sleeps. He hasn't slept right since the leak started. He'll be dead on his feet by dark."

Ruiz nodded slowly. The Okonkwo brothers exchanged a look. Tam stared at his hands.

Four more men joined them by noon, word moving through the crew the way water moves through a hull—silently, finding every seam. By afternoon Dario counted seven committed, plus himself. Eight. The longboat's full complement.

Which meant four staying, and Dario didn't let himself think about who.

* * *

It fell apart at dusk, because of course it did. Because Oscar Fairweather slept like a cat, one ear open, and because the rain had stopped for the first time in thirty hours and the whole world was dripping and quiet, and quiet carries sound a long way over water.

Dario was at the longboat's painter, untying the knot with hands that wouldn't stop shaking, when the deck hatch opened above him and Oscar came up into the last grey light.

For a long moment neither of them moved. The river slid past below, high and brown and patient. Somewhere across the drowned valley, the Halcyon lay under forty feet of silted water, holding whatever she held.

"You want to tell me what this is?" Oscar asked. His voice was level. That was the frightening thing about it. Dario had seen him angry, had seen him afraid, had seen him drunk-grieving once a year on the anniversary—but this flat calm was new, and new things on Oscar Fairweather usually meant something had broken underneath.

"It's not what it looks like."

"It looks like mutiny, Dario."

"It's survival." Dario straightened, made himself meet those grey eyes. "Seven men are coming with me. We run downstream, we get help, we come back with equipment that can actually raise your father's boat. This—" he gestured at the listing deck, the pump hoses snaking everywhere like intestines, "—this is suicide dressed up as loyalty. And you know it. You've known it for days."

"I know the permits expire in nineteen days."

"I know they do. And I know Kessler's steamer is sitting downstream waiting for exactly that. You think I don't see it? You think any of us don't?" Dario took a step forward, and behind him he heard the others coming up out of the hold, Ruiz and the brothers, gathering at his back like he was something worth gathering behind. "You're so busy fighting your father's ghost that you'll drown the whole crew doing it. Well, not me. Not them. Not this time."

Oscar looked past him at the faces. Counting. Dario watched him count, watched him arrive at the number, watched something close behind his eyes.

"Half my crew," Oscar said quietly.

"Half your crew that hasn't eaten properly in a week. Half your crew that's been pumping seawater out of a hole in the hull with their bare hands while you stand at the wheel staring at a wreck." Dario's voice cracked and he pushed through it. "I'm sorry, Oscar. I am. But I'm not burying anyone else on this river."

Something moved through Oscar then—not anger, exactly. Recognition. As if Dario had finally said the true thing, the thing under all the other things, and it landed harder than any accusation could have.

"Get away from the longboat," Oscar said.

"No."

"Get away from the longboat, Dario, or I swear on my father's grave—"

"Your father's grave is forty feet down and full of silt!" It was out before Dario could stop it, cruel and true, and he saw it hit Oscar like a fist. "That's what this is! Fifteen years, Oscar. Fifteen years running up and down this river because you couldn't face one flooded valley. And now you've got the rest of us standing in it with you, pumping, starving, waiting for a storm to finish what the river started!"

Oscar crossed the deck in three strides.

Dario had fifteen years of muscle memory and a foot of height on most men, and none of it mattered. Oscar hit him low and hard, and they went down together onto the wet planking, and suddenly there were hands everywhere—crew pulling at both of them, shouting, the Okonkwo brothers hauling Dario back while Ruiz got an arm around Oscar's chest. Someone swung a lantern wild and its light swept the deck like a searchlight, catching faces twisted with fear and fury and the awful shame of men watching their officers fight.

Dario tasted blood. His own or Oscar's, he couldn't tell.

"Enough."

The voice cut through all of it, small and clear as a bell over water.

Ilsa stood at the base of the wheelhouse stairs, soaked to the knees from working the forward pump, ink stains still ghosting her fingers, her survey maps clutched under one arm like a shield. She was the smallest person on the deck and somehow the only one standing still.

"Both of you," she said, "are wrong."

* * *

She walked between them the way she must have walked into river towns her whole life—unhurried, certain of her ground. She planted herself in the lantern light where everyone could see her, and she laid one of her charts flat on the cargo hatch, weighting the corners with four iron washers from her pocket.

"Show me the depth soundings again," she said to Dario. "The ones you took yesterday, off the eastern mudflat."

Dario wiped his mouth. "What?"

"The eastern mudflat. Where the channel bends north. You sounded it at dawn, before the fight started. I saw you marking the slate." Her eyes were steady on his. "How deep?"

"Six feet at low water. Maybe less. It shoals fast—the flood's been dumping silt there all week."

"So it's getting shallower." She turned to the crew, to all of them, her voice rising just enough to carry. "A mudflat, six feet deep and getting shallower, wide enough to take a hundred-ton hull, half a mile from here. And you—" she pointed at Dario, "—want to abandon a damaged boat in rising water instead of putting her somewhere she can't sink."

Silence. Rain ticked against the chart.

"She's right," Ruiz said slowly. "Careen her. Beach her on the flat, let the tide leave her, recaulk the seams at low water. My uncle ran a careening yard outside Port Serevan twenty years. It's done all the time."

"With what tools?" Dario snapped, grasping for solid ground. "With what caulking? With what food while we sit on a mudflat?"

"With the caulking from the longboat's emergency stores, which you were going to steal anyway," Ilsa said, without heat. "With the sailcloth awning as a work shelter. And with eleven men instead of eight, because no one has to choose between the captain and living." She looked around at the faces, at Tam's bandaged hands, at the brothers, at Dario bleeding from the lip. "Or you can take the longboat downstream tonight, seven of you in a boat built for open water in a rising monsoon river, and I'll chart for you the exact spot where the current will put you on the Devil's Teeth if the wind turns northeast. Which it will. I've been reading this sky since before we entered the reach."

Nobody spoke. The wind, as if summoned, shifted—a soft sigh out of the northeast, cold and smelling of the deep interior.

"There," Ilsa said. "Hear it?"

Dario looked at the chart. At the mudflat marked in her precise hand, six feet and falling. At the longboat, riding sullenly at its painter. At Oscar, standing rigid with Ruiz's arm still half around him, blood at the corner of his mouth, watching Ilsa with an expression Dario had never seen on him before—something between suspicion and surrender.

And Dario felt the money in his coat pocket, folded flat against his ribs, heavier than lead. Delay the salvage until the permits expire. That was the job. That was what he'd sold. And a beached, repaired, floating Marigold was the one thing that could undo it—because a floating Marigold could still win the race.

If he agreed to this, he wasn't just saving the crew. He was killing his own betrayal. Or feeding it. He honestly didn't know which anymore.

"Low water's at four in the morning," he heard himself say. "We'd have maybe five hours on the flat before she floats again. Five hours to find the seam and stop it." He looked up, met Oscar's eyes across the ruined space between them. "It's not enough time. You know it's not enough time."

"It's enough time to try," Oscar said. His voice was rough. "That's more than I gave you credit for wanting, Dario. A try."

The words went in deeper than any punch. Dario held still under them.

"All hands," Oscar called, loud enough to reach the whole deck, the whole boat, the whole drowned valley listening in the dark. "We beach her at first light. Every man on the lines. Nobody leaves, nobody fights, and anybody who wants to settle anything else settles it with me after the hull is sound." He paused, and his gaze found Dario one more time. "After the hull is sound."

The crew dispersed in silence, no cheering, no relief—just tired men moving toward their bunks with the strange lightness of people who have stepped back from an edge without quite knowing how.

Dario stayed at the rail. Below him the longboat knocked gently against the hull, and he thought about cutting it loose anyway, alone, tonight, and letting the river decide what kind of man he was.

Behind him, footsteps. Ilsa, rolling up her chart.

"Why?" he asked her. "Why stop it? You owe nothing to this crew. You've been lying to us since the crates."

She finished rolling the chart before she answered. "Because I've seen what happens to boats that lose their crews," she said. "And I've seen what happens to crews that lose their boats. Neither one survives the monsoon." She tucked the chart under her arm and paused at the companionway. "Besides—I need this boat afloat, Mr. Voss. More than you know."

She went below. Dario stood at the rail a long time, the money pressing against his ribs like a second heart, and watched the northeast wind bend the rain sideways across the water—and far downstream, faint as a struck match, a light blinked twice from the darkness where Kessler's steamer waited.

Someone had signaled. Someone always signaled.

And Dario Voss knew, with a sick certainty settling into his bones, that before morning he would have to decide which side of the water he was standing on—for good.
