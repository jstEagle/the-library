## 15. Careening on the Mudflat

The mudflat appeared at dawn like an answer to a question nobody had asked out loud.

It lay on the eastern bank where the river widened and forgot itself—a great brown shelf of silt exposed by the falling water between squalls, flat as a table, long enough to take the Marigold's hundred and ten feet if we came in fast and committed. I'd marked it two days ago without telling anyone, the way I marked everything, but Ilsa had seen me watching it and said nothing, which was its own kind of conversation.

"We ground her on purpose," I told the crew, gathered under the dripping awning of the afterdeck. Rain came down in ropes. The Marigold listed four degrees to port and had listed four degrees to port for six days, and every man aboard knew what four degrees meant when the pumps were running two watches out of three. "We drive her up at speed, heel her over on the ebb, and recaulk the strakes while she's dry. Two days' work. Three if the river argues."

"And if the river argues back?" Dario asked. He stood at the back of the group, arms folded, and there was something in his face I couldn't read—hadn't been able to read for a week now, a shut door where my first mate used to keep his windows open.

"Then we argue longer." I looked around at them. Six men left of the nine who'd signed on at Vellamouth, rain streaming off their hats, exhaustion carved into every jaw. "I won't pretend this is anything but what it is. She leaks because someone cut my caulking, and I haven't found out who, and I'm not going to pretend that doesn't sit in my chest like a stone. But she's our boat, and she got us here, and I don't leave boats to drown. Not this one. Not any."

Nobody cheered. But nobody walked to the longboat, either, and after the mutiny at anchor three nights past, that was a victory I'd take.

Ilsa stood apart, under the rail, her charts wrapped in oilskin against her chest like a child. Since the night I'd thrown her work into the river and she'd stayed anyway, we'd spoken in the clipped currency of necessity—soundings, rations, pump rotations. But her eyes had followed me through every argument since, and I'd begun to notice that I noticed.

"Captain," she said, when the crew dispersed. "The ebb turns at nine. If we're going to do this, we do it on the turn."

"I know when the ebb turns."

"I know you know. I'm telling you I know too."

Something in me that had been clenched for a week loosened half a turn. I almost smiled. "Then stand by your line, cartographer."

* * *

We came in at eight knots with the flood still under us and the rain coming sideways, and I drove the Marigold at that mudflat the way you drive anything you love—harder than is kind, because kindness gets people killed. The bow took the silt with a groan like a living thing, and the whole hull shuddered, and then we were sliding, sliding, the river pushing us up the flat as the water fell away beneath us, until with one last grinding lurch we settled, heeled to starboard, stranded in a world of mud and rain with the river retreating fifty yards to our left like a tide going out on some brown sea.

"Off your watches!" I shouted. "Shores and props! She settles wrong and we're here until Christmas!"

The crew moved. I'll give them that—they moved. Dario took the shoring party without being asked, and I watched him drive a prop into the mud with a violence that had nothing to do with carpentry, and I thought: *soon*. Soon I'd ask him. Soon the stone in my chest would have to come out. But not with forty feet of hull to caulk and the monsoon leaning on us.

By noon the starboard side stood dry in the air, weeded and barnacled and ugly as truth, and the men went at it with scrapers while the rain fell on their backs. I found the damage by the second hour. Three strakes aft of the engine room, the caulking muntz cut out clean—clean as a surgeon's line, not the ragged work of rot or grounding. I ran my thumb along it and felt the cold of it go all the way through me.

"You've found it." Ilsa crouched beside me on the staging, her trousers black to the knee with silt, a scraper in her hand she clearly had no idea how to use.

"Found the wound. Not the knife."

"Oscar." She said my name carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "You've been watching all of us for a week like we're suspects in a trial. Dario most of all. It's eating you alive, and it's going to split this crew before the river does."

"And what would you have me do? Love them instead?"

"I'd have you *ask*."

I looked at her. Rain ran off the brim of her hat and down the stubborn line of her jaw. "Ask Dario if he cut my boat open? He'd lie. Or worse, he'd tell the truth, and then I'd have to do something about it, and I don't know what I'd do, Ilsa. That's the truth of it. I've spent fifteen years making sure I never owed anyone enough to be betrayed by them. And now I'm beached on a mudflat with a crew I can't trust and a woman I—" I stopped.

She didn't let me stop. "A woman you what?"

The rain filled the silence. Down the flat, the crew sang one of their hauling songs, low and off-key, the words half drowned.

"Come on," I said, and stood, and handed her the scraper. "If you're going to be aboard my boat, you're going to work. And I'll tell you a story while we do. Fair trade. You've been carrying secrets since Vellamouth; you can carry one of mine."

* * *

We worked the after strakes through the afternoon, she scraping the weed off under my direction, getting better at it with a quickness that annoyed and pleased me in equal measure. And I talked. I hadn't meant to. It came out the way bilge comes out of a pump—everything at once, all of it foul, and then, at the end, cleaner water.

"I was nineteen," I said. "The night the Halcyon went down. I was aboard—my father's boat, the family's whole life. The concession, the cargo, the deed, everything my grandfather had built on this river. We were running down from the reach in the early monsoon, same as this. Rain like a wall. My father at the wheel, and me in the bow because he said a Fairweather learns a river from the front of a boat before the back."

Ilsa's scraper slowed.

"We hit the shoal off Corvin's Teeth. Or—that's what I believed for fifteen years. That's what the inquiry believed. Shoal, squall, bad luck. My father went down with her. Eleven souls, four saved, and I was one of the four because he—" My hands stopped on the hull. The barnacles were cold and knobbled under my palms. "Because he put me in the longboat. He put me in the longboat and went back for the manifest box. And the Halcyon went under in four minutes, and I watched it, Ilsa. I watched my whole inheritance stand on its stern and point its nose at the sky like a dying animal, and then the river closed over it, and I swore I'd never let a river close over anything of mine again."

"Which is why you never stop moving."

"Which is why I never stop moving." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "You think it's charm, the way I run cargo port to port, never a night longer than I need. Dario thinks I'm chasing money. The crew thinks I'm chasing something. I'm not chasing. I'm *fleeing*. There's a difference, and the difference is that a man who's chasing can stop when he catches what he wants. A man who's fleeing stops only when he's caught."

"And what's catching you now?"

I looked at her. Rain, mud, ink stains on her fingers that no amount of river water would ever fully lift. "You are," I said. "This is. The reach is. My father's boat is sitting in sixty feet of silt up that valley with his hands on the wheel, and I'm going to have to go down there, and I don't know what I'll find, and I've had fifteen years to imagine it and every version is worse than the last."

She set down the scraper. She took my hands off the hull and held them, both of hers around both of mine, and her palms were rough from the work and warm anyway.

"My turn," she said. "Since we're trading ballast."

* * *

She told me about her father, the surveyor, who'd charted the Serevan's drowned reaches for the crown and died of the fever with his maps unfinished and his daughter's apprenticeship unfinished with them. She told me how Kessler's commission had come to her—generous, flattering, *finish your father's work, Miss Brandt*—and how she'd taken it hungry and asked too few questions, and how she'd told herself the seal on the old survey was just an antiquity, just history, until she'd watched my face the day she'd shown it to me and understood she'd been carrying a knife the whole time without knowing whose throat it was for.

"I threw your charts in the river," I said.

"I know. I watched them float away. Every hour of my father's last year, ink and paper, gone in a minute." Her voice didn't waver, but her hands tightened on mine. "And I stayed. Do you want to know why I stayed, Oscar? Because you threw them and then you looked at me—furious, betrayed, ready to put me off at the next trading post—and you looked at me like I was still a person. Kessler looks at people like they're tonnage. You looked at me like I was crew. I've been charting rivers since I was twelve years old and nobody, not one soul on this water, has ever looked at me like I was crew."

The rain came down harder. Somewhere aft, Dario's voice rose over the hauling song, and I noticed I wasn't watching him anymore. I noticed that, and I let it go.

"My father," I said, "used to say a boat is just a promise you make to the river and keep paying on. I thought he was being poetic. He was being literal. The Halcyon was a promise, and somebody scuttled her, and I've spent half my life running from the debt without ever once asking who I owed it *to*." I turned my hands over so I was holding hers. "You said the manifest fragment you salvaged—there was writing on it you didn't show me."

Her face did something complicated. Guilt and decision fighting for the same ground.

"There was," she said. "And I can't tell you what it says yet. Not because of Kessler—Kessler's commission is ash, you burned it when you burned my charts. Because of me. Because I made a promise to someone before I understood what I was promising, and I need one more day to find a way to keep it and still be honest with you. Can you give me a day?"

Fifteen years ago I'd have said no. Five years ago I'd have said no. A man who's fleeing doesn't extend credit; a man who's fleeing settles every account in cash and moves on.

"One day," I said.

She exhaled, and it was like watching cargo come off a boat—the whole frame of her riding higher in the water. "One day."

* * *

We worked until dark. The crew worked with us, and the strangest thing happened somewhere in the second watch of caulking, which is that the work stopped being punishment and became, briefly, almost a pleasure—the mallets going in rhythm, oakum and pitch, the hull slowly sealing itself against the river strand by strand. Dario worked the strakes below me, and twice he looked up as if to say something, and twice the something died in his throat. I didn't push. Ilsa was right about that much: you ask, and you wait, and you let a man come to you carrying his own weight.

At full dark we knocked off, and the crew ate in the rain under canvas, and the river lay off to the west like a dark country waiting to take us back.

Ilsa found me at the rail. "Tomorrow," she said. "What I have to show you. It should be at first light, before the crew's up. Before anyone's up."

"That ominous?"

"That important." She hesitated at the top of the ladder down to the deck, silhouetted against the last grey light. "Oscar. The night your father went down. You said four were saved."

"Four."

"And the manifest box. Your father went back for it." Her voice was very quiet. "What if he wasn't going back for the manifest? What if he was going back to make sure something never came up at all?"

I turned. The rain stood between us like a curtain.

"What do you know about my father's last night, Ilsa?"

"Tomorrow," she said. "First light. Bring the oilskin packet if you've kept it. And Oscar—" she gripped the rail, and the ink stains on her fingers were black as pitch in the dark, "—bring it even if you haven't opened it. *Especially* if you haven't opened it."

She went down the ladder and left me at the rail with the rain and the dark river and the letter I'd carried for six days unopened, riding against my ribs like a second heartbeat, and for the first time since I'd pulled it from my father's cabin, I wanted to open it—and was afraid that whatever was inside would make the running stop for good.
