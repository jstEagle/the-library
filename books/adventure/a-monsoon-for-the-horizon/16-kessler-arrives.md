## 16. Kessler Arrives

Maren Kessler had spent her whole life learning to read weather the way other people read faces, and the sky over the drowned reach that morning told her everything she needed to know. The rain had thinned to a grey whisper after nine days of siege, and beneath it the river lay flat as poured pewter. Flat water meant falling rain upstream. Falling rain meant the flood was easing. And an easing flood meant the window was open.

She stood on the foredeck of the *Corvus* with her hands folded behind her back and let the mist bead on her face. Behind her, her chief engineer coughed in the way he did when he wanted permission to speak.

"Say it, Brandt."

"Current's running two knots against us through the narrows," he said. "We'll burn half our coal getting up there."

"We'll burn all of it if I tell you to." She didn't turn around. "The permits lapse in four days. The Fairweather claim reverts to the crown at monsoon's end, and every diver on this river will be crawling over that wreck by August. We go now."

Brandt went. That was the difference between her crew and Fairweather's, she told herself, and then immediately hated the thought, because thinking about Oscar Fairweather had become a habit she couldn't break, like pressing a bruise.

Fifteen years she'd carried his name around like a stone in her boot. Not hatred—she'd learned long ago that hatred was a luxury for people with time on their hands. It was something colder and more patient than that. Her father had died with a map of the Serevan spread across his deathbed and his finger resting on the drowned reach, whispering about what the Fairweathers had taken from him. He'd died ruined, and ruined men leave their children instructions whether they mean to or not.

Maren had simply been better at following them.

"Captain." Her second mate appeared at the rail, dripping. "Signal from the trading post skiff. They say the Marigold's beached on the eastern mudflat. Careening."

Maren allowed herself a thin smile. So the leak had done its work. Dario Voss had earned every coin she'd paid him—the caulking knife work alone had been worth the price—and the delay it bought was the whole architecture of her plan. A boat on a mudflat couldn't argue with a steamer holding position over a wreck.

"And Fairweather?"

"On the flat with the rest of them. Working the hull."

Of course he was. Oscar Fairweather would sooner let his own hands rot than ask another man to do his work. She'd watched him do it for years, back when they were young and the rivalry between their fathers hadn't yet curdled into something the children inherited like debt. He'd been handsome then, in the way of boys who were good at things, and insufferable, and she'd have followed him up any river on the Serevan if her father hadn't caught her looking.

That was another lifetime. This one belonged to the contract.

She'd drafted it herself, clause by clause, in a rented room above a chandlery in Port Serevan while the ink-stained cartographer woman sat across from her taking notes. Maren thought of Ilsa Reyne now with something close to fondness—not because she liked her, but because the woman had proven exactly as pliable as commissioned paper. Find the reach. Chart the depths. Report the wreck's condition. Nothing in her instructions had mentioned sabotage or salvage law, and nothing needed to. You didn't tell a tool what you were building. You just kept it sharp.

The fine print had done the rest. Clause eleven: *the contracting party retains sole and exclusive right to recover, claim, or otherwise possess any property, cargo, or documentation recovered from the vessel Halcyon, regardless of the physical agent performing recovery.* Oscar had signed it without reading past the split. Men who lived on rivers always assumed water was the only thing that could drown them.

"Sound the whistle when we clear the narrows," she said. "Two long blasts. Let them know we're coming."

* * *

They came out of the narrows at midday under a sky the color of wet slate, and Maren stood at the rail watching the drowned reach open before her like a held breath finally released.

She had seen charts of it. She had paid for charts of it. But no chart prepared her for the actual valley—the hills drowned to their waists, dead trees standing in forty feet of water with their arms raised like drowned congregations, the rooftops of the old ferry town just breaking the surface downstream, soft with fifteen years of silt. Somewhere under all that grey water lay the Halcyon, wedged in the old channel with her captain still at the wheel.

Maren felt something move in her chest that she refused to call grief.

"There." Brandt pointed. The Marigold sat heeled over on the mudflat like a beached whale, her starboard side stripped bare, lines running up into the trees where the crew had winched her upright. Figures moved along her flank in the rain—small, indistinct, industrious.

And standing at the waterline, hip-shot, arms crossed, watching the Corvus come on with the particular stillness of a man deciding whether to reach for a weapon.

Oscar Fairweather. Fifteen years hadn't softened him so much as weathered him, the way driftwood weathers—harder, lighter, stripped to grain. Even at this distance she could see him doing the arithmetic: steamer, depth of water, distance to the wreck site, days remaining on the permits. He'd always been fast with numbers. It was the reading-people part he'd never mastered.

"Take us to hover over the site," she ordered. "Drop anchor. All four."

The Corvus swung her ponderous bulk into position above the drowned channel, and the moment her anchors bit, Maren knew she'd won. Any boat that wanted to work the wreck now would have to negotiate with her anchors first.

She had the launch lowered. Four men, rifles slung, more for theater than use—Oscar wouldn't start a shooting war over silt, not with witnesses from the trading post camped within eyeshot. She stepped into the bow herself. If this was going to be a knife fight, she intended to hold the knife.

The Marigold's crew gathered along her tilted deck as the launch grounded on the mudflat. Maren counted them: eight, maybe nine, hollow-eyed from the siege, ribs showing through their shirts. Rations had run thin; she could smell it on them, the particular sourness of men who'd been eating boiled rope. Good. Hungry men listened to offers.

Oscar came down to meet her at the waterline, and up close he looked worse than the distance had suggested—grey-faced, rain-soaked, a cut over one eyebrow crusted with dried blood. But his eyes were the same. Clear, level, and absolutely unwilling to look away first.

"Maren."

"Oscar." She let the silence stretch. "You've been busy."

"Recaulking. Your friend Dario's knife work didn't hold."

It was a probe, tossed out casual as a fishing line, and she watched him watch her for the reaction. She gave him none. "I don't know what you're talking about. I know what my contract says, though. Shall I recite it? Clause eleven is my favorite."

Something flickered behind his eyes—the exact moment the arithmetic finished and the answer came up ugly.

"You never needed me," he said quietly.

"I needed your knowledge of the reach. Which I had. Through other channels." She gestured vaguely upstream, toward where a certain cartographer had once boarded his boat with ink-stained fingers and a commission she'd never fully explained. "You were never the partner, Oscar. You were the vehicle. I'm sorry you're only seeing it now."

"You're not sorry."

"No," she agreed. "I'm not."

Behind him, his crew had gone very still, and Maren saw the fracture lines in them plainly—men who'd bled for this salvage suddenly understanding they'd been working someone else's claim. One of them, a big Samoan-looking fellow with a caulking mallet still in his hand, took a step forward, and Oscar put out an arm without looking.

Then Dario Voss pushed through the crowd.

Maren had expected many things from this confrontation. She had not expected Dario to walk straight up to Oscar Fairweather, in front of God and the trading post witnesses and the falling rain, and stop three feet away with his face the color of old ash.

"Captain," Dario said. His voice cracked on the second syllable. "There's something you need to hear. From me. Before she says another word."

"Dario—" Maren started.

"Stay out of it." Dario didn't even look at her. That, more than anything, told Maren how far past saving the man was—he'd stopped being afraid of her somewhere in the last nine days, which meant he'd found something he feared more. "Captain. The leak. The caulking wasn't worn. I cut it. Clean cuts, four seams, the night before the squall. She paid me." He jerked his chin at Maren without turning. "Paid me to slow us down so the permits would lapse. Paid me again at the trading post to signal her position. Every signal. Every delay. It was me."

The rain filled the silence.

Oscar hadn't moved. Maren watched him with the detached fascination of a woman observing a structure take a load it was never designed for—the stillness spreading through him, the jaw tightening, the eyes going somewhere flat and far away. She'd seen that look once before, on the night fifteen years ago when word came downriver that the Halcyon had sunk, and a boy named Oscar had stood on the Port Serevan docks hearing the news and made himself into something that could survive it.

"Dario," Oscar said at last, and his voice was terribly quiet. "Get off my boat."

"Captain—"

"Get off my boat." Louder now, cracking across the mudflat. "You cut my hull with my crew sleeping above the waterline. You could have killed every man aboard. Ilsa can't swim. You knew that. You knew it and you took her money anyway."

"I know." Dario's voice broke completely. He fumbled at his belt and pulled free a leather pouch, heavy with coin, and held it out with both hands like an offering at an altar. "Take it back. Take all of it. Put me off here if you want—maroon me, report me at the post, I'll stand whatever court you want. But let me make it right first. Captain, please. There's still time. Three days on the permits. Let me make it right."

"How?" Oscar's laugh was terrible. "How exactly does a man make right cutting his own ship's throat?"

Dario looked at him for a long moment. Then he turned and pointed—at the Corvus, riding fat and confident over the wreck site, her four anchors strung out like a spider claiming a web.

"Her anchors," he said. "All four, marked on her cable buoys. I've been watching her since she dropped. I know her anchor watch rotation—I trained two of those men myself, back when I ran cargo for Kessler Lines before you hired me. I know when they sleep, when they drink, when the fog comes off that water thick enough to hide a longboat." His hand was shaking, but his voice had steadied into something Maren recognized with a cold drop in her stomach: the voice of a man who had decided. "Give me a longboat and a dark night, captain, and I'll cut every line she has. Leave her swinging helpless on the flood while the Marigold takes position over the wreck. You want me to make it right?" His eyes were wet and blazing. "Let me cut her loose."

Maren felt the plan turn over in her chest like a fish showing its belly. She'd accounted for everything—the contract, the fine print, the delays, the divers, the weather. She had not accounted for guilt. Guilt was the one current on this river she'd never charted, and it was running hard against her now, written all over the face of the man she'd bought.

"Fairweather," she said sharply. "Don't listen to him. The man's a traitor twice over—today to me, tomorrow to you. Anything he offers is worth exactly what you've already paid for it."

"He's worth more than that," Oscar said, not taking his eyes off Dario. "He's worth the truth. That's more than anyone else on this river has given me lately." He looked at her then, and there was something new in his face that made her take an involuntary half-step back in the wet sand. Not rage. Something much worse. Pity. "You came all this way, Maren. Fifteen years, a steamer, a bought first mate, a forged commission. All for a hull full of silt and a dead man's paperwork."

"The deed," Maren said, and heard her own voice go hungry, and hated it. "Your father sank the Halcyon with the original concession deed aboard. My father spent thirty years and everything he owned trying to prove that claim. I'm finishing his work."

"Your father swindled half the families on this river," Oscar said flatly. "My father died rather than let him have it. Whatever you think you're finishing, Maren, it isn't his work. It's his greed."

For a moment—one raw, unguarded moment—something moved behind Maren's eyes that looked almost like pain. Then it was gone, sealed away behind twenty years of practiced composure, and she was the iron merchant's daughter again, the woman who'd built a shipping line out of her father's ruins.

"Clause eleven," she said softly. "Read it tonight, Oscar. Read every word. Then decide whether sentiment is worth more to you than the law." She turned to her men. "Back to the launch. We've wasted enough tide."

She paused at the waterline, one foot in the launch, and looked back at the Marigold heeled over on her mudflat, at the ragged crew lining her rails, at Dario Voss standing in the rain with a pouch of blood money still extended in hands that wouldn't stop shaking.

"Three days, Fairweather," she called. "Then the crown takes everything, and neither of us gets anything. Think about whose patience is longer."

The launch pulled away. Maren did not look back again, because looking back was a weakness, and because she already knew—with the sick certainty of a woman who had just watched her own weapon turn in her hand—that the next time she saw Oscar Fairweather, he would be standing over her anchors' cut cables, and the race would already be lost.

She just didn't yet know how badly.

* * *

That night, aboard the Corvus, Maren stood at her cabin window with a glass of brandy she wasn't drinking and watched the fog come off the drowned reach in slow white ribbons.

Below, on the black water, a longboat slipped out from the shadow of the Marigold's hull. Four shapes at the oars, pulling easy, making no wake. It turned upstream, toward the Corvus's stern anchor buoy, and vanished into the fog.

Maren set down the glass. She was moving for the door before the sound reached her—the small, distinct, unmistakable splash of a cable being cut.

"BRANDT!"
