## 17. The Letter in the Oilskin

The letter sat on the chart table for two days before Oscar touched it.

He'd carried it up from the Halcyon's cabin wrapped in its oilskin like something still breathing. He'd set it down between the depth soundings and the tin of spare pencils and then he'd walked away from it the way you walk away from a dog you know will bite. Twice he'd crossed the Marigold's deck in the rain to check the pumps. Three times he'd checked Dario's knots on the mooring lines. Once he'd stood at the rail for the better part of an hour watching the brown water slide past, doing nothing, which was the one thing he had never been good at.

Ilsa let him alone. She was good at that too—better than he'd ever given her credit for. She worked at the aft table with her charts and her ink-stained fingers, saying nothing, but every time he looked over she was looking at him, and not the way she looked at currents. The way you look at a man standing on ice you're not sure will hold.

On the second night the rain eased to a whisper and the river went quiet in that way it did between squalls, holding its breath, and Oscar found himself alone in the wheelhouse with the packet and no excuse left in the world.

*Cornered,* he thought. *By a dead man's handwriting.*

He picked it up. The oilskin was stiff with fifteen years of cold water and had gone the color of old teeth. His father had wrapped it well—three folds, wax-sealed at each corner, the kind of wrapping a man does when he means whatever's inside to outlast him. Which meant his father had known. Had known the night he tied off at the drowned reach, had known what was coming, had sat down in that little cabin with the lamp turned low and written to a son who wasn't there.

Oscar had been nineteen. He'd been forty miles downstream, drinking bad rum and arguing with a girl whose name he could no longer remember, while his father wrote him a letter by lamplight and then walked his own ship into the deep water on purpose.

His hands were steady. That surprised him. Fifteen years of running had taught his hands to be steady at exactly the moments they shouldn't be.

He broke the first wax seal.

* * *

The paper inside was dry. Dry as dust, dry as bone, and when he unfolded it, a smell came off it—not rot, not river, but lamp oil and tobacco, the smell of the Halcyon's cabin, the smell of his childhood, so sudden and complete that he had to put the page down flat on the chart table and stand there with both hands gripping the edge until the wheelhouse stopped tilting.

Then he read.

*Oscar.*

*If you are reading this, then you went down to me after all, and I am sorry for it, because I hoped you would stay away. I hoped you would hate me instead. Hate is lighter cargo than grief. I have carried both, and I know the difference.*

*I will be brief, because the water is coming and because you were never a boy for long speeches.*

*You know the story they will tell: that the Kesslers took our concession fair, that I drank the Halcyon onto the bar, that Fairweather luck ran out the way Fairweather luck always runs. Let them tell it. But you are my blood, and blood deserves the truth even when truth is worth nothing.*

*Here is the truth. Aldous Kessler has spent four years buying the river out from under the families on it. The Voss leases. The Amari tolls. Half the trading posts between Serevan Bend and the falls—he holds their paper, and where he couldn't buy, he forged, and where he couldn't forge, he burned. I have seen the ledgers. I have names. When the crown auditors finally come—and they will come, because men like Aldous always overreach—I want the record to exist somewhere he cannot reach it.*

*But more than that: he wants the concession. Our concession. Father's grant, sealed under the old charter, covering the drowned reach and every landing on it. He offered me three times its value last spring. I told him no. Last month he stopped offering and started threatening, and I understood that the concession would leave this family one way or another—either through my signature or through my corpse.*

*So I chose a third way.*

*A deed recovered by a blood heir is honored under the permit law. You know this—it is why the old families guard their papers like relics. A deed lost with a ship may be claimed by the heir who recovers it, within the term of the permits, before salvage rights revert to the crown. Aldous knows it too. It is why he cannot simply kill me and take the papers. It is why he needs the wreck to stay lost until the permits lapse and the claim dies with them.*

*So tonight I am going to make sure it stays lost—but only for a while. Only long enough for you.*

*I have sealed the original deed in the strongbox and welded it shut. The manifest fragment in the cabin will say enough to bring a diver to the hold, but not enough to tell him what he's found. And I am going to take the Halcyon into the deep channel myself, at flood, and put her down on the silt where only a Fairweather would think to look.*

*They will call it drink. They will call it bad luck. Let them. A man who scuttles his own ship to keep a promise is not a drunkard, Oscar. He is a vault.*

*Ninety days before the permits expire, go to the port authority at Serevan Bend and file your intent as heir. Do it quietly. Then come find her. She'll be wedged in the silt below the second bluff, bow upstream, the way I'll leave her. The strongbox is under the floor plates aft of the galley. Bring a cold chisel and don't trust anyone whose family name ends in Kessler.*

*I know what I'm asking. I'm asking you to stop running, and I know better than anyone that stopping is the hardest thing a Fairweather ever does. Your mother used to say we were born with the horizon in our pockets and never learned to spend it. She was right. But listen to me, boy: the river doesn't want you moving forever. The river wants you keeping what's yours.*

*One more thing, and then I'll light the lamp and get underway.*

*Whatever you find down there, whatever this costs you—the years, the anger, the name you've been hiding behind other boats and other rivers—none of it was wasted. A charge kept alive is not a debt. It's a fire banked in the ash, waiting for someone with the sense to blow on it.*

*Blow, son.*

*Your father,*
*Edmund Fairweather, Master of the Halcyon*

*P.S. — If Maren Kessler comes to you with a contract, read the fine print twice. Her father taught her arithmetic before he taught her kindness, and I never saw evidence of the second lesson.*

* * *

Oscar read it again. Then a third time, slower, the way you sound a channel you mean to run at night.

Outside, the rain had thickened again, drumming on the wheelhouse roof, and the lantern swung gently with the Marigold's roll, throwing his shadow back and forth across the chart table like a man pacing.

Fifteen years.

Fifteen years he had built his whole life on the story the river told—that Edmund Fairweather had been proud, and proud men founder; that the Halcyon had gone down heavy and drunk and unlucky; that the best thing a son could do was become a different kind of man entirely, one who never owned anything, never moored anywhere, never gave the river—or anyone—a chance to take something that mattered.

And all that time his father had been lying on the bottom of the drowned reach with his hands on the brass, holding position. Holding the line. Not a drowning man. A sentry.

"God," Oscar said aloud, and his voice cracked on it. "You stubborn old bastard."

He laughed, or something near it, and wiped his face with the heel of his hand, and found his eyes were wet, which hadn't happened since the funeral that wasn't—since the empty coffin the crown had provided, since the handful of rivermen who'd bothered to come, since Dario at seventeen standing at the back with his cap in his hands.

Dario.

The name landed like a lead weight. He thought of the midriver trading post, of Dario coming back aboard with wet cuffs and a smile that didn't fit his face. He thought of the caulking cut clean as a surgeon's line. He thought of Maren Kessler's contract, and the fine print he'd skimmed in the tavern because he'd been too busy being angry to be careful.

*Read the fine print twice.*

He'd read it once. He'd signed it once. And somewhere in that contract, folded into language dull enough to sleep through, Maren Kessler might already own everything he'd just inherited.

No. Not yet. Maybe not at all. He didn't know, and not knowing was its own kind of drowning.

What he knew was this: the deed existed. The strongbox existed. The law existed, and the permits expired in days, and the clock that had chased him for fifteen years without ever quite catching him had finally, finally shown him its face—and it wasn't the face of a debt collector. It was the face of his father, patient at the wheel, waiting for him to arrive.

It had never been a burden. All those years of running, he'd thought he was fleeing a wreck. He'd actually been carrying a charge—one he hadn't known he held, passed down in blood if not in words, kept alive by a dead man's stubbornness and a living man's refusal to stop moving. Every mile he'd run had kept him alive to stand here. Every port he'd fled had taught him water he now needed. Even the running had been service, though he'd never enlisted.

The river wanted him keeping what was his.

Oscar folded the letter along its old creases, careful, reverent, the way you fold a flag. He put it inside his coat, against his chest, where it lay warm and light as a coal.

Then he stepped out into the rain.

Ilsa was at the aft table under the awning, hunched over her charts, and she looked up when the hatch banged, and whatever she saw in his face made her put down her pen.

"It's done," he said. "The deed's real. My father sank the Halcyon himself—with the concession papers welded in the strongbox—to keep them from Aldous Kessler." He crossed the deck, rain streaming off him, and gripped the back of the chair across from her. "The permit law honors a deed recovered by a blood heir. That's me. That was always me."

Isla stared at him, and then, slowly, she smiled—the first unguarded smile he'd ever seen her wear, wide and fierce and lit from underneath. "Oscar. That's—you. You're not the salvager. You're the *claimant.*"

"I'm the fool who signed a contract with the daughter of the man he stole it from," Oscar said. "I need to see that contract. Tonight. Every clause, every sub-clause, every word in letters too small to read by lamplight."

"You think there's something in it?"

"My father warned me about Kessler fine print from the bottom of the river," Oscar said. "I intend to find out whether Maren learned the family trade." He was already moving toward the locker where the ship's papers lived, pulling the contract free, spreading it on the chart table beside her half-finished survey of the reach. "Get your magnifier. Get your lamp. You read faster than I do."

She was already reaching for the lamp. "And if she owns it?"

Oscar looked at the contract, at the dense gray columns of legalese waiting there like silt over a wreck, and felt something in his chest settle into place—something that had been loose and rattling for fifteen years.

"Then we've got three days to find the flaw in it," he said. "Same as finding a channel through a sandbar. There's always water, Ilsa. You just have to know where to look."

He bent over the first page, and outside the rain came down on the drowned reach like applause.
