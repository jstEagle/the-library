## 18. The Race Against Expiry

**Oscar**

Three days.

Oscar Fairweather stood at the Marigold's rail and watched the rain come down in gray sheets over the drowned reach, and he did the arithmetic the way his father used to do it—on his fingers, out loud, so the river could hear him and hold him to it.

"Three days on the permits. Two days of air left in the divers' tanks if Kessler's engineer is honest, which she isn't. One strongbox welded shut at forty feet." He flexed his fingers. "And one patched hull that wants to die."

The Marigold sat at anchor a hundred yards upstream of the wreck site, riding low even for her. The recaulking on the mudflat had saved her life but not her dignity; the new seams wept a slow, shameful trickle that Dario pumped out every hour like a man bailing guilt. Below them, visible only as a darker stain in the brown water, lay the Halcyon—and inside the Halcyon, wedged in fifteen years of silt, the strongbox the divers had found with their fingertips and could not open with their hands.

Kessler's steamer, the *Vantage*, lay anchored two hundred yards downstream. Close enough that Oscar could see the yellow lamps of her wheelhouse through the rain. Close enough to hear her generators humming all night, charging the lights and pumps and the compressor lines that fed her divers.

She had arrived with the falling rain three days ago, waving his own signature at him across the water. *Salvage value split per contract.* And then, when he'd torn the contract up and thrown the pieces into the river, she'd smiled the way her father must have smiled, and said, "Fine print, Oscar. I never needed your cooperation. Only your knowledge of the reach."

She'd had it now for three days. Her divers had been down four times.

His had been down once.

"Captain." Ilsa came up beside him, hooded, her charts wrapped in oilcloth under her arm like a swaddled child. She didn't look at him. They were still repairing that—the space between them where the thrown charts used to be. "The current's shifted overnight. The flood crest from the high valleys will pass through tomorrow midday. After that, another rise."

"Which means?"

"Which means the silt around the wreck is going to move. Either it scours away and gives you clean access—" she turned to look at him, rain running off her chin, "—or it buries the Halcyon past anyone's reach, forever. Kessler's engineer knows it too. That's why she's diving in this weather. She's not careful. She's desperate."

Oscar watched the Vantage's deck lights blur in the rain. Desperate people made mistakes. He'd built fifteen years of a life on that principle.

"Then we don't need to be better than her," he said slowly. "We need to be luckier than her, once, at the right moment."

"That's not a plan, Oscar. That's a prayer."

"It's both. It's what my father would have called it." He looked at her then, and something passed between them that wasn't forgiveness exactly—it was heavier than that, more useful. "Get me every sounding you've taken since we anchored. Every reading. If the river's going to hand us one clean hour, I want to know which hour it is."

* * *

**Dario**

Dario Vasquez hadn't slept properly in nine days, and the last time he had, it was because he'd drunk himself under the galley table.

He kept the money in a tin beneath his bunk. Forty pieces of gold, Kessler's agent's price for delay—not even a fortune, that was the humiliating part. A debt's worth. A coward's wage. He'd counted it every night since he'd taken it, as if counting might change what it was, and every night it stayed the same: the exact weight of his name turning rotten in his mouth.

Tonight he took the tin out and carried it up on deck.

The rain had thinned to a drizzle. Downstream, the Vantage glowed like a chapel. Dario stood at the stern rail a long time, turning the tin over in his hands, listening to the river move beneath him—dark, patient, indifferent to what men owed each other.

He thought about the night the Marigold's caulking was cut. He hadn't done it. He wanted that recorded somewhere, in some ledger that mattered: *Dario Vasquez, first mate, thief of wages, did not cut the caulking.* But he knew who had—one of the deckhands Kessler had bought back before Oscar ever signed on, a quiet man named Pell who'd slipped ashore at the trading post and never come back. Dario had known and said nothing, because saying anything would have meant explaining why he understood exactly how a man got bought.

He thought about Oscar barring the way at the mutiny, arms spread, half-mad with exhaustion, saying *nobody leaves this boat while I'm standing on it* —and meaning, underneath it, in the language they'd spoken for ten years without words: *don't leave me.*

He thought about Ilsa talking them all down off the rail with a plan drawn in rain on a hatch cover, and how she'd looked at him afterward—not with contempt, which he could have borne, but with the patient attention of someone charting a reef. Marking him. Waiting to see if he'd wreck himself or steer clear.

The tin was cold in his hands.

"Well," Dario said aloud, to the river, to the tin, to whatever part of himself was still worth saving. "Debts get called in eventually."

He went below and woke the two crewmen he trusted—old Tomas, who'd fished the Serevan before Oscar was born, and the boy Fen, who idolized the captain with the pure stupid heat of the young. He told them everything. The money. The agent. Pell. All of it, laid out on the galley table between the tin and a lamp, ugly and complete.

Tomas listened with his arms folded. When Dario finished, the old man spat into the bilge sump, which was as close to absolution as the Marigold's company ever came.

"And what do you want to do about it?" Tomas asked.

"The Vantage sits on four anchors," Dario said. "Two bow, two stern. Her compressor line runs to the dive stage on the port side. If those anchors went loose in a rising flood—with the current shifting tomorrow midday—she'd swing downstream. Off the wreck. Maybe hard aground on the tail shoal."

Fen's eyes had gone wide and bright. "You want to cut her loose."

"I want," Dario said carefully, "to make sure her anchors are properly maintained. And if they happen to fail, well." He picked up the tin of blood money and weighed it in his palm one last time. "Rivers are careless things."

* * *

**Maren**

Maren Kessler slept badly aboard the Vantage, and woke at the third bell to the sound of her engineer shouting.

She came up the companionway with her revolver already in her hand—an old habit from her father's stories of the river wars—and found the aft deck in chaos. Rain. Lanterns swinging. Men hauling on lines that suddenly had nothing at their ends.

Both stern anchors. Gone. Not dragged, not fouled—*gone*, the cables cut clean an inch above the shackles, the shackles themselves still shackle-shaped and useless on the seabed. The Vantage swung lazily, ponderously, on her remaining bow anchors, her stern walking downstream with each pulse of the flood current, and the dive stage on her port side was dragging through water that grew shallower by the minute.

"Tail shoal," her engineer said grimly, pointing. Ahead and to starboard, the brown water wrinkled over the long spine of gravel that guarded the reach's lower channel. "Twenty minutes, maybe thirty, if the current keeps building. We have to run out the kedge and warp off, or we ground."

"Then warp off," Maren snapped. "And put a diver down on my anchors—I want to see those cuts."

"You'll see them from the surface, ma'am. Nobody's diving tonight. Current's wrong, visibility's zero, and the compressor intake is half-swamped as it is."

She stood in the rain and did the arithmetic herself, because there was no one else she trusted to do it. Warp off the shoal: two hours minimum, and by then the flood crest would be through, and the silt she'd spent three days and a small fortune mapping would be moving like a landslide. Dive the wreck again: impossible until the water settled, which might be days, which might be never, because the permits expired in—

She checked her watch against the lantern light.

Sixty-two hours.

Downstream of her, barely visible through the rain, the Marigold rode at anchor with her single riding light burning steady as a votive candle. Maren stared at that light for a long moment, and felt something cold settle in her chest that had nothing to do with the weather.

Her father had taught her the first rule of salvage on her twelfth birthday, drawing in spilled sugar on a tavern table: *the wreck belongs to whoever holds position over it.* Not whoever found it. Not whoever owned it. Whoever held position.

"Ma'am?" the engineer said. "Orders?"

Maren closed her fist around the watch chain. Somewhere out in the dark, someone had just spent their soul to cut her anchors, and she found—this was the strange part, the part she'd examine later, much later, in the privacy of losing—that she couldn't even entirely hate them for it. It was a riverman's move. It was her father's kind of move.

"Run out the kedge," she said. "Warp us clear of the shoal. And wake the agent who paid our friend Vasquez." Her voice came out flat and cold and very much her father's daughter. "I want to know if he sold us once or twice."

* * *

**Oscar**

They came for him at dawn—or rather, Dario came for him at dawn, soaked to the skin, hollow-eyed, carrying a tin of gold and a confession that poured out of him like the bilge water he'd been pumping for a week.

Oscar let him finish. All of it: the debt, the agent, the payment, the silence about Pell, and finally the anchors, the cuts, the Vantage swinging loose on the flood.

When it was done, the two men stood in the grey light of the wheelhouse and the rain ticked against the glass, and Oscar found that the anger he'd expected—the hot, clean anger of betrayal—wasn't there. In its place was something more tired and more terrible: recognition. Fifteen years ago, a man had looked at the same river and the same ruinous arithmetic and chosen to sink rather than surrender. Dario had just chosen, in his own small drowning way, to cut himself loose from the sinking thing.

"You could have cost us the boat," Oscar said quietly. "If Kessler had grounded on the shoal and lost her whole stern section, and the inquiry traced it to this crew—"

"She'd have taken the concession anyway. You know that. This way—" Dario set the tin on the chart table and pushed it across. "This way it's paid back. All of it. And if you want my papers and my pay and my hide off this boat at the next landing, Captain, I'll go without a word."

Oscar looked at the tin. Forty pieces of gold, bought with betrayal, returned with sweat and cut hands and a night's work in freezing rain. Debts called in. Courage drawn from the unlikeliest well on the river.

He pushed the tin back.

"Keep it," he said. "Put it in the crew fund. Call it hazard pay for anchor maintenance." He held Dario's gaze until the man's face cracked—shame and relief and something dangerously close to tears all fighting for room. "You're first mate of this boat until you're dead or worse, Dario. Now get forward. Ilsa says the crest comes through at midday, and when it does, the silt over that strongbox is going to move. I intend to be sitting directly over the Halcyon when it does."

* * *

**Ilsa**

Ilsa had charted rivers on three continents, and she had never seen one change its mind the way the Serevan changed its mind at midday.

It began as a sound—a low, vast groan rolling up the valley like the mountain clearing its throat. Then the water rose, not gradually but in a single sustained shrug, a foot in an hour, and the color of it changed from brown to the pale ochre of new-cut earth, and the whole drowned reach became a moving thing, a single animal turning over in its sleep.

On the bottom, she knew, the silt was lifting. Fifteen years of settled grave-dirt peeling away from the Halcyon's hull in slow, suffocating clouds, scouring some places bare and burying others deep. A window. An hour, maybe two, where the old wreck lay exposed to any diver bold enough to drop into water moving hard enough to strip paint.

Kessler's steamer was warping frantically off the tail shoal, her kedge line taut as a harp string, her dive gear useless in the current.

The Marigold, patched and leaking and game, weighed anchor and moved.

Oscar conned her himself, standing in the wheelhouse doorway with rain streaming off him, calling depths to Fen at the winch, and Ilsa stood beside him with her sounding board braced against the rail, calling the current's moods back to him. They worked without ceremony now, the two of them—a shorthand built in squalls and silences, her numbers meeting his instincts halfway, the boat sliding downriver on the flood with the current instead of fighting it, exactly the way the river wanted to carry her.

"Mark!" Ilsa called, watching her lead-line. "Thirty feet. Twenty-eight. Bottom's hardening—we're coming onto the wreck shelf."

"Hold us here." Oscar's voice was calm in a way she recognized now as its opposite. "Fen—second anchor out, astern. Let's sit on her proper."

By two o'clock the Marigold lay moored fore and aft directly above the Halcyon, rocking in the flood surge, her shadow falling across the place where a strongbox waited with a deed inside it and sixty hours left on the world's patience. Downstream, the Vantage finally swung free of the shoal and began working her way back up against the current—hours behind, engines laboring, too late to dive today.

Ilsa found Oscar at the stern rail, watching the water that covered his father's grave.

"They'll be back at first light," she said. "With everything she has."

"I know."

"And the box is welded shut. Even if your divers reach it—"

"My father was a fairweather in every sense except his name," Oscar said. He turned to her, and the rain ran down his face, and beneath the exhaustion she saw something lit and steady, a pilot's light burning in bad weather. "He sank that boat on purpose, Ilsa. A man who plans a sinking plans for the opening of it. Whatever's holding that box shut, he built it to be opened—by the right person, at the right time." He looked back down at the brown, roaring water. "Tomorrow I'm going down myself. And I'm going to find out what my father wanted me to know."

Below them, unseen, the river went on rising toward the horizon, carrying the monsoon in from the sea—and somewhere in its grip, forty feet down, the Halcyon waited with her secret coming loose at last.
