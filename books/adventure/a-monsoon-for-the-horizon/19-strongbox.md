## 19. Strongbox

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and the silence it left behind was worse than the storm. Oscar stood at the Marigold's rail and watched the drowned reach go gray and then gold, the water so still it looked poured, and three days left on the permits ticking down somewhere behind his ribs like a second heart.

Kessler's steamer, the *Vantage*, sat anchored two hundred yards downstream, her lamps burning all night like a challenge no one had spoken aloud. Her divers had been in the water since first light yesterday. They'd come up with silt and nothing else.

Oscar's divers knew where to look.

"Strongbox is at thirty-one feet," Dario said, coming up beside him. His voice was rough from three nights of little sleep and one act of sabotage he hadn't yet been forgiven for—only deferred, which Oscar suspected weighed heavier. "Welded shut. Wedged under the Halcyon's forward hold beam, right where your father would have put it. Where nobody would find it without tearing the ship apart."

"My father didn't want it found," Oscar said.

"No," Dario agreed. "He wanted it *kept*."

Below, on the main deck, Ilsa was checking the dive lines, coiling rope with quick precise hands, her charts weighted under a stone against the wind that wasn't blowing yet but would. She'd barely slept either. None of them had. The whole boat felt like a held breath.

Oscar went down to her.

"The Vantage will run her divers again at sunup," he said. "We go now, in the dark, while their lamps are still lit and their men are still eating."

Ilsa looked up. Ink stains still lived in the creases of her fingers, even after weeks of river water; he found himself looking for them the way other men looked for omens. "The current under the hull is bad this time of day. Cold shock, zero visibility if you stir the silt."

"I know."

"You're not twenty-five anymore, Oscar."

"No," he said. "I'm something better. I'm a man who knows exactly what he's diving for."

She held his gaze a moment longer than the conversation required, and whatever passed between them needed no chart. Then she nodded and reached for her own dive suit.

"You're coming down?" he said.

"It's my map that found the box. And you'll want someone up there who can read a lock mechanism when your hands are too numb to think." She pulled the suit's collar closed. "Besides. If Kessler's men come early, somebody has to be underwater to hear them coming."

---

The Halcyon lay on her starboard side in forty feet of brown water, wedged into the old channel where the flood of fifteen years ago had dropped her, and the strongbox sat in the shadow of her forward hold like a heart that had never stopped beating.

Oscar went down the line hand over hand, the cold closing around him like a fist, and the wreck emerged from the dark by degrees—first the rail, furred with weed, then the tilted deck, then the black rectangle of the hold opening beneath him like a mouth.

Ilsa came down beside him, her lamp beam cutting a cone through the murk. She moved well for a cartographer. She moved like someone who had decided fear was a luxury.

The box was exactly as the divers described: iron, man-sized in ambition though small enough for two men to carry, welded along every seam with beads of metal gone smooth with fifteen years of silt. Oscar ran his gloves over the welds. Even underwater, even through leather, he could feel the deliberateness of them. His father had done this work himself, or paid someone who understood that the job was forever.

Ilsa's lamp found the hasp. There—a slot cut into the weld, narrow, deliberate. Not a flaw. An entrance.

"He left a way in," she signaled, tapping the slot, then pointing at the padlock hanging through it, corroded green but intact.

Oscar stared at it. Fifteen years he'd assumed the Halcyon went down screaming. But here was patience. Here was a man with time to weld a box shut and leave one careful door in it—and only one person that door could be waiting for.

*A blood heir,* the letter had said. *The law honors what blood recovers.*

His father had built him a door and waited fifteen years for him to knock.

Oscar took out the cold chisel and hammer they'd brought down lashed to the line, set the chisel against the padlock's shackle, and swung.

Underwater, sound traveled strangely—the blows came back to him through his own bones, through the water, through the dead ship all around them, and he had the mad impression that the whole reach heard it, that his father heard it, wherever fathers went when their boats sank under them. The shackle was soft with corrosion. On the fourth blow it gave with a crack he felt more than heard, and the padlock dropped away into the silt, turning slowly, like something finally allowed to rest.

But the welds above the slot were another matter. The door itself wouldn't move until the seam around it was opened, and the seam was iron, and iron underwater yields to nothing but time.

Ilsa solved it. She'd brought the long drift-pin from the Marigold's tool locker, and she set it in the seam and leaned her whole weight against it, and Oscar hammered the pin deeper, inch by inch, the two of them working in the cone of lamplight like the last two people in the world, breath rasping through regulators, hands going from pain to numbness to something beyond both.

The seam split with a groan that rolled through the wreck.

And then Ilsa's lamp swung sharply toward the open water beyond the hold, and she grabbed his arm.

Through the murk, far off but unmistakable: lights. Two of them, moving. Coming down the line from the direction of the Vantage.

*Kessler's divers.*

They had maybe two minutes.

Oscar put his shoulder against the box lid and heaved. It ground open an inch. Silt belched out of the gap in a choking cloud, and inside—

Inside was oilskin. Bundles of it, tied with tarred cord, packed tight as a nest.

He tore one bundle free and shoved it inside his suit, against his chest, and Ilsa did the same with a second, and then the lights were close enough to show shapes—two divers in Vantage gear, lamps swinging toward the glow of theirs—and Oscar made the only decision there was to make.

He kicked for the surface, dragging Ilsa with him, leaving the box gaping and empty of everything that mattered.

---

They broke the surface gasping, and the Marigold's crew hauled them over the rail, and behind them the water boiled as Kessler's divers surfaced too, finding an open box and nothing in it, and Oscar heard—actually heard—one of them shout across the water, furious, the words torn apart by distance.

"Deck," Oscar said, spitting river water. "Now. Everybody."

Dario had the crew mustered before Oscar finished wringing out his sleeves. Downstream, the Vantage was lowering a boat. Men in it. Armed, probably—it was Maren Kessler, and she hadn't come this far to lose gracefully.

"They can't take it off us in the water," Ilsa said, peeling back her hood, hair plastered to her face, eyes bright as a woman who had just robbed a grave and loved it. "Whatever's in those bundles—if it's what we think it is, it needs witnesses. Legal ones. Not a fight."

"The trading post," Oscar said. "Hollis keeps a ledger and a stamp and six bargemen who saw everything that's happened on this reach for a month. Get us there."

"We're aground on a mudflat, Captain," Dario said gently.

"Then get us *off*."

What followed was ninety minutes of the ugliest seamanship of Oscar Fairweather's life. They rocked the Marigold free with the tide, ran her half-flooded down the reach with the pump going and every soul aboard bailing, while the Vantage's launch cut the water behind them, closing, closing—and then Hollis's trading post swung into view around the bend, its dock crowded with bargemen drawn out by the commotion, and the launch, seeing witnesses, sheered off.

The Marigold hit the dock hard enough to crack a fender. Oscar didn't care. He came ashore with the oilskin bundles dripping under his arm and Ilsa at his shoulder and half the river watching.

Maren Kessler stepped off her launch at the dock's far end anyway, because of course she did. She walked toward him through the crowd with her chin up and her hands empty, and stopped ten feet away, and said, "You don't even know what you're carrying."

"I know whose it is."

"Salvage law says—"

"The permit law," Oscar said, and his voice carried, because fifteen years of shouting over engines had taught him how, "says a concession deed recovered by its blood heir stands above any salvage claim, any contract, any fine print Maren Kessler ever wrote." He untied the tarred cord with fingers that shook—from cold, he told himself, only cold—and unrolled the oilskin.

The deed was there. His father's name at the top. *Fairweather River Concession, entire reach, Serevan headwaters to the drowned valley.* The seal intact. The ink legible after fifteen years underwater, because his father had wrapped it the way he'd done everything—carefully, stubbornly, waiting.

Oscar held it up over his head, dripping, and turned slowly so the whole dock could see it, and the bargemen of Hollis's post—six of them, seven, more, men who had watched the Kesslers squeeze this river for a generation—began, quietly, to applaud.

Maren Kessler stood very still in the middle of it. Whatever she'd expected—fight, flight, fury—this quiet wasn't it. He watched something move behind her face, something older than rivalry.

"You could have told me," she said, low enough that only he heard. "Fifteen years, Oscar. You could have just *told me* what he did."

"And you'd have believed me?"

She didn't answer. She looked at the deed, at the crowd, at Ilsa standing at his side with ink-stained fingers and river-soaked charts, and then she turned and walked back to her launch, and the crowd parted for her, and let her go.

Oscar lowered the deed. His arms were shaking now for real. Ilsa took one end of the oilskin without being asked, sharing its weight, and stood close enough that their shoulders touched, and neither of them said anything about it, because everything worth saying had already been said somewhere between thirty-one feet down and the surface, in cold water, without words.

Dario appeared at his elbow, hat in his hands. "Captain. The permits. Three days—you'll want Hollis to witness the registration before—"

"Hollis," Oscar called, "get your ledger. And somebody find me a pen that works after a flood."

The bargemen laughed. Somewhere upstream, the rain was already gathering again, dark clouds stacking over the horizon like a fleet coming home.

Oscar looked at them and, for the first time in fifteen years, didn't look away.

* * *

But Maren Kessler did not weigh anchor. Her steamer sat in the reach all night, lamps burning, and at dawn she sent a single boat to the Marigold with a sealed envelope and no message—and inside, in her sharp upright hand, a request for one hour of his time before he signed anything, and a sentence that stopped his breath:

*There are things about the night the Halcyon sank that your father's letter does not say. I know because my father was there.*
