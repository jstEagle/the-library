## 20. What Maren Carries Home

Maren Kessler had rehearsed this moment for eleven years, and in every version of it, she was not the one standing on a mudflat with rain running off her chin like she'd forgotten how to close her mouth.

The *Corvane* sat at anchor fifty yards out, her steam gone cold in the boiler, her crew lined along the rail with the particular stillness of men watching their employer lose. On the mudflat itself, the Fairweather salvage camp had become something Maren hadn't planned for: a town. The beached and recaulked *Marigold* gleamed wet under the tarpaulins. Cook fires smoked under oilcloth shelters. And at the center of it all, Oscar Fairweather stood holding a sheet of vellum aloft like a man displaying a fish he'd caught fair, while witnesses from the trading post—two clerks, a customs runner, and old Sef the fuel-barge pilot, who would talk about this until he died—pressed close to see the seal.

The blood-heir clause. She'd read it herself, years ago, in the annexes of the concession code, and dismissed it as a curiosity. A deed recovered by blood heir supersedes all lapsed claims. She'd assumed the deed was ash, or silt, or legend.

Instead it was in Oscar's hand, and the clerk from the trading post was writing down the time and date in a ledger that would outlive them both.

"You'll want to countersign," the clerk said to her, not unkindly. "As contesting party. It closes the record."

Maren signed. Her signature came out steady, which surprised her. Eleven years of building the Kessler line into something her father would have recognized, and it ended with her handwriting looking exactly like his.

* * *

They gave her the cook shelter because there was nowhere else to put her, and because Ilsa—the cartographer, the stowaway, the woman who had played both sides of this river better than Maren ever had—insisted on it with a look that brooked no argument. Rain hammered the oilcloth overhead. Someone had left tea, real tea, and Maren held the cup without drinking it, watching the water bead and run down the canvas seam like the river trying to get in one more time.

Oscar came in alone. He'd changed into dry clothes but hadn't bothered with dryness beyond that; his hair stuck up where he'd towelled it once and given up. He looked, she thought, tired in a way that had nothing to do with the night's work. Fifteen years of running had ended last night in a flooded hold, and the man standing in front of her was still deciding what shape he was without the running.

"Fairweather," she said.

"Kessler." He sat across from her, unhurried, and poured himself tea from the same pot. "You cut Dario loose before you came upstream."

"I did. He's on your side of the water now. Consider him your problem."

"He confessed. Night before you arrived. Offered to make it right." Oscar turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "He cut your anchor lines. That's how we got position on the wreck."

"I know. I watched him do it." She finally drank the tea. It was oversteeped and bitter and perfect. "I've been paying that man's debts for two years, Fairweather. Did you know that? Every port between here and Vell's Landing, some creditor or other comes aboard asking after Dario Marchetti, and I settle it, because a first mate who's scared is a first mate who's mine." She set the cup down. "Turns out scared men aren't anyone's. They're just scared."

"Is that why you're here? To tell me about Dario?"

"No." She looked at the rain. At the fire. At the man who had beaten her, and who was sitting across from her drinking bad tea instead of gloating, which was somehow worse than gloating, because it meant he'd already stopped thinking of her as an enemy, and enemies were simple. "I'm here because the clerk asked me if I intended to file an appeal, and I found I didn't have an answer."

"The deed's valid. You read it."

"I read it." Her father's voice lived in those words—*read it yourself, Maren, never trust a man's summary of a document*—and she pushed past it. "The deed is valid. The clause is valid. Your claim is clean, and my lawyers could find nothing in three days of telegraphed argument, because there is nothing to find. Aldous Fairweather sank his own ship rather than let my father take what he'd swindled half the river to reach." She laughed, short and ugly. "Do you know what the funny part is? If he'd surrendered it—if he'd just handed over the concession in '09—my father would have run it into the ground inside five years. He was brilliant at acquisition and hopeless at keeping anything alive. Everything he bought, he bought to bury the last thing he'd lost."

Oscar said nothing. He was good at that, she remembered. Fifteen years ago, two young pilots racing cargo up the Serevan, they'd talked for hours about nothing, and even then he'd known when silence was the more generous offer.

"My father died ruined," she said. It came out flat, factual, the way she'd practiced saying it so it wouldn't come out any other way. "Not poor—ruined. There's a difference. Poor men sleep. He spent the last six years of his life chasing the Fairweather claim through courts and survey offices and every drunk old pilot who claimed to remember where the Halcyon went down. He mortgaged the Kessler yards twice. The second time, the banks took them." She turned the cup again. "He died in a rented room above a chandlery in Corvane's home port, with charts spread all over the floor, marking soundings in a drowned reach he'd never see. I was nineteen. I sold his coat to pay for the funeral."

The rain filled the pause.

"So when the permits were lapsing—when I learned the crown would let the Fairweather concession die and revert, and that salvage rights would open—I thought, here it is. Here's the work. Finish it. Bring up the hull, bring up the manifest, close the account he died holding open." She met Oscar's eyes at last. "I didn't want your river, Fairweather. I wanted my father's ending. And I told myself that was the same thing for long enough to hire your crew, sabotage your hull, and put forty-one people at risk in monsoon waters."

"Forty-two," Oscar said quietly. "You're counting Ilsa wrong."

Maren blinked. "What?"

"She was yours. Commissioned by you. Whatever you think she cost me, she also chose to stay when she could have walked away with your money. So count her." He leaned back. "And count my crew, half of whom took your coin and half of whom didn't, and all of whom are still here pumping bilge in the rain. You didn't put forty-one people at risk. You put everyone at risk, including yourself, and the only reason you're not at the bottom of the reach is that my father was a better engineer than either of us gives him credit for."

It should have made her angry. It landed instead like a diagnosis—accurate, delivered without cruelty, impossible to argue with. She looked down at her hands. They were her father's hands, broad-knuckled, ink-stained at the edges from a lifetime of contracts.

"What happens now?" she asked. "Formally."

"Permits renew in my name within the week. Concession's registered at the trading post. The Halcyon's hull is mine to raise or leave, and the strongbox contents are mine outright." He paused. "The wreck itself, though. The raising of it. That's contract work. Big contract work. More tonnage than the Marigold can lift, more divers than I can feed, and a window that closes when the flood season turns."

Maren stared at him. "You're not—"

"The Kessler line has the only steam crane barge on this stretch of the river. Has had it since your father built it, back when he still built things instead of buying them." Oscar set down his cup. "I'm offering you a subcontract. Salvage recovery, standard rates, ten percent over market because I'm feeling generous and because your crew worked in that storm without deserting, which says something about you even if it doesn't say everything. You raise the Halcyon under my direction. Your name goes on the recovery log next to mine. When the histories get written—and this river writes its histories in taverns, so they will—it says a Kessler crane brought up the Fairweather wreck."

"That's—" She stopped. Started again. "That's charity."

"It's arithmetic." His voice stayed level, but something moved behind it. "Your father swindled half the river's families, Maren. My father scuttled his own life's work to stop him. Between our two names there's thirty years of damage, and I can't repair any of it by taking another thing from a Kessler. What I can do is give the name one job that puts something back instead of pulling something out." He shrugged, awkward, a man unused to offering things. "Duty answered with work. It's the only currency this river respects."

Maren looked at him for a long time. The rain eased, somewhere in the middle of it, settling into the steady grey murmur that meant the storm had decided to live here a while.

"If I take it," she said slowly, "I want terms in writing. Full rates, no favors. And I want the right to refuse any dive I judge unsafe, in writing, without penalty."

"Done."

"And Fairweather." She stood, because staying seated felt too much like surrender and standing felt, at least, like choice. "This doesn't make us friends."

"No," he agreed. "It makes us colleagues. Friends take longer."

Despite herself—despite eleven years and a dead father and a ruined name—she felt the corner of her mouth move. "One more thing. The letter. Your father's letter. Does it say what happened to the second survey party? In '08. My father funded it. Four men went upriver and never came back, and he carried it his whole life, whether he deserved to carry it or not."

Oscar was quiet a moment. "There's a second packet in the strongbox. Sealed. Addressed to no one." He hesitated. "I haven't opened it yet. When I do—if there's an answer in it—you'll hear it from me before you hear it from anyone else."

She nodded once, sharply, because her throat had closed without permission, and walked out into the rain.

* * *

The *Corvane*'s deck was quiet when she came aboard. Her crew pretended very hard to be busy, which was its own kind of courtesy. At the rail, her chief engineer—a grey-whiskered man named Polt who had served her father before her—stood waiting with a lantern.

"Well?" he said.

"We have a contract." She heard her own voice come out steadier than she felt. "Salvage recovery. Under Fairweather's direction. Standard rates plus ten."

Polt was silent for a moment. Then, carefully: "Your father would have called that surrender."

"My father," Maren said, "called everything surrender except the things that killed him." She took the lantern from the old man's hand and hung it herself on the hook by the wheel, the way it had hung every night of her childhood aboard the Kessler barges, a small light kept burning against the dark water. "Weigh anchor at first light. We'll go down to the reach and look at what we're raising."

Below decks, she unpacked her father's last chart—the drowned-reach soundings, marked in his cramped hand, corrected in places by guesses he'd never admitted were guesses—and pinned it above her bunk where she'd see it every morning. Not as a map anymore. As a debt, transferred.

Outside, the rain moved off downstream toward the horizon, and for the first time in eleven years, Maren Kessler slept without dreaming of the river.

She woke instead to a knock at dawn and Oscar Fairweather standing on her deck, soaked, holding the sealed second packet in both hands like something that might bite.

"I opened it," he said. "You need to sit down."
