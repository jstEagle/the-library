## 21. Concession

The clerk at the Concession Office in Serevan had ink on his fingers and a face like a man who had been waiting all morning for something interesting to happen. He read the deed twice, then a third time, holding it up to the window light as though the paper might confess to forgery.

"Fairweather," he said. "There was a Fairweather concession once."

"There still is," Oscar said.

The clerk looked up. Behind him, shelves of ledgers rose to the ceiling, each spine stamped with a year, the whole wall a history of men who had claimed pieces of the river and mostly lost them. "Elias Fairweather. Drowned reach. That permit lapsed—no, wait." He pulled a ledger down, licked his thumb, turned pages. "It didn't lapse. It *lapsed pending recovery*. There's a clause. Old one. Nobody's invoked it in—" he counted backward with his finger, lips moving, "—thirty years."

"My father wrote that clause into his filings himself," Oscar said. "He was a careful man about paper."

"And you're the blood heir."

"I'm the blood heir."

The clerk set the ledger down and looked at him for a long moment, the way men look at a river gauge when the water is higher than it should be. Then he uncapped his pen. "Name for the registry?"

Oscar opened his mouth and found, to his considerable surprise, that nothing came out. Fifteen years of giving false names at way-stations, of signing manifests as *J. Marlow* or *T. Vance* or, once memorably, *A. River*, which had gotten him laughed out of a customs house in Kolvane. Fifteen years of being no one in particular, because being an Oscar Fairweather had gotten his family killed—or so he'd believed, right up until the letter in the oilskin told him otherwise.

"Oscar Elias Fairweather," he said finally.

The pen scratched. The sound was very small and very loud.

* * *

They registered the Marigold that same afternoon, moored at the drowned reach, permanent berth, and the harbor master—a woman named Okonkwo who had chased Oscar out of three ports over the years for unpaid docking fees—stamped the papers with something close to a smile.

"An address," she said, reading back the entry. "You're telling me I can actually find you now."

"That appears to be what I've done."

"I don't believe it."

"Neither do I," Oscar admitted. "But the ink's dry."

She handed the papers across the counter and held on to them a half-second longer than necessary. "Your father used to run mail up this stretch, you know. Before your time on the water. Halcyon carried letters for every village from here to the cloud line. People trusted him with things they wouldn't tell their priests." She let go. "Funny what comes back around."

Oscar took the papers and walked out into the heat feeling as though someone had slipped a stone into his pocket without his noticing—something heavy, but not unwelcome. Something he might keep.

* * *

The Marigold sat at her new berth looking, in Oscar's honest assessment, like a dog that had been washed against its will. The recaulking had held through the last weeks of the monsoon; the patched planking was paler than the rest of her hull, a scar she'd wear for years. Dario was aloft in the rigging doing something to the stays that may or may not have been necessary, and the crew—what remained of the crew, the ones who'd stayed through the mutiny and the race and the cold water of the strongbox—were loading the last of Kessler's subcontracted salvage gear onto her deck under contract terms that Maren herself had drafted, fair to the point of being almost apologetic.

Ilsa was at the rail with her charts spread out, weighting the corners with brass fittings, working by the flat gray light that came after rain. Her sleeves were rolled to the elbow. There was ink on her fingers again—the good kind this time, the kind she put there herself.

Oscar climbed aboard and stood watching her work for a while, because watching Ilsa work was one of the few pleasures he'd discovered late in life and intended to make up for.

"You're redrawing the reach," he said.

"I'm redrawing everything." She didn't look up. "Kessler's charts were wrong. Deliberately wrong, in places—she had me mapping shoals that don't exist and missing channels that do. I think she wanted the reach to look unnavigable so nobody else would come looking." Her pen paused. "I'm fixing my own lies. It's slow going. Lying is fast; the truth takes measurements."

"How far have you got?"

"From the sandbar crossing to here. Maybe forty miles of honest river." She straightened and rubbed the small of her back. "Another two hundred above us nobody's charted properly since before the war. The old surveys are garbage. Half of them were drawn by men who never left their offices."

Oscar leaned on the rail beside her. Below, the drowned reach lay quiet under its new water level, the hills' shoulders just breaking the surface where the valley had flooded, trees standing dead and silver in the shallows like the masts of a fleet that had given up. Somewhere under all that brown water, the Halcyon rested with her hold empty and her captain's cabin cleared, her hull sold off plank by plank under the salvage contract. It was strange, how little grief that gave him now. The boat had been a grave for fifteen years. Graves were for the living to visit, and he had finally visited.

"Ilsa."

"Mm."

"The permits are in my name. The concession too. All of it—water rights, salvage rights, mail rights if I want them, and apparently I want them." He watched a heron stalk the shallows with the patience of a debt collector. "Which means somebody has to actually run the thing. Survey the upper reaches. Chart the channels. Keep the maps current, because the river redraws itself every monsoon and a stale map kills people."

She was very still. "That sounds like a job for a cartographer."

"It does."

"One with ink-stained fingers and a documented history of lying to you."

"A reformed one," Oscar said. "Reformed liars make the best mapmakers. They know all the ways a map can lie, so they guard against them."

She turned to look at him then, and her eyes had that quality he'd first noticed among the cargo crates—sharp, amused, slightly dangerous, like water finding the crack in a hull. "And what would I be charting it *as*? Because I've been commissioned twice on this river already, and both times by people who wanted something from it. I'm not doing a third."

"As a partner," Oscar said.

The word sat there between them. He let it.

"Not an employee. Not a passenger. Not a stowaway we decided to tolerate. A partner. Half the concession's survey work is yours if you want it, and the charts carry both our names, and when the river changes—and it will change, it always changes—we go look at it together." He shrugged, aware suddenly of how exposed his shoulders felt without cargo to fuss over. "I spent fifteen years learning to read water and refusing to read anything else. You spent however many years drawing rivers you'd never seen. Seems wasteful for us to keep those skills apart."

Ilsa looked back down at her charts. For a long moment the only sound was the heron, the water, and Dario dropping something heavy in the rigging and swearing about it with real artistry.

"Both names," she said.

"On every sheet."

"Equal say in the routes."

"Equal say."

"And when you get restless in March—you'll get restless in March, everyone who runs rivers gets restless in March—I don't want you disappearing upstream alone and leaving me a note."

"I've never left anyone a note in my life."

"Exactly my point."

Oscar laughed. It came out of him easily, which still surprised him sometimes, laughter—like the address, like the name in the registry, like the deed folded in oilskin in his cabin. Things that had been impossible for fifteen years, arriving all at once, requiring almost no effort at all. That was the joke of it, he thought. He had spent a decade and a half bracing against a door that had never been locked.

"So that's a yes?" he said.

Ilsa picked up her pen. "That's a yes. But I'm charting the March run myself, and you're carrying the instruments, and if you complain about the weight I'm putting it in the log."

"Partners," Oscar said, and found the word fit his mouth better than any of the false ones ever had.

* * *

Evening came down the valley the way it did now that the rains were ending—not crashing, but settling, like a bird choosing its branch. Oscar cooked on deck because the galley still smelled faintly of bilge and no amount of scrubbing had fixed it, and the crew ate together under the first clear stars in six weeks. Dario told the story of cutting Kessler's anchor lines for perhaps the ninth time, each retelling adding a shark, a storm, or in tonight's version, a crocodile, and the crew groaned and heckled and let him tell it anyway, because that was what forgiveness sounded like on a working boat: bad jokes, tolerated generously.

Later, when the others had gone below, Oscar stood at the wheel. He didn't need to stand there—the Marigold was moored, tied fast, going nowhere—but the wheel was where Fairweathers stood, and for the first time in fifteen years that sentence didn't taste like ash.

He thought about his father at this same wheel, hands on brass, holding a boat steady while the water came up around her. All those years Oscar had imagined it as surrender—his father going down with the concession, drowning the family's future rather than fight for it. Now he knew it for what it was: a holding action. His father hadn't surrendered the future. He'd sunk it somewhere safe and left coordinates, in the only language he trusted—paper, seals, law—and waited fifteen years for someone to come dig it up.

*Patience,* the letter had said, near the end, in handwriting gone shaky but legible. *The river keeps what's given to it. So do I.*

Below decks, lamplight moved behind the porthole of the chart room, where Ilsa was working late, laying down the first true lines of the upper river. Tomorrow they'd take her up past the cloud line, into water no honest map had ever touched. Dario had the watch until midnight and had promised, hand on heart and eyes shifty, to wake Oscar if anything more dramatic than a fish occurred.

Oscar stayed at the wheel a while longer, doing nothing, needing nothing, the mooring lines creaking their slow music, the dark water sliding past the hull toward the sea he no longer wanted to reach.

For the first time since he was nineteen years old, Oscar Fairweather was exactly where he meant to be, and tomorrow he would still be here, and the day after that.

He found he could hardly wait.

Above the eastern hills, faint already, the horizon was gathering its next monsoon—and for once, he was glad to be standing still to meet it.
