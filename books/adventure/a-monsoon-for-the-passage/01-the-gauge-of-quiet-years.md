## 1. The Gauge of Quiet Years

The morning Jude Fairweather arrived at Mile 114 of the Kestrel Pass line, the fog was doing what fog always did up there—sitting in the cut like something that had paid for the privilege, thick enough that she could have been standing in any decade at all. She liked that about the mountain. The mountain had no interest in what year it was. Neither, increasingly, did she.

She set her instrument case down on the gravel shoulder and let the tripod legs find their own way open, the way they'd done ten thousand times before, her hands working without instruction from the part of her that was still watching the fog. Standard gauge. That was the joke of it, the joke she'd carried privately for twenty-two years: she had spent her whole working life measuring the distance between two rails, four feet eight and a half inches, the width that had carried empires and then outlived them, and somewhere along the way she had let the same gauge measure her. Everything at its proper distance. Everything held to tolerance. Nothing permitted to wander off true.

The line had been closed for thirty-one years. She knew this the way she knew the height of a rail: not as a fact she'd learned but as a fact she'd become. The Kestrel Pass section had been decommissioned the autumn she turned twenty-six, and she had been sent up to survey its decay every year since, first for a ministry that cared, then for a ministry that pretended to care, and now, in these last four years, for a heritage trust that cared only insofar as decay could be photographed and laminated and hung in a visitor center nobody visited. She was the only person who came up here with instruments anymore. Everyone else came with cameras.

She walked the cut with her measuring wheel clicking against the sleepers, and the sleepers gave under her boots with a sound like old men clearing their throats. Creosote gone to dust. In places the timber had rotted clean through and the rail floated above nothing, held up by ballast and habit. She recorded it. She recorded everything. Ballast washout at 114.2, moderate. Vegetation incursion at 114.4, birch saplings through the fourth sleeper, three of them, the tallest about as high as her hip. A culvert at 114.6 choked with scree from the winter's freeze-thaw. Her pencil moved in the little weatherproof book with the economy of long practice, each entry stripped of anything that wasn't measurable. She had trained herself, over years, to write like a form.

It was not that she felt nothing up here. That was the thing people got wrong about her, the few people there were to get it wrong. They assumed the detachment was emptiness. It wasn't. It was a fullness she had decided not to look at directly, the way you don't look directly at a lamp. The mountain was full of her grandfather. He had engineered this line's upper section—she'd grown up knowing it the way you know your own middle name, without ever once being asked to think about it—and when she walked the cut she was walking inside the shape of his thinking, the grades he'd chosen, the curves he'd bent around the mountain's stubbornness. She could feel his decisions under her boots. She had never once said this out loud to anyone.

The fog began to lift around eight, pulling back up the valley in rags, and the sun came through sideways and lit the rail like a fuse. Jude stopped at the old water tower footing and got out the thermos. The tower itself had come down in a storm the year before she was born, her father used to say, though her father said a great many things and most of them were weather. All that remained was a stump of dressed granite and four anchor bolts gone orange with rust, and she sat on the granite because she always sat on the granite, and drank her coffee, and looked down the valley at the river.

The river ran along the floor of the cut for the next six miles, close enough to the old line that the engineers—her grandfather's generation, the men with the transits and the mules—had built the roadbed as a kind of argument with it. The river wanted the valley floor; the railway had taken a strip of it and dared the water to argue. For seventy years the water had been winning, slowly, the way water wins. She could see from here where the bank had slumped below the old bridge footing at Mile 118, a brown scar in the green, and she made a note to walk down and look at it before the week was out. It was on her list. Everything was on her list. The list was how she kept the years from accumulating, because a day with items on it was a day that could be completed, and a completed day did not have to be counted.

She had stopped counting birthdays at forty. Not as a declaration—she'd never told anyone, there had been no one to tell—but as a slow administrative decision, the way a line gets closed. First you stop the through traffic. Then you let the vegetation come in. She was forty-six now, or forty-seven; she'd have to do the arithmetic, and she noticed she was doing the arithmetic, and made herself stop, and felt the small familiar ache of that, the ache that was not quite regret and not quite grief but lived in the house next door to both and borrowed their tools.

* * *

The letter had come three weeks ago, and it was in the glovebox of her truck, and she had not opened it.

She knew what it was. Solicitors' envelopes were unmistakable, that heavy cream stock, that font that tried to look like handwriting. *Fairweather, E., Estate of — Final Distribution of Assets.* Her grandfather had been dead nine years, and the estate had been in probate for most of those nine years because the estate was mostly paper—deeds to land that had changed countries twice, mineral rights in a valley that was now under a reservoir, shares in companies that had been dissolved, nationalized, renamed, dissolved again. Her grandfather had lived his whole working life on the wrong side of every border redraw, and dying had not exempted him from it. The solicitor had written to her twice before, and she had answered the first time, and the second time she had meant to and hadn't, and now there was a third letter and the glovebox was where unopened things went to become permanent.

It wasn't the paperwork she was avoiding. She was a surveyor; paperwork was water to her. It was what the letter stood at the end of. A final distribution of assets meant the estate was finished, meant there would be nothing left of his that wasn't hers or the state's, meant the last official thread connecting him to the world would be cut and tied off, and after that he would be only what she remembered, and her memory was not a reliable archive. Her memory had already lost the sound of his voice. She had noticed it going about five years ago—she could hear the shape of his sentences, the dry gravel of them, but the actual timbre, the note of the man himself, had slipped out somewhere without her catching the door.

So the letter stayed in the glovebox, and every morning she looked at it through the smoked glass of the windshield the way you look at weather, and every morning she got out of the truck and went to work instead, because work was the one inheritance she had accepted cleanly. The instruments had been his. The transit, the levels, the chain, the little brass-bound case of drawing instruments with the velvet gone bald inside—they had come to her when she was nineteen, handed over without ceremony on the porch of the house that no one lived in anymore. *They'll keep you honest,* he'd said, which was the closest he had ever come to saying anything at all, and she had built a whole life inside that sentence without ever examining its foundations.

She was honest. That was the trouble with her, if she was honest about it. She was honest the way a rail is honest—fixed at both ends, carrying whatever weight was put on her, deflecting by fractions of a millimeter under loads no one could see, and never, ever wandering off true. She had been engaged once, at thirty-one, to a man who taught hydrology and laughed easily, and she had ended it in a restaurant with the same economy she used in her field book, each sentence stripped of anything that wasn't true. *I don't think I'm built for it,* she'd said, and he'd said, *Built? Jude, nobody's built,* and she had known he was right and done it anyway, because knowing and doing were two different gauges and hers had never been adjustable.

* * *

By afternoon she had worked her way up past the tunnel at Mile 117, the long one, the one her grandfather had driven through the mountain's shoulder in the years before the war, and she stood at its north portal with the cold air breathing out of it at her, slow and constant, the mountain exhaling seventy years of dark.

The portal masonry was sound. She checked it every year with the same tenderness she checked nothing else—the joints, the weep holes, the hairline map of cracks she had photographed so many times she could have drawn them from memory. The tunnel was the best-built thing on the line. It would outlast everything above it. She sometimes thought that if she could choose a way to be remembered it would be the way the tunnel was remembered: not visited, not admired, simply holding, doing its one job in the dark for longer than anyone who ordered it built would live.

Inside, forty paces in, where the daylight failed, there was a date chiseled into the lining. 1938, and beneath it two initials she had never asked about. She ran her thumb over them now, out of ritual rather than curiosity. The stone was cold and certain. Everything up here was cold and certain except her, and she had learned to be grateful for the company.

She was backing out of the portal, folding the tripod, when she heard it.

At first she took it for the mountain doing its ordinary work—the deep, unhurried conversation of rock under strain, the pops and grinds of a slope in thaw that she'd heard a thousand times and logged as ambient. But this had a direction to it. It came from down-valley, from below the cut, from the river side, and it was not a pop or a grind. It was a long tearing sound, mineral and enormous, like the mountain clearing its throat of something it had been holding a very long time.

She came out into the light and stood very still.

Down the valley, maybe a mile and a half as the fog flew, a column of dust was standing up off the south bank of the river—standing up and leaning, the way a tree leans when its roots have let go. She had seen slides before. She had logged eleven of them on this line over the years, had walked their debris fields with her wheel, measuring. This was bigger. This had the slow, total quality of something geological rather than seasonal, a whole shoulder of the hillside deciding, after centuries, to be somewhere else.

The dust column folded, drifted, and the sound arrived properly then—a rolling thunder with a strange wet undertone to it, and she understood the wet part a half-second before her mind put the rest together. The slide wasn't going across the valley. It was going into the river. A hillside's worth of mountain, sliding into a river already fat with the season's first melt, and the river, which had spent seventy years losing its argument with the engineers, had just been handed a new one.

She got the binoculars out of the case with hands that were, she noticed, entirely steady. That was the strange gift of her temperament: when the world moved, she went still. She found the scar on the south bank, raw brown, still sloughing, and traced the river downstream of it and saw what she had known she would see—the water backing up, ponding, going glassy and wrong behind a growing dam of debris, and below the dam, where the flow was pinched to a fraction of itself, the riverbed emerging into the daylight for the first time in living memory.

She lowered the binoculars. Her heart was doing something she chose not to measure.

Below Mile 118, where the old bridge footing stood—the footing she had noted that very morning, the slumped bank, the item on her list—the river was draining away like water out of a held hand. By tomorrow, or the day after, when the impounded water either topped the debris dam or broke it, that stretch of riverbed would lie open to the sky: seventy years of silt, of drowned things, of everything the river had taken and kept.

Jude stood in the cut with the fog gone and the light going gold and long, and she thought, with the detachment she had spent a lifetime perfecting: *That footing will need surveying. The channel's changed. There'll be work.*

It was a true thought. It was also, she understood, standing there in the last of the light with the mountain's dust still drifting down the valley, a thought she was using the way she'd used every thought for twenty years—to keep from feeling the other one, the one rising in her like the water rising behind the dam. Because her grandfather had engineered this line, and this river, and this valley, and he had walked it before the water came, and the river had been keeping things for seventy years, and some animal part of her, some part older than her discipline, had just understood that tomorrow she was not going to be measuring decay.

She was going to be measuring what the river had kept.

She wrote it in the field book anyway, in her stripped and honest hand: *Slide, south bank, approx. 118. River impounded. Channel dewatering below. Survey footing at first light.* Then she closed the book, and stood a moment longer in the enormous quiet after the thunder, and did not let herself think about the glovebox, or the letter, or the fact that for the first time in years she had counted a day—counted it precisely, from the fog at dawn to the dust in the gold light—because it was a day that had changed the shape of every day to come.
