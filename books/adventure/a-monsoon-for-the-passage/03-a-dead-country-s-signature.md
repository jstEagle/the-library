## 3. A Dead Country's Signature

The chest sat on the workbench all night, and Jude did not touch it.

She had dried it as best she could by lamplight—sponged the silt from its brass bindings, stood it on end to let the river drain from its seams, wiped it down until the wood showed its grain again, dark teak gone nearly black with seventy years of wet. Then she had hung the lamp, banked the shed stove, and sat across from it with her hands wrapped around a tin mug of cold tea, watching it the way one watches a stranger who has asked to sleep in your house.

The seal was stamped into a lead disc set into the lid's center, and the lamplight caught it in a way that made the embossed figure seem to move: a mountain with three rivers falling from its shoulders, and beneath the mountain, a ribbon bearing letters in a script she did not read but recognized the way one recognizes a face from an old photograph—the alphabet of a country that had stopped existing before Jude was born. Her school atlases had still carried it, in the older editions, the name printed across a territory that later maps divided among three neighbors like an estate settled out of court. The nation itself had lasted barely thirty years. Its signature, though, was still legible, pressed into lead, and it was signed all over her workbench in her own shed.

She had told herself she would open it in the morning. That was a lie she told the way surveyors tell lies: precisely, with known error margins. She was not going to open it in the morning. She was going to open it as soon as the light was decent, and she knew it, and the chest knew it, sitting there in the dark like a patient thing.

Sleep came anyway, the way sleep comes to people who have stopped expecting much of their nights—suddenly, and without ceremony, and full of water.

* * *

Morning came gray through the shed's one window, and with it the smell of the river, which had followed her home in her clothes and her hair and, she suspected, permanently into the grain of the floorboards. She made tea she did not drink and set the kettle on the stove for the warmth of it more than anything, and then she stood over the chest with her grandfather's pry bar—the good one, the one with the worn hickory handle she'd inherited along with the shed, the instruments, the maps of closed lines, and the silence—and worked the hasp loose.

The lock had surrendered long ago. Seventy years of river water had done what rivers do to iron: turned it to a red bloom, a crust that flaked away under the pry bar like dried blood. The lid came up with a groan and a breath of air that was not the smell of rot, which was what she had braced for, but something older and stranger—paper and wax and cold mud and, faintly, impossibly, kerosene. The smell of a camp. The smell of a lamp burning in a tent while someone wrote by it. Her eyes stung and she told herself it was the kerosene.

Inside, packed with a care that undid her more than anything else in the chest, were the papers.

Not dumped. Not jumbled by the river's long handling. *Packed.* Oilcloth bundles, tied with waxed cord, laid in rows like instruments in a case. Someone had wrapped each bundle against the water, and the water had come anyway, and the oilcloth had held anyway, and here they were: dry. Dry after seventy years in a flooded riverbed, dry as the inside of a sealed tomb, and Jude stood with her hands hovering over them for a long moment before she touched them, because there are objects you handle and objects you approach.

The first bundle was the expedition log.

She untied the cord with fingers that felt too large and clumsy, and opened the oilcloth on a ledger bound in canvas, its spine cracked, its pages swollen slightly at the edges where damp had found the one seam the wrapping couldn't cover. The handwriting inside was small and exact and slanted hard to the right, the hand of someone writing against time or cold or both, and it began, in ink gone the brown of old tea:

*Day 1. Departed the railhead at dawn. Party of eleven. Weather fair. The surveyor says we will make the first camp by dark if the porters' loads hold, and the porters say the loads will hold if the surveyor's mouth holds shut. Morale high.*

Jude read it twice. Then she read it a third time, aloud, in the empty shed, because there was something in the voice of it—dry, wry, tired in advance of the tiredness to come—that made silence feel like the wrong response. A voice from a country that no longer existed, joking about porters, and the joke landing across seventy years and a flooded riverbed and the whole collapsed geography of the twentieth century, landing in her shed, on her workbench, in her hands.

She turned pages. The days accumulated. The river grew in the entries the way rivers do in journals—first a nuisance at the fords, then a presence, then a character, then, near the end of the ledger, something closer to a god being petitioned. The handwriting changed too. The early entries were confident, almost jaunty. By the middle pages the slant had steepened. By the last quarter the letters were cramped, the ink thinning, the entries shorter: *Rain. Rain. The gauge washed out again. R. says the map will not survive the season if we do not survive it first. We press on.*

*We press on.* Three words repeated like a heartbeat through the last twenty pages, and Jude found she had stopped sitting and was standing, had been standing for some time without noticing, reading the way you read a letter addressed to someone who is no longer alive to receive it.

There were photographs. A second oilcloth bundle held them, wrapped in waxed paper, and she laid them out on the bench in a row, eleven faces in the first one, arranged on a rail flatcar with the mountains behind them and the particular stiff joy of people photographed at the beginning of something. She counted them twice. Eleven. She looked at their faces a long time—not searching for anything, just looking, the way you look at strangers in an old photograph and feel the strange vertigo of knowing how the story ends without knowing the story. A woman in the back row held a theodolite against her shoulder like a rifle, and she was laughing at something outside the frame, and her laugh was so present in the picture that Jude had to set it down.

The later photographs were different. Fewer faces in each. She laid them out in order and the party thinned across the images the way the party had thinned across the log—nine, then seven, then a group shot in front of a tent with the river brown and high behind them and nobody laughing. The last photograph was of a map itself, pinned to a board, half-drawn, a line running up a river valley and stopping. Just stopping. Mid-route. Mid-stroke, almost, as if the pen had been lifted and never set down again.

The third bundle was the map.

It was the real one, not the photograph of it—a survey sheet on linen-backed paper, drawn in the same exact hand as the log, and Jude unrolled it with the reverence she reserved for instruments and the dread she reserved for other things, and it was beautiful. That was the word, and she did not shrink from it. She had spent her life around survey drawings, had drawn hundreds herself, and she knew the difference between competent and good and this. This was the work of a master. The contour lines flowed like water finding its level. The river ran down the center of the sheet rendered in a double stroke with soundings noted in a margin hand, and the valley walls rose in hachures so fine they looked woven, and the route of the expedition climbed the sheet in a dotted line with elevations and distances and camp positions marked in a code she half recognized—old style, colonial-era, the notation her own charts still used because her own charts had been drawn by the same tradition, the same school, possibly the same—

The map ended.

Not at the edge of the sheet. In the middle of it. The line ran up the valley, camp by camp, day by day, matching the log, and then it stopped at a tributary junction with no note, no break symbol, no *route continues overleaf*, nothing. The pen had been lifted. The sheet had been folded, wrapped, sealed in this chest with the seal of a dead country, and sunk in a river, and the line had never been continued.

Jude stood over it for a long time. The kettle had long since gone quiet. The light through the window had moved across the bench and climbed the wall and she had not noticed, and when she finally looked up, the day was half gone and the chest still held one bundle she had not opened.

It was smaller than the others. Tied differently—tighter, the cord knotted twice, the oilcloth wrapped in three layers, as if whoever packed it had known this one mattered most, or had known it least. She untied it slowly.

Inside were loose pages. Field notes, fragments, a few letters begun and not finished. And at the bottom of the bundle, a single sheet of survey paper, covered in the margin notes of an engineer—calculations, load figures, bridge spans, the working arithmetic of keeping eleven people alive on a river that was eating the route behind them—and at the top of the sheet, in the same hand that had signed every calculation, a name.

*E. Fairweather.*

Jude did not move. She is not sure, afterward, that she breathed. The shed was very quiet, the way it is quiet after a river has taken something, and the name sat on the page in brown ink, seventy years old, written by a hand she knew—not the handwriting, she had never seen this handwriting, but the *name*, the name that was hers, the name that had come down to her the way the shed had come down to her and the pry bar and the maps of closed lines and the silence, the name that had been her grandfather's name and his father's joke and her whole inheritance of a man she had known only as an old man who measured things and did not talk about the war or the years before it or why he kept a locked drawer in a desk she had inherited and never opened.

*E. Fairweather. Engineer.*

Her grandfather had not been on the expedition. Her grandfather had not *known* of the expedition. Her grandfather had been a rail surveyor who lived and died within forty miles of this valley, who had taught her to read contours before she could read words, who had never once, in all her childhood, mentioned a river journey, a party of eleven, a half-drawn map, a dead country's seal.

Except that he had. Not in words. In the only language he had ever been fluent in. Because she knew that margin hand. She had grown up watching it. She had inherited notebooks full of it—decades of it, the closed lines, the shuttered gauges, the surveys he had made alone up valleys no train would ever run through, and she had always read them as the habit of an old man who could not stop working. She had read them as eccentricity. She had read them as grief with nowhere to go.

She read them now as a continuation. As a man spending forty years finishing a line that stopped mid-sheet, alone, in secret, on paper no one was meant to compare against a chest at the bottom of a river.

Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake with a kind of detached interest, the way she watched her own instruments register something she could not yet explain—a tremor in the needle, a deflection with no known source. She set the page down on the bench and pressed her palms flat against the wood and the wood was steady and her hands were not, and the last entry in the log was still open beside her, the one she had read an hour or a lifetime ago, and she looked down at it again and read the final line, the one written in the steepest, thinnest hand of all:

*Day 61. The engineer says the river will take the valley before it takes the truth. We have sealed the maps. If the party does not come home, let the river keep us, and let whoever finds this know we were here, all eleven, and that we were sent, and that we did not turn back.*

Below it, a signature block. Eleven lines. Ten of them signed in different hands, some confident, some failing. The eleventh line was blank, and beside it, in the margin, in the hand she now knew better than her own, someone had written:

*Signed for the eleventh, who could not hold a pen. — E.F.*

Jude Fairweather, who had spent thirty-one years measuring the world so she would never have to measure herself, stood in her grandfather's shed with her grandfather's name in her grandfather's hand, and the river's water was still on her boots, and somewhere down the valley the river ran on toward the sea, carrying whatever else it had kept.

She did not hear the footsteps on the gravel outside. She did not see the figure pause at the shed's open door, silhouetted against the gray morning, looking in at her and at the open chest with the patience of a man who had traveled a long way and intended to be paid for it.

She was still staring at the blank eleventh line when he cleared his throat.
