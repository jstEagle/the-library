## 4. The Man Who Counts Other People's Papers

Elias Voss had a theory about small towns, and it was this: they were archives that didn't know they were archives. Every shuttered window was a closed file. Every old man on a bench was an unindexed volume, waiting for someone patient enough to open him. You did not interrogate an archive. You browsed it. You let it believe it was choosing what to show you.

He stepped off the bus at half past four in the afternoon with a canvas satchel over one shoulder and a rain jacket folded over his arm, dressed the way he always dressed when he worked: expensively but forgettably. Good shoes, wrong for the mud. A shirt that said *university* without naming one. He had learned long ago that the most useful costume in his profession was the one that made people want to tell him things—made them feel flattered by their own recollections, as though memory were a gift they were bestowing rather than a liability they were surrendering.

The town had no name worth remembering, which suited him. It existed because a railway had once decided to exist here, and it would outlast the railway by a generation or two, the way a stain outlasts the spilled thing. Above the rooftops he could see the shoulder of the mountain line, its viaducts stitched across the green like old sutures. Decommissioned. Closed lines. Somewhere up there, a woman named Fairweather spent her days measuring decay.

Voss smiled at the thought, and a woman passing with a basket of greens smiled back, puzzled and pleased, and he tipped his head to her as though they shared a joke. That was the other thing about small towns: they were starved for charm. They gave it away cheap.

* * *

The guesthouse was called the Stationmaster's Rest, though no stationmaster had rested in it for forty years. Its proprietor was a broad, slow-moving man named Dhillon who kept a register so outdated that Voss's signature was the first new ink on the page since the previous monsoon. Voss wrote his name in careful, slightly old-fashioned script—*Elias Voss, historical researcher*—and watched Dhillon read it upside down with the frank hunger of a man who had nothing else to read.

"Historical," Dhillon said. "Like the railway?"

"Exactly like the railway." Voss set down his satchel. "I'm writing a book. The colonial engineering projects—the lines nobody finished, the surveys nobody used. There's a whole vanished world in these hills, Mr. Dhillon, and I intend to find it."

"A book." Dhillon said the word the way another man might say *gold*. "There was a surveyor came through last year doing measurements on the old line. Woman. Quiet as a heron." He leaned in. "You'll want to talk to old Prasad at the tea shop. He remembers everything. Everything. Flood years, who married who, the year the river changed course—" He stopped, and something moved behind his face, some small animal of caution lifting its head. "That was a strange year. The river changing."

"The river changes course?" Voss asked lightly, as though the question were a coin tossed into water, already forgotten.

"After the landslide. Two months back, maybe more. Cut a new channel below the third bridge. Left all that—" Dhillon waved a hand, searching. "All that old ground showing. Mud and stones and things." He picked up Voss's key from the hook. "Room two. Hot water in the evenings if the generator's behaving."

"Thank you." Voss took the key. Then, casually, turning back as though struck by afterthought: "Did anything interesting come out of the river? When it changed course? These rivers give up the strangest things. I've seen entire locomotive boilers, temple bells, once a trunk of letters from 1911."

Dhillon's hand paused on the counter. It was a pause of perhaps two seconds, but Voss had built a career inside pauses like that one. Two seconds was the width of a door left ajar.

"Not that I heard," Dhillon said.

*Not that I heard.* Not *nothing*. Voss climbed the stairs to room two, sat on the bed with its floral coverlet, and allowed himself the small, private pleasure of a hypothesis confirmed. Somewhere downstream of a landslide, something had surfaced. And somewhere between the riverbank and the tea shop, word of it had traveled faster than the government's paperwork could travel—which meant it had traveled by mouth, by rumor, by the electric current of a town with nothing better to talk about.

Which meant the Fairweather woman had found something. Or someone else had, and she had heard of it first.

He unpacked methodically. On top of his folded shirts lay a folder, thin and gray, stamped with an emblem from a ministry whose name had changed three times in seventy years while its functions never changed at all. Inside the folder: a personnel photograph of Jude Fairweather, taken for a work permit eight years ago—a narrow face, watchful eyes, hair pulled back with the indifference of someone who considered her own appearance a clerical matter. A copy of her employment contract with the decommissioning authority. A genealogical abstract: granddaughter of Edmund Fairweather, engineer, b. 1909, d. 1994. And a single typed instruction, unsigned, on ministry letterhead:

*Assess and recover any materials pertaining to the 1947 Northern Boundary Expedition. Discretion essential. No formal record of recovery to be created.*

Voss had read the instruction perhaps two hundred times, and each time he noticed the same elegant dishonesty in its phrasing. Not *destroy*. Never destroy; destruction left gaps, and gaps invited questions. *Recover.* As though the papers were lost property being returned to a loving owner, rather than evidence being lowered into a deeper grave than the river had dug.

His grandfather would have appreciated the phrasing. Colonel Aurel Voss had been a man who understood that history was not what happened but what was filed, and that the filing clerk was the true author of nations. Elias had grown up on the colonel's knee listening to stories that were all weather and scenery and no people—expeditions that became *administrative closures*, men who became *personnel written off*, a border that became *the settled question*. It had taken Elias thirty years and a great deal of quiet archive work to understand that every soft phrase in his grandfather's vocabulary was a knife wrapped in cloth.

He did not hate his grandfather. That was the thing people assumed, and they were wrong. He loved him, in the way you love a load-bearing wall: with respect, and with the knowledge that your whole house stood on it. What Elias felt was something more complicated—an inheritance of silence so complete that breaking it felt less like honesty than like vandalism. Who was he to knock holes in the wall that held up everything?

And yet. The instruction in the folder. The train ticket. The mud on his good shoes by tomorrow noon.

He was, he told himself, simply the next clerk in a very long chain of clerks. Someone had to count other people's papers. If not him, then someone less careful, someone who would burn where he only shelved. He was, if anything, the merciful version of the job.

He almost believed it. He had practiced believing it for so long that the belief had worn smooth, like a stone carried in a pocket.

* * *

The tea shop occupied the ground floor of what had been the railway's parcel office, and old Prasad occupied the corner table the way mountains occupy horizons—permanently, and by right. He was perhaps eighty-five, small and brown and wrinkled as a walnut, with eyes that went straight past Voss's shirt to something underneath it.

"You're the writer," Prasad said, before Voss had ordered anything.

"I'm hoping to be. Books take longer than people expect."

"Everything takes longer than people expect. Except dying." Prasad poured him tea unasked, unprompted, as though the transaction had been arranged decades ago. "You're not really a writer."

Voss laughed, genuinely delighted. "What gave me away?"

"Writers ask about the railway. You asked the guesthouse man about the river." Prasad sipped his own tea. "I hear things. This is my shop. People come in and talk, and I sit here like furniture, and furniture hears everything."

"And has the furniture heard anything about the river lately?"

Prasad looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the light was going amber, and moths had begun their evening argument with the lamp. "Two months ago the river moved and showed everybody its basement," he said. "The surveyor woman went down into the mud. She was down there a long time. She came up carrying something heavy, wrapped in her coat, and she walked home with it and she hasn't shown it to anyone." He set down his cup. "Except maybe the Osei girl."

Voss kept his face pleasant and still, and inside his chest something turned over slowly, like a page. "Osei girl?"

"Came two weeks ago. Foreign, but her grandmother was born upriver, in the valley. Cartographer family." Prasad pronounced the word carefully, having clearly rehearsed it. "She's been asking about the expedition. The one from '47. The map people." His voice dropped, and the drop was involuntary; Voss recognized it as the sound a community makes around its own wound. "Eleven of them went up the river in the wet season and none came down. My father helped search. We found one boot. One. My father wouldn't eat fish for a year afterward."

"And now the granddaughter comes asking."

"Now the granddaughter comes asking, and the surveyor finds something in the mud, and then you arrive asking about rivers." Prasad refilled Voss's cup, unhurried. "In my experience, when three people come looking for the same drowned thing in one season, the thing wants to be found. Or somebody wants it not to be found. Either way, there will be trouble, and trouble is bad for tea sales."

"What do you want, Mr. Prasad?"

"Me?" The old man smiled, and it was a young man's smile briefly wearing an old man's face. "I want my father's fish-eating back. I want the eleven names said out loud instead of whispered. But I am furniture, and furniture wants nothing." He pushed the teapot toward Voss. "Drink. And when you go see the surveyor woman—and you will go see her—you can tell her old Prasad says: the river gives back what it keeps, but it charges interest."

* * *

He found her the next morning, or arranged to find her, which amounted to the same thing. The rail shed sat at the edge of town where the cobbles gave up and the gravel began, a corrugated-iron building sweating in the early heat, its door propped open with a section of rail. Through it he could see surveying instruments on benches, charts pinned to walls, and—working at a table beneath the single high window—a woman with her sleeves rolled, drawing a line with a ruling pen so fine it seemed less drawn than breathed onto the paper.

Jude Fairweather looked up when his shadow crossed the threshold. Her eyes did the thing he'd predicted from the photograph: they measured him. Height, build, hands, shoes, the satchel. He watched her do it and admired the thoroughness of it. Here was a person who assessed the world the way he assessed archives—item by item, silently, forever.

"Ms. Fairweather." He extended his hand. "Elias Voss. Historical researcher. I'm writing about the colonial engineering projects—the closed lines, the abandoned surveys. Your name came up in the literature more than once. Your grandfather's too, actually. Edmund Fairweather. A remarkable career."

She shook his hand. Her grip was dry and brief. "Remarkable how?"

"Longevity, mostly. Forty years on the same network. Most engineers moved on after independence; he stayed with the dead lines like a doctor staying with a dying patient." Voss gestured at the shed, the charts, the beautiful ordered clutter of her trade. "May I? I promise I'm harmless. Writers are the least dangerous people in the world—we only ever kill our darlings."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but the acknowledgment that a smile had been offered and declined on technical grounds. "What do you want to know?"

"Everything you're willing to tell me. The closure schedules. The condition surveys. How a line dies—what it looks like, year by year." He let genuine enthusiasm color his voice, because it was partly genuine; he did love this material, the archaeology of infrastructure, the way iron rusted into history. "And if you'll forgive the personal question—your grandfather's papers. Did the family keep any? Letters, field notes? For the book, of course. Primary sources are gold."

Here it was. The pause. He watched her have it, watched the half-second in which she decided what he was, and he saw her arrive at the answer *academic nuisance, manageable*, and he felt the particular professional satisfaction of being underestimated correctly enough.

"There are some papers," she said. "Nothing organized. Boxes at a solicitor's, mostly. Estate things I haven't gotten around to."

"You haven't opened your own grandfather's estate?"

"It's been busy season. Landslide, bridge footings, the river doing what it does." She turned back to her drafting table, a dismissal shaped like a change of subject. "The river's been generous with surprises this year, if that helps your book."

"Has it?" Voss wandered, hands clasped behind his back, admiring a wall chart of the mountain division with the air of a man killing time. "Rivers usually keep their secrets. That's why we love them."

"That's why some people love them."

"And you?"

"I measure things," Jude Fairweather said. "Whether they're secrets or not."

It was beautifully done. No denial, no confirmation, nothing to catch on. He felt a stab of something adjacent to respect, and beneath the respect, coldly, the recognition of a problem: this was not a woman who could be charmed into confession. She would have to be maneuvered, or pressed, or frightened—and the ministry's instruction said *discretion*, and discretion meant he had come with a penknife to a job requiring leverage.

So he lied, pleasantly, professionally, the way he had lied a thousand times. "Well. If anything surfaces—if the river gives up anything with a stamp on it, a seal, a mark of provenance—I'd be grateful to know. Institutions pay decent money for documented salvage, and I know collectors who'd pay indecent money. Consider me a broker of other people's paper." He smiled his best smile, the one that had opened doors on four continents. "No pressure. Just a card."

He laid the card on her table. She looked at it without touching it.

"I don't sell things I find," she said.

"No? What do you do with them?"

"Same as you, I imagine." She met his eyes, and for one unguarded instant he saw not the surveyor but something older and wearier underneath, a woman who had spent years counting miles so she wouldn't have to count anything else. "I put them where they belong."

* * *

Outside, the heat had thickened. Voss walked back along the old station road, past the platform where weeds grew through the joints of the concrete, past a signal post rusted mid-swing like a stopped clock. He turned the morning over in his mind, item by item, the way he'd been trained.

She had the chest—he was certain now, certain in the way that required no evidence, the way his grandfather had been certain about borders. She had it, and the Osei girl knew, and old Prasad knew that they both knew, and a town that ran on rumor had begun its slow electrical hum. Within days, if not hours, the hum would reach the district office, and the district office would reach the ministry, and then the machinery he was supposed to prevent would start grinding on its own, clumsy and loud, and whatever was in that chest would be seized in daylight instead of shelved in darkness. His employers hated noise worse than they hated truth.

Which meant his timetable had just collapsed from weeks to days.

At the guesthouse, Dhillon was sweeping the steps. "Any luck with your research?" he asked cheerfully.

"Oh, enormous luck." Voss paused on the bottom step. "Mr. Dhillon, a small favor. The surveyor—Ms. Fairweather. Does she keep a boat? I ask because I may want to charter transport upriver myself, and I'd rather hire locally than bring in strangers."

Dhillon's broom slowed. "Upriver? In this season? Monsoon's coming, sahib. First rains any week now. Nobody goes upriver before the rains except fools and—"

"—and writers," Voss said, smiling. "We're a little of both."

He climbed to room two and took out the gray folder, and beneath the typed instruction he wrote, in his careful old-fashioned script, a date: forty-eight hours. After that, he would file the paperwork that made the chest state property, and the polite phase of his visit would end, and he would become what he had always been underneath the charm—a man employed by the dead to keep the dead buried.

He told himself again that he was the merciful version of the job.

Downstairs, faintly, he could hear Dhillon's radio, and beneath the music the announcer's voice reading the forecast: heavy rain advancing from the coast, earlier than expected, earlier than any year on record.

Voss listened to it, and for the first time since stepping off the bus, he felt something he refused to call dread.
