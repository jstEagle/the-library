## 5. Half a Map

The rain came at dusk, as it had begun to do every evening that week, and Jude Fairweather worked by lamplight with the shutters open because the shed smelled of kerosene and old paper and she could not bear to make it worse.

She had not slept. That was nothing new—she had gone whole seasons without sleeping properly, back when she still counted the miles she covered in a year the way other people counted money. But this was a different kind of wakefulness. The chest sat open on the trestle table where she'd left it, brass fittings green with seventy years of river, and the documents lay spread across her charts like a hand of cards someone had dealt and walked away from.

She had spent the day telling herself she would not look again. She had spent the evening looking.

The expedition map was a single sheet, linen-backed, ending mid-route at a place the cartographer had labeled only with coordinates and a small, frustrated cross—the universal shorthand of a surveyor interrupted. *Incomplete*, the margin said, in a fine, slanted hand. *Route continues north-northeast along the eastern escarpment. To be resumed.*

*To be resumed.* Jude had read those three words perhaps forty times now, and each time they landed differently. The first time, they had meant nothing. The second time, they had meant a dead woman's optimism. Now they meant something she was afraid to name.

Because she knew the eastern escarpment. She knew it the way you know a face you've been studying in a mirror your whole life without realizing it was a face.

She rose, joints protesting the way they always did after a day in the wet, and went to the steel cabinet against the far wall—the one the railway company had issued her fifteen years ago, dented at the corner where she'd dropped it down a cut embankment and never bothered to report. Inside were her charts. Not the company's charts, the official ones, the ones with their approved margins and their approved silences. Her own. The closed-line surveys she had made over a decade of being sent to measure things the company had already decided to forget.

She pulled them out and carried them to the table, and for a long moment she simply stood there, the rolled linen in her arms, looking down at the expedition map with the feeling of a woman about to do something that could not be undone.

There are moments in a life—she would think about this later, much later, when she had learned to think in years instead of miles—when you understand that the next small action is not small at all. Unrolling a chart is a small action. Laying it flat beside another chart is a small action. But some small actions are doors, and you feel the draft coming through them before your hand is even on the handle.

She unrolled her chart of the eastern escarpment lines and weighted its corners with the brass paperweights her grandfather's estate had not contained—she'd bought them herself, at a market, years ago, because a surveyor should have brass paperweights and no one had ever given her any.

The two sheets lay side by side. Then, because the thought had been building in her all day like pressure behind a dam, she moved hers on top of the other.

The lamplight came through the linen. Both maps were drawn on the same stock, she saw now—she had noticed it before, the same weave, the same faint yellowing, the same watermark of a paper mill that had closed before Jude was born. But seeing the sheets overlap was different from knowing they matched. Seeing them overlap was like hearing a chord after a lifetime of single notes.

The expedition's route ended at the frustrated cross. Her own chart began—her very first closed-line survey, the one she'd made at twenty-six, green and meticulous, measuring a branch line the company had abandoned in 1961—began eleven miles south of that cross, running north-northeast along the eastern escarpment.

Along the escarpment. The words from the margin. The route that was *to be resumed*.

Jude stood very still.

It could be coincidence. She made herself say it aloud, to the empty shed, because she was a surveyor and surveyors do not trust the eye before the instrument. "It could be coincidence." The escarpment was the escarpment. Any route north from the river would follow it. Any line drawn by any hand in any decade would run the same way, because the land permitted only so many ways through.

But the land did not choose the scale. The land did not choose the contour interval. And when she bent closer, lamplight pulling the shadows long across the table, she saw the contour interval was the same—twenty feet, an odd, old-fashioned interval the company had abandoned before her time. She saw the same convention for marking scree, the same little hooked symbol for a spring, the same habit of placing the north arrow not at the corner but at the lower left, slightly tilted, like a signature in the margin of a letter.

She had grown up, in whatever way you could call it growing up, with her grandfather's old survey sheets pinned to the walls of a house that smelled of pipe smoke and damp. She had learned her symbols from them. She had learned her north arrow from them. She had thought, all these years, that she was imitating a dead man's style because it was the only style she'd been shown.

She was not imitating it. She was continuing it.

Her hands were not shaking—not yet—but they were very precise as she worked, and precision was how her fear showed itself, the way other people's fear showed itself in trembling. She took out her loupe. She took out her scale rule. She began, methodically, the way she did everything, to compare.

The linework first. The expedition cartographer drew contours with a fine, even hand, but with a slight thickening on the downstream side of every ridge—a habit Jude had never seen in any other surveyor's work, a habit she had absorbed without knowing she'd absorbed it, because it was how she drew them too, and she had never once wondered why. She checked six contours. All six thickened on the downstream side. All six of hers did too.

Then the annotations. The expedition map's margins were dense with notes in that fine slanted hand—elevations, bearings, observations about water levels and rock stability, the working thoughts of a mind that could not leave the land alone even while drawing it. And here Jude had to set the loupe down and walk to the window and stand looking out at the rain for a while, because she had recognized the handwriting.

Not the style. The hand.

She had seen that hand on the backs of photographs. She had seen it on the flyleaf of a Kipling she still owned and had never reread. She had seen it, once, on a birthday card she'd kept in a drawer for twenty years and never told anyone about, because the card had arrived two weeks after the funeral, posted by a solicitor's clerk, and the message inside had been four words long: *The lines are yours.*

She had thought it meant the railway lines. The closed ones, the forgotten ones, the inheritance of a man who had spent his life measuring a country's arteries and watching them be tied off one by one. She had framed it, in her mind, as a professional bequest—a dead engineer passing his trade to the only grandchild who'd shown any aptitude for it.

*The lines are yours.*

She went back to the table.

The expedition map ended at the cross. Her chart began eleven miles south of the cross. But between those two points—she saw it now, saw it with the whole of her attention, the way you see a thing only when you stop being able to unsee it—there was no gap at all. There was a seam. The expedition's last contour ran off the edge of its sheet at a bearing of three-four-one degrees. Her first contour, drawn thirty years later by a woman who had never heard of the expedition, picked up at the sheet edge on a bearing of three-four-one degrees, at the same elevation, at the same contour interval, with the same downstream thickening, as though the line had simply paused for a few decades, the way a sentence pauses at a comma, and then continued.

She laid her other charts out. The whole sequence. Twelve years of closed-line surveys, sheet after sheet, running north along the escarpment, up into the high country, toward the passes that the border—the old border, the dead country's border, the border that had been erased and redrawn and erased again—was supposed to cross.

The expedition's map was half a line. Her charts were the other half.

And the hand was the same hand.

She sat down on the stool, because her legs had opinions about standing. The rain thickened against the roof, a sound like static, like a crowd murmuring just below the level of words.

He hadn't joined the expedition, then. That was what the log had implied—*E. Fairweather, engineer*, listed among the eleven names, a junior posting, a young man's job. She had spent the night before telling herself a simple story: her grandfather, young, had gone up that river with ten others, and the river had kept them, and he alone had come back, or come back changed, and had spent his life surveying shuttered lines because the work had marked him. A story of damage. A story she could hold.

But the maps told a different story, and maps always did. Maps were honest in a way people were not; they could not help it. The maps said: he came back. He came back, and he did not stop. He spent decades—she did the arithmetic against the dates on her own sheets, and the arithmetic was obscene—*decades*, in his fifties, in his sixties, in the years after she was born, after she'd started carrying his old instruments into the field—resuming the route. Sheet by sheet. Line by line. Alone, or nearly alone, under cover of company contracts and decommission surveys, walking the expedition's unfinished road and drawing it in the same hand, with the same conventions, the same north arrow tilted like a signature, finishing what eleven people had died—or vanished, or been left—trying to do.

And he had never told anyone. He had never published it. He had pinned the sheets to the walls of a damp house and taught a granddaughter to draw contours with a downstream thickening, and he had written four words on a card and died with the rest of it in his chest—no. Not in his chest. In a chest. In a brass-bound chest in a riverbed, where the river had kept it for him until the river changed its mind.

*The lines are yours.*

She laughed, once, without any humor in it, and the sound startled her in the empty shed. Because she had thought she was invisible. She had thought she had made herself invisible—no birthdays counted, no letters opened, a woman who measured other people's forgotten things and was never measured herself. And all the while she had been a line on someone else's map. A continuation. A *to be resumed*. He had looked at a small girl with muddy knees and a talent for bearings and seen not a child but a survey party of one, provisioned and trained and dispatched decades in advance, and he had never asked her consent, because the dead cannot ask, and he had been dead in every way that mattered long before his heart stopped.

She was angry. She was surprised by how angry she was—anger with a hot, specific core, anger at a man she had loved in the wordless way you love the person who taught you your own mind. He had used her. No—he had *trusted* her, and those were not the same thing, and she could not yet tell which of them the truth was, and that not-knowing was the worst of it.

She looked at the overlaid sheets for a long time. The seam ran through them like a scar. Eleven miles of escarpment where the line paused and resumed, and on either side of the seam, the same hand, thirty years apart, drawing the same country toward the same destination.

The border. The old border, the true one, the one the expedition had been carrying proof of when it stopped existing. His charts didn't show a border—she checked, and they didn't, not once, not a single line of jurisdiction in twelve years of work. He had drawn only the land. Only the truth of the land, the contours and the water and the passes, and he had left the meaning of it for someone else to say aloud.

For her to say aloud.

The lamplight flickered. Outside, the river was talking to itself in the dark, running high and brown with the first of the monsoon's mood, and Jude thought: *he walked this in the dry seasons. He waited out the rains the way I do. He knew this river the way I know it.* And then a colder thought, arriving with the patience of all cold thoughts: *and someone else may know it too.*

Because the chest had been found, and found things travel. Because a man had come through the town not three days ago asking quiet questions about river salvage, a man with an archivist's soft hands and an institutional smile, and she had lied to him, and he had believed her, or pretended to, and either way he was not finished. Men like that were never finished. They were like contour lines themselves—they always came around again.

She began, carefully, to separate the sheets. Her charts she would keep—no one had any claim on them; they were company work, decommission surveys, worthless paper as far as any ministry was concerned. But the expedition map, and the logs, and the photographs of eleven young faces smiling on a riverbank in a country that no longer existed—those were the things a man like Voss would want to make disappear, and she understood now, with the seam scarred across the table in front of her, that they were also the things she could not let disappear. Not anymore. Not when the other half of the line was in her own handwriting, in her own blood, in her own stubborn downstream-thickened hand.

She was rolling up the charts when she heard it—a vehicle on the track above the shed, engine braking hard, the way vehicles braked when they had business and wanted you to know they'd arrived under their own power. Headlights swept the shutter slats, laid a ladder of pale bars across the table, across the seam, across the two halves of one long line.

Then footsteps on gravel, unhurried, and a voice she knew, calling pleasantly through the rain:

"Miss Fairweather? Sorry to trouble you again. I find I have a few more questions."

Jude stood in the lamplight with her grandfather's half-map in one hand and her own in the other, and did not answer, and counted, without meaning to, the seconds between the footsteps and the door.
