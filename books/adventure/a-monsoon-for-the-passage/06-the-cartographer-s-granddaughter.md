## 6. The Cartographer's Granddaughter

Mara Osei had learned to read grief in the posture of strangers, and the woman at the rail shed wore hers like a coat two sizes too small—shoulders drawn in, hands busy with a task that did not need doing. She was re-inking a line on a chart that was already perfect.

Mara stood in the doorway of the shed for a long moment before knocking on the frame, letting the rain drip off her coat onto the gravel. Three days she had been in this valley town, three days of asking careful questions at the ferry office and the provisioner's and the one bar with a television that worked, and every answer had circled back to the same name: Fairweather. The surveyor who lived past the water tower. The surveyor who had pulled something out of the river after the slide.

*The surveyor who lies well,* said the man at the bar, who had asked her own questions right back at her, smiling all the while, and whose card still sat in her pocket like a splinter. Elias Voss. *Archival Recovery Division.* A title that sounded like it had been designed by committee to mean nothing.

But it was not Voss she had come four thousand miles to find.

"Miss Fairweather?" she said.

The woman looked up. She was younger than Mara had expected, or perhaps Mara had simply expected everyone from her grandmother's stories to be old. Her hair was tied back with what looked like a length of surveyor's twine, and there was graphite on her cheekbone like a bruise she'd forgotten about.

"You're not from the ministry," Jude Fairweather said. It wasn't a question. "They send men in cars."

"I'm not from anywhere you'd have heard of." Mara stepped inside, out of the rain, and let the shed's warmth close around her—the smell of machine oil and old paper and, beneath it, something faintly metallic that made her pulse jump before she could name it. "My name is Mara Osei. My grandmother was Adaeze Osei. She was a cartographer. In 1947 she joined an expedition up this river, and she never came home."

The woman's hands went still over the chart.

For a moment neither of them moved. Rain ticked against the tin roof. Somewhere down the valley, thunder cleared its throat and thought better of it.

"I don't know anything about that," Jude Fairweather said.

It was a good lie. Mara had spent two years learning what good lies sounded like—in archives, in consulates, in the mouths of officials who had signed letters denying her grandmother had ever existed—and this one had the same texture: smooth on top, hollow underneath. She almost admired it.

"You have graphite on your face," Mara said gently, "and you're holding a pen you've stopped using, and when I said my grandmother's name your breathing changed." She unslung her bag and set it on the workbench between them, deliberately, the way one sets down a wager. "I didn't come here to accuse you of anything. I came because I've been looking for her for two years, and everything dead ends in this river."

Jude Fairweather looked at the bag. Then at Mara. Something moved behind her eyes—not trust, not yet, but the recognition of a fellow inhabitant of some country neither of them had chosen.

"What makes you think I know anything?"

"Because three days ago," Mara said, "a landslide moved your river, and a chest came out of it, and a man named Voss arrived the day after asking questions he already knew the answers to. And because—" she opened the bag, carefully, the way she always did, as though the contents might startle — "because my grandmother's last letter home mentions a brass-bound case with a seal on it, and describes the seal, and I have read that description so many times I could draw it in my sleep."

She drew it now. That was the thing people underestimated about cartographers' granddaughters: they inherited the hands. She took a pencil stub from the bench without asking and sketched it on the corner of a scrap of chart paper—a circle, a mountain peak rising through it, three stars above, and beneath, in a script that had died with its country, a single word.

*Zavreth.*

Jude Fairweather did not look at the drawing. That was how Mara knew. People who had nothing to hide looked; curiosity was reflexive. Guilt kept its eyes on your face.

"That's not a real country," Jude said quietly.

"It was real for eleven years." Mara turned the paper toward her anyway. "It's real enough that both our governments pretend it wasn't. Real enough that when it dissolved in '48, its maps were pulped, its records were sealed, and the eleven people who walked into these mountains carrying proof of where its border ran were written off as lost to weather." She smiled, and knew it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "Weather. As if weather keeps secrets that well."

The rain thickened against the roof. Jude Fairweather set down her pen with great care, aligning it parallel to the chart's edge, and Mara understood that she was buying time, and let her buy it.

"My grandmother wrote letters the whole way upriver," Mara said, softer now. "Smuggled ones—she didn't trust the expedition mail, and she was right not to. My mother kept them in a biscuit tin under her bed for forty years and never told me they existed until she was dying. Eleven letters. The eleventh is dated six days after the date the official record gives for the expedition's end." She watched that land. Watched the surveyor's face do the arithmetic. "Six days, Miss Fairweather. Someone was alive six days after history says they all drowned. And in that letter she writes about an engineer who argued with the expedition lead about a route through the gorge. She doesn't give his name. She calls him *the one who measures twice.*"

The silence that followed had weight, like high water.

"I should ask you to leave," Jude said at last.

"Yes," Mara agreed. "You should."

Neither of them moved.

* * *

Later—much later, after tea that tasted of iron and a conversation conducted in the careful half-sentences of two people circling a fire neither wanted to admit was burning—they sat across the workbench from each other with the chest open between them.

Mara had known it would be there. Knowing did nothing to prepare her.

It was smaller than she had imagined, the brass bindings green with seventy years of river, the wood dark as old blood. And there on the lid, pressed deep enough to survive the silt, the seal: the peak, the stars, the word. Her grandmother had described it in the fourth letter, written from a camp that no longer appeared on any map: *We carry the country's signature into a place the country has decided to forget. I find this either very noble or very foolish, and I have not yet decided which.*

Mara touched the seal with one finger. The metal was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature.

"She described this exactly," Mara said. Her voice came out strange, thinner than she intended. "Word for word. I used to think she was being dramatic."

"She sounds like someone I would have liked."

"You sound like someone who has already read everything in this chest."

Jude Fairweather held her gaze for a long moment. Then, instead of answering, she lifted out the top layer of oilcloth and turned it toward her, and Mara forgot how to breathe.

Photographs. Dozens of them, sleeved in waxed paper that had done its job faithfully across seven decades. And there—third from the left in the first one, standing at the rail of a river steamer with a theodolite case hugged to her chest like a child—her grandmother. Young. Laughing at something outside the frame. A woman Mara had only ever known as a sepia portrait on a funeral shelf, solemn and middle-aged and forever posthumous, suddenly alive, suddenly twenty-six, suddenly wet with river spray and delighted about something.

"Oh," Mara said. It was not a word so much as a wound opening.

She reached for the photograph and Jude let her take it, and Mara held it in both hands the way you hold a bird, and cried without any particular drama, the tears simply arriving the way rain arrives, because there was nowhere else for them to go. Jude Fairweather did not offer comfort and did not look away, and Mara was grateful for both. Pity would have been unbearable. Witness was bearable.

"She never came home," Mara said finally, wiping her face with her sleeve like a child. "My mother grew up with a hole shaped like her. I grew up with a hole shaped by the hole. And here she is laughing on a boat." She set the photograph down with exaggerated care. "What I want to know, Miss Fairweather, is why she was laughing. What were they, that day? Before whatever happened happened?"

"I don't know," Jude said. And then, quieter, in the tone of a person stepping off a ledge: "But I think I might be able to find out. If—if we do this together."

"Together." Mara turned the word over. It had edges.

"I found this chest alone. I opened it alone. I've spent four days alone with it, and I'll tell you honestly what that's been like: it's been like holding a live wire and pretending it isn't humming." Jude pushed a hand through her hair, dislodging the twine, and for the first time she looked less like a locked door and more like a tired woman in a shed. "There's a man in town asking questions. Voss. He's been to see me twice. He smiles like a handshake and he wants this chest buried so deep even the river won't remember it. I lied to him. I'm apparently good at lying, which I did not know about myself until this week, and I don't love what it says about me."

"No," Mara said. "You don't. But you keep doing it."

"Yes." A ghost of a smile. "So. You found me through two years of dead ends and a smuggled letter. He found me through channels I don't want to imagine. We're both standing in this shed because of what's in this box, and only one of us works for the people who buried it." She spread her hands. "That's my whole argument. It's not much."

"It's not much," Mara agreed.

They regarded each other across the chest, across the photographs, across seventy years and four thousand miles and the entire architecture of suspicion that two careful women build between themselves before they permit themselves allies.

Here is what Mara did not say: that she had rehearsed this meeting on the plane, and again in the ferry office, and that in every rehearsal she had been more guarded than this. Here is what she also did not say: that Voss had approached her on her second night in town, bought her a drink she hadn't touched, and offered to fund the rest of her search—expenses, access, sealed files—in exchange for keeping him informed of her progress. That she had said *I'll consider it*, which was true, and had meant *never*, which was also true, and had then lain awake wondering whether refusing him outright would put them both in danger, and whether accepting and deceiving him would make her the kind of person her grandmother would have mapped around rather than for.

And here is what Jude Fairweather did not say, though Mara watched her decide not to say it, watched the sentence rise and get swallowed: something about a map, something about half of one. Mara saw the shape of the omission clearly. She was, after all, her grandmother's granddaughter, and reading terrain was the family trade. The surveyor was holding something back. Perhaps the biggest thing.

Good, Mara thought. So am I.

"Alliance, then," she said aloud. "On terms."

"Name them."

"One: everything either of us learns about the expedition goes into a shared record. No private hoards. I've seen what happens to truth when one person owns it—it becomes leverage, and leverage gets people killed, or worse, forgotten."

Jude nodded slowly. "Two: nobody lies to each other. About the past. About the present we can lie to all we like—Voss, the ministry, anyone with a badge. But between us—" 

"Between us, the maps stay honest." Mara extended her hand. "Three: when we find out what happened to them—all of them, all eleven—we publish it. Whatever it costs. Whoever it embarrasses. Especially if it embarrasses us."

"Us?"

"Our governments. Our families. Whoever's grandfather signed whatever order got them written off." She said it lightly and watched Jude flinch, filed the flinch away, and hated herself a little for filing it. "Someone buried this, Miss Fairweather. Burial requires a gravedigger, and gravediggers have descendants, and descendants have inheritances. I've spent two years learning that everyone in this story is carrying somebody's secret whether they know it or not."

Jude took her hand. Her grip was calloused and strong and did not let go quite as quickly as a stranger's would have.

"Fairweather," she said, as if they hadn't already exchanged names an hour ago, as if the handshake required a new one.

"Osei," Mara answered, and felt something settle in her chest that she refused, for the moment, to call hope.

* * *

They worked until the light failed. Jude brought out the logs, the field notebooks, the half-finished route map with its line ending mid-gorge like a sentence cut off by a hand over the mouth, and Mara read her grandmother's marginalia in the fading daylight with her fingertips resting on the page as though touch might conduct meaning. *Check this bearing twice,* Adaeze Osei had written beside a tributary junction, in handwriting that slanted hard left, urgent even in ink. *R. insists the gorge is passable. R. has not looked at the water.*

"R.," Mara murmured. "Who's R.?"

"The expedition lead, maybe. Rowntree—I think. There's a manifest." Jude hesitated, and Mara saw her weigh something, and then she pulled a folded sheet from under the workbench's false bottom—she had a false bottom, of course she did, the woman organized secrecy like a filing system—and laid it flat. Eleven names. Eleven ranks and trades. Mara ran her finger down the column and stopped.

*Adaeze Osei — Cartographer.*

Her own finger, trembling, on her own name in another century.

And below it, fifth from the bottom:

*Elias Fairweather — Engineer.*

Mara looked up. Jude was watching her with an expression stripped of all its earlier armor—raw, braced, waiting to see what Mara would do with the knowledge that the surveyor sitting across from her was the granddaughter of the expedition's engineer, and had known it since the day she opened the chest, and had said nothing while Mara performed her grief like testimony.

"You knew," Mara said. Not accusing. Measuring.

"I suspected from the first entry. I confirmed it yesterday." Jude's voice was steady but her hands weren't. "I didn't tell you because I don't know yet what it means. Whether he was one of the eleven who died, or one of the ones who—" She stopped. Started again. "Whether he was a hero or a coward or just a man who measured twice and still got it wrong. I don't know. And I couldn't hand you my family's name alongside yours until I knew whether I was handing you an ally or an apology."

The rain had softened to a fine hiss. Outside, the light was going blue and grainy, the valley filling up with evening like a basin with water.

"So we're both suspects," Mara said. "In each other's investigation."

"We're both evidence," Jude corrected. "That's worse."

Mara laughed—a short, surprised sound, the first in days—and then closed her hand around the manifest, around the eleven names, around her grandmother's name most of all, and felt the alliance click into place somewhere beneath the suspicion, the way a bearing clicks into a sightline: not yet trustworthy, but fixed, for now, on the same distant point.

One condition remained unspoken between them, and both women knew it, and neither raised it: that whichever of them discovered the truth first would have to choose what to do with the other. Mara told herself she would choose generously. She was almost sure she believed herself.

It was full dark when they finally blew out the lamp, and Mara was gathering her coat when Jude went very still over the last logbook, the final one, its pages swollen with river water into soft grey waves.

"Mara," she said. "Look at the last entry. Look at the date."

Mara leaned close. The ink had bled but the figures held, and her grandmother's slanted hand had written them, and the date was six days after the end of the world.

Below it, one line, half-dissolved:

*E.F. has taken the case upriver. If anyone reads this—*

The rest was water.
