## 7. Monsoon Warning

The notice came on a Tuesday, folded inside an envelope so crisp it might have been ironed. Jude found it pinned to the door of her rail shed with a brass tack, driven in with the kind of precision that was itself a message: *we know where you work, we know where you sleep, and we are not hurried.*

She read it standing in the doorway while the morning fog burned off the cuttings below the old line.

*NOTICE OF INTENT TO SEIZE.* The letterhead belonged to a ministry she'd never heard of, though its address was a government building she passed twice a week. *Pursuant to statutes governing salvage of state antiquities and submerged cultural property, all materials recovered from the Kessarin riverbed between grid marks 44 and 61 are hereby declared property of the state. Surrender is required within forty-eight (48) hours of service of this notice.*

Below the statute numbers, in smaller type, a name: *E. Voss, Special Archivist, Office of Records Reconciliation.*

"Records Reconciliation," Jude said aloud, to nobody. The words had the flavor of institutional poetry—something that sounded like tidying and meant erasure.

She looked up the river, toward the gorge where the landslide had shifted the current and given up its dead. The water ran brown-green and patient, as it always did, as if it hadn't just handed her a country's signature and her grandfather's handwriting. Forty-eight hours. Voss had been in town four days, asking his quiet questions at the ferry office and the supply store, wearing his academic charm like a coat that fit him slightly too well. She had lied to him smoothly, told him she'd salvaged nothing but bridge hardware, and she had hated how easily the lie came out of her mouth, how natural her own voice sounded when it was hiding something.

Now the hiding had a deadline.

She went inside and barred the door behind her, which was absurd—the shed had one window with a cracked pane and a lock older than she was—but the gesture steadied her hands. The chest sat where it always sat now, under a canvas tarp in the corner, brass bindings dull with river patina, the dead nation's seal pressed into its lid like a wax thumbprint from history. Beside it, stacked in the order she'd come to think of them, were the expedition logs, the photograph envelopes, and the half-map ending mid-route at a place called Serem Gorge, where the ink simply stopped, along with eleven lives.

And beneath those, in her own steel document case, her grandfather's charts of closed lines—the survey she'd inherited without ever being told what it was half of.

Jude crouched beside the chest and rested her palm flat on the lid. The metal was cool even in the building heat. Somewhere under her hand, page seventy-something held the last entry naming E. Fairweather, engineer, in a clerk's careful script, as if he were cargo to be inventoried rather than a man who had walked into these mountains and never walked out—or worse, had walked out alone, carrying something no one was meant to know he carried.

Forty-eight hours. She had spent years measuring things—spans, clearances, rates of decay—and she knew exactly how long forty-eight hours was. It was nothing. It was two sunrises. It was less time than it took the ministry to process a leave request.

But it was enough time to load a boat.

* * *

Mara arrived at dusk, coming up the track from the ferry landing with her pack riding high on her shoulders and rain already greasing the air—not falling yet, but *waiting*, the way it had waited all week, the whole sky holding its breath above the watershed. She was small and quick and carried herself like someone who had learned in childhood that doors open faster for people who look like they belong on the other side of them.

"You got it too," she said, before hello. Not a question.

"Pinned to my door." Jude stepped back to let her in. "Yours?"

"Slid under the door of my room at the Rest House. Same statute numbers. Same forty-eight hours." Mara set down her pack and looked at the chest with the expression she always wore around it—hunger and grief fighting for the same ground. "He's not even pretending anymore. He wanted us to run."

Jude had reached the same conclusion somewhere around the third cup of coffee that morning. A seizure notice served quietly, with a deadline too short for lawyers—that wasn't procedure. That was theater. Voss didn't want the chest surrendered through channels; channels left paper trails, and paper trails left questions. He wanted them frightened, moving, and—if he was lucky—doing something illegal enough that whatever happened to them afterward would be their own fault.

"He wants us on the river," Jude said. "During the first monsoon surge."

"Yes."

"And you're still standing here packing."

Mara smiled, and there was nothing warm in it. "My grandmother walked into these mountains with eleven strangers and a sealed order she probably never saw. I can manage a boat."

They worked by lamplight because the shed's single bulb threw shadows wrong and light right could be seen from the track. Jude laid out her inventory the way she'd lay out a survey: everything weighed, everything assigned a place, nothing trusted to memory alone. Rice and lentils in oilcloth. Fuel for the engine, decanted into unmarked jerry cans. Her instrument case—levels, theodolite, ranging rods broken down—because a surveyor without instruments was just a woman with opinions about terrain. Rope. Tarpaulins. The medical kit she'd kept for a decade against a need that had never come, bandages gone brittle at the folds.

The chest went in last, wrapped in the tarp, lashed amidships where the hull was strongest, wedged between the fuel and the ballast stones she'd brought down from the old bridge footing. It rode low and heavy, the way truth rides in any vessel.

"You're sure about the route?" Mara asked, watching her knot the lashings. Downriver was the obvious escape—downriver was away from Voss, toward the coast, toward lawyers and newspapers and daylight. But downriver was also where Voss would expect them to go, and the ferry landing had eyes.

"I'm sure about the opposite of the route." Jude tightened the final hitch. "We go up."

Mara stared at her. "Up. Into the gorge. In the first week of the rains."

"The expedition went up. The map goes up. Everything we're trying to understand is upstream, and every day we spend running downstream is a day Voss controls the current." She said it and heard, underneath the strategy, the other thing—the true thing. Upstream was where her grandfather's margin notes began. Upstream was where the closed lines on her inherited charts met the open questions on the expedition's half-map, and she needed to stand on that junction with her own feet before anyone took it from her.

Besides, she thought, watching the lamp flame bend in a draft that smelled of wet iron and distant thunder—nobody follows a woman upriver into a monsoon. Nobody sane.

"We'll need a guide for the upper water," Mara said slowly. "Past Serem the channel braids. My grandmother's letters mention rapids that don't exist on any chart."

"I know three men who used to run timber down that stretch. One of them still speaks to me." Jude blew out the lamp. "We leave at first light, before the notice expires and before anyone thinks to watch the landing."

Outside, the thunder had stopped being distant.

* * *

They loaded in the dark, which was either tradecraft or desperation depending on how you counted it, and both women were counting it as both. The boat was an old long-tail workboat Jude had bought two seasons ago for footing inspections, its engine temperamental, its hull patched with fiberglass over original teak. By lantern light she checked the plug, the bailer, the spare shear pin, the fuel line—her hands moving through the liturgy of small craft the way they moved through bridge inspections, every touch a question and an answer.

Dawn came up the color of wet slate. The river had risen overnight—she could see it in the mud line on the pilings, a full hand-span higher than yesterday, the water carrying more debris now, branches and torn leaves riding the current like scouts sent ahead of an army. The monsoon wasn't arriving. It was arriving *now*, and they were going to be on the water inside its opening argument.

Arun, the timber man, was waiting at the landing as promised, a wiry gray-stubbled figure who looked at the loaded boat, then at the sky, then at Jude, and delivered his entire opinion in one sentence: "You are either very brave or very stupid, and the river does not care which."

"That's why I'm paying you," Jude said.

He grunted, stowed himself aft with the dignity of a man boarding his own funeral barge, and took the tiller extension without being offered it.

Mara crouched by the chest one last time, checking the lashings, and Jude watched her lips move—counting, or praying, or reading names only she could see. Then she climbed aboard and took the bow, and Jude cast off the line, and the current took them the way the current takes everything: immediately, indifferently, completely.

The first hour was easy water, wide and slow, the banks sliding past green and dripping. Jude stood at the stern beside Arun and watched the town diminish behind them—the rail shed, the water tower, the ministry's reach shrinking to a smudge—and felt something loosen in her chest that she refused to call relief. Ahead, the valley narrowed by degrees, the hills closing in like a book beginning to shut.

By midmorning the light had gone strange, bruised purple under a cloud deck that had been building since dawn. The air pressure dropped until her ears popped. Arun spat over the side and muttered something in dialect that Jude didn't catch but understood anyway.

Then the rain came.

Not gradually. There was no courtesy of drizzle, no warning sprinkle. The wall of it came down the valley like something released from containment—a gray roaring curtain that swallowed the far bank, then the near bank, then everything, and the sound was enormous, a sound like the sky tearing along a seam. Jude hauled the tarp over the supplies and herself and shouted instructions that dissolved into noise. The river answered the rain within minutes, rising visibly, the current quickening from walking pace to something with intent, the water going from brown to the color of strong tea laced with silt and old leaves.

"Serem Gorge!" Arun bellowed, pointing ahead to where the hills pinched together and the river funneled between black rock walls, its surface suddenly corrugated with standing waves. "In this? We should tie off!"

"Tie off where?" Jude shouted back, and he had no answer, because there wasn't one—no eddy, no landing, nothing but rock and current and the chest lashed amidships with a dead country's seal staring up through the tarp.

Mara turned from the bow, hair plastered to her face, eyes wide and bright with something that was terror and exaltation in equal measure. "There's no turning around!" she screamed, and laughed, actually laughed, and Jude thought: *yes. This. Whatever this is, we're in it now.*

The boat hit the gorge entrance and the world became water. Jude dropped to the engine, throttle wide, steering by instinct and by the half-map in her head—her grandfather's margins had described this stretch in a surveyor's shorthand she'd memorized without meaning to: *keep right at the fang rock, the left channel drowns in high water.* She saw the fang rock materialize out of the rain, black and fanged indeed, and hauled the tiller hard over, and the boat swung past it close enough that spray off the rock hit her face like thrown gravel.

Behind them, faint under the roar, she thought she heard another engine.

She told herself it was echo. The gorge made echoes of everything—thunder, water, her own heartbeat. But her hands stayed cold on the tiller all the same, and when she glanced back through the curtain of rain, the way they had come was already gone, erased, a white wall of storm where a riverbank used to be.

Ahead, the gorge narrowed further, and the water rose to meet it, and the first camp of eleven lost people waited somewhere upstream in the drowning rain—with her grandfather's notes, with Mara's grandmother's trail, with whatever the river and the mountains had decided to keep after seventy years.

Jue Fairweather put her shoulder to the tiller and drove into it.

Whatever they were running from, they were running toward it now.
