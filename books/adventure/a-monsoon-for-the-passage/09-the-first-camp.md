## 9. The First Camp

The river told them where to look, the way rivers always do—by lying.

Jude stood at the bow while Mara held the tiller against the current, and she watched the water's color change from the brown of stirred silt to a strange, bruised green, and she knew before either of them said it that the channel split ahead around some obstruction old enough to have grown its own geography. Her grandfather's margin note had been characteristically spare: *Camp One above the green water. Good gravel bar. Mosquitoes beyond description.* Seventy years, and the mosquitoes had kept their appointment. The gravel bar had not.

What remained of it rose out the river like the spine of a drowned animal—a ridge of compacted silt and stone, furred with young alder, holding back a slack pool that the main current bypassed entirely. The landslide that had given Jude the chest had also given her this: a river in the middle of rearranging itself, pulling back its sheets like a patient sitting up in bed, exposing everything it had been asked to hold.

"Look at the cut bank," Mara said, and her voice had gone quiet in the particular way it did when she was reading terrain the way other people read letters.

Jude looked. The flood had sliced a clean face into the bar's downstream edge, and in that face, layered like the pages of a book someone had pressed flat, were the strata of decades. Dark organics. Pale sand. A band of grey ash from some forest fire no one alive remembered. And near the bottom, just above the oldest gravel, a horizon of things that did not belong: the corner of something canvas-colored. A straight edge too straight to be geology. A ring of rust bleeding outward into the sediment like a wound seen through skin.

"That's not drift," Jude said.

"No," Mara said. "That's a camp."

They beached the boat in the lee of the bar, in water so still it held their reflections upside down and unbroken, and Jude stood for a moment in the shallows simply looking at the place. She had spent her professional life reading ruins—the rail line had taught her that every abandonment leaves a grammar, that decay has tenses. But this was different. This was not a thing that had been left. This was a thing that had been *stopped*, mid-sentence, and buried before it could be finished.

"Logs say they broke camp on the ninth of June," she said. "Nineteen forty-seven. Moved up to the gorge survey. Four days' travel."

"Then we should find nothing here," Mara said. "Or almost nothing. A fire ring. Tin. Things not worth carrying."

Jude nodded, but her eyes were still on the cut bank, on the canvas-colored corner, on the fact that whatever was buried here had been buried *deep*—beneath seventy years of flood deposits, beneath the ash, beneath the sand of a hundred high waters. The river had not merely covered this camp. It had committed it to the archive.

"Let's open the book," she said.

* * *

They worked the way she'd been taught to work a site—slowly, from the outside in, though neither of them was an archaeologist and neither of them pretended otherwise. Mara cut turf blocks with the machete and laid them aside like the pages she was saving for last. Jude troweled. The ground gave up its secrets in the order the ground always chooses, which is to say grudgingly, and in an order that told its own story.

First the ordinary evidence of an expedition doing what expeditions do: a fire ring of stacked river stones still holding, in its heart, a lens of charcoal so dense it looked like lacquer. Tin ration tins, opened not with can openers but with knives and, in one case, an axe, their labels long gone but their contents' ghosts remaining—a residue of rust the color of old blood, a smell, impossibly, of rendered fat that rose when the air touched them after seventy years and made Mara step back and put her hand over her mouth. Buttons. A buckle. A spoon with its bowl worn thin and its handle bent into a hook, the kind of adaptation a man makes when he's been somewhere long enough to stop being a visitor.

"Three tents," Jude said, mapping the post-holes with her surveyor's eye, pacing the distances. "A cook fly here, anchored to that stump. A latrine trench there, backfilled. This is a camp built to be left."

"Built to be left," Mara repeated. "You're sure?"

Jude was sure. She had read a thousand abandoned worksites and knew the difference between a camp abandoned in haste and one abandoned in order. Haste leaves things scattered, half-buried, valuable things in the fire pit because no one had time to be careful. Order leaves things stacked. Order leaves the site swept. Order is what you make when you expect to come back—or when you want whoever finds the place afterward to believe that you left cleanly, that nothing happened here, that the record and the ground agree.

Everything here was orderly. And everything here was wrong.

The wrongness accumulated the way silt does, grain by grain, until Jude could no longer pretend she wasn't standing in it. The ration tins were too many. She counted them twice, then a third time, stacking them in rows the way she stacked anything she needed to think about, and the arithmetic refused to cooperate with the logbook. The expedition's manifest, which she had read so many times she could recite it, listed provisions for twenty-two days at full strength. The tins in the ground, allowing for what the men would have carried out with them to the gorge, accounted for thirty-eight days. Sixteen days of food that had no explanation. Sixteen days of a camp that was supposed to be empty, eating.

"Maybe the manifest was wrong," Mara offered, but she didn't sound like she believed it, and Jude was grateful to her for not pretending.

"Maybe," Jude said. "Or maybe the ninth of June isn't when they left."

It was the child's shoe, though, that stopped them both.

Mara found it, because Mara dug the way she did everything—thoroughly, silently, with an attention that Jude was coming to recognize as a form of devotion. She was clearing the floor of the middle tent, the smallest one, the one whose post-holes sat slightly apart from the other two as if it had been added later or set aside for something that needed its own space, when her trowel knocked against something soft.

The mud had made a mold of it. Mara lifted it in two hands, cradling it, and let the water of the slack pool wash the silt away in brown ribbons until the shape emerged: a small leather shoe, a child's shoe, the kind with a single strap and a buckle, the sole worn smooth along the outside edge in the pattern of a foot that favored one side. It had been mended. Someone had stitched the torn seam at the heel with waxed thread in stitches so small and so patient that Jude's eyes stung before she understood why.

"No log mentions a child," Mara said. Her voice was very level, the levelness of someone holding something down. "No member of the expedition had a child with them. The personnel files—my grandmother's letters—eleven men, all adults, all—"

"I know."

"Then whose—"

"I don't know."

They looked at the shoe together, turning it in the light. It was a good shoe, well made, and it was worn—not new, not lost off a mule in passing, but *lived in*. A child had walked in this shoe for months. A child had been at this camp long enough to wear the sole into its particular signature, long enough for the heel to tear and be mended, and no one had written a single word about it. Not in the logs. Not in the letters Mara's grandmother had smuggled out, letters that named everything, that complained about the rain and the colonel and the quality of the tea. Eleven men, and not one of them had put a child into a sentence.

"People don't not mention children," Jude said slowly. "Unless they've been told not to. Or unless—" She stopped. She heard herself finish the sentence in another register, the one she didn't use. *Unless mentioning it would have been dangerous. Unless the child was the reason for the silence and not an accident of it.*

Mara set the shoe down on the tarp with the care of a woman handling a reliquary, and neither of them said anything else for a while. The river went on past the bar, making its green water sounds, indifferent and enormous.

* * *

They found the tent flap at the end of the second day.

It was the cook fly's rear panel, folded—not dropped, folded—into a canvas square that the mud had welded into a single dense block. Jude carried it to the boat and spent the evening soaking it free in the slack water, working the sediment out of the weave with her thumbs, an hour of small patient labor while Mara cooked rice over the spirit stove and pretended not to watch. The canvas came clean the way old things come clean, all at once and then not quite, holding its stains the way old people hold their opinions.

She had it spread flat on the tarp in the last light when she saw the marks.

Scratches. Inside the fold, on the surface that would have faced *inward*—the surface that would have faced a person lying under it, or sitting under it, or crouched under it in the dark. Not cuts, not tears. Scratches. Made with something small and hard, a nail or a tack or the tine of a fork, pressed into the weave and dragged.

They were counted.

Jude knew counting when she saw it. Her grandfather's field books were full of tallies—chain lengths, culvert counts, days of rain—four vertical strokes and a diagonal across them, five to a group, the oldest arithmetic in the world, the arithmetic of people keeping themselves together by keeping count. These were the same. Groups of five, neat at first, the strokes short and controlled, a hand that was being careful. Then, further along the canvas, the groups grew ragged. The strokes lengthened. The diagonals slashed through four strokes, then through five, then through six and seven, the counter losing discipline as the count grew long, as the hand grew tired, as whatever was being counted stopped being a record and became a plea.

She counted them the way she counted everything, twice, and then a third time because the number would not sit still in her mind.

The official record said the expedition ended on the ninth of June. The official record said the camp was struck, the party moved upriver, and the party was lost—lost in the gorge, lost in the flood, lost to the river, a clean and final word that closed eleven files and drew a line under the whole affair. The scratch marks under the cook fly said something else. They said that after the ninth of June, someone had kept counting. They said that someone had counted through June, and through July, and into August, and that the counting had not stopped until the strokes became so wild they were barely strokes at all, just wounds in the canvas, a tally of one hundred and eleven days.

One hundred and eleven days past the end of the expedition, someone had still been here. Still under this canvas. Still counting.

"Jude." Mara's voice came from very far away, or perhaps Jude had gone very far away. "What is it? What did you find?"

Jude couldn't answer at first. She knelt over the canvas in the failing light with her fingers hovering above the marks, not touching them, the way you hover over a sleeping face. The mosquitoes came out of the alders in their evening columns and she did not move to swat them. Somewhere in her chest, beneath the surveyor's calm she had worn for so long it had grown into her like a root into a pipe, something was rising that she had no professional language for.

The log said nine days of travel to the gorge. The tins said sixteen extra days of eating. The scratches said one hundred and eleven days of someone. The shoe said a child. And the record—the beautiful, orderly, official record, the record her grandfather's half-map had been folded inside of, the record she had begun to think of as the truth the river had kept—said none of it. The record said: *lost.* One word, tidy as a backfilled trench.

"They didn't all die in June," she said finally. Her voice came out strange, flattened. "Someone was alive here in September. Someone was alive here for months after the expedition ended, and they were counting the days, and when they stopped counting—" She stopped again. The last strokes on the canvas were not an ending. They were a hand that could no longer hold the nail.

Mara came and knelt beside her. She looked at the tally for a long time. Then she said, very quietly, in the voice she used for her grandmother's letters: "The river didn't lose them. The river was where they put them."

Jude thought of the camp's terrible order. The stacked tins. The swept ground. The backfilled trench. A site arranged so that whoever came after would find exactly what the record said they should find—and the one thing that couldn't be arranged away, the one witness the arrangers hadn't thought to bury, scratched into the underside of a tent flap and folded face-inward like a letter no one was meant to read.

But someone had folded it. Someone had folded it and left it, face-in, at the bottom of a cook fly, in a camp built to be left. Her grandfather's camp. Her grandfather's hands, or the hands of a man he ate beside for a hundred and eleven days longer than the world was told.

"He knew," Jude said. "He had to have known. He was the engineer. He signed the supply manifests. He knew there were sixteen extra days of rations and he knew who was under that canvas and he carried the map out and he—" Her voice cracked on the last word, and she was surprised to find there were tears on her face, because she had not cried at her grandfather's funeral, or her father's, or at any of the small losses of her uncounted years, and she could not have said whether these tears were for the child in the mended shoe or for the old man who had spent seventy years knowing what she now knew, alone with it, folding it away, face-in.

Mara put her hand on Jude's shoulder and left it there, and they knelt together in the mosquito-dark over a piece of canvas that had outlasted everyone who touched it.

It was full dark when Mara spoke again. "There's a village," she said. "Twelve miles downriver. My grandmother's letters mention it—the last stop before the gorge. The people there traded with the expedition. If anyone saw what happened after June ninth, if anyone saw who was left behind, or who came downriver, or who didn't—"

"Then they'll have remembered," Jude said. She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, the surveyor again, or wearing the surveyor like a coat over something new. "Seventy years is nothing, in a place that keeps a shrine."

They broke camp in the dark, working fast, the boat loaded by lantern light. Jude took one last look at the cut bank before they pushed off—at the strata, at the ash line, at the orderly grave of an orderly lie—and she found she was no longer afraid of what the river had kept.

She was afraid of what the living had kept, and for how long, and what they had done to keep it.

The village was twelve miles down. The rain, when it finally came that night, came like an answer.
