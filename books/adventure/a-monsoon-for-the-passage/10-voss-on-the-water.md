## 10. Voss on the Water

The boat was called the *Merciful*, which Elias Voss found either an oversight or a joke, and he had not yet decided which amused him more.

She was a river launch of the old colonial pattern—teak planking gone silver, a canopy of patched canvas, an engine that coughed to life with the reluctance of a civil servant asked to work past five. Her owner, a broad man named Deng who smelled of diesel and betel nut, took Voss's money without counting it twice and asked no questions about cargo or destination. This was the quality Voss valued most in the river people: a professional incuriosity, purchased cheaply, that the archives back home would have called discretion and billed for at four times the rate.

He sat beneath the canopy with his document case squared on his knees and watched the town fall away behind them. The monsoon had not yet arrived in earnest—the sky held its water the way a debtor holds his purse, grudgingly, promising everything and paying nothing—but the river had already begun to swell. It ran brown and muscular now, carrying whole trees sideways, and where a week ago there had been sandbars there were only brown suggestions of sandbars, drowned and waiting.

"Porters," Deng had said when Voss requested two additional men. "I have porters."

The porters were waiting at the jetty before dawn. They were brothers, or claimed to be—there was a family resemblance in the heavy brows and the way they both looked at Voss's shoes rather than his face—and they were introduced as Tam and Odell, though Voss suspected neither name belonged to either man any more than the canvas duffels between their feet contained luggage.

He did not ask to see inside the duffels. He had already seen enough the night before, when he'd watched them load the boat by lantern light: the long shapes wrapped in sacking, laid among the rice sacks and fuel drums with a tenderness that no porter extends to luggage. When one of them shifted a sack and the shape beneath clinked—not the clink of tools, but the oiled, muffled clink of metal parts kept apart from their magazines—he had turned away politely and studied the moonless water.

They carried the rifles badly concealed because they were not soldiers. That was the trouble with hiring muscle through intermediaries of intermediaries: what arrived was never quite what was ordered. True soldiers concealed weapons the way good surveyors conceal instruments—naturally, as extensions of the body. These two carried theirs like secrets, and secrets, Voss knew from thirty years in the archive service, are the heaviest things a man can transport. You could see the weight of them in the set of their shoulders, in how carefully they sat, in the way Tam's hand drifted toward the sacking every time another boat passed.

Voss made a small note of this. Not a written note—he wrote nothing down anymore that he did not intend to survive—but a mental entry, filed under liabilities, cross-referenced under expenses that would need explaining.

* * *

The river narrowed north of the confluence, and the country changed character around them. The lowland paddies gave way to jungle-clad hills that rose straight out of the water, black-green and dripping, so close in places that the overhanging branches swept the canopy and left wet stripes across the canvas. Mist stood in the gullies like something poured. There were no villages here—or none that showed themselves—and the only sounds were the engine's laboring rhythm, the water slapping the hull, and occasionally the far, hollow boom of a hillside giving up some portion of itself to the rain-fed earth.

Landslides. The whole valley was shrugging off its skin. Voss had read the geological reports; he always read the reports. It was why he was good at his work. Most men in his position relied on informants, on files, on the slow drip of other people's memories. Voss relied on paper—the duller the better—because paper did not lie, it merely waited. A geological survey from 1963 had described this stretch of river as "subject to catastrophic reconfiguration during high water." Which meant, translated out of the language of committees, that the river rearranged its own furniture whenever it felt like it, and whatever lay buried in its bed might as easily be exhumed as entombed.

Which was precisely what had happened. A chest, brass-bound, stamped with the seal of a nation that had been erased from the atlas in 1948, pulled from the silt by a railway surveyor with an unremarkable name and, if the local reports were accurate, an unremarkable manner.

Unremarkable. Voss had read her file twice on the flight over, then a third time with the particular attention he reserved for things that refused to be interesting. Jude Fairweather, forty-one years old—no, he corrected himself, she did not advertise her age; the file said thirty-eight, but files said what people wanted them to say, and a woman who let her hair go grey at the temples and wore men's boots and measured dead railways for a living was a woman who had stopped performing for observers. Thirty-eight, forty-one; it hardly mattered. What mattered was the pattern. Fifteen years with the survey service. No promotions sought. No transfers requested. A commendation in her fourth year that she had reportedly never mentioned. Colleagues described her as competent, quiet, and—this word appeared in three separate assessments, which made it a fact rather than an adjective—*detached*.

Detached people were either empty or full. Voss had built a career on knowing which. An empty detachment was simply absence, a life with nothing in the rooms. But a full detachment—a person who had locked the rooms and swallowed the key—that was a different architecture entirely, and it usually meant there was something in those rooms worth locking away.

And now the river had handed her a key.

* * *

They made camp that first night in a bend where the current slackened, tying off to a fig tree whose roots gripped the bank like fingers. Deng cooked rice and dried fish over a spirit stove, and the smell of it hung in the mist. The porters ate separately, downriver, and spoke to each other in a dialect Voss half-recognized from the border provinces—the old provinces, the ones that had changed hands four times in a century and belonged, in the end, to whoever held the maps.

He sat apart with his case open on his knees, working by torchlight behind the mesh of a mosquito net, and assembled the message.

It had taken him two days to draft, which was, he admitted privately, two days longer than such a thing should take. He had written threats before. He had written orders, requisitions, memoranda of destruction—the quiet documents by which nations tidy themselves. He had once composed, in a single afternoon, the certificate by which eleven sets of expedition records were transferred from "active" to "closed—no further interest," a phrase he had chosen himself and had never since been able to read without hearing, faintly, the sound of a drawer sliding shut.

But this was not a threat and not an order. This was an offer. And offers required a different craft, because an offer must be *wanted*. It had to arrive not as pressure but as permission—as though the recipient had been waiting all along for someone to give her a reason, and here, at last, was the reason, gift-wrapped in officialdom.

He knew what she wanted. Everyone wanted something, and the wanting was always legible if you read the right papers. Jude Fairweather wanted nothing—which is to say, she wanted the one thing that could not be surveyed: an explanation for the shape of her own life. A woman does not spend fifteen years measuring abandoned railways out of love of decay. She measures them because someone taught her that the measuring matters, and then that someone went away, or failed her, or died with his work unfinished, and the measuring became a form of prayer conducted in the church of the closed line.

Her grandfather. E. Fairweather. Engineer, expedition of 1947. The name had surfaced in the third day of Voss's digging, and when it did he had sat very still at his desk for a long moment, the way a man sits still when a mine he buried decades ago turns out to have been lying directly beneath his own path all along.

The grandfather had been on the expedition. The granddaughter had pulled the expedition's chest from the river. And somewhere between those two facts lay the lever, and Voss intended to find it and lean his full weight upon it.

He typed the message slowly on the field transmitter Deng had rigged beneath the canopy, encrypting as he went, addressing it to the relay station that served the upper river settlements—knowing that Fairweather's boat, if she was on the water, would be monitoring. Surveyors always monitored. It was in the blood: listen for signals, plot positions, keep the line open.

The message read:

*MADAM FAIRWEATHER. ELIAS VOSS, NATIONAL ARCHIVE SERVICE. I WRITE WITH NO THREAT INTENDED AND NONE REQUIRED. THE MINISTRY HOLDS IN ITS CUSTODY THE COMPLETE SERVICE RECORD OF EDWARD FAIRWEATHER, ENGINEER, INCLUDING MATERIALS NEVER RELEASED TO THE FAMILY: FIELD CORRESPONDENCE, PERSONAL EFFECTS RETURNED AFTER 1947, AND HIS FINAL REPORT, UNREDACTED. THESE ITEMS ARE SCHEDULED FOR ROUTINE DESTRUCTION UNDER RETENTION POLICY 9-C. I HAVE THE AUTHORITY TO SUSPEND THAT SCHEDULE. YOU HAVE SOMETHING THE MINISTRY BELIEVES IS ITS OWN. I PROPOSE AN EXCHANGE OF CUSTODIES. NO PAPERS CHANGE HANDS THAT EITHER PARTY WOULD REGRET. I AM ON THE RIVER. I WILL WAIT FOR YOUR ANSWER AT THE CONFLUENCE. — V.*

He read it four times. On the fourth reading he deleted the sentence about regret—it sounded too much like conscience, and conscience was a currency he could not afford to flash—and replaced it with *NO RECORDS OF THIS EXCHANGE WILL EXIST AFTERWARD*, which was both truer and more comforting, in the way that oblivion comforts those who fear exposure more than loss.

Then he paused, thumb above the key, and allowed himself the honesty of one private minute.

Because here was the thing he did not put into messages, did not put into memos, did not put anywhere at all except the locked room behind his own eyes: the record he was offering her was real. Edward Fairweather's final report existed. Voss had held it. He had read it in the reading room below the archive, alone, with the ventilation humming, and he had understood within the first page why it had never been released, and by the last page he had understood something worse—that the report was not the problem. The report was honest. It was the *order* the report protested against that was the problem, and the order bore a signature that Voss had grown up seeing on the flyleaf of books, on birthday letters, on the single faded photograph that stood on his mother's mantel until the day she died.

His grandfather had signed the abandonment. Had written off eleven men—twelve, counting the cartographer's unborn grandchild, though no one counted that then—to bury a map. And Elias Voss had spent his entire career, whether he admitted it or not, walking backward through the archive stacks toward that signature, filing things, closing drawers, keeping the corridor dark so that no one else would reach it before he did.

He told himself it was duty. He was very good at telling himself things; it was, after all, the family talent.

He sent the message.

* * *

The answer came back the next morning, or rather the absence of one came back, which was its own kind of answer. The relay confirmed transmission. The relay confirmed delivery to an active receiver on the upper river. And then: silence.

"She heard it," Tam said, crouched at the rail cleaning a rifle he seemed to believe Voss didn't know he had. His English was accented but functional. "The woman. She heard."

"I imagine she did."

"And she says nothing?"

Voss watched the water. The mist was burning off the hills in ragged columns, and the light coming through it had the pale, washed-out quality of old photographs—the same light, he thought, that lived in the expedition pictures in the sealed boxes, the young faces squinting into a sun that no longer existed anywhere but in silvered emulsion.

"Silence is a position," he said. "It means she is considering. If she had decided to refuse me, she would have answered to tell me so—refusal wants witnesses. If she had decided to accept, she would also have answered, quickly, before I grew suspicious. Silence means she hasn't decided." He folded his hands. "Which means the offer is correctly weighted. Heavy enough to matter, light enough to lift."

Tam looked at him with an expression that suggested he had followed perhaps a third of this and trusted perhaps none of it, and returned to his rifle.

Odell appeared at the canopy's edge, dripping, having been forward checking the sounding line. "Water's rising fast," he reported. "Deng says the real rain comes tonight or tomorrow. Says we should think about turning back."

"Deng thinks about his engine and his fee," Voss said pleasantly. "I will do the thinking about destinations." He rose, steadying himself against the boat's sway, and looked north up the narrowing gorge, where the river disappeared between walls of green and stone, toward the country where a dead expedition had walked and a living one was now, at this hour, poling its way up the same brown water with a brass-bound chest lashed under canvas.

He thought about the girl—no, the woman; he kept making that error, thinking of her as a girl, because the file photograph was twenty years old and showed a round-faced cadet squinting under a surveyor's cap. He thought about her sitting in some leaking shelter tonight with the radio crackling, reading his words by lamplight, and he tried to imagine what she would feel. Temptation, he hoped. The specific, corrosive temptation of being offered your own history at a price. He had priced it carefully: not money—money would have insulted her—but the one thing she could not get anywhere else. Her grandfather's voice, unredacted. The chance to hear, across seventy years, what the man had actually said.

That was the elegance of it, and he did not flatter himself to see it as elegant: if she accepted, she became complicit, and complicit people were manageable forever. If she refused, she revealed her route by refusing near the confluence, and the porters would know where to wait. And if she wavered—if she sat up all night with the message and could not choose—then she was already beaten, because a person who cannot choose has surrendered the choosing to whoever applies the next pressure.

Three doors, and every one of them opened onto his corridor.

Only—

He frowned slightly, looking at the water, at the drowned trees riding past with their roots in the air like drowning men.

Only there was a fourth possibility he had not priced, and it nagged at him the way a misfiled document nags: the possibility that she did not want the record at all. That a woman who had stopped counting her own birthdays might have stopped wanting explanations too. That he had built his beautiful lever out of a longing she had sawed off years ago and left to rust on some shuttered platform in the hills.

The mist thickened again toward evening, and the first true rain came not as drops but as a sound, far up the valley—a long, low rushing, like a crowd applauding something terrible. Deng killed the engine and they drifted, and in the silence Voss heard the monsoon arriving behind them, filling the world from the south, closing the river's back door.

He checked his watch. Forty hours to the confluence.

"Make ready," he said to the porters, and did not specify for what, and they did not ask, and the rifles stayed badly concealed, and the rain came down.
