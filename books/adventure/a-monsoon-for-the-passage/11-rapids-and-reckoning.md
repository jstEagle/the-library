## 11. Rapids and Reckoning

The rain came at dawn the way a debt collector comes—politely at first, then all at once.

Jude heard it before she saw it: a sound upstream like a freight train that had jumped its rails and was coming through the forest instead. She had spent twenty years listening to rivers for a living, and she knew this sound the way other people knew their mothers' footsteps on stairs. It was the river changing its mind.

"Mara." She said it once, quietly, and Mara was already awake in the bow, already looking upstream with her grandmother's field notebook pressed flat against her chest as though the paper could be protected by devotion alone.

"How long?" Mara asked.

"Minutes. Maybe less."

The gorge had been closing around them since first light—walls of black basalt rising sheer on either side, so narrow in places that Jude could have thrown a stone from one bank to the other, if there had been banks to stand on. There weren't, anymore. The monsoon's first true surge had swallowed them overnight, and what had been gravel shallows when they made camp was now a brown torrent running eight feet higher, carrying whole trees past them like matchsticks, spinning them slowly in the eddies before sucking them downstream.

The boat was a river boat, built for this water by people who understood it, and Jude had trusted it completely right up until the moment she understood the water had stopped being this water and become something older and angrier—a thing the gorge remembered from before there were boats, or people, or countries to draw lines across.

"Ride it out here?" Mara shouted over the roar.

"No." Jude pointed downstream, where the gorge bent hard to the left around a shoulder of rock. "There's a drop around that bend. I can hear it. If we go through that blind we're cargo, not crew." She was already moving, hauling the chest toward the centerline, lashing it down with the rope they'd used for the fuel drum. "We need an eddy. Any eddy. We get out of the current before the bend or we don't get out at all."

"And if there isn't one?"

"There's always one," Jude said, and did not add: *if you know where to look.* Her hands were doing the arithmetic without her—thrust, drag, the boat's draft, the current's speed where the water piled up against the outside wall. This was the part of her work no one ever saw, the part that didn't go into reports: the way a surveyor read a landscape not as scenery but as forces, everything pushing on everything else, and her job, always, had been to stand in the middle of it with a notebook and refuse to be moved.

She took the tiller. "Get low. Hold the chest down—if it shifts, we capsize."

Mara threw herself across the brass-bound box, arms wrapped around it, cheek pressed to the wet wood as if she could listen to the dead through it. Maybe she could. Jude had stopped being surprised by anything Mara did with that chest.

The bend came up faster than she'd calculated—the flood had added speed to everything, the whole river running like something chased—and then she saw it, exactly what she'd feared: beyond the bend the gorge narrowed again, and the water went through the gap standing up, a line of white chaos where the river dropped maybe six feet into a churning hole, and below the hole, mercifully, a backwater curling along the left wall where the current doubled back on itself.

An eddy. One eddy, small, half-hidden behind a fang of rock, and they were going to hit it traveling sideways or die trying.

"Left wall!" she screamed, and hauled the tiller over.

The boat fought her. Of course it fought her; a boat in floodwater is an argument between two stronger wills than yours, and all you can do is be the tiebreaker. The stern swung. The river grabbed the hull and tried to take it straight into the standing wave, and for three seconds—one, two, three, she counted them because counting was how she kept her body from panicking while her mind worked—they were going to go through the drop broadside, and broadside meant swamped, and swamped in this gorge meant the walls would keep them.

Then the fang of rock did what rock does: it split the current. The main flow slammed past their bow, but a tongue of slower water curled in behind the stone, and Jude threw her weight against the tiller and felt the boat answer—not willingly, but it answered—and they slid into the eddy spinning like a coin settling on a table, twice around, three times, the gorge roaring past them close enough to touch, and then stillness. Relative stillness. The kind of stillness that exists inside a bell while someone is striking it.

They had come through. The boat sat in the backwater, shipping perhaps four inches of water, rocking gently in the counter-current, and above them the walls of the gorge went up and up into rain.

* * *

They bailed in silence for ten minutes. It was Mara who finally spoke, and what she said was not about the rapids.

"The chest is wet."

Jude looked. Water had found it anyway—floodwater always finds everything, that was practically its job description—and it had come in not over the top but through the seams, wicking up through the brass binding, pooling in the bilge beneath the box so that the bottom boards had been sitting in it like feet in a cold bath. When they lifted it, water ran from under the lid in a thin brown thread.

"We open it," Mara said. "Now. On the thwarts. Everything out, everything dried—"

"Not everything." Jude's voice came out flatter than she intended. "You know what's in there. Logs, photographs, the half map. That can all come out. But the inner compartment—" She touched the floor of the chest, the section beneath the false bottom that they had discovered in the rail shed and agreed, without ever quite discussing it, not to open. A sealed compartment, soldered shut, stamped with the same vanished nation's seal as the rest. Whatever E. Fairweather and the cartographer Osei had hidden in there, they had hidden it against exactly this: against water, against time, against people like Voss. Opening it would destroy the seal. And the seal, Jude suspected, was itself evidence—whoever had soldered it shut had done so with intent, and intent left signatures.

"If we don't open it," Mara said, "the compartment floods anyway. Soldered tin in a flooded chest—it'll hold the water *in*, Jude. It'll sit there like a coffin and everything inside it will rot. At least out here we can dry the pages."

"I know." Jude stared at the seal. Rain hammered the gorge, drummed on the tarp they'd rigged, ran off her hood in ropes. "I know. I just wanted you to say it first."

Mara almost smiled. It was a drowned sort of smile. "My grandmother crossed an ocean to leave me things I didn't ask for. You crossed a river. Between us we should be able to open one box."

* * *

They got the chest up onto the thwarts and rigged the tarp above it as a roof, and then, kneeling in four inches of bilge water with the gorge thundering beside them, Jude worked the solder seam with her surveyor's knife and a flat pry bar from the tool roll.

It was slow, brutal work. The solder was good—seventy years old and still holding, laid down by someone who had expected it to hold forever, or at least longer than they lived. Jude found herself thinking about that person's hands. Her grandfather's, possibly. The thought made the work harder and easier at once.

"You could wait," Mara offered quietly. "Dry weather. Proper tools. A week from now—"

"A week from now Voss has a seizure order and two men with rifles." The pry bar bit deeper. "And the river doesn't give second warnings. We nearly lost everything an hour ago. We won't get a third chance at gentle." She wiped rain and sweat from her eyes with her shoulder. "Besides. Whoever sealed this meant it to be opened eventually. By someone. Sealed things are letters, Mara. They're just slow ones."

The seam gave with a crack like a knuckle popping, and then another, and then a long tearing peel as the solder surrendered along one full side. Jude switched to the knife, working it around, patient, until the lid of the inner compartment lifted free.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed canvas, layered like something buried with a pharaoh: documents.

They brought them out one at a time, and even soaked as the outer wrapping was, even after seventy years, the inner layers had done their work—the oilcloth had held, mostly, mostly—and the pages that emerged were damp at the edges but intact. Survey sheets. Tables of figures in a hand Jude recognized with a jolt that went through her like cold water, because she had seen that handwriting her whole life on the margins of closed-line charts: her grandfather's, younger, more certain, the letters upright and unhesitating.

And beneath the survey sheets, a document different from the rest. Heavier paper. An official letterhead bearing the seal of the country that no longer existed, and text in two languages, and at the bottom—

Mara made a sound. Small, involuntary, the sound of someone finding a door in a house they thought they knew every room of.

Jude read the letterhead. Then she read the signature block. Then she read it again, because the words refused to arrange themselves into sense on the first pass.

It was an order. Dated six weeks after the expedition's official last entry. An order concerning the disposition of the survey party, written in the bureaucratic language of abandonment—*assets unrecoverable, personnel written off, records sealed pending review*—and it was signed by an officer whose name meant nothing to her, countersigned by two others whose names meant nothing, and it proved, in the flat indifferent grammar of empire, that the expedition had never been lost at all.

They had been left. Deliberately. Written off like damaged equipment, and the map they carried sealed away with them, and the whole affair folded shut and soldered closed like the compartment in her hands.

The ink was beginning to run.

It happened while she was reading—she saw it start, a feathering at the edge of the signature, the black line softening, bleeding outward into the wet paper fibers like a bruise spreading under skin. Seventy years dry, and now that the water had finally reached them, the pages were dying by inches, every damp minute dissolving a little more of the only proof in the world.

"Tarp," Jude snapped, all business because business was the only thing keeping her hands steady. "Everything spread out. Every page separated—wet pages stick, and if they stick, we lose them both. Photographs face-up, nothing touching. We dry them page by page if we have to hold them over the stove for a day."

They worked. The gorge roared. They laid documents across every dry surface the boat possessed—thwarts, deck boards, the upturned fuel drum, the inside of the tent fly—and hung photographs from the ridge line with wooden clothespins like strange laundry, faces from 1947 dripping in the rain of 2020-something, eleven young people smiling out of sepia at a future none of them had been permitted to have.

Mara handled her grandmother's materials. Jude handled her grandfather's. Neither spoke much. There was a particular silence that falls between two people drying the belongings of their dead, and they both knew how to keep it.

It was near dusk, the light going copper in the gorge, when Jude turned a page of the official order to check whether the reverse side had survived, and saw the protest.

It was tucked behind the order—three pages, handwritten, dense. A formal objection, drafted and signed by members of the expedition themselves. Eleven names at the bottom, some faded nearly to ghosts. She read the opening line, and the gorge seemed to fall away, and there was only the page and the rain and the handwriting she knew better than her own:

*We, the undersigned, having completed in full the survey for which we were contracted, do hereby record our objection to the order dated—*

E. Fairweather. First signature. His name, his hand, his protest against the erasure of himself and ten others, filed into a sealed box and soldered shut, delivered to no one, kept by a river for seventy years.

He had known. He had known what was happening, and he had signed his objection, and he had sealed it in the dark with the map, trusting it to whoever came after.

Trusting it to her.

Jude sat back on her heels in the failing light, surrounded by her grandfather's drying words, and felt the last of her detachment go—not torn away but simply released, like a knot she'd been tying tighter for thirty years finally allowed to slip. She had measured her life in miles because miles couldn't disappoint her. Miles never signed orders. Miles never wrote off a boatload of people and called it administration.

But people had. And one of them had been hers. And one of them had fought it, in the only way available to a man with a pen and a soldering iron and no country left to appeal to.

"Jude." Mara's voice, very quiet. She was holding one of the photographs, the one they'd found earliest, the group shot at the expedition's start. She turned it so Jude could see, and pointed with one finger at the young man at the edge of the frame, tall, squinting against the sun, one hand resting on a surveyor's tripod as naturally as if it were grown there.

"That's him," Mara said. "Isn't it. Your grandfather."

"Yes."

"He's standing next to my grandmother." Mara's finger moved to the woman beside him, laughing, mid-sentence, caught forever in the act of explaining something wonderful. "Look at them. They look like they're telling each other jokes."

"They probably were." Jude's throat was tight. "Seventy years in a box, and the joke's still in there somewhere."

Mara laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her, wet-eyed—and then her expression changed. She was looking past Jude, downstream, at the mouth of the gorge where the river flattened and widened toward the lowlands.

"Jude," she said again, differently now. "How far is it to the confluence?"

Jude followed her gaze. Calculated. Felt the number land in her stomach like a stone.

"Two days," she said. "Three, if the water stays high."

Mara nodded slowly. "Voss knows the route. He has the government charts—we only have the dead ones. He won't stop for weather. He won't stop for the rapids." She looked down at the documents spread across the boat, eleven lives' worth of proof, still damp, still fragile, still bleeding a little at every edge. "Whatever we're going to do with all this, we have to decide before the confluence. Because after it, the choice stops being ours."

Jude looked at the protest in her hands—her grandfather's handwriting, the first signature, the ink still trembling wet at the edges—and made herself say the thing she had been refusing to say since the solder cracked.

"Then tonight we copy everything we can. Every figure, every name, every word. In pencil, in our own hands, twice over, one set each." She met Mara's eyes. "If the river wants the originals back, it can have them. But it's not taking the names twice."

Upstream, deep in the mountains they'd fled, thunder rolled along the gorge like something enormous turning over in its sleep, and the rain, which had slackened at dusk, began again.
