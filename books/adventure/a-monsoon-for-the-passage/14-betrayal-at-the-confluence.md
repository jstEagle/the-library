## 14. Betrayal at the Confluence

The guide's name was Anselm, and Jude had trusted him for eleven days, which in her private accounting made the betrayal almost impressive. He was a lean man with a fisherman's shoulders and a habit of humming when he thought no one was listening—old river songs, work chants, the kind of music that gets into a person's hands. He poled their boat through the shallows with an ease that had made Jude feel, for the first time since leaving the rail shed, that someone else knew what they were doing.

That was why it worked, she understood later. Trust is just a door you leave unlatched because you like the way the light comes through it.

She noticed the first wrongness at dawn on the twelfth day, when the mist still lay on the water like wet wool. Anselm had stopped humming. She hadn't realized how much she'd come to count on the sound until its absence left a hole in the morning, the way a missing tooth does. He stood at the stern with his pole stilled against the current, and he was looking back downriver—not the casual glance of a man checking weather, but the fixed, patient stare of someone waiting for something to arrive.

Jude said nothing. She'd spent thirty years reading terrain for a living, and terrain never lied to her, but people did, and she had learned long ago that the correct response to a suspicion you cannot prove is silence. You watch. You wait. You move your valuables.

*Move your valuables.*

The thought arrived with such clarity that her hands went cold around her coffee tin. The chest sat amidships under the tarpaulin, lashed between the fuel drums where it had ridden since the gorge—the chest, with everything inside it now: the logs, the photographs, the completed border survey, the signed order that read like a death warrant written in tidy bureaucratic hand. Everything. In one box. On one boat. Because she had been tired, and tired people consolidate.

"Mara," she said quietly, not looking away from Anselm's back. "How much farther to the confluence?"

Mara looked up from her grandmother's notebook, which she had been transcribing by lamplight most nights, her handwriting small and fierce. "Two hours. Maybe three if the current's against us." She followed Jude's gaze. "What is it?"

"Anselm stopped singing."

Mara frowned. "So?"

"So men who sing while they work are men who've decided the work will go on." Jude set down the tin. "When they stop, they're thinking about something else."

They could not have turned back even if they'd wanted to. The river behind them was already rising with the season's second surge, brown and muscular, and the only path forward ran through the confluence where the blackwater tributary met the main channel—the place on the expedition map marked with her grandfather's small, precise triangle and a note in pencil so faint Jude had needed firelight to read it: *Good anchorage. Deep water. Watch the far bank.* Seventy years old, and still the most useful sentence anyone had spoken to her all monsoon.

*Watch the far bank.*

She began to work while Mara kept watch on Anselm, and she worked the way she'd learned to work in survey camps before dawn: fast, silent, without wasted motion. The chest came open under the tarpaulin. She took the border survey—the folded sheets of the completed route, the thing every death and every signature had orbited—and she did not hesitate over them, though part of her wanted to weep at handling them again, at the weight of E. Fairweather's protest letter, at the ink that had bled and dried and bled again during the gorge and somehow survived.

Her surveying instruments were packed in a leather case designed for exactly this kind of treachery, though its designers had imagined thieves and rain, not archivists with rifles. The case held her theodolite, her levels, her plumb bobs, her chain bands coiled in oiled cloth. It held false bottoms, because instrument cases always do—compartments for spare lenses and adjustment tools—and Jude slid the survey sheets flat beneath the padded tray where the theodolite sat, then laid the instrument back on top of them as if it had never moved. The case weighed nothing different. Looked nothing different. A government man would see a surveyor's kit and think *tools*, because to men like Voss, tools were never the dangerous things. Papers were. Papers were the only things that frightened governments.

She kept out three pages—not the survey itself but the signed abandonment order, the confession in triplicate—and tucked them inside her shirt, against her skin, where the paper went warm immediately and felt less like documents than like a second heartbeat.

Then she closed the chest, lashed the tarpaulin, and sat back on her heels with her hands steady and her mouth dry, and watched the confluence open up ahead of them through the mist.

It was beautiful, the way rivers are when they meet—two waters refusing to mix, the black tributary running alongside the brown main channel for a hundred meters like two thoughts held side by side in one mind. Her grandfather's triangle sat exactly where the deep water pooled. The far bank rose steep and green, terraced with old landslides, and the mist clung to it in layers.

"Anselm," Mara said, and her voice had changed—it had gone flat and careful, the voice of a woman who had grown up learning which questions got people disappeared. "Why are we slowing down?"

Anselm didn't answer. He brought the pole around and let the current take the bow sideways, gently, expertly, turning them toward the near bank where the water eddied behind a gravel spit—and Jude saw, standing among the trees on that spit with the patience of furniture, four men.

Two were porters from the outfitter in the last town, men who had carried their fuel drums and smiled at Mara's jokes. Two were strangers, broad and quiet, and the rifles they carried were not concealed badly anymore. They weren't concealed at all.

"No," Mara breathed. "No, no—Anselm—"

"I'm sorry," Anselm said, and the terrible thing was that he sounded like he meant it. He wouldn't look at either of them. His eyes stayed on the water, on the pole, on anything human-shaped. "My daughter. There's a hospital in the capital. They said—" He stopped. Started again. "He showed me papers. Official ones. I thought—I believed I was helping return stolen property. That's what the papers said. Stolen property."

"It's not property," Mara said, and her voice cracked on the word. "Those are people. Those are *names*—"

"They're papers," Anselm said, and now he did look at her, and his face was wrecked, and Jude understood that he would carry this the rest of his life, whatever the rest of his life held. "I'm sorry. Get out of the boat. Please don't make them make you."

The boat ground against the gravel. One of the porters waded out to hold the gunwale, and the other stood with his rifle slung but his hand on it, and the two strangers came down to the waterline with the unhurried confidence of men being paid by the day.

And there, stepping out from behind the largest of them, adjusting his cuffs as though he'd merely arrived at a lecture rather than an armed robbery, was Elias Voss.

He looked terrible, Jude thought with a distant, clinical satisfaction. The river had gotten into him somewhere along the way—there was mud dried on his trouser cuffs and his hair was wrong and there were shadows under his eyes that no archive ever gave a man. But his voice, when it came, was as smooth as ever, pitched low, almost gentle, carrying across the water the way voices do in river country.

"Miss Fairweather. Miss Osei." He inclined his head to each of them, courteous as a funeral director. "I want to say first that I regret the theater. I asked for a meeting. My superiors preferred certainty."

"Your superiors," Mara said. She had climbed out of the boat and stood ankle-deep in the shallows with her grandmother's notebook clutched to her chest, and she was shaking, but she did not step back. "Which superiors? The ones who abandoned them? The ones who wrote 'lost, presumed drowned' over eleven living people because the map was inconvenient?" Her voice rose off the water. "You're not recovering property. You're finishing what your office started in 1947."

Something flickered across Voss's face—something old and tired—and Jude filed it away even as her heart hammered, because a crack in a wall is information, and information was the only weapon she had left.

"The disposition of historical materials," Voss said, "is not a matter for sentiment. The chest, please. And any documents removed from it. I know some pages are unaccounted for; my people inventoried the damage after the gorge incident, and the totals don't reconcile." His eyes settled on Jude, mild and merciless. "You've had time to be clever. Don't make me be thorough."

*Thorough* meant the instruments. She knew it the way she knew load limits on old bridges—by feel, by the hum in her own bones. He would take the chest, yes. He might even take her pack, her clothes, the lining of the boat. But a theodolite case was sacred ground to a certain kind of man; it was equipment, state-registered, boring. And besides—

Besides, she had put the order and the protest letter against her skin, and no inventory reached skin.

"You can have the chest," Jude said.

Voss blinked. She watched him recalibrate, watched suspicion war with relief behind his eyes, and she kept her face doing what faces do when a woman surrenders: grief, defeat, the small collapse of someone who has run out of river.

"It's the right decision," he said, softer now, almost kind, and she hated how much he wanted her to believe he was kind. "For what it's worth—and I know it isn't worth much coming from me—your grandfather's service record exists. All of it. When this is settled, I can arrange for you to receive it. Every page. He deserves to be remembered accurately, Miss Fairweather. That's all I'm trying to do here. Remember accurately."

"Liar," Mara whispered.

"Yes," Jude agreed, and stepped aside so the porters could reach the chest.

They cut the lashings. They hauled it up the gravel spit between them, grunting, and one of the strangers pried the lid with a knife blade, and Jude watched seventy years of dead men's handwriting pass out of her keeping into the hands of the office that had killed them, and she let her face break, because breaking was true and also useful. Let him believe the whole inheritance lived in that brass-bound box. Let him carry it downriver believing he had won.

Voss stood over the open chest for a long moment. Rain had begun—not the monsoon's full violence yet, but the fine preliminary drizzle, the sky clearing its throat. He lifted the top logbook with both hands, carefully, the way one handles a relic or an explosive, and Jude saw his thumb trace the edge of a page, and saw him close his eyes briefly, and thought: *he knows what's in here. He's known all along. This isn't recovery for him. It's burial duty. Family business.*

But she didn't understand that yet—not fully. Understanding would come later, in a room with ropes on her wrists and his grandfather's name between them. For now there was only the rain thickening, and the river rising, and Voss giving orders in a language she half knew.

"You'll be escorted downstream," he told them. "There's a launch waiting below the falls. You'll be treated courteously, fed, returned to the rail head within the week. I give you my word."

"Your word," Mara said.

"My word," Voss said, and for one strange moment he looked like a man pleading. "Please. Some things are heavier than truth. You'll learn that, if you live long enough."

The rain came harder. The porters shouldered the chest. And Jude, standing in the rising shallows with the abandonment order warm against her ribs and the border survey sleeping under her theodolite ten feet away in a case no one had touched, watched the men who had her past walk up a gravel bank into the trees—and heard, beneath the rain, beneath the river, a sound she knew from thirty years of listening to mountains: a deep, subterranean groan, the sound of saturated earth letting go.

The far bank moved. Just slightly. Just enough.

Her grandfather's pencil note surfaced in her memory, seventy years patient: *Watch the far bank.*

Not for snipers, she realized. Not for soldiers.

For the landslide.

"Voss!" she shouted, and her voice tore through the rain. "Get off the spit! Now!"

He turned, annoyed, rain streaming down his face, the chest already vanishing upslope with his porters—and above them the whole green face of the hill shrugged its shoulder, and the world became noise.
