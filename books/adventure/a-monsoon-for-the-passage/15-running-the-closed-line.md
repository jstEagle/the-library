## 15. Running the Closed Line

The tunnel mouth was a black seam in the cliff, half-swallowed by creeper and the collapsed spoil of a century. Jude had walked past it a hundred times in her working life without giving it a second glance—one more dead artery in the body of a railway that had stopped breathing decades before she was born. On her grandfather's map, folded now against her ribs inside her oilskin, the line was marked in his precise, patient hand: *Tunnel 9 — 1,840 yards — grade 1:110 falling east — spring seep at 600, 1,200, 1,700. Do not enter after heavy rain.*

The rain had been heavy for two days.

Behind them, up the switchback trail, she could hear the porters' boots on wet stone—two men, maybe three, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who knew their quarry had nowhere left to go. The chest was gone. Taken at the confluence with a rifle barrel resting against Mara's collarbone while the guide who'd sold them out wouldn't meet her eyes. But the border survey—the completed border survey, the thing that had gotten eleven people written out of the world—was zipped into the padded sleeve of Jude's theodolite case, and the men behind her did not know that, and Jude intended to keep them not knowing all the way to the sea.

"Tell me you're joking." Mara stood at the tunnel mouth, water sheeting off her hood, staring into the dark like it might stare back. "Tell me the plan is not the tunnel."

"The plan is the tunnel." Jude was already checking the track, crouching with her lamp, laying her palm against the first rail the way a doctor lays a hand against a wrist. The rail was rusted to a lace, flaking at her touch, but beneath the rust the web still held its shape. "My grandfather mapped this stretch in '46. He noted the drainage. There are culverts at the low points—stone, not pipe. Stone holds."

"Stone holds," Mara repeated, flat. "That's your assessment. Stone holds."

"It held for seventy years." Jude stood. "It'll hold for forty minutes."

She'd found the handcar twenty yards inside, where the maintenance crews of another empire had left it when the line closed—tipped against the tunnel wall, its timber bed gone soft as bread, but its frame intact and its handle assembly surprisingly true. She and Mara hauled it upright together, the two of them straining against the weight, and the sound of it scraping onto the rails rang down the tunnel and came back to them doubled, tripled, a bell tolling in the dark that Jude knew could be heard from the mouth of the tunnel by anyone standing there.

Which was, she supposed, the point. Let them hear it. Let them think the two women were already rattling away down the line, and come in after them, and meet the water.

Or maybe she was lying to herself and it was simply panic making noise. Both were possible. Both had been possible for three days now.

"Jude." Mara's voice came low through the dark, and in it was the thing they'd stopped saying to each other since the confluence, the thing that sat between them like a third passenger: *we lost it, we lost her grandmother's shrine-country, we lost the chest, and all we have left is what you hid in your instruments and my nerve.* "How far?"

"Eighteen hundred yards. A mile and change. The portal on the east end opens above the river gorge—my grandfather's note says the old loading ledge is still there. From there it's a descent to the water, and the river runs down to the ferry crossing at Kanda."

"And the water in here?"

Jude swung her lamp down the tunnel. The beam died about thirty feet out, swallowed whole. She could hear it, though—could hear the seep, a whisper of water finding its way through the mountain's joints, patient as everything else in this country. "Rising," she said. "But the grade falls east. We'll be going with it."

"That's supposed to reassure me?"

"It's supposed to be true. I've stopped promising more than that."

Mara made a sound that might have been a laugh in a better year, and swung herself onto the handcar's rear seat. Jude took the handles. Her shoulders had been screaming since the gorge, since the surge, since she'd stood waist-deep in freezing water holding a boat together with her body while the chest took the river inside it—but her shoulders had been screaming for years, in one way or another, and she'd learned long ago that the body's complaints were just weather. You surveyed through weather.

She pumped. The handcar lurched, shrieked, caught, and rolled.

* * *

The dark in the tunnel was not like other dark. Jude had worked tunnels before—had walked a hundred of them with her lamp and her chain, measuring decay—but there was a difference between walking a tunnel with a survey crew's floodlights and flying down one on a dead man's handcar with a penlight zip-tied to her chest and the sound of pursuit somewhere behind. The dark here was total and wet and old. It pressed on the lamp beam like a thumb. The tunnel breathed, a slow tidal exhalation from its far end, and the breath smelled of stone and river and the mineral ghost of seventy-year-old smoke.

The rails sang under them. That was the thing she hadn't expected—the singing. Every joint in the rotted track struck a note as the wheels crossed it, a cracked iron percussion, and the notes ran ahead of them down the tunnel and came back as echo, so that they traveled inside their own noise, wrapped in it, hidden by it. Jude found a rhythm. Pump, glide, pump, glide. Her grandfather had been an engineer; somewhere in the blood there was apparently a sense for load and counterload, for the moment to push and the moment to let the machine carry you. She had spent her whole life thinking she'd inherited nothing from him but a locked box of maps and a silence. It turned out she'd inherited his hands.

"Seep at six hundred yards," she called back, when the beam caught the shine of wet stone widening into a trickle. "Right where he said."

"You're reading his notes out loud?"

"Keeps me honest." It was more than that and they both knew it. She was reading his notes the way you'd read letters. *Spring seep at 600,* he'd written in 1946, in a hand so careful it looked typeset, and beneath it, in pencil, added later, in a hand gone ragged: *still running true, 1971.* Thirty years between the two lines. Thirty years of a man coming back to a dead tunnel to check on its water. She had spent three days trying not to think about what kind of loneliness that was.

The trickle at six hundred yards was a trickle. They crossed it with a hiss and a spray.

At nine hundred yards the first culvert came, a stone arch low on the left wall, and Jude made Mara shine the lamp into it while she held the car's brake lever with her whole weight. The arch was running—water pouring through it in a brown rope, thicker than it should have been, thicker than a trickle upstream should have fed it.

"That's the mountain draining," Mara said. "Isn't it."

"That's the mountain draining." Jude pumped them on. "It's been draining for two days. It's had time to get a head start on us."

"And when we catch up to the head start?"

Jude didn't answer. Ahead, the rails sang, and the dark ate the light, and somewhere behind them—faint now, or maybe imagined, or maybe not—the sound of boots on stone had been joined by another sound, a lower one, a grinding she felt in her teeth before she identified it: rock moving against rock, the mountain shifting its weight, the slow collapse of a portal or the fast collapse of one.

"Pump," she said.

"I am pumping."

"Pump like the men behind us are already in the water."

* * *

At twelve hundred yards the second spring found them, and this one was no seep. It came through the crown of the tunnel in three places, silver threads thickening as the lamp swung across them, and the ballast beneath the track had gone soft enough that the handcar's wheels cut twin furrows through standing water. Jude's arms were beyond burning now, into the flat white country past burning, and she pumped anyway, because behind them the grinding sound had stopped being ambiguous. She glanced back once—once—and saw the lamp beam catch it: the west portal, a distant coin of grey light, and something moving in the coin. Men. Two silhouettes, then three, wading through water that came to their knees at the tunnel mouth and was, by the look of the spray they kicked up, coming in fast.

"They're in," Mara said. "Jude. They're in the tunnel."

"I know."

"The water—"

"I know." Her grandfather's note again, surfacing unbidden: *Do not enter after heavy rain.* And beneath it, in the ragged pencil: *They never listened either.* She'd thought that meant survey crews. Standing in the rising black water on a dead man's handcar, she understood it had meant something else entirely, something from 1947, something she would not have time to ask about until—until later, if there was a later, if the mountain let them keep the appointment.

The culvert at twelve hundred was running like a wound.

They shot past it. At fourteen hundred the water reached the ties. At sixteen hundred it reached the axles, cold as confession, dragging at the car's frame, and the grade that had been their friend all the way down was now the enemy's too—the water was falling east with them, accelerating, a river being born in the dark behind them and learning its strength. Jude could hear it now over the rails' song: not a trickle, not a seep, a *push*, a broad soft roar of water shouldering through stone, and she knew with a surveyor's certainty that the culverts were overwhelmed, that the mountain was emptying its two days of rain into this throat all at once, and that the coin of grey light behind them was about to go out.

"Off the car at the seventeen hundred mark," she shouted. "There's a cross-adit—my grandfather marked it—maintenance gallery, climbs to the old ventilation shaft. It comes out above the loading ledge."

"You said the tunnel goes through!"

"The tunnel goes through into the river!" The water was at her knees. The car was beginning to yaw, the rotted ties letting the wheels wander. "Seventeen hundred! Watch for the white paint—"

The lamp caught it: a smear of white on the right-hand wall, ghost of a stencil, *ADIT 3*, and below it a black square of doorway that her grandfather had drawn on a map seventy years ago with a note in the margin she'd read a hundred times without understanding until this exact moment: *adit 3 — climbable — E.F. checked 1949, 1953, 1961.* He had checked it. Three times across twelve years, a man walking alone into a dead tunnel to make sure the escape route worked. He'd been keeping the door open for someone. It had taken seventy years for the someone to arrive.

They went through the doorway as the water took the handcar.

Jude felt it more than saw it—the sudden lightness at her back, the machine gone, and behind it in the dark the sound of the river arriving like a freight train through a station, a wall-roar that filled the tunnel from wall to wall and snuffed the grey coin of the west portal out of existence. She didn't look back. She climbed. The adit floor rose in rough-cut steps, slick with seventy years of seep, and Mara climbed above her with the theodolite case strapped across her chest like a child, and the water came into the adit behind them ankle-deep, knee-deep, chasing them up the steps with the patience of something that had all the time in the world and knew they didn't.

The gallery was narrow, timbered, breathing. They climbed it on hands and knees in places, through a black that the penlight barely dented, and twice the mountain spoke around them—deep groans, the timber creaking, small stones ticking down past their shoulders—and twice they stopped and pressed themselves against the wet rock and waited for the mountain to finish its sentence. It finished. They climbed.

Then, ahead, grey. Not the grey of the portal behind them but the grey of daylight, thin and ragged, filtering down through a shaft choked with vine. Mara reached it first and tore at the growth, and suddenly they were out—out onto the old loading ledge, a shelf of cut stone jutting over the river gorge, with the whole grey-green world spread out below them and the rain coming down soft and steady as if nothing at all had happened inside the mountain.

Behind them, the tunnel's east portal exhaled. Water burst from it in a brown torrent, carrying the handcar's broken frame, carrying ballast, carrying—Jude made herself watch, made herself know—carrying the light of the men's lamps, and then not carrying it. The lamps went out of the torrent one by one, or didn't come out at all. The roar diminished. The river took what it had taken and kept the rest.

Mara sat down on the wet stone, hard, and put her face in her hands. Jude stood at the ledge's edge with the rain running off her chin, and looked down at the river—*their* river, the one that had given up the chest, the one that had taken the boat, the one that kept everything and gave back only what it chose—and felt, beneath the exhaustion and the cold and the grief, something she had no name for. Not triumph. Nothing so clean. Just the knowledge that her grandfather had checked this door three times, and the door had held, and she was standing in the room it opened onto, alive, with the truth strapped to her chest.

"He's dead," Mara said at last, her voice raw. "The guide said three men. I counted three lamps."

"I know."

"Voss will send more."

"He'll send more." Jude shouldered the theodolite case. Below, the river ran east toward Kanda, toward the ferry crossing, toward the sea and the world where paper could still be published and names could still be read aloud. "But he'll be sending them to a river that's already decided. Come on. We've got half the truth, no boat, and about six hours of light."

Mara looked up at her, rain and river-water and something else on her face—something that might, in a different life, have been hope. "And what do we do with half the truth?"

Jude started down the goat track toward the water, her grandfather's map dry against her ribs, her grandfather's door closed behind her.

"We finish it," she said. "It's what he left it to us for."
