## 16. What Voss Wants Buried

Elias Voss had always believed that a man's character revealed itself in what he chose to carry.

His father had carried a cigarette case, silver, dented at one corner, and a photograph of a woman who was not his mother. His grandfather had carried, according to the family legend that had been polished smooth by three generations of careful retelling, nothing but a fountain pen and a sense of duty. And Elias himself—Elias carried a leather folio stamped with the crest of an archive that did not officially exist, containing orders that did not officially exist, authorizing him to do things that would never appear in any record he controlled.

He sat in the stern of the chartered boat as it moved upriver through the drowned green country, and he watched the two porters pretend their rifles were fishing rods, and he thought about his grandfather's hands.

They had been beautiful hands. That was the terrible thing about them—the thing no one warned you about, that monsters came with beautiful hands. Aldous Voss had played Chopin on Sunday evenings, had folded letters into crisp thirds with a single motion, had signed documents with a flourish so elegant it looked like a signature on a marriage certificate rather than on the paper Elias now kept in a fireproof box beneath the floorboards of his flat: *Abandonment of Expedition, Authorization for Non-Recovery, by order of—* and then the name, and then the date, and then eleven men and women struck from the rolls of the living with the same fluid grace he used to sign Christmas cards.

*Non-recovery.* As if they were parcels that had gone astray. As if the rain had simply misplaced them.

"Sir." The elder porter, a man called Deng whose loyalty had been purchased in the currency of a debt rather than money, nodded toward the bend ahead. "The village."

Voss lifted his binoculars. Through the heat-shimmer he could see the shrine at the water's edge—a small stone structure furred with moss, hung with faded cloth, tended the way riverside people had tended such places since before there were maps to be buried. He had read the file on this village. It appeared in none of them. That was the point of files like his; they were not records of what had happened but records of what had been prevented from happening, which was a far more delicate discipline.

The two women were there. He could see them—Fairweather tall and sunburned beside the shrine, the Osei girl kneeling, her hands pressed to the stone as if it were warm. As if something under the stone answered her.

Voss lowered the binoculars and felt the familiar ache behind his sternum, the one he had learned to call indigestion for forty years.

---

He had been nine years old the first time he understood.

It was a Sunday, after the Chopin, and his grandfather had taken him into the study to show him the globe—the old one, the pre-war one, with its pastel empires and its borders drawn in confident ink. Aldous Voss had spun it slowly, a godling turning his creation, and stopped it at the river country, and tapped the glass over a stretch of green.

"There," he said. "That is where I saved three nations from a war."

And Elias, nine years old and full of Sunday light, had asked how.

His grandfather had smiled—that smile, the one from the photographs, the one that made strangers trust him with their luggage—and said, "By knowing when to stop looking. Most men think courage is going forward. Sometimes, my boy, courage is closing the book."

It had taken Elias thirty years to understand that the book his grandfather closed had people inside it.

---

The plan was simple, because simple plans survived contact with rivers. They would take the women at the confluence where the tributary narrowed, take the chest, and release them—release them, always release them; Voss had never once authorized violence beyond the theatrical, because corpses generated inquiries and inquiries generated readers, and readers were the one species his archive existed to starve. He would offer Fairweather the trade first. Her grandfather's full service record. Every page. In exchange for the chest, unopened, returned to the current that had kept it so faithfully for seventy years.

He knew she would refuse. He hoped she would refuse, in the way a man hopes for the thing that will ruin him, because refusal meant she was better than he was, and someone had to be.

Deng brought him tea, sweetened the local way, and Voss drank it looking at the water.

"You have done this before," Deng said. It was not quite a question.

"Once," Voss said. "A long time ago. A different river."

"And the papers?"

"Went back into the ground." He turned the cup in his fingers. "All except one page. I kept one page."

Deng waited, with the patience of a man who had learned that silence extracted more than questions.

"It was a list," Voss said, before he could stop himself, which was unlike him, which was the river getting into him, the way these places got into everyone eventually, dissolving the seals a man built around his interior. "Eleven names. My grandfather's handwriting. He'd written them out—not in the official record, in his private notebook. All eleven. With dates of birth." He heard his own voice go flat and distant. "He knew their birthdays. He wrote them down like he was going to send cards."

"And did he?"

"He burned the notebook. I took the page out first." Voss set the cup down. "I was twenty-six. I told myself I kept it as evidence. Leverage. Insurance." The ache behind his sternum shifted, settled, became what it had always been. "I kept it because it was the only place in the world where those names were written next to the word *born* instead of the word *lost*."

Deng said nothing. Downriver, thunder assembled itself among the hills like a slow animal standing up.

---

They took the women at midday, at the confluence, and it went almost exactly as planned, which should have satisfied him and did not. The Fairweather woman did not reach for anything when the rifles came out; she stood very still with her hand resting on the chest's brass binding, and looked at Voss with an expression he had spent his career studying in other people's faces and had never once expected to receive: recognition. Not of his face. Of his situation.

"You've read it," she said. "You know what's in it."

"I know what's in everything," Voss said, and hated the sentence even as it left him, its institutional smoothness, its inherited polish. It was his grandfather's sentence. It had come out of his mouth wearing his grandfather's voice.

"Then you know it wasn't lost," Mara Osei said. She was holding her grandmother's field notebook against her chest like a shield, or a child. "You know they were abandoned. You know who signed it."

"I know a great many things that are not true," Voss said, "in the sense that they exist in no record anywhere, and therefore cannot be said to be true at all. Truth, madam, is a function of custody. Whoever holds the paper decides what happened. This has been the entire science of my profession for a hundred years, and we are very good at it."

"Your grandfather signed the order," Fairweather said quietly. "Didn't he."

The rain began then, the first fat drops of the afternoon storm striking the river like fingertips testing a surface. Voss did not answer, and his not-answering was an answer, and he watched her understand it—watched the recognition in her face complete itself into something worse than anger. Pity, almost. The look one surveyor gives another when the instrument reads a number too large to record.

"We'll take the chest," he said. "You'll be released downstream with your boat and your instruments. No one will follow you. In exchange—" he drew the envelope from his folio, the service record, E. Fairweather's entire official life in thirty-one pages, "—you may have this. Everything the archive holds on your grandfather. Which is more than the archive knows it holds."

Jude Fairweather looked at the envelope for a long moment. Then she looked at the chest, and then at him again, and Voss saw her make the calculation—not the one he'd offered, but another one entirely, some private arithmetic running behind her eyes, and he realized with a cold, slow horror that she had already done something. Already hidden something. Already divided the truth the way her grandfather must once have divided it, half here, half there, so that no single custody could kill it.

She handed over the chest without a word.

They released her, as promised. He watched the boat recede through the rain, two figures hunched under a tarpaulin, growing small and then gray and then gone, and Deng said, "You let her keep something."

"Yes," Voss said.

"Why?"

Voss opened the chest right there in the rain, under the tarpaulin, and found what he had known he would find: the logs, the photographs, the half-map—all of it intact, all of it authentic, and all of it, every page of it, already published in his mind's eye, already printed, already read aloud in a village shrine by voices that did not care about custody. Because a woman who gave up the chest that easily had given up only the chest. The rest of her inheritance was somewhere in those surveying instruments, folded into theodolite cases and stenciled onto tripod legs, traveling downstream at this moment in the company of the only two people alive who wanted it to survive.

He had traded a body for a soul and kept the body.

"Because," Voss said, "her grandfather protested the order. Did you know that? E. Fairweather filed a formal protest—signed, dated, witnessed. The only signature on that document that isn't a coward's." He closed the chest. "Two grandfathers, Mr. Deng. Two men on the same river, seventy years ago. One signed the paper that erased eleven lives, and one signed the paper that objected, and both of them went home and raised families and never said a word, and here we are, the grandsons, still carrying what they couldn't." He laughed, briefly, without any humor in it at all. "Mirror images. Except hers chose memory, and mine chose Chopin."

---

That night, anchored below the confluence while the storm worked itself out against the hills, Voss took out the page.

He had carried it for forty years and had never once allowed himself to read it slowly. Eleven names in Aldous Voss's elegant hand, each with a date of birth, each with a small annotation in the margin—the cartographer, *drew better than any man I employed*; the engineer, *objected, honorable*; the botanist, *sang*. His grandfather had known them. Had eaten with them, perhaps. Had shaken their hands in some dockside morning and written down their birthdays like a man intending to remember, and then had signed the other document, the real one, the one with the seal and the weight of state behind it, and had gone home and played Chopin.

Elias Voss had spent his whole career believing he protected governments. Standing between the present and the past, keeping the dead quiet so the living could sleep. It was a good story. It had a uniform and a salary and a leather folio with a crest on it.

But sitting in the lamplight with the rain coming down and the page trembling slightly in his beautiful hands—his grandfather's hands, he realized, he had his grandfather's exact hands—he finally admitted what Jude Fairweather had seen in a single look across the water: that there was no government left that remembered this expedition, no ministry that would fall if the map surfaced, no nation whose peace depended on eleven names staying drowned. There was only him. Only the last custodian of a crime, guarding a vault whose owners had all died, keeping a secret for a man who had been dust for decades, out of nothing but inheritance and habit and the awful human difficulty of admitting that your grandfather was not the man on the pedestal.

He wasn't protecting a country. He was protecting Aldous Voss. He had been protecting him his whole life, the way you protect a parent—reflexively, gratefully, long past the point where protection serves anyone but the protector's need to keep loving them.

And across the dark water, downstream, a woman was carrying the other half of the truth in her surveying instruments, and she had chosen, already, irrevocably, to give the dead their names.

Voss folded the page away. Then, after a moment, he took it out again, and smoothed it flat on his knee, and read all eleven names aloud, very quietly, into the rain—something no Voss had ever done—and the sound of them was smaller than he expected and heavier than he could lift.

In the morning, he gave the order to turn the boat downstream.

Not to pursue her. Deng asked, and he did not answer, because the answer was a thing he would not know how to say until he was standing in front of her with the page in his hand—but the boat turned, and ran with the flood, and the monsoon came on behind them like a decision being made.
