## 21. Miles Versus Years

The letter had waited eleven months in the bottom of a duffel bag, and Jude Fairweather had carried it through a monsoon, a manhunt, a flood, and a funeral that wasn't a funeral, and now it sat on the kitchen table of her rail shed looking exactly as unimposing as it had the day the solicitor's envelope first arrived: heavy cream paper, a wax seal she'd never broken, her name written in a hand she hadn't seen since she was nine years old.

*Jude Fairweather, Esq., care of the Northern Survey Office.*

Esq. Her grandfather had never once called her by anything but *poppet* or, when she'd earned it, *engineer*. He had not lived to know she'd become a surveyor at all. He had died believing, perhaps, that the closed lines had closed over her too.

She made tea she didn't drink. She sat with the letter through the whole length of an afternoon, the light moving across the table in a slow diagonal, and when the sun finally touched the shed's western wall and began to climb it, she picked up the letter opener her grandfather had given her—a brass thing shaped like a rail spike, a joke gift from her twelfth birthday—and slit the envelope in one clean motion.

Inside there was no legal language. No inventory of assets, no deed to the shed, no instructions about the model locomotives or the surveying instruments she already owned. There was a photograph and a single sentence.

The photograph was small, the size of a postcard, its edges gone soft and sepia. Eleven people stood on a riverbank in front of a boat hung with drying nets, and every one of them was laughing or nearly laughing, caught mid-gesture, mid-joke, mid-life. A woman in the front row held a theodolite like a bouquet of flowers. A young man at the edge had his arm slung around the shoulders of a lean, sunburned figure with a surveyor's chain coiled over one shoulder like a pet snake.

E. Fairweather. Her grandfather, thirty-one years old, grinning like a man who had no idea what the river was going to ask of him.

Jude turned the photograph over. On the back, in the same hand as the envelope, one line:

*Finish it or forgive it.*

She read it four times. Then she set it down, very carefully, the way you set down something that might still be alive, and walked out of the shed into the evening because the walls had become too small to hold what she was feeling.

* * *

She went back to the line the next morning, and for the first time in her working life, she brought nothing to measure with.

No chain. No level. No notebook, not even the battered one she'd kept since her apprenticeship, its pages soft as cloth from handling. She wore her oldest boots and carried only water, the photograph in a waterproof sleeve over her heart, and the strange, weightless sensation of walking without an instrument in her hand—as though she'd taken off a backpack she'd forgotten she was wearing and her body kept reaching for the strap.

The line began, as all her lines had begun, at the old mile marker zero below the town, a granite post worn smooth by a century of weather and neglect. Someone had chalked a crude heart on it sometime in the last year. Jude touched the stone in passing, the way you'd touch a doorpost, and started walking.

She had measured this line so many times she knew it the way you know your own pulse. She knew where the ballast had slumped, where the drainage culverts had rusted through, where the cuttings held snow in winter and heat in summer like cupped hands. She knew the exact chainage—she could have recited it—where the embankment gave out over the first ravine and where the tunnel mouth swallowed the track into darkness. She had walked it with instruments, with clipboards, with the flat professional attention of a woman cataloguing a corpse.

She had never once walked it just to walk it.

The morning was cool and enormous. Mist stood in the valley in long white bars, and the rails—where they survived—caught the early light and threw it back in two bright threads running ahead of her into the country, like the line was still going somewhere, like it had never been closed at all, only resting. A thrush was working the cuttings. Somewhere below, the river ran high and brown with the tail of the monsoon, carrying the last of the season's water down toward the coast, toward the village, toward the shrine that now had a roof on it and a stone with eleven names cut deep enough to outlast the rain.

She had done that. They had done that. She and Mara, with a water-stained duplicate and a grandmother's notebook and a confession that had arrived, at the very end, in a plain envelope with no return address, postmarked from a city Voss had once called home. *I am not asking forgiveness,* the letter had said, in a hand that shook only at the signature. *I am asking that the record show I know what my father did. That is all the honesty I have left.* Jude had read it aloud in the shrine with the rest, and the elder had listened with his eyes closed, and when it was done he had said, in his unhurried way, that the river keeps what it keeps and gives back what it gives back, and that a man who confesses has at least stopped lying to the water.

She thought about Voss sometimes now, without the old flinch. Two grandfathers, two signatures on the same terrible page—one protesting, one ordering—and two grandchildren who had spent their whole lives walking the consequences without knowing they were walking on anything at all. She had hated him for a season. She found, walking now in the clean cold air, that what remained was something more like recognition, and a sorrow she didn't have to carry for him. He had his own line to walk. He was walking it, wherever he was, in whatever direction a man walks when he has finally stopped measuring his grandfather's sins in miles and started measuring them in years.

The first tunnel came at the four-kilometre mark. She knew the number without checking; that was the trouble and the gift of her, that every distance she'd ever walked had lodged itself in her like shrapnel, permanent, precise. She had once believed that was all she was: a container for measurements. A woman who knew how far everything was and how near nothing was.

She stood at the tunnel mouth and breathed the cold breath of the mountain. Inside, her hand-car journey with Mara still lived in the dark—the screech of the rotted sleepers, the water rising behind them like a hand closing, Mara's laughter, wild and disbelieving, when they'd burst out the far portal into daylight and rain. She could have told you the tunnel's length to the metre. Instead she stood there and let the dark be dark, and the memory be a memory, and neither of them a measurement.

Then she walked through it, and the light on the far side was so sudden and so gold that she laughed out loud, alone, the sound bouncing off the rock, and an echo came back that might have been laughter too.

* * *

Near the old halfway water tower she sat down on the ballast and took out the photograph and looked at it properly for the first time—not hunting for her grandfather, not cross-referencing faces against the expedition roster she now knew by heart, but just looking. Eleven young people at the beginning of the thing that would end them. She knew their names now the way the village knew them, the way the published account knew them, each one attached to a face, a role, a last entry. Adeyemi, the cartographer, Mara's grandmother, holding the theodolite like flowers. Okonkwo, the boatman. Lindqvist, the doctor, whose ration ledger had recorded, in the final weeks, giving his own portion away in increments so small no one would notice. The child's shoe from the first camp had a name now too, or nearly one—a daughter of a riverside family, the elder had said, taken in by the expedition when her village flooded, lost with them at the gorge. They had put her name on the stone as well. The smallest name, cut the deepest.

And E. Fairweather, who had come home when the others didn't, and carried the question of that homecoming for fifty years like a stone in his boot, and never told his granddaughter anything except how to read a contour line and how to keep her instruments dry, which was, Jude understood now, the same lesson twice.

*Finish it or forgive it.*

He had left the choice to whoever found his work, and his work had found her, and she had finished it—the route published, the border documented, the abandonment order dragged into the light with every signature attached. And she had forgiven it—not the order, never the order, but the man who signed the protest and then went silent for fifty years; the man who chose his granddaughter's quiet life over the dead's loud justice and hated himself for it in whatever language engineers hate themselves in, which is the language of unfinished drawings. She had finished his drawing. She had forgiven his hand. Both, and she had not known until this morning, standing at mile zero with nothing in her hands, that both were possible, that the letter had never been a fork in the line at all but a junction where both tracks ran true.

A train passed on the far valley line—the living line, the one she'd signed on to survey in the spring, the one that carried passengers and produce and the ordinary noise of people going somewhere. She watched it move along the valley floor, small and bright and purposeful, and felt no envy and no grief, only the pleasant ache of a body that had walked six kilometres before noon and would walk six more.

That was the other thing, the thing she was only now letting herself notice: she had stopped counting the kilometres. Somewhere in the last hour the numbers had gone quiet. Not gone—she could summon them if she wished, the chainages and the gradients, the whole architecture of her profession still standing in her head like a well-kept shed—but they had stopped being the point. She was not measuring the line. She was walking it. And the line, walked instead of measured, turned out to be a different country entirely: thrush-song and rail-shine and the smell of wet granite, the particular gold of morning through a tunnel mouth, the memory of a friend's laughter in the dark. A country you could only enter by putting the instruments down.

Her whole life she had measured herself the same way she measured rail—by distance covered, by lines closed, by the accumulating mileage of a woman moving steadily away from everything. She had counted her birthdays the way she counted sleepers: without interest, as infrastructure. She had told Mara once, on the boat, in the rain, that she didn't know how old she was without doing arithmetic, and Mara had looked at her with such plain sorrow that Jude had laughed to break it, and the laughter had not quite worked.

But the river had given up its chest, and the shrine had its stone, and the dead had their names, and this morning her grandfather's photograph was warm from her own body, and she was sitting on a closed line in the sun doing nothing at all, and it was enough. It was more than enough. It was, she realized with a feeling like a knot untying after years, *time*—time passing through her and being hers, unmeasured, unconverted, just spent, gloriously spent, on a morning and a walk and a memory.

She was thirty-eight years old. She knew it suddenly, the way you know a note is in tune. Thirty-eight, and her grandfather had been thirty-one in the photograph, and the difference between them was seven years and a whole life, and she was going to spend the rest of hers counting forward.

* * *

She reached the end of the walkable line at midday, where the track gave out for good into a washout the river had taken decades ago, and stood at the edge of the break looking down at the water that had unmade it. Her grandfather's line, her line, ended here. It had ended here for seventy years, and she had come to that ending a hundred times with a clipboard, and this was the first time she'd come to it and simply said, silently, to the man in the photograph, *There. Finished. Forgiven. Go on now.*

The wind moved through the valley. The river said what rivers say, which is everything and nothing, patiently, forever.

She turned back, and the walk home was downhill and golden, and she did not count a single mile of it.

In the shed that evening she did three small things. She pinned the photograph above her workbench, between the published route map and the child's shoe drawing Mara had made from memory, so that the young faces looked out over the instruments forever. She wrote to the solicitor a one-line reply—*Estate received in full; no further action required*—and sealed it with the rail-spike letter opener, which had finally, after eleven months, done its job. And she opened a fresh notebook to its first page, and instead of a chainage or a date or a client's name, she wrote:

*Year one.*

Then she put the kettle on, and outside, far off in the hills where the closed lines slept under the grass, the first dry-season wind came down the valley smelling of dust and sun, and the rails at the tunnel mouth caught the last of the light and held it, two bright threads running on into the dark, going somewhere, going on.
