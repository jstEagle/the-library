## 1. The Tide Keeps No Records

The tide had gone out farther than the charts said it could.

Ulyana Iversen noticed it the way he noticed most things—sideways, without letting his face change. He was standing on the abandoned spur at Kilometer 41, running a level across rails that hadn't carried a train in eleven years, when he realized the sea had withdrawn past the last benchmark. Past the rocks. Past everything.

He lowered the instrument and looked properly, which was itself an act of small rebellion. Fifteen years of surveying closed lines had taught him to look without seeing: to record gradients and gauge widths and never once ask what lay beyond the paper's edge. The Provisional Concession Company paid him to measure dead things precisely, and precision, he had learned, was a respectable form of blindness. You could spend a whole career being exact about nothing.

But the sea had pulled back like a curtain drawn by some enormous indifferent hand, and there was no pretending he hadn't seen what stood revealed below the cliff line: a mouth of black stone, arched and dripping, breathing out cold air that smelled of wet granite and something older—something like iron, like deep water held in the dark for centuries.

The company charts showed nothing there. He knew because he had drawn half of them himself, or corrected them, or quietly let errors stand because correcting them meant asking questions. The coastline at Kilometer 41 was rendered as featureless cliff, a line and a hatching pattern, the cartographic equivalent of a shrug.

Ulyana checked his watch against the tide tables in his satchel. The tables were company documents; they were wrong too, or the moon was misbehaving, or the world simply declined to be filed. A spring tide of unusual violence, the almanac would say later, if anyone bothered to record it. But the tide keeps no records. That was its luxury and its terror—it could reveal things and then erase the evidence, and no one would ever have to know.

He should have gone back to camp. The light was failing, the foreman wanted the spur report by morning, and Brannoc Vey did not tolerate wandering. *The line is the line,* Brannoc liked to say, as if repetition were a philosophy. *We measure what we're paid to measure.* Ulyana had built his entire life inside that sentence. It fit him the way an old coat fits—loosely enough to hide in, tightly enough that taking it off would feel like exposure.

He climbed down anyway.

* * *

The rock was slick with weed and the retreat of the water still ran off it in silver threads. Ulyana descended with the unhurried competence of a man who had scrambled down a hundred cuttings and embankments, testing each hold, keeping three points of contact, telling himself the whole time that this was reconnaissance, due diligence, a matter of drainage assessment for the spur's eventual rehabilitation—and knowing, in the place where he kept the truth about himself, that it was none of those things.

The cave mouth was taller than a man and twice as wide, its arch worn smooth in a way that water alone didn't explain. At its threshold, cut into the stone at knee height, were marks.

Ulyana crouched. His breath went strange in his chest.

They were survey marks—but not company marks. Not the Provisional's stamped cipher, not the older Crown Survey's crown-and-bar, not even the pre-concession railway board's crossed levels. These were older than any system he knew. Spirals, mostly, cut deep and confident into basalt, each one enclosing a smaller spiral, and beside them lines radiating outward like the spokes of a wheel seen edge-on, like water finding its way downhill. Below the spirals, someone had carved a long horizontal groove with short vertical ticks along it—a scale, or a calendar, or a counting of something. And beneath all of it, half-worn by ten thousand tides, a shape he recognized despite having no right to recognize it:

A river. Drawn from source to sea in a single unbroken line, the way no modern surveyor would ever dare draw it, because no modern surveyor had ever walked it end to end.

The Vell River. The river that fed the concession's reservoirs, turned the turbines at Halloway Bend, filled the water towers that made the whole rail line possible. Every chart Ulyana had ever touched agreed that the Vell rose in the Kestrel Massif, forty miles north, in country the company had sealed behind permits and fences and a silence so complete that even the crews joked nervously about it. *Don't map what you can't bill for,* Tam had said once, laughing, and then stopped laughing when nobody joined him.

But this carving showed the river coming out of the ground somewhere else entirely. Somewhere south. Somewhere near here, under this cliff, under the sea's jurisdiction—the line descending through the stone toward the cave floor itself, as if the source of the Vell were not a mountain spring but something vast and hidden beneath the coast, rising and falling with the tide.

Or as if whoever carved this had known something the company had spent a great deal of money ensuring no one else would know.

Ulyana knelt there until his knees ached. The cold came up through the stone and into him, and he let it, because it was easier than admitting what was actually happening to him, which was that fifteen years of careful invisibility were cracking open like ice in spring. He had made himself a man who measured other people's forgotten things so that no one would ever measure him. And now the sea had rolled back and shown him a question carved in stone older than every document in the company archive, and the question was aimed directly at the foundation of his quiet life: *What if the maps are lies? What if you've been drawing them?*

His hands moved before his decision caught up with them. He took out his notebook, then thought better of it—ink and spray wouldn't survive the detail—and instead tore sheets from the roll of tracing paper he used for overlay work. He pressed them flat against the carvings and rubbed with the side of a soft pencil, the way he'd learned as a boy copying brasses in a church in a country he no longer named. The spirals came up ghost-pale and perfect. The river line came up. The ticked scale came up.

Four rubbings. He folded them into oilcloth and pushed them deep into the bottom of his satchel, beneath the tide tables, beneath the level book, beneath fifteen years of harmless exactitude.

The water was already turning. He felt it before he saw it—a change in the air pressure in the cave, a sound like the world inhaling. Far out, the sea had begun its return, a gray seam widening between the exposed rocks.

He climbed fast, and did not look back, and told himself the whole climb that he would decide tomorrow whether the rubbings existed.

* * *

Tam was waiting at the top of the cutting.

The boy sat cross-legged on a fallen rail chair, mending a torn strap on the instrument case with waxed thread, and he didn't look up when Ulyana crested the embankment—which meant he already had looked up, already seen everything, and was performing indifference with the transparent skill of a sixteen-year-old who had survived by reading adults. Tam was a stowaway who had become an apprentice who had become, though neither of them said it, something closer to a son. Ulyana had found him asleep in the equipment wagon outside Marrow Junction three years ago, hollow-eyed and stubborn, and had told himself he was turning the boy over at the next station. He had told himself that for six weeks.

"Level's packed," Tam said, pulling the last stitch tight. "Brannoc wants the figures tonight."

"They'll have them."

"You were down there a while." Still not looking up. "For a drainage check."

Ulyana felt the satchel's weight shift against his hip, four sheets of paper suddenly heavier than the instruments, heavier than the level, heavier than the whole honest apparatus of his measured life. He could lie. Lying was easy; he'd had practice. *Slippery footing. Wanted a look at the culvert. Nothing there.*

"Nothing there," he said.

Tam finally raised his eyes. They were quick and dark and missed nothing, and they rested on the satchel a moment too long before returning to the strap, and Ulyana understood with a lurch that the boy had seen him fold the oilcloth, had perhaps watched the whole descent, had been sitting here doing arithmetic with what he knew.

"Right," Tam said mildly. "Nothing there."

The first wave hit the rocks below, soft and enormous, and somewhere under the churn the cave mouth closed like a book. By morning the tide would be ordinary. By morning the charts would be correct again, and the coast at Kilometer 41 would be a line and a hatching pattern, and a man could go on measuring dead things precisely for the rest of his quiet life.

Ulyana walked back toward the camp lights with his apprentice silent at his shoulder, and for the first time in fifteen years, the map in his hands was not an answer.

It was a question. And it had already been seen.
