## 2. Miles Are Not Years

The rubbings did not sleep. Ulyana knew this the way he knew a rail was cracked before his hammer told him—by a wrongness in the sound of things, in the way the camp settled around him at night. He had rolled the paper sheets into the hollow core of his spare surveyor's tripod, waxed them against damp, and capped the ends with cork, and still they seemed to hum through the bamboo of his bunk like a struck tuning fork. Fifteen years he had kept himself flat and quiet as standing water. Now something under the floorboards of his life would not stop ringing.

He worked by lamplight after the others turned in, comparing what he'd taken from the cave wall against the concession's sealed river surveys—the ones Brannoc kept locked in the steel document case that rode under the foreman's cot like a sleeping dog. Ulyana had copied those pages years ago, out of habit more than suspicion; an engineer who did not memorize everything was an engineer who deserved to die on his own line. He spread the copies now beneath the lamp's tin shade and felt the old cold move up his arms.

The carvings were a gauge. That was the only word for it. Whoever had cut those spirals and notch-chains into the cave rock had been measuring the river—not its surface, but its *source*, marking flood heights across seasons no company chart acknowledged. And laid beside the sealed surveys, the notches said plainly what the surveys lied about: the Vey River did not rise in the Grey Tarns where the concession's maps placed it. It rose higher, farther north, through country the company had never surveyed because it had never been permitted to. The line's water rights—every culvert, every bridge pier, every tank at every watering station—were built on a river that existed only on paper.

Ulyana sat back and looked at his hands. They were steady. That frightened him more than shaking would have.

*You could burn it,* he thought. The tripod core, the copies, all of it, into the cookfire at dawn, ash into ash, and tomorrow the tide would come back and take the cave, and the world would go on being the size he had made it.

But he thought of Tam's face on the shingle that morning, salt-wet hair plastered down, eyes wide with the particular hunger of the young when they have seen something true. Tam had not looked away from the cave mouth. Tam had wanted to go *in*. And Ulyana had spent fifteen years teaching himself to look away from everything, and here was this boy, this stowaway he had never formally claimed and could not now imagine the camp without, looking straight at the one thing Ulyana had ever found worth seeing.

The map in his hands had become a question. He hated it. He held it anyway.

* * *

Tam counted the enforcers' boots before he counted their faces: nine pairs, heavy-soled, wrong for the mud of the work camp, arriving on the morning supply barge like nails driven into soft wood. He crouched behind the water barrels with the practiced ease of a boy who had learned invisibility before he'd learned anything else, and watched them fan out through the camp with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once been told no.

"Stolen documents," the tall one said to Brannoc, who stood in the middle of the tramway with his arms folded and his face doing the thing it did when he was furious and polite at the same time, which was mostly jaw. "Company property. Survey materials removed from restricted archives. We're authorized to search."

"Authorized." Brannoc let the word sit in the mud between them. "By whom?"

"By the Provision Company." The man smiled the way a coupler smiles. "Which owns the ground you're standing on, the rails you're standing next to, and, until the permits lapse, you."

They went through the bunkhouse first. Tam pressed himself flatter against the barrel staves and thought about the tripod. It stood in Ulyana's corner like it always did, unremarkable, one leg wrapped in oilcloth for the damp. Tam had helped wrap it. Tam knew what slept inside it.

His heart went strange and loud. He thought: *they'll find it, they'll find it, and Uncle Ulya will be marched onto the barge and I'll have done nothing, again, I'll have hidden again.* Because that was the lesson of Tam's whole life before the camp—hide, be small, be cargo—and Ulyana had broken that lesson open for him, had taught him levels and gauges and how to read water, and now the boy's whole body wanted to stand up out of the mud and say *it's mine, I carried it, take me.*

He didn't. He stayed behind the barrels, hating himself in a new and complicated key, and listened to the enforcers turn over bunks.

* * *

They found nothing, because there was almost nothing to find, and because Brannoc stood in doorways and asked each searcher, pleasantly, whether his mother had taught him to fold another man's shirts that way. But the tall one saved Ulyana for last, and did it in front of everyone, on the tramway, in the grey noon light.

"You were on the coastal spur yesterday. Low tide. Unusual conditions." The man consulted a card he did not need to consult. "Company records show no authorized survey of that section."

"I walked it," Ulyana said. "Alone. Checking drainage before the rains. You're welcome to my notes." He handed over the field book—the real one, the boring one, columns of culvert elevations—and watched the man flip through it with fingers too clean for the work.

"And your apprentice? He was seen on the spur as well."

Here it was. Ulyana felt the moment arrive the way you feel a bridge load shift—everything balanced, everything about to be decided by what you did in the next second. Tam was twenty feet away, frozen mid-task with a grease pot in his hands, watching him.

"The boy wandered off after gulls," Ulyana said. "He's twelve. Boys chase birds. If the company wants to file charges against seabirds, I'll draft the report myself."

Someone in the crew laughed—a short, strangled sound, quickly swallowed. The enforcer closed the field book and looked at Ulyana for a long moment, and whatever he saw there was apparently not enough, or not provable, and he turned and gave the order to reboard the barge.

That night Tam did not come to the fire. Ulyana found him in the tool shed, sitting on a crate in the dark, and the boy's face when the lantern light reached it was worse than anger—it was the face of someone who has been given something and told it is worthless.

"You lied," Tam said.

"Yes."

"To them. For me. Like I'm—" His voice cracked and he pushed through it. "Like I'm a thing you keep. Like I don't get to know things. You didn't even ask me what I saw in the cave. You just decided."

Ulyana set the lantern down. He had rehearsed a dozen sentences on the walk over and none of them survived the boy's eyes. "I decided to keep you safe," he said finally. "It's the only decision I've made well in fifteen years."

"That's not safety. That's just a smaller cage." Tam stood up. He was growing too fast; his wrists stuck out of his sleeves like afterthoughts. "I saw the markers too, Uncle Ulya. I helped take the rubbings. If it's stolen, then it's half mine, and if it's a crime, then I'm half a criminal, and you don't get to carry that alone just because—" he stopped, jaw working, "—just because everyone else you ever loved left, or died, or whatever happened before me."

The words landed harder than any enforcer's boot. Ulyana stood in the lantern light and understood, with the slow dread of a man watching a gauge climb past red, that silence was no longer protecting anyone. The company had sent nine men to a work camp of thirty because a boy had been seen on a beach. They would send ninety next time. And the crew—Brannoc, Oyelaran in the cook wagon, little Sefa who oiled the points—they were all of them standing inside the blast radius of what Ulyana had carried home from the tide.

"I need you to promise me something," Ulyana said quietly. "If they come back—if they ever put hands on you—you give them the tripod. You tell them it was yours, that I made you carry it, that you didn't know what was inside. You let me be the thief."

"No," Tam said.

"It wasn't a request."

"It wasn't an answer either." The boy picked up the lantern and walked out, leaving Ulyana alone in the smell of grease and rain coming, and Ulyana sat down on the crate his apprentice had warmed and put his head in his hands.

* * *

Three days later the barge came back, and this time it brought six men and a written order: the coastal spur was closed to all personnel pending a full company survey. Restricted. Fenced. And pinned to the mess tent's post, a notice in three languages with the Provision Company's seal at the bottom—

*All survey materials pertaining to hydrographic sources are hereby declared company property. Possession constitutes theft.*

Brannoc read it twice, took it down, and folded it into his breast pocket with great care, the way a man pockets a debt. Then he looked across the camp at Ulyana, and his face said what his voice could not say in front of the enforcers still standing on the barge deck:

*I know. And we are out of time.*

That night, Ulyana packed the tripod, woke Tam with a hand over the boy's mouth and a finger to his own lips, and pointed north, upriver, toward the source the company had forbidden—while somewhere behind them, in the dark, the first thunder of the monsoon rolled off the hills like a door closing.
