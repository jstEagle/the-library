## 3. Upstream Against the Monsoon

The river did not want them to go up.

Ulyana had spent fifteen years learning what water could do to a line—how it undercut ballast, how it swelled culverts until they burst like boils—but he had never before tried to walk against a river in the first week of monsoon. The Ilyen had shed its dry-season manners entirely. It ran brown and muscular, shouldering past boulders, dragging whole trees along its flanks like a man carrying firewood. Every tributary that fed it from the hills came down swollen, and each one meant a crossing, and each crossing was a negotiation with something that did not negotiate.

Tam walked ahead when the footing allowed and behind when it didn't, and said almost nothing. That was worse than anger. Anger would have been a thing Ulyana could carry.

"You lied to them," Tam had said on the first night, hunched under a tarp strung between two leaning trunks while the rain hammered it like a debt collector. "Brannoc asked you straight out if you had the rubbings. And you looked him in the eye."

"I looked at his shoulder," Ulyana said. "It's easier."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Ulyana admitted. "It isn't." He fed another stick to the small fire they'd coaxed under the tarp's drip line. The flame bent sideways, blue at the base. "When you're older, someone will ask you to choose between a truth and a family, and you'll discover they aren't two things. They're one thing tearing itself in half. You'll lie, or you won't, and either way you'll lose something you can't name for years."

"I'm thirteen. I already lost it."

The rain filled the silence for both of them.

They moved by day through country no surveyor had charted honestly—the concession maps showed this valley as *unproductive terrain*, which was company language for *terrain we have decided not to look at*. But Ulyana looked. He looked at the terraces cut into the hillsides, old ones, older than any permit, their retaining walls fitted without mortar by hands that had known this river's moods across centuries. He looked at the carved markers that appeared now beside the water at intervals: the same spiral-and-notch symbol from the sea cave, worn but unmistakable, each one positioned where a flood would pass over it and leave it standing.

Whoever had cut these stones had been keeping records longer than the Provision Company had existed. The tide kept no records; these people had kept them anyway.

On the third day the valley narrowed and the markers led them away from the river itself, up a side gorge where the water came down white and deafening, and there, wedged above the gorge mouth, hung the bridge.

It was a bad joke of a structure—two cables of braided fiber slung between anchorages of living rock, with cross-planks rotted to lace and a handline gone gray as old bone. It swayed even in still air. In the wind coming down the gorge it swung like a struck bell.

"The source is beyond this," Ulyana said. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. He was reading the markers: they converged here, funneled across the gorge, and continued up the far side toward a cleft in the cliffs where the air itself seemed to come from—the air smelled of deep stone and cold, the smell of water that has never seen sun.

"Then we cross," Tam said.

"It'll hold us. Probably."

"You said *probably* about the last crossing too, and I went in up to my neck."

"And you learned to swim better for it."

Tam looked at him then—really looked, the way he'd stopped doing since the night of the lie—and something in his face was not forgiveness exactly, but a decision to postpone judgment. Thirteen-year-olds are capable of great mercies and great cruelties, often in the same hour.

They crossed single file, Ulyana first, testing each plank with his boot heel before committing weight. The gorge wind gusted in long pulses. Halfway across, forty feet of roaring nothing below, Ulyana understood with total clarity that he had brought a child into this because he could not bear to send him back to the camp alone, and that this was love and selfishness in equal measure, indistinguishable, as most such things are.

He was three planks from the far anchorage when the cable sang.

Not snapped—*sang*, a high groaning note as the near anchor shifted, and the whole bridge dropped a foot and swung toward the gorge wall. Ulyana threw himself flat onto the planks. Behind him Tam cried out, and the sound was very small against the water.

"TAM. Stay flat. Crawl. Don't stand up."

The boy crawled. The bridge bucked beneath him like a living thing trying to shed a burden. And then the near cable let go—not all at once but strand by strand, with sounds like tearing cloth, and the span tilted, and the satchel strap holding the maps slid off Ulyana's shoulder.

He watched it fall. He watched Tam watch it fall. And then Tam did the thing Ulyana would remember for the rest of his life, in whatever life he had left: the boy dove.

Not off the bridge—along it, hand over hand down the sagging handline, upside down over the void, and caught the satchel strap one-handed as it swung past the gorge wall on the pendulum arc. His other hand held the fraying cable. His feet found nothing. The river took his boots' weight and pulled.

"Hold on!" Ulyana was moving backward along the tilting planks, on his belly, faster than he'd moved in fifteen years of careful invisible life. "Tam! Hold!"

"I've got it!" The boy's voice cracked with terror and fury together. "I've got your *maps*, old man, get me *up*!"

Ulyana got an arm around the handline, hooked the boy's collar, and hauled with a strength he didn't know he owned, and for one endless moment they were both suspended over the brown roar—then the far cable, mercifully, mercifully, held, and they collapsed together onto wet rock at the anchorage, the satchel crushed between them, Tam shaking so hard his teeth chattered audibly.

"You absolute fool," Ulyana said, and his voice broke on the word fool, because he meant himself.

"You'd have let them drown," Tam said. "The maps. You'd have just watched."

"Yes," Ulyana said. "That's why you're braver than I am. And that's exactly why I'm never letting you do it again."

Above them, past the cleft in the cliff, the air breathed cold and ancient, and the true source of the Ilyen waited to be read.

* * *

Brannoc stood in the foreman's tent with the rain drumming the canvas and the company man's letter open on his table, and hated every word of it.

*Retrieve the engineer and the documents. By any means necessary. Failure will be noted.*

By any means necessary. Brannoc had worked for the Provision Company nineteen years and had learned to translate its language fluently. *By any means necessary* meant a broken arm was acceptable. It meant a body at the bottom of a gorge was a paperwork problem, not a moral one.

He thought of Ulyana's face the night the enforcers searched the camp—that perfect blankness, the lie delivered so smoothly Brannoc had almost believed it, and had believed it, God help him, had vouched for the man with his own signature. And he thought of Tam, who had come to him afterward, white-faced, and said only, *"He's going upstream. He has to. You'd understand if you saw what he saw."*

Brannoc had spent nineteen years believing that a man could keep his head down and his crew fed and call it honor. The letter on his table proposed to test whether that belief survived contact with orders.

He took out a second sheet of paper. He wrote a resignation—one sentence, undated—and folded it into his breast pocket unsealed. Then he saddled a horse, told the camp cook he was inspecting the upper culverts, and rode into the monsoon after his people, telling himself he would decide what he was when he caught them.

He knew, riding, that he had already decided. A rope bridge had failed in the gorge above the third marker stone—he found the frayed cable anchored in the rock, strands torn, and below it the river running high—and Brannoc stood in the rain looking at that broken bridge and understood that somewhere beyond it, his engineer and his apprentice were either dead or about to change everything.

He began to climb.

* * *

The source was not a spring. It was a verdict.

The cleft opened into a cathedral of stone where the river rose full-born from a fissure in the rock floor, cold and impossibly clear, and the walls were covered—*covered*—with carvings. Generations of them. Water levels scratched year by year, dates in a script older than the concession, older than the rail gauge, going back further than Ulyana could read but not further than he could understand: the highest marks clustered in cycles, and the newest cycle had begun.

And there, bolted to the rock above the flood line, a brass plate bearing the Provision Company's crest, its engraved text half corroded:

*SOURCE SURVEY — OFFICIAL RECORD — DISCHARGE INSUFFICIENT FOR PROVISION OF LINE AND TERRITORY.*

Insufficient. Ulyana put his hand against the fissure and felt the water pushing past his fingers with the pressure of a fire hose, and laughed—a short, disbelieving sound that echoed off the carvings. Insufficient. The company had measured this torrent and called it a trickle, because a trickle justified the falsified water rights, and the falsified rights justified the concession, and the concession paid for everything, including the enforcers now hunting two soaked figures through the hills.

He spread the salvaged rubbings and the sealed-survey discrepancies on the driest ledge he could find, and Tam held the lantern, and together they matched mark to mark, cave carving to forged record, until the chain was complete and unbreakable.

"They buried it," Tam whispered. "All of it. Right here where anyone could find it."

"Where no one was permitted to look," Ulyana said. "There's a difference, and the difference is the whole world."

He took out his own notebook—the honest one, the one he'd kept for fifteen years of closed lines and deliberate invisibility—and began to draw. The true source. The true discharge. The true map, with the false one annotated beside it in red. When he finished, he wrapped the pages in oilcloth, and he did not hesitate, because Tam had taught him something on a broken bridge about what a man dives for.

"We take this downriver," he said. "To the telegraph office at Kiln Junction. To the papers. To everyone."

"They'll ruin us," Tam said. "The crew. Everyone."

"They'll try." Ulyana looked at the boy—at the maps clutched to his chest, at the river rising behind them, at the whole dishonest edifice of the Provision Company balanced on a brass plate and a lie. "But a family built on a lie drowns slow. One built on truth drowns fast, if it drowns at all. I know which drowning I can live with."

They turned back down the gorge as the rain thickened toward evening—and found fresh hoofprints in the mud, and a horse tied under a lean-to, and a broad-shouldered figure climbing the last stretch of trail toward them with his hat plastered to his head and his face unreadable in the gloom.

Brannoc. Three days early, off all routes, carrying no warrant Ulyana could see.

"Ulyana," the foreman said, stopping ten feet away, rain streaming off his jaw. "Show me what's in the satchel. And then tell me why I shouldn't arrest you."

Behind him, far downstream, the first thunder of the season's worst storm rolled up the valley like a door closing.
