## 4. A Monsoon for the Provision

The maps went out on the ninth day of the rains, and the world ended quietly, the way a rail line ends—not with catastrophe but with a notice posted at the yard office and men standing in the wet reading it twice.

Ulyana read it last. He had come down from the high country alone, Tam left at the mission hospital in Kessil Vale with a lung full of river water and a fever that the sisters swore would break. He stood in the rain outside the concession office while the clerk nailed the paper to the board, and he watched his own handiwork come home to him: *By order of the Provisional Authority, all permits held by the Meridian River & Coast Concession are void pending resurvey. All personnel are dismissed effective immediately.*

Dismissed. Fifteen years of closed spurs and sealed culverts, fifteen years of measuring the world so carefully that no part of it could touch him, and now the whole apparatus had been swept away by forty pages of inked linen and a monsoon.

He felt nothing like vindication. He felt like a man who has finally set down a weight and doesn't know what to do with his arms.

* * *

Brannoc found him three days later at the cave mouth, because Brannoc had always known where Ulyana would be. The tide was coming in over the black shelf rock, filling the lower gallery inch by inch, and Ulyana sat above the waterline with his back against stone older than any chart, watching the sea erase the carvings for another twelve hours.

"You refused it," Ulyana said, without turning. He'd heard the uneven stride a hundred yards off; fifteen years will teach you a man's walk better than his face.

"They offered me chief of surveys for the whole territory." Brannoc lowered himself onto the rock with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions about tides. "On condition I bring you in. Quietly. Before the hearings."

"And?"

"And I told them I've spent eleven years trying to stop you from wandering off, and I wasn't going to start succeeding now." He passed over a flask. Rain drummed on both of them. "The crew's scattering. Osei's got an offer in the copper country. The Ferreira twins are taking a coastal packet south tomorrow. Maren signed on with a salvage outfit out of the harbor this morning."

Ulyana drank. The liquor burned down through him and settled somewhere under his ribs like a lit lamp. "Good," he said. "They should go. There's nothing here."

"There's you here," Brannoc said. "Which is more than there's ever been anywhere else, if you ask them. Which is why none of them have actually left yet, whatever they've signed. The packet sails when the weather clears. The salvage job starts next week. Everyone's waiting to see what you do, you stubborn ghost of a man, and you're sitting in a cave watching the tide."

"The tide keeps no records," Ulyana said. It had been his joke once, his creed. Now it came out sounding like an apology.

"No," Brannoc agreed. "But we do."

* * *

Tam's fever broke on the fourth night, and Ulyana was there for it—had been there since the second day, sleeping in the corridor chair, refusing the sisters' offers of a cot with the particular stubbornness he usually reserved for surveying disputes. When the boy's eyes opened and focused, the first thing he said was:

"Did the maps get out?"

"They got out."

"Then it was worth it." Tam tried to sit up, failed, grinned anyway. His lips were still gray. "I saved the rubbings. You can fire me later."

"I'm not your foreman anymore," Ulyana said. "Nobody is. That's what dismissal means."

Tam considered this with the gravity of the very ill and the very young. "So who do I apprentice to now?"

And Ulyana, who had spent fifteen years ensuring he would never be responsible for anyone, who had chosen closed lines and expired permits the way other men chose celibacy, heard himself say: "Whoever will have you. We'll find someone."

But he was already thinking about instruments. About teaching a boy to hold a level true, to read water, to know that a map is a promise made to strangers.

* * *

The rains broke in the third week of October, the way they always did—suddenly, indecently, as if the sky had simply gotten bored. The morning after, the harbor filled with light and the crews began, at last, to actually leave.

Ulyana walked the yard one final time. The concession locomotives stood cold under tarps, and the provisional authority's engineers were already staking out the first honest baseline along the old right-of-way, their chains bright, their faces unfamiliar. A new company had bought the franchise at auction—cheaper now, without falsified water rights inflating its value—and they were hiring.

He did not apply. He told himself it was principle, though he suspected it was fear wearing principle's coat. He had done his one great thing. Men who did one great thing were permitted to disappear afterward; it was practically expected. He had a ticket south. He had fifteen years of practice at leaving.

He was at the harbor office buying that ticket when Maren found him.

She didn't say anything at first. She just stood beside him at the counter, close enough that he could see the salt dried white on her jacket collar, and waited until the clerk looked away.

"Osei turned down the copper job," she said.

"The pay there is triple."

"He knows. The twins missed the packet. Deliberately, I think—they let it sail without them and then acted surprised." She turned to face him. "Brannoc's got everyone camped at the old mile-twelve shelter. They've been there four days. They're waiting for you to ask them something, Ulyana. Nobody wants to say it first because you'll only get embarrassed and quote regulations at us."

"Ask them what?" His voice came out rougher than he intended. "There's no crew. There's no concession. There's a new company that doesn't know our names."

"There's a resurvey," Maren said. "Forty miles of line that's been lied about for twenty years, and a new authority that needs every chain measured true, and a boy in the hospital who's going to need work he can be proud of when he's on his feet. And there's a chief of surveys position open, because the man they wanted for it refused it on principle." Her mouth twitched. "Refused it until this morning, actually. This morning he applied. Condition of acceptance: you run the field parties or nobody does."

Ulyana looked out at the harbor. The light lay on the water like hammered tin. Somewhere upriver, in a high green country the charts had never honestly drawn, a spring fed a river that fed a line that fed all of them, and for the first time in fifteen years the thought of being known—of being counted, mapped, recorded in the ledger of other people's lives—did not feel like drowning.

He tore up the ticket.

* * *

They signed on a Tuesday, all six of them, in a rented room above the harbor office that smelled of ink and wet wool. Tam came in a wheelchair, furious about the wheelchair, radiant anyway. Brannoc read the contract aloud in the voice he used for safety briefings, as if honesty were a regulation they were all now required to observe. When it came to the signature lines, the Ferreira twins argued briefly about who would sign first, settled it with a coin, and then both signed at once, which was very like them.

Ulyana signed last. His hand paused over the page—not from doubt, but from the strange, vertiginous awareness that he was writing himself into a record that would not expire. Not a permit. Not a season. A crew. A family, if he could bear the word, and he found, testing it silently, that he could.

Outside, the first dry light of the year lay along the rails running east toward the mountains, toward the source, toward forty miles of line waiting to be measured true. The provisional engineer handed him the field book—blank, unnumbered, honest—and Ulyana opened it to the first page and wrote, in the careful hand of a man beginning rather than ending:

*Mile zero. Crew of seven. Weather clear.*

Beside him, Tam leaned over the wheelchair arm to look. "What are we calling the survey?" he asked.

Ulyana thought of the cave, the carvings, the tide that kept no records and the people who did. He thought of a provision—not the company's word for a contract, but the older meaning: what is provided, what is carried forward, what you bring with you through the flood.

"We're calling it what it is," he said, and dipped his pen. "*A Monsoon for the Provision.* First entry: everything upstream is true."

The pen touched paper, and the line began.
