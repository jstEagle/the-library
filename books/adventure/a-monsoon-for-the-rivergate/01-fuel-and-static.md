## 1. Fuel and Static

The morning the Halvorsen expedition went quiet for good, Jude Yarrow was elbow-deep in a drum of avgas, arguing with a goat.

"Off," she said. "That's not for you."

The goat—Kettle Bend's collective pet, answerable to no one and named, by committee, Something Awful—stood with its front hooves on the wing strut of her plane and regarded her with the flat patience of an animal that had never once been told no successfully.

"That's hundred low lead," Jude explained. "You drink that, you'll be a very fast goat for about four minutes."

From somewhere behind her, Odile's voice drifted out of the open hangar doors, unhurried as ever. "She's not listening to you."

"She never listens to me."

"She doesn't listen to anyone. It's why we respect her." Odile emerged wiping her hands on a rag that had once been white, in some previous geological era. She was a small woman built like a coil spring, gray-streaked hair cropped short, forearms mapped with burn scars she referred to collectively as *my education*. Ten years she and Jude had been running cargo up and down the Rivergate together, and in all that time Odile had never once asked where Jude came from, only whether the left magneto was still running rough. It was, Jude thought, the deepest kindness anyone had ever shown her.

The medicine run was the best kind of flight: downriver to San Isidro and back, ninety minutes each way over green water, with a stop at the mission clinic to trade banter with Sister Anselm and steal coffee. Routine. Routine was the whole architecture of Jude's life—load lists and fuel logs and weather briefings, a schedule so ordinary it was practically camouflage. Nobody looked twice at a supply pilot. That was the point of being one.

She finished the drum, shooed the goat off with a boot toe, and began loading crates while Odile ran the preflight. Kettle Bend woke around them the way it always did: the generator coughing awake at the cookhouse, Marnie Okafor's kids chasing each other between the fuel bowsers, old Desi sweeping the strip's edge with a broom he'd been threatening to replace since before Jude arrived. The settlement sat on a bluff above the river like something the water had given up trying to claim—a scatter of tin roofs, a windsock gone pink from sun, two hangars, and a single gravel runway that flooded every wet season and was declared unfixable every dry one.

"You hear from the survey lot?" Odile asked, tapping the tires.

"Not since Tuesday." Jude slid the last crate into place and lashed it down. "They're due a check-in tomorrow anyway."

Dr. Marcus Halvorsen's expedition had been at Kettle Bend three weeks ago, resupplying before heading upriver into the high basin—geological survey, twelve people, too much equipment and not enough humility, the way survey teams always were. Halvorsen himself was a big silver-haired man who shook hands like he was arm-wrestling you and called everyone *pilot* regardless of rank or gender. He'd stood on the strip eating Marnie's fried plantain and told Jude the basin was going to rewrite the textbooks.

"Geology waits for no monsoon," he'd said, grinning.

"Monsoon disagrees," Jude had said, and they'd both laughed, and that was the last easy conversation she'd had about him.

* * *

San Isidro was hot and loud and smelled of diesel and frying fish, which meant it smelled like home to exactly nobody, including Jude, and she loved it anyway. She delivered the medicines to the clinic, argued cheerfully with Sister Anselm about payment—the sister insisted on paying in coffee and prayers, and Jude had long ago decided to accept both—and was drinking the first cup on the clinic porch when the radio crackled.

"—Yarrow, do you copy? This is Halvorsen party, do you—"

Jude set the cup down. The handheld hissed, spat, and produced half a sentence out of static, a man's voice stretched thin across distance and interference.

"—copy, Rivergate base. We've got—" A burst of noise swallowed everything. Then, faintly, oddly calm: "—found something you need to see. Not what we— coordinates follow, but Jude, it's the old sealed—" Static ate the rest. When the voice came back it was farther away somehow, or maybe just smaller. "—transmitting again at—" And then nothing but the river-hiss of dead air.

Jude pressed the transmit key. "Halvorsen party, this is Yarrow. Say again your last. I lost you after *sealed*. Come back."

Nothing. She tried twice more, walked out to the airstrip for better line-of-sight, tried again. The frequency stayed empty in the particular way empty frequencies have—not silence, exactly, but a held breath.

Sister Anselm appeared beside her, squinting against the glare. "Trouble?"

"Probably just weather building upriver. Pre-monsoon static gets ugly." Jude turned the radio over in her hands. "They'll check in tomorrow on schedule."

But she wrote down the fragments anyway, in grease pencil on her forearm, because that was the other thing routine had taught her: the difference between a worry and a fact, and how facts liked to be written down while they were still fresh. *Found something.* *Not what we—* *Old sealed—*

Sealed. There weren't many things in the Rivergate basin that anyone would call sealed. Jude looked at the word on her arm for a long moment, then scrubbed at it with her thumb until it smeared into nothing, and flew home.

* * *

Evening at Kettle Bend came gold and slow. Odile was under the plane's cowling when Jude landed, and didn't come out until the engine had ticked itself cool, at which point she surfaced holding a spark plug like a doctor holding a bad X-ray.

"Number three's tired," she announced. "We're all tired. I sympathize with number three."

"I got a call from the Halvorsen party today. Half a call." Jude hopped down onto the gravel. "Garbled. They found something up there, and then they cut out mid-sentence."

Odile considered this, turning the plug in her fingers. "Pre-monsoon static's been filthy all week. Desi couldn't raise his brother in Puerto Verde for two days."

"Yeah. Probably that."

"But?"

Jude opened her mouth to say *nothing*, because *nothing* was the correct answer, the safe answer, the answer she'd been giving for ten years to questions nobody asked. But the evening light was doing something honest to the air, and Odile was looking at her with those patient mechanic's eyes that could tell a failing bearing by sound alone, and the lie stuck in her throat like a fishbone.

"But I don't like it," she said finally. "There was something in his voice. He sounded—" She hunted for it. "Relieved. Like a man who's been carrying something heavy and just put it down. Who talks like that about a rock find?"

"A geologist," Odile offered.

"A geologist who says *you need to see this* to a supply pilot instead of calling the university." Jude shook her head. "It's nothing. They check in tomorrow. If they miss it, I'll think about it then."

Odile nodded slowly, and Jude knew she'd filed the whole exchange somewhere in the vast organized warehouse of her mind, next to every other thing Jude almost-said. That was the arrangement. That was the deal, unspoken these ten years: Odile didn't ask, and Jude didn't lie, and between those two mercies they'd built something that worked.

They ate at the cookhouse—rice and beans and Marnie's infernally good pepper sauce—and played cards with Desi, who cheated openly and without shame, and by full dark the worry had been sanded down to a dull grain Jude could live on top of. She went to bed in the room behind the hangar, fan turning its slow circles, and slept the sleep of a woman whose day had been exactly the size she'd planned.

* * *

Tomorrow came, and the expedition did not.

Juke gave them until noon. She was on the radio at 0700, 0900, 1100, calling the scheduled frequency into the static, and each time the static answered with its long indifferent breath. By noon she'd developed a headache sitting just behind her eyes, the kind that meant she'd been clenching her jaw without noticing.

"They missed check-ins before," Odile said. They were in the hangar, and Odile was pretending to work on the landing gear while actually watching Jude pace. "Last season the Reyes brothers' dredge crew went dark for six days. Turned out their radio died and they spent the week fishing."

"This isn't a dredge crew. Halvorsen runs his comms like a religion. He carries a backup set." Jude stopped pacing. "And he said *coordinates follow* and then never sent them. Men like that don't forget to send coordinates."

"So call it in. Let the officials earn their salaries."

"I did. This morning." Jude's laugh came out flat. "The regional office says pre-monsoon weather, expect delays, file a report if they miss the second check-in. Which they now have. So I filed a report. They'll get back to me in five to ten business days, at which point the monsoon will have made landfall and the question will be academic."

Odile set down her wrench. In the dim of the hangar her face was hard to read, but her voice wasn't. "You know something I don't."

It wasn't a question. Jude looked at the plane—at the workhorse old bush bird that had carried medicine and mail and machine parts and once, memorably, a wedding party of nine—and felt the familiar shape of the decision assembling itself inside her, the one she'd been pretending not to feel since yesterday's porch.

"The transmission," she said. "He started to say something. *It's the old sealed—* and then static took it." She kept her voice level through sheer professional habit. "There's one valley upriver anybody ever bothered sealing. Company did it thirty years ago. Fenced it, posted it, buried whatever needed burying, and paid everyone downstream to forget the address."

Odile was quiet a moment. "How do you know that?"

"Everybody knows that." It was smooth and it was practiced and it was, for the first time in ten years, a lie to Odile's face, and it tasted like copper. Jude looked away, at the tools, at the light falling through the hangar door in a long dusty blade. "Look. Somebody should fly up and take a look. Reconnaissance. Low pass over the last known position, see if the camp's there, see if they need extraction before the weather closes the window. That's not heroics, that's logistics. We're the closest asset."

"We are also," Odile said, "a two-person operation with one tired magneto and a fuel budget that assumes we don't do anything interesting."

"I'm not asking you to come."

"No," Odile agreed. "You're announcing you're going and giving me time to object, which you will ignore." She wiped her hands forever on the rag and stood, and something in her expression shifted—not anger, not quite; more like a woman recalibrating a machine she thought she understood. "Ten years, Jude. You've flown past that valley a hundred times. I've watched you do it. You go wide around it like it owes you money. Now suddenly you can't wait to look inside."

"It's about the expedition."

"Mm." Odile picked up her toolbox, which was how she ended arguments she intended to win later. "Weather window holds till Thursday. If we're going, we go tomorrow at first light. I'll service number three tonight." At the hangar door she paused, backlit and silhouetted and impossible to argue with. "And someday—you don't have to say when—you're going to tell me what that valley seals up besides rocks."

She left. Jude stood alone in the hangar with the smell of oil and rain coming, and pulled the crumpled fragment of the transmission from her pocket, and read it again by the thin light.

*Found something you need to see.*

Somewhere upriver, in a country of gray water and older secrets, Dr. Marcus Halvorsen was waiting on a voice that hadn't answered yet. Jude folded the paper away, checked the fuel log she wouldn't need until dawn, and began, quietly, to plan a flight she'd spent ten years making sure she'd never have to take.
