## 2. The Missing Week

The first missed check-in, Jude let go.

That was the protocol, after all. The Halvorsen survey was scheduled to radio Kettle Bend every seventy-two hours, and weather in the upper basin was a liar with excellent credentials—it would promise you clear skies and then hand you three days of sleet sideways through the gorge. Everyone upriver knew the drill: miss one call, wait it out. Dr. Halvorsen himself had drilled it into her years ago, back when she was nineteen and still answering to her real name, tapping the radio housing with two fingers and saying, *The river doesn't keep appointments, Yarrow. Neither should we.*

She'd been Jude Marsh for eleven years now. Long enough that the name fit like broken-in boots.

So when Tuesday's check-in came and went with nothing but static, she logged it in the strip book—*Halvorsen exp., no contact, wx suspected*—and went back to repacking the medicine crates for the clinic at Two Forks. Odile was under the Cessna with a grease gun, humming something that might have been a hymn or might have been an advertisement for motor oil, and the morning proceeded exactly as mornings were supposed to.

"You're counting," Odile said from beneath the plane.

"I'm inventorying."

"You're counting. You've counted the same crate of penicillin four times." Odile rolled out on the creeper, a smear of grease across one cheekbone like war paint, and squinted up at her. "One missed call is weather. You know that."

"I know that."

"Say it like you believe it and I'll believe you."

Jude put down the manifest. "It's probably weather."

"*Probably,*" Odile repeated, with the satisfaction of a woman who had won something, and rolled back under the plane.

The second missed check-in was Friday, and by then the weather had stopped being a plausible excuse even to the people who wanted it to be one. The front that had parked itself over the upper basin had moved east on Wednesday, dragging its clouds behind it like a sulky child. The aviation office in Corvane reported clear windows all the way to the headwaters. Which meant that when the Halvorsen expedition failed to raise anyone again at 0900, it wasn't the sky keeping them quiet.

The officials were very kind about it. A man from the regional survey authority called the strip office on the long-line, his voice carrying the particular patience of someone who had explained this to worried relatives before. Atmospheric interference. Equipment failure. Expeditions went dark all the time; it was practically a rite of passage. Give them another week. The river had a way of returning what it took.

"The river," Jude said into the receiver, "also has a way of not doing that."

"Ma'am?"

"Nothing. Thank you for your time." She hung up gently, which took more effort than slamming it would have.

Odile was in the doorway of the office, wiping her hands on a rag that had started life white sometime during the previous administration. She didn't say anything. She just looked at Jude with the expression she reserved for engines that were making a sound they shouldn't—a listening expression, patient and slightly grim.

"They're calling it weather," Jude said.

"Mm."

"And equipment failure."

"Mm-hm."

"And general expedition foolishness. Halvorsen's team is six people and a geologist who once tried to cross the Serrat rapids in a rubber raft because, quote, the data was time-sensitive."

"So we're agreed it's nonsense," Odile said. "Which means the real question is why you've been standing at that window since dawn looking like a woman deciding whether to jump."

Jude turned back to the glass. Beyond it, Kettle Bend arranged itself in its usual order: the strip, the fuel drums, the row of tin-roofed buildings leaning on each other like tired drunks, the river running wide and brown and indifferent past all of it. Eleven years she'd watched this view. It had never once asked her about her grandfather.

She'd pulled the coordinates before Odile came in. That was the thing—the thing sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone. The last transmission from the expedition hadn't been entirely garbled. Buried under the static, Halvorsen's voice had read out a position fix, slow and deliberate, the way he read things when he wanted them remembered. She'd written it down without thinking, out of habit, the way she wrote down everything.

Then she'd looked at it. And she'd known.

Not because she'd ever been there. She'd been raised three thousand miles from this basin, in a house with company portraits on the walls and a father who spoke about the Rivergate holdings in the careful voice other men used for cancer diagnoses. But she'd spent her childhood reading the reports nobody else wanted—the old surveys, the sealed assessments, the maps marked RESTRICTED that Elias Yarrow's company had produced and buried and produced and buried again until burying them became its own department. She'd read them the way other children read adventure serials: hidden under blankets, by flashlight, heart pounding.

And there was a valley upriver, in those pages. A tributary cut that the company had bought out, fenced, and formally closed in the year her father was born. The internal memos called it Site R-7. The maps called it nothing at all—the cartographers had simply stopped drawing it, as if a valley could be unmade by omission. The coordinates in Halvorsen's last transmission sat inside that blank space like a coin dropped in snow.

"Jude." Odile's voice, closer now. "Wherever your head just went, come back."

"The last fix they transmitted." Jude kept her eyes on the window. "It's in the closed country. Above the Serrat forks. Land the company sealed off before I was born."

A pause. "Sealed how? Mining claim?"

"Sealed sealed. Fences, easements, a legal apparatus you could wallpaper a courthouse with. Nobody surveys Site R-7. Nobody's supposed to remember it exists." She finally turned. "Halvorsen remembered."

Odile set the rag down. In eleven years of partnership, Jude had learned that this was how Odile processed alarm—not with volume but with tidiness, putting things in their proper places before acting. "You're thinking of flying reconnaissance."

"I'm thinking about it."

"No, you're not. Thinking is what I'm doing right now, weighing fuel against weather against the fact that our only aircraft is mid-inspection with the left magneto due for replacement. You've already decided. You're just waiting for me to argue so you can feel reasonable."

"That's—" Jude began, and stopped, because it was entirely accurate.

"Jude." Odile crossed the office and stood in front of her, grease-stained and solid and impossible to lie to, which was the whole trouble with Odile. "If a survey team is hurt up there, every hour matters, and I'll fly with you with the bad magneto and my lucky wrench and no complaints. But if you're going because of some ghost from whatever past you don't talk about—and yes, I've noticed the past you don't talk about, I'm not a fool, I share a hangar with you—then say so. Because ghosts make terrible navigators."

Jude looked at her hands. There was grease in the creases that never quite washed out anymore, a decade of it, layered like sediment. She thought about telling Odile everything, right there, in the office that smelled of avgas and old paper. *My name isn't Marsh. My grandfather owned every fence around that valley, and the reason it's sealed is a reason I was taught never to ask about.* The words got as far as her teeth.

What came out instead was: "Six people are missing, Odile. Whatever else is true, that comes first."

It wasn't a lie. That was the trick of it, the thing she'd gotten good at over eleven years: never lying, just choosing which truths to stand in front of. Duty was real. Six people were really missing. And behind duty, silent as a stowaway, sat everything else.

Odile studied her for a long moment. Then she sighed, the sigh of a woman surrendering to a battle she'd already fought internally and lost. "Fine. But conditions. One: we take the spare magneto and I install it tonight, no arguments. Two: we file a flight plan that says 'reconnaissance, upper basin,' which is true and also tells the authorities precisely nothing. Three—" she held up a third finger, and her voice gentled in a way that was worse than shouting, "—if we find something up there that scares you worse than the missing people do, you tell me. Not today. Not tomorrow. But you tell me."

Jude nodded, because nodding cost nothing.

They worked through the afternoon in the easy rhythm they'd built over years—Odile swearing cheerfully at the magneto housing while Jude drained sumps and checked control cables and tried not to look north, where the mountains stacked themselves against the sky in ranks of gray and green, hiding the valley among them like a secret kept so long it had grown its own weather.

By dusk the Cessna was legal again, fueled, and loaded with the things you brought when you didn't know what you'd find: first-aid kit, tow rope, flares, the good thermos. Odile went home to sleep. Jude said she'd follow.

Instead she sat in the office with the lamp low and spread the old restricted surveys across the desk—the ones she'd copied years ago and kept folded inside a manual for a radio model nobody used anymore. Site R-7. Contour lines closing around a blank space. A handwritten margin note in her grandfather's own slanted script, from a memo she'd found in an archive box she should never have opened: *No further remediation authorized. Let the river handle it.*

Let the river handle it.

Outside, the actual river ran dark past the strip, carrying everything downstream except, apparently, the truth.

Jude folded the maps away, turned off the lamp, and lay down in the bunk behind the office, where she did not sleep so much as wait for morning with her eyes closed.

At 0540 the sky over the eastern ridge went the color of pewter, and she was already dressed.

* * *

They took off into a morning that couldn't decide what it wanted to be—low cloud shredding off the ridgelines, sun breaking through in patches that lit the river up like hammered tin and then abandoned it. Odile flew the first leg; Jude rode in the right seat with the chart on her knees and the coordinates burning a hole in her breast pocket.

Neither of them talked much. The engine, freshly magnetoed, ran smooth as a held breath.

Past the Serrat forks the country changed. The settlements thinned out and vanished; the river braided and unbraided; the forest came down to the water's edge like it had somewhere to be. And then, ahead, the tributary cut opened between two shoulders of mountain—the valley that official maps declined to acknowledge, green and deep and, from this altitude, perfectly ordinary.

Except for the smoke.

One thin thread of it, rising from the valley floor near the confluence, too small to be a camp and too deliberate to be chance. Someone down there was signaling.

Or someone down there was burning evidence.

"Jude," Odile said quietly, eyes on the thread of smoke. "Tell me you see that."

"I see it." Jude's mouth had gone dry. Below them, the blank space on the map scrolled past, holding whatever it had held for sixty years. "Take us down. Slow."

Odile banked toward the valley, and Jude braced herself—against the landing, against the answer, against the name she could feel waiting for her at the bottom of it like sediment at the bottom of a flooded river.
