## 3. What the Valley Keeps

Odile Marchand had a theory about rivers: they were like old men at a wedding, all of them convinced they were still the most important thing in the room. The Rivergate proved her right every single time she flew it. From the air it wound through the basin with the smug confidence of something that had been carving its name into the earth since before anyone thought to object.

Today it was showing off.

"Look at that," she said, mostly to herself, banking the Cessna wide around a bend where the channel had swallowed its own banks whole. The water ran the color of strong tea, brown and muscular, carrying entire trees downstream like a man carrying firewood who no longer cared whose wood it was. "Jude. Look at that."

"I'm looking," Jude said from the right seat, and she was—had been looking for two hours without blinking much, which Odile knew from experience meant she was looking *inward* and using the windshield as an excuse. Her hands rested on her knees instead of the yoke, which for Jude was roughly equivalent to wringing them.

"You're looking at your own skull," Odile said. "The river's over there."

That earned her half a smile. Progress. Since Kettle Bend, since the garbled transmission and the missed check-ins and the coordinates Jude had gone pale over, her partner had been running on some private fuel Odile couldn't pump, couldn't price, and couldn't refill. It worried her more than the weather did, and the weather was doing its level best to be genuinely alarming—a gray ceiling pressing down out of the north, fat and low, the sky's way of clearing its throat before the monsoon said something rude.

"Channel's up three meters at least," Odile went on, because talking was how she thought and flying was how she prayed, and she intended to do plenty of both. "Halvorsen's team picked their camp on the second terrace, so unless they were idiots, they're dry. Idiots is possible. Academics camp like people who've never met water."

"They're not idiots," Jude said quietly.

"No," Odile agreed. "But one of them is Tomas Ferreira, and I once watched that man try to open a fuel drum with a geological pick, so I'm keeping my expectations flexible."

She took them lower. The river spread below, braided and furious, and along its edges the flood had done its inventory-taking—snagged canoes, a drowned garden plot, a fishing platform peeled off its posts and dragged fifty meters downstream like a dog that had grabbed the wrong toy. Odile had flown relief runs through three monsoon seasons now. She knew what water did to the things people built. She knew what it did to the things people *were*, too, though that was harder to see from altitude.

"There." Jude's voice came out strange. Flat. "Past the confluence. That cut in the tree line—that's the valley."

Odile looked, and felt the temperature of the flight change.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the thing nobody told you about places with bad histories: from the air they looked like nothing. A side valley, green and ordinary, a stream feeding into the main river through a notch in the hills. But the trees inside it grew in rows too straight, reclaiming ground that had been cleared and graded by machines, and near the mouth of it Odile could just make out shapes under the vegetation—concrete pads, a rusted gantry, the geometry of industry going soft at the edges as the forest digested it.

And at the valley's mouth, on a gravel flat above the flood line: the expedition camp. Or what was left of it.

"Oh no," Odile said, and put the nose down.

* * *

The camp had been stripped. Not looted—stripped, deliberately, the way you strip a camp when you're leaving in a hurry or when you never intend to come back. Tents gone. Crates gone. The satellite dish lay face-down in the mud like a beached ray, its cable cut clean. A cook fire had been drowned by rain and kicked apart. Two tent-footprint rectangles pressed into the grass, and nothing standing on either of them.

Odile set down on the gravel flat with more care than she'd used on anything in years, eyes flicking between her approach path and the tree line, because a stripped camp meant people had left in a direction, and people who leave in a hurry sometimes leave things behind that they didn't mean to.

Or people.

"Stay with the plane," Jude said, already unstrapping.

"That's my line," Odile said. "You stay with the plane. You fly worse than I do and you know it."

For once, Jude didn't argue. That was its own kind of frightening.

They worked the site together in the wet hush after engine noise, the river roaring its one long note beyond the bank. Odile read the ground the way she read engines—by what was missing and what was worn. Boot traffic converging toward the river, heavy, repeated. Drag marks. A sled or a litter, maybe. And there, half-tucked under an overturned equipment case, a boot sole pointing at the sky.

Attached, thankfully, to a leg.

"Tomas!" Odile was moving before she finished deciding to. "Tomas Ferreira, if you're dead I am going to be so annoyed, because I owe you twenty dollars."

The leg twitched. Then a groan, then a face emerging from under the case—gray, stubbled, one eye swollen nearly shut, but alive and focusing on her with unmistakable recognition and unmistakable relief.

"Marchand," he croaked. "Thank God. Thank—water. Please."

Water first, then the good painkillers from the med kit, then the story, in pieces, while Odile splinted what turned out to be a badly broken ankle and a gash on his scalp that had bled enough to scare him and not enough to kill him. He'd fallen trying to haul gear down to the river four days ago, he thought—four days alone, feverish, with a broken radio handset and a camp that everyone else had already left.

"The others?" Jude asked. She crouched across from him, very still, the way she got when she was holding herself together with both hands. "Dr. Halvorsen?"

"Gone downriver." Tomas drank again, greedily. "Part of them. We split. After—" He stopped. His good eye went to Jude, then away, and Odile saw something pass over his face that she didn't like at all. Not fear exactly. Calculation. The look of a man deciding how much truth a situation could hold.

"After what?" Odile prompted, tying off the splint. "Tomas. We flew two hundred kilometers through pre-monsoon soup because your check-ins stopped. You can skip to the interesting part."

He laughed, which turned into a cough. "You'll think we're crazy."

"I think you're crazy already. It'll save time."

So he told them. Halvorsen's survey hadn't been mapping floodplains, whatever the permits said. Three days in, following an old drainage adit into the valley's sealed workings, they'd found a company archive room—dry, intact, deliberately walled up. Core samples. Thousands of meters of drilled core, logged and boxed, dating back decades. Water tables. Tailings seepage data. Contamination records for the aquifer that fed every settlement downriver, including, Tomas said, looking directly at Jude in a way that made Odile's neck prickle, "including everywhere downstream of here."

"Elias Yarrow signed the closure reports himself," Tomas said. "Said the valley was remediated. Clean. The cores say otherwise. They say they knew. They drilled the proof themselves and then they buried it behind a wall."

The river roared. Somewhere in the trees a bird complained about all of it.

"And Halvorsen wouldn't leave it," Tomas went on. "Wouldn't take photos and go. He wanted the cores—physical evidence, chain of custody, the whole chain. So we split the loads. Half the team went ahead downriver to the shingle bar at Kilometer Forty to wait for extraction. Halvorsen and two others stayed with the samples, moving slow, keeping them dry. I was ferrying the last of the camp gear when the bank gave way under me." He gestured at his ankle with bitter economy. "Four days. I heard planes twice. Nobody slowed down."

"We weren't looking for a man under a box," Odile said gently. "We were looking for a camp that wasn't here."

"It's not here," Tomas said, "because they took everything. Everything except me." He tried a smile; it didn't hold. "There's something else. Before they sealed the adit back up—Halvorsen made us seal it, said leaving it open would bring company people sniffing—he stood in that archive room a long time. Reading the logs. And he said—" Tomas frowned, reaching for it. "He said, 'She doesn't even know what she's carrying.' Just like that. Nobody knew who he meant. But he looked at the manifest of the old drilling crew afterward for a long time. At one name."

Odile felt it before she understood it: the silence beside her, the particular quality of Jude Yarrow—not-Yarrow, *Ferreira, don't be absurd*—going quiet in a way that had mass to it.

"Which name?" Jude asked. Her voice was steady. Odile had known her six years and had never once heard steadiness cost this much.

Tomas looked at her for a long moment, fever-bright and blunt.

"You know which name," he said. "I think you've always known."

* * *

They loaded Tomas into the back of the Cessna with the tenderness of the exhausted and the urgency of the storm building behind the northern ridgeline, black and organized now, a weather system with opinions. Odile did the preflight twice. Her hands were fine. It was everything else that shook.

Because here was the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since Kettle Bend: Jude had recognized those coordinates instantly. Had gone white over a valley sealed before Odile was born. Had argued against this flight with reasons that sounded reasonable and were actually barricades. And now a half-dead geologist was lying in the cargo bay having essentially said *your grandfather buried poison and lies down there*, and Jude was staring through the windshield at the wall of storm coming down the valley like it was a mirror.

"Kettle Bend?" Odile asked, hand on the starter. "Or do we keep looking?"

Jude was quiet long enough that Odile turned in her seat. Her partner's face was doing something Odile had never seen it do—failing to decide what to hide.

"There's a storm front sitting on the divide," Jude said finally. "We can't outrun it and we can't cross it loaded. There's a hunting cabin up the east fork, old Yarrow Company survey shelter. It'll hold."

"A Yarrow shelter," Odile repeated.

"It's the only roof in forty miles."

It wasn't the reason, and they both knew it wasn't the reason, and Odile started the engine anyway, because the sky was turning the color of a bruise and some conversations you have to land first.

As they lifted off the gravel and swung south, ahead of the wall of gray, Odile caught her partner's profile—jaw set, eyes forward, ten years of careful anonymity dissolving somewhere behind her ribs—and thought: *the past doesn't stay buried any better than core samples do.*

The rain reached them five minutes later, and it did not stop.
