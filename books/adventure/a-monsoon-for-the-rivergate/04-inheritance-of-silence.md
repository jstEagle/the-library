## 4. Inheritance of Silence

The storm front came up the valley like a slow animal, all shoulders and patience, and by the time they'd tied down the plane at the old survey camp it had swallowed half the sky. Jude worked the tie-downs with numb fingers while thunder rolled somewhere behind the ridgeline, patient as a debt collector.

"Canvas first," Odile said, hauling the tarp off the cargo rack. "Then the radio. If that antenna fills with water we're deaf for two days."

They got the tent up in twenty minutes, which was fifteen minutes longer than Odile's record and forty minutes faster than Jude had ever managed alone. By then the rain had arrived in earnest—not the polite drizzle of Kettle Bend's ordinary afternoons but a gray wall of it, hammering the canvas, turning the world down to the size of their one lamp.

Tomas lay in the tent's corner, sleeping under two blankets and a dose of the good painkillers from the medicine crate. His leg was splinted as well as either of them could manage. He'd told them everything he knew twice: the split expedition, the underground chamber, Halvorsen refusing to leave without "the cores." The third time through, he'd drifted off mid-sentence, his breath going long and even.

Which left Jude sitting on a supply crate with nothing to do but listen to the rain and feel Odile's eyes on her.

"You've been quiet since the shingle bar," Odile said. She was cleaning the camp stove with a rag, though it didn't need cleaning. It never needed cleaning. It was just something she did when she was thinking. "Quieter than usual, I mean, which is saying something. You make a Trappist monk look chatty."

"I'm thinking."

"You're hiding. There's a difference. I've watched you do both for six years and I can tell them apart."

Jude looked at the lamp. At the rain. At Tomas's sleeping face, slack and young-looking despite the gash across his brow. Somewhere upriver, in a valley the maps marked with a neat printed word—*RESTRICTED*—a man who had once taught her to read contour lines was guarding crates of something worth killing over.

"The coordinates," Jude said. "From the last transmission. You asked me how I recognized them so fast."

"I remember."

"You said I read them like I'd seen them before."

Odile set down the rag. "I remember that too."

Jude took a breath. It went in crooked, caught on something. Six years of flying with this woman, six years of engine grease shared and meals split and the particular intimacy of trusting someone with your life at eight thousand feet—and still the truth sat in her chest like a stone she'd swallowed whole and learned to live around.

"My name isn't Yarrow," she said, and then heard herself, and laughed, a short ugly sound. "That's—that's not how you say it. That's backwards. My name *is* Yarrow. Everything else is the lie."

* * *

The valley had a name once, before the company took it over. Her grandfather's letters called it Merrow's Deep, after some prospector nobody remembered. Elias Yarrow called it Site Seven, because names were for places you intended to keep.

She'd been eleven the summer he drove her up there in the company truck, her seat propped on phone books so she could see out the windshield. The road had been new then, graded smooth, lined with white stakes. She remembered thinking it looked like a runway.

"This is ours," Elias had said, and his hand on her shoulder had been heavy and warm and certain. "Someday all of it. You'll understand when you're older what a thing that is."

She hadn't understood. She'd understood the swimming hole behind the company house, and the way the kitchen smelled on Sundays, and the way her mother went quiet whenever the river came up in conversation—the way she'd go stand at the window and watch the water with an expression Jude couldn't name until years later, when she finally had the vocabulary for it. *Grief.* Her mother had been grieving a river.

The fish kills started when Jude was thirteen. Dead fish lining the banks like silver coins spilled from a torn pocket. The company men came through with clipboards and calm voices and explained about natural algal blooms, seasonal phenomena, nothing anyone could have prevented. Her grandfather repeated it at dinner, easy as grace. And her mother, washing dishes, had said nothing, and scrubbed one plate so long and so hard that Jude had feared it would go through.

The last time she saw the valley she was seventeen. There had been a hearing—she remembered fluorescent lights and men in suits saying *within acceptable parameters* until the words lost meaning—and afterward her mother had stood in the parking lot in the rain and said, "You can inherit a company or you can inherit the truth, and this family has only ever had room in the will for one." Then she'd gotten in the car and left Jude standing there, and that was more or less the end of the conversation they'd been having for seventeen years.

Jude changed her surname the way other people changed haircuts. Moved to Kettle Bend. Learned engines. Learned to love the river the way you love someone you've wronged—carefully, from a distance, bringing gifts. Medicine runs. Supply runs. Ten years of small restitution paid to the wrong account, because the right one was underwater.

* * *

"—and that's the whole of it," Jude finished. "Elias Yarrow was my grandfather. The valley is mine. Or will be, when the lawyers finish whatever lawyers do. I've spent ten years pretending otherwise, and now your friend Tomas has a broken leg and Dr. Halvorsen is missing, and it's my fault, or my family's, which—I'm learning—is possibly the same thing."

The lamp hissed. Rain drummed. Odile hadn't moved.

"Well," Odile said at last. "*Yarrow.*"

"It's a stupid name."

"It's a *loaded* name. It's a name with a body count." She stood up, and the tent suddenly felt very small. "Six years, Jude. Six years I've flown with you, slept in ditches with you, pulled you out of a river in 'forty-one—you remember that? When the Cessna went down and you were bleeding and delirious and you kept apologizing? To *me*, for crashing?"

"I remember."

"What were you apologizing for, Jude? Really?"

And there it was, the question she'd been dodging for a decade, delivered in a voice gentler than it had any right to be. Jude looked down at her hands. Engine grease in every crease, permanent as ink.

"For existing," she said quietly. "For being the last one of them. My mother died hating what her father built and I inherited it anyway, the whole poisoned estate, and I thought if I just—if I flew enough loads, fixed enough engines, kept my head down and my name changed, maybe it would—" She stopped. Started again. "It doesn't work. I know it doesn't work. Avoidance isn't penance, it's just avoidance with better posture."

Odile was quiet for a long moment. Outside, thunder moved overhead like furniture being dragged across heaven's floor.

"I'm angry," Odile said finally. "I want you to know that. Not—not at the secret, exactly. Secrets are heavy and I understand carrying them. I'm angry because you let me care about you while you stood inside a burning house insisting it was fine. Because Tomas is lying there with a shattered leg and Halvorsen is upriver in weather that kills people, and you *knew*. You knew what that valley was before we ever took off, and you let me fly into it blind because you were managing your shame instead of telling me the truth."

"That's fair."

"It's not about fair. Fair would be nice but it's not the point." Odile crouched in front of her, forearms on knees, close enough that Jude could see the storm reflected in her eyes. "The point is you don't get to carry this alone anymore. Not because I'm noble, but because I'm already in the plane. Whatever we're flying into, I'm in it. So the next time you decide something about my life, you ask me first. Understood?"

"Understood."

"And you're going to tell me the rest. All of it. What's in those crates, what Halvorsen found, why the company sealed the valley—because I've done the arithmetic, Jude, and companies don't seal valleys out of modesty."

So Jude told her. The tailings ponds. The processing chemicals that had leached into the water table for thirty years. The settlement her grandfather had bought cheap and buried deeper, the hearings, the dead fish, the town downstream whose cancer rates had been a statistic before they'd ever been a tragedy. She talked until the lamp burned low and Tomas stirred and slept again, and the storm wore itself hoarse against the canvas.

When she finished, Odile reached over and took her hand—greasy, trembling, hers.

"There," she said. "Was that so hard?"

"Yes."

"Good. Hard things should cost something." She squeezed once and let go. "Now sleep. Tomorrow the front lifts, and we're going upriver to collect a mad old scientist and whatever he's so determined to die for. And Jude—"

"Yeah?"

"When we find him, you're going to introduce yourself properly. Full name. Let him choke on it a little."

Despite everything, Jude felt her mouth twitch. "You're vindictive."

"I'm thorough. There's a difference." Odile banked the lamp low and settled into her bedroll. "Sleep, Yarrow. First honest night you've had in ten years. Try to enjoy it."

But sleep came slowly, and when it came, it brought the valley with it—the white stakes along the new road, her grandfather's warm heavy hand, her mother at the window watching the river as if it might yet say something back. And beneath the dream, faint but insistent, threaded a sound that wasn't thunder at all: a plane engine, far upriver, circling. Circling. As if something up there in the gray knew she was coming, and was waiting to see whose name she'd arrive under.
