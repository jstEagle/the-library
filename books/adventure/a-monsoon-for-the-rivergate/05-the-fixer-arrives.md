## 5. The Fixer Arrives

The charter into Kettle Bend was a twenty-year-old Cessna with a fuel gauge that lied and a pilot who chewed betel nut like it owed him money. Anton Reyes sat in the back seat with his briefcase on his knees and his tie already loosened, watching the Rivergate basin unspool beneath him in shades of green so aggressive they seemed personal about it.

He had read the file twice on the flight from the coast. He had read it a third time because the third time was where the interesting parts lived—the parts that didn't make it into the summary memo. *Jude Yarrow. Age 34. Pilot, freight and medevac contract work out of Kettle Bend airstrip settlement. No criminal record. No registered address beyond a hangar lease. Granddaughter of Elias Yarrow, founder, Yarrow Mining Company.*

That last line was the whole job, really. Everything else was scenery.

The strip at Kettle Bend was grass and gravel and a windsock that had given up any pretense of pointing anywhere useful. The pilot set down hard enough to rattle Reyes's teeth, and Reyes stepped out into air that hit him like a wet towel—heat, humidity, and underneath it all the faint mineral tang of the river, which he recognized from the reports as something that should not have been there at all.

"Mr. Reyes?" A man in a company polo shirt stood by the fuel drums, sweating through the collar. Devlin, the site liaison. He'd been sent up two weeks ago to establish a presence, which in corporate language meant: sit near the radio, drink the local coffee, and report anything that moved.

"Devlin." They shook hands. "How's our friend?"

"Quiet. Flies most mornings, back by dark. Works on her plane evenings. Doesn't drink at the cantina—buys bottles and takes them home. Talks to maybe four people total."

"Which four?"

"The mechanic, mostly. Odile Marchand. The nurse at the clinic. The old woman who runs the supply store. And—" Devlin hesitated, consulting his notebook like a schoolboy afraid of being wrong, "—the dog. There's a dog. She talks to the dog quite a lot."

Reyes considered this. "What does she say to the dog?"

"I don't—I didn't think that was relevant—"

"It's always relevant what people say when they think no one's listening. That's when they're telling the truth." He snapped his briefcase open, checked the envelope inside for the fourth time since the coast, and closed it again. "Where is she now?"

"Hangar. She's been in the hangar since six this morning."

* * *

Reyes had done this work long enough to know that the first approach mattered more than everything after it. Come in too hard and people dug in; come in too soft and they smelled the condescension. The trick was to arrive as something boring. A safety consultant was perfect. Nobody feared a safety consultant. Nobody remembered them either.

He walked across the strip in his city shoes, which immediately collected a fine gray dust that would never fully leave them, and found the hangar doors rolled halfway open against the heat. Inside: a De Havilland Otter up on jacks, its cowling off, its engine exposed like a patient mid-surgery. And under the engine, legs sticking out at an angle that suggested either deep concentration or a complete disregard for her own spine, Jude Yarrow.

A woman with close-cropped silver hair and forearms like bridge cable stood nearby holding a torque wrench and watching the doorway with an expression Reyes could only describe as pre-hostile. That would be Odile Marchand.

He stopped at a respectful distance and cleared his throat.

"You're blocking my light," said the legs.

"My apologies." He stepped sideways. "Ms. Yarrow? My name is Anton Reyes. I'm a safety consultant with Corvane Risk Management—we've been retained by several of the upstream operators to look into the Halvorsen expedition situation."

The legs went still. Then she slid out from under the engine, sat up, and looked at him, and Reyes felt the particular professional satisfaction of a man whose research has paid off. She looked exactly like the photographs of her grandmother that hung in the lobby of the company's founding building—same jaw, same level gray eyes, same way of looking at a person as though measuring them for a coffin.

"We filed our last position report nine days ago," she said. "You're a little late for safety consulting."

"Safety consulting is mostly about being late." He smiled. "I understand you knew Dr. Halvorsen."

"I flew supplies for his survey. That's not knowing someone."

"It's more than most people here can claim." He let that sit. Odile had drifted closer without appearing to move, the wrench hanging loose in her hand in a way that was somehow louder than if she'd been brandishing it. "Ms. Yarrow, I'll be direct, because I find people in places like this appreciate directness. The expedition is overdue. The company has assets and personnel interests in the basin. We're offering a retainer to local pilots who might have information—a route flown, a smoke sighting, a radio fragment. Five thousand for anything that locates them. Twenty-five thousand if you can bring them out."

Jude wiped her hands on a rag with great deliberation, as though the rag were the more interesting conversationalist.

"And if I just tell you to get off my strip?"

"Then I'll get off your strip," Reyes said pleasantly. "And I'll come back tomorrow, because I'm paid to be persistent, and eventually you'll want something I can give you. Everyone does. Fuel contracts. Landing rights. A quiet word when the licensing board comes around next spring." He paused. "I'd rather we were friendly about it."

Odile made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been the beginning of an assault.

* * *

They ended up, absurdly, at the cantina, because Jude said she'd talk where there was coffee and witnesses, and Reyes understood the second part was the point. He bought three coffees from the old woman who ran the place, who looked at him the way farmers look at weather they don't trust, and carried them to the corner table where Jude sat with her cap pulled low and Odile planted beside her like a fence post with opinions.

"Twenty-five thousand," Jude said, turning her cup. "That's a strange number. Specific numbers mean someone did math. Who did the math, Mr. Reyes?"

"The math," Reyes said, "is what I'm paid to protect."

"See, I figured." She looked up at him then, and he watched her decide something. "Here's my information. Nine days ago I got a garbled transmission from Halvorsen's team. Coordinates in the upper valley. Weather closed in. That's everything I know, and I'd have told the civil aviation office the same thing if they'd bothered to ask instead of filing it under 'monsoon season.'"

"That is helpful," Reyes said. "Thank you." And then, because the moment was right and the file was burning a hole in his briefcase and his instructions had been explicit: "You should know that I know who you are, Ms. Yarrow."

The cantina noise seemed to recede, or perhaps that was just his own pulse.

"Elias Yarrow's granddaughter," he said quietly. "Raised off the books after your mother cut ties. You changed your name at nineteen, took ferry contracts nobody else wanted, and built yourself a life ten hours' flight from anything with your family's name on it. It's a good life. I respect it." He slid the envelope onto the table between them but kept two fingers on it. "But the survey team is sitting on top of Valley Seven right now. And Valley Seven is a subject the company feels—strongly—about."

Odile had gone very still. Not looking at Jude. Looking at the table, at the envelope, at the space between them where a decade of careful anonymity had just been run through with a fork.

"You're mistaken," Jude said. Her voice was even. Too even. "My name is Yarrow because it's common upriver. Ask anyone."

"Of course." Reyes withdrew his fingers from the envelope. "Keep the retainer whether you take the job or not. Consider it a courtesy." He stood, straightened his jacket, and allowed himself one last line, delivered gently, almost kindly, the way you'd hand someone bad news at a funeral:

"The company doesn't hate you, Ms. Yarrow. That's the thing people never understand. It wants you home. There's a seat on the board with your name on it, and a version of events with your signature under it, and both of those are worth considerably more than twenty-five thousand." He put a card on the table. "When you fly upriver—and you will, because I've read your file and you're exactly the kind of person who flies upriver—remember that loyalty runs in families. Even broken ones."

He left money for the coffees, nodded to the old woman, and walked back out into the heat, feeling the eyes of the entire cantina on his back like sunburn.

* * *

From the porch of the supply store, Reyes watched the hangar through binoculars he'd bought in a duty-free shop and pretended not to enjoy. Devlin hovered at his elbow with a clipboard.

"She'll fly," Reyes said. "Tonight or tomorrow. Watch the fuel drums—if she takes extra, she's going far."

"And if she finds them first?"

"Then we follow her." He lowered the binoculars. In the hangar's open doorway, Jude Yarrow stood talking to her mechanic, gesturing once, sharply, at the sky as if accusing it personally. "And Devlin—when we follow her, we do it politely. She's not a problem to be removed. She's an heir to be recovered. There's a difference, and the difference is the entire reason I'm here instead of some man with a shovel."

Devlin wrote that down. Reyes suspected he'd written down the wrong half of it.

Inside the hangar, the Otter's engine turned over with a sound like a grudging animal waking up, and Reyes checked his watch, and began, quietly, to make arrangements.
