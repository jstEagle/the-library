## 6. Upriver in the Gray

The weather had turned the color of dishwater overnight, and by morning it had opinions.

Jude stood on the dock at Kettle Bend with her hands jammed in her jacket pockets, watching the Rivergate run brown and swollen past the pilings. It carried whole trees now. It carried fences. Yesterday it had carried what Odile swore was somebody's outhouse, spinning sedately downstream like a little wooden boat with delusions.

"That's not a river anymore," Odile said beside her, coffee steaming in the cold damp. "That's a parade."

"We fly above the parade."

"We fly *under* the ceiling. That's the part I don't love." Odile blew on her coffee. "Cloud deck's sitting at eight hundred feet, maybe lower in the valley. You want to thread a gorge in murk thick enough to chew, be my guest, but I'd like it noted for the record that I said words about it."

"Noted. Duly." Jude checked her watch. "Halvorsen's been dark six days. If the monsoon cells stack up like the forecast says, we might get one window. Maybe two. After that, nobody flies upriver for a week, and whatever's left of that expedition spends a week finding out what river water does to people."

Odile was quiet a moment. "You knew him. Halvorsen."

"He taught me to read a drill log before he taught me to drive." Jude said it flat, and hated how flat it was. "Come on. Pre-flight."

The Cessna—*Sparrowhawk*, because Odile named everything, including the generator, which answered to *Gertrude*—lifted off the strip at first light and immediately began to regret it. The air was soft and heavy, the kind that made the plane feel like it was flying through wet felt. Jude kept them low, skimming the treetops where the murk thinned, following the river because the river was the only map that made sense in weather like this.

Below them, the world was drowning politely. Channels that had been ankle-deep gravel in the dry season ran brown and fast. Side creeks had jumped their banks and were making a run for the main channel like latecomers to a wedding.

"Contact," Odile said suddenly. "Two o'clock, on the bar. That's a tent. That's—Jude, that's *people*."

Jude banked. Below, wedged on a shingle bar maybe two hundred meters long and shaped like a crooked comma, was a camp that had stopped pretending to be a camp. Two tarps. A scatter of orange crates stacked and covered with a groundsheet, weighted down with rocks like somebody expected the river to steal them personally. And figures—four, five—standing in a line, one of them waving both arms over their head with the desperate, mechanical rhythm of someone who had been waving for a long time.

"Bar's maybe three hundred meters of dry ground," Odile said. "Water's on both sides, and the near channel looks like it's running four, five knots."

"Too soft to land wheels, too short for floats to be comfortable."

"So we do it uncomfortable."

Jude grinned despite everything. "You're learning."

"I'm *suffering*. It looks similar from the outside."

---

Odile had done maybe four hundred water landings in her life, and she would tell you—had told Jude, at volume, on multiple occasions—that every single one of them was a negotiation you entered from a position of weakness. The river didn't care about your feelings. The river cared about depth, debris, and current, and it would take your floats apart at the seams if you got greedy.

She took the controls on final because Jude's hands were steady but her eyes weren't, and Odile had learned to read that difference years ago. The approach was downwind of the bar, over the slow water in the lee, wheels up, floats kissing the surface with a sound like a long exhale. The *Sparrowhawk* planed, slowed, and swung around to nose into the gravel shallows with a grinding crunch that Odile felt in her teeth.

"Everyone alive?" she called, before the prop had even stopped.

A woman in a soaked field jacket stumbled down the bank toward them. Her face was sunburned and hollowed, her hands shaking so hard she had to grab the strut to keep hold of it.

"You're not military," she said. "Please tell me you're not with the company."

"We're with nobody," Odile said. "We're with the medicine run and a bad attitude. Who's hurt?"

The counting was the worst part. Odile did it while Jude talked with the tall, gray-bearded man who came down the bank last, moving like his joints had been replaced with river gravel. Dr. Halvorsen. He looked twenty years older than the man from the photographs Jude kept not-looking-at in her head.

Five alive. Odile counted them twice, because the woman—her name was Sefa, the expedition's hydrologist—kept saying a name that wasn't on the list.

"Ruth," Sefa said. "Ruth Okonjo. She was in the second boat when the channel took it. Three days ago." Her voice cracked down the middle. "We found her pack. Downstream, snagged. Just the pack."

Odile had hauled enough drowned gear out of rivers to know what *just the pack* meant. She put a hand on Sefa's shoulder and left it there.

"We'll take you all," Odile said. "It'll take three trips. We've got fuel for it, barely."

"There are the samples," Halvorsen said from behind them. His voice was wrecked but level, the voice of a man who had rehearsed a sentence until it stopped shaking. "The crates. They come too. They come *first*."

Odile looked at the orange crates under their tarp, then at the five living, breathing people on the bar, and felt something cold settle in her chest, because she recognized that tone. She'd heard it from men guarding payrolls and from one memorable fisherman guarding a cooler of contraband roe. It was the tone of a person who had decided the cargo mattered more than they did.

"Doc," she said carefully, "we can fit people or crates. Not both, not on the first lift."

"Then the crates go first."

"Doc—"

"*They go first.*" He caught himself, closed his eyes, and when he opened them there was something underneath the command that looked horribly like grief. "Ruth died carrying one of them across the bar. I will not have it be for nothing. I'm sorry. I know what I'm asking. I'm asking anyway."

Behind him, the others said nothing, but none of them looked surprised. That was the part Odile would remember later. None of them looked surprised.

---

Jude stood at the edge of the water with Halvorsen while Odile organized the loading, and the river talked the whole time, brown and busy, carrying its parade of wreckage past them.

"Six days of silence," Jude said. "Your sat phone died the first night, we figured. But you missed two check-ins before that."

"Because I turned it off." Halvorsen looked at her, and his eyes were the same pale, assessing gray she remembered from lecture halls and drill sites, except now they had a feverish brightness behind them. "I needed time to think about who to trust, and I remembered that you fly the Kettle Bend route. I turned the radio off and I prayed the weather would keep everyone else away."

"That's not praying, that's sabotage."

"I'm an old man, Jude. I'm allowed one act of sabotage." He gestured at the crates. "Do you know what's in them?"

"Rocks."

"Core samples. Sixty meters of core from a sealed adit in a valley your grandfather's company filled with concrete and forgot about." He said *your grandfather* without emphasis, without cruelty, just laid it down between them like a stone on a table, and Jude felt the ground tilt under her in a way that had nothing to do with the river. "Tailings leachate. Arsenic, lead, mercury, in concentrations that would make your hair fall out reading the numbers. The aquifer under the eastern basin has been drinking it for forty years. Every village downstream. Every well."

The river roared past. Jude found she was gripping the float strut hard enough to hurt her palm.

"You knew," she said. "The coordinates in the last transmission. You sent them knowing I'd recognize the valley."

"I hoped you would."

"You *used*—"

"I chose you," Halvorsen said, "because you're the best pilot on this river and the only one who'd come. We can argue about the rest when we're all alive. Right now I need you to do the thing you do, which is fly, and I need you to fly these crates like they're made of glass and my whole life is inside them." He paused. "Because it is."

Jude looked at the crates. She looked at Sefa, sitting on the gravel with her arms around her knees, staring at the water where a pack had washed up without its owner. She looked at the brown parade of the river, carrying somebody's fence, somebody's tree, somebody's whole drowned life toward the sea.

"Odile loads first," Jude heard herself say. "People first. Then the crates, and I fly them myself, every trip, and you sit in the right seat and tell me exactly what I'm carrying and why I should care." She stepped closer. "And Doc? When we're on the ground and everyone's breathing, you and I are going to have a conversation about the difference between choosing someone and *using* them."

"Fair," Halvorsen said. "I'd expect nothing less from a Yarrow."

"Don't," Jude said, very quietly. "Not yet. Not until I've decided what that word means."

* * *

Odile made the first run with four passengers and a fuel gauge she didn't trust, down through the gray with the river unspooling beneath her like a rope someone was paying out too fast. Sefa sat in back with her forehead against the window, watching the bar shrink behind them, watching it take Halvorsen and the last of them into its crooked-comma embrace, and Odile thought: *one down, two lifts to go, and the sky's getting lower by the hour.*

On the second approach the water had risen. She could see it eating the downstream end of the bar in patient bites, and Halvorsen stood at the upstream end with the crates, loading them one at a time like a man building a wall against a tide.

"Last of it," he shouted over the engine, hauling the final crate aboard. "That's all sixty meters."

"That's not all of it," Odile said, jerking her thumb at the sky, where the western horizon had gone a bruised, boiling purple-gray. "That's coming too. Everybody in. Jude, we're heavy and we're racing a storm cell—"

But Jude was already looking downstream, where the murk was lifting just enough to show the channel, and the channel was showing them something new: white water where there had been none that morning, and a debris line moving fast, whole trees standing on end in the current like drowned men rising.

"Change of plan," Jude said. "We're not making two more trips. We're making one, and it's going to be the worst flight of our lives."

Odile looked at the sky, then at the river, then at her partner's face, which wore the expression Odile had learned to fear and follow in equal measure.

"Gertrude's going to kill us," she said.

"Gertrude's not here. Gertrude's safe in the hangar."

"Then *we're* going to kill us." Odile swung into the pilot's seat and started the engine. "Fine. But if we die, I'm naming the storm after you."
