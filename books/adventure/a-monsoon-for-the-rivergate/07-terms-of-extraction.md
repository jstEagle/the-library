## 7. Terms of Extraction

The shingle bar was maybe forty meters of gray stone and driftwood, and Dr. Anders Halvorsen had spent six days on it learning the particular humility that comes from watching a river decide what you are worth.

He sat with his back against a crate—the crates, rather, all nine of them, arranged in a defensive crescent like he was besieged, which in every way that mattered he was. His left boot had given up its argument with the river two days ago and he'd wrapped the foot in a flour sack. His glasses were held together with surveyor's tape. He was sixty-eight years old, and he had never once in his life been comfortable, which he considered his single greatest professional qualification.

Comfort, Halvorsen believed, was where science went to die.

Across the fire pit—ash now, the wood too wet to burn—he watched Jude Yarrow sleep. Or pretend to sleep. She'd been pretending for an hour, lying on her side facing away from him with the rigid stillness of a woman composing a speech. Odile was awake, though; Odile was always awake, it seemed, tinkering with something by lantern light, and when their eyes met she gave him a look of frank appraisal, the look of a woman doing arithmetic on how much trouble a man was worth.

Halvorsen smiled at her. It was, he knew, an irritating smile. He cultivated it.

* * *

He had known, of course.

That was the thing Jude would want to say first, once she stopped pretending to sleep, so he might as well get it straight in his own head: yes. He had known who she was from the very beginning. From the moment her name came up on the manifest for Kettle Bend resupply runs—J. Yarrow, sole operator—and he'd pulled the file out of pure academic habit and read it twice, then a third time, sitting very still in his office at the university while the radiator knocked like something trying to get out.

Elias Yarrow's granddaughter. The heir nobody talked about, because the family had done such thorough work making sure there was nothing left to talk about. The mother relocated. The name changed on paper but not quite enough. A girl who walked away from a fortune at nineteen and buried herself in engine grease four hundred kilometers upriver, as if distance were a kind of amnesia.

Most people would have called that a conflict of interest and routed around her. Halvorsen had called it a qualification.

He was not proud of it. That was important too. He was not proud, exactly—but he was not sorry, and he suspected those were different countries, and he had lived long enough to know you could die in one and be buried in the other.

Because here was the arithmetic he'd done in that office, the arithmetic no ethics board would have approved: core samples proving thirty years of contamination don't do any good in a university vault. Evidence needs a courier. And couriers come in two kinds—people with nothing to lose, who can be bought or frightened cheaply, and people with everything to lose, who cannot.

A woman with nothing to lose takes the money. A woman with everything to lose takes the samples, because handing them over means admitting the last ten years of her life were a hiding place, and people don't admit that unless you make them.

So he had chosen her route. Requested Kettle Bend specifically. Scheduled his check-ins through her frequency. Built the whole expedition's supply line through the one pilot in the basin whose grandfather had signed the orders that poisoned the valley.

It was, he reflected, watching her shoulders stay too still by the dead fire, either the best or worst decision of his career. Possibly both. Most of his decisions had managed both; that was what made them decisions instead of opinions.

Tomas stirred under the tarp and muttered something about water levels. The boy had a gash down his calf that Odile had cleaned and stitched with a competence Halvorsen found quietly moving—one more item on the ledger of things he hadn't planned for and should have. He'd planned for weather, for corporate interference, for equipment failure. He had not planned for how much he would come to like these people, and how much that liking would cost him now, sitting here about to be hated by someone he'd already begun to think of as—

Well. As family, if he were being honest, and Halvorsen made a practice of honesty at precisely the moments it was least convenient.

* * *

She stopped pretending at first light.

He heard her sit up, heard the small sounds of her checking the plane's tie-downs before she'd even rubbed the sleep from her eyes—a pilot's piety, order before self. Then she came and stood over him, arms crossed, backlit by a sky the color of wet slate, and said nothing, because she was waiting for him to start, and he respected that. Interrogators waited. So did daughters.

"Before you begin," Halvorsen said, "I want to state for the record that I'm about to deserve whatever you're going to say."

"You knew." Her voice was flat. Not loud. Loud would have been easier; loud was weather, and weather passed. This was flat the way bedrock is flat. "You knew who I was. The whole time."

"I did."

"The route. The frequencies. The—" She stopped. He watched her arrive at it, watched the whole architecture assemble itself behind her eyes, and he did not look away, because looking away would have been the final insult. "You built your supply line through me. On purpose."

"Yes."

"Why?" And there it was—not why did you do it, but the rawer question underneath, the one that had kept her awake: *why me, what is wrong with me that made me useful.* "Was I a joke to you? Some kind of—symbolic gesture? Fly the evidence with Yarrow blood on it?"

"No," Halvorsen said. "Though I'll admit the symmetry appealed to my vanity. No—it was practical. You're the best pilot in this basin, Jude. Possibly the only one who could fly these loads in monsoon conditions. But that's not why." He levered himself up against the crate, flour-sack foot and all, and met her eyes. "I chose you because you have something to lose. Everyone else I could have hired has a price. You don't. Your price isn't money. It never was. Your price is the truth about your own name, and the only way anyone ever pays that is by carrying it home."

The silence after that lasted long enough for the river to change its mind about something downstream—a deep shifting groan, the bar settling.

"You used me," she said finally.

"Yes."

"You let me fly medicine runs past a valley my family sealed, for ten years, and you never said a word."

"I told myself I was giving you time," Halvorsen said. "That was a lie I found comfortable, and I've already confessed my feelings about comfort." He took off his ruined glasses, cleaned them on his shirt with great care, put them back on. "Here is what I actually believe, and you may throw it back at me if it helps: I believe you would have found this valley eventually. People like you always do. The only question was whether you'd find it alone, with nothing in your hands, or whether you'd find it with nine crates of proof and a reason to carry them."

"So this was mercy."

"This was engineering," Halvorsen said. "Mercy would have been leaving you in Kettle Bend. Engineering is what gets the truth out alive. I'm sorry they feel similar from the inside. They don't."

She turned away from him then, and he let her, and he listened to the river and thought about Tomas's leg and the one they'd lost—Maren, gone into the rapids on the third day, and he would carry that until they buried him, the weight of a name he'd led into this valley—and he thought: *she will not forgive me today. Good. Forgiveness today would mean I misjudged her.*

Odile appeared at his elbow with a tin cup of river-boiled tea that tasted, generously, like boiled river. She didn't hand it to him so much as hold it out and wait for him to earn it.

"You knew she'd say yes," Odile said. It wasn't a question.

"I hoped," Halvorsen said. "Hoping is what old men do instead of planning."

"No," Odile said. "You planned. I've seen your field notes. There's a contingency schedule in there with three alternate airstrips and a fuel cache. You planned." She looked across the bar at Jude's back, at the set of her shoulders. "You just planned around a person instead of a river. Rivers forgive you. Eventually."

"That," Halvorsen said, taking the tea, "is the most damning thing anyone has said to me in forty years, and I once testified before a parliamentary committee."

Something moved at the corner of Odile's mouth. Not forgiveness—amusement, which in Odile's economy was worth considerably more.

* * *

Jude came back an hour later.

Halvorsen had expected shouting. What he got was worse and better: she crouched down in front of him, forearms on her knees, close enough that he could see the exhaustion under her eyes and the thing underneath the exhaustion, which was not rage anymore. Rage had burned off in that hour like morning fog. What remained was load-bearing.

"Nobody else can fly these loads," she said. "Not in this. Not nine crates downriver through pre-monsoon cells with the strip at Kettle Bend possibly compromised. Nobody else knows the channels, and nobody else is stupid enough to try."

"That is an accurate summary," Halvorsen said carefully.

"So understand something." She tapped the crate between them, once, like knocking on a door. "I'm not doing this for you. I'm not doing it for your study or your career or your beautiful engineered redemption arc. I'm doing it because those samples are the only true thing my family ever produced, and I am going to be the one who carries them. Me. Not you. You don't get to use me and also get credit for my conscience."

"No," Halvorsen agreed. "I don't."

"And when this is over, you and I are going to have another conversation, and it's going to be longer than this one, and you're going to hate it."

"I look forward to it," he said, and meant it, and was surprised by how much he meant it.

She stood, and looked downriver, where the clouds had begun organizing themselves into something with intent. Then she said, almost to herself: "First cell hits by tomorrow night. Maybe sooner. We fly today—all three loads, crew first, then the crates."

"There's a fuel cache at the old mission bend," Halvorsen offered. "Two drums, marked with a red X. I put them there in April."

Her head turned slowly. "Of course you did."

"It seemed prudent."

"You've been planning this since April." She laughed then—a short, disbelieving sound with no humor in it and, underneath, the first pale thread of something that might eventually become humor, or might become something better. "You absolute old spider."

"I prefer 'architect,'" Halvorsen said, "but I've been called worse by better."

She was already walking toward the plane, and Odile fell in beside her, and Halvorsen remained seated against his crates with his terrible tea, watching the two of them bend over the load plan in the grass, heads together, arguing already—he could see it in the way Odile kept jabbing a finger at the southern channel while Jude shook her head.

Downriver, the horizon had gone the color of a bruise coming up. The first cell would make landfall tonight, and between here and the coast lay two hundred kilometers of rising water, a sabotaged strip, and a man named Reyes whom Halvorsen had met once, years ago, at a conference neither of them should have attended.

He finished the tea. It was, as always, unbearable.

"Fly well, Jude Yarrow," he said quietly, to no one, using the name deliberately, the way you'd set a lamp in a window. "Bring it home."
