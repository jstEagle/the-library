## 8. The Monsoon Makes Landfall

**JUDE**

The sky over the valley had gone the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom.

Jude stood on the shingle bar with her boots in six inches of water and watched Halvorsen's people load the first crate into the Otter like it was a coffin they were trying not to jostle. Which, in a way, it was. Forty years of core samples. Forty years of proof that the Yarrow Company had known exactly what it was burying in that sealed valley, and had buried it anyway.

"Careful," Halvorsen said for the fourth time, hovering as Odile's nephew-by-association—no, that was just Tomas, who seemed to have adopted himself into every family within reach—slid the crate onto the cargo rails.

"Doc," Tomas said, "if I drop it, we'll all be dead of the flood before you finish yelling at me."

"He's got a point," Jude said. "Load faster."

The plan was simple, which meant it was doomed. Three flights downriver to Kettle Bend. Crew and crates split between them so no single disaster could kill the whole truth. Jude flying lead, Odile in the second plane, and Halvorsen's old survey bird—patched, grumbling, held together by prayer and baling wire—carrying the lightest load because nobody trusted it with anything heavier than hope.

The monsoon was supposed to be two days out. The monsoon, as it turned out, had never read the schedule.

The first cell came down the valley like a freight train with a grudge, and the rain hit the shingle bar sideways, hard enough to sting.

"Go, go," Jude shouted, and hauled herself up into the Otter's door as Odile fired the engine behind her.

---

**ODILE**

Odile had flown through weather that would make grown pilots write letters home. This was different. This was the sky itself changing its mind about being a sky.

She flew second in line, twenty meters off Jude's wing, watching the river below them swell from brown to something closer to boiling chocolate. The current had picked up whole trees and was carrying them downstream like matchsticks, and one of those matchsticks, she noted with professional interest, was roughly the size of the airstrip at Kettle Bend.

"Kettle Bend, this is Otter Two," she said into the radio, for the ninth time.

Static answered. Static, static, and then—faintly, threaded through it like wire through cloth—a voice she didn't recognize saying something about *strip* and *not* and a word that might have been *closed* or might have been *crashed* or might have been nothing at all.

"Jude." She keyed the interplane frequency. "I'm getting traffic from Kettle Bend. Or traffic-shaped noise. Someone's saying the strip's a problem."

A pause. Then Jude's voice, tight: "Define problem."

"I couldn't. The rain's eating my antenna along with everything else."

"Then we land on the water if we have to. The river's wide enough below the bend."

"The river," Odile said, "is currently auditioning for the role of ocean."

"Odile."

"I know. I know. Flying."

---

**TOMAS**

Tomas sat in the back of the second plane with his arm strapped to his chest and his heart somewhere around his tonsils, watching Dr. Halvorsen hold a crate like it was a sleeping child. Which, honestly, after what they'd found down there, maybe it was.

"You're doing fine," Halvorsen said, apparently to the crate.

"Thanks, Doc."

"I was talking to the samples."

Tomas grinned despite everything, which was his entire personality and he knew it. "You know what's funny? Back at camp, when Rennie said we should leave the cores and run, I almost agreed. I thought, it's rocks. It's just rocks in tubes."

Halvorsen looked at him then, and his face in the gray light was very old and very steady. "It's never just rocks in tubes, Tomas. Rocks are where people hide their sins. They think nobody digs that deep."

"Well," Tomas said, as the plane bucked and the rain went from heavy to biblical, "somebody dug."

"Somebody always does. Eventually."

The plane dropped—a long, stomach-lifting fall—and Tomas heard Odile swearing in three languages above him, one of which he was fairly sure she'd invented.

---

**JUDE**

Kettle Bend appeared out of the murk all wrong.

Jude saw it in pieces: the fuel drums first, tipped and scattered like dropped toys. Then the windsock, torn half away and streaming limp. Then the strip itself, and her stomach did something complicated and painful, because the strip was fine—mostly fine—the strip was *intact*, except for the fresh trench carved across its midpoint, deep and deliberate, machine-cut, the kind of damage that didn't happen by accident or even by storm.

Somebody had taken a blade to her runway while she'd been upriver saving the world.

"Reyes," she said aloud, and the name tasted like copper.

Below, the settlement looked abandoned. No lights. No movement on the dock. Marta's kitchen dark, and Marta's kitchen was never dark; the woman kept a lamp burning for lost pilots the way other people kept cats.

"Otter One to Two," she said. "Strip's sabotaged. Deliberate. We're going to the river."

Silence, then Odile: "Copy. Water landing. Try not to drown anything important."

"That's your whole safety briefing?"

"It's the abridged version. The full version has jokes."

Jude brought the Otter around, low, riding the wind like a drunk riding a horse, and picked her stretch of river with the cold clarity that only arrived when everything else was on fire. Wide water below the bend. Current running hard but straight. Trees on both banks waiting like spectators.

She flared. Touched. The hull slapped the surface once, twice, and then they were planing, throwing spray, alive.

Behind her, through the sheeting rain, she watched Odile's plane come down the same corridor of gray—

And she watched the wind shear take it.

---

**ODILE**

Later, Odile would reconstruct it in pieces, the way you reconstruct any bad memory: not as a story but as a pile of evidence.

The gust from the west, sudden, the kind that doesn't exist until a monsoon invents it. The left wing lifting like the plane had opinions about landing here. The correction—too much, too late, because physics keeps office hours and does not do favors. The bank coming up. The water coming up faster.

She remembered thinking, with absurd calm: *well, this will be expensive.*

Then impact—not the crash itself, which her body declined to record, but the aftermath. Cold. Roaring. The world upside down and full of river. Her shoulder screaming a note she'd never heard before, high and total, the note of something broken properly.

Hands on her. Tomas's hands, she realized—Tomas, who had unstrapped himself mid-ditch and gone out the emergency window into a flooding river without a single thought for his own arm, because that was who Tomas was, an idiot, a dear idiot.

"I've got you," he was shouting. "I've got you, I've got you—"

"Plane," she managed.

"Forget the plane!"

"The *samples*, you beautiful fool—"

They got her onto the shingle at the river's edge, four of them hauling, Halvorsen directing operations with the serene authority of a man who had clearly survived worse and intended to be insufferable about it. Rain hammered down. The second plane sat half-sunk in the shallows, nose-down, tail up, looking embarrassed.

Odile lay on her back in the mud and laughed until her ribs joined her shoulder in protest.

"Something funny?" Jude demanded, dropping to her knees beside her, hands hovering, afraid to touch.

"You should see the other guy," Odile said. "Also me. I am also the other guy."

"Your shoulder—"

"Is dislocated or destroyed, yes. Help me sit up before the river decides otherwise."

Jude's hands were shaking. Odile noticed, because she noticed everything, and because Jude's hands never shook—not in weather, not in trouble, not ever. She caught one of them and held it, muddy against muddy.

"Hey," Odile said. "Look at me. Everything important is still on this beach. You. Me. The rocks in tubes. The rest is plumbing."

Jude made a sound that was almost a laugh and mostly not, and pressed her forehead to Odile's wet hair for exactly three seconds, which from Jude was a sonnet.

Then she stood up, wiped her face, and became the pilot again.

"Third flight's cancelled," she said. "We're all going in one plane. Tomas, splint her properly. Doc, get in. Somebody find out if that survey bird can still float."

"It cannot," said the survey bird's pilot, arriving soaked and apologetic. "It also cannot fly. I would describe its condition as archaeological."

"Fine. Strip it for parts. Everyone in the Otter."

"And the samples?" Halvorsen asked.

Jude looked at the half-sunk plane, at the crates they'd already pulled free, stacked on the shingle under tarps, forty years of buried sin gleaming wet in the lightning.

"They ride up front," she said. "Next to me. Where I can keep an eye on them."

---

**HALVORSEN**

From the copilot's seat, Halvorsen watched Jude work the overloaded Otter off the flooded shingle and into the teeth of the storm, and permitted himself, for the first time in thirty years, to feel something adjacent to hope.

He had chosen her route years ago, quietly, deliberately—an old man's chess move, putting the evidence in the hands of the one person whose stake was blood-deep. He had known she'd hate him for it. He had been right. He could live with right.

But watching her now—flying a drowning river with a broken mechanic, a wounded boy, and a cargo of truth lashed to the floorboards—he understood he had underestimated her. He had thought she carried her grandfather's name like a stone. He hadn't understood she was carrying it like a fuse.

Lightning walked across the water ahead of them, and through the static, faint and impossible, the radio coughed out a voice: clipped, corporate, patient.

*"Otter One, this is Reyes. We should talk. I'm at the coast strip, Ms. Yarrow. Waiting."*

Beside him, Jude's jaw set, and her hands stayed steady on the yoke, and she flew on into the rain without answering—straight toward the last safe airstrip, and whatever was waiting there.
