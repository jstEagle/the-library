## 9. Last Safe Airstrip

The valley was drowning behind them.

Jude watched it happen in the mirror of her own eyes—every glance at the rearview struts showed the Rivergate gone brown and swollen, the shingle bar where they'd found Halvorsen already a rumor beneath the current. The monsoon had come in like a landlord changing the locks. No negotiation, no notice. Just water, everywhere, taking back everything the dry season had pretended to own.

"Tell me you're not looking back," Odile said from the right seat. Her arm was strapped tight against her ribs where the emergency landing had wrenched it, and she'd refused—refused—to stay behind with the injured. She sat beside Jude now with her good hand on the throttle quadrant like it was a piano she intended to play very badly.

"I'm navigating."

"You're brooding with a heading."

"That's what navigation is."

Odile snorted, then winced, then laughed anyway, which was very Odile: doing everything the hard way because the hard way had better company.

In the back, lashed down under cargo netting, sat forty pounds of core samples in steel tubes—the whole poisoned history of the Yarrow Company, drilled out of the valley floor in neat silver cylinders. Dr. Halvorsen had kissed each crate before they loaded it, which Jude had chosen to find charming rather than unhinged. Beside the samples, wrapped in a tarp and shivering through the after-shock of everything, rode Priya Nair's photographs, Halvorsen's field notes, and one dead man's waterproof journal that they'd promised his sister would reach daylight.

Ahead: Saltmere Strip. The last safe airstrip on the coast, which is to say the last strip the company hadn't bought, burned, or bureaucratically smothered. Two hundred miles of storm between here and there, with an eye—a genuine, miraculous eye—opening in the cell wall like a door someone had left ajar out of pity.

Jude flew into it.

* * *

The eye was not peace. It was the held breath before a scream. The world went strangely gold around them, clouds stacked in gray cathedrals on every side, rain hanging motionless in the air like the sky had forgotten what it was doing. The Cessna bucked once, twice, then steadied, and for eleven minutes they flew through a corridor of impossible calm.

"Tomas would have loved this," Odile said quietly. Tomas, who'd waited three days on a riverbank with a broken leg and a sense of humor intact, who'd told them the valley "kept its receipts." He was at Kettle Bend now, sedated and splinted, alive because Odile had put a plane down on moving water with a busted wing and a prayer.

"He'd have tried to land in it."

"He'd have *named* it. Something stupid. 'The Hallway.' 'God's Corridor.'"

"'The Eye of the Yarrow,'" Jude said, and then wished she hadn't, because the name sat in the cabin like a third passenger.

Odile looked over. Her face did the thing it always did when Jude flinched from herself—not anger anymore, not since the night of the confession and the long angry silence and the longer forgiveness—but something steadier. An anchor's expression.

"You could just say it," Odile said. "Whenever you're ready. No ceremony required."

"I'm flying a plane."

"You're always flying a plane. That's the point I've been making for ten years."

The far wall of the eye came up green-black and total, and Jude pushed the nose into it, and the world came apart around them in sheets.

* * *

Saltmere Strip appeared the way salvation usually does: all at once, sideways, and later than comfortable. A gravel runway wedged between a fishing town and the sea, windsock standing straight out horizontal, rain hitting the windshield so hard it sounded like applause from a very large, very hostile audience.

Jue landed it. Of course she landed it. Ten years of medicine runs and mail runs and runs nobody asked about had built hands that didn't ask permission of weather. The wheels touched, the plane slewed, corrected, forgave her, and rolled to a stop beside a hangar with a rusted roof and a woman standing under it in a yellow raincoat, arms crossed, radiating the specific impatience of journalists everywhere.

Priya Nair was shorter than her byline suggested and moved faster than her luggage. She had the crates unloaded before Odile finished chocking the wheels, photographing everything, asking questions in a voice pitched to carry over storms.

"Yarrow Company core samples, Valley Seven, chain of custody documented?" she said, pen flying.

"Documented," Halvorsen said, appearing at her elbow like a damp ghost. "Signed, dated, and drilled by people who thought no one would ever look."

"People are going to look now." Priya's grin was quick and sharp as a fish knife. Then she looked up—at Jude, at the plane, at the sky still chewing itself on the horizon—and her grin softened into something more careful. "You brought this through that."

"Somebody had to."

"Somebody *chose* to." Priya stuck out her hand. "Whatever your name is, I'm putting it in the story."

And that—that small sentence, offered kindly—was when Anton Reyes stepped out from behind the hangar.

* * *

He was dry. That was the first obscenity of it. In the middle of the first monsoon of the season, at the edge of the map, the man stood under an enormous black umbrella in a charcoal coat, not one drop on him, as if the weather had been paid off too. Two men flanked him at a respectful distance, wearing the studied blankness of people whose job descriptions were verbs like *escort* and *discourage*.

"Ms. Yarrow," Reyes said.

The name hit the wet gravel and rang.

Behind her, Jude heard Odile go still. Heard Priya's camera click once, soft as a heartbeat. Halvorsen made a sound that might have been a sigh or might have been exactly what he'd planned all along.

"Not today," Jude said.

"It's always today somewhere." Reyes smiled the way accountants smile—accurately, without warmth. "Here's my arithmetic. You have forty pounds of rock and some paper. I have a company. Not a *share* of a company. The company. Elias Yarrow's seat, the board, the name, the whole rotten cathedral—you inherit it clean if those tubes go into the sea. One phone call. The contamination becomes a competitor's smear campaign. Your friends' medical bills vanish. Kettle Bend gets its new runway. Everyone you love stops being in danger, effective immediately."

Rain drummed on the umbrella. Somewhere behind the hangar, the sea threw itself against the seawall like something trying to get in—or out.

"And if I don't?"

Reyes spread his hands, regretful, reasonable. "Then you're a woman who flew stolen corporate property across state lines during a natural disaster, and my lawyers will make your life a paperwork blizzard until you forget what sunlight looks like. And Ms. Yarrow—" he leaned in, voice dropping to something almost gentle, almost kind, the voice of every adult who'd ever explained the rules of the house she grew up in— "the name follows you either way. You can spend it or be spent by it. Those are the only two options anyone ever gets."

Jude looked at him for a long moment. Rain ran off her chin. Behind her she heard Odile shift her weight, ready to do something brave and unnecessary.

And Jude started to laugh.

It surprised even her—bubbled up out of ten years of grease and static and careful anonymity, out of a childhood of locked filing cabinets and a grandfather's portrait that watched every hallway like a creditor. She laughed until her ribs hurt, and Reyes's composure flickered for the first time, the umbrella tilting a degree.

"What," he said, "is funny."

"You are. This is. You think the name is the prize." She wiped her eyes. "I spent a decade running from it, and you know what I learned? It was never worth running *from*. It's a name. It's two syllables. It doesn't own the valley. It doesn't own the water. It doesn't own me—it never did. My grandfather signed his name to poison. So fine." She stepped forward, out from under any umbrella, into the full rude weight of the rain, and let it flatten her hair and soak her shirt and mark her, visibly, undeniably, as a person who had stopped hiding.

"My name is Jude Yarrow. Granddaughter of Elias Yarrow, who lied to everyone including himself. And I am signing *my* name to this instead." She jerked her thumb at the crates in Priya's arms. "Every tube. Every page. Every dead fish in Valley Seven and every sick kid downstream. That's my inheritance. That's the only part I'm claiming."

She turned to Priya. "Print the name. Spell it right."

"Oh, I will," Priya said, and her camera clicked like a lock turning.

Reyes stood in the rain a moment longer, umbrella immaculate, doing arithmetic that no longer balanced. "You understand what happens next," he said quietly.

"Yeah," Jude said. "The truth happens next. I hear it's got momentum."

He turned and walked toward the fuel office, and his men fell in around him, and the rain closed over the space where he'd been like water closing over a stone.

* * *

They reloaded nothing. There was nothing left to carry but themselves.

Odile found Jude at the plane afterward, checking tie-downs she didn't need to check, hands busy because hands were easier than hearts. The storm was moving off down the coast, grumbling, taking its tantrum elsewhere. The light coming under the cloud deck was the color of new tin—bright, rinsed, beginning again.

"So," Odile said. "That was a speech."

"It was a refusal."

"It was a speech-shaped refusal. With a punchline." Odile leaned against the strut, careful of her strapped arm, and grinned at her. "Ten years I've flown with you. Ten years of 'just call me Yarrow, it's common enough.' And now you're handing it to a journalist like a business card."

Jude tightened a strap that was already tight. "It felt different saying it. Out loud. On purpose."

"How'd it feel?"

She considered. The rain was stopping. Somewhere in town a kid was yelling about it, delighted, and gulls were reclaiming the air, and Halvorsen was inside explaining chain-of-custody law to a journalist who clearly knew more about it than he did, and the world was exactly as broken and exactly as salvageable as it had been yesterday—except that Jude Yarrow was standing in it with her name on, like a label, like a signature, like a claim staked.

"Like rainfall," she said. "Loud. And clean."

Odile bumped her shoulder gently against Jude's good one. "Come on, heiress. Let's get you home."

But as they walked toward the hangar, Priya Nair came out at a run, raincoat flapping, holding her satellite phone like it was ringing fire.

"You need to hear this," she said. "Kettle Bend's calling every number I've got. Reyes's men didn't stop at the strip—they went for the town. And your friend Tomas is missing from the clinic."

The gulls kept wheeling overhead, screaming their bright terrible news, and Jude was already turning back toward the plane.
