## 10. A Name Like Rainfall

The monsoon ended the way it always did at Kettle Bend—not all at once but in stages, like a guest who kept forgetting things and coming back for them. First the thunder moved off downriver to bother someone else. Then the rain thinned to a drizzle that couldn't decide. And one morning in late September, Jude stepped out of the hangar with a mug of coffee and realized the sky had gone the particular washed blue it only achieved after months of being wrung out.

She stood there a while, just looking at it. Ten years at Kettle Bend and she'd never once done that without a reason—never stood still in the open with nothing to check or fetch or fix. It felt strange. It felt, she decided, like something she could get used to.

Behind her, inside the hangar, Odile dropped a wrench and said a word that would have gotten her fined in three jurisdictions.

"Everything all right in there?" Jude called.

"The landing gear's fine," Odile said. "The *manual* is a liar."

Jude went back in. The Cessna sat on jacks with her cowling off, looking less like an aircraft than a patient mid-surgery, and Odile was elbow-deep in the nose gear assembly, hair tied back with what appeared to be a strip of cargo strap, grease on her forearms up past the elbow like she'd lost an argument with the plane personally. Which, in fairness, she had.

"You know," Jude said, leaning against the workbench, "most people thank their aircraft for saving their lives."

"Most people's aircraft don't shimmy on short final like a dog shaking off a bath." Odile extracted a bushing, held it to the light, and scowled at it as though it had said something rude about her mother. "I thanked her. I thanked her *extensively*, on the river, while we were both dripping. This is different. This is maintenance."

It was true that they'd both thanked the plane. Jude remembered the moment clearly: wheels kissing the water-soaked shingle, the airframe groaning, Odile's hands steady on the yoke despite the gash above her eye, and then silence—the good kind, the kind where everyone you loved was breathing. She'd patted the instrument panel and said *thank you* out loud, and meant it more sincerely than she'd ever meant anything she'd said in a boardroom she no longer admitted to having sat in.

That had been six weeks ago. The stitches above Odile's eyebrow had come out; the bruise on her ribs had faded from storm-purple to a sickly yellow she showed off like a medal. The plane was nearly whole. They were getting there too, most days.

* * *

The story broke on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays were the day nothing happened at Kettle Bend, so naturally the world chose one to detonate.

Priya Nair's series ran under a headline Jude never managed to read all the way through without her chest doing something complicated: *THE RIVERGATE PAPERS: DECADES OF CONTAMINATION, AND THE COMPANY THAT KNEW.* There were maps. There were internal memos with Elias Yarrow's initials in the margins. There were the core samples, logged and chain-of-custodied and photographed from eleven angles, pulled from sealed ground her grandfather had paid to make sure no one would ever look at again.

And there was her name. Not buried in paragraph nineteen, either. Priya had called her the night before publication and asked, gently, how she wanted to be identified.

"Just Jude Yarrow," she'd said. "No titles. No 'estranged heir.' Just—" and here she'd paused, because she'd been about to say *just a pilot*, and that wasn't quite it anymore either. "Just say I flew them out."

Priya had written exactly that. *Jude Yarrow, who flew the expedition's survivors and evidence out of the valley ahead of the monsoon, declined to comment on her family's estate but provided this reporter with forty years of company correspondence.*

Which was true, and better than anything Jude could have said aloud.

The investigations came next, the way weather comes: first the regional environmental authority, then the national one, then a parliamentary committee that issued a summons with enough official letterhead to wallpaper the hangar. Lawyers wrote to her. Journalists wrote to her. A documentary producer wrote to her twice, the second time offering, inexplicably, a "story consultant fee" that would have bought three new engines.

Odile read that one over her shoulder and snorted. "You'd be terrible on camera. You'd answer every question with a fuel weight."

"I would not."

"'How did it feel to discover your grandfather's crimes?' 'Approximately nine hundred kilos, depending on headwind.'"

"That's fair," Jude admitted.

What surprised her most was how little she minded any of it—the letters stacked on the kitchen table, the name in print, the way people at the airstrip café looked up when she walked in now. For ten years she had treated her name like a live wire, something to step around carefully. Now it was just a name. People said it the same way they said *pass the sugar*. Marta at the café had started giving her the corner table without being asked, which Jude suspected was less about fame and more about the fact that she tipped badly when distracted and Marta had learned to manage her.

Not everything healed evenly. That was the thing nobody warned you about—that recovery wasn't a flight plan with an ETA. Tomas got his cast off and was back to telling exaggerated stories within the week, but he stopped going near the river after dark, and nobody mentioned it. Halvorsen, whose lungs the doctors said would never fully forgive the valley, spent his convalescence writing his own account in longhand, refusing a laptop on principle, and sent Jude pages to fact-check with notes like *verify date* and *you were right, I was wrong, put that in*. She framed the last one. He pretended to be embarrassed. He was not.

And Dr. Amara Osei, who had died in the rapids carrying samples she'd refused to let go of—they named the research station after her, eventually, the one that would be built on the decontaminated banks if the cleanup actually happened, which was a word that required a great deal of faith these days. Jude had gone to the memorial. She'd stood at the back, as she always had, and then, halfway through, she hadn't. She'd moved forward and sat down among the expedition families, and no one had asked her why she was there, because everyone knew, and that was its own strange mercy.

* * *

On a bright afternoon in October, with the Cessna's landing gear finally signed off and the smell of fresh hydraulic fluid making the whole hangar feel optimistic, the mail plane brought a parcel.

Jude knew what it was before she opened it. She'd known since the lawyers finished sorting the estate—since the letter arrived informing her, in language designed by committee, that as sole surviving descendant she was entitled to the personal effects of Elias Yarrow, deceased. She'd let the box sit in Odile's spare room for a month. Then another two weeks. Today, apparently, was the day.

She carried it out to the hangar steps, where the light was good and Odile was pretending to inventory tie-downs within easy conversational range.

Inside: a pocket watch that didn't run. A photograph of a woman Jude didn't recognize, laughing, holding a fish. And letters. Dozens of them, bundled with string, in her grandfather's cramped engineer's hand—the same hand she'd seen reproduced in newsprint a hundred times now, always attached to memos about cost containment and liability buffers.

She'd read the public ones already. Priya had copies. Those letters were the reason a parliamentary committee existed now.

These were different. These were addressed to *my granddaughter*, unsent, undated in any coherent order, found in a drawer the lawyers had catalogued as "miscellaneous correspondence, personal."

Jude untied the string.

The first one was old—she could tell by the paper, by the way the ink had gone brown. *To the child I hope will forgive me,* it began, which was a hell of an opening line, and she almost put it back in the box right there. But Odile had drifted closer, and Odile was watching her with that expression—not pity, never pity, just attention, steady as a compass—and so Jude read it instead.

It was about the valley. Of course it was. But it wasn't a defense. It was a man in 1974 writing *I told myself the seepage numbers were wrong because the alternative was that I had built my life on top of a lie, and I have discovered today that both things are true, and I do not know how to be a person who holds both.* He wrote that he'd signed the sealing order. He wrote that he kept the original survey data anyway, hidden, because some part of him "refused to finish the burial." He wrote, near the end, *If anyone finds this, find the data first. Then find whoever is brave enough to carry it out. I suspect it will need to be someone with nothing left to lose, or everything.*

Jude laughed—a short, wet sound that surprised her.

"What?" Odile said.

"He thought it would take someone with nothing to lose." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, careful of the paper. "He hired me to fly medicine runs into the basin for a decade and never knew he'd picked someone with *everything* to lose. That's the joke. That's the whole joke."

"He wasn't wrong about the carrying part, though."

"No," Jude said softly. "He wasn't."

She read for an hour while the light went gold and long across the apron. Some letters were unbearable. Some were almost funny—he'd had a dry streak in him she'd never been allowed to see, jokes about board members and a running feud with a company chef over the proper way to cook rice. Some were just a lonely man talking to a granddaughter he'd driven away and didn't know how to call back, writing *I am told she flies now* in one, in a tone of such naked astonishment and pride that Jude had to set it down and look at the mountains for a while.

When she finished, she bundled the letters back together—but loosely, she noticed. She didn't retie the string tight.

"Well?" Odile asked.

"He was a coward," Jude said, "who spent thirty years trying to become something else and mostly failing, except in the ways that mattered." She considered this. "I can work with that."

"High praise, from you."

"I'm a generous spirit."

"You're a woman who once described a sunrise as 'adequate visibility.'"

"And I stand by it."

* * *

They closed the hangar at dusk, the way they always did, Odile killing the big lights while Jude checked the door latch twice out of habit neither of them questioned anymore. Outside, the evening had come in soft and clear, the first stars showing over the ridge, the river talking to itself somewhere below in the dark the way it had talked all summer, all decade, all the poisoned years before that, indifferent and enduring and, starting soon, with any justice at all, getting cleaner.

A truck rattled up the access road—Marta, closing late, headlights sweeping the strip—and she leaned out the window as she passed. "Yarrow! Pie's on the counter for tomorrow, don't let Odile eat it tonight!"

"It was ONE time," Odile protested, but the truck was already gone, taillights bobbing down toward the settlement.

Jude stood a moment longer, looking up at the sky. Somewhere out there, past the ridgeline, the valley was flooding and draining and beginning, season by season, to give up what it had been made to keep. Somewhere south, a committee was reading documents with her grandfather's initials in the margins. And here, in Kettle Bend, there was pie with her name on it—her actual name, spoken casually, by someone who liked her, attached to dessert.

Ten years ago she'd have called that nothing. She'd have said belonging was something you inherited or something you faked, and she'd had neither, so she'd settled for engine grease and radio static and a name kept folded small in her pocket.

But names weren't like that. She knew that now. Names were like rainfall—you didn't get to choose when yours fell, or how hard, or what it landed on. You only got to choose whether you stood out in it or ran for cover.

Jude Yarrow turned off the hangar lights herself, took her partner's arm, and walked home through the clear night, unhurried, staying out in the open the whole way.
