## 1. The Weight of Fair Weather

The trouble with hiding in a port town is that ports are full of people whose job is finding other people.

Salagdan had taught me this the way it teaches everything: slowly, with salt air and rust. Nine months I'd been here, running a repair shop out of a corrugated shed two streets back from the fish market, patching radar units for trawler captains who paid in cash, gossip, and occasionally fish I didn't ask about. It was honest work. It was boring work. Boring was the product. I sold boredom at competitive rates.

The radar unit on my bench belonged to the *Mariana's Luck*, and its problem was a cracked waveguide and a captain who believed weather was a personal insult. I had the waveguide half-soldered when the bell over the door rang, and I knew before I looked up that it wasn't a customer, because customers knock first when the door sticks. This man let the bell announce him like a herald.

He was dressed wrong for Salagdan—linen trousers without a single salt stain, shoes that had never met a fish scale, a smile calibrated to the millimeter. He set a leather folio on my bench, directly on top of the *Luck*'s schematics, which told me everything about how much he respected other people's work.

"Hazel Reyes." He said it like he was confirming a reservation. "Mr. Okafor sends his regards."

"Nobody by that name works here," I said, and picked the folio up off my schematics and set it on the counter between us. "You want a radio fixed, I can do that. Anything else, the tide's going out."

"Of course." The smile didn't move. That was the professional part—the smile was load-bearing. "Mr. Okafor is a patient man. He understands that men fall on hard times. He also understands that hard times end, and when they do, accounts come due. With interest." He tapped the folio once. "There's no pressure today. Consider this a courtesy call. A reminder that the world has a long memory, even if you've tried to give yours amnesia."

"You memorized that?"

"I write my own material." He glanced around the shop—at Dev's tool wall, immaculate; at June's chart table, currently holding nothing but a mug of cold tea; at the corner where Teo had been sanding a hull patch until I'd sent him to buy lunch twenty minutes ago, because I'd seen this man's car parked outside the market an hour earlier and wanted the kid clear of the room. "Nice operation. Small. Quiet. The kind of thing that would be a shame to see... restructured."

"There it is," I said. "I was wondering when we'd get to the euphemism."

"We're getting ahead of ourselves." He straightened his cuffs. "Mr. Okafor wants you comfortable, Mr. Reyes. Comfortable men pay. Desperate men run, and running is expensive—for everyone." He pushed the folio an inch toward me. Inside, I knew, would be a number. The number hadn't changed in nine months: what I'd borrowed to keep my crew fed after the last contract collapsed, plus interest compounding like a rumor. "A courtesy copy. For your records."

"I don't keep records."

"Everyone keeps records." He smiled wider, showing me the whole calibrated thing. "Some just don't know they're keeping them yet."

Then he left, and the bell rang behind him, and I stood there breathing through my nose until my hands stopped wanting to throw something.

* * *

Dev found me ten minutes later still staring at the waveguide like it owed me money too.

"So," he said. He was a big man built like something assembled from spare engine parts, and his conversational range ran from grunts to full diagnostic lectures with almost nothing between. "That guy."

"That guy."

"The suit?"

"The suit."

"He leave the paper?"

"Yeah."

"You gonna read it?"

"Nope." I picked up the soldering iron. "Numbers don't change. Reading them just ruins the soldering."

Dev considered this with the gravity he gave all things, then nodded once and went back to the outboard motor he'd been resurrecting since March. That was the arrangement: nobody said the word *debt* out loud, and in exchange nobody asked why a crew that used to chase supercells across three continents now spent its days fixing fishing electronics in a town so small the tide was front-page news.

June came in at four, as she always did, carrying the day's gossip from the harbor office like a courier with diplomatic pouches. She was fifty-one and had navigated ships through weather that made grown meteorologists lie down, and she could read a room the way she read a barometer—which is to say, accurately and without mercy.

"Teo's buying lunch with money he doesn't have," she reported, setting her mug on the chart table. "He's trying to impress the girl at the noodle stall by ordering for three. Also, your visitor's car left twenty minutes ago, headed south. Also—" she paused, and I felt the pause coming like a front, "—there was a charter flight into the strip this morning. Private. Nobody on the manifest anyone recognized."

"Tourists."

"In hiking boots and expedition-grade shells? In September?" She sipped her tea. "One of them asked the harbormaster about road conditions north. Toward the passes."

I kept my eyes on the waveguide. "Lots of hikers this time of year."

"Not lots of hikers who ask about Kirat Ridge."

The soldering iron hissed against the joint. I'd been holding it a half-second too long, and the solder bloomed into a gray flower that would have to be redone. I set the iron down carefully, because throwing it would be admitting something.

"Kirat Ridge is closed this time of year," I said. "Monsoon locks the passes by mid-October. Anybody asking about it in September is either stupid or—"

"Or planning around the window," June finished. She watched me with those flat gray eyes that had talked three crews out of killing themselves over nineteen years. "Hazel."

"It's fine."

"You're doing the voice."

"What voice?"

"The one where you say 'it's fine' and mean 'I'm counting exits.'" She rinsed her mug in the rain barrel we kept by the door, unhurried. "Whatever the paper says, whatever the suit said—we're not running again. Last time we ran, we ended up *here*. I like it here. The fish are good. But I'm telling you now so you don't decide alone: next time we run, I want it discussed. Like a family."

"We're not a family," I said. "We're a limited liability arrangement."

"Mm." She hung the mug on its nail. "Tell that to Teo, who spent his own lunch money on your favorite noodles because you skipped breakfast again."

She went back out to check the mooring lines on the skiff, and I sat there with the ruined solder joint and the noodles cooling in their bag, and I thought about how the worst part of hiding isn't the fear. Fear you get used to, the way you get used to a bad knee. The worst part is the vigilance—the constant low-grade arithmetic of it. Every stranger's accent catalogued. Every new face at the harbor cross-referenced against nine years of people who might want things from me. Every phone call taken outside, every route home varied, every conversation with Teo conducted in the careful dialect of a man pretending his life started eighteen months ago in a rented shed.

It was exhausting in a way that didn't show. Like holding your breath for nine months.

Teo came back at five with grease on his sleeves and a story about the noodle-stall girl that lasted eleven minutes and contained no actual information. Twenty-two years old, all elbows and enthusiasm, the kind of kid who'd shown up eight months ago asking if the famous Hazel Reyes storm crew took apprentices, and who I should have turned away and didn't, because he reminded me of someone, and because a man pretending his past is finished apparently still can't resist being needed by it.

"Boss says you skipped breakfast," Teo said, shoving the noodle bag at me.

"Boss talks too much."

"She says you talk too little, and one of you has to compensate."

I ate the noodles. They were good. Somewhere south, a car with a polite man in it was reporting back to someone named Okafor that the asset was located, contained, and sufficiently uncomfortable. Somewhere north, strangers in expedition shells were asking about a ridge I had sworn off with the kind of vow people only make at funerals.

And I sat in my shed, fixing other people's weather instruments, waiting for fair weather that I knew—professionally, expertly, better than any man alive—never actually comes. Fair weather is just a storm that hasn't filed its paperwork yet.

* * *

It came at closing time, three days later, wearing a smile I remembered too well.

I heard the heels first—instantly recognizable, because Ingrid Voss was the only person in the hemisphere who crossed a fish-market street like a boardroom floor. Then the bell, then the shadow filling the doorway, and there she was: silver-streaked hair cut short as a verdict, expedition jacket worth more than my truck, and that smile—the one that had beaten me to three contracts and one very public humiliation, the one that meant she'd already done the math and liked her numbers.

"Reyes." She looked around the shed with frank appraisal, taking in the tool wall, the chart table, the sleeping loft where I sometimes heard Teo practicing knot drills at midnight. "Charming. You've really committed to the small life."

"Voss." I didn't stand. "Shop's closed."

"For repairs, maybe." She walked in anyway, because doors were suggestions to her, and set a document case on my bench—on top of the *Luck*'s schematics again, and I wondered briefly if they trained that. "I need a crew. Yours, specifically. Logistics escort, high-altitude, six weeks, triple your going rate—and before you quote me your going rate, I know what it is, because I invented half of it."

"We don't do fieldwork anymore."

"I know." Her smile widened by a degree. "That's why I came to you and not some hungry young outfit. Hungry outfits ask questions. You—" she tapped the document case, once, precisely, "—you need this job so badly you'll take it first and hate yourself later. It's efficient. I respect efficiency."

"Where?"

She let the pause stretch, watching me, and I understood that she already knew the answer would land like a hook under the ribs, and she'd chosen it deliberately, the way she chose everything.

"North," she said. "Through the passes. Before the monsoon locks them." Another tap on the case. "Kirat Ridge, Reyes. I need you to take me home to the place that ended you."

Outside, the harbor lights came on one by one, and somewhere past them the monsoon was already gathering over water, patient as debt, and Ingrid Voss stood in my workshop smiling like a woman holding a contract I couldn't afford to refuse—which was true, and worse, she knew exactly why.
