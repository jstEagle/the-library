## 2. Terms Written in Rain

The rain came in off the harbor the way it always did in Salagdan—sideways, personal, like it had a grudge against specifically me. I watched it hammer the tin awning over my workshop and thought about how nine years ago I'd have been able to tell you, to the minute, when it would stop.

Now I just waited for it to stop. That was the whole of my meteorological career these days: waiting.

Ingrid Voss sat on the only clean stool in the shop with her boots crossed at the ankle and a leather folio balanced on her knee, looking around at my kingdom of half-dismantled radar units and cable spools like she was appraising a property she intended to buy low. She hadn't changed much. Same close-cropped silver hair, same jaw that looked carved by someone in a hurry, same way of occupying a room as if the room had applied for the privilege.

"You've done well for yourself," she said.

"I'm aware of how it looks."

"It looks like a man hiding." She smiled to take the edge off, which was Ingrid's specialty—she could put a razor in a gift box and you'd thank her for the wrapping. "I said *well*, not *happily*."

Dev was pretending to work on the compressor in the back corner, which meant he was listening with his entire body. June had not even pretended. She stood by the door with her arms folded, watching Ingrid the way you watch weather coming over a ridge line—noting its shape, estimating what it would cost you.

Teo was out getting noodles, which was a mercy. The kid would have agreed to whatever Ingrid said before she finished saying it.

"Let's skip the part where you flatter me," I said. "You flew eleven hours to sit in a leaky shed. Say the thing."

Ingrid opened the folio. Inside was a contract, printed on actual paper, which told me she wanted this to feel official, ceremonial, binding in ways beyond ink. People who intend to screw you use paper. People who respect you shake hands.

"Kirat Ridge," she said. "Western approach, the old survey route. I need a team up and down inside three weeks, ahead of monsoon onset. Cargo is one climatologist and his instruments. Dr. Ansel Brandt. You may have heard the name."

I had. Everyone who'd ever cared about atmospheric science had. Ansel Brandt had published papers that got cited like scripture, then gone quiet for years, then resurfaced doing fieldwork nobody could get funding for because nobody could explain what he was measuring. He was either the last honest man in climatology or the best liar in it, and the two categories overlapped more than people liked to admit.

"Not interested," I said.

"You haven't heard the number."

"I don't need to hear the number. There is no number."

"Triple your going rate," she said, "plus a completion bonus that clears every account you owe money on, with enough left over to retool this shop into something you'd actually want to wake up for."

The rain filled the silence for me, which was considerate of it. I made myself look at the contract instead of her face. Triple rate. My going rate was a fiction anyway—I hadn't taken a field contract in four years, so my going rate was whatever number I invented to make clients go away. Triple a fiction was still a fiction, but a bigger one, and debts are not fictional. Debts have names attached. Debts send polite men to your workshop who compliment your soldering iron while telling you they know where you buy your groceries.

"The Kirat Ridge," I said carefully, "is a bad idea in any season, and a suicidal one in pre-monsoon. The approaches destabilize first. You know that."

"I know that most guides won't touch it. Which is why I need a guide who will."

"And there isn't one."

"There's one," Ingrid said quietly. "He's sitting in front of me pretending there isn't."

I set the contract down on the bench, face up, and looked at it the way you look at a diagnosis. The ridge name was right there in the header. *Kirat Ridge, western survey corridor.* Nine years ago that corridor ate a base camp, six climbers, and my brother Daniel, and it did it while I stood on a shoulder two kilometers away holding a forecast I'd signed my name to—a forecast that said the window was clean. It wasn't clean. I'd read the pressure data wrong, or wanted to read it wrong, or someone upstream had fed me numbers that were wrong, and the inquiry never quite settled which, and neither did I. That was the poison of it. Not knowing whether I'd failed or been failed. I built my whole exile on top of that question like a house on an unexploded shell.

"I don't guide anymore," I said.

"You repair radar units in a town where the biggest weather event of the year is a fishing boat losing its awning." Ingrid leaned forward. "Hazel. I watched you work for six years. You were the best field reader I ever saw, and I've seen them all. This job needs exactly that, and it needs someone who knows that ridge's moods better than anyone alive, because—" she paused, and I watched her decide whether to say the next thing honestly, and watched her decide yes, because honesty was also a tool she kept sharp— "because the man I'm taking up there is chasing something on that ridge, and I need someone beside him who understands what the mountain does to people who chase things."

"That's poetic," I said. "Poetry costs extra."

"Everything costs extra. That's why I brought a number."

Dev dropped a wrench in the back, loudly, on purpose. June hadn't moved. She was still watching me, and I could feel her reading the whole negotiation off my posture—the way I kept my eyes on the contract, the way my thumb had found the scar on my palm where the ice took a piece of me that year, the way I hadn't actually said no a second time.

Because here's the ugly arithmetic I was doing behind my face: triple rate plus bonus equals debt cleared plus syndicate gone plus a life where nobody could walk into my shop and be polite at me again. That was the story I was telling myself. Money. Purely transactional. A man takes a job because a man owes money; it's practically a genre.

And underneath it, quieter, in the voice I kept locked in the back room: if I say no, then the ridge wins. Then it stays the place that ended me, and I stay the man it ended, forever, in a leaky shed, waiting for rain. Saying no would mean admitting that after nine years I still couldn't stand on that ground without coming apart—and I would rather have walked back into the storm than admit that to Ingrid Voss, of all people, who had testified at the inquiry, who had defended me until defending me cost her something too.

So I negotiated. Brutally, because that was the only way I knew how to do anything that scared me.

"Hazard pay doubles after day ten," I said. "If we're pinned past onset, every day over is billed at quadruple."

"Done."

"Equipment replacement at full value, no depreciation arguments. If I lose a radar unit to that ridge, I'm not eating the cost."

"Done."

"My crew gets veto power. Any member can call abort and we turn around, no penalty clause, no argument. Including the rookie."

Ingrid raised an eyebrow. "That's generous of you."

"It's not generosity. It's insurance against me making a stupid decision high on a mountain because my pride got involved." I almost laughed at myself saying it. Too late for that particular insurance policy. "And one more thing. Nobody touches the scientist's instruments except him. We carry sealed cases, we don't ask what's in them. Whatever Brandt is measuring, it's his problem, not mine. I move cargo. That's all this is."

"That's all this is," Ingrid repeated, and something flickered across her face—too fast to name, gone before I could price it. "Agreed."

She slid the pen across the bench. I picked it up. My hand was steady, which felt like a betrayal; hands should shake for this kind of thing. I signed. The scratch of the nib sounded very loud under the rain.

"Departure in five days," Ingrid said, standing, folding the folio. "Trailhead at Dzomphu. Ansel meets us there. Pack for everything—the models say we have a window, but the models have been wrong about this season all spring."

"How wrong?"

"Early," she said. "Everything says early." And she smiled at me one more time, that wrapped-razor smile, and let herself out into the rain.

* * *

For about forty seconds after the door closed, nobody spoke. Dev went back to the compressor and made it clank meaningfully. I stared at the contract lying on my bench like a dead bird.

June finally unfolded her arms and came over. She didn't pick up the contract. She picked up the kettle instead and put it on the burner, which was June's version of a debriefing—you talked while water heated, or you didn't talk, but you stayed in the room.

"Triple rate," she said.

"Plus bonus."

"Mm." She got down two mugs, then, after a glance at Dev, a third. "You know what I noticed? You never asked what Brandt's actually studying up there."

"He's a climatologist. They study climate."

"On the Kirat Ridge. Western corridor." She poured nothing into nothing, warming the mugs, giving her hands something to do. "Nine years ago this month, Hazel."

"I can count."

"Can you?" She turned and looked at me straight on, and June's straight-on look has ended tougher men than me. "Because you just signed a contract to walk back onto the exact ground where Danny died, and you did it without blinking, and now you're standing there telling yourself it's about the money. And I'm supposed to pretend I believe that, because we're a family and families let each other have their stories."

The kettle started its low climb toward a whistle. I watched it instead of her.

"So say something," she said.

"No," I said. "You already said it."

She held my eyes another moment—longer than comfortable, shorter than cruel—then nodded once and handed me a mug of hot water with nothing in it, because we were out of tea and neither of us mentioned it. That was worse than the speech. The speech I could survive. It was the mug of plain hot water, the quiet *I see you and I'm staying anyway*, that sat in my chest like swallowed stone.

Behind us, Dev cleared his throat. "So, uh. Do I start packing the good ropes? Or the ropes we don't mind losing?"

"Both," I said. "Pack both."

Outside, the rain kept falling, and somewhere east of us, past the passes, the Kirat Ridge stood in cloud doing whatever it had been doing for nine years. Waiting, probably. Mountains are patient. It had only ever needed me to come back once.
