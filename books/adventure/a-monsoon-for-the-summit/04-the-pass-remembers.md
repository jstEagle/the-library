## 4. The Pass Remembers

Three days up, the mountain stopped pretending to be scenery.

The first day had been kind—pine smoke, terraced barley, Teo asking questions like a man who thought altitude was a personality trait. The second day the trees gave out and the trail turned to stone and Ansel Brandt started looking at the ridge line the way a man looks at a woman he owes money to. By the third morning we were above everything green, walking through cloud that came apart and reassembled around us like it was deciding whether we were worth the trouble.

I navigated. That's what I did. June handled the route plan on paper, but on the ground it was mine—the pacing, the bearing calls, the little corrections nobody noticed because they happened before anyone needed them. Nine years of not thinking about this mountain, and my feet still knew its grammar. Left of the moraine where the guidebooks said right, because the right side had a hidden crevasse field that had eaten two porters in '09 and never made the maps. I called the correction without breaking stride.

"You've been here before," Dev said. Not a question. He was walking beside me with his hands in his pockets, which for Dev meant he'd been watching me for an hour.

"Everyone's been here before," I said. "It's a famous ridge."

"Famous for what?"

"For being tall," I said, "and expensive to rescue people from."

He let it go, which was its own kind of conversation. Dev had worked for me four years and had learned that I answered questions in layers, like an onion with a lawyer. He also knew that when I started giving accurate, useless answers, the true one was underneath somewhere, bleeding.

Ansel was ahead with Ingrid, moving well for a man whose hands shook every time the ridge came out of cloud. I'd clocked that on day one—the tremor, the way he flexed his fingers like he was warming them over a fire only he could feel. I'd assumed fear. Fear was fine. Fear was professional. Half the people who climb mountains are running from something and the other half are running toward it and the mountain doesn't care which.

But there was a specific quality to how Ansel looked at Kirat Ridge. Not the way you look at something that might kill you. The way you look at something you've already made arrangements with.

* * *

We crossed the old base-camp site at noon.

You wouldn't have known it was anything. That's the thing about high places—they don't keep graves, they keep geometry. A flat shelf of gravel between two lateral moraines, a stream running milky with glacier flour, a few flat stones that might once have been someone's kitchen. The 2016 expedition had put forty tents on this shelf. Weather stations, a comms tent, a dining fly with a propane heater and a banner. I knew because I'd designed the layout. I'd stood roughly where I was standing now, nine years younger and nine years more certain, and told forty people the window was good.

The window was not good.

The wind came down off the summit headwall at two in the afternoon on October 11th and by midnight eleven people were dead, including my brother Marcus, including the expedition's meteorologist, who had been me. They'd called it an unprecedented event. A freak convergence. An act of God with a press conference. The inquiry used words like "unforecastable" and "beyond reasonable prediction," and I let them, because the alternative was admitting that I had seen the pressure trace three hours early and talked myself out of my own instruments because the sponsors were paying by the summit day and Marcus was so close and I wanted the ridge to be kind.

The ridge is not kind. The ridge is arithmetic.

"Water stop," I said, and my voice came out level, which cost me something I didn't itemize. "Ten minutes. Fill everything."

Teo dropped his pack gratefully and went for the stream. June sat on a stone and ate without tasting, her eyes doing their quiet inventory of the sky. Dev wandered, which was what Dev did—he couldn't walk past junk without reading it, the way some men can't walk past a newspaper headline. The shelf was littered with nine years of other expeditions' leavings: shredded polypro gone gray, a stove leg, the ribbed aluminum skeleton of something that had been a shelter before the wind got opinions about it.

I kept my back to all of it and checked the barometer. Falling faster than the forecast said. I noted it, logged it, told no one yet, because telling people things makes the things real and I wanted ten more minutes of the world where my brother's campsite was just gravel.

Then I heard Dev go quiet.

Dev going quiet is louder than most men shouting. I turned. He was crouched by the stream bank, half in the water, holding something small up to the light. Rust-orange. Oval. I knew the shape before I knew the object, the way you know a voice through a wall.

A carabiner. Old style, oval gate, the finish bubbled with corrosion. Stamped into the spine, shallow but legible: **M.R.**

Marcus Reyes. My brother had his gear custom-stamped because he said if the mountain kept him, he wanted it to know his name while it did.

"Hey, Hazel." Dev's voice was very careful. "You want to see something?"

"No," I said.

He looked at me across twenty meters of gravel, holding my brother's name in his fist, and I watched him decide—watched him weigh the truth against whatever he could see happening behind my face—and then he closed his hand around it and stood up and said, "Never mind. Trash. Let's move when you're ready."

He pocketed it. I watched him do it. We both pretended I hadn't.

That's the thing about found family: sometimes love looks exactly like lying to a man's face while he watches you do it, and both of you agree to call it mercy.

* * *

We made the next camp by four, a rock bivouac below the icefall with walls Dev built in ninety minutes out of spite and experience. Ansel spent the evening hunched over his sealed cases, checking seals, checking again. Ingrid cooked. She was better at it than she wanted anyone to know—a woman who'd summited eight-thousanders boiling pasta like she was feeding a family, and maybe that was the tell, maybe everything about Ingrid Voss was a tell if you'd known her as long as I had.

June took first watch. I took second, which meant I lay in my bag staring at the roof of black nylon from eleven to two, listening to the wind test our walls like a landlord, and then traded places with June and sat with the radio and the stars.

She came out at three anyway. She always did. June didn't trust night any more than she trusted people, and she trusted me more than either, which was why she sat down next to me without a word and poured tea from the thermos and let the silence be a shared language instead of an accusation.

"She chose you on purpose," June said finally.

"I know."

"Do you?" She wrapped both hands around her cup. "Because I ran the numbers, Hazel. Triple pay for a logistics run. Nobody pays triple for logistics. And she didn't interview us. She didn't check references. She asked for you by name before I even sent the crew list."

"Ingrid and I have history."

"Everybody has history with everybody. This isn't history. This is architecture." June looked at me, and her face in the starlight was patient and terrible, the face of a navigator who has already computed the answer and is waiting for you to arrive at it honestly. "She built this expedition around your grief. I just haven't figured out what for."

"Maybe she needs a guide who knows the route."

"The route's on maps, Hazel. Bad ones, but maps." She drank her tea. "Whatever she wants, it's not the route."

I didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that wasn't also a confession, and I wasn't drunk enough on altitude to make one. But June stayed anyway, shoulder almost touching mine, keeping watch over a man who was supposed to be keeping watch, and neither of us said the true thing, which was that I'd signed that contract knowing all of this and would sign it again, and that the debt I was really paying had never had a number on it.

Ingrid came out at four.

She moved quietly for a big woman—she'd always had that mountaineer's economy, no gesture wasted—and she settled onto the rock on my other side with a mug June poured her without being asked, and for a while the three of us watched the icefall glow faintly under the stars like a frozen waterfall deciding whether to fall.

"You knew the base-camp site," Ingrid said. "Yesterday. You corrected the route before Dev saw the moraine. You walked through Marcus Reyes's camp like a man visiting a house he sold."

"Good memory," I said.

"Hazel." She said my name the way she used to say it across competition briefing rooms, back when we were the two best forecasters of our generation and hated each other professionally and loved each other in the way rivals do, which is to say completely and at arm's length. "I picked you because of the disaster. Not despite it."

The wind moved. Somewhere below, the icefall ticked and groaned, settling its accounts.

"Eleven people died on that ridge," she went on, "and the world got a story about weather. Unprecedented. Act of God. And everyone who could have contradicted that story was dead or disgraced or—" she glanced at me, "—hiding in a port town patching radar units for fishermen. I need someone who knows what the ridge took. Not the route. The *cost*. Because what Dr. Brandt is carrying up this mountain isn't instruments, Hazel. It's a question. And the answer is going to make some very powerful people very unhappy, and when it does, I need the one man alive who already knows what it feels like to be blamed for the sky."

I looked at her. Nine years, and she still had the same trick of making truth sound like strategy.

"And if I'd said no?" I asked.

"You wouldn't." She finished her tea. "You'd have followed us up here on your own money just to stand on that shelf again. I just saved you the airfare."

She went back inside. June looked at me. I looked at the ridge, black against the slightly-less-black sky, and felt the old pressure trace unspooling behind my eyes—three hours of warning I'd talked myself out of, eleven names I carried like ballast.

Below us, faint but unmistakable, the barometer in my pocket ticked downward again. Faster than it should have. Faster than any forecast.

The monsoon was coming early. And somebody on this mountain had known it before I did.
