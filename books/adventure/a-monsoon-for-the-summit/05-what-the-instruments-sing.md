## 5. What the Instruments Sing

I want it on the record that I was not snooping.

Snooping implies intent. What I did was closer to tripping over a truth with my hands full of cable, and by the time I understood what I'd found, there was no polite way to put it back. That's my story. I've been telling it to myself for three days now, and it's starting to hold its shape, like a tent pitched in bad ground.

Here's how it actually went.

High camp sat at five thousand four hundred meters on a shelf of scree the ridge had shrugged off sometime in the last ice age, close enough to the summit wall that when the wind came off it, you could hear the mountain breathing—a long inhale through the couloirs, a rattle of spindrift on the exhale. Dev had pronounced the site "structurally insulting" and then built us a kitchen out of cargo sleds and spite that would've survived artillery. June had plotted our escape routes in her head the way other people say grace. And Ansel Brandt had asked me—me, Teo Okafor, twenty-three years old, one season of paid mountaineering experience, hired mostly because I could carry two loads and laugh at Dev's jokes—to help him deploy the sensors.

"Your hands are younger than mine," he'd said, and something in my chest had gone off like a flare.

You have to understand what this expedition meant to me before you judge what I did after. Six months ago I was rigging zip lines for tourists in Manali, calling myself an alpinist because I owned a down jacket. Then Hazel Reyes looked at me across a table of instant noodles, asked if I could tie a bowline blindfolded, and said, "Good enough. Don't die. It's paperwork." That's all it took. I would have followed that man into a wood chipper if he promised it counted as field experience.

So when Dr. Ansel Brandt—the man whose papers I'd actually read, whose name was in the footnotes of the footnotes of things I'd studied at two in the morning—handed me a sensor array and said *your hands*, I became, briefly, the best version of myself. Careful. Precise. Worthy.

The arrays were beautiful. I'll say that for them. Eight units, each no bigger than a shoebox, machined from some gray metal that drank the light, with antenna stubs like insect legs. The cases they traveled in were foam-lined and stenciled with a shipping company I later realized didn't exist. Ansel handled them the way Hazel handled his brother's memory: carefully, constantly, without ever looking at them directly.

"Barometric lattice," he told me, when I asked what they measured. "Pressure differentials across the ridge face. Standard mesoscale work."

"Standard," I repeated, because he'd said it like a man rehearsing.

He smiled at me. He had a good smile, warm at the edges, and I noticed—for the first time, but not the last—that it arrived about half a second after the rest of his face decided to do it.

* * *

We placed the first four sensors along the eastern spine while the sun climbed and the cold lost its teeth. Ansel worked fast, better than fast—he worked like someone who'd done this exact thing many times, somewhere he couldn't talk about. Each unit went into a drilled socket, got leveled, got calibrated. He talked while we worked, and I hung on every word, because that's what I did those days. Hung on things.

"You know what nobody tells you about weather?" he said, tightening a bolt with fingers that were, I noticed, perfectly steady up here at altitude, though they shook whenever the summit wall came into view below camp. "It's not chaos. Everyone loves to call it chaos. Butterfly wings, et cetera. But large-scale systems—" he tapped the sensor housing "—large-scale systems keep appointments."

"Like monsoons," I said, showing off.

"Like monsoons." He glanced at me, and for a moment the warmth in his face was real, unguarded, almost grateful. "The forecast models treat the Kirat massif as passive terrain. A wall the weather breaks against. But terrain isn't passive, Teo. Terrain *participates*. This ridge has a shape, and shapes have resonances."

I nodded along, understanding maybe sixty percent, which felt like a hundred.

That was the morning. The afternoon is where I tripped.

* * *

The last four sensors needed to go higher, on the shoulder below the icefall, and Ansel wanted their telemetry verified against the base unit before we descended. The base unit lived in his tent, which was technically against expedition protocol—Hazel's protocol, which was *nobody's gear in anybody's tent but their own*—but Ansel was the client, and clients outrank protocol, or so I believed right up until the moment believing it cost me.

He sent me into the tent to fetch the verification tablet. Just that. "Blue case, left side." And the blue case was there, but under it, half-shoved beneath his sleeping bag like a teenager hiding a magazine, was a second tablet, screen still glowing, still running whatever he'd been looking at when we'd come off the shoulder.

I want to be honest about the next part, because everything afterward depends on it: I looked. Not for long. Maybe ten seconds. But I looked, and I read, and I understood more than I wanted to.

The screen showed a pressure series. Days of it, stacked in overlapping bands, and running through the bands like a thread through cloth was a repeating signature—a spike-and-decay pattern, identical each time, cycling on a period the annotation called *T ≈ 41 h*. Next to it, in Ansel's cramped handwriting on a digital note: **Predictable. Reproducible. Cell genesis is topographically forced — trigger window ±90 min.**

Below that, a second note, newer, the letters pressed hard enough to blur: **If this holds, it doesn't just forecast the storm. It schedules it.**

I stood there in the dim green light of a tent at five thousand meters holding a blue case, and the mountain breathed outside, and I thought: *schedules it.*

I'm not stupid. I grew up on flood stories; my grandmother's whole village has a church bell dedicated to a cyclone. I knew what a scheduled storm was worth. Not to science—science publishes, science shares. I knew what it was worth to people who don't publish. Insurance syndicates. Governments. Anyone who could look at a valley and see not weather but a delivery window. Ansel's own note said it plainer than I could: whoever held that signature didn't need to make a weapon. They just needed to know, ninety minutes early, exactly where the sky was going to fall—and sell tickets to everyone standing under it.

The screen dimmed. I set the second tablet back exactly as I'd found it, angle and all, and took the blue case out into the light, and handed it to Ansel, and said nothing.

He took the case. He looked at me—one beat too long, the way Hazel looks at weather that's about to turn—and said, "Thank you, Teo."

And here is the thing I can't stop chewing on: I said, "Anytime."

Not *what's on the other tablet.* Not *schedules it?* Just *anytime,* bright and eager, a dog waiting to be told he's good. Because for ten seconds I'd been trusted adjacent to something enormous, and instead of fear I'd felt—God help me—I'd felt *included.* Like the expedition had quietly grown a room only certain people could enter, and I'd been handed the key without asking, and the key was warm in my pocket.

That feeling lasted until dinner.

* * *

Dinner was Dev's dal, which is to say dal with opinions, and the four of us plus Ansel crammed into the kitchen sled while the wind read us its terms and conditions. Hazel sat with his back to the wall, always, eating in slow unhurried bites, and I watched him watch Ansel, and I watched Ansel not watch him back, and the whole scene suddenly looked different to me, lit from inside by what I'd seen on that screen. How much did Hazel know? Had Ingrid told him? Was the entire crew a prop in a play whose script I'd glimpsed one page of?

June caught me staring. She catches everyone staring; it's her superpower. She raised one eyebrow maybe two millimeters, and I shook my head, and she let it go, and somehow that was worse than being asked.

After dinner Hazel pulled me aside to check the anchor lines above camp—we'd had wind-shift warnings all evening—and while we worked, our headlamps making twin pools of white in the dark, he said, casually, in the tone he used for casual things that weren't:

"Ansel treating you all right?"

"Yeah. Great. He's—yeah. Teaching me a lot."

"Mm." Hazel tested an anchor with two sharp tugs, listening to the snowpack answer. "Teo. If anyone ever asks you to carry something you wouldn't carry in front of the whole crew—you tell me. That's not a rule. That's the whole job."

My mouth said, "Sure, boss."

My stomach said something else entirely, because I was already carrying it. I'd been carrying it since the tent. And the terrible arithmetic of it was this: telling Hazel meant admitting I'd looked. Looking meant I'd already broken the small trust to protect the big secret, and the big secret wasn't mine to give, and Ansel had thanked me, and I'd said *anytime,* and somewhere between the blue case and the anchor line I had stopped being crew-who-might-be-told-something and become crew-who-was-keeping-it.

Complicity, it turns out, doesn't announce itself. It arrives as a compliment.

* * *

I couldn't sleep, which at altitude is normal, except tonight my heart was going like I'd summited twice. Around midnight I crawled out to take a leak and found Ansel already outside, standing at the edge of camp, facing the summit wall. No headlamp. The moon was up and the icefall glowed like something internal, organ-like, and above it the plume off the summit streamed east, long and silver, combed flat by winds we couldn't feel down here.

Ansel didn't startle when I came up beside him. Maybe he'd heard me. Maybe he'd been waiting.

"Beautiful, isn't it," he said.

"Yes sir."

"It's going to come early." He said it quietly, conversationally, the way you'd mention rain might delay a picnic. "The models say four days. The ridge says sooner. Forty-one hours, Teo. Give or take." He turned and looked at me, and in the moonlight his eyes were very clear and very tired. "You saw the screen."

The world got very loud and very quiet at the same time.

"I wasn't—" I started.

"I know you weren't. I left it out." He exhaled, breath smoking in the cold. "I'm tired of carrying it alone, and you're the only person on this mountain who reads my field. So. Now you know what sings in those boxes." He held out his hand—not demanding, offering, which was somehow more frightening. "Can I trust you?"

And there it was. The warm key, held out in the open. Everything in me that had been starving for six months—since Manali, since the noodles, since *good enough, don't die*—leaned toward that hand like a plant toward a window.

I shook it.

"Nobody else knows?" he asked.

"Just me."

"Keep it that way. For now. Please." He squeezed once and let go, and walked back toward his tent, and I stayed out in the cold watching the plume stream east, counting hours I didn't fully understand yet—forty-one, minus the ones already spent.

Behind me, a boot scraped rock.

Hazel stood by the kitchen sled in his sleeping bag like a disgruntled heron, headlamp off, and I couldn't tell in the dark how long he'd been there, or how much the moon had shown him: Ansel's hand, my hand, the handshake, the space between us where a secret had changed owners.

"Cold night for philosophy," he said.

"Yeah," I said. "Couldn't sleep."

"Mm." He scratched his jaw. Above us, right on schedule, the wind shifted, and the plume bent, and far up in the dark the mountain made a sound I'd never heard it make before—deep, rhythmic, almost patient. Like something clearing its throat before it spoke.

"Get some sleep, Teo," Hazel said, and went back inside.

I stayed out another minute. Forty hours, give or take. And for the first time since Manali, I wasn't sure which side of the weather I was standing on.
