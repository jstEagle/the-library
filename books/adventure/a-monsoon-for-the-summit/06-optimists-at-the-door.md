## 6. Optimists at the Door

The satellite phone rang at 4:17 in the morning, which is the hour when all bad news dresses itself in static.

I was already awake. High camp does that to you—the wind plays the tent fly like a badly tuned instrument, and your body keeps a ledger of every creak that isn't Dev snoring. I'd been lying there doing arithmetic I didn't like: oxygen remaining, food remaining, days until the monsoon window closed, and the number of lies Dr. Ansel Brandt had told us divided by the number of lies we'd chosen not to notice. The quotient was ugly.

I answered on the third ring because answering on the first tells people you're afraid.

"June Okafor?"

The voice was smooth as poured concrete. Male, mid-forties, the accent of someone who'd learned English from money and never unlearned it. Behind him, wind—real wind, valley wind, not our ridge wind.

"Who's asking?"

"Let's say I'm an optimist." A pause with a smile in it. "I believe everyone's debts can be settled amicably. Even yours."

I sat up. Teo stirred across the tent and I put my hand over the mouthpiece until he resettled, which is a skill you develop raising younger brothers and then, apparently, keeping for the rest of your life.

"You have the wrong expedition," I said.

"I have exactly the right one. Four crew, one climatologist, one Ingrid Voss. Kirat Ridge, high camp, above the icefall." He said it the way you read someone's mail back to them. "My name doesn't matter yet. What matters is that in roughly thirty-six hours, I'll be standing where you're standing, and I'd hate for that meeting to be awkward."

"Then don't come," I said. "It's monsoon season. The passes are closing. Ask any navigator."

"I did, actually. She was very good. Former military, excellent handwriting on her charts, tragic career ending." Concrete again. "You'd be amazed what a man can buy if he stops pretending he wants friends."

I looked at the tent wall, at the gray pre-dawn pressing through the fabric, and I did the thing I do instead of panicking, which is inventory. One satphone, encrypted-ish, purchased by Ingrid Voss from a supplier in Salagdan whose name I now intended to remember forever. One caller who knew our position to within a valley. One expedition that was supposed to be simple logistics.

"Who else is coming?" I asked, because the question itself was a test. Innocent people ask *what do you want*. Guilty ones ask *how many*.

"Two gentlemen joining me," he said pleasantly. "Old colleagues of Ms. Voss. They invested in her once, before her reputation went into receivership. They consider this trip a chance to recoup." He let that sit. "They also hold paper on a certain Mr. Reyes. And on a mechanic named Devraj Iyer, though I suspect you knew that. And on a young man named Teo Alonzo, whose brother signed something foolish in Manila."

The wind filled the silence while I decided whether to feel cold. I chose not to. Cold is a luxury; I had work to do.

"Here's my counteroffer," I said. "You turn around, and nobody has to learn how resourceful we are."

"Ms. Okafor." He actually chuckled. "Resourcefulness is why we're bringing two of everything. Sleep well. Tell Hazel I said hello—he won't remember my face, but he'll remember my car. It was parked outside his workshop for eleven days."

He hung up before I could tell him what I remembered about his voice, which was nothing, which meant he'd been careful, which meant he'd done this before.

* * *

I didn't wake Hazel.

Understand: it wasn't mercy. It was triage. Hazel at 4 a.m. with syndicate news is Hazel with a lit fuse and no plan, and I've watched him burn through three years of careful invisibility in under an hour once already, back in Salagdan, over a parking ticket that wasn't a parking ticket. He runs toward fires. It's his best quality and the reason his brother isn't here, though he'd gut me for saying so, so I say it only to myself, at 4 a.m., holding satellite phones.

Instead I woke Ingrid.

She was in the equipment tent, awake—which should have told me everything right there, a woman sitting fully dressed in the dark at 4:30, her headlamp off, her hands around a thermos she wasn't drinking from. When I relayed the call, word for word, including the part about the car, she didn't blink.

"Eleven days," she said. "That tracks."

"That's your takeaway? That *tracks*?"

"June." She set the thermos down with the deliberateness of someone buying time. "How much do you know about my last expedition?"

"The one that ended your funding, your marriage, and your standing in three professional societies? The highlights."

"Then you know my backers lost a great deal when it collapsed. And you know they don't forgive." She looked at me straight, which I'll grant her; liars with weak technique look away. "They found us because I let them find us."

The wind hit the tent and I let it fill the space where my composure used to live.

"Say that again slowly."

"This job was never a job. Not for them, and—" she exhaled, "—not entirely for me. Ansel needed an escort he could trust. I needed bait good enough to draw the syndicate out of Salagdan and onto ground where their money means nothing. You were the best crew I could hire and the most believable cargo. I'm sorry. Both of those things are true at once, and I know how that sounds."

I want to report that I felt betrayed. What I actually felt was worse: I felt *vindicated*, in the small mean way you feel vindicated when the thing you suspected at the trailhead turns out to be true. I'd watched Ingrid route our approach to avoid the syndicate's known checkpoints too precisely. I'd watched her check the satphone's call log twice a night. Nobody guards a phone like that unless the phone is the whole point.

"And the data?" I asked. "Ansel's instruments. Are those real or are they props?"

"They're real. That's the part I couldn't control." For the first time she looked tired, genuinely tired, ten years tired. "My former backers think Ansel is carrying a weather key—a predictive signature for the ridge's storm cell. If it works, it's worth more than all of us combined. If it works, whoever holds it owns every government and insurer on the monsoon's path." She paused. "And if it doesn't work, they'll still kill us for wasting their time."

"So we're hostages with a deliverable."

"We're negotiators," she said, "who haven't opened talks yet."

I left her tent without agreeing to anything, which she noticed, because she called after me—*June*—in a voice that wanted absolution, and I kept walking, because I hadn't decided whether she'd earned it, and I don't hand out credit I can't verify.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about being the navigator: your entire profession is deciding, ahead of time, what to do when things go wrong. Every route I've ever charted contains routes inside it—the bailout line, the fallback line, the line marked in pencil that I pray I never ink. So while the others slept, I sat in my tent with my headlamp hooded red and drew contingencies the way other people breathe.

Contingency one: run. Not viable. Thirty-six hours, two valleys, collectors behind and monsoon ahead. Running converts us from witnesses into prey, and prey gets shot where nobody audits the paperwork.

Contingency two: pay. We had nothing they wanted except the drives and ourselves. Non-starter, but useful as theater—every negotiation needs a door the other side expects you to take.

Contingency three: split the enemy. Two backers plus one caller equals three agendas wearing one coat. Men like that cooperate exactly as long as cooperation pays. My job, over the next day and a half, was to figure out what each of them wanted individually and make sure those wants stopped fitting in the same tent.

And contingency four—the one I wrote smallest, in the back of the notebook, where I keep the truths I'm not ready to say out loud:

Tell Hazel nothing. Manage him. Keep him pointed at the mountain and away from the men, because Hazel against the syndicate is a match thrown into a dry field, and I love that reckless son of a bitch too much to watch him torch himself settling scores. He thinks he's protecting us by carrying everything alone. Fine. Then I'd protect him the same way, and neither of us would ever have to admit we learned it from each other.

Dev would need to know—partly, eventually. Dev can hotwire a generator and read a man's intentions from fifty meters; he'd earned a fraction of the truth. Teo got nothing. Teo was currently up at the sensor array helping Ansel, glowing like a boy handed his first real responsibility, and I would sooner chew gravel than let the syndicate's shadow fall across that.

At dawn I climbed to the cook ledge and made coffee for six with hands that didn't shake, because that's the job too. Hazel came down from the ridgeline looking like a man who'd slept standing up, took the cup, and studied my face with those storm-reader eyes of his.

"You look like weather's coming," he said.

"Forecast says clear for thirty-six hours," I said, and gave him the smile I use on customs officials, and hated myself for it, and did it anyway.

Behind him, Ingrid watched me from the equipment tent and didn't look away, and I understood that we had entered a new arrangement, she and I: two women keeping secrets from the same man, for opposite reasons, both claiming love.

The satphone buzzed in my pocket. A text this time, just coordinates and a single line:

*Optimists arrive tomorrow. Do have the data ready.*

I deleted it, checked the battery, and started drawing the route I hoped we'd never take.
