## 7. Whiteout Terms

The barometer started lying to us at four in the morning, and I knew it was lying because I'd rebuilt it myself.

I sat in the vestibule of the high camp tent with the instrument cradled in my lap like a sick bird, watching the needle do something needles aren't supposed to do. It had dropped forty millibars in six hours. Not fallen—*dropped*, the way a stone drops, with intent, as if the atmosphere above Kirat Ridge had made a decision about us overnight and was now simply executing it.

"June," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I felt, which is the one trick I never lost. "Get up."

She was awake before I finished the sentence. June sleeps like a sentry, one ear always on the weather, which in nine years of working with her has saved my life at least twice that I know of and probably more that I don't.

"Tell me it's the sensor," she said.

"It's not the sensor."

"Tell me anyway."

"I rebuilt it myself."

She closed her eyes for a second, the way you do when someone confirms the thing you already knew, and then she was moving—boots, jacket, out into the dark to wake Dev and Teo. I stayed with the barometer a moment longer. Outside, the wind had that sound. You spend enough years chasing storms and you learn their vocabulary, and this one wasn't talking yet, but it was clearing its throat.

The forecast had given us until tomorrow evening. Ansel's models, Ingrid's satellite feeds, three independent sources all agreeing the monsoon pulse wouldn't reach the ridge for another thirty-six hours minimum. And here it was, a full day early, arriving like a guest who'd read the invitation wrong on purpose.

Or exactly right on purpose. That was the thought I couldn't shake, sitting there in the cold with the needle buried in the red.

* * *

By dawn we could see it coming up the valley, and it didn't look like weather. It looked like architecture.

A wall of cloud stacked from the glacier floor to somewhere above our ability to see, lit from inside by lightning that flickered without sound—the distance eating the thunder before it reached us. Behind it, higher up, the summit pyramid of Kirat had vanished entirely, swallowed so cleanly it might never have existed. The mountain was being unmade in front of us, and we were standing on the part that went first.

"That's not a monsoon cell," Dev said. He had his binoculars up and his voice had gone flat, which for Dev meant frightened. "That's a *system*. That's got structure. Look at the rotation."

"I'm looking."

"Nobody forecasts around that. Nobody climbs under that."

"Ansel did." I said it before I could stop myself, and then I made myself say the rest of it, because Teo was standing there with his ears open and I'd rather he hear it from me. "His data predicted this. The pressure signature. The ridge generates its own storm cell when the monsoon front interacts with the topography—it's not random, it's repeatable. It's a machine, and the monsoon is just the fuel."

Teo looked at me the way he looks at Ansel, hungry and a little afraid, and asked the question I'd been circling all morning: "Then why didn't anyone tell us it would come early?"

And there it was. The hole in the middle of everything, big enough to walk an expedition through.

Because if Ansel's instruments were good enough to find the signature—and they were; I'd helped calibrate them, I'd watched the numbers come off the sensors at high camp two nights ago and felt something cold move through me that had nothing to do with altitude—then his instruments were good enough to know the timing. A man doesn't build a machine that precise and then misread his own output by twenty-four hours. Unless the twenty-four hours were the point. Unless somebody wanted us pinned above the icefall when the collectors arrived, wanted the storm to be the lock on the door instead of the wall across it.

I looked down-valley, where the cloud hadn't reached yet. Somewhere back there, two valleys and a day's hard travel behind us, were men whose politeness I remembered from Salagdan—the collector with the soft voice and the ledger, the two of Ingrid's former backers who'd bought our debt the way you buy a used truck, sight unseen, because the engine was guaranteed to be worth more than the price. They'd have gotten the same satellite data we did. They'd have known the window was closing. And they'd come anyway, because they weren't coming for us. They were coming for what Ansel was carrying, and storms don't scare men who think of weather as inventory.

"Hazel." Ingrid stood at the edge of camp with the wind pulling at her hood, and she had that look she gets, the one that means she's three moves ahead and wants credit for it. "We descend now, ahead of the cell. We can beat it to the icefall if we leave within the hour."

"We can't."

"The icefall's stable in these pressures for another—"

"Ingrid." I turned to face her fully. "Your backers are two valleys back. They left when we did. If we run for the icefall, we meet them in the narrows, in whiteout, with them carrying guns and us carrying everything they want. Is that your plan? Because it's a good plan, if the goal is to hand it all over."

Something moved behind her eyes—not surprise, exactly. Recalculation. She'd known they were coming; June had told me about the satellite call, or most of it, and I'd watched Ingrid carry that knowledge for two days like a stone in her cheek, turning it, saying nothing. She'd built this expedition the way you build a trap, and I think some part of her had always intended to be caught in it too.

"You have another idea," she said.

"I have a plan."

"Not the same thing."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

Here is what nine years of running from a mountain taught me, and what I finally understood at eight thousand meters with the sky coming apart above me:

The storm wasn't the obstacle. The storm was the proof.

Ansel's whole secret—the thing his backers would kill for, the thing the syndicate would trade lives for—was that Kirat Ridge makes its own weather on a schedule. Predictable. Repeatable. A key that turns the same lock every time the monsoon arrives. Whoever holds that data doesn't need to control the ridge. They need to control the *forecast*. Everyone else on this mountain—the climbers, the villages in the valleys, the crews on the glaciers—they'll all be reading the official numbers, trusting the models, planning their lives around a forecast that can be quietly rewritten by whoever owns the signature. You don't weaponize a storm. You weaponize the certainty that there won't be one.

And the storm coming at us a day early wasn't a failure of prediction. It was the prediction, working perfectly, in front of witnesses. Ansel knew. Maybe he even needed us to see it—to have people who could testify the model held, that the machine ran true. Or maybe he just hadn't cared whether we lived long enough to appreciate the elegance of it.

Either way, the same fact cut both ways, and I was the only one on this ridge who'd been cut by it before.

Nine years ago, a team stood on this mountain with a forecast they didn't share, and my brother climbed into weather they knew was coming, because the risk assessment in the official file had been edited for investors. Marcus died believing the sky was honest. I've spent nine years believing nothing, and it turns out both positions will kill you.

So here was the clarity, arriving the way terror does—clean, total, almost restful:

The collectors couldn't cross the icefall in this. Nobody could. But they didn't need to. They'd wait at the base, dry and patient, knowing the storm would eventually squeeze us down to them like juice from a fruit. Every route off this ridge ends at the icefall. We were pinned, exactly as designed.

Unless we weren't.

There's a line on the west flank—I saw it nine years ago from the disaster site, a seam in the rock and ice that the old surveys called unclimbable and that I'd stared at for three days while waiting for helicopters that came too late. It drops into the western drainage, the long way, the brutal way, five days instead of two, through country where the syndicate's maps end and the monsoon hits differently. It's not a route. It's a wound in the mountain. But wounds go somewhere.

If we took the drives west, the collectors would sit at the icefall waiting for cargo that never arrived. And if Ingrid's friends wanted their payment, they could explain to the syndicate how their bait got away.

I didn't tell Ingrid. That's the part I keep turning over—the ease of it. Two weeks ago I'd have laid it all out, negotiated, traded, found the version where everyone got a piece. Instead I stood in the wind with the storm eating the summit and kept my mouth shut, and understood that I'd stopped being her employee somewhere back on the glacier, and started being something she hadn't planned for: a man with nothing left to lose who'd finally decided what he wasn't willing to lose.

Dev, June, Teo. Ansel, maybe, if he could be made to understand. The drives, going into a crevasse the size of a cathedral. My brother's mountain keeping what it keeps.

"Pack for five days," I told the crew, loud enough to carry over the rising wind. "West flank. We move in twenty minutes."

Ingrid's head came around. "The west flank is a death sentence."

"So's the icefall," I said. "At least this one's mine."

Behind her, the first thunder finally arrived, rolling up the valley like something enormous changing its mind—and above us, the last of the summit disappeared, and the monsoon took the mountain for its own.
