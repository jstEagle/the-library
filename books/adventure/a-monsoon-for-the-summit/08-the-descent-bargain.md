## 8. The Descent Bargain

I have negotiated with warlords, insurance adjusters, and one Norwegian prince who collected glaciers the way other men collect stamps, and I can tell you that every negotiation is the same animal wearing different clothes. You find out what the other side wants more than they want you, and you sell it to them at a markup.

The problem at the icefall was that what the syndicate wanted most was Hazel Reyes, and I had promised myself—somewhere around the third switchback of the day before, somewhere around the moment I realized the monsoon had jumped its forecast by a full day like a horse refusing a fence—that I would not give them Hazel Reyes.

Everything else was negotiable. That was the arithmetic I'd carried up the mountain in my chest along with my heartbeat: three drives of Ansel Brandt's data against four lives and a debt that had been accruing interest since before any of us met. It was good math. It was the kind of math I'd built a reputation on, back when I had one, back when "Voss" meant something besides *the woman who bet on a ridge and lost her funding, her credibility, and nearly her team*.

This whole expedition, if I'm honest—and the icefall seemed like a place for honesty, all that blue ice holding us up like a congregation holding its breath—this whole expedition had been my con from the start. Not against Hazel. Against the people who'd written me off. I needed the ridge's storm signature confirmed, I needed it confirmed by someone the world would believe, and I needed the men who'd ruined me to be standing close enough to watch when it happened. The debt collectors finding us wasn't bad luck. I'd let them find us. A lure works best when the fish doesn't know there's a hook.

I want that on the record, wherever records go when a mountain swallows them: I knew exactly what I was doing right up until the moment I watched someone else do something braver.

* * *

The collectors came up the glacier at noon, two valleys behind schedule and moving like men who'd been promised bonuses. There were six of them, roped competently enough that I could tell at least two had real mountaineering backgrounds—the syndicate didn't send tourists after nine years of hunting a man. Their leader was a broad, patient-looking fellow called Marat who spoke softly the way dangerous men do when they've never needed volume.

He stopped forty meters below our camp, where the icefall's seracs leaned over the route like drunks leaning over a railing, and he smiled at me first, which I appreciated professionally even as it chilled me.

"Frau Voss." He said it correctly, which told me he'd done his homework too. "You look well for a dead woman."

"Flattery," I said. "At this altitude. You'll make me faint."

"We have an arrangement to conclude." His eyes went past me to the tents, cataloguing. Dev standing at the cook tarp with a spanner held loosely, uselessly, bravely. June beside him doing nothing at all, which from June was louder than shouting. Teo looking terrified and furious in equal measure. Ansel sitting on a case like a man at his own trial. And Hazel, slightly apart, watching me with an expression I couldn't read, which frightened me more than Marat did.

"The data," Marat said. "Dr. Brandt's instruments. Your backers want them. We want the Reyes account closed—principal, interest, and the cost of this excursion." He spread his hands. "It happens we can satisfy both parties with one transaction. You give us the drives. We consider everyone's obligations settled. Everyone walks down together."

"Together," I repeated.

"I'm not a monster, Frau Voss. Storms are coming. Even we don't work in weather like this." He glanced up at the sky, which had gone the color of wet slate, and I saw genuine respect there. Whatever else the syndicate was, they were professionals. It's the amateurs who kill you; the professionals just bill you.

I looked at Ansel. He looked back at me with his hands flat on the instrument case, and I understood that he would rather die on this ice than surrender those drives, and that he might get his wish, and that some part of him had planned for that possibility the way he'd planned everything else—including, I suspected, hiring a guide whose brother the ridge had already taken. Ansel Brandt was not a man who wasted anything, including other people's grief.

"Give me a minute with my crew," I said.

"You have five."

* * *

Here is what I intended to say in that minute: *We trade the drives. The data is a pressure signature, a forecast key—it will be weaponized or it will be buried, but either way it isn't worth your lives, and I refuse to spend you.*

Here is what actually happened: I got as far as "we trade" when Hazel stood up.

"No," he said.

"Hazel—" I kept my voice level, the voice I'd used on clients and crevasse-rescues alike. "It's the only play. They clear the debt. We all descend alive. The data was always going to end up in someone's hands; better theirs than a crevasse, better a sale than a grave—"

"You've done the math," he said. Not angry. That was the worst part. He sounded almost gentle, the way you talk to someone standing too near an edge. "Triple pay, cleared debts, everyone home. Clean transaction. Very Voss."

"It's the only version where nobody bleeds."

"It's the version where you finally buy your reputation back," he said, "with my brother's mountain."

The wind came through the col then, a long low moan off the summit ridge, and I swear the icefall answered it—a deep structural groan underfoot, the seracs settling their weight. Nobody moved. Marat's men had stopped pretending to check their ropes.

"Hazel," I said, quieter. "I chose you because you know what this ridge took. I need you to remember what it took, and let the data go."

"Oh, I remember," he said. And then he smiled, and it was the smile of a man who has been carrying something heavy for nine years and has finally decided where to set it down. "That's why I can't let it go. Watch."

* * *

What happened next took perhaps ninety seconds, and I have replayed it so many times since that it has worn grooves in my memory like a rope through a carabiner.

Hazel walked past me toward the handoff point—not toward Marat, but toward the base of the great leaning serac above the route, the one every one of us had been eyeing nervously for two days. He moved with the unhurried economy of a man doing routine work, and that was the genius of it: nothing about him read as threat. He knelt at the ice anchor where the fixed line was set, as if checking it for the descent.

Dev appeared beside him with the spanner. I still don't know what passed between them—some crew language older than speech—but Dev began unclipping charges from the rack on his harness, the small shaped cutting charges we carried for emergency route-breaking through icefalls, and Hazel placed them. Four of them. At the load-bearing joints of the serac's downstream face. Placed not to drop the thing on us—

Placed to drop it on the route. *Their* route. The glacier highway Marat's six men were standing on.

June, meanwhile, had drifted to the radio and was speaking into it in a low, flat voice, and I caught only fragments: *"...east flank... when it goes, you move on my mark..."*

They'd planned this. Not today—maybe not consciously at all—but the shape of it had been waiting in these three people the way a storm waits in a pressure gradient. All it needed was the trigger.

"Hazel," I said, loud now, no longer caring who heard. "There are six men on that route."

"And none of them die," he said, without turning. "If they're smart. If they're fast. That's the bargain I'm offering instead of yours." He looked back at me once, and his eyes were terrible and calm. "Run when it goes. Or stand here and negotiate with gravity."

Then he turned to Marat and raised his voice across the forty meters of blue ice.

"Gentlemen! New terms!" He held up one of the detonators, small and black and casual as a cigarette lighter. "Your route is mined. My crew leaves this camp in ten minutes, down the east flank, all of us together, and you will wait until we're clear before you follow—or you'll be following in pieces. No drives. No data. No deal. Just a long walk down in bad weather, and my professional advice to take it."

Marat's face did something complicated. I watched him calculate—men like him are always calculating—and I watched him arrive at the same answer I had: that Hazel Reyes was not bluffing, that the serac above them was the size of a cathedral, and that the sky behind the summit ridge had begun to boil.

"You're a fool, Reyes," Marat called back. But he was already unroping his men from the fixed line, already stepping back off the route, and there was respect in it, grudging and cold.

"Frequently," Hazel agreed. "Dev?"

"On your mark."

Hazel looked at the sky. Looked at the ridge. And I understood, in the last second, that he wasn't timing the charge to his own convenience—he was timing it to the mountain's. To the first tremor of the storm cell Ansel's instruments had predicted, the ridge's own signature arriving early, arriving *now*, the air pressure dropping so fast my ears popped. He was going to add his small explosion to the mountain's large one and let the monsoon take the credit.

"Now," Hazel said.

The charges cracked—four sharp reports swallowed instantly by the wind—and the serac shrugged. It didn't fall so much as lean further, shedding a wall of ice across the lower route with a sound like a continent clearing its throat. The powder cloud rolled up white and total, and through it I heard Marat's men running, boots on ice, alive, scrambling for high ground on their side of the new wall of rubble that now sealed the syndicate's approach like a door closing.

Then the storm arrived in earnest and erased everything else.

* * *

We moved within minutes, because staying was dying. East flank, single file, June navigating by compass and memory while the world shrank to a gray circle ten meters wide. Dev half-carried Teo. Ansel stumbled along clutching nothing—he'd left the cases, left the drives, left nine years of obsession in a tent at the icefall, and he turned once, mid-whiteout, and shouted at Hazel over the wind:

"You fool! Do you know what you've destroyed? That data could have saved lives! It could have *predicted*—"

"So could honesty," Hazel shouted back, not slowing. "Nobody ever tried it."

Ansel made a sound that was half fury and half something else, something closer to grief, and then the storm took even that.

And I brought up the rear, last in line, the way leaders should be, thinking about the minute I'd asked for and hadn't used. Thinking about how I'd climbed this entire mountain on a ladder of other people's needs—Hazel's debt, Ansel's obsession, my own hunger to matter again—and how Hazel had just burned the ladder and started climbing freehand.

I had engineered all of it. The bait, the backers, the beautiful clean transaction. And in ninety seconds a disgraced storm chaser had dismantled my life's work and replaced it with something I didn't have a ledger for.

Behind us, muffled by the roar, came the sound of the syndicate regrouping on their side of the ice wall—and ahead, through a tear in the cloud, one glimpse of the descent route: a knife-edge arête, corniced, exposed, the only way down, and the full weight of the monsoon rolling onto it like a tide.

June's voice crackled in my earpiece, calm as a woman reading a grocery list.

"Ingrid. We have a problem with the next section."
