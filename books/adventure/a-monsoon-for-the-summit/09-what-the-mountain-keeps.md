## 9. What the Mountain Keeps

The storm came down on us like a debt collector with a grudge and a schedule.

We were crossing the shoulder below the summit ridge when the sky simply quit pretending to be weather and became a verdict. Wind hit us sideways, hard enough that I had to lean into it like a drunk leaning on a lamppost, and the snow didn't fall so much as travel horizontally with intent. Visibility dropped to the length of my own arm. Somewhere behind me, Dev was shouting rope commands in his calm mechanic's voice, the one he used for engines and for me when I was being an idiot.

"Rope team tight!" June called from the rear. "Teo's clipped. We move on Hazel's count."

I gave the count. My voice held. That's the thing about nine years of practice at falling apart quietly—you get very good at the outside of it.

Ansel was on my rope, third position, and I could feel him through the line, a tremor running down the kernmantle like a bad bearing. Not cold-shakes. Something else. I'd been watching him all morning watching the sky, and there was a look on his face I recognized from mirrors: a man standing in front of the thing he'd spent years telling himself he wanted.

* * *

The col where we sheltered was a scoop of rock maybe four meters deep, enough to break the wind if we pressed against the back wall. We got the tent half-pitched before the gusts made it a joke, so we huddled instead, six people and a stove that Dev coaxed to life with the tenderness other men reserve for newborns.

That's when Ansel reached for his pack.

I want to be fair to him here, because what happened next wasn't villainy. It was desperation wearing a lab coat. He pulled out the satellite transmitter—the backup unit, the one Ingrid didn't know he carried—and started assembling it with hands that shook so badly he dropped the antenna coupling twice.

"No," I said.

He didn't even look up. "The window is now, Hazel. The cell's peaking. If I transmit the full dataset during the event itself, it corroborates every—"

"I said no."

"Hazel." Ingrid's voice, low, from across the stove. A warning or a question; with her it was always both.

"The backers need this," Ansel said, and now he did look up, and his eyes were wild and wet and utterly certain. "Do you understand what this is? A repeatable pressure signature. A storm key. This ridge generates a forecastable mesoscale cell and nobody on Earth knows it but us. The consortium will fund twenty years of research off one transmission. Medicine, agriculture, disaster mitigation—"

"And arms," June said quietly. She hadn't moved from the tent flap. "You left out arms."

"That is not my department."

"It's everybody's department," she said. "It just takes a while to arrive."

Ansel turned back to the transmitter. Teo was staring at him like a kid watching a parent shoplift. And I understood, with the strange clarity that only comes at fourteen thousand feet in a whiteout, that we had arrived at the exact center of the thing I'd been walking toward for nine years.

So I told him the truth.

* * *

"My brother's name was Mateo Reyes," I said. "Everyone called him Teo, which is why our Teo over there flinched the first week whenever I said it. He was twenty-six. He was the best forecaster I ever knew, better than me, better than anyone at Voss's outfit, and in 2016 he was working a private expedition on this exact ridge for a company called Meridian Atmospheric Solutions."

The stove hissed. The wind screamed past the col's mouth like it wanted in.

"Meridian had modeled something like what you're holding. Not as good—crude, partial—but they had a window. They knew a storm cell was going to stall over the upper mountain with a probability they put at thirty percent. Thirty percent is a coin flip with extra steps, so they rounded down. They had sponsors waiting on summit photos and a press release about 'predictive meteorology in extreme environments,' and rounding down was cheaper than delaying."

Ansel had gone very still. The transmitter sat in his lap like a sleeping animal.

"Mateo argued. He filed a dissent—actual paper, actual signature. They let him keep his spot because firing him would've looked like what it was. On day six the cell stalled early. It wasn't thirty percent anymore. It was a hundred percent, and it was on top of them, and eleven people died in a whiteout two hundred meters below a camp they'd have reached by noon if anyone had listened." I heard my own voice go flat, which is how I know I mean something. "My brother wrote the forecast that would have saved them. It was in his pack when they found him. Nobody read it until after."

"Hazel," Ingrid said softly.

"I'm not finished." I looked at Ansel. "You know what happened to Meridian? Nothing. Reorganized, rebranded, absorbed. The lead scientist got a chair at a institute in Geneva. The data got sold twice. And the families got settlements with nondisclosure clauses, which is how a man can lose his brother to greed and not be allowed to say the word greed out loud."

"You burned your career saying it anyway," Ansel whispered. "The 2017 hearings. You testified that commercial forecasting was unregulated and lethal. They called you unstable. They dug up your debts, your—"

"They dug up everything except the part that mattered, which is that I was right." I crouched down to his level. Snowmelt dripped off my hood between us. "And here's what I've learned since, Ansel, the thing I'd have saved myself nine years of misery by knowing: you cannot hand a weaponizable truth to good intentions and expect the intentions to survive the trip. You're transmitting to a consortium whose due diligence includes syndicate collectors. You think that's an accident? You think Ingrid's former backers found us by luck?"

Ansel's eyes flicked to Ingrid. Ingrid, to her credit, didn't blink.

"They'll launder it," I went on. "Six months after your transmission, your storm key is a pricing model for insurance companies deciding which valleys are worth saving. A year after that, it's a targeting package. You'll be famous, and Mateo will still be dead, and somewhere down-mountain there'll be another rope team rounding down their percentages because the model said they could."

"The science is real," Ansel said, but the certainty had gone out of it, leaving only grief.

"Yeah," I said. "That's the worst part. It's real. So was his."

* * *

For a long moment the only sound was the stove and the storm arguing about who owned the mountain.

Then Ansel did something I didn't expect. He laughed—one broken note, no humor in it—and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes.

"I told myself it was different," he said. "I told myself the whole flight up. I built the sensor array myself. I lied to my own funders about the calibration runs. I carried a second transmitter past three checkpoints, and every time my hands shook I told myself it was the altitude." He lowered his hands. He looked old, suddenly, the way men do when a story they've been living in collapses. "It was never the altitude."

"No," I agreed. "It never is."

"I knew your name," he said. "From the beginning. Before Ingrid hired you. Your testimony was required reading in my doctoral program—'the Reyes objection,' we called it. I told myself hiring you was penance. It was cover. If anyone questioned the expedition, who would suspect the man who hated monetized forecasting of building one?" His voice cracked. "I used your grief as camouflage, Hazel. I'm sorry. I'm—I don't have a word big enough."

"There isn't one," I said. "But you're using it correctly."

He looked at the transmitter for a long time. Then he unclipped it, set it on the stone, and reached deeper into the pack. He brought out the drives—three of them, sealed in waterproof cases, small enough to hold a hurricane apiece—and held them out to me.

"You should drop them," he said. "Not me. Me, I'd find a reason. Some compromise. A copy, a delay, a noble-sounding institution." A ghost of a smile. "You've had nine years of practice at knowing exactly what to burn."

I took them. They weighed almost nothing. That's the trick of data—it's the lightest thing in the world until it's the heaviest.

* * *

There's a crevasse forty meters beyond the col. I'd clocked it on the way up—a slot where the glacier had shrugged, maybe eight meters deep, floored with old snow. Not a grave. A filing cabinet.

June came with me, roped at two meters, because of course she did. Neither of us spoke while we walked. The storm had eased a fraction—not ended, monsoons don't end on request, but the ridge had paused to take a breath, and we borrowed it.

At the lip of the crevasse I stopped and held the drives in my palm. Three little cases. Twenty years of someone's brilliant, terrible work. My brother's unfinished forecast, in a sense, completed by a stranger and aimed at the same cliff.

"You don't have to narrate," June said. "In case you were thinking of making a speech."

"I'm always thinking of making a speech."

"I know. It's exhausting." But she put her gloved hand over mine, over the drives, for one second. "Mateo would've liked the nerd," she said. "That's the hell of it. He'd have liked him, and argued with him for a decade, and bought him dinner after."

"Yeah," I said. My throat was doing something complicated. "That's the hell of it."

I dropped them in. No ceremony. They fell, and the blue dark took them, and the mountain closed its ledger without comment.

What surprised me was what came up out of me afterward—not relief, nothing so tidy. It was more like setting down a load I'd forgotten I was carrying, and discovering my shoulders remembered every kilometer of it. I stood at the edge of that slot in the driving snow and I said, out loud, into the wind, "There. Now nobody gets to use you," and I wasn't talking to the drives.

June pretended not to hear. She's good like that.

Behind us, Ansel stood at the col with his empty pack hanging off one shoulder, watching, and Teo had a hand fisted in the man's sleeve like he was holding him to the earth. Ingrid was already breaking camp, because Ingrid processes revelation through logistics. Dev was coiling rope.

And far below us, down in the valleys we'd crossed three days ago, I could feel it in my teeth the way you feel a train through a station floor: the syndicate, still coming, two days out and closing, with debts unpaid and tempers short and nothing left on this mountain worth killing for—which, historically, has never once stopped anybody.

The monsoon swallowed the ridge behind us, whole and final, and we turned downhill toward home with empty packs and a long way to go.

"Well," June said. "Now we just have to survive being broke and famous for the wrong reasons."

"One catastrophe at a time," I said, and led us down.
