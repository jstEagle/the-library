## 10. A Monsoon for the Summit

The monsoon reached Salagdan three weeks after it swallowed the ridge, arriving the way bad news always does—slowly, then all at once, then everywhere at once, until you forgot there had ever been a dry season.

I was on the roof of the workshop when it came, tarping over a leak that Dev swore he'd fixed in April and June swore he hadn't. The first drops hit like punctuation. Then the sky simply opened, and the whole port town disappeared into a gray roar, and I stood there with my arms out and let it hammer me flat.

"Teo." Hazel's voice from below, not sharp, just certain. "You'll rust."

"I'm not equipment."

"Everything's equipment if you leave it out in weather long enough."

That was the closest thing to philosophy Hazel Reyes offered anymore, and I took it inside with me, dripping, and dried off by the woodstove while the rain did its work on the world.

* * *

Here is what the expedition became, officially: a failure.

The papers ran it small—a climatological survey abandoned ahead of an early monsoon, one team member treated for frostbite, no findings published. The backers' foundation issued a statement about "unforeseen meteorological conditions," which was true in the way a knife wound is technically a change in skin condition. Ingrid Voss gave one interview, sitting very straight, saying she took full responsibility for the expedition's collapse, and by the end of the week the climbing forums had decided she'd lost her nerve, that the great Ingrid Voss had finally met a mountain that broke her.

She let them believe it. I watched her do it, at the tea house near the pier, letting a table of guides speculate loudly within earshot, her face arranged into something almost like humility. Later I asked her why she didn't correct them.

"Reputation is a tool," she said, stirring her tea. "Right now mine works better broken."

The syndicate never came for any of us. That wasn't luck. June figured it out before anyone—she figures everything out before anyone—and told us in her flat way over dinner: the debts were gone. All of them. Hazel's, hers, Dev's, even the small ugly one Teo had been carrying since he was nineteen and stupid in a city far from here. Paid in full, quietly, through three different intermediaries, no notes attached.

Ingrid had settled the accounts herself. With what, none of us asked, because we'd all seen what her former backers would pay to make a problem disappear, and we understood that whatever she'd traded, she'd traded it so completely it could never come back for us.

"She bought us out," Dev said.

"She paid us off," June said.

"She set us free," I said, and they both looked at me with the particular pity reserved for rookies who still believe in sentences like that.

But the truth was somewhere in the middle, the way truth usually is, and the middle was this: we were poorer than we'd ever been, and lighter too. The workshop still stood. The radar units still needed patching. And nobody—not the syndicate, not the backers, not the mountain—was coming to collect.

* * *

Hazel worked six days a week now, and I mean worked, not the careful idling he used to do, the deliberate underemployment that had been its own kind of hiding. He repaired radar units for the fishing fleet, recalibrated the town's ancient storm-warning siren, taught a Saturday class for harbor kids on reading cloud fronts. His hands didn't shake. I kept checking, because I remembered how they'd shaken on the ridge, whenever Kirat came into view, and I wanted to be sure the steadiness was real and not another performance.

It was real. I don't know exactly when the change happened. Maybe at the icefall, when he buried the syndicate's route rather than hand Ansel over. Maybe higher up, in the whiteout, when he stopped a scientist twice his age with nothing but his voice and the truth about his brother. Or maybe it happened later, quietly, somewhere in the weeks after, the way a bone sets.

Above his workbench, where he'd once hung a faded photo of a storm cell he'd chased across three states, there was a carabiner. Rusted, pitted, stamped with initials worn nearly smooth. J.R. Nobody asked about it. Once, a customer—a fisherman's wife picking up a transceiver—pointed at it and asked if it was a good brand. Hazel said, "Best one ever made," and went back to his soldering iron, and I had to walk outside and stand in the rain for a minute.

Dev found it, back on the pass. I know that now. He never said so, but I saw him look at it once, the day Hazel hung it up, and Dev has a face that keeps secrets badly, and what was on it wasn't guilt. It was relief.

* * *

Ansel Brandt sent letters. Actual letters, paper, from a university two continents away. The first one Hazel read standing at the bench and then fed into the stove without comment. The second one sat on the shelf for a week before June opened it, read it aloud, and summarized: "He says he understands. He says he's sorry. He says the data would have saved lives someday, and Hazel says so would honesty, sooner." The third letter Hazel actually answered. I know because I addressed the envelope for him, and because his reply was four lines long and took him two hours to write, and I have decided never to tell anyone what it said, including this account, because some things belong to the two men who were on the ridge and no one else.

Ansel was walking again by then. The frostbite took two toes and none of the rest of him. In his last letter he wrote that he was teaching undergraduates now, and that he'd started his lectures with a case study about a forecast that was monetized and a risk that was lied about, and that he never named the mountain or the year, but every semester some student in the back row would go pale, and Ansel had learned to recognize that pallor as the beginning of wisdom.

"He's building a monument out of caution," June said.

"Hazel built one out of silence," Dev said. "Same material. Different architect."

I thought they were both wrong, but I was twenty-three and knew less than I thought I did, so I kept it to myself, which was also new for me.

* * *

The ridicule, when it came, was mostly harmless. Salagdan is a small port; it runs on gossip the way boats run on diesel. We were the crew that got turned around by weather, the famous Hazel Reyes—I use the word famous loosely, the way you'd use it about a man known for one thing nine years ago—who couldn't even finish a simple logistics job. The harbormaster teased Dev about it every time he picked up parts. A guide I'd worked with before the expedition clapped me on the shoulder and said, "Next time check the calendar, kid," and I laughed along, because laughing along was free and arguing cost more than we had.

But here's what I noticed, and I've been turning it over ever since: Hazel didn't care. The old Hazel—the one I signed on with, the one who flinched at his own name—would have needed the story corrected, managed, survived. This one just shrugged. Let them laugh. The mountain had already taken everything worth taking; what remained was ours, and it wasn't much, and it was enough.

June started a chart on the workshop wall. Not routes, not forecasts—an actual chart of the monsoon season, rainfall totals, barometric drift, the ordinary bookkeeping of ordinary weather. She said it was for the fishing fleet's benefit. I think it was for Hazel's: proof, updated daily, that a man could watch the sky without waiting for it to punish him.

* * *

On the last evening of the rains—or what passes for the last; the monsoon doesn't end so much as lose interest—Ingrid came by the workshop. No contract this time. Just an umbrella she refused to open and a bottle of something amber that Dev produced glasses for before she'd finished knocking the water off her boots.

We sat around the stove while the rain ticked on the tin roof, and nobody talked about the ridge for a long time, and then Ingrid said, to no one in particular, "You know what I can't get over? He planned it. Before the collectors ever showed. Hazel made a plan he didn't share with me, and it was better than mine, and I have been insufferable about it ever since."

"You have," June said.

"It's the only dignified response available to me."

Hazel said nothing. He was looking up at the carabiner, the way he did sometimes—not often, just sometimes, the way you visit a grave you've finally stopped apologizing to. And I watched his face and saw something I'd spent a year learning to read on that other face of his, the public one, the guarded one, and never once seen there: nothing to hide.

"The passes will open in March," Ingrid said, into the quiet. "There's talk of a survey. Nothing dramatic. Weather stations, mapping. Honest instruments." A pause, the length of a woman deciding whether to say the next thing. "They'll want experienced people."

"We're busy," Dev said, grinning.

"Extremely," June said, not grinning, which from June meant the same thing.

Everyone looked at Hazel. He turned the glass in his hands once, twice, and I braced myself—for the flinch, the deflection, the old arithmetic of debt and distance.

Instead he said, "Ask me in spring."

And Ingrid smiled like a woman who had just been paid in full.

* * *

After they left, I stayed behind to bank the stove, the way Hazel trusts me to now—really trusts, checks-my-work-and-finds-it-good trusts, which took a year and a half and a mountain to earn and was worth both. The rain had softened to a drizzle, the town lights smeared gold down the hillside, and somewhere out past the breakwater the fleet rode at anchor, riding out the last of it.

I looked at the carabiner on its nail. J.R., worn nearly smooth. A man I never met, whose death built the silence his brother lived in for nine years, and whose brother had finally, at terrible cost, climbed out of it—not clean, not absolved, none of us were that—but out.

The mountain kept its secret. The drives are at the bottom of a crevasse under forty meters of seasonal ice, and the ridge will keep generating its impossible storm whether anyone profits from it or not, and that felt right to me in a way I couldn't yet argue for. Some knowledge is a load-bearing wall. You don't sell it. You live under it.

I banked the coals, hung up my jacket, and stepped out into the drizzle. Down the street, light still burned in the workshop window, and through it I could see Hazel at the bench, steady-handed, repairing a radar unit for a fisherman who'd pay him late and joke about it tomorrow, and above him the small rusted shape of the thing he'd carried up a mountain and carried home.

For the first time since I'd known him, Hazel Reyes wasn't watching the horizon.

He was watching his work.

And the rain fell on Salagdan like it falls anywhere—without malice, without memory, just weather, just water, just the world going on—and I stood in it a moment longer than I needed to, because I was twenty-three, and it was the end of the season, and I belonged to people who had chosen each other over everything the mountain and the money had offered instead.

Then I went inside, because there were units to patch by morning, and Hazel had taught me that the forecast you can act on is the only one worth having.
