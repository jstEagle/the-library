## 1. Miles, Not Years

Silas Villalobos measured his life in distances now, not years.

Three years since he'd walked out of his mother's house with a duffel bag and a grudge, and in that time he had covered — by his own careful arithmetic, kept in the back pages of his survey notebooks — four thousand one hundred and sixty kilometers on foot, another nine thousand by truck and mule and river barge, and one memorable six hundred by canoe when the road east of Puerto Hondo simply gave up and slid into the wet season's mud. He did not count days. Days accumulated into months, months into years, and years were the currency of people who stayed put, who had houses with gutters that needed cleaning and neighbors whose names they knew. Silas had none of those things. He had contour lines.

The highlands above the Río Claro basin were good country for it: steep enough to demand attention, empty enough to forgive it. For eleven weeks he had been running a contract survey for a hydroelectric consortium that wanted to know whether the upper valley could hold a reservoir without drowning three villages' worth of grazing land. The answer was no, but nobody paid him for answers, only for measurements, so he measured. He walked transects at first light, shot elevations until the heat made the air shimmer like water over glass, and in the evenings sat outside his tent with a tin cup of coffee going cold while the light drained off the ridgelines in long amber sheets.

It was honest work. It was also, if he was being truthful with himself — which he tried not to be after dark — a way of keeping moving fast enough that nothing could catch him.

Things still tried.

There was the letter that had followed him from the last job, forwarded twice, its envelope soft and gray from handling. He knew the handwriting before he touched it: small, precise, the letters of an engineer even though she was an archaeologist, every character built rather than written. *Mijo.* One word on the card inside. He had read it standing in a post office in San Fermin, read it four times, then folded it into the notebook where he kept the dead pages — the ones with mistakes crossed out — and told himself he would answer when the survey ended.

He would not answer when the survey ended. They both knew it. That was the arrangement they had arrived at without ever discussing it: she sent word, he carried it, and the carrying was its own kind of conversation, the only kind either of them could survive.

And there were the calls. The consortium maintained a radio at their field office down in Tres Arroyos, and twice a month the operator would flag him down with a message slip. *Your mother telephoned the San Fermin exchange again. She asks if you are well. She asks you to call collect.* Silas would take the slip, thank the operator, and later feed it to his cook fire along with the day's packaging, watching the paper curl and go to ash. It wasn't cruelty. It was triage. If he let himself hear her voice — that voice that had taught him to read animal sign before he could read words, that had gone cold as river stone the night he'd accused her of what he'd accused her of — he would have to say something, and everything he might say was either a lie or a wound.

So: miles. He gave himself miles instead. Four thousand one hundred and sixty, and counting.

* * *

On the morning the telegram came, the sky over the highlands was doing something strange.

Silas noticed it before anything else, because weather was the first language he'd ever learned. The clouds had come up from the southeast overnight in a low, bruised shelf, and the air had that pressed-in quality he associated with the lowlands in monsoon season — heavy, expectant, the whole atmosphere holding its breath. Wrong for this altitude, wrong for this month. The birds were quiet in a way that wasn't peace.

He ate breakfast watching the ridge to the south, where the cloud bank sat like something waiting to be introduced. Then he shouldered his theodolite case and walked the morning transect anyway, because a man who stopped working every time the sky looked thoughtful would never work at all.

The line ran along a spur above the Río Claro's headwaters, through stands of aliso with bark the color of old bone. He set his stations, shot his angles, wrote his numbers in the small square hand that everyone who'd ever seen his notebooks said was exactly like his mother's. He hated that they said it. He hated more that they were right.

He was halfway back to camp at midday when he saw the mule coming up the trail.

It was the consortium's pack mule, and leading it was Tomás Iriarte, the field clerk from Tres Arroyos, a round, sweating man who had never in eleven weeks climbed higher than the second switchback and clearly resented being asked to now. Silas watched him laboring up the spur and felt the particular dread that experienced men feel when someone comes looking for them in the middle of nowhere. Nobody climbs to you with good news. Good news waits. Bad news hires a mule.

"Villalobos." Iriarte reached him, wheezing, and fumbled inside his jacket. "Came in on the morning wire. Marked urgent. Marked personal."

"I don't take personal wires."

"It's got your name on it twice." Iriarte held out the envelope the way men hold out things they don't want to touch themselves. "And the operator said the sender called the office directly after. A woman. Government, by the sound of her. She wants confirmation you received it."

Silas took the envelope. Heavy stock, official watermark, the franking of the national survey authority — not the hydroelectric consortium, not any of the outfits he contracted for. The government. In three years of walking away, the government had never once spent money finding him, and he had counted on that the way other men counted on gravity.

He opened it there on the trail, because not opening it would have been worse.

The telegram was brief. Telegrams always were; brevity was how institutions hid the size of what they were saying.

*GOVERNMENT SURVEY TEAM ELEVEN PERSONNEL MISSING VERRAZA VALLEY STOP LAST CONTACT NINE DAYS AGO STOP CAMP FOUND INTACT NO SIGN OF MOVEMENT STOP SEARCH COORDINATOR ILSA REYES REQUESTS VILLALOBOS BY NAME STOP REPEATS BY NAME STOP RESPOND THROUGH NEAREST STATION STOP*

Silas read it twice. The trail seemed to tilt very slightly under his boots.

The Verraza. He knew the name the way you know the name of a place from childhood stories — the drowned river valley two hundred kilometers north, the one the government had been mapping for a decade in anticipation of the dam project that would put it all under forty feet of green water. He'd never worked there. But he knew the terrain reports, because reading terrain reports was what he did instead of sleeping, and the Verraza was famous among surveyors: a floodplain so flat and silted that it erased tracks within hours, a river that braided and unbraided like something alive, a landscape that lied to strangers and tested everyone.

Eleven people missing in country like that. Nine days. Camp intact, no sign of movement — which meant either the terrain had swallowed the evidence, or someone had arranged for there to be none.

And a search coordinator named Reyes who had asked for him *by name.*

He didn't know any Ilsa Reyes. Which meant she knew him — knew his reputation, or worse, knew his history, knew whose son he was and what he could do with a stretch of broken ground. People who wanted Silas Villalobos specifically generally wanted one of two things: the tracker, or the son. Both roads led back to the same woman, and he had spent three years building distance between himself and that woman, kilometer by kilometer, like laying a wall.

"Bad?" Iriarte asked.

"They lost eleven people."

Iriarte whistled low. "In the Verraza? That's the dam survey, isn't it? My cousin said—" He caught Silas's expression and stopped. "You're not going."

"I'm not going."

"You just got here. Well — eleven weeks ago. You've got six weeks left on the contract. The consortium—"

"The consortium can bill me." Silas folded the telegram along its creases, precisely, the way his mother folded maps. "Tell your office I'm taking the rest of my pay in equipment credit. I'll need rope, lamp oil, and whatever tinned meat you've got too much of."

Iriarte stared at him. "You just said—"

"I know what I said." Silas looked down at the telegram in his hand, at the words *BY NAME* repeated like a knock on a door. "That was before I finished thinking."

But that wasn't true either, and he knew it. Nothing about this decision had waited for thought. The moment he'd read *VERRAZA VALLEY*, some part of him that he'd believed safely buried had stood up inside his chest and started walking, and the rest of him was just trying to keep pace.

* * *

He burned the telegram that evening, but he made himself wait until full dark to do it, and he made himself read it one last time first.

Camp was pitched in a hollow below the spur, sheltered from the wind that came off the high passes at night. He cooked rice and dried fish, drank his coffee, checked his instruments out of habit — theodolite clean, levels true, the brass calipers wrapped in oilcloth in the bottom of the instrument case. His calipers. Bought new in San Fermin three years ago, because he'd left the old pair behind in his mother's house along with everything else he couldn't carry, and a surveyor without calipers was half a surveyor, and he had refused to be half of anything ever since.

The fire settled. The strange southern cloud bank had swallowed the stars to the horizon, and the air still had that held-breath weight, and somewhere far off — so far he might have imagined it — thunder spoke once, like a clearing of a throat.

Silas unfolded the telegram across his knee.

Eleven people. Camp found intact. No footprints beyond the perimeter — he supplied that detail himself, filling in the gaps the way the wire's compression demanded, because *no sign of movement* in a government dispatch meant exactly that, meant tents staked and food cooked and instruments laid out and eleven human beings gone as if the ground had opened and closed behind them. And the Verraza floodplain didn't open. It recorded. Every step, every drag, every boat pulled to a bank wrote itself into the silt for anyone who could read it.

Someone had to go read it. Someone had already decided that someone was him.

*By name.* He turned the phrase over. There were perhaps four trackers in the country with the reputation to be requested by name for a disappearance in difficult country, and Silas had spent three years making sure he wasn't one of them — taking jobs that asked for endurance but not attention, measuring reservoir basins instead of hunting for what moved through them. He had been careful. Careful as a man erasing his own tracks, and he knew better than anyone how hard that was to do completely.

Which meant Reyes hadn't found him through reputation. She'd found him through records, through contracts, through the boring administrative skeleton of his life — or someone had handed her his name. And the only person alive who understood both halves of him, the tracker and the son, well enough to recommend him for a job like this—

No. He shut that door. He'd gotten good at shutting that door; it was the one skill the last three years had actually taught him.

He fed the telegram to the fire corner-first, holding it until the flame took the paper and the words went orange and then black — *ELEVEN PERSONNEL*, *NO SIGN OF MOVEMENT*, *BY NAME* — and the ash lifted off in the draft and scattered toward the dark southern sky. It was a ritual he'd performed with letters before. It had never once worked. Burning a thing didn't unsend it; it just meant you carried it in a different pocket.

He banked the fire, crawled into his tent, and lay down in his bedroll with his boots on, which was itself an admission, because a man planning to stay asleep takes his boots off.

Sleep did not come. Sleep had competitors.

His mind kept walking the Verraza without his permission — laying out the floodplain from the terrain reports, the braided channels, the oxbow lakes, the silt flats that went to the horizon like a page waiting for handwriting. Eleven people had written something there, or had it written for them, and the monsoon was coming — that was the other thing the wire hadn't said but every surveyor knew: the Verraza had maybe weeks left before the rains turned the whole valley into one brown sea, erasing the page entirely. Whatever story was written in that mud had a deadline.

And underneath the professional arithmetic, quieter and worse, ran the other current. Because *by name* wouldn't leave him alone. Because a government coordinator who knew to ask for Silas Villalobos knew things about him, and knowledge traveled in this country along certain routes, and one of those routes began in a house he'd sworn never to enter again, with a woman who folded maps the way he folded telegrams.

Maybe it was nothing. Maybe Reyes had simply done her homework. Maybe the Verraza was just a job — eleven strangers, a vanished camp, a clock ticking toward flood season, and a fee that would keep him in boots and coffee for a year.

He rolled onto his side. Outside, the thunder spoke again, closer now, and the first fat drops hit the canvas overhead — rain out of the south, weeks early, falling on ground that had no business feeling monsoon-weight yet. The whole year was arriving ahead of schedule, as if the season itself had heard the news.

Silas lay awake listening to it, and somewhere past midnight he admitted what he'd known since the trail, since the first line of the wire: he was going. He had been going since he read the word *VERRAZA*. The burning, the boots, the sleeplessness — all of it was just the body catching up to a decision the blood had already made.

Restlessness curdled slowly into dread as the rain thickened. Because if someone had given Reyes his name — if his mother's hand was anywhere inside this — then the past hadn't been chasing him at all these three years.

It had simply known where he was going next.

He rose before dawn, struck camp in the gray light, and started north.
