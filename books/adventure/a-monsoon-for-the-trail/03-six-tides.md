## 3. Six Tides

Ilsa Reyes had learned to measure disasters the way other people measured weather: by the weight of the air before they arrived. The Verraza valley had been pressing down on her for three days now — a low, humid pressure that made the canvas of the command tent sag and made the coffee taste like river water no matter how strong she brewed it. She stood at the map table with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, her shirt dark at the spine with sweat, and watched the man she'd summoned from the highlands read the terrain laid out in front of him.

He didn't look at the photographs first. That was the thing she noticed, the thing that told her the telegram had been worth the expense and the delay. Most men — most *trained* men — went straight for the images of the abandoned camp, hungry for the human drama of it, the empty tents, the cold food. Silas Villalobos went instead to the contour sheets. He put one finger on the floodplain and dragged it slowly downstream, following the river's old channel as it wandered through the drowned farmland, and his lips moved slightly, as if he were reading.

"Eleven people," he said. It wasn't a question.

"Eleven. Government survey team, contracted through the Ministry of Waterways. They were mapping the submerged topography ahead of the seasonal closure." Ilsa kept her voice level, official, the voice she used when the truth underneath it was anything but level. "Last radio contact was nine days ago. Routine check-in, everything nominal. The next morning, nothing."

"And the camp?"

"You've seen the photos."

"I want to hear you say it."

She looked at him properly then. He was younger than his file suggested — or rather, he was exactly as young as his file suggested, twenty-nine, and it was only the way he held himself that made her think otherwise. Weathered without being old. Watchful without being nervous. There was something coiled in him, some tension that never quite resolved into movement, and she recognized it because she'd seen it in soldiers coming home from long postings, in her own father after the mines closed: a body that had been braced against something for so long it had forgotten any other posture.

"Tents staked," she said. "Food cooked and half-eaten. Instruments deployed and calibrated. Boots inside the tents. Eleven bedrolls that show sleeping bodies. And not one footprint beyond the perimeter stakes. Not one. The ground around that camp is undisturbed mud, Mr. Villalobos. Soft enough to hold a print for weeks. It's as if eleven people walked out of their own lives and left their shadows behind."

His finger stopped on the map. For a moment he said nothing, and Ilsa felt the silence take on texture — not confusion, not doubt. Something closer to grief, though she couldn't have said why.

"Show me where the fleet is," he said.

* * *

The Meridian Salvage Consortium had arrived on the river two days after the team went silent, which was either remarkable efficiency or damning foreknowledge, depending on how much faith you had in coincidence. Ilsa had very little. She walked Villalobos out of the command tent and up the levee path to where the valley opened below them, and there it was: the Meridian flotilla strung out along the lower reaches like a rosary of steel beads. Three dredging barges. A crane ship whose boom stood against the sky like the mast of some impossible cathedral. Tenders running back and forth between them, kicking up white wakes in the brown water.

"They filed salvage claims yesterday," she said. "Blanket claims. Anything of pre-colonial origin within the survey corridor, they assert prior discovery rights under the riverbed commerce statutes. Their lawyers are already in the capital filing injunctions against our own ministry. We are, at this moment, legally arguing with them about who owns things we haven't found yet."

"And what did they find?" Villalobos asked quietly. "Or think they found?"

Ilsa hesitated. This was the part she hadn't put in the telegram, the part she'd flown in person to deliver, because paper could be intercepted and this could not be. "Their sonar is better than ours," she admitted. "We know that much. Two nights ago one of their tenders ran a grid pattern over a section of the floodplain four kilometers downstream of the missing team's camp. Systematic. Professional. They weren't searching for survivors, Mr. Villalobos. Survivors float. You search for survivors upstream of where they vanished, along the banks. They were mapping the bottom."

Villalobos watched the tenders for a long moment. His face gave away almost nothing, but his hands — she noticed his hands — had gone still in a way that seemed deliberate, the stillness of a man holding something down.

"The monsoon," he said.

"Six tides." She said it plainly because there was no kind way to say it. "The forecast models came in this morning. The pressure trough over the cordillera has deepened faster than anyone projected. First significant rainfall reaches the headwaters in six tides — call it seventy-two hours, maybe less. After that, the river comes up. Not gradually. The Verraza floods like a bathtub filling; the gorge narrows above the valley and everything funnels down at once. Within thirty hours of the first rain, the entire floodplain will be under six meters of moving water. Silt load so heavy you couldn't see your hand in front of your face at two meters' depth. Current fast enough to sweep a diver into the gorge system below."

"And stays flooded how long?"

"A year. Maybe more, if the rains run heavy. Everything below the new waterline is unreachable until next season. Every artifact, every instrument, every—" she paused, choosing the word carefully— "*body* — sealed under the floodline until the river decides otherwise."

Villalobos turned from the view. In the flat light of the overcast afternoon his eyes were a pale, unreadable gray-green, the color of the river itself, and Ilsa thought: *he has his mother's eyes.* She'd met Dr. Marisol Villalobos once, years ago, at a conference where the great archaeologist had held a ballroom full of skeptics spellbound with slides of a ceramic vessel pulled from the Verraza silt. The same pale gaze. The same refusal to be hurried.

"So we have six tides," he said, "to find eleven missing people, determine whether they're alive, recover whatever Meridian is willing to kill for, and beat a salvage fleet with better equipment and legal cover to a site we haven't located yet." He said it without inflection, an inventory, and somehow that was worse than if he'd shouted. "Is that the assignment?"

"That's the assignment."

"And my authority?"

"Whatever you need. Boats, personnel, access to every scrap of data we have. You report directly to me and I'll absorb whatever political damage follows." She meant it. She'd already written the letter resigning her commission, folded it once, and put it in the inner pocket of her field jacket, because Ilsa Reyes did not begin operations she wasn't prepared to end honestly. "What I can't give you is time. Time is the one currency the river doesn't honor."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon in the command tent while the light failed and the generators coughed and the rain, when it finally began, came down soft and persistent on the canvas like fingers testing a lock. Ilsa spread the materials out piece by piece, watching him the way she'd watch a surgeon work — not interfering, cataloguing.

He read the sonar overlays first, all of them, including the ones her own analysts had flagged as noise. He spent ten minutes on a stretch of riverbank that everyone else had dismissed and asked her three questions about the reed beds there — when they'd last been surveyed, what the water level had been at last contact, whether the livestock grazing patterns in the aerial photos predated or postdated the disappearance. She answered what she could. When she couldn't answer, he wrote the questions down in a small waterproof notebook with a pencil stub, in handwriting so small and precise it looked typeset.

"Your team searched the banks," he said. "On foot."

"Four days. Sixty personnel. Grid pattern, both banks, twelve kilometers downstream of the camp."

"And found nothing."

"Not a track. Not a fiber. Nothing."

He set the pencil down. "That's not possible," he said, and there was no arrogance in it, only the flat certainty of a man stating that water flows downhill. "Eleven people don't move through wet alluvial soil without leaving sign. Not unless they leave by water, and even then the embarkation point shows. Someone cleaned those banks, Ms. Reyes, or someone wants us to believe they were cleaned, and both possibilities mean the same thing." He looked up at her. "This wasn't a vanishing. It was a removal. Somebody took your team somewhere specific, and they did it carefully enough that they knew trackers would come looking."

The word *removal* sat in the air between them. Ilsa had been using softer words in her reports — *disappearance*, *loss of contact* — words that kept the ministry's lawyers comfortable and her own superiors unalarmed. But she had stood in that empty camp herself, on the third day, and touched the cold coffee pot, and she had known. She'd known the way you know a house has been robbed even before you find the open window: the quality of the silence was wrong.

"There's one more thing," she said. "And I need you to understand why I'm showing it to you privately."

She took the evidence case from the lockbox beneath the chart table — a battered aluminum case with a government seal cracked diagonally across the lid — and opened it on the table between them. Inside, on grey foam, lay the personal effects recovered from the camp: watches, a pipe, reading glasses, a woman's hair comb. And at the center, alone in its own cutout, a brass caliper.

It was a beautiful instrument, old-fashioned, machined decades ago, its jaws worn bright from use. The handle had been broken once and repaired — repaired badly, lovingly, wrapped in waxed linen thread in a crisscross pattern that no manufacturer would use and only one person Ilsa had ever heard of would bother with.

Silas Villalobos went very still.

She watched him look at it. She watched the color leave his face and return, watched his jaw tighten and loosen, watched him do the arithmetic of recognition — because it was arithmetic, she realized, not emotion, or rather emotion arriving through the cold gate of arithmetic. He knew the object. He knew it the way you know your own front door.

"Where," he said, and his voice had dropped half an octave, roughened, "exactly. Was this found."

"In the lead surveyor's instrument case. Closed. Latched. As if it belonged there." She hesitated. "Mr. Villalobos, does it belong there?"

"No." The answer came instantly. "That's my mother's caliper. She's had it forty years. She repaired the handle herself when I was nine — I held the lamp while she did it. She wouldn't lend it to her own director." He stared at the brass as though it might speak. "If it was in that case, she put it there. Or someone wanted it to look that way."

"Your mother," Ilsa said carefully, "has been unreachable for eleven months. Her institute says she's on sabbatical. Her apartment in the capital was cleared out in March. No forwarding address. I requested her file the day your team went silent, because her name was on the original survey proposal — did you know that? The Verraza project was built on her preliminary work. Her methodology. Her maps." She let that settle. "And now her caliper is sitting in a dead man's instrument case in a valley she helped discover, eleven days after eleven people evaporated from it."

The rain thickened against the canvas. Somewhere outside, a generator stuttered and caught. Villalobos reached toward the caliper and stopped, his fingers hovering a centimeter above the brass, not touching it — the gesture of a man approaching something that might be hot, or holy, or both.

"She's alive," he said. It wasn't hope in his voice. It was worse than hope. It was conviction, arriving fully formed, the way convictions do when they've been waiting a long time in the dark for permission. "You don't abandon this. People don't abandon this. She'd sooner bury it with herself." His hand closed into a fist above the foam. "Which means either she was here eleven days ago—"

"—or someone is using her to bring you here," Ilsa finished. "Yes. I've considered that. I flew to the highlands and put a telegram in your hands anyway, because whoever did this made one mistake." She leaned across the table. "They assumed the tracker they needed wouldn't notice the caliper. Or they assumed he'd notice and stay away. Either way, they don't know you. I'm betting on that, Mr. Villalobos. I'm betting that the one person in this country who can read this river is also the one person they couldn't predict."

For a long moment he said nothing. Outside, the river moved past in the dark, broad and patient and full of everything it had taken, and the first real gusts of the coming monsoon bent the palms along the levee like women wringing laundry.

"Six tides," Silas Villalobos said at last. He picked up the caliper, weighed it in his palm as if measuring something invisible, and slipped it into his jacket pocket. "Then we'd better not waste the first one talking."

Ilsa was already reaching for the tide charts when the radio crackled to life on the side table — the watch post downstream, a young operator's voice tight with adrenaline, reporting lights on the water: the Meridian crane ship had weighed anchor and was moving upriver, against regulation, against the closure order, straight toward the drowned heart of the valley.
