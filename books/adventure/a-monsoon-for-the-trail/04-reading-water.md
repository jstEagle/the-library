## 4. Reading Water

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and Silas was grateful for it, though he would never have said so aloud. Rain erased things. It was the great equalizer of the trail, the solvent that dissolved the difference between a careful man and a careless one, between a fugitive and a lost hiker. But rain also *wrote*, if you knew its hand — and the storm that had come through the Verraza valley the night the survey team disappeared had written a great deal.

He crouched at the edge of the camp perimeter, boots in the mud, and let himself go still. This was the part people never understood about tracking, the part they thought was mystical or genetic or some trick of sharp eyes. It wasn't. It was patience. It was letting your own noise drain out of you until the ground's voice came through — the way his mother used to say you couldn't hear a letter until you stopped talking over it.

*Stop talking over it,* she'd said, twenty years ago, kneeling beside him in a dry wash in the Chiriquí highlands, her finger hovering over a line of disturbed gravel. *The ground is always telling you something. Your job is to shut up long enough to read it.*

He shut up now. He read.

* * *

The camp itself told him the first thing: nobody left in a hurry. Eleven tents staked properly, guy-lines tensioned with the even, habitual care of people who expected to sleep in them again. The cook fire had been raked and banked, not scattered — someone had planned to return to it. The mess tins were washed and stacked. A pair of reading glasses sat folded on top of a field journal, weighted against wind by a smooth river stone placed there deliberately, the way you'd mark your place in a book you meant to finish.

Eleven people had gone to bed expecting morning.

Silas walked the perimeter slowly, hands clasped behind his back like a man strolling a garden, though every step was a question and the mud answered each one. The ground around the camp was churned — but churned *evenly*. That was wrong. Eleven people moving through soft floodplain soil for days would leave a hierarchy of prints: deep ones where weight concentrated, shallow ones at the edges, drag marks from gear, scuffs where someone turned sharply. This was uniform. Combed, almost.

Someone had walked the camp's edges after the fact, deliberately blurring the record.

He found the tool that did it within ten minutes: a length of sapling, stripped of bark, one end worn smooth and muddy. A poor man's rake. Whoever carried it had made a circuit of the camp — Silas counted four circuits, actually, overlapping arcs of artificial disturbance laid down after the last real footprints, because in two places the fake texture sat *on top of* a boot heel impression, and nowhere did a boot sit on top of the combing. Sequence. The most basic sentence in the language of ground: this happened, then this.

His pulse picked up. Not fear. Something older and less comfortable — the feeling of a lock yielding under a key cut years ago for a different door.

*You're good at this,* his mother had told him once, and it hadn't been praise. It had been a warning, delivered in the tone she reserved for observations she wished weren't true. *You're good at this, mijo, and it will keep you moving when you should stay.*

He knelt at the eastern boundary, where the combing ended, and looked past it.

Beyond the fake texture, the true ground resumed, and the true ground said: eleven people had walked out of this camp on their own feet. He could pick out at least seven distinct boot patterns in the overlap — different sizes, different wear, one left print deeper than its right, a limping man. They'd moved in a loose group, unhurried, toward the river. No bound wrists dragging between the prints. No stumble-steps, no wide panicked spacing, no sign anyone had been forced at speed. Whatever had gotten them out of their tents at night and down to the water, it hadn't been violence. Not here, anyway.

It had been authority. Or the shape of it.

* * *

The river ran brown and swollen with the storm's leavings, maybe eighty meters across here, fast enough to carry whole trees. On the far bank, the drowned farmland began — old cane fields gone to marsh, fence lines standing in a meter of water like the ribs of something long dead. The government maps called this the Verraza floodplain. The people who'd lived there before the dam project upstream called it nothing anymore, because there were no people, only water learning to be a lake.

Silas worked the near bank downstream from where the boot trails converged.

This was the discipline his mother had drilled into him hardest, back when he was fifteen and restless and wanted to chase every interesting line at once: *Follow the water's margin, not the water. Rivers edit everything, but they edit along their edges first.* So he moved along the seam where mud met current, three steps, stop, read, three steps, stop, read — and forty minutes downstream of the camp, the seam gave him what he was looking for.

Broken reeds.

Not broken by current — current breaks reeds all leaning the same direction, combed flat downstream like hair. These were broken in a trampled patch, stems snapped at angles, some stamped into the mud flat, and the patch was roughly boat-shaped. Six meters, maybe seven. Big enough for eleven people if they sat close, which they had, because along the landward edge of the patch the mud held the ghost-prints of knees and palms — people steadying themselves as they climbed aboard, in the dark, quietly.

And here was the detail that took Silas's breath and held it a moment: the reeds at the patch's downstream tip were bent *upstream*. Curled back against the current's grain, still holding the memory of a push-off. Someone had poled away from this bank hard enough, in the dark, to bend living cane backward.

They'd left by boat. At night. Under their own power, mostly, and without a sound worth remembering.

He stood in the shallows a long time, water seeping cold through his boot seams, and let the picture assemble itself the way a photograph develops: men going tent to tent with a lantern hooded low, voices kept level, the particular calm of people delivering orders they expect to be obeyed. Eleven surveyors dressing, packing nothing, walking in loose file to the river. A boat waiting. A pole pushing off into the current. And afterward, a man with a sapling rake, walking the camp's edges in patient circles, erasing the scene until it read like a vanishing.

No shots fired. No struggle. No scene.

Somebody had wanted them *gone*, not dead — or at least wanted them gone quietly enough that death, if it came later, would happen somewhere the ground couldn't testify.

* * *

By midmorning the sky had begun to stack its gray plates again, monsoon weather queuing up over the western ridges, and Ilsa Reyes found him knee-deep in a side channel half a kilometer below the embarkation point, studying a silt line with the intensity other men reserved for scripture.

"You've been out here since before light," she said. It wasn't a question. She stood on the bank with a radio in one hand and a face that hadn't slept either.

"Your team didn't vanish," Silas said.

He heard her breath catch — small, quickly mastered, but there. "Say that again."

"They walked out of camp on their own feet. Seven distinct boot patterns minimum, probably more under the overlap. One of them favors his left leg. They went down to the river in a group, unhurried, and boarded a boat about six, seven meters — big enough for all eleven. Somebody poled them off the bank and took them downstream." He straightened, wiped his hands on his thighs, and pointed back upriver. "Afterward, one person spent the better part of an hour raking the camp perimeter with a stripped sapling to blur the tracks. Four passes. They missed two spots where their fake texture overlaps original prints — sequence gives them away. They were thorough, but they were working in the dark and tired."

Ilsa came down the bank carefully, a woman suddenly aware the ground might be listening. "Taken by boat," she said slowly. "Then they could be anywhere."

"Downstream." Silas turned to face the current, the drowned fields, the gray horizon where the river widened toward country none of the maps bothered to detail. "Whoever ran this didn't want a firefight at the camp. No shots, no blood, no witnesses' bodies. They wanted the team removed like furniture — quietly, cleanly, while the valley emptied for the storm. That takes organization. Local knowledge. And a reason to make sure nobody finds eleven government surveyors until it's too late to matter."

"Too late for what?"

"That," Silas said, "is above my pay grade. But I'll tell you what's not." He crouched again and touched the silt line along the channel bank — a pale band of fine sediment lying atop darker mud, deposited by the storm surge, unbroken except where he'd just disturbed it. "See this? Storm laid it down after the boat left. Nothing has crossed this channel since. Your eleven people went downstream, and whatever happened to them happened down there—" he nodded at the widening river, the drowned country, the gathering weather— "and the clock on finding any of it alive is the same clock as the monsoon."

Ilsa was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed — flatter, more careful, the voice of a woman recalculating how much she could trust the man she'd sent for. "The Meridian fleet is already moving upriver. Two barges sighted yesterday below the gorge. If your boat went downstream—"

"Then it went toward them." Silas felt the words settle into place with an ugly click. "Or was taken by someone who answers to them. Either way, the search area isn't a circle anymore. It's a line." He looked up at her. "And we're losing it tide by tide."

She studied him for a long moment, this gaunt man standing in the water like he'd grown there, and something in her expression shifted — the skepticism draining out of it, replaced by the wary relief of someone handing a heavy load to shoulders that look like they can hold it.

"The coordinator in the capital said you were the only one who could read this valley," she said. "I confess I wondered."

"Wondered what?"

"If three years away rusts a man."

Silas looked down at his own hands, mud to the wrist, steady as stone. Three years of measuring roads for men who'd never walk them, of choosing contracts by how far they landed from home, of telling himself the skill was just a trade like any other, nothing sacred in it, nothing inherited. And now here he stood in his mother's river, in his mother's mud, speaking her language fluently — and for the first time since he'd walked out her door, the ground under him felt like it was holding him up instead of sliding away.

"It doesn't rust," he said. "It waits."

He turned downstream, toward the drowned fields and the distant smudge of smoke on the horizon that might have been a barge's galley fire, and the river ran on ahead of him, carrying its secrets toward the sea — unless he could outrun the water and read them out of it first.
