## 5. The Fleet on the Horizon

Tomas Beck had a theory about rivers, and he shared it freely with anyone who would listen, which was most people, because Tomas Beck made listening feel like a privilege.

"A river is just a road that lies about itself," he'd say, standing at the rail of the *Meridian Grace* with his hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring his own property. "It tells you it's going one way while it's cutting another. It shows you sandbars that aren't there and hides the ones that are. You can't trust a river. But you can hire someone who's learned its language." And then he'd smile — white teeth, easy creases around the eyes, the smile of a man who had never once been refused anything he actually wanted — and whoever he was talking to would laugh, and by morning they'd have signed something.

The theory was sound. The smile was the product. Everything else was inventory.

Beck stood at the rail now as the fleet came upriver through the drowned country, six vessels in loose formation, riding low with equipment. Behind them the water stretched brown and flat to the horizon, broken here and there by the crowns of trees that had spent their whole lives on hillsides and were now, abruptly, islands. A church bell tower stood in six feet of water two miles back, its cross tilted, patient. Somewhere under all that brown water was a valley that had farmed corn for four hundred years, and somewhere under the silt the river had laid down over that corn was a ship older than any of the countries whose flags flew on the boats now hunting her.

Forty feet down, if the survey data was right. Intact. Loaded.

Beck had read the report eleven times. He'd read it in the seaplane coming up from the coast, read it again over dinner, read it a final time at three in the morning with a cigar burning down between his fingers while the pilot slept. Pre-colonial trading vessel, hull preserved in anoxic mud, cargo manifest unknown but consistent with a full outbound run. Ceramics, likely. Metalwork. Possibly textiles, though those never survived unless the gods were feeling generous. Enough to fund Meridian's next decade. Enough to rewrite three chapters of every textbook printed in the last century. Enough, if handled correctly, to make certain very patient men in the capital extremely grateful, and gratitude in the capital was a currency that appreciated faster than gold.

And enough, if handled incorrectly, to end careers. Several of them. His own included, if he let it come to that.

Which was why his orders were written the way they were. Reach the site first. Secure the salvage claim. And bury any official report — the phrase had been used in the briefing without irony, which Beck appreciated; he liked working for people who didn't insult him with euphemisms. There were eleven government surveyors somewhere ahead of them who had found something they weren't supposed to find, and the last communication from that team had come nine days ago, and no one at the agency seemed particularly eager to explain why.

Beck wasn't paid to ask why. He was paid to arrive before the questions did.

"Captain says we'll make the confluence by dark," said Odile Ferran, appearing at his elbow the way she always did, silent as gull droppings on a railing. She was his operations chief, a compact woman of fifty with forearms like mooring line and a face that gave away nothing except when she wanted it to. "Two of the barges are lagging. The *Corvina*'s pump is acting up again."

"The *Corvina*'s pump has been acting up since Cartagena. Tell her engineer that if it's still acting up tomorrow I'll act him up personally."

"He'll be moved by the poetry."

"They usually are." Beck turned from the rail. Below them, the deck of the *Grace* was organized chaos done properly: divers checking regulators, crane crews greasing cable, a pair of hydrographers hunched over a sonar console in the shaded chart room with the blinds half-drawn against the heat. Good people. Expensive people. People who had learned not to wonder too loudly about what was in the sealed manifest pouches or why the fleet's radio schedule skipped certain frequencies. Loyalty, in Beck's experience, was mostly a payroll function, with a small supplement paid in the currency of never letting your people be surprised. Surprises made men think. Thinking made men talk.

"How long to the survey grid?" he asked.

"Fifty hours if the channels hold. Less if the river keeps dropping." Odile consulted nothing; she kept the river in her head the way other people kept phone numbers. "The floodplain's braiding out past the second marker buoys. Channels that were eight feet deep last season are showing eighteen inches. We may need to lighten the barges or shift cargo to the shallower hulls."

"Do both. I don't want to lose a day to plumbing." He paused. "And the agency? Any movement on their side?"

"That's the interesting part." Odile produced a folded sheet of paper — actual paper, because Odile trusted paper the way she trusted nothing with an antenna. "Our friend in the logistics office sent the daily traffic summary. The search coordination office filed a request yesterday. Personnel requisition. They've brought in an outside specialist."

Beck took the page and read it twice. It was bureaucratic boilerplate, names redacted, but the shape of it told the story: a tracking consultant, contracted through the regional office, authorized for riverine terrain work. Deployed to the Verraza valley. Already in country.

Someone was reading the ground where eleven people had stopped existing.

"Well," Beck said. "Isn't that tidy."

"You're pleased," Odile observed. It wasn't a question. She had worked for him nine years and had catalogued his smiles the way a lepidopterist catalogues specimens, and she knew this one — the slow one, the one that started in the eyes — meant he'd found something useful in what everyone else would call a problem.

"I'm pleased," Beck confirmed, "because a man who reads ground is a man who finds things. And right now, Ms. Ferran, finding things is the entire industry we're in." He handed the page back. "Find out who he is. Name, history, what he's been doing for the last few years. If our friends in the logistics office are worth the retainer, they'll already know. If they don't, that tells me something too."

"And if he gets close?"

Beck looked out at the water again. The light was going amber and long, laying gold across the flooded treetops, and it was genuinely beautiful, this drowned world, beautiful the way a fire is beautiful from across a valley. Somewhere out there a man was crouched over a dead campsite reading silt like scripture, and somewhere under the silt lay a fortune, and somewhere between the two of them was a story that ended with only one man walking away rich.

"If he gets close," Beck said pleasantly, "then he gets close to us. And everything that comes close to this fleet becomes either crew or cargo. That's the whole business model, Odile. Has been since the beginning."

* * *

He held court at dinner the way he did every night, because dinners on the *Meridian Grace* were not meals but instruments. The long table in the aft saloon seated fourteen, and Beck filled it with a rotating cast — engineers, dive masters, the hydrographers, occasionally a barge captain pulled off duty — and he fed them well and listened more than he talked, and by the time the coffee came he knew things about his own operation that no memo would ever tell him. Who was frightened. Who was greedy. Who had noticed the sealed manifests and said nothing, and who had noticed and said something, quietly, to the wrong person.

Tonight the table held the dive team leads, the sonar chief, and a young lawyer from the coast office named Prasad who was aboard to handle claims documentation and who laughed at Beck's jokes with the desperate precision of a man who had correctly identified which jokes mattered.

"So the priest says to me," Beck was saying, gesturing with his spoon, "he says, Mr. Beck, do you fear death? And I tell him, Father, I fear paperwork. Death is quick and it's honest about what it wants. Paperwork takes years and lies the entire time." Laughter around the table, real from some, performed from others. Beck collected both. "But I'll tell you what I do respect. I respect the dead. Every wreck I've ever worked, I've treated her like a grave first and a bank second. You show respect to the dead, they don't haunt you. Show disrespect—" he spread his hands, "—well. Ask anyone who's worked a site with me. Nothing goes missing. No shortcuts on the safety lines. We do it clean."

Prasad nodded earnestly. One of the dive leads, a scarred veteran named Okonkwo, looked at his plate and said nothing, and Beck noted the silence and filed it. Okonkwo had worked the Marrow Deep job two seasons ago, and the Marrow Deep job had a widow in it, and widows were the kind of thing a careful man remembered even when his employer preferred forgetting. Not a problem yet. A thing to keep smooth. Beck was very good at keeping things smooth; it was most of what corruption actually was, day to day — not violence, not drama, just an endless, cheerful, tireless smoothing.

After dinner he walked the deck alone, as he did, checking lights, checking lines, exchanging a word with the watch. The night was thick and warm and smelled of diesel and river rot and, faintly, of rain that hadn't arrived yet but was thinking about it. Out on the black water, fish jumped, or something jumped. The drowned valley breathed around them.

In the chart room the sonar chief had left the day's runs displayed, and Beck stood over them for a long time. The floodplain rendered in greens and yellows, channels threading through it like veins in a leaf. And there, in the sector the government surveyors had been working when they stopped transmitting, a cluster of grid lines, dense and deliberate. They had been looking for something specific. They had found it, or nearly. And then eleven professionals with radios and satellite phones had become a rumor.

Beck tapped the grid with one finger. Whatever was down there, the agency had known about it before the survey team ever launched — that was the only reading that made sense, because agencies didn't send eleven people and a quarter-million dollars of equipment into a flooding valley on a fishing expedition. Someone upstairs had known. Someone upstairs had also, presumably, known what the team might find, and had decided after the fact that the finding was inconvenient. Hence the silence. Hence the requisition for a tracker who could pick up a trail that had been very carefully erased.

Which meant the erasure hadn't been clean. Which meant whoever had done it was hurried, or careless, or both. Hurried and careless people left trails. Trails could be followed. Trails could also, if you got ahead of them, be redirected.

He went to his cabin and poured two fingers of rum and sat at his desk with the fleet's personnel file open on the table — not the electronic one, the paper one, the one Odile maintained in a locked drawer and updated in pencil. Eleven surveyors' faces looked up at him, passport photos, names, next-of-kin. He'd studied them on the plane. Ordinary faces. Competent faces. Faces that had believed they worked for a government rather than for the men who owned the government's debts.

His satellite phone sat in its cradle, and beside it, weighted under a brass compass, a single sheet with a phone number written in his own hand. He was permitted one call per week to the capital, unrecorded, or so he'd been assured, in the tone of voice that meant recorded. He had not needed to use it yet. He wondered idly whether the eleven missing surveyors had been somebody's idea of tidying up, and whether he was being kept on a shorter leash than he liked to pretend, and decided, as he decided most nights, that the question changed nothing about the work. The work was the work. Get there first. Claim it. Bury the report. The rest was weather.

He was reaching to turn off the lamp when Odile knocked — two soft raps, her code for *not urgent but interesting.*

She came in and put a single typed page on his desk. "The tracker," she said. "Name's Silas Villalobos."

Beck read the name and felt something turn over gently in his chest, the way a large engine turns over, slow and heavy, before it catches.

Villalobos.

He knew the name the way everyone in this business knew it. Dr. Marisol Villalobos, the archaeologist, the woman who had pulled the San Telmo cache out of a hillside twenty-five years ago and rewritten the trade routes of an entire pre-colonial world, the woman whose name opened doors and closed investigations, the woman whose signature on an authentication letter was worth more than a court ruling. A legend. And legends, in Beck's long experience, were load-bearing structures: you didn't get to see what they were holding up until something knocked one down.

And her son — estranged, according to the thin file Odile had assembled, three years gone from the family name, formerly a trail surveyor himself, reportedly the best ground-reader in the region's civil corps before he walked away from it.

Beck leaned back in his chair and smiled, and this time there was no audience, so the smile was allowed to be what it actually was, which was hungry.

"Odile," he said softly, "do you know what my grandmother used to say about obstacles?"

"I try to avoid knowing things about your grandmother."

"She said an obstacle is just a resource standing in the wrong place." He tapped the page. "This man is going to follow the trail our friends tried to erase. He's going to follow it straight to whatever the survey team found, because that's what trackers do — they don't stop at the body, they go to the reason for the body. And when he gets there, he'll be tired, and alone, and officially employed by an agency that may or may not want him to succeed." He laced his fingers behind his head. "A man like that doesn't need to be stopped, Odile. He needs to be *hired.*"

"And if he won't be hired?"

"Then he's a resource in the wrong place, and we move him." Beck said it the way he said everything, warmly, reasonably, the voice of a man discussing freight schedules. "But they almost always say yes. Everyone has a price. The expensive ones just take longer to quote it."

Odile gathered her page and paused at the door. "There's one more thing. Small. Probably nothing."

"Probably-nothing things are my favorite kind."

"Our contact at the fuel depot downriver says a woman's been asking about our fleet. Buying passage on supply runs, asking which boats go where. Middle-aged. Keeps to herself. Pays cash." Odile shrugged. "Could be a journalist. Could be nobody."

"Description?"

"Tall. Gray coming in at the temples. Carries herself like she owns the deck she's standing on." Odile met his eyes. "And she asked about you by name."

Beck turned that over. A watcher. A rival. A wife of one of the missing, perhaps, following the same river he was. The water was getting crowded, and crowded water meant currents, and currents could be used.

"Let her ride," he said at last. "Watch her. Don't touch her. Anyone curious enough to buy passage toward us is curious for a reason, and reasons are valuable." He smiled again, and raised his glass an inch toward the dark window, toward the black river running south, toward whatever was waiting for all of them under forty feet of patient silt. "Besides. I do love an audience."

* * *

At dawn the fleet rounded the great bend below the confluence and saw, far ahead, a smudge of smoke rising from the government staging camp — thin, orderly, domestic. And beyond it, barely visible in the morning haze, the low green wall of the Verraza valley, where the river widened and slowed and began, quietly, to drown.

Beck stood at the rail with his coffee and watched the smoke, and thought about a tracker named Villalobos somewhere in that haze, kneeling in the mud, reading the ground, getting closer.

"Come find us," he murmured to the smoke, and drank his coffee, and gave the order to make way.
