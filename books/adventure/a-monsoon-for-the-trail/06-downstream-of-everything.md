## 6. Downstream of Everything

The river did not care that eleven people were missing. That was the first thing Silas understood about the Verraza, standing on its brown shoulder at first light with his pack straps cutting into his collarbones and the mist lifting off the water in slow, indifferent ribbons. The river had drowned these farms a generation ago and never once apologized. It had taken the fences, the orchards, the church bell from the steeple at San Remedios — divers still swore they could hear it tolling under the floodplain when the current ran right — and it had kept going, wide and patient and full of silt, like a debt collector who already knew he would be paid.

Silas had been reading water since before he could read words. His mother had taught him that too, though he tried not to think about her this morning, and failed within the first hundred yards.

*Don't look at the river,* she'd said once, crouched beside a creek in some other lifetime, her finger tracing the surface without touching it. *Look at what the river touches. Water tells you where it's been. Everything else lies.*

So he looked at what the river touched.

The floodplain here was a drowned country pretending to be a lake. Rooftops broke the surface downstream like the backs of sleeping animals — tile and corrugated iron furred with algae, chimneys leaning at angles that spoke of slow subsidence rather than violence. A fence line ran out of the water and back in again, posts bleached bone-white above the waterline and black below it, marking a property line for land that no longer existed except in deeds and memory. Somewhere beneath all of it were roads, doorsteps, the roots of mango trees, an entire parish's worth of ordinary Tuesdays. The government maps called it Zone Four, Inundated. The people who had lived there called it nothing anymore, because the people who had lived there were gone, scattered upriver to towns that smelled of diesel and dry dust instead of mud and green rot.

Silas moved along the old levee road, which the water hadn't quite swallowed. It gave him footing and it gave him a line to follow, and following lines was the only thing he had ever been good at that didn't require him to be someone's son.

He had left Ilsa Reyes's camp before dawn without telling her. He'd left a note, because leaving nothing at all felt like the kind of cruelty that came back around, but the note had been four words: *Downstream. Back by dark.* She would be furious. She would also understand. There was a particular kind of person who became a search coordinator, and in three days of working alongside her Silas had learned that Ilsa Reyes was the kind who understood that grief and procedure ran on different clocks, and that sometimes you had to let one run ahead of the other.

Eleven people. Eleven sets of gear left behind as if the owners had stepped out to watch a sunset. No tracks beyond the perimeter — not because there were no tracks, he now believed, but because the tracks ended at water. And water, his mother had taught him, was not the absence of a trail. Water was a trail that moved.

*Everything that goes into a river comes out somewhere,* she used to say. *Rivers are honest. They just take their time about it.*

He thought about the brass caliper again, and made himself stop thinking about it, and thought about it anyway. The repaired handle. The solder scar shaped like a comma. Of all the instruments laid out neat as surgery in that abandoned camp, it was the one object in the world he could have identified blind, by touch alone, in the dark, at any point in the last thirty years. He had watched her hands close around it ten thousand times. He had heard her swear at it when the hinge slipped. He had once, at nine years old, stolen it from her kit to use as a pretend spaceship part and hidden it so well that she'd torn the house apart for two days, and when he'd finally confessed she hadn't punished him — she'd sat him down and shown him how the repair worked, how a broken thing could be mended so carefully that the mend itself became a signature.

A signature. That was exactly what it was. Someone had wanted him to read it.

Or — and this was the thought he kept shoving underwater like a man holding down something that floated — someone had simply owned it, and worn it, and lost it, and the world was full of coincidences, and wanting your mother's ghost in every shadow was just another way of never leaving home.

The sun climbed. The mist burned off. The river kept its counsel.

* * *

He found the first sign of them a little past midday, six kilometers below the vanished camp, where the levee road dipped toward an old ferry crossing and the current slowed against a stand of dead acacias.

It was a reed bed, or what had been a reed bed before the flooding killed the roots and left the stalks standing like a congregation of gray needles. The stalks were broken. Not everywhere — that was the point, that was the whole art of it — but along a single narrow corridor maybe two meters wide, where the stems lay flattened in one direction only: downstream. Broken reeds told you weight had passed through, and the direction of the break told you which way, and the height of the break told you how high off the ground the weight had ridden. These were broken low. Waist height. Dragged, not carried.

Someone had come out of the water here, or gone into it.

Silas knelt at the edge of the corridor and let himself go quiet the way he'd learned to go quiet, the way that used to make his mother say *there he is* with a pride that had once felt like sunlight and now felt like a debt being collected. The mud at the waterline held prints, half-dissolved by the last rain but legible to anyone willing to get their knees wet. Boots. At least four distinct sets, military pattern, deep heel lugs. Two of the sets moved heavy — the impressions deep at the toe, shallow at the heel, the gait of men carrying loads that shifted. Loads that might have been crates.

Might have been people.

He followed the corridor through the reeds with his heart going hard and slow, the way it did when his body understood something before his mind agreed to. The drag marks led to the water's edge and stopped, and the mud there was churned into a paste scored by the keel-line of a shallow boat, and then there was nothing but river, brown and broad and moving at the speed of a lie.

But boats leave trails too. Not on the water — on the banks, at the places they stop. He cast upstream and downstream along the shoreline, quartering it the way he'd quartered a thousand hillsides, and found what he was looking for forty meters on: a mud berm smoothed by repeated hull contact, a mooring stake driven fresh into the old roadbed, its wood still pale where the bark had been cut away. Someone had used this crossing more than once. Recently. Deliberately. This wasn't where the team had been taken. This was a stop on a route.

A route meant a destination. A destination meant he wasn't chasing a mystery anymore. He was chasing a schedule.

He stood there a long moment, water seeping cold through his boots, and let the anger arrive. He'd been expecting it since the caliper. Anger was a trail emotion; his mother had taught him that too, though she'd framed it kinder. *When the trail makes you angry, that's the trail telling you it matters.* He hadn't understood her then. He understood her now, standing over boot prints in drowned farmland, and hated that understanding her was getting easier the further he went.

* * *

The net came an hour later, and everything changed.

He smelled the site before he saw it — the sweet-rot stink of a fish weir left untended, weed and dead catch composting in the shallows. It was set in a backwater off the main channel, behind a collapsed boathouse whose roof had folded into the water like a hand closing. Subsistence gear. Local work, the knots tied in the old river-fashion, the kind of net a family would check every morning and mend every night. Except no family had checked this one in weeks, and the reason was hanging in it.

At first, in the failing light and the tangle of weed, he took it for debris. A bundle. Then the bundle resolved, and his mind refused it, and offered it again, and he was already wading before he'd decided to move, the water climbing his thighs, his hands reaching into the mesh with a gentleness that belonged to no situation he was in.

It was not a body. He would carry that small mercy with him for the rest of his life, whatever the rest of his life turned out to hold. It was what remained of one: clothes mostly, and bones where the river had done its slow thorough work, and — weighted into the knotwork with a stone wrapped in twine, sunk deliberately, hidden by someone who had wanted it found eventually but not easily — a badge.

A surveyor's badge. Government issue, brushed steel, the crest of the mapping directorate stamped into the face. The number beneath the crest was half-eaten by corrosion, but Silas didn't need the number. He'd spent three days memorizing the personnel file Ilsa had given him, all eleven names, all eleven faces in their ID photographs, the way you memorize anything you intend to find alive.

Elena Vasquez-Roa. Junior hydrographic surveyor. Twenty-six years old. In her photograph she had the stiff half-smile of someone who'd been told to look pleasant and hadn't fully committed. In the file notes, her supervisor had written: *exceptional with the sonar rig, asks good questions.*

She had asked good questions, Silas thought. And someone had answered her with a fishing net and a wrapped stone.

He stood in the backwater holding the badge in both palms, and the river moved around him, and the light went from gold to copper to nothing much at all, and he did not move. Grief came first — it always came first, that was the order of things, grief was the body's honesty arriving ahead of the mind's politics — and it came for Elena Vasquez-Roa, twenty-six years old, exceptional with the sonar rig, and for the eleven empty tents and the food cooked and cooling, and for the fact that somewhere in this drowned country seven or eight or however many more bundles might be hanging in weirs and wedged under rootmats and folded into culverts, each one a name he knew, each one a photograph with a half-committed smile.

Then the grief did what grief does when you feed it enough injustice. It hardened. He felt it happen physically — a setting in the chest, a cooling, the loose heat of sorrow annealing into something with edges. Not rage. Rage was loud and stupid and burned off by morning. This was older than rage. This was purpose with a grudge.

They hadn't all been taken alive. Which meant the ones who were taken alive had been taken by people willing to kill, and the ones doing the taking had been careful — the reed corridor dragged, the boat route reused, the badge weighted and hidden rather than discarded. Careful killers were organized killers, and organized killers on a government river during a salvage dispute meant one thing: Meridian. The fleet was already moving upriver; Ilsa had said it with her jaw tight, the way you say a thing you can't yet prove. Salvage rights. Buried reports. A wreck worth fortunes sitting under forty feet of floodplain silt, and eleven public servants who'd had the misfortune to map their way too close to it.

And somewhere in the middle of it, a brass caliper with a comma-shaped solder scar, laid out neat as surgery in an abandoned tent.

His mother's caliper. In a camp where eleven people stopped existing.

He waded out with the badge wrapped in his neckerchief, tucked against his chest like something with a heartbeat. On the bank he built no fire and ate nothing. He opened his notebook — paper, pencil, the tools that didn't die when the rain came — and drew the site: the weir, the backwater, the collapsed boathouse, the bearing lines to the chimney of San Remedios visible downstream and the split-trunk acacia upstream. He wrote the date, the time, the tide stage. He wrote *Vasquez-Roa, E., remains recovered (partial), foul play certain.* His handwriting came out steady, which surprised him. Underneath it, after a moment, he added a single line that was not procedure:

*Not a job anymore.*

He looked at the words for a long time. Three years he had measured his life in distances covered — contract to contract, valley to valley, always the next ridge between him and whatever he'd walked away from. Distances were safe. Distances didn't ask you who you used to be. But distance was a coward's unit, he saw that now, standing over a dead woman's badge in a drowned parish while the monsoon stacked itself up over the coast like a fist. Time was the real measure. Six tides, Ilsa had said, and two of them were already spent, and the river was rising faster than any forecast, and somewhere downstream or upstream or hidden in plain sight among the salvage crews, the answer to all of it was wearing his mother's hands.

He closed the notebook. The last light was dying over the flooded rooftops, and the first stars came out over the water, and far down the channel — so far it might have been imagination, except Silas had stopped trusting his imagination and started trusting his eyes — he saw navigation lights. Not one set. A string of them, moving upriver in formation, slow and patient and full of intent, the way a fleet moves when it believes no one is watching.

Meridian, coming to collect.

Silas shouldered his pack and turned his back on the lights, angling cross-country through the drowned orchard toward the old mission road, toward Ilsa, toward the radio he would need before midnight. Behind him the river kept moving, honest and slow, carrying everything toward the sea.

*They're not hiding the team,* he thought, and the thought landed cold and complete, the way the truest ones do. *They're hiding the wreck. The team was just in the way.*

And one of the people who knew exactly where that wreck lay — had known for thirty years, had built her whole celebrated life on top of it — was either dead in a fishing net, or running, or waiting for him.

He walked faster. The hunt had stopped being a job somewhere back in the reeds. Now it was a race, and the clock was the tide, and the prize was the truth, and for the first time in three years Silas Villalobos knew precisely, without a single doubt, which direction he was going.
