## 7. What My Mother Taught Me

The river taught him nothing that night. It only repeated what it had already said — *they went downstream, quietly, in the dark* — and Silas sat with his back against a strangler fig and let the repetition wash over him like rain he refused to acknowledge.

Three years of walking away, and every mile of it had been walked on ground his mother had mapped first.

He knew this the way he knew his own heartbeat, which is to say: he tried not to think about it, and thought about nothing else. The way he'd read the silt lines that morning, noting where the current slowed and dropped its sediment in pale ribbons — she'd shown him that when he was nine, kneeling at the edge of an irrigation channel outside Cusco, drawing her finger through the mud. *The water writes everything down, mijo. It's the only honest historian.* The way he'd spotted the drag mark disguised by rain, because she'd taught him that a lie in the landscape always covers too much ground. *People who hide things are tidy. Nature isn't. When you find tidiness in the wild, you've found a human being.*

And the caliper. God, the caliper. Brass, worn silver at the jaws, the handle cracked years ago and repaired with a wrap of tarred cord in a pattern so distinctive it might as well have been a signature. He'd known it from ten feet away. He'd known it before he'd known anything else about that camp, before the tents and the cooked food and the eleven absences, and for one obscene moment his heart had lifted — *she's here* — before the rest of him caught up and reminded him that his mother was three years gone and possibly dead and certainly not standing in a government survey camp in the Verraza valley holding a caliper she'd owned since before he was born.

Unless she was.

Silas pulled his knees up and watched the black water slide past and made himself do the thing he'd been avoiding for three years, which was to remember.

* * *

It had started, as these things do, with something small.

A Tuesday. That was the joke he'd told himself later, that the whole architecture of his life had come down on a Tuesday. He'd been twenty-six, freshly certified as a trail surveyor, working his first season on his mother's excavation in the highlands above the old Verraza tributaries — the same valley, though he hadn't let himself think of that until now, and the coincidence sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. Dr. Marisol Villalobos's greatest discovery had come out of that country: the Sunstone of Yarumal, the pre-colonial ceremonial plaque that had rewritten three chapters of every textbook, that had put her face on two magazine covers and her name on a wing of the national museum. Silas had grown up in the shadow of that stone. He'd held it once, gloved, reverent, while his mother watched him with an expression he'd spent years afterward trying to name and had finally, in the worst hour of his life, understood.

Pride. And underneath the pride, something else. Something that looked, from certain angles, like fear.

On the Tuesday, he'd been cross-referencing excavation records against the field notebooks — routine work, the kind of drudgery a senior archaeologist hands to her son because she trusts him and because no one else will do it. Layer dates. Strata numbers. Core sample logs. He'd been at it for six hours in the site office, a converted shipping container that smelled of diesel and old paper, when the numbers stopped agreeing with each other.

Not dramatically. That was the thing he kept returning to, in the months after, in the years after. The discrepancy wasn't dramatic. A core sample logged at 4.2 meters in the notebook appeared at 3.1 meters in the stratigraphic record. A carbon date shifted by ninety years between two documents that were supposed to describe the same handful of soil. Small things. Tidy things. And Silas, who had been raised on the gospel that nature is never tidy, felt the hair rise on his arms before his mind caught up.

He checked again. He checked a third time. He stayed in the container past midnight with a headlamp clamped to his skull, pulling records, laying them side by side, building the discrepancy out grain by grain the way his mother had taught him to build a trail profile out of broken reeds and bent grass. By two in the morning he had a pattern, and the pattern had a shape, and the shape was a story: artifacts surfacing in strata where they could not have been, dates massaged, provenance chains with links that led nowhere and then, miraculously, somewhere. All of it orbiting one object.

The Sunstone.

He remembered sitting very still in the dark of the container, listening to the generator tick, feeling the discovery move through him like cold water through a wetsuit. He remembered thinking, with a clarity that still shamed him: *I can fix this. I'll show her. She'll explain. There's an explanation.*

There was not an explanation.

He'd confronted her the next morning in her tent, before the crew arrived, because some part of him — the part that loved her, which was all of him, which had always been all of him — wanted to give her privacy. Wanted to give her the chance to tell him whatever terrible or mundane truth there was before the world got hold of it. He laid the papers on her folding table in a neat row, the way she'd taught him to lay out evidence, and said, "Mom. Tell me I'm reading this wrong."

She hadn't even looked at the papers.

That was the detail that broke him, later, when he replayed it. Not denial, not anger, not the wounded confusion of an innocent woman. She'd looked at *him*. At his face, his hands, the door of the tent. Reading him the way she read terrain, calculating, and Silas had understood with a lurch that he was watching his mother decide something.

"You're tired," she'd said. "You've been in that container too long. Cross-referencing will do that — you start seeing patterns in noise."

"The core samples don't match the strata log."

"Transcription errors."

"Fourteen of them?"

"Silas." His name in her mouth, in that tone — the one she used on junior staff, on bureaucrats, on anyone who stood between her and the work. "Do you know what I've given up for this? Do you know what I've buried? Your father. Twenty years of Sundays. Every school play, every fever, every night you cried for me in a hotel room in another country — I did that so that this—" she swept her hand toward the site, the valley, the invisible stone at the center of it, "—could exist. So that people would know who was here before them. And you want to stand in my tent with a stack of paper and call me a liar."

"I didn't say liar."

"You didn't have to. You laid it out like a case file." Her voice had dropped, and that was worse; the shouting had been heat, but the quiet was ice. "I taught you that layout. I taught you to read strata like trails. Everything you're using against me right now, I put in your hands. So yes — I'll say the word you're too much of a coward to say. Ungrateful. You are ungrateful, mijo, and worse, you're careless, and if you take this pile of misreadings to the institute you'll destroy thirty years of real work over transcription errors, and you'll do it because it makes you feel important."

Every word had found its mark. That was the horror of it — she'd known exactly where he was soft, because she'd built him, and she'd fired on all of it. But underneath the flinching, some stubborn surveyor's part of him had stood its ground and taken bearings, and what it fixed on was this: she had never once said *look at the records with me*. Never said *sit down, let's go through it line by line, you'll see*. An innocent woman accused by her own son would have demanded exactly that. Would have grabbed the papers out of his hands.

His mother had looked at him the way you look at rising water, and calculated.

"I'm going to ask you one more time," he'd said. "Tell me I'm wrong."

The silence had lasted four seconds. He'd counted. Counting was another thing she'd taught him — *a tracker counts, Silas, seconds and paces and tides, because memory lies but arithmetic doesn't* — and he'd hated her a little in that moment for arming him even now, even here.

"No," she'd said finally. "You're not wrong about everything. But you're wrong about what matters, and someday, if you ever grow up enough to understand the difference, you'll come find me and apologize."

Then she'd turned back to her table as if he'd already left, and Silas had stood there long enough to understand that he had just been dismissed from his own life, and he'd walked out.

He'd packed that afternoon. She hadn't come to the road. He'd told himself he wasn't waiting for her to, and had waited anyway, standing by his truck with his duffel in the dust until the light went long and gold and the crew's vehicles started coming down the track, and then he'd driven away with the windows down because the cab suddenly felt like a sealed room.

Three years. Fourteen hundred miles a year, give or take. He'd measured them obsessively, contract to contract, highland to coast, as if distance were a currency he could pay down the debt in. He'd ignored her letters — two, early on, both short, neither an apology, both forwarded twice before he stopped updating his address. He'd ignored the calls. He'd built a life out of waypoints, and waypoints, unlike homes, required no forgiveness.

* * *

Now he sat under the strangler fig in the Verraza valley, where it had all begun and where, apparently, it intended to end, and the bitter arithmetic of it closed around him like a fist.

Because here was the irony, and it was not a clever irony, it was a cruel one, the kind the river specialized in: every skill he was using to hunt the truth of the vanished team was hers. The silt lines. The tidy-lie principle. The counting. The patience, the long stillness, the habit of letting the landscape talk first. He had spent three years telling himself he'd walked away from her, and the truth was that he couldn't walk across a room without using her eyes. She was in his hands. She was in the way he crouched, the way he breathed slow when he wanted to hurry, the way he never trusted a straight answer from moving water. You could leave your mother. You could not leave what she taught you. It traveled lighter than any duffel and it never, ever wore out.

And she had known it. Standing in that tent, she had known that the boy she raised would be dangerous precisely because of what she'd given him, and she'd sent him away anyway, and Silas turned that over and over in the dark like a stone with something underneath it.

*You're wrong about what matters,* she'd said. *Someday you'll come find me and apologize.*

Or, he thought now, watching the first gray suggestion of dawn bleed into the eastern ridgeline: someday you'll come find me, and one of us will learn which of us owes the apology. Because the caliper was real. The caliper was in a vanished team's camp in the valley where her whole life's work had started, and either someone had stolen it, or Marisol Villalobos — alive, unaccounted for, erased from every record Ilsa Reyes could produce — had walked into that camp under a name that wasn't hers, carrying the tools of the trade she'd supposedly left behind.

Eleven people had gone into the water at night, tied and quiet, or into boats, or into graves. Somewhere downstream, a surveyor's badge waited in a fishing net for morning. And somewhere upstream or down, a salvage fleet was moving with the confidence of men who had been told things they should not have known.

Silas stood, stiffly, knees cracking, and broke camp in the dark with movements so efficient they were almost silent — her movements, always her movements. He coiled the rope the way she'd shown him. He checked his bearing twice and counted his paces as he walked, because memory lies but arithmetic doesn't.

Downstream, the river widened toward the drowned farmlands, and somewhere along it, he would find the answers, or the fleet would find him first. He could see their lights now, faint on the southern horizon where the channel bent — three, maybe four vessels, running without running lights, which told him everything he needed to know about the kind of men aboard.

He adjusted his pack, took a bearing on the water, and started walking — into the floodplain, into the past, into whatever his mother had left buried in the country that made her.

The river, honest historian that it was, would keep writing. Silas meant to read every word.
