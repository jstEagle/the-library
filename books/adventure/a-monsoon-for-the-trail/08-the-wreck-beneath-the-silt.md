## 8. The Wreck Beneath the Silt

The sonar room smelled of hot electronics and cold coffee, and Ilsa Reyes had been in it for eleven hours when the river finally gave up its secret.

She had learned to read the sweeps the way Silas Villalobos presumably read mud — patiently, suspiciously, letting the data accumulate until it confessed something true. The screen before her rendered the Verraza floodplain in false color: green shallows shading into blue, then indigo, then a black so deep it looked less like water than like absence. Forty feet down through that absence, past layers of silt laid down by a century of monsoons, the return signal was drawing a shape.

A shape with a keel.

"Run it again," she said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. She hadn't spoken aloud in hours and the room's other occupant — young Okonkwo from hydrography, who had the constitution to work nights and the decency not to comment on her coffee consumption — glanced up from his console.

"We've run it four times, ma'am."

"Run it a fifth. I want to be wrong in private before I'm right in front of witnesses."

Okonkwo smiled and reset the sweep. Ilsa stood, pressed her palms flat against the table's edge, and watched the transducer's fan of sound crawl across the drowned valley floor one more time.

The Verraza had been a working river for as long as anyone had written about it — a brown, muscular artery carrying trade and silt and the occasional drowned ambition down from the highlands to the coast. When the government had begun mapping its lower valley ahead of the dam project, nobody had expected much. Sediment cores, channel depths, a few bored reports filed and forgotten. The survey team that had vanished two weeks ago had been doing exactly that kind of unglamorous work: walking the banks, sounding the channels, cataloguing a landscape scheduled to disappear under a reservoir.

They had found something instead. And then they had stopped answering their radio.

Ilsa had spent those two weeks telling herself the missing team was the whole story. Eleven people gone without tracks; a tracker hired out of the highlands because he was the only name on three separate recommendation lists; a salvage fleet moving upriver with lawyers and guns and the patience of vultures. That was already more crisis than her career had budgeted for.

But the team's own instruments, recovered from the abandoned camp and now slaved to her console, had recorded everything they'd measured before they were taken. And buried in that data, half-finished, annotated in a hurried hand, was a set of soundings no one at the agency had bothered to interpret.

The shape on the screen resolved again, and this time Ilsa let herself see it.

* * *

It lay on its side in an ancient oxbow, a channel the river had abandoned centuries ago and then mercifully entombed. The hull was intact — not the scattered debris field of a wrecked ship but a vessel that had settled, whole, into soft mud and been swallowed. Nineteen meters stem to stern. Hardwood, dense enough that the sonar returns came back almost offended by the obstruction. Along the midsection, the imaging suggested cargo: massed, ordered, stacked with intent.

The software offered a probability estimate for the hull's age based on sediment stratification above it. Ilsa made Okonkwo recalculate twice, using different reference cores, before she accepted the number.

Four hundred years. Minimum.

Pre-colonial. A trading vessel from the era the history books covered with a single dismissive paragraph — the age before the maps, when the river kingdoms supposedly traded in nothing worth remembering. If the ship was what it appeared to be, it wasn't just a discovery. It was a correction. Every museum exhibit, every textbook chapter, every smug certainty about what this valley had been before outsiders arrived with ledgers — all of it would have to be rewritten around whatever sat in that hold.

Worth fortunes, yes. The salvage fleet circling upriver knew that much. But worth more than money: worth the past itself, pried loose and held up to the light.

Ilsa became aware that she was gripping the edge of the table hard enough to whiten her knuckles. She made herself let go.

"Log it," she said quietly. "Timestamp, coordinates, full imaging suite. Two copies, one of them offline." She hesitated, then heard herself add, "And don't mention it to anyone outside this room. Not yet."

Okonkwo looked at her with the particular wariness of a junior officer realizing his superior has stopped trusting the institution that employs them both. "Ma'am?"

"The Meridian fleet has a better intelligence network than we do. Until I know how, everyone else can wait forty-eight hours." She said it flatly, as if it were procedure. It wasn't procedure. It was fear, wearing procedure's uniform.

He nodded slowly and began logging.

* * *

She should have gone to sleep. Instead she pulled the missing team's field notes — the physical ones, recovered from the camp, photographed page by page — and started cross-referencing.

That was where the doubt crept in. Doubt always entered through paperwork; Ilsa had learned that over twenty years of government service. Guns you could see coming. It was the documents that ambushed you.

The team's leader, a methodical senior surveyor named Adeyemi, had kept meticulous records right up until the final days. His core samples from the floodplain were textbook work: stratigraphy logged centimeter by centimeter, dates cross-checked against three methods. And his samples told a story that didn't match the artifacts.

Because there were artifacts already. That was the part nobody upstairs wanted to discuss. Three weeks before the team vanished, the agency's regional office had received a preliminary submission — photographs, measurements, provenance claims — describing pre-colonial trade goods recovered from the Verraza floodplain. Ceramics. Metalwork. Beads of a composition that shouldn't have existed this far inland. The submission had come with documentation so complete, so professionally prepared, that it had been fast-tracked for authentication. The signature on the covering analysis belonged to the most celebrated archaeologist of the generation.

Dr. Marisol Villalobos.

Ilsa had stared at that name for a long time the night the file first crossed her desk. Everyone knew Marisol Villalobos. Her excavation reports were taught in universities. Her career-defining find — the Verraza cache, the discovery that had rewritten the region's trade history fifteen years ago — had been authenticated, celebrated, and enshrined. There were stamps issued. There was a school named after her.

And here was her signature, three weeks ago, on new documentation supporting claims in the same valley — claims that had drawn a salvage fleet upriver like blood in water.

Except now Adeyemi's cores were telling a different story.

Ilsa spread the printouts across the sonar room's second table and built the discrepancy with her own hands, sample against sample, layer against layer. The sediment above the buried wreck was old — centuries of patient deposition, undisturbed. Whatever lay in that oxbow had been there since before anyone alive could remember. But the artifacts in the Villalobos documentation, the celebrated cache, carried soil residues and concretion patterns consistent with shallow, recently disturbed deposits. Consistent, Adeyemi had noted in a marginal hand that grew shakier as the pages progressed, *with material introduced into the floodplain within the last few decades.*

Introduced. Planted.

She sat back. The sonar hummed. Somewhere above her, rain had begun ticking against the deck plates — the leading edge of a season that would end all arguments in six tides.

There were innocent explanations. There were always innocent explanations; she'd spent her career hearing them. Contaminated samples. Misattributed signatures. A junior researcher's error laundered through a famous name. Adeyemi himself might simply have been wrong — he'd been working fast, under pressure, racing the calendar.

But Adeyemi had also vanished. So had ten of his people. And the last entries in his field notes weren't about sediment at all.

Ilsa turned to the final photographed page. The handwriting had deteriorated into a scrawl, written fast, maybe by lamplight, maybe in fear:

*Samples don't lie. Someone went to enormous trouble to make them lie. If anything happens to us, the originals are somewhere safe. The river keeps secrets better than ministries do.*

Below that, a single line, underlined twice:

*Ask why she signed it.*

* * *

Ilsa walked out onto the aft deck because the walls had become unbearable.

Night had come down on the river, and the Verraza moved beneath the survey boat's lights like something alive and indifferent — brown water carrying whole trees, silt, the dissolved history of a hundred tributaries toward the coast. Upstream, past the dark bend, the Meridian fleet rode at anchor: running lights strung along three vessels like carnival jewelry, generators droning, cranes silhouetted against a sky bruised purple with coming weather. They were waiting too. Waiting for the same six tides, betting the same fortune, and Ilsa no longer believed their presence was coincidence.

She thought about the woman whose signature sat at the bottom of the poisoned file. Marisol Villalobos had not been seen in public for months — sabbatical, the university said; grief, the gossip said, though grief for what, no one agreed. A scientist that famous did not simply evaporate. And a scientist that famous did not put her name on documentation that a careful man like Adeyemi would die — the word arrived in Ilsa's mind unbidden and stayed — *die* disputing.

Unless the signature meant something else entirely. Unless the famous name had been used rather than given. Unless the woman herself was somewhere inside this tangle — inside the fleet, inside the disappearance, inside the forty feet of black water where an honest ship had waited four centuries for liars to start digging around it.

"You should sleep, ma'am." Okonkwo had come out behind her, a thermos in each hand. He passed her one without asking. "Whatever's in that data will still be wrong in the morning."

Isla almost laughed. "You mean right."

"I mean whichever keeps you up." He leaned on the rail beside her, respectful of the silence for a moment, then: "The tracker. Villalobos. Same name as the archaeologist."

"Yes."

"You think that's coincidence?"

"I think," Ilsa said, watching the river carry the night downstream, "that this valley has stopped producing coincidences." She turned the thermos in her hands. "His mother trained him. Everything he knows about reading ground, he learned from her. And now he's tracking a disappearance that leads straight through her most famous work."

"That's either very good luck or very bad design."

"Design," Ilsa said. "Almost certainly design. The question is whose."

Rain thickened against the deck. Downriver, the floodplain waited with its secret, patient as only buried things are patient. Six tides until the monsoon sealed it for another year — six tides for the truth and the money to fight over which one got to the hull first.

In the morning, she decided, she would send for Silas Villalobos. Not with the search warrant language of the agency. With the truth, or as much of it as she dared put on paper. Because the sonar image of that intact hull was burned into her now, beautiful and terrible, and every hour she sat on Adeyemi's last note was an hour someone else controlled the story.

*Ask why she signed it.*

Ilsa drank her coffee cold and watched the lightning walk the horizon upstream, counting the seconds between flash and thunder, measuring a storm that was coming faster than any forecast promised.
