## 9. Bargaining with Pirates

The river narrowed at the old ferry crossing, and Silas felt the change before he saw it — a stillness in the water that had nothing to do with calm. The current here ran deep and quiet over drowned orchards, and the surface lay flat as poured glass, which meant anything moving beneath it or behind him would show itself too late.

He had been on the water since dawn, poling his borrowed skiff through the flooded farmland where fence posts stood up out of the current like the ribs of something long dead. Behind him: three days of trail, cold now, scattered by rain. Ahead of him: the bend where the Verraza deepened into the salvage fleet's territory. He knew he was being watched. He had known since noon, when the egrets lifted off the water in a line too straight to be natural — birds don't flush in formation unless something big is coming upriver against the flow.

So when the flagship rounded the bend, black-hulled and riding low with machinery, Silas did not reach for the pole. He sat back, laid his hands open on his knees, and waited.

* * *

Tomas Beck came aboard the way other men came into their own kitchens.

He stepped down from the Meridian gunboat's rail onto Silas's skiff without asking, without ceremony, without so much as a wobble — a heavy man made graceful by practice, wearing a linen shirt gone soft with sweat and a smile that arrived a half-second before the rest of his face, like thunder before lightning. Two crewmen stayed on the gunboat, close but not threatening. Everything about the arrangement said: *I don't need force for this. Force is for people who matter less.*

"Silas Villalobos." Beck pronounced the name carefully, giving each syllable its full weight, the way a man handles an artifact he's appraising. "The tracker. Ilsa Reyes's ghost from the highlands." He looked around the skiff with frank curiosity — the coiled rope, the dented kettle, the survey maps weighted under a stone. "Smaller boat than I expected."

"Smaller man than you expected, probably," Silas said. "Everyone says so."

Beck laughed, and it was a good laugh, warm and unforced, and that was the most dangerous thing about him. Silas had met men who wore menace like armor. Beck wore charm, and underneath it the menace was all the more real for being hidden.

"I'll be honest with you," Beck said, lowering himself onto the skiff's thwart as though he'd been invited, as though this were his boat and always had been. "I hate dishonesty. It's inefficient. So let me be honest: I know who you are. Not just the name on Reyes's telegram — I mean who you are. Son of Marisol Villalobos. Raised on this river. The boy who could read silt lines before he could read words." He tapped the side of his own head. "That kind of knowledge doesn't wash out. Three years away, ten years away — it doesn't wash out."

Silas said nothing. On the water behind them, the flagship's generators hummed a low industrial note, and somewhere below it he could hear the rhythmic clank of a dredge being tested.

"You've been following the missing team's trail downstream," Beck went on. "Very good work, by the way. The drag mark at the second channel — disguised with rainwater, very clever of whoever did it, and very clever of you to see through it. My people missed that. My people have sonar, satellite uplink, divers on retainer, and they missed what you found with your eyes and a stick." He spread his hands. "Which brings me to my offer."

"There it is," Silas said.

"There it is." Beck leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the charm dropped away entirely, and what was underneath was not cruelty but something colder and more patient: appetite. "Here is the truth of the situation, Mr. Villalobos. There is a wreck in this valley. Pre-colonial. Intact. Forty feet down in floodplain silt, which means it has been preserved the way things almost never are — complete, undisturbed, a sealed room from four hundred years ago. Ceramics. Textiles. Trade goods from three empires. And records, if the hull held, written in hands that history books say never touched this river." His voice dropped, went almost reverent, and Silas understood that the reverence was real, and that it changed nothing. "This is not money. This is immortality. Whoever opens that hull writes the next hundred years of scholarship. Whoever doesn't—"

"Gets arrested for looting a protected site," Silas said. "Salvage rights don't apply to archaeological material in a government survey zone. You know that better than I do."

"I know that governments move slowly," Beck said pleasantly, "and rivers move fast. Six tides, they're saying. Maybe five now. After the monsoon, the valley floods to the old bluffs, the channels shift, and that wreck sits under forty feet of new water and two meters of new silt until next year — assuming it isn't torn apart by debris in the surge. Next year there will be a different agency, a different budget, a different minister. Or no recovery at all. Rivers keep secrets, Mr. Villalobos. That's practically their profession." He shrugged, a big easy shrug. "I'm offering you a way to make sure the secret gets opened by someone who'll treat it properly. Me. Help me locate the site — fast, before the water comes — and you get full credit. Co-discoverer. Your name beside mine in every paper, every museum plaque. No bureaucracy, no committee, no year of waiting while lawyers argue about permits while the silt closes over everything. Just the find. Clean and quick."

"And the eleven missing people?"

Something flickered across Beck's face — not guilt; Silas doubted the muscle existed — but calculation, brief and smooth as a fish turning under the surface. "A tragedy," he said. "And one I'd very much like to solve. Find them, and I'll fund the search myself. Helicopters, divers, whatever Reyes needs. Consider it a signing bonus."

"They were taken downstream at night," Silas said quietly. "By boat. Quietly. Someone didn't want a scene."

"Bandits," Beck said. "The valley's full of them this time of year. Terrible business." He smiled again, and this time the smile arrived late, after the eyes had already finished their arithmetic. "So. Do we have a partnership?"

* * *

Silas looked at the man for a long moment, and what he felt — and this shamed him, later, how strongly he felt it — was temptation.

Not because of credit. He had walked away from his name once already; names sat lightly on him. It was the *speed* that tempted him. Five tides. A wreck buried in shifting silt that even sonar struggled to resolve, and a search grid that would take Ilsa's team days to run properly. Beck had the equipment, the divers, the dredges, the fuel. If the missing team's trail led anywhere near that wreck — and Silas's gut told him it did, told him the whole dark business of their disappearance was tangled in the hull of that ship like fishing line around a propeller — then Beck's machine could get him answers in hours instead of days. Answers about eleven people. Answers about a brass caliper with a repaired handle that he hadn't been able to stop seeing every time he closed his eyes.

All he had to do was shake the hand of a man who smiled like that.

His mother's voice rose in his memory, unbidden, from some excavation pit years ago, her hands black with river clay: *The ground never lies, mijo. People lie. Instruments lie. The ground just waits.* She had taught him that shortcuts through soft ground were how surveyors died — that the fast route across a floodplain was the one that swallowed you. She had taught him everything, and then she had taught him, with one forged artifact and one slammed door, that even the person who teaches you to read truth can lie about it.

He thought about all of that in perhaps two seconds. Then he thought about the badge in the fishing net, weighted down with stones, drifting in the drowned cornfields. Seven days dead, the coroner would later say. Taken alive, and not kept alive.

"No," Silas said.

Beck's expression didn't change, but the temperature of it did. "No?"

"No partnership. No shared credit. No skipping the bureaucracy." Silas picked up the pole. "You want to find that wreck, find it with your own machines. When you do, Ilsa Reyes will be waiting with a warrant, because whatever happened to those eleven people, you're standing in the middle of it, and the middle is exactly where I'm headed."

Beck stood, unhurried, brushing off the seat of his trousers as though the skiff might have left a stain. For a moment Silas read the man's weight shifting, the glance toward the two crewmen on the gunboat, the small economy of a decision being weighed — *this one could disappear as easily as the others* — and Silas let himself be seen reading it. Let Beck see that he'd seen. Sometimes the only protection a lone man had was making himself expensive.

"You're your mother's son," Beck said at last, softly. "Stubborn in exactly the same places."

The words landed harder than any threat could have. Silas kept his face still, but inside, something turned over — because Beck had said it not as an insult but as data, filed away, useful. Which meant Beck knew Marisol Villalobos well enough to compare. Which meant—

"Good luck with the monsoon," Beck said, stepping back up onto the gunboat's rail with that same kitchen-ease. "It's coming early this year. They always come early when there's something worth drowning." He paused, one hand on the rail, and gave Silas a last look that was almost fond. "Change your mind, signal the flagship. Any light pattern you like. We'll know it's you."

The gunboat peeled away upriver, and the dredge-clank faded, and the egrets came back down to the water one by one, and Silas stood in the returning silence hating how much his hands wanted to shake.

* * *

He poled on through the late afternoon, and it was nearly an hour before he understood why the meeting had left a splinter in him deeper than the refusal itself.

Beck's flagship had passed close — closer than it needed to, a little theater of intimidation, showing him the cranes and the dive compressors and the ranks of crew along the rail. Silas had catalogued it all automatically, the way his mother had trained him to catalogue: hull numbers painted over, crane capacity, the depth-rated submersible slung amidships like a fat silver grub. But there had been a moment, one moment, as the flagship drew level — a gap between two deck containers where a smaller tender was moored, and a woman standing on the tender's deck with her back half-turned, checking a line.

She had been doing nothing remarkable. Nothing at all. Standing with a coil of rope in one hand, looking downriver, waiting.

But she had been standing in a particular way — weight settled low, shoulders square but not stiff, chin slightly tilted as if listening to the water rather than watching it — and Silas knew that posture the way he knew the smell of wet clay. He had grown up watching it across excavation trenches and campfires and kitchen tables. He had spent twenty years learning to read terrain from a woman who stood like that, and three years trying to forget her, and neither project had ever quite finished.

Marisol was dead. That was the official word — lost on a survey upcountry, two years ago, a landslide, a closed file. He had grieved it badly, angrily, the way you grieve someone you were already grieving. Dead women did not stand on tenders in the Verraza valley checking mooring lines for salvage fleets.

But dead women also did not leave their brass calipers in abandoned government camps, repaired handle and all, laid out neat as a signature.

Silas stopped poling. The skiff drifted. Around him the flooded orchards stood silent in the failing light, and the first far-off mutter of thunder rolled down from the mountains like something clearing its throat.

One glimpse. Half a profile, thirty yards, dusk coming on. He had refused Beck's bargain, and in refusing it he had thrown away his one legitimate reason to board that flagship — and now the thing he wanted most in the world was moored behind a corrupt man's guns, and the water was rising, and he had five tides, maybe fewer, to find a way back to a deck he had just sailed away from.

He dug the pole in and turned the skiff, not upriver after the fleet — he wasn't ready, wasn't armed, wasn't foolish — but toward the distant lights of Ilsa Reyes's field station, poling hard, his heart going like a drum.

Because if Marisol Villalobos was alive, then she hadn't sent him a caliper.

She had sent him a trail.
