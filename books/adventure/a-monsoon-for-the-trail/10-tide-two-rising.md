## 10. Tide Two, Rising

The river had gained a hand's width overnight.

Ilsa Reyes stood on the survey barge's deck at first light and watched the water climb the pilings of the drowned ferry landing — a black line creeping up weathered wood, patient as a debt collector. The forecast had promised six tides before the monsoon sealed the valley. The river was not honoring the forecast.

"Half a meter above the seasonal mean," said Okonkwo, her hydrologist, crouched at the gauge with a grease pencil and the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. "And that's before the upper tributaries report in. If the cloud cover over the headwaters is what the satellite suggests—"

"It's worse."

"It's worse," he agreed. "Ilsa, we should be thinking about pulling the instruments off the lower stations today. Not tomorrow. Today."

She didn't answer immediately. Below her, through forty feet of tea-colored water, lay the reason none of them could leave: a hull older than any nation that claimed this river, intact, freighted with whatever history had been loaded aboard it four centuries ago. And somewhere downstream, closing fast, the Meridian fleet with its cranes and its lawyers and its appetite.

"We pull nothing yet," she said. "We work faster instead."

Okonkwo gave her a look that said *that is not how rivers negotiate*, but he wrote it down anyway, because he was a good man and good men write things down even when the person giving the orders has stopped listening to arithmetic.

Ilsa went below to the chart room, where the air smelled of diesel and wet paper, and where the second tide of her professional life was already going out.

* * *

The radio traffic had been strange since dawn.

Not strange in content — position reports, weather advisories, the usual liturgy of a government operation — but strange in rhythm. Three of the relay stations were confirming messages they shouldn't have needed to confirm. The Verraza district office had requested a copy of the survey route *again*, though they'd had it for two weeks. And a supply launch out of Puerto Cielo had asked, casually, whether the team would be working the eastern channel or the western one after tomorrow.

Casual questions were how leaks dressed themselves.

Ilsa sat down at the chart table and spread the route map flat, weighting the corners with coffee mugs and a brass paperweight shaped like an anchor — someone's idea of humor, years before she'd inherited it. The route was simple enough: eleven people, three boats, working upstream from the old ferry landing through the drowned farmland, sounding as they went, following the deep channel toward the site the sonar had flagged.

Eleven people. She made herself think of them as people, because the paperwork kept trying to turn them into a number. Eleven names on a manifest. Eleven sets of gear left standing in tents at a campsite that looked, by every account Silas Villalobos had given her, like a photograph of itself — everything in order, everyone gone.

Villalobos. She turned the name over in her mind the way she'd been turning it over for days. The tracker had arrived sullen and reluctant and then, within hours, had read their vanished colleagues' last movements out of the mud like a man reading a letter aloud. *They went downstream by boat, at night, quietly.* Not lost. Taken. And taken by someone who knew exactly where the survey team would be, and when, and which channel they'd use.

Which meant someone had known the route.

Ilsa pulled the distribution list. It was supposed to be short. Her office, the district coordinator, the ministry liaison, the security desk. Seven copies, seven signatures, each transmission logged. She ran her finger down the column of names and stops.

At the bottom, in the log from nine days before the disappearance, was a request she remembered approving without thinking: the ministry liaison's office asking for the route "for coordination with regional assets." Standard language. She'd initialed it between meetings, half-reading, the way you initial a hundred documents a week.

She stared at her own initials for a long time.

Then she got up, locked the chart room door, and did something she hadn't done since her early days in the service: she started pulling files without authorization.

* * *

The liaison was a man named Ferran Duarte, and Ilsa had known him for eleven years. He'd been at her promotion ceremony. He'd lent her money once, in a bad month, and never mentioned it again. He sent his daughter's school photos around every autumn like clockwork, a girl with her mother's serious eyes.

That was the problem with betrayal. It never came from strangers. Strangers couldn't get close enough.

Duarte's file was clean. Of course it was clean; men like Duarte didn't leave fingerprints, they left favors. But the transmission log wasn't clean, and transmissions don't have loyalties. The route document had been forwarded twice beyond its authorized distribution — once to a satellite uplink registered to a coastal shipping concern, and once, fourteen minutes later, to a receiver whose registration had been scrubbed so thoroughly that the scrubbing itself was a signature. Nobody bothered to erase a receiver unless the receiver mattered.

She cross-checked the shipping concern against the Meridian fleet's charter records, which she'd pulled under the pretext of assessing salvage claims. The concern leased barges. Meridian leased the same barges, through a holding company, through another holding company, through a lawyer in the capital whose name appeared — she felt her stomach tighten as she found it — on the donor rolls of the minister who had appointed her own director.

The chain was elegant. It was almost beautiful. Route to liaison, liaison to uplink, uplink to barge company, barge company to Meridian, Meridian to the minister, the minister to her director, her director to her. A circle drawn around the whole valley, and she had spent two weeks standing inside it, calling it security.

The missing team hadn't stumbled into anything. They had been delivered. Their route had been sold before their boats ever left the dock, and whoever took them had simply waited at the end of the paper trail with the patience of the river itself.

Ilsa sat back in the chair and listened to the barge creak around her, the hull flexing against water that was still rising, and understood that she was alone in a way she had never been alone before. Not stranded-alone — she'd been that, on icebreakers, on night patrols. This was different. This was the aloneness of being the only person in a building who knows the building is on fire, when the fire department works for the arsonist.

Who could she take this to? Her director? The director whose appointment had been paid for by the same money that bought the barges? The security desk, whose duty officer signed off on Duarte's requests without reading them — or worse, read them perfectly? The ministry? The ministry was the top of the chain, or close enough that the difference wouldn't save her.

She thought about Silas Villalobos, out on the water somewhere with his mother's caliper and his three years of silence, reading silt lines while the river rose around him. He trusted no one either. Maybe that was why she'd fought to get him assigned. Not just the tracking. The distrust. It took one to know one.

There was a knock at the chart room door. Ilsa's whole body went rigid, the files sliding under a chart with a motion she was surprised to find her hands already knew.

"Yes."

"Ma'am?" It was Okonkwo. "Radio for you. Priority routing." A pause. "It's the district office. They're asking for an update on the tracker's investigation. They've asked twice already this morning."

Twice. Asking about Villalobos now, not the route. Because the route had already been used up, and the only loose thread left was the man walking along it.

"Tell them the investigation is ongoing and updates will come through proper channels," she said. "Proper channels, Okonkwo. Word for word."

She heard him repeat it, heard the puzzlement in his voice, and hated herself for the next thing she had to do, which was say nothing more. Okonkwo was good. Okonkwo was honest. But honest men transmit what they hear, and what she knew could not be transmitted anywhere inside the circle.

* * *

By afternoon the river had taken the lower instrument station.

She watched it go from the deck — a tripod of sensors and solar panel, installed with such care ten days ago, now standing knee-deep in brown water, its data cable trailing off into the flood like a severed nerve. One of the crew waded out and recovered it, cursing cheerfully, and the cheerfulness hurt worse than complaint would have. They didn't know. They thought this was weather. Weather was honest.

Two tides gone. Four left, if the river kept its word, and the river had stopped keeping anyone's word.

Ilsa climbed to the wheelhouse roof with a thermos she didn't drink from and looked south, down the drowned valley, where the water had swallowed fence lines and orchard rows and the roofs of barns until the farmland was a rumor beneath the flood. Somewhere down there, Villalobos was following the dead. Somewhere further, the Meridian fleet was coming upstream with its lights burning at night like a carnival that ate people. And somewhere in between — she was sure of it now, sure the way you're sure of a shape in the dark — was the answer to what had happened to her eleven.

Her eleven. When had they become hers? She'd signed the manifests. She'd approved the route. She'd initialed the request that killed them, maybe, with a pen she probably still had in her desk drawer, and she had not even looked up from her second meeting of the morning while she did it.

She took out her notebook — the paper one, the one that lived in her coat and appeared in no inventory — and wrote down the chain: *Duarte. Uplink 4471-C. Barge lease, Meridian via Halvard Holdings. Minister's donor roll. Director.* Then she tore the page out, folded it small, and considered the river.

No. Evidence went nowhere unless it went somewhere safe. She sewed the page into the lining of her life vest instead, behind the flotation panel, with a needle from the med kit and twenty minutes of clumsy, furious stitching. If she went into the water, the water could keep it. The river, at least, had never pretended to be on anyone's side.

The radio crackled. "Barge *Inquisidor*, this is fleet vessel *Cormorant*, inbound your position, requesting clearance to pass." Meridian, already. Beck's voice, when he came on himself a minute later, was warm as bathwater: apologies for the intrusion, admiration for the government's diligence, an offer of fuel and fresh water, anything she needed, neighbors on the river ought to help each other.

"Appreciated," Ilsa said. "We're fully provisioned."

"Of course you are." A smile in the voice, and under the smile, faint as a fish under ice, something else. "Fair tides, Coordinator Reyes. Though I hear they're getting scarce."

She set the handset down and stood a long moment in the wheelhouse, watching the *Cormorant*'s running lights come on early against the darkening sky, a string of lanterns strung across the throat of the valley.

Four tides. A leak in her own office. A fleet at her door. And one unreliable, resentful, brilliant tracker somewhere downstream whom she had no way to warn — except that he'd left his camp that morning, Okonkwo reported, moving fast toward the flooded villages, following something only he could see.

She went below, unlocked the chart room, and began, very carefully, to plan a betrayal of her own.
