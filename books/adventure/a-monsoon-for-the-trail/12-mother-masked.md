## 12. Mother, Masked

The name stitched into her jacket said *R. Ochoa*, and for three years Marisol Villalobos had answered to it the way a woman answers to a coat that doesn't fit — without complaint, without warmth, and with a constant, low-grade awareness of every seam.

She stood at the rail of the *Meridian Vesper* in the gray hour before dawn, watching the river run brown and swollen below her, and she did what she had done every morning since signing aboard under Beck's indifferent gaze: she counted things. Eleven crew visible on deck. Four barges lashed astern. Two days, maybe three, before the sonar teams found what they were looking for. And one telegram, three years old now, folded so many times its creases had gone soft as cloth, still living in the inside pocket where her heart should have been.

*You were right about the stratigraphy. I was wrong about everything else. — S.*

He had sent it two weeks after he walked out. She had never answered. By the time she worked out what an answer would cost — the whole confession, the whole ruin, the whole of a life's work laid open on a kitchen table like a dissected specimen — the silence had grown its own weight, its own gravity, until answering seemed harder than fleeing. So she fled. First from the university, then from the country's memory of her, and finally, when the government survey team went into the valley and did not come out, from her own name entirely.

The river took the rest. Rivers were good at that.

"Ochoa." A deckhand touched her elbow, and she made herself not flinch. "Beck wants the sediment logs on his table by six."

"Tell him they're coming," she said, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her. Three years of practice. Three years of being nobody, of keeping her hands busy and her mouth shut and her eyes moving. She had been a legend once; now she was a middle-aged lab tech with a forged credential and a talent for core samples that nobody questioned because nobody looked at middle-aged lab techs twice.

That was the joke of it, she thought, climbing down to the sampling deck. She had spent thirty years teaching students that the past is never buried — that it waits, patient as silt, for someone to dig. And then she had spent three years trying to bury herself, and discovered she'd been right all along. The past waited. It waited in the floodplain below this barge, forty feet down, where a pre-colonial trading vessel lay intact in the clay like a held breath. It waited in the field notes of eleven missing surveyors. It waited in a brass caliper with a repaired handle, if Silas had found it, and she had to believe he had found it, because she had left it there to be found.

* * *

She had planned it carefully, the way she planned everything except the things that mattered.

When the news broke — *government team missing, Verraza valley, foul play suspected* — Marisol had been working a dredging contract two hundred miles downstream, anonymous, invisible, safe. She had read the wire report four times and understood immediately what no one else could: the team hadn't stumbled onto the wreck. They had been following *her* data. The old excavation records, the ones with the discrepancies Silas had found and she had explained away with a smile and a citation, had been quietly re-examined by her own former protégé, Elena Vasquez, who led the vanished team. Elena had written to her once, a year ago: *I have questions about the Corazal artifact, Doctor. I would rather ask you than anyone else.*

Marisol had not answered that letter either. There was a pattern there, if she was honest, and honesty at six in the morning on a salvage barge was a dangerous luxury.

Elena had gone looking for answers in the floodplain. The team had gone silent. And Meridian Salvage — which should not have known the coordinates, could not have known them unless someone inside the agency sold them — had filed claims within seventy-two hours and started upriver with a fleet.

So Marisol had done the arithmetic that cowardice makes possible. If she stayed hidden, Beck would find the wreck, strip it, and destroy whatever Elena's people had hidden aboard — including, very likely, the proof that the Corazal artifact was planted. That proof could end her. It could also, if it reached the right hands honestly, be the only absolution she would ever be offered: truth told by someone other than herself, complete and undeniable, ending three years of running.

Either way, she needed to be standing next to it when it surfaced.

Getting aboard Meridian had been almost insultingly easy. Beck hired fast and checked slowly; his fleet ate technicians the way rivers ate banks. She had presented R. Ochoa, sediment specialist, references forged with the same skill she'd once used on stratigraphic diagrams — and there was a bitter symmetry in that she refused to examine too closely. Her hands still knew how to make false things look true. That was, after all, the entire problem.

What she had not planned — what no amount of planning survived — was the morning, nine days ago, when she looked up from a core sample log and saw a man stepping off Ilsa Reyes's survey launch at the fuel dock, and knew him at fifty meters in the rain.

Silas.

Her body had reacted before her mind did: a full-body jolt, the log nearly slipping from her hands, her feet actually taking half a step toward the rail before she caught herself against the winch housing and held on like a woman in weather. He was thinner. His hair was longer, tied back carelessly, and he moved the way he had always moved — reading the ground under his boots even across a plank dock, weight low, eyes doing that constant quartering sweep she had taught him when he was seven years old, calling it a game, hiding buttons in tall grass.

*Look with your whole body, mijo. The trail is always talking. Most people just don't speak it.*

He had learned. God, he had learned. And she had taught him every word of it, and then she had lied to him with the same mouth, and he had walked out, and she had let him go because chasing him meant explaining, and explaining meant the end of everything she had built.

Everything she had built. She looked at the words now the way she looked at contaminated samples — turning them over, testing them, watching them fail. What had she built? A reputation resting on one artifact that another archaeologist had placed in that trench with her knowledge and her silence. A career that Elena Vasquez had died — or worse, disappeared — questioning. A son who measured his life in miles put between himself and her.

And yet.

She watched him from the galley window that morning while he talked to Reyes on the dock, and the longing hit her with a physical force that embarrassed her. Ninety feet of rain-gray air between them, and she could not cross it. She catalogued the reasons with scientific thoroughness: Beck's men everywhere; her cover blown means her usefulness ended; Silas walking toward her means questions she has spent three years not answering. All true. All correct. And underneath them, plain as bedrock beneath topsoil, the real reason: she was afraid. Not of Beck. Of her son's face when he saw her. Of the moment his eyes went from recognition to understanding to that cold, precise anger he wore better than any of her colleagues ever had.

Cowardice, she thought, was not the absence of courage. It was courage applied everywhere except the one place it was owed.

* * *

"You've been staring at that sample for ten minutes, Ochoa."

Tomas Beck. She kept her eyes on the core section and let the familiar chill move through her and out. Beck had a way of arriving behind people, soundless on a steel deck, a big amiable man with a fisherman's patience and a shark's priorities. She had spent weeks learning his rhythms: how he charmed the crew, how he doled out information in rationed spoonfuls, how he watched everyone the way she watched sediment layers — for discontinuities.

"Sediment doesn't rush," she said. "That's rather the point of it."

He laughed, easy and warm, and leaned on the table beside her. Up close he smelled of coffee and diesel. "Reyes's tracker is still out there. You hear anything on the radio nets?"

"The tracker." She kept her tone bored, professional. "The one who read your drag marks?"

Something flickered behind Beck's friendliness — quick, controlled, gone. So he knew she knew. She had listened to the radio traffic like everyone else; the story of the surveyor who found the boat-launch site at the drowned farm had traveled the fleet in a day. *Someone wanted them gone without a scene.* Someone had succeeded, mostly.

"He's good," Beck said. "Good men are useful or they're problems. Usually both." He tapped the core sample with one thick finger. "This the layer you flagged?"

"Anthropogenic fill. River-deposited, but sorted wrong for natural process. Something heavy sat here long enough to change the deposition pattern." She spoke in the flat cadence of the technician she was pretending to be, and hated how fluently the lie came. "Your sonar anomaly sits under it."

"Our prize," Beck said softly, and for a moment the charm dropped and she saw the thing underneath, the naked appetite, and understood exactly what kind of man she was riding a river with. Then the charm came back up like a drawbridge. "Six tides, Ochoa. Weather service says the monsoon's early. We get this done or the river keeps it another year." He pushed off the table. "Get me the full log by six."

She got him the log by six. She also copied it, and the sonar overlays, and the channel charts, hiding the duplicates in a waterproof pouch taped inside the ventilation housing above her bunk, because whatever else Marisol Villalobos was — fraud, coward, fugitive from her own name — she was still, at bottom, a scientist, and scientists keep their data. When this ended, however it ended, the record would exist somewhere outside Tomas Beck's control. It was a small thing. It was the only honest thing she had done in three years, and she did it every night with hands that shook slightly, and she had stopped asking herself why.

She knew why. The answer walked on two legs and read trails and was currently somewhere upstream, hunting the people who hunted her secret.

* * *

On the fourth evening she saw him again, and this time he saw nothing but the river, and she stood in the shadow of the deck crane and let herself look.

He was in one of the survey launches, passing the fleet at a distance, heading upriver toward the narrows where the channels braided and shifted. Even at two hundred meters she could read him: the set of his shoulders as he studied the waterline, the way he raised binoculars not to the far bank but to the near mudflats, reading silt lines the way she had once read him bedtime stories. The light was going gold behind him and the river ran bronze and he was twenty-six years old and she had missed — she counted, because counting was what she did instead of weeping — four birthdays, two monsoons, one graduation she'd learned of through a department newsletter, and every single day of the three years since he'd stood in her kitchen and said, *I used to think you were the most honest person I knew,* and picked up his pack.

Run toward him or run away. The question had lived in her chest for nine days like a swallowed stone. Every rational analysis said away. Away was survival; away preserved the position she'd built inside Meridian, the access to the evidence, the chance — thin, desperate, real — to surface the truth herself, on her own terms, and hand it to him finished. *Here, mijo. Here is the whole story, documented, verified. Now you can hate me for what I did instead of what I hid.* As if there were a difference. As if the hiding weren't the crime.

But her feet, her traitorous, aging, stubborn feet, wanted toward. Toward the dock where he'd stood. Toward the voice she could still hear saying *look with your whole body*. Toward the single act of courage she had deferred for three years, compounding interest the whole time.

She gripped the rail. The river slid past, carrying everything it carried — silt, seeds, the drowned valley's fences and orchards, and somewhere in its brown depths, forty feet down, a ship full of secrets and probably, by now, some of Elena's people too. Seven accounted for, the radio said. Four unaccounted. Marisol did the math on those four every night, and every night the math came out the same: *your fault, your fault, your fault, your fault.*

Below her, the launch turned into the narrows and shrank to a speck, then a suggestion, then nothing.

"Beautiful evening," said a voice behind her, and she turned to find Beck's first mate, Duarte, watching her with mild, professional curiosity. "You know that tracker? You were watching him pretty hard."

The lie arrived on schedule, smooth as poured concrete. "Everyone watches him. Man found your launch site from a rain-soaked mudflat. I'm a scientist. I'm curious." She smiled, and Duarte smiled back, and she noted — filing it with the rest of the data, because data was all she had left — that Duarte's smile didn't reach his eyes, and that he lingered a moment too long before moving off down the deck.

They were watching her now. Or someone was. Beck collected useful people the way the river collected driftwood, and sooner or later he tested every piece.

That night she added the new copies to the waterproof pouch and lay in her narrow bunk listening to the current push against the hull, and she made herself a promise with the shape of a plan around it: when the divers went down, when the hold opened, when Elena's field notes came up into the light — she would be there, and she would take them, and she would find her son before Beck did, and she would say the ninety seconds' worth of truth she owed him even if it killed the last thing left between them.

Because the alternative — the river rising, the wreck sealed, the truth drowned for another year, the silence stretching into permanence — was simply the rest of her life, continued indefinitely, under a name that wasn't hers.

*In the morning,* she told the dark overhead. *I'll decide in the morning whether I'm running toward him or away from him.*

But she already knew, had known since the fuel dock, since the caliper, since the telegram soft as cloth against her heart: the answer wasn't a direction. It was a tide. And the tide was coming in whether she waded out to meet it or stood on this steel deck pretending to be R. Ochoa until the water closed over her name forever.

Two decks below, a diver's radio crackled: sonar contact confirmed, hard return, hull-shaped, forty-one feet down.

The wreck had spoken. And every soul on the river — the corrupt, the grieving, the searching, the hiding — turned toward the sound.
