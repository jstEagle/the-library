## 13. The Forgery

The dead drop was a fish trap.

Silas understood it the moment he saw it, and the understanding landed in his chest like a stone dropped into still water. The trap hung from a leaning bamboo pole at the edge of a drowned orchard, half a mile downstream of where the survey team's last known position had been plotted. It was the wrong kind of trap for the wrong kind of fish, set at the wrong depth, and it had been maintained — the reed lashing renewed, the mouth cleared of silt — long after the eleven people who'd set it had stopped being able to maintain anything.

Someone had come back. Or someone had left instructions for someone else to come back, and that someone else was either dead, missing, or waiting to see who came looking.

He poled the skiff through the flooded rows of what had once been a guava orchard, the trees standing to their shoulders in brown water, their leaves furred with silt. The rain had stopped an hour before, and in its absence the valley had gone quiet in a way that felt rehearsed, as if the whole landscape were holding its breath between acts. Somewhere upstream, the Meridian fleet was working its dredges. He could feel them the way you feel a storm behind a mountain — not seen, but present, a pressure on the air.

He tied off at the pole and stood over the trap for a long moment before touching it.

*Check everything twice, and then check the thing you didn't think to check.* His mother's voice, in his mother's cadence, delivered in his memory with the merciless clarity that memory reserves for the people we've tried hardest to forget. He had learned tracking at her knee, literally — kneeling in the red dirt of her first excavation site at seven years old, learning to tell a footprint made by a running man from one made by a man pretending to run. Every skill he was using in this valley had been poured into him by the woman whose forgery had, if these notes were what he believed they were, poisoned everything she'd ever taught him.

He lifted the trap out of the water.

Inside, among the small silver fish that had blundered in and couldn't find the way out, there was an oilcloth bundle, tied with tarred cord and weighted with a split piece of lead shot. The fish were still alive. He poured them back into the flood and watched them vanish into the murk, and then he sat down in the bottom of the skiff, in two inches of bilgewater, and unwrapped the bundle with hands that were not entirely steady.

A field notebook. Waterproof paper, government issue, the kind Ilsa Reyes's agency used. The cover was stamped with a serial number he'd seen once already, photographed on a clipboard in the search coordinator's tent — the property of Dr. Emilio Sarrazin, team leader of the Verraza survey. Eleven names on that roster. Sarrazin's was the first.

The notebook was damp but legible. The handwriting was small, precise, and increasingly hurried as the pages turned.

Silas told himself he would read it as a tracker reads a trail — clinically, looking for direction, for speed, for the weight of what had passed. He told himself that whatever it said about his mother, he had spent three years preparing to hear it. He had built his whole adult life on the assumption that she was guilty. He had accused her of it to her face, in a tent that smelled of coffee and rain, and watched her face do something he had never seen it do before or since — go completely, perfectly blank, like a wall coming down.

He had walked out on that blankness. He had spent three years walking.

He opened the notebook and read.

* * *

The first third was routine survey work. Grid coordinates, sediment cores, magnetometer readings — the patient cartography of a valley scheduled to drown. Sarrazin's team had been mapping not just the terrain but the terrain's memory: where the old riverbed had run before the dam, where the pre-colonial trade routes had crossed, where the floodplain had swallowed and preserved what the trade routes had left behind. The wreck was there in the notes from the beginning, a hypothesis before it was a fact — *anomaly cluster 7, depth approx. 12 meters below current floodline, signature consistent with hull timbers, pre-contact construction.*

Then, on a page dated eleven days before the disappearance, the handwriting changed. Not the letters — the pressure. The pen had begun to press harder, as if the writer were angry at the paper.

*Cross-referenced core samples against the Villalobos catalog today. The stratigraphy is wrong. It is not subtly wrong. It is not ambiguous. The artifact layer at Site V-4 — the layer that produced the Ceremonial Plaque, the find of the century, the piece that built the Institute and named a generation of scholarship — sits in sediment that was deposited a minimum of four hundred years AFTER the plaque's established date. Either our dating methods are wrong, or the plaque was buried. Deliberately. Recently. By someone who wanted it found.*

Silas read the sentence three times.

He had said these words himself, three years ago, standing in his mother's tent with her excavation ledgers open between them, his finger on the discrepancy he'd found in the soil logs — a discrepancy so small that only someone raised on her methods, taught to read dirt the way she read dirt, would ever have caught it. *The layer is wrong,* he had said. *You know the layer is wrong. You were there when it was cut.* And she had looked at him with that wall coming down behind her eyes and called him ungrateful, and he had called her a fraud, and the word had hung in the wet air between them like something neither of them could take back, because he had meant it, and because she had not denied it.

He had spent three years telling himself that the not-denial was proof. Now a dead or missing stranger's handwriting was telling him the same thing, and it felt nothing like vindication. It felt like the floor of the world giving way a second time, in the same place, over the same wound, and discovering that the first fall had never healed at all — that he had only been walking on it, all these miles, the way you can walk on ice that looks solid and is not.

He kept reading. It was the only direction left.

*Checked the Institute's published section drawings against our cores. The published drawings do not match the site. I have been to V-4. I stood in that trench as a student. I remember the stratigraphy, and it is not what the papers say. Marisol Villalobos redrew the sections. She must have. No one else had access, and no one else would have dared. God help me, I worshipped her. Every one of us did. She taught half the archaeologists on this coast how to read soil, and she taught us with a forged artifact sitting in a glass case in the capital, and we built our careers on top of it like houses on a bad foundation.*

*I don't know why. I keep turning it over. Money? The Institute's funding was secured by the plaque. Legacy? She was young, it was her first major dig, the diver died, the season was a failure, and the plaque appeared in the last week like an answered prayer. Maybe it started as something small. Maybe it started as a story she told herself. That's the thing about fraud — I don't think it begins with a lie. I think it begins with a truth you can't bear, and then the lie grows around it like scar tissue, and one day you can't find the original wound.*

Silas stopped reading. He sat in the bilgewater of the skiff in a drowned orchard and let the notebook rest on his knee, and for a while he did not read anything at all, because the river was making its small sounds against the hull and the guava trees were dripping and somewhere in his body a three-year-old argument had finally, horribly, been settled in his favor.

He had been right.

He had wanted, for three years, to be wrong. He had constructed his whole exile on the possibility that he had been wrong — that if he walked far enough, some version of his mother would catch up to him and prove it, and he could come home. But underneath the wanting had been the knowing, the tracker's knowing, the part of him that read trails and could not unread them, and that part had been right from the first night, and being right felt like being robbed.

*The diver,* he thought. *The diver died.*

The elders in the flooded-bank village had told him. Marisol's first expedition, decades ago, the local diver who went down into the old channel and didn't come up. They had given Silas the man's carved token and said the river gives back what is owed. He had carried the token since, turning it over in his pocket at night, feeling its worn grooves, not yet understanding what it was for.

Sarrazin had understood. The notebook turned to it on the next page, the handwriting pressed so hard it had nearly torn the paper.

*I found the diver's family. The granddaughter keeps his things. Among them, a dive log — his own, kept in pencil, waterproofed in wax the way the old river men did it. On his final dive he was sent down to place a marker at the anomaly. He came up and told Marisol what he saw: not a wreck. A scatter. Old timbers, yes, but disturbed, mixed with modern material, a section of chain that could not have been pre-colonial. He told her the site was contaminated, that whatever was down there had been moved or disturbed within the last century. Three days later he was dead, and a week after that, the plaque was in the ground at V-4, and the season was saved, and no one ever asked what a length of modern chain was doing in a pre-contact layer.*

*She didn't kill him. I want to believe she didn't kill him. But I think she used his death. I think the river handed her a tragedy and she built a career on top of it, and the diver's family has spent forty years believing the river took him, when really it was the beginning of the lie.*

*The evidence is real. The dive log, the core samples, the original field photographs from V-4 that show the section before it was redrawn — I have them, or I know where they are. And I know now why the eleven of us are being watched. It isn't the wreck Meridian wants buried. It's this. The wreck is worth a fortune. The truth is worth more to the people who would lose everything if it came out, and there are people in my own agency — I'm certain of it now, certain the way you're certain of weather — who would rather the valley drowned with all of us in it than let this report reach the capital.*

*So I'm not sending it. Reports get lost. Reports get edited. Couriers get bought. I've watched it happen for thirty years and I am not going to watch it happen to this.*

*The river keeps its secrets better than any government keeps its promises. So the evidence goes where the river already keeps its oldest secret — down. Into the wreck. Into the hold of the vessel that started all of this, forty feet under the floodplain, where it will sit until someone comes for it honestly or the water takes it forever. I've marked the location in a way only a surveyor would think to look for, and I've left copies of the key with people I trust, and I'm trusting the river with the rest.*

*If you're reading this, I'm sorry. I'm sorry you had to be the one. Check the trap again — there's one more thing.*

There was one more thing. A second bundle, smaller, wrapped in the same oilcloth, tucked into a seam of the trap's woven body that Silas would have missed if he hadn't been told to look. Inside: a brass key, river-green with age, and a strip of waterproof paper on which Sarrazin had written a single line in cipher — a surveyor's cipher, grid-referenced, the kind that meant nothing without the grid it referenced.

Silas had the grid. It was in his head, because Ilsa Reyes had put it there, because the search coordinator had trusted the one tracker who could read the terrain — and because Sarrazin, dead or drowned or disappeared, had apparently reasoned that the agency's own missing-persons search would eventually bring exactly one kind of person to exactly this stretch of river, and that the person would be able to read what was written.

The cipher resolved in Silas's hands in under a minute. Coordinates. A bearing and a depth. The location of the hold, the location of the hiding place, the location of the truth — sitting forty feet down in the dark, in the belly of the vessel his mother had spent a career pretending she'd found honestly, guarded by nothing but water and the six tides left before the monsoon sealed it for a year.

* * *

He sat with it until the light began to fail.

The valley turned gold, then copper, then the deep bruised purple that came before full dark, and the flooded orchard became a field of black trees standing in black water, and Silas Villalobos did not move. The notebook lay open on his knee. The brass key lay in his palm, cold and river-green, and he kept closing his hand around it and opening it again, as if checking whether it was still there, whether the truth was still there, whether it would dissolve if he stopped believing in it.

His worst accusation had been right. That was the fact, and the fact did not care what it did to him.

He tried to assemble the old anger and found it wouldn't hold its shape. Anger needed a living target, and the target had become something stranger — a young woman forty years ago, kneeling at the edge of a drowned channel, hearing that her expedition had failed and her diver was dead, and reaching for a lie the way a drowning woman reaches for anything. He could see it now with terrible clarity, could reconstruct it the way he reconstructed any trail: not from malice but from pressure, not from evil but from a wound that got buried and kept growing, scar tissue over scar tissue, until the original truth was forty feet down and unreachable and the lie had a glass case in the capital and a generation of scholarship built on top of it.

And she had let him walk out. Three years ago, with the word *fraud* hanging between them, she had let him walk out rather than say *you're right, help me fix it.* That was the part that still burned. Not the forgery — the silence. The wall. The blank face that had told him nothing and sent him into the world carrying a wound of his own, unhealed, for three years and several thousand miles.

Unless the wall had been protecting him. Unless there were people — the people Sarrazin had been certain of, the people inside Ilsa's own agency, the people who had made eleven surveyors vanish — who would have come for anyone who knew, and she had looked at her son and chosen the wall because the wall was the only thing that might keep him outside the blast radius.

He didn't know. That was the truth of it, the honest tracker's truth: he had a trail, and the trail said *forgery,* and the trail said *evidence in the hold,* and the trail said *eleven people taken quietly downstream in the night,* and beyond that the sign was cut, the way sign is always cut eventually, and what lay past the cut was not something reading could reach. It would take a conversation. It would take her voice, her actual voice, saying the whole story — and she was either dead in the river or hiding somewhere in the Meridian fleet with her shoulders held the way he remembered them, and he had seen her, hadn't he, for half a second on Beck's dock, a woman with his mother's posture, and he had not gotten a second look.

He put the notebook, the key, and the cipher in the oilcloth and rewrapped the bundle, and he did not put it back in the trap. The trap had done its work. The river had given back what was owed, or at least the first installment of it.

He poled out of the orchard in the last light, running without lights, keeping to the drowned hedgerows where the fleet's dredges wouldn't look. The coordinates in his head were simple and enormous: a bearing, a depth, a hold. Two tides gone. Four left. And somewhere between him and the wreck lay the Meridian fleet, a corrupt salvage master who now knew his name, a government agency with a leak in it, and — possibly, impossibly, half a second of a second look away — his mother.

The river carried him downstream toward all of it, and in the dark ahead, at the edge of the fleet's anchor lights, a launch was already moving to cut off his wake.
