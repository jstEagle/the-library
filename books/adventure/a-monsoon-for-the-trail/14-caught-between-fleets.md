## 14. Caught Between Fleets

Tomas Beck had a theory about men who were good at one thing: they could always be bought, because the thing itself was the price.

He'd built Meridian Salvage on that theory. Divers who could hold their breath longer than anyone — bought with better air. Pilots who could thread the narrows at flood stage — bought with their own boat and a flag to fly from it. Even Ilsa Reyes's government handlers, God bless their little rubber stamps, had been bought with something as cheap as a routed telegram. Everyone had a single hinge, and Beck had spent thirty years learning where hinges lived.

The tracker was going to be easy. He could already tell.

They'd taken him at the second channel marker past the drowned church, which Beck considered almost insulting in its simplicity. Four men on a low barge, no weapons drawn until the last moment, and the man had simply stopped poling his skiff and looked at them with an expression Beck recognized from mirrors: *finally*. Three weeks of reading silt and following ghosts down a river that was rising by the hour, and when the net finally closed, Silas Villalobos hadn't even reached for the knife at his belt.

Now he sat across the chart table in Beck's cabin aboard the *Meridian Rose*, wrists loose in his lap because Beck's people hadn't bothered to tie them, and he was studying the charts upside down. That was the first confirmation. Ordinary prisoners looked at their captors. Trackers looked at the terrain.

"You're not going to ask why," Beck said, pouring two fingers of rum into a tin cup and sliding it across. The cabin rocked gently. Somewhere below, the pumps worked against the river's slow insistence, a heartbeat under everything.

"I know why." Villalobos's voice was flat riverwater, no current in it. "You can't find the site. Your sonar's good but your readers are bad. You've got divers who'll go down forty feet into a collapsing floodplain and you don't have one man who can tell them *where*."

Beck smiled and let it widen. "And?"

"And the channels shift every tide. What your pilots mapped three days ago is already a lie." He turned the chart right-side up without being asked, tapped a finger on the braided confusion of lines where the old Verraza channel met the drowned farmland. "Here. And here. Your approach runs through what used to be somebody's orchard. The channels will have moved twice since you drew this."

"Show me."

Villalobos looked up, and for a moment Beck saw the whole architecture of the man's pride: he wanted to show him. It was practically leaking out of him. Three years walking away from the only thing he was ever any good at, and put a shifting channel in front of him and his hands started moving before his mouth agreed to anything.

*There's the hinge,* Beck thought. *Not money. Not safety. Usefulness.* Men who measured themselves in miles would walk into any cage if you told them the miles mattered.

"Not yet," Beck said pleasantly, and drank his rum. "First, some housekeeping. I like my partners informed. It keeps them honest — or at least it keeps them calculating, which is nearly as good."

He opened the drawer beneath the chart table and laid out a folder. Inside: photographs, telegrams, a personnel file with a government seal stamped over in red CANCELLED. He turned the file around so the name faced Villalobos.

He watched the man read his own mother's name.

It was a small pleasure, watching it land. Not the flinch — Beck had expected a flinch, and there wasn't one, which was more interesting. Instead Villalobos went very still, the way water goes still over something deep, and Beck understood he was looking at a man doing arithmetic he'd already done and gotten an answer he hated.

"She's alive," Villalobos said.

"She's aboard." Beck leaned back. "Has been for eleven days. Came up the river on the *Corvina* under the name Elena Ruiz, dive tender, excellent references, none of them real. She's been running my sample logs and keeping my divers' rotation schedule." He let that sit. "She's very good at schedules. I imagine she learned organization raising a boy who grew up to be you."

"What does she want?"

"To be near the wreck. Which is where she is, so everyone's desires are currently aligned, which is my favorite kind of arrangement." Beck refilled his own cup, unhurried. "Though I'll confess her compliance has been — negotiated. She works for me because the alternative was being handed to the authorities as a stowaway on a vessel operating under contested salvage rights, and because—" he tapped the folder, "—I know what she did. Or rather, I know what the missing team found out she did. Planted artifact. Falsified provenance. Her entire career resting on a piece of pottery she buried herself in a pit outside Santa Rosa twenty-two years ago. You knew that part already, didn't you? That's why you left."

The stillness again. Deeper this time. Beck had seen men take bullets with less composure, and he filed the observation away: this one's wounds were all old, all scarred over wrong, and scar tissue was the best kind of leverage because the man himself kept it polished.

"Here's the shape of things," Beck went on, warming now, because this was the part he genuinely enjoyed — not cruelty, nothing so crude, just the clean pleasure of a mechanism engaging. "In approximately four tides, the monsoon arrives and this valley becomes a lake for a year. Before that happens, I need to reach a pre-colonial trading vessel sitting intact under forty feet of silt, recover what's valuable, and make certain that certain documents — field notes, core samples, a dead surveyor's honesty — never surface. Your mother helps me do that. In exchange, when we're done, she walks away with her reputation intact and my discretion thrown in free of charge."

"And me?"

"You—" Beck spread his hands, magnanimous, "—are the final piece. Because your mother can read a lab report and my pilots can read a gauge, but nobody on this fleet can read *water*, and the water between here and that wreck is rewriting itself every six hours. I need someone who can look at broken reeds and tell me where the deep channel ran yesterday and where it's going tonight. That's you. That's always been you, according to her file, which is extensive, and according to her, which is more interesting."

Something moved behind Villalobos's eyes at that — *according to her* — and Beck pressed it gently, the way you press a bruise to confirm it's yours.

"She talks about you, you know. When she thinks I'm not listening. Down in the sample room, to the young diver with the bad ear. Little stories. Teaching stories. How to read a drag mark disguised by rain. How the river tells you where it's been if you shut up long enough to listen." He shrugged. "She taught you everything you are, and then you walked out on her, and now the only place either of you can be is on my boat, working for me, toward the same patch of mud. Family reunions are like that. Everybody comes home eventually. Usually broke."

Villalobos stood. Beck's two men by the door shifted, hands moving, but Beck raised a finger — wait — because he wanted to see this. The tracker crossed to the porthole and looked out at the brown water sliding past, the fleet strung out behind them like beads on a wire, the sky in the west gone the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom.

"If I read your channels," Villalobos said, without turning around, "she goes free. Before the dive. Not after. Before. She steps off this fleet at the village on the flooded bank, and she's gone, and whatever happens after doesn't touch her."

Beck considered the request with the seriousness it deserved, which was none, and gave it the answer it deserved, which was yes-shaped.

"Done," he said. "Before the dive. My word on it."

His word was worth exactly the paper it wasn't written on, and both of them knew it, and that was fine too. A lie agreed upon was the strongest joint in any structure.

Villalobos came back to the table. Sat down. Picked up the chart.

And then he did the thing Beck would think about later, in the bad hours, when he tried to work out where the mechanism had slipped: he began to read the water aloud. Calmly. Precisely. Pointing out where the old channel had silted shut, where the flood current was cutting a new throat through the orchard, where the fleet would ground itself if it followed Beck's maps instead of his finger. He was magnificent. He was exactly what Beck had paid for, hunted for, leveraged into place.

He was also, Beck realized much too late, teaching him. Every word about the water was true, and every true word was also a wall — steering the fleet's approach through channels that would take days to unspool, buying time with accuracy, spending Beck's four tides like a man who knew the exchange rate better than the bank.

But that was later. In the moment, Beck just felt the warm click of the trap closing, all its teeth seated, leverage complete: the skill he needed bound to the woman he controlled, the woman bound to the secret, the secret bound to the wreck, the wreck bound to the rising river. Everything hinged. Nothing slipped.

"One more thing," Beck said, as Villalobos rolled the chart and marked the first waypoint. "Your mother asked for a message to be passed along, if I ever got you aboard. Her exact words." He savored it. "'Tell him the caliper was a message. Tell him to count the tides.'"

Villalobos's hand paused over the chart pencil. Just a pause. Then he wrote the waypoint in a small, exact hand, and said, "Take me up on deck. The light's going, and I want to see the reeds before dark."

Beck led him up himself. No escort needed anymore — or so he believed. On deck, the river lay wide and brown and patient, and the western sky had committed to its bruise, and somewhere below decks a woman with a false name was counting the same tides they all were, though Beck would have bet his flagship she was counting them differently.

Four tides to the monsoon. Two days of Villalobos's genius ahead of him. A fortune under the mud, and a fire under the whole arrangement that only he could feel.

He stood beside his new reader-of-water and watched the reeds bend, and thought: *everyone has a hinge.*

He was right about that. What he was wrong about — fatally, wonderfully wrong — was whose door the hinge was on.

* * *

Below decks, in the sample room that smelled of diesel and river mold, Marisol Villalobos logged the day's core samples in a hand that did not tremble, because she had trained it not to, years ago, in another life, signing reports she knew were lies.

The young diver with the bad ear had brought her word an hour ago: they'd picked up a tracker off a skiff at the church marker. Tall. Alone. Reading the banks with binoculars before the barge ever reached him.

She had known, of course. From the moment the fleet changed course two days back — a correction too precise to come from Beck's own pilots — she had known who was reading the water for them. She'd stood at the galley porthole and watched the western sky ripen toward monsoon and done the arithmetic every mother dreads: *he came for me, and I am the reason he's in the cage.*

She finished the log entry. Closed the book. Set her palm flat on the cover, the way she used to set it on her son's head when he was small and furious and needed holding down rather than holding.

Four tides. One dive. One chance to reach the evidence before Beck burned it, and one son aboard who believed she was a prisoner, when the truth was worse and simpler: she was exactly where she had chosen to be, paying exactly what she owed, and the bill was coming due in front of the one person she'd spent three years making sure would never have to watch her pay it.

Above her, boots crossed the deck. His boots. She knew the rhythm; she'd listened to it through tent canvas for eighteen years.

Marisol blew out the lamp and began, in the dark, to count.
