## 15. Tide Four, Drowning

The order came down at 0400, typed on agency letterhead and delivered by a man who wouldn't look her in the eye.

*All field operations in the Verraza corridor are suspended pending internal review. Coordinator Reyes is to return to the regional office by 1200 hours and surrender all survey materials.*

Ilsa read it twice, standing in the doorway of the river station with rain beginning to stipple the tin roof above her. The paper was crisp, official, signed by a deputy director whose name she'd only ever seen on budget memos. It was also, she understood with a clarity that felt almost physical, a lie wearing a uniform.

Suspended pending review. The same language they'd used on the Verraza team's radio traffic before it went dark. The same bureaucratic anesthetic that had let eleven people walk into a valley and evaporate while the ministry shuffled paperwork and the Meridian fleet moved upriver like a slow, well-funded tide.

"You're to hand over your files," the courier said. He was young, damp, uncomfortable. "There's a transport leaving from the dock at noon."

"I know how transports work," Ilsa said.

He flinched as if she'd raised a hand. She hadn't. Three weeks ago she would have apologized. Three weeks ago she had still believed the institution was clumsy rather than complicit — that the leaks were sloppiness, the delays incompetence, the redactions embarrassment. But she'd spent two nights going through the access logs on the survey route data, and what she'd found there had rearranged her understanding of everything: the route of the missing team had been pulled from the secure archive four days before the expedition launched, using credentials belonging to a deputy director's office. The same office now ordering her home.

They weren't recalling her because the operation was over.

They were recalling her because she was getting close, and a coordinator who vanished quietly was easier to explain than one who disappeared loudly.

"Coordinator Reyes?" The courier shifted his weight. "Do you need help packing?"

"No," she said. "I need you to tell them I left in a huff. That I was insubordinate. That I threw the letter in the river." She looked out at the water, gray-green and rising, already licking at the second step of the dock stairs when it should have been three steps below the first. "Tell them whatever makes them stop wondering where I am."

* * *

She took the smallest boat in the station's inventory — a flat-bottomed skiff with a temperamental outboard and no registration worth tracing — and she loaded it the way she'd learned to load boats during her field years, before the promotions and the offices and the meetings where maps were discussed by men who had never been rained on. Fuel, water, dried food, the waterproof case with Silas Villalobos's last known coordinates, her sidearm, and the file she was technically surrendering: the sonar plates, the core sample anomalies, the access logs printed on paper because paper couldn't be remotely deleted.

By 0530 she was on the water. By 0615 the rain had stopped being weather and become a condition of existence.

The Verraza in the fourth tide was not a river anymore. It was a slow-motion argument between the old channel and the new floodplain, and the floodplain was winning. Fields she'd charted dry on the first day were now shallow lakes studded with the black crowns of drowned trees. A fence line ran into the water and kept going, its posts marching down into murk like soldiers walking off a pier. Somewhere beneath all that brown silk lay the wreck — forty feet of pre-colonial hull and history and money — and somewhere upriver, in that drowned country, were Silas Villalobos and, if her instincts were right, a woman who should have been dead.

Ilsa had never met Marisol Villalobos. She knew the name the way everyone in the discipline knew it — the great excavation, the artifact that had rewritten the coastal trade routes, the monograph still assigned to graduate students. She knew the scandal-shaped hole in the record too, the way the elder Villalobos had withdrawn from public life after her son's accusations, though the accusations themselves had never been formalized, never investigated, just absorbed into the family silence like a stone dropped in deep water.

And she knew, from the moment she'd watched Silas read the abandoned campsite — crouched over silt lines with his mother's brass caliper turning slowly in his fingers — that this was not a search anymore. It was a story with two endings, and which one you got depended on who reached the wreck first.

The outboard coughed. She throttled back, feathered it, coaxed it back to life. Rain ran off her hood in ropes.

"Keep it together," she told the engine, or possibly herself. "Four more miles."

* * *

She found his camp at the mouth of a side channel where the flooded forest opened into a pocket of high ground — a low bluff of compacted clay that the rising water hadn't claimed yet, fringed with cane and a single massive fig tree whose roots gripped the bank like knuckles.

She saw the tarp first, strung low between two saplings, sagging under accumulated rain. Then the cold fire ring, ash darkened but dry-sheltered. Then the gear laid out with a precision that stopped her heart for a half-second before her mind caught up: bedroll rolled tight, pack squared against the tree trunk, mess kit stacked, boots placed side by side with the laces tucked in.

No tracker abandoned a camp like that. No tracker *could* — not in a hurry, not at gunpoint, not drowning. You fled a camp and it looked like a scuffle, a scatter, a story told in overturned things. This was different. This was a camp arranged the way a sentence is arranged. This was a message written in equipment.

"He wanted someone to find it," she said aloud, and her voice sounded strange and small under the fig tree with the rain coming down.

She stepped carefully, reading the ground the way he'd taught her without meaning to — not through instruction but through watching, three days of watching him move through terrain like a man reading aloud. The mud around the fire ring held prints: his, size eleven, deep at the heel. And others. At least six distinct sets, heavy-treaded rubber soles, the kind issued in bulk to salvage crews. They'd come in the night or the gray before dawn. There was no struggle in the mud. There was a conversation — two figures standing close, weight settled, patient — and then the whole party moving down toward the water, toward the skiff wake she could still faintly read in the disturbed silt along the bank.

Taken. Not killed. Taken, and allowed to arrange his camp first, which meant whoever took him wanted him cooperative. Which meant leverage, not execution.

Which meant Beck knew exactly who he'd caught.

Ilsa stood in the ruined quiet of the camp and felt the full architecture of it assemble itself in her chest: the leaked route, the vanished team, the recall order, the fleet moving upriver with government patience and pirate methods. And now the one tracker who could navigate the shifting channels was inside Meridian's net, along with —

Her eyes snagged on the boots again. On the pack. On the thing sitting on top of the pack, placed deliberately, centered, visible from ten feet away, from twenty, from the water itself.

The caliper.

Brass, green with age at the joints, and even from here she could see the repair — the handle wrapped in fine copper wire over a hairline fracture, a mending done by someone who valued the tool too much to replace it and too proud to hide the scar. Silas had turned it over in his fingers at the first campsite like a man holding a live coal. His mother's. He'd known it instantly; she'd seen the recognition hit him like weather.

Now it sat on his pack in plain sight, in a camp built to be read.

Ilsa approached it slowly and did not touch it at first. She crouched, studying the placement. Centered. Elevated. Facing the water — facing the approach any searcher would take. This wasn't abandonment. Abandonment was leaving a tool behind because you had seconds. This was *display*. Someone had come back to this camp after Silas was taken — or Silas had arranged it in the minutes before — and set the caliper where it could not be missed.

A message. But to whom?

To her. It had to be to her. He'd known she'd follow — or he'd gambled on it. And what was the message? She turned possibilities over like stones. *I'm alive.* Yes, that was in it. *They took me downstream.* Also in it, in the direction of the wake. But there was something else, something in the choice of object specifically. Not his knife, not his compass, not his own gear. *Her* caliper. The mother's tool, left behind by the son.

Unless it wasn't left by the son.

Ilsa went very still under the fig tree, rain drumming the tarp above her.

If Marisol Villalobos was alive — and every thread of this case insisted she was — then the woman hiding among Meridian's crews would have had access to this camp. Would have known its meaning. Would have understood that a caliper left in plain sight would be read by exactly two kinds of people: a tracker trained by its original owner, and a coordinator smart enough to ask questions. Either Silas had left it as a signal about his mother — *she's here, find her* — or Marisol herself had left it as a signal about her son — *they've taken him, and I couldn't stop it, and this is the only way I can speak without dying.*

Or they had left it together, in some stolen minute she couldn't reconstruct, and the message was simpler and worse than either:

*We are both inside the net. Come carefully.*

Isla closed her eyes for one breath. When she opened them, the camp looked different — not abandoned but *occupied*, haunted, two ghosts layered over the mud. Mother and son, both aboard Meridian's ships, both held by the same man who had ordered eleven government surveyors erased from a valley. And she was standing in the rain with a skiff, a pistol, a folder of evidence, and a career she had just finished burning behind her at 0400 that morning.

She thought about the transport leaving the dock at noon. About the deputy director's signature. About the version of her life where she obeyed, filed, survived, and spent the rest of it reading reports about bodies recovered after the monsoon.

"Damn it," she said softly, and picked up the caliper.

It was lighter than she expected. Warm, somehow, despite the rain — or maybe that was her hands. She wrapped it in cloth and put it in her breast pocket, against her sternum, where she could feel it when she breathed.

Then she did what the message asked. She read the rest of the camp.

* * *

It took her an hour, kneeling in the clay, and by the end of it her knees ached and her eyes burned and she had a route.

The heavy-tread prints converged at the bank and ended at a landing scar where a boat had idled against the mud — twin scours in the silt, a slick of bilge oil rainbowing on the floodwater. But there was more. Off to the east, half-hidden under cane, a second set of tracks: smaller, lighter-soled, a woman's stride, short and economical. They came down from the high ground after the main party left, circled the camp once — pausing longest at the pack, at the caliper — and returned uphill, toward the ridge trail that climbed away from the floodplain.

Marisol had been here. After. Alone. Ilsa was certain of it the way she was certain of sonar returns: the stride matched the posture she'd glimpsed once, briefly, on Beck's dock — a woman standing among Meridian crewmen with her shoulders square and her weight balanced like someone who had spent decades kneeling at excavation edges.

And the tracks told a story Ilsa read twice to be sure of it. The woman had come to the camp, had stood over the pack, had placed or adjusted the caliper — the prints at the site were fresher than the mud's drying allowed for anyone else — and then had walked away up the ridge. Toward Meridian's forward anchorage. Back *into* the net. Not fleeing. Returning.

Because her son was in it, and she would not leave without him.

Ilsa rose, legs stiff, and looked upriver. Through the rain and the drowned trees she could see the far bend where, according to the charts she'd memorized and then burned, the channels braided and shifted with every tide — the labyrinth Beck needed a tracker to thread, the approach to the wreck. Somewhere in that maze: two skiffs' worth of armed salvage men, a corrupt fleet master, a forged legacy, a buried ship, and the two Villaloboses, caught in the middle of all of it like flies in amber that hadn't hardened yet.

The fourth tide was rising around her boots. She could measure it against the fig tree's roots — an inch since she'd landed, maybe more. The forecast gave the monsoon's leading edge thirty-six hours. The forecasts had been wrong twice already.

She pushed the skiff back into the current, climbed in, and started the outboard. It caught on the third pull, coughing, then steadying into its rough heartbeat.

"Hold on," she said — to the engine, to the caliper against her chest, to two people she'd never properly met. "I'm coming. Both of you."

The skiff swung into the current and the floodplain swallowed the shoreline behind her. Ahead, the channels braided away into the rain, and the water was rising faster than anything she'd ever measured, and she was one woman in a tin boat running toward the largest salvage operation on the coast with stolen evidence in her pocket and no institution left to answer to.

For the first time in fifteen years, Ilsa Reyes realized, she answered to exactly one authority: the truth, and whoever was still alive to hear it.

Upstream, lightning stitched the clouds above the ridge line, and beneath the thunder — or perhaps she imagined it, perhaps it was only the river learning its new voice — she thought she heard the low boom of dynamite, far away, counting down.
