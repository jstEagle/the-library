## 16. The Long Dive

They woke him before dawn with a boot to the deck planks and a mug of coffee that tasted like rust and river.

"Up," said the man who'd kicked him. Voss, Beck's dive master — a slab of a man with a neck like mooring cable and hands that had clearly done things hands shouldn't do. "Beck wants you on the water in twenty minutes."

Silas sat up slowly, working the stiffness out of his shoulders where the cabin bench had spent the night punishing him for existing. His wrists were free now — they'd stopped binding him after the second day, once it became clear he wasn't going anywhere. The *Meridian Star* was anchored mid-channel in water too deep and current too strong for swimming, and everyone aboard knew it. The river itself was his cage, and the river was patient.

Twenty minutes later he stood at the stern rail watching the light come up gray over the floodplain, and Beck came to stand beside him with the easy warmth of a man greeting an old friend he intended to betray by lunchtime.

"Beautiful morning," Beck said. "You can smell it coming, can't you?"

Silas could. Under the wet-iron smell of the river there was something else now, something green and electric, the monsoon gathering itself beyond the ridgeline like a held breath. Four tides gone. Two left, maybe less if the forecasts were lying, and everything about the last week suggested the forecasts were lying.

"You wanted me up early," Silas said. "So say it."

Beck sipped his own coffee. "The channels shifted overnight. My sonar man says the approach we mapped two days ago is dead — the sandbar at the elbow moved forty meters east and there's a new scour hole where the deep line used to run. My divers go down blind, they burn air fighting current, and we lose the tide." He turned and smiled, and it was genuinely a good smile, which was the worst thing about him. "But you read water the way my sonar reads bottom. So this morning you're not a prisoner. You're a pilot."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I put divers down anyway, lose two of them to the current, and blame you for the delay in front of the crew." Beck shrugged. "I'd rather not. Wasted men make the others nervous, and nervous men make mistakes, and I hate mistakes. Also—" he said it lightly, the way a man sets down a heavy object and pretends it weighs nothing, "—your mother is aboard, and she's asked me, politely, not to hurt you. I'd hate to disappoint her."

There it was. The leverage, restated daily, polished like brass. Silas looked out at the water and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wouldn't cost him. He'd known since the night Beck first put him in irons that refusal was a luxury priced in other people's blood. All that was left to him was the question of what obedience could be made to hide.

"I'll need the survey charts," Silas said. "All of them. And a skiff with a sounding pole. If I'm reading your channel for you, I read it from the front, not from memory."

Beck's eyes narrowed a fraction — the merchant weighing a purchase — and then he laughed. "You want to scout it yourself. Of course you do. Trackers." He waved at Voss. "Give him the skiff. Two men with him. And Silas—" the smile again, warm as bathwater, "—the water's deep and the current's hungry. Don't get any ideas."

*Too late,* Silas thought.

* * *

The channel below the elbow was a liar, and Silas loved it for that.

He poled the skiff down the drowned farmland with two of Beck's men riding silent behind him — brothers, he guessed, from the way they moved, twin silences in matching oilskins. The floodplain opened around them like a drowned cathedral: fence lines standing in six feet of water, the tops of mango trees breaking the surface like drowning swimmers' hands, a church bell tower leaning where the soft ground had given way beneath it. Somewhere under all that water lay forty years of fields, three villages' worth of foundations, and one pre-colonial trading vessel buried in the silt like a secret kept so long it had become part of the earth.

He sounded the channel as he went, driving the pole down and reading the resistance — mud, mud, hard clay, the sudden hollow knock of scoured gravel. He called depths back to the brothers, who marked them on a slate. It was good work, honest work, the kind of work that used to feel like prayer to him before he'd learned what prayer could be spent on. Behind them, the *Meridian Star*'s dive barge followed at anchor-distance, its crane arm folded like a sleeping insect.

And all the while, some quiet room in the back of his mind was doing arithmetic.

The descent line. He'd watched them rig it yesterday from his bench-cabin window: four hundred feet of braided steel cable anchored to a concrete block, running through a block-and-tackle on the barge's crane, with guide ropes strung from the surface buoy down to the wreck site forty feet below. The divers would descend hand-over-hand along the guide rope, work in shifts, and come up the same way. In rising current, that line wasn't convenience. It was life. Lose the line, and a diver in zero visibility forty feet down had maybe ninety seconds of coherent thought before panic took the rest.

He couldn't cut it. Cut cable told a story — clean ends, bright metal, sabotage written in a language even Voss could read. But cable could *chafe*. Cable could wear against a rough anchor shackle, fray strand by strand over hours, fail at the worst moment without ever announcing why. Chafe was just the river being the river. Chafe was plausible deniability wearing work gloves.

He needed the anchor block. And the anchor block sat on the bottom.

* * *

The opportunity came at midday, when Beck himself came out to the site and decided, in his expansive way, that Silas should see the target with his own eyes.

"You keep looking at my water like it owes you money," Beck said, hauling him toward the dive station. "Fine. Look at it properly. Suit up."

Silas hadn't dived in three years. The suit smelled of rubber and someone else's sweat, and the regulator tasted of the compressor's oil, but when he rolled backward off the platform into the brown water, something in his chest unclenched that had been clenched since the highlands — because the water didn't care who he was, or whose son, or whose prisoner. The water only cared whether he paid attention.

He paid attention.

Forty feet down, the world was the color of strong tea. His torch beam carved a cone through suspended silt, and the brothers flanked him with their own lights, and the descent cable ran down past him into the dark like a plumb line dropped from heaven. He followed it, hand over hand, feeling the current lean on him sideways with the patience of a large animal.

Then the hull rose out of the murk, and every plan in his head went quiet.

It was enormous. That was the first thing — not the romance of it, not the history, just the sheer animal fact of a ship's hull looming out of the darkness like a hillside that had learned to float. The silt had taken her gently, cradling rather than crushing; she lay heeled slightly to port, her timbers black with age but sound, her frames still standing in their rhythm like ribs beneath skin. Barnacles had colonized her in white continents. A school of small silver fish poured through a gap in her planking, turned as one body, and poured out again, and the light caught them and scattered them into coins.

She was beautiful. Not museum-beautiful, not the beauty of glass cases and careful labels — beautiful the way a mountain is beautiful, indifferent and complete, having needed no one's permission to exist. Three hundred years old, maybe more, and she had crossed an ocean when his people's maps still had blank spaces on them, and she had died here in the shallows of a river that had since swallowed whole towns above her grave, and she did not care. She simply waited.

*This,* Silas thought, hanging weightless in the tea-dark water with his heart hammering against the regulator's rhythm, *this is why they kill for it.* He understood Beck suddenly, understood the salvage fleet and the forged permits and the eleven vanished surveyors — not forgave, never that, but understood. Some things made human greed look almost reasonable, the way a bonfire makes moths look reasonable. The wreck didn't ask to be worth dying for. It just was.

And that was exactly why he couldn't let Beck have her. Because men like Beck didn't see the waiting. They saw tonnage and auction estimates, and they would strip her frames for timber value and her hold for gold and leave the ribs bleaching on a barge deck, and the beauty would be spent like everything else they touched.

His torch swept along the hull toward the anchor point, and the quiet room in the back of his mind reopened for business.

The concrete block sat in the silt at the base of a scoured hollow, and the cable's lower termination was shackled to an iron eye cast into the block — and here was the gift the river had left him: the eye was rough-edged, pitted with corrosion, a jagged collar of rusted iron where decades of flooding had eaten at the casting. The cable ran across it at an angle, and the current pushed the cable against that ragged edge with every pulse of the tide.

It was already chafing. Barely. A few bright strands glinting where the galvanizing had worn through.

All he had to do was help.

He drifted close, torch angled away, and let his gloved hand find the cable where it crossed the eye. The brothers were ten feet off, sweeping their lights along the hull, counting frame stations for Voss's survey. The current pulsed. Silas worked two fingers under the cable and lifted it a half-inch, easing it fully onto the sharpest edge of the corroded collar, then pressed down — gently, so gently — dragging it back and forth across the metal. Once. Twice. Ten times. The sound of it was lost in the water's murmur. Bright wire showed through the dark strands now, a wound perhaps two inches long, frayed ends beginning to bloom like fiber-optic flowers.

Then he let the current take the cable back against the eye naturally, drifted away, and rejoined the brothers as though he'd been admiring the barnacles.

Above them, faintly, the barge's crane groaned as the surface crew tested the load. The cable hummed. Held. It would hold for hours yet — through the afternoon's dives, maybe through tomorrow morning's. And then, somewhere in the middle of the most critical operation Beck would attempt, with divers deep and the tide turning and the monsoon breathing down all their necks, the river would quietly take the line apart, and no one would ever prove it hadn't simply been the river's idea.

Hours. Maybe half a day. It wasn't much. But he'd learned on this river that half a day was a country you could hide a revolution in.

* * *

They surfaced into late-afternoon light gone the color of weak cider, and the sky to the west had changed while they were under. The clouds over the ridgeline had organized themselves into something with intent, a bruise-colored mass with a smearing underbelly, and the wind had come around and picked up a raw edge. The brothers noticed it. Everyone on the barge noticed it. Only Beck seemed energized by it, moving along the deck with his coffee cup, telling anyone who'd listen that they'd dive at first light and have the hold open by noon.

Silas toweled off, said nothing, ate what they gave him, and watched the cable.

At dusk, Voss ran the evening inspection — a big man's thoroughness, hands walking the line from crane to waterline, checking shackles and splices. He reached the surface end of the descent cable, examined the upper termination, found it perfect, and moved on. The lower end, forty feet down in the dark, pressed against a rusted eye by a patient current, he trusted completely. Why wouldn't he? Steel didn't lie.

Steel didn't. Rivers did. His mother had taught him that — taught him that water was the most honest thing in the world right up until it wasn't, that every current carried two stories and you'd better learn to read both. He wondered, not for the first time that week, what she was doing right now, somewhere below decks on this floating theft, wearing someone else's name. Whether she'd seen the sky. Whether she was doing her own quiet arithmetic, in her own quiet room.

The first drops hit the deck near midnight — fat, sporadic, tasting of dust and distance. Not the monsoon yet. Just the outriders, the scouts. By the time Silas closed his eyes on his bench-cabin bunk, the rain had found a rhythm against the deck plates overhead, steady as a drum, and he lay listening to it and doing the last of his arithmetic.

First light: Beck's divers go down. Mid-morning: the hold comes open, and whatever evidence the missing team hid inside it comes up into Beck's hands — unless. Late morning, if the current did its work: the descent line parts, divers scramble, the operation stalls, and in the confusion of a rescue drill nobody watches one tracker very closely. And Ilsa Reyes was somewhere upriver with a boat and a conscience, and Marisol Villalobos was somewhere below decks with three years of silence and, if she was half the woman who'd raised him, plans of her own.

Two tides left. One frayed cable. It would have to be enough.

Outside, the rain thickened, and far downstream — he couldn't have said how he knew, only that the river told him the way it tells trackers things — the water began, very quietly, to rise.
