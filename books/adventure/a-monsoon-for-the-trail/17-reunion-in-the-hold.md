## 17. Reunion in the Hold

The water in the hold came up to Silas's chest, and it was warmer than the river outside — older water, black water, water that had sat in the dark of a hull for four hundred years and learned the taste of wood. His headlamp cut a cone through it, and in the cone floated everything the river had been keeping: seeds, silt, a single bright fish that moved like a thought.

Above him, through the broken deck, came the sounds of Beck's operation — the groan of a winch, the clank of dive gear on planking, voices flattened by the water into something like prayer. He had been given a job: mark the cargo grid for the recovery cranes. He had been given a guard: a broad man named Ferro who stood on the deck above with a rifle slung across his back and boredom in his shoulders. And he had been given a deadline, because Beck always gave deadlines, the way other men gave handshakes.

Silas marked the grid. He was good at his work even when he hated it, and maybe especially then, because his mother had taught him that a surveyor's honesty lived in the marks he made when no one would check them. He drove the brass pins into sound wood, tied the lines taut, and all the while his eyes went to the far end of the hold, where the hull curved up into shadow like the inside of a ribcage.

The evidence was there. He knew it the way he knew rain coming — not by seeing it, but by the shape of everything around it. The dead team's leader had hidden the field notes aboard the wreck, trusting the river over the government, and Beck had spent three tides and two men's lives getting divers down to find them. What Beck hadn't counted on was that the notes were wrapped in waxed canvas and tucked into the joint of a structural beam, forty feet under a floodplain, in a hull that shifted with every tide. Finding them was one thing. Getting them out whole was another.

Ferro's boots crossed the deck overhead. Silas counted the steps out of habit. Eleven, pause, turn, eleven back.

Then the rhythm broke.

A voice — not Ferro's, lighter, sharper, a woman's voice pitched low and urgent — and Ferro answering with something that wasn't boredom anymore. Silas killed his lamp and went still in the water, letting the black hold take him, and listened the way his mother had taught him to listen: not to words, but to weight.

* * *

Marisol Villalobos had been "Renata" for eleven months, and in eleven months she had learned that a false name is a kind of diving suit: it keeps you alive down in the deep places, and it slowly kills you anyway.

She had joined Beck's fleet in Puerto Hondo with a forged certification and a face the salvage crews had no reason to know, because the world had spent three years believing Dr. Marisol Villalobos was dead — a rumor she had started herself, in the worst week of her life, when the first anonymous letter arrived with photographs of her own excavation records and a single line typed beneath them: *We know what you did. We know what the river knows.* She had let them believe it. It was easier to hide from a threat when the threat thought it had already won.

She had told herself she was tracking Beck to protect the site. That was true. She had told herself she was tracking Beck because if his divers reached the evidence first, he would burn it, and the last honest thing in her life would die with it. Also true. But beneath both truths, in the silt where she kept the things she didn't say, she had known for eleven months that she was also tracking the one person on earth who would never believe she was dead: her son. Because Silas did not believe in disappearances. Silas believed in tracks.

And now, on the deck of the *Meridian Star*, holding a pry bar she had taken from the tool locker with hands that would not stop shaking, she heard Ferro say into his radio, "The woman wants the hold cleared. Beck's orders — nobody down while we pull the samples," and she understood that the moment had arrived the way moments do in the field: not when you're ready, but when the ground shifts under you.

Because she knew what "pulling the samples" meant. She had watched the dive plan change two days ago, watched the waxed canvas come up in the net and Beck's face when he saw the government seal on the wrapping, watched him order it sealed in the forward compartment "for drying" — and she had watched him set a charge kit beside it, casually, the way a man sets down a cup. Beck did not intend to dry anything. Beck intended to make the evidence and the wreck and everyone who could testify about either of them into one undifferentiable ruin, and call it the monsoon.

She had ninety seconds, maybe. She had spent three years not being Silas's mother, and she had perhaps ninety seconds to become it again, in front of him, with guns and floodwater between them.

She swung the pry bar into the back of Ferro's knee.

* * *

The fight on the deck above lasted eleven seconds, and Silas tracked every one of them by sound: the crack of the strike, Ferro's weight going down, the rifle clattering across planking, a woman's grunt of effort — a grunt he knew. Not from memory. From *before* memory. From a childhood of listening to his mother haul gear up riverbanks, the particular economy of her breath, the way she spent it like a miser and cursed like a sailor when she overspent.

He was moving before he decided to move, hauling himself up through the broken deck on lines he had tied himself, and his head broke the surface of the flooded hold into lamplight and rain and the sight of a woman in a Meridian coverall standing over an unconscious man with a pry bar in her hands and her gray-streaked hair plastered to her face.

He had imagined this meeting for three years. In the imagining it had always been on land, in daylight, with words prepared. It was none of those things. The rain was coming down in ropes. The deck tilted with the tide. His mother stood in the middle of it looking at him like a woman looking at a door she had nailed shut from the inside.

"Silas." His name in her mouth sounded like it weighed more than the pry bar.

"You're dead," he said. It was the stupidest thing he had ever said, and he said it anyway, because his mind had been handed too much at once and had dropped everything except that.

"I know." She set the pry bar down, slowly, the way you set down a weapon in front of a frightened animal, and he realized with a jolt that he was the frightened animal. "I needed to be. Listen to me — we have very little time, and I am going to tell you everything, and you are not going to believe half of it, and you shouldn't, not yet. But the evidence — the field notes, the ones the survey team hid — Beck has them in the forward compartment with a demolition charge. He's going to sink the site with everything in it. Including us, if we're still aboard."

"The samples are here?" His voice came out flat. Tracker's voice. He was grateful for it. "In the hold?"

"In the forward compartment. Watertight bulkhead, still sealed — that's why he chose it. Silas—"

"You falsified it." The words came out of him like water finding a crack. "The Calzada figure. The one in every textbook. You *planted* it."

* * *

There it was. Three years of silence, and the first real sentence between them was an accusation, and Marisol found, absurdly, that she was relieved — because an accusation was at least a thread, and you could follow a thread.

"Yes," she said.

The word went out of her like a tooth coming free. Around them the rain hammered the deck, and the river breathed against the hull, and Ferro lay unconscious in a spreading puddle, and her son looked at her from four feet away as if she were a stranger he had been dreaming about.

"It was my second season at Calzada. I was thirty-one, I was out of money, the grant committee was meeting in six weeks, and the trench was empty. Not disappointing — empty. I had staked my whole career on a site that had nothing to say. And there was a dealer in the valley — a man named Ochoa, dead now, good — who had artifacts. Real ones, from the trade routes, but from the wrong stratum, the wrong river, forty miles downstream. And I told myself the most dangerous sentence in archaeology. I told myself: *it probably came from here anyway.*" She laughed, and there was nothing in it. "I buried it myself. At night. Alone. I went back the next morning and found my own life's work, and I wept over it, and the committee funded me for eleven years."

Silas said nothing. The rain said everything.

"The letters started three years ago. Someone had Ochoa's ledger — his sales, his dates. Someone had photographs. They didn't want money. They wanted the site. There are people — there were people — who needed Calzada discredited, because a real excavation on that stretch of river would have surfaced things they'd spent decades burying, and my forgery gave them the lever. Discredit the find, discredit the archaeologist, and everything around her goes dark too. I couldn't fight it from inside my own name. So I died." She spread her hands. "And I have been dead for three years, and being dead is very quiet, and I deserve the quiet, and I hate it."

"You could have told me." His voice cracked on the last word, and she watched him hate it for cracking. "You let me be the one who found it. You let me be the one who accused you. You let me walk out that door thinking I'd destroyed my own mother over a mistake — and it wasn't a mistake, it was *true*, and you let me leave anyway—"

"Yes."

"*Say something else.*"

"What do you want me to say?" And now her voice rose, and eleven months of Renata fell off her like a coat. "That I was a coward? I was. That I chose the work over you, every time, and then when the work turned on me I chose my own skin over you *again*? I did. That I have read every letter you never sent — because your friend Ana kept me posted, did you know that, did you know I have known where you were for three years, every contract, every valley, and I never once came, because I had decided you were better off with a dead mother than a fraudulent one?" She stopped. Breathed. The rain filled the pause. "I have had three years to compose my defense, Silas, and I'm telling you: I don't have one. I have a confession. It's what I've got. It's what I came up here with a pry bar to buy time to give you."

* * *

Silas stood in the rain and felt the three-year wall between them develop its first crack, and the crack was this: she was not asking to be forgiven. She had not, in ninety seconds, once asked.

"Show me the compartment," he said.

Her face did something he had never seen it do — something young, something unguarded, gone in an instant. She turned and led him aft along the tilting deck, past coiled line and stacked dive tanks, to a riveted bulkhead door with a Meridian padlock and, bolted beside it at ankle height, a demolition charge in a waterproof case, its wire running up into a firing box that Beck himself would carry.

"Two charges," Marisol said. "One for the evidence, one for the channel walls upstream. He's not just erasing the site — he's going to collapse the approach and let the monsoon do the rest. Anybody in the water when it goes—" She didn't finish. "The lock. Can you?"

Silas looked at the padlock, and at the bulkhead, and at the wire. Then he looked at the water rising through the deck grating, black and patient, and felt the tide in his bones — tide five, the river had told him that morning, though the forecasts still said four. The river had been early all week. The river was not going to wait for Beck's schedule.

"The lock's a seven-pin, salt-corroded," he said. "I can't pick it in time. But I don't need to." He put his hand flat against the bulkhead and felt it — the hum of the hull, the groan of wood taking the tide's weight. "The compartment's watertight, which means it's buoyant. When the water outside gets high enough, the pressure differential and the hull flex will pop that door at the seam. Beck's own flood is going to open it for us. We just have to be here when it does, and we have to get the canvas out before his charge does."

"And if the tide's early?"

"Then we get it out *fast*."

She looked at him then — really looked, the surveyor's look, reading terrain — and something passed between them that was not forgiveness and not absolution but was, for the first time in three years, *alignment*. Two people facing the same direction.

"Your grandmother taught me that trick," she said quietly. "Reading pressure through a hull. I never taught you."

"I know," Silas said. "I learned it from your field notes. The ones you thought I never read."

* * *

They worked. It was the strangest grace of the whole terrible night: they worked well together, wordless, the old choreography of mother and son on a dig site — she braced the pry bar against the seam while he fed line through the grating to give them purchase when the door gave, and neither of them said *I'm sorry* and neither of them said *I love you*, because the wreck was groaning around them like a living thing and the water was at their knees and rising, and some words keep better than others.

The padlock stayed on its hasp. The door did not.

It went with a sound like a whale surfacing — a boom of released pressure and a gout of black water — and the forward compartment exhaled four centuries of dark, and there, lashed to a cargo ring, sat a waxed canvas bundle with a government seal, dry as the day a dying man had trusted it to the river.

Marisol had it in her arms when the deck lights died.

Every light on the *Meridian Star* — gone, all at once, and in the sudden dark came the sound Silas had been dreading since dawn: the deep, rolling *whump* of a charge going off upstream, and the river answering with a roar that was no longer a river's voice but a wall's.

"The channel," Marisol breathed. "He's early. He's collapsing it *now*—"

The hull shuddered. The water around their legs began, very softly, to pull — not with the tide's patience but with the flood's hunger, drawing them aft, drawing everything aft, toward the breach and the dark and the monsoon coming down the valley like a debt being called in.

Silas got his hand on the line, and his mother's hand closed over his, and above the roar he shouted the only plan there was: "Hold on to the evidence. I'll get us out — I read floods better than he does."

The river pulled. They held.
