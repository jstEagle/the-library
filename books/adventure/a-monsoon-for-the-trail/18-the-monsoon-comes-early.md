## 18. The Monsoon Comes Early

The first sign was the birds.

Silas had been on deck of Ilsa's borrowed patrol boat for an hour, watching the western sky bruise itself purple, when the herons came downriver in a ragged, silent column — hundreds of them, flying low and fast, not feeding, not calling, just leaving. He'd seen that exodus once before, as a boy, the day a flash flood took out the old ferry crossing at Puerto Hondo. His mother had pulled him off the bank by his collar and told him: *The animals read the river better than we ever will. When they run, you run.*

He turned to shout a warning below decks and felt it instead — a pressure change, a thickening of the air, like the whole valley had drawn a breath and forgotten to let it go.

Then the horizon simply disappeared.

Not darkened. Not clouded. *Erased.* A wall of rain and wind where the sky used to be, moving upriver against the current with a low continuous roar that Silas felt in his back teeth. The fifth tide wasn't coming. It had already come, hours ahead of every forecast, and it was coming as something no chart had a name for.

"Get us to the east bank!" he yelled down the hatch. "Now! The channel's going to become a blender!"

Ilsa Reyes appeared at the top of the ladder, hair whipping, already soaked though the rain hadn't reached them yet. "The divers," she said. "Beck's divers are still down."

Silas looked at the water — brown now, confused, beginning to turn against itself in slow spirals where the flood pulse met the outgoing tide — and understood exactly what he was looking at. A river at war with itself. And forty feet beneath the surface, men on umbilical lines who were about to learn what that meant.

* * *

Marisol Villalobos heard the birds too, from the cramped cabin she'd occupied for eleven days under the name Elena Sarto, Meridian's "materials archivist," a title Beck had invented for her because it required no credentials and invited no questions.

She had spent those eleven days doing what she did best, which was reading other people's records the way her son read terrain. She knew which crates held the core samples Beck's men had quietly pulled from the wreck two nights ago. She knew they didn't match the artifacts catalogued in her own celebrated monograph, published twenty-two years ago to international acclaim. She knew, because she had written the lie herself, and lies have a texture that truth never does — smoother, more convenient, always slightly too perfect.

And she knew, because she'd overheard the radio traffic an hour ago, that Beck had ordered the descent line rigged with charges.

She had told herself she came aboard to protect the evidence. That was half true. The other half was standing somewhere on this river right now, and she had seen him only once, briefly, through a porthole — a man walking a dock with his mother's stride, and she had gripped the bulkhead so hard she left marks in her own palm.

Now the storm erased the horizon and Marisol made the decision she should have made eleven days ago.

She opened the crate with the samples, wrapped them in oilcloth, and walked out onto the flooding deck carrying twenty-two years of fraud in her arms.

* * *

Tomas Beck stood on the bridge of the *Meridian Star* and watched the wall of water come, and for the first time in his career felt something adjacent to respect.

He had planned for the monsoon. Of course he had — six tides, a schedule, a window. What he had not planned for was the schedule breaking. The storm was twelve hours early and moving like it had a grudge, and every hour of it ate into the one thing he couldn't buy, bribe, or salvage: time.

And there was the other problem, sitting in a locked drawer three feet from his elbow. The field notes. The dead surveyor's meticulous, damning documentation that the great Dr. Villalobos had planted her signature artifact — and that Beck's own divers had confirmed this morning, pulling cores from the wreck that matched nothing, that proved the site was real but her discovery was not. Evidence that would exonerate no one. Not the dead team. Not the government that leaked their route to him. Not the fleet whose contracts depended on a legend. And certainly not Tomas Beck, who had put four men in the river himself and signed the order that silenced eleven.

A junior officer stumbled onto the bridge, bracing against the roll. "Sir, the dive team — the surface line's fouled, we can't—"

"I know." Beck's voice was calm. Calm was his native language. "Recall everyone topside. Now."

"And the wreck, sir? The recovery—"

Beck looked out at the churning brown water, at the sonar plot showing the beautiful intact hull of a pre-colonial trading vessel buried in the floodplain — a ship that could rewrite history books, or bury them. He thought about the tracker the government had sent, the one with the famous name, currently loose on the river with a talent for finding things Beck needed lost. He thought about the woman in cabin six who read his manifests too carefully. He thought about the drawer.

"Gentlemen," he said pleasantly, "how much demolition charge do we still have aboard?"

The officer blinked. "For the channel work? Two hundred kilos, maybe—"

"Good. Rig it along the north cut. If the river wants this valley, we'll help it along." He smiled the smile that had closed a hundred deals and opened no graves anyone could prove. "We're not recovering the wreck anymore. We're retiring it. Full collapse — banks, channel, hull. Nothing surfaces, nothing testifies."

"Sir — there may still be divers in the water—"

"There may," Beck agreed, "still be witnesses anywhere, Mr. Okafor. That has rather been the theme of the week." He turned back to the window, watching the storm eat the far shore. "Rig the charges."

* * *

Ilsa Reyes saw the flash before she heard anything — a stutter of orange light deep inside the rain curtain, upstream, where the *Meridian Star* sat anchored over the site. Then the sound arrived, flattened and wrong, less a bang than a punch delivered through the water itself.

"The north cut," Silas said beside her, binoculars useless in the rain. He lowered them anyway. "They're blowing the channel walls. The silt shelf holding the floodplain—" He stopped, jaw working. "They're going to drop the whole valley floor on top of it. Bury the wreck under fifty feet of slurry."

"Why?" Ilsa demanded, though she already knew. She'd spent two days tracing the leak in her own agency, following a trail of requisition forms and deleted radio logs, and she knew exactly why men like Beck did arithmetic like this. Bodies were recoverable. Evidence was not.

"Because the wreck is the filing cabinet," Silas said. "And he's shredding it."

The patrol boat lurched as the first true gust hit, and Ilsa grabbed the rail and made the calculation she'd been avoiding since dawn. The divers. Her missing team — seven accounted for, four lost, and if any of the seven were still being held aboard Meridian vessels, they were aboard ships about to be caught between collapsing banks and a five-meter storm surge.

"We go in," she said.

"In *that*?" The young pilot stared at her.

"Silas can read this water better than your charts can. Can't you?"

He was quiet a moment, watching the river — the spiral eddies marking where the flood pulse met the ebb, the way the reed beds bent all one direction, the seam of cleaner water threading downstream along the east bank where the current hadn't fully turned yet. It was the same look he'd worn reading the abandoned camp, the same look his mother must have worn her whole life, and Ilsa suddenly understood that she wasn't looking at a man using a skill. She was looking at a man wearing an inheritance.

"There's a lane," he said finally. "Along the drowned orchard, the trees will break the surge. It closes in twenty minutes, maybe less." He met her eyes. "Twenty minutes after that, there is no east bank, no channel, and no wreck. There's just a lake with a secret."

"Then we're spending our twenty minutes well," Ilsa said. "Take us in."

* * *

The rain arrived all at once, the way a door slams.

Marisol was halfway across the *Star*'s flooded aft deck when it hit — a horizontal wall of warm water that knocked her against the rail and stole the breath she'd been rationing. The sample case was lashed to her chest with cargo strap. Twenty-two years of her life, wrapped in oilcloth, digging into her sternum with every step.

Ahead of her, moored to the *Star*'s port quarter, sat the workboat Beck kept for "channel surveys" — which everyone aboard understood to mean runs nobody logged. Its engine cover was up. Someone had been maintaining it against the storm, which meant someone believed there would be a use for it.

Behind her, boots on steel. Voices shouting over the wind. And then, cutting through everything, the flat electronic bark of the ship's PA:

*"All hands — demolition detail to the north winch. All hands—"*

Demolition. She had heard enough radio traffic to know what that word meant tonight. Beck wasn't salvaging the past anymore; he was executing it, and everyone who carried it in their heads.

Marisol ran for the workboat.

She was three steps from the gunwale when a hand closed on her arm and spun her around — Okafor, the junior officer, young enough that his face still broadcast everything. "Dr.— *Elena*, you can't be out here, get below—"

"Okafor." She said his name the way she used to say it to graduate students, with absolute authority and no room for doubt, a voice she had spent three years not using. "There are charges rigged on a channel with people in the water. You know it. I know it. In ten years, when someone asks you what you did during the Verraza collapse, you are going to answer me right now, or you are going to live with it."

His grip loosened. The wind screamed. Somewhere upstream, another charge went off and the whole river shuddered like a struck drum.

"Help me launch the boat," Marisol said, gentler now. "Please."

He looked at her for one long second — then shoved past her to the winch controls, and together, in the teeth of the arriving monsoon, they dropped the workboat into a river that was trying to become an ocean.

* * *

Silas felt the second explosion through the hull before Ilsa's radio confirmed it, a deep concussion rolling up through the keel like the river clearing its throat.

"Bank's gone," he shouted. Ahead, through the rain-smeared windshield, the eastern shoreline of the old channel was visibly failing — a quarter mile of floodplain sagging, sliding, surrendering its grip in long grey tongues of liquefied silt. The water over the wreck site was turning the color of milk chocolate, thickening, dying. In an hour it would be impenetrable. In two, whatever lay beneath it would be sealed under a new geology, and every truth buried with it would belong to the river for good.

And then, through the chaos, he saw it: a small workboat fighting out from the flank of the *Meridian Star*, running the collapsing channel with more skill than luck, one figure at the helm hunched against the rain.

One figure whose posture he knew.

Three years of not thinking about it, and his body identified her instantly — the set of the shoulders, the forward lean into weather, the particular stubborn angle of a head refusing to look back. He had inherited that posture. He had spent three years trying to walk differently and failed.

"That's—" Ilsa started.

"I know who it is." His voice came out strange, scraped hollow. The woman who had taught him to read water was driving a stolen boat straight into a collapsing channel, alone, carrying something lashed to her chest like a heartbeat.

The river chose that moment to demonstrate its opinion. A surge rolled out of the broken north cut — not a wave but a *displacement*, the whole surface lifting and tilting as millions of tons of silt-laden water went looking for somewhere to be — and Silas watched the workboat climb it, stall at the crest, and begin to slide sideways toward the boil where the orchard channel met the main current.

Every trick his mother ever taught him arrived at once, unbidden, in her own voice: *Don't fight the eddy, enter it late. Let the river spend itself before you spend yourself. The water always shows you the seam, mijo — it always, always shows you.*

"Helm!" he roared. "Hard to port, then follow my hand!"

The pilot obeyed. Silas leaned over the bow rail into the horizontal rain, arm outstretched, reading the flood the way no one had ever taught him — not the river his mother knew, but this new animal, this drowning, furious thing — and found the seam: a ribbon of green-grey water threading between two boils, brief as a lie, leading directly across the path of the drifting workboat.

They crossed it at full throttle. The workboat, spinning, caught the same seam thirty seconds later, and the two boats converged in the middle of a collapsing world — close enough now that Silas could see her face under the streaming hood, older, thinner, unmistakable.

Close enough that he could see her mouth shape his name.

The river slammed the hulls together, and his mother reached across the gap with both arms, and Silas — who had burned a telegram, walked away from miles, and sworn off every road that led home — reached back.

Above them, on the bridge of the *Meridian Star*, Tomas Beck watched the two boats meet through the rain-streaked glass, checked his watch, and gave the order for the final charge.
