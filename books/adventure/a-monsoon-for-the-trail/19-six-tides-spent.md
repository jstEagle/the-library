## 19. Six Tides Spent

The fifth tide did not arrive. It detonated.

Silas heard it before he saw it — a sound like the valley clearing its throat, a low grinding roar rolling up from the south where the river bent through the drowned orchards. Then the water came, not as a rise but as a shove, a brown wall shouldering through the channel and tearing the reed banks loose in slabs the size of doors.

"Off the bank!" he shouted. "Get off the bank!"

Marisol was already moving. Of course she was. Thirty years of fieldwork had wired her legs to obey the river before her ears did, and she was halfway up the slope of the old levee with the evidence case strapped to her back before Silas finished the sentence. Ilsa Reyes came behind her, hauling the skiff's painter over her shoulder, her agency jacket — the one she'd never wear again — already soaked to the shoulders.

Behind them, across the channel, the *Meridian Star* sat low and fat in the churning water, her floodlights carving cones through the rain. And along her rail, men were running with something long and heavy in their hands.

"They're going to blow the levee," Marisol said. Not a guess. A diagnosis.

Silas looked at the slope they were climbing, the old flood-control embankment the government had abandoned decades ago, and understood in one cold flash what Beck had understood an hour ago: if the channel collapsed, the wreck and everything in it — every core sample, every field note, every page of proof that Marisol Villalobos had built her life's greatest discovery on a lie — would go under forty feet of new silt, and no honest dredge would ever find it. Beck didn't need to win. He only needed to bury the game.

The first charge went off with a flat cough, not a roar, and the levee two hundred meters downstream shrugged and slumped into the river like a sleeping man rolling over.

"Move," Silas said. "Now. Cut across the orchard — the high ground runs all the way to the narrows."

"The narrows is where their boats are," Ilsa said.

"The narrows is where their boats *were*," Silas said, and pointed at the water.

* * *

He read the flood the way his mother had taught him to read a trail — except the flood was a trail being written in front of him, letter by letter, faster than any tracker could follow. That was the trick she'd never taught him, because she'd never had to learn it herself: a flood has a grammar. The surface told him where the water had been; the sound told him where it was going.

He listened. Under the roar there was a deeper note, a grinding bass note from the west, from the old quarry cut — water finding a second channel, the river splitting itself around the collapsing levee and pouring into the low ground. Which meant the main channel, the one Beck's flagship sat in, was about to lose half its depth in the space of twenty minutes.

"The *Star* draws four meters," he said. "In twenty minutes she'll draw mud. Beck's trapped himself."

"Then he'll be desperate," Marisol said.

"Then he'll be stupid," Ilsa said. "Desperate men with dynamite and nothing left to lose."

They ran. The drowned orchard was a maze of black trunks standing to their waists in moving water, and Silas led them through it by feel — by the lean of the trunks, each one bent downstream by decades of seasonal current, each lean a compass needle. His mother had taught him that on his first expedition, when he was nine: *Trees remember the water, mijo. People forget. Trust the trees.* He had spent three years trying to forget her. It turned out she was written into his hands, into the way he planted his feet on wet stone, into the instinct that made him grab Ilsa's arm half a second before the ground beneath them liquefied into a sinkhole and swallowed a wheelbarrow whole.

They came out of the orchard onto the spine of high ground above the narrows, and Silas stopped, and his breath went out of him.

The *Meridian Star* was dying. Not sinking — worse. She was settling, listing gently to port as the channel drained around her, her hull finding the sandbar that the flood had just built beneath her keel. Her floodlights swung drunkenly. On her deck, tiny with distance, men were cutting loose her launches, and one of the launches was already in the water, running for the levee breach with a searchlight stabbing the rain.

"Beck's coming ashore," Ilsa said.

"He's coming for the case," Marisol said. She had set it down between her feet, both hands on the handle, and Silas saw that her knuckles were white. "Everything is in here. The core samples. Ochoa's field notes. The photographs of the stratigraphy. Enough to hang Beck and enough to — " She stopped.

"To end you," Silas said.

She looked at him. Rain ran down her face and she did not wipe it away, and in the lightning-flash he saw that she was not afraid of the water or the dynamite or the men in the boat. She was afraid of the case, of what it would do to her name, and she was carrying it anyway.

"Give it to me," Silas said.

"You don't understand what's — "

"I understand exactly what's in it. I read Ochoa's notes in the dead drop. I've known since the tide was low, Mamá." The word came out of him like something dislodged, and he didn't take it back. "Give it to me. You carry the skiff lines. You're faster on the rope than either of us and you know it."

For a moment she was nine kinds of proud and nine kinds of broken, and then she handed him the case, and her fingers held on to his one second longer than the handoff required.

The second launch was in the water now. And the third charge, the big one, went off under the levee a hundred meters north, and the world tilted.

* * *

The levee did not slump this time. It *unzipped*. A seam of brown water shot through the embankment, widened, and then a whole section of the old flood wall folded outward and the river poured through the gap with a sound Silas felt in his teeth. The floodwave came down the orchard channel toward them, and behind it, riding the surge, came Beck's launch — riding the very water his own dynamite had made, using the collapse as a slingshot, because Tomas Beck had never met a disaster he couldn't turn into a shortcut.

"Down," Silas said. "Behind the trunk line. Let the wave pass us, let *him* pass us — he can't stop in this current, he has to run it through the narrows."

"And the narrows is a graveyard at flood stage," Ilsa said, reading his plan the way he'd read the terrain. "The channel bends around the quarry spit. At this volume, anything drawing more than a meter goes onto the rocks."

"Anything driven by a man who doesn't listen to the river," Silas said.

The wave hit. It went over them like a hand — they'd wedged themselves in the V of three intertwined trunks, and the water broke around the knot and spared them, mostly, though it tore the skiff lines out of Marisol's grip and took one of Ilsa's boots and tried, with a patience like a living thing, to peel Silas and the case out of the world. He locked his arms around the case and breathed when the water let him breathe, and through the brown chaos he watched Beck's launch come down the channel with its searchlight swinging, its engine screaming, Tomas Beck standing at the wheel with his coat gone and his hair flat and his face lit up with the pure joy of a man who believes the river works for him.

The launch hit the bend at full throttle.

Silas saw it happen in pieces: the bow lifting as Beck compensated late, the searchlight swinging up and catching, for one white instant, the quarry spit — black rock, standing water, a wall — and then the launch struck the spit with a crack that the thunder was gracious enough to cover, and the searchlight went out, and the river took the boat and folded it around the rocks like paper.

Men came out of the wreck of it. Two of them made the spit and clung there. Beck made the spit. Silas watched him drag himself out of the current, watched him stand up on that black rock with the flood tearing at his legs, and for one strange, suspended moment Beck turned and looked upstream — looked directly at the trunk line where Silas was buried to his chest in brown water with his mother's sin strapped to his back — and Silas would have sworn, later, that the man smiled. That even stranded on a rock in a drowning valley with his fleet beached and his empire dissolving, Tomas Beck looked like a man making a note of an account still open.

Then the river rose over the spit's lower shelf and the moment was gone, and there was no more time to watch, because the water around Silas's waist had begun to move in a new direction, and his mother's voice was in his head saying *the flood will tell you where it wants to go — your only job is to want the same thing.*

* * *

They rode it.

There was no other word for what they did, though Silas would spend months failing to explain it to inquiry boards. The flood wanted the quarry channel — the old cut, deep and straight, the river's new favorite — and so they gave themselves to it, the three of them and the case, riding the surge down the quarry channel in the dark with the rain coming sideways and the lightning showing them, one flash at a time, the walls of the cut sliding past. Marisol swam at his left shoulder and Ilsa at his right, and nobody spoke, because speaking meant drinking the river.

Twice the channel tried to kill them. The first time was a strainer — a fallen fig tree lodged across the cut, its branches combing the surface — and Marisol was the one who saw it, who screamed his name in a voice he hadn't heard since he was a boy, and they dove together, all three at once, under the branches and through, and came up coughing on the far side with the case still between Silas's arms. The second time was the eddy at the cut's mouth, a whirlpool the size of a courtyard, and there it was Ilsa who saved them — Ilsa, who had spent her whole career reading reports instead of rivers, who read this one anyway, and shouted *left, hard left, the seam, swim the seam* — and the seam was there, a ribbon of slack water hugging the wall, and they climbed it hand over hand into the willows above the old ferry landing.

They lay in the mud under the willows and the rain hammered the leaves above them like applause, and nobody moved for a long time.

Then Marisol started laughing. It was not a sane sound. It was the laugh of a woman who had spent three years hiding under a false name on a pirate's flagship and had just outswum the evidence of her own fraud, and Silas found, lying in the mud beside her, that he was laughing too, and that Ilsa was laughing, and that the three of them sounded like something none of them had been in a very long time.

"The case," Ilsa said finally. "Tell me the case is sealed."

"Double-gasketed," Silas said. "Ochoa's people built it to survive a year underwater. It'll survive an hour of us."

"Then we walk," Ilsa said. "The government barge is holding at the ridge camp. It's four miles overland, and every mile of it is going underwater by morning."

"Then we don't stop walking," Silas said.

They didn't. And somewhere in the second mile, in the dark, with the flood roaring through the valley below them and the monsoon finally, fully, arrived — the rain no longer an event but a condition, a climate, a new fact of the world — his mother fell into step beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, and said, without preamble, as if they were still nine miles and thirty years from finished:

"Your grandfather taught me the tree trick. Did I ever tell you that?"

"You told me you invented it."

"I did tell you that." She was quiet for three steps. "I lied about a great many things. That was the smallest one."

Silas kept walking. The case was heavy on his shoulder and the rain was in his eyes and the valley was drowning below them, taking the wreck and the fleet and the whole drowned century of it down into the brown water, and he found that the fury he had carried up the river like ballast — three years of it, then six tides of it — had gone strangely quiet. Not gone. He was not a man things simply left. But quiet. Quieted, the way a river quiets when it finally reaches the sea it has been arguing with all its life.

"You could have told me the truth three years ago," he said. "In the tent. That night."

"Yes," she said.

"That's it? *Yes?*"

"Yes," she said again. "I could have. I was a coward with a distinguished career. And you were right about everything, and I made you pay for being right, and there is no version of this walk where that sentence is not true." She looked over at him, rain and lightning and the raw honest dark. "You don't have to forgive me tonight, Silas. You just have to keep walking in the same direction. The rest takes as long as it takes."

Behind them, far downstream, something enormous groaned — the *Meridian Star*, maybe, settling deeper onto the sandbar that had caught her, the river's own hand closing around Beck's empire and holding it for whoever came to collect. Ahead of them, on the ridge, a light blinked three times. Green. The government barge's signal. Ilsa raised her own lamp and answered, and her hand, Silas noticed, was steady.

They climbed toward the light, the three of them, with the truth between them getting heavier and lighter at the same time.

And at the treeline, where the ridge began, Marisol stopped and turned once to look down at the drowned valley — at the water closing over the narrows, the orchard, the wreck, the whole argument of her life — and Silas stopped with her, and waited, and did not hurry her, because he was learning that this too was a thing his mother had taught him without ever teaching it: how to let someone look at what they'd lost before they walked away from it.

"We should keep moving," Ilsa said gently, from above. "The barge won't hold the ridge past dawn."

"We're coming," Silas said.

But his mother was still looking down at the water, and what she said next, he would carry the rest of his life.

"The diver," she said. "The one from my first expedition. The one whose token you carry." Her voice was very quiet under the rain. "I never told anyone what really happened to him. And tomorrow, when they take our statements — I'm going to tell them. All of it. Whatever it costs."

She turned from the valley at last, and took her son's arm, and they climbed together into the light.
