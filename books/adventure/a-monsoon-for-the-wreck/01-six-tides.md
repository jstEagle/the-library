## 1. Six Tides

The river gave up its dead the way old men give up secrets: slowly, grudgingly, and only when the current felt like it.

Kestrel Delacroix had been dredging the shallows off Gullet Bend for two days, dragging the *Marrow's Grace* through water the color of steeped tea, when the chain went taut and refused to come up. He killed the winch. He lit a cigarette. He waited for the snag to shake loose, because in eleven years of salvage he had learned that most of what the river held was garbage — fence posts, drowned mules, the occasional debt-ridden fool who'd decided the water was cheaper than his creditors.

The snag did not shake loose.

He backed the boat off, circled, and hooked it again from the downstream side, and this time the winch took the strain with a groan that traveled up through the deck plates and into his boots. Something rose out of the murk trailing silt like a bride's veil, and Kestrel leaned over the rail and looked at it for a long moment before he let himself believe it.

A chest. Iron-banded, lead-sheathed, sealed at every seam with black pitch. And stamped into the lid, deep enough to survive a century underwater, was a crest: a bridge broken in half, crowned by three stars.

Veymar.

Kestrel hadn't heard that name spoken aloud since he was a boy, back when the river maps still carried it. Veymar had been a nation once — a river nation, all tolls and trade law, sitting fat on the confluence until the floods came one monsoon season and took the whole country under. No war. No surrender. Just water, patient as a creditor, until there was nothing left to collect from.

He hauled the chest aboard and stood over it dripping on his deck, and he did not open it. That was the first strange thing, and he knew it even as he did it. Eleven years ago he'd have had a crowbar in his hand before the water finished draining off the lid. But something about the seal made him want to keep it whole a little longer, the way you keep a letter unopened when you already know who died.

He ran cargo instead. Two hundred sacks of rice upriver to Corvane Landing, because hunger paid better than curiosity and curiosity had never once fed him.

* * *

They came at dusk, three gunboats riding low and dark, no running lights, holding station a half-mile astern like wolves that hadn't decided yet whether he was worth the trouble of a chase.

Kestrel counted them twice from the wheelhouse window while pretending to check his wake. Fleet boats. He knew the lines — shallow draft, armored pilothouses, the twin exhaust stacks that meant engines built for pursuit rather than freight. The Meridian Salvage Consortium didn't run gunboats for show. They ran them to remind the river who owned it.

His hands were steady on the wheel. His mouth was dry.

At full dark, a signal lamp blinked from the lead boat. Three long flashes, two short. A code so old it predated the Consortium itself, and Kestrel felt it land in his chest like a hook finding flesh.

*Come alongside. We should talk.*

Nobody used that code anymore except one person. Nobody alive.

He anchored in the lee of a drowned churchyard — the spire still broke the surface at low tide, a black finger accusing the sky — and watched the lead gunboat slide up alongside with the smooth arrogance of a thing that had never been told no. A gangway came across. Boots on his deck. And then a figure filled his wheelhouse doorway, broad as a doorframe, silver at the temples now but with the same heavy jaw, the same eyes like wet slate.

Captain Ilsa Voss. His mentor. The woman who'd taught him knots, navigation, and how to read a river's mood by the smell of it. The woman whose crew he'd walked away from eleven years ago, and whose crew had kept moving without him afterward, right up until the night they stopped moving too.

"Kestrel." She said his name like she was setting down something fragile. "You look terrible."

"You look rich."

"I am." She stepped inside, uninvited, and her gaze went straight past him to where the chest sat lashed under canvas amidships. She didn't pretend otherwise. She'd never wasted time pretending. "You dredged something today."

"Fence posts."

"I bought your dredge log from the harbormaster at Corvane." She smiled, and it was almost warm, which was worse than cold. "Chain tension spiked at 1400 hours. You brought up four hundred pounds of dead weight and then covered it with a tarp. You always were a sentimental bastard."

Kestrel leaned against the wheel. "Say what you came to say, Ilsa."

She reached into her coat and set a document on his chart table. Heavy paper. An embossed seal — not Veymar's broken bridge, but the Consortium's: a net drawn tight around a river's delta.

"A concession permit," she said. "Salvage rights to the Veymar exclusion zone. All of it. Every wreck, every artifact, everything the flood left behind." She tapped the paper once. "It expires in six tides. After that, the zone reverts to open claim, and the Consortium's lawyers stop being polite about it."

"And you're offering it to me."

"I'm offering to buy what you pulled up. Name a price." Her slate eyes held his. "Because we both know what's in that chest, Kestrel. And we both know you can't open it alone, and you can't sell it anywhere the Consortium doesn't have eyes, and you can't outrun three gunboats in a rice barge with a tired engine. So the smart move — the move I taught you — is to take the money and let smarter people do the dangerous part."

Kestrel looked at the permit. Six tides. Six turns of the water, and then the last drowned country in the world belonged to whoever got there first.

He thought about saying yes. God help him, he thought about it honestly — the money, the clean exit, the end of running rice and scrap up a river that had already taken everything he loved. It would be so easy to stop wanting things.

But underneath the dread, unwelcome as a stowaway, something else had woken up. Hunger. The chest sat under its canvas like a heartbeat, and for the first time in eleven years Kestrel Delacroix wanted to know what was inside more than he wanted to keep moving.

That scared him more than the gunboats did.

"No," he said.

Ilsa studied him for a long moment. Whatever she saw in his face, it made her sigh, and the sigh sounded almost like grief.

"I taught you better than this," she said. "Six tides, Kestrel. Then the gloves come off, and my employers don't send people who remember tying your first bowline." She moved toward the gangway, then paused at the rail, silhouetted against the running lights of her fleet. "One more thing. The chest isn't salvage. It's bait. Somebody wanted it found — just not by us. Ask yourself who seals a message in a dead nation's language and drops it where a lonely dredger might bite."

Then she was gone, and the gunboats withdrew downriver, their wakes slapping against his hull like slow applause.

* * *

Kestrel sat up all night with the chest between his feet and a lantern turned low, and near dawn he did what he'd sworn not to do. He cracked the seal.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, lay no gold. No coin, no plate, no jewels. A single sheet of vellum, dense with script in the Veymar hand — and beneath it, a smaller page in trade-common, written recently, ink barely faded:

*To whoever pulls this up: the charter is real. The wreck holds the original. Permits die in six tides. Come to the flooded cathedral before the fleet does, or the river belongs to them forever.*

*— O.*

Kestrel read it three times. Then he folded it away, banked the lantern, and stood at the wheelhouse window watching the first gray light come up on the water.

Six tides. A drowned cathedral somewhere south. A stranger named O. who knew things about him he'd never told anyone — including, somehow, that he'd be the one to bite.

He should burn the letter. He should sell the chest to Ilsa and run rice until his engine died and the river finally collected.

Instead he rang the engine telegraph to *ahead slow*, and the *Marrow's Grace* turned south, toward the wreck, carrying a captain who had spent eleven years learning that stopping was the same as drowning — and who had just, against every instinct he had left, started moving toward something he couldn't leave behind.
