## 2. The Cartographer's Debt

The chest sat on the deck all night, and Kestrel did not sleep.

He'd lashed it to the mast instead of opening it, which told him something about himself he didn't care to examine. A man who hauled up a sealed box stamped with a dead nation's seal and then tied it where he could watch it — that was a man waiting for the box to do something. Or for someone to come for it.

Someone came at dawn, but not from the river.

"Kestrel Delacroix." The voice came from the gangplank, low and precise, an accent that had been sanded down by years of exile until only the vowels remained sharp. "You look terrible."

Odile Marchand stood at the rail in a coat too heavy for the season, her gray-streaked hair pinned back like she was still reporting to the Imperial Survey Office and not sleeping in ferry terminals. She carried a leather tube slung across her back the way other people carried knives.

"You're supposed to be in Vellport," he said.

"I was in Vellport. Then gunboats with no registry started asking after you, and I remembered I owed you a life." She stepped aboard without invitation, the way she always had. "So here I am, paying it off early, before the interest compounds."

"The fleet's already found me?"

"They found your wake. You dredge loud, Kestrel." She looked at the chest, and something crossed her face that he'd only seen once before — the day the Survey Office struck her name from its rolls. Recognition. Grief. "Open it."

"Not until you tell me why a disgraced cartographer is standing on my deck at dawn."

"Because I drew the map that says what's inside it." She sat down on a crate, unhurried, making herself at home in his misery. "Six years ago the Empire commissioned a survey of the drowned Veymar concession. Officially: salvage assessment. Unofficially, they wanted one document. The charter."

Kestrel kept his face still. "Charter of what?"

"Of everything." Odile leaned forward. "When Veymar went under — the floods, the bankruptcy, the whole nation sliding into the river — their crown charter never surfaced. It grants perpetual riparian rights to the entire navigable length. Every dock, every toll, every mile of water from the headwaters to the delta. Whoever holds that paper doesn't own treasure, Kestrel. They own the river. Legally. Enforceably. Forever."

The morning fog moved between them. Somewhere downstream, a fleet was burning fuel to find him.

"And you know where it is," he said.

"I know where it *was*. The cathedral wreck. And I know the permits expire in six tides, because I helped write the expiration clause, back when I thought I was drawing maps instead of traps." She smiled without warmth. "The Empire buried the location. The fleet's owner has been looking for it for a decade. And last night, Captain, you pulled up the bait they've been trailing behind them like a lure on a line."

"Bait."

"That chest? Open it."

He cut the lashings. Inside, under oilcloth, lay no gold, no plate. A single sheet of vellum, blank except for a seal — Veymar's twin towers beneath rising water — and a line of script in a dead language Odile translated without touching it:

*"Whoever raises this from the deep holds our claim. Let the river judge the worthy."*

"It's a promise," Odile said quietly. "A placeholder. The real charter sits in the wreck, sealed in the reliquary vault. That copy tells whoever finds it that the claim is live, transferable, and waiting. It's an invitation. Someone wanted you to find it specifically — or wanted anyone to find it, so the fleet would follow them to the wreck and do the dangerous work."

Kestrel closed the chest. His hands were steady, which surprised him. "Then I throw it overboard and we both forget this conversation."

"You could." She shrugged. "But the permits expire in six tides, and when they do, the wreck becomes open salvage to every hull on this river — including hers. And you've spent eleven years running cargo because stopping means remembering. Don't tell me this isn't the first thing that's made you want to stop."

He didn't answer. That was answer enough.

* * *

Odile's POV

Odile had catalogued four hundred wrecks in her career and trusted exactly two people, and one of them had died owing her money.

She watched Kestrel now from the wheelhouse as he argued with his deckhand — a wiry girl with Sefa's jaw and none of Sefa's patience — and felt the old cartographer's habit of measuring distances assert itself. Distance from Kestrel to the girl: three feet, and a chasm. Distance from Kestrel to the chest: ten feet, and a lifetime.

Sefa Delacroix had come aboard at Colette's Landing with one bag and a grievance, summoned by a letter Kestrel clearly hadn't expected her to answer. Niece. Estranged. She handled lines like she'd been born to it and spoke to her uncle like he owed her a decade of explanations, which, by Odile's reckoning, he did.

"—and you didn't think to mention there were *gunboats*?" Sefa was saying.

"I mentioned it."

"You mentioned it like a man mentioning weather. 'Might rain, might get shot at, tie off the aft line.'"

"That's how weather works on this river."

Odile let them wear themselves out. Families were not her discipline. Wrecks were. And there was a third problem, one she'd discovered at dawn when she'd gone looking for the bilge pump and found instead a boy curled among the spare canvas like a stowaway who hadn't learned yet that stowaways got thrown off.

He couldn't have been more than seventeen. Lean, salt-scoured, with the callused hands and equalized ears of a working diver. When she'd shone the lamp on him he hadn't run. He'd said, "I can hold my breath four minutes and I know the cathedral wreck's outer gallery," and then, when she asked who sent him, "Nobody. I sent myself. My village drowns a little more every spring, and I heard there's a paper down there that could fix that. Or sell it to someone who would."

Now she brought him up on deck, by the collar, metaphorically speaking.

"This is Tam," she announced. "He's been living in your canvas since Colette's Landing."

Kestrel turned. Sefa turned. Tam stood very straight, the way boys do when they expect to be hit.

"Heard the whole speech about the charter," Tam said. "Walls are thin. I dive. You need a diver. Seems simple."

"It isn't simple," Kestrel said. "It's a death sentence with paperwork."

"Everything on this river is." The boy didn't blink. "My village will be underwater in five years either way. At least this way I'd be underwater doing something."

There was a silence. Odile watched Kestrel weigh it — she could see the scales moving behind his eyes, the same scales that had weighed eleven years of motion against one night of stillness. He lost crew once by stopping. Now the river was offering him a reason to move fast enough to lose more.

"Four minutes," Kestrel said finally. "And you follow orders like they're scripture."

"Yes, Captain."

"Don't call me that yet." But his voice had changed, and Odile, who mapped coastlines for a living, recognized a coastline changing shape when she saw one. A crew was accreting around him the way silt accretes around a hull: against everyone's will, one grain at a time.

* * *

They cast off at noon, six tides on the clock, and Odile spread her charts across the wheelhouse table while Sefa took the wheel and Tam, uninvited, memorized everything.

"The cathedral sits in the flooded district, forty miles upriver past the Split," Odile said, tracing the line. "Deep channel most of the way. But the fleet knows we'll go, and they're faster."

"So we don't beat them," Sefa said. "We arrive when they're already busy being wrong."

Odile looked at her. "You've done this before."

"I've run cargo with a smuggler uncle. I've done worse than this before breakfast." Sefa's hands were easy on the wheel, her eyes hard. "Don't mistake me for crew, though. I'm here because my mother asked me to look after him, and my mother is dead, and I keep my promises. The minute this turns stupid, I'm gone."

"Understood," Odile said, and meant it, and privately began drafting a chart titled *Things This Girl Will Deny Feeling.*

Tam appeared at the door with four cups of tea nobody had asked for. He handed them out. Nobody thanked him. He stayed anyway, leaning in the doorway, drinking his own.

That night, anchored in the reeds below the Split, Odile came topside and found Kestrel alone at the stern, watching the dark water.

"Regrets?" she asked.

"Several. They're keeping."

"Good. Regrets mean you're planning to survive long enough to have them properly." She unrolled a fresh sheet against the rail and began sketching by moonlight — the cathedral, from memory, its drowned nave, its reliquary vault below the waterline. "One thing you should know before we commit. The fleet's owner. I've seen her manifests. She's not hiring salvagers, Kestrel. She's hiring lawyers and gunboats in equal measure. Whatever she wants from that wreck, she intends to make it legal afterward."

Kestrel was quiet a long moment. Downriver, faint as a rumor, a searchlight swept the far bank and slid away.

"She's coming," he said.

"She was always coming. The question is what we do in the five days she gives us." Odile capped her pen. "Get some sleep, Captain. Tomorrow we recruit the rest of our luck."

Behind them, unnoticed, Tam slipped back down into the hold — and stopped, halfway down the ladder, staring at the chest lashed near the mast.

At the blank vellum inside.

At the way, when the moon hit it just right, faint lines were beginning to show through the surface, like a map waking up.
