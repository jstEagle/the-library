## 3. What the River Keeps

The *Marrow* died at dawn, and Kestrel knew it before he opened the engine hatch.

Not a rattle. Not a cough. A silence — the particular, surgical silence of an engine that had been murdered. He knelt in the bilge and ran his thumb along the fuel line and found the cut, clean as a barber's work, made with a blade that knew engines the way a priest knows scripture. Beside it, wedged under a pipe clamp, a sliver of river-glass. Fleet glass. They'd been aboard while the crew slept.

"They came through the water," Tam said from the hatch above, dripping, her dive knife still on her belt. She'd been swimming the anchor line out of habit, not caution. "Cut the sea-cock screen, slipped in through the well. I felt the wake go past me. Thought it was a log."

"Logs don't leave tips." Kestrel held up the glass.

Odile appeared at his shoulder, chart tube clutched to her chest like a child. She'd slept in her boots for three nights now, and it showed in the gray of her face. "They didn't take anything," she said. "That's worse."

"It's a message." Kestrel stood, wiped his hands on his thighs. "We can't run. So we'll walk."

Sefa was at the wheel, pretending to steer a dead boat downriver, her jaw set in that way she'd inherited from a brother nobody talked about anymore. Kestrel watched her a moment too long. Three days ago she'd come up the gangplank with a duffel bag and a grudge, and he still didn't know which weighed more.

* * *

The fleet found them by midmorning, but not with guns this time.

Two gunboats slid alongside, close enough to read the rust blooms on their hulls, and a launch puttered between them carrying a single passenger: a woman in a white linen suit that had no business on a working river, spotless, as if the water itself were afraid to touch her. She introduced herself as Madame Corvane. She did not offer a hand.

"You have something of mine," she said pleasantly, standing on the *Marrow*'s deck like she owned the planks. "And I have something of yours."

Kestrel's stomach dropped before she finished the sentence. He looked past her, at the nearer gunboat, where two crewmen held a small figure by the arms. Sefa. Her mouth was gagged with river-cloth, but her eyes were furious, and furious eyes meant alive, meant fighting, meant time.

"She boarded my tender at the fuel stop," Corvane said. "Looking for you, I imagine. Family is so often reckless."

Kestrel kept his voice level. It cost him. "She's my deckhand."

"Of course." Corvane smiled like a ledger balancing. "Here is the arithmetic, Captain. The charter, or the girl. You have until the tide turns — four hours. After that, the river keeps what it keeps." She glanced at the dead engine hatch, almost fondly. "You're stranded anyway. I did you a courtesy. Now you can't do anything stupid."

Then she stepped back into her launch, and the gunboats withdrew upstream, taking Sefa with them, and the *Marrow* sat dead in the current like a gutted fish.

Nobody spoke for a long minute. The river slapped the hull. Odile's knuckles were white around the chart tube.

"You're not actually considering it," Tam said finally.

"I'm considering everything," Kestrel said. "That's the job."

"The charter legalizes her claim over the whole river. Every town, every crossing, every boat. People who've fished these waters for six generations would be paying tolls to a woman in a clean suit."

"I know what it does."

"Then—"

"Sefa is nineteen," Kestrel said, and the words came out harder than he intended, cracking something open. "I've already buried one crew because I wouldn't stop moving long enough to look after them. I am not doing it twice. Not her."

He turned to Odile. "How far to the wreck?"

Her eyes went wide. "Captain—"

"How far?"

"Six miles. Maybe seven. Through the Narrows, then the drowned orchard, then the cathedral reach." She swallowed. "But the tide turns in four hours, and without the engine we can't fight the current. We'd be poling against the monsoon surge. Nobody poles the Narrows. Nobody."

"Good," Kestrel said. "Then nobody will be looking for us there."

* * *

They stripped the *Marrow* of everything that could serve as a pole — boathooks, a broken oar, the spare mast cut into three lengths. Kestrel took the bow. Odile navigated from memory, calling depths from a chart she'd drawn herself years ago and been disgraced for drawing too honestly. Tam swam the shallows ahead, reading the bottom with her hands.

The Narrows took everything they had.

The current came down through the rock throat like a freight train, brown and muscular, and for every foot they gained they lost two to the river's opinion of their efforts. Kestrel planted his pole and leaned until his shoulders screamed, and the boat crept, inch by inch, up a river that wanted them gone. His palms split by the second mile. He wrapped them in cloth and kept poling.

At the drowned orchard, the water went quiet and strange — old fruit trees standing to their crowns, dead branches combing the surface, and somewhere beneath them the roofs of a village that Veymar had been when Veymar was a nation. Tam surfaced beside the hull, breath ragged.

"There's a channel," she gasped. "Under the trees. Deep enough. But Captain—" she hesitated. "There are bodies down there. Old ones. Sitting in the doorways."

"Veymarites?" Odile asked softly.

"People," Tam said. "That's all I know how to call them."

Kestrel poled them through without looking down. But he thought about it, all afternoon, all those patient dead in their doorways, waiting for a tide that never came back for them. He thought about stopping. About what it meant, finally, to stop — not the way a boat stops, dead in the water, but the way a person does, choosing a shore.

The tide began to turn as they entered the cathedral reach.

He felt it first in the pole — the current shifting its grip, the river changing its mind. Behind them, downstream, the water started running backward toward the sea, dragging silt and leaves and everything loose toward the coast. Ahead, rising out of the flood plain like the ribcage of some drowned god, stood the cathedral: towers breached, nave flooded to the clerestory windows, bells hanging silent in a belfry full of river birds.

And moored in its shadow, gunboats. Four of them. Corvane's launch tied alongside.

Sefa stood on the gunwale of the nearest boat, ungagged, guarded by two crewmen — and she was looking straight at the *Marrow*, and she was shaking her head, slowly, deliberately, telling him to go away, telling him she wasn't worth it.

Kestrel shipped his pole. His hands were ruined meat. He looked at the woman who had raised half herself on stories of him and hated him for them, and he said, quietly, so only his crew could hear:

"Get the charter ready."

"You're trading it," Odile said. Not a question. Her face did something complicated — relief and grief fighting for the same ground.

"I'm trading it for her." He met Tam's eyes, then Odile's. "The river's been keeping things from people my whole life. Today it doesn't get to keep her."

* * *

Corvane received him on her launch's aft deck, Sefa brought forward and released into Kestrel's arms the moment Odile handed over the chart tube. Sefa gripped him hard enough to bruise, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing either of them knew how to say yet.

"A pleasure doing business," Corvane said, unrolling the charter across her charts, checking the seal against her loudeye. "You're a smarter man than your reputation, Captain Delacroix."

"No," Kestrel said. "I'm exactly the man my reputation says. That's why you should count your crew."

Corvane frowned. "What?"

"Your divers checked the wreck's crypt last week. Did they check the reliquary vault beneath it? Because the charter has a twin — a countersigned duplicate, sealed in lead, lodged in the vault. Imperial law requires both halves to validate a claim. You have paper, madame. You don't have the claim."

It was a lie. Mostly a lie. Odile had told him about the vault two nights ago — a possibility, a rumor in the cartographer's guild records, unconfirmed. But Corvane's face flickered, just once, and Kestrel saw that the rumor was true enough to matter.

"That's impossible," Corvane said. "My surveyors—"

"Missed it. Cartographers usually do." He nodded toward Odile, standing at the rail behind him. "Hers don't."

For a moment Corvane simply looked at him, and Kestrel understood that she was recalculating everything — the permits, the tides, the monsoon building black on the southern horizon. Then she smiled again, thinner now.

"Then I'll need you to show me the vault, Captain. Consider it part of the price. Your boat stays impounded until the transaction completes." She gestured, and crewmen began casting off the *Marrow*'s lines, towing her dead hull toward the cathedral's flooded west door, into the dark.

The first raindrop hit the deck like a thrown stone.

Kestrel stood with his niece trembling against his side, his hands bleeding through their wrappings, and watched the monsoon come down the valley wall like a curtain falling on the whole rotten play — and thought, with a calm that surprised him:

*Good. Let it come.*
