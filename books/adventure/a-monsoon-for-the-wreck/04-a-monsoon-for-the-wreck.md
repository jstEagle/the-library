## 4. A Monsoon for the Wreck

The cathedral had been drowning for two hundred years and still refused to lie down.

Kestrel came through the broken rose window feet-first, Tam's line humming against his harness, and the flooded nave opened below him like a throat. Silt hung in the water in slow gold curtains. Where the altar had been, the river had planted a forest of black coral, and fish moved through the pillars the way congregants once moved toward communion — unhurried, certain they belonged.

He'd dived wrecks for thirty years. He knew how to read them: where the hull would give, where the current pooled, where the dead kept their valuables. This one he read differently. This one felt like a held breath.

The charter was in the crypt. Odile's charts said so, and Odile's charts were never wrong — only ever paid for too dearly.

* * *

They were waiting for him at the crypt stair, lanterns burning behind glass, because even salvage barons liked their light dry. Captain Vess stood with her hands clasped behind her back like a woman at a funeral she'd paid for. And beside her, small and silver-haired and immaculate in a diving suit worth more than Kestrel's boat, stood the owner of the fleet.

Maren Veymar-Loch. The name on half the permits expiring up and down this river. The last living claimant of a drowned nation.

"Captain Delacroix." Her voice carried strangely underwater, softened by the medium, made almost kind. "You brought me my charter."

"You sent me a chest," Kestrel said. His rebreather rasped. "Bait floats better than truth."

"And you bit. That's why I chose you." She gestured, and Vess unrolled a document case against the crypt wall — inside it, pages of vellum gone the color of tea, sealed with the same crest that had stamped his dredged chest. The Veymar charter. Sovereign grant of navigation, toll, and dominion over three hundred miles of river, to the bearer and their heirs, in perpetuity.

"Do you know what I've spent twenty years buying?" Maren said. "Permits. Concessions. Leases that expire every six tides, renewed by men who die and are replaced by worse men. Paper that lets me work the river as long as the river's owners allow it. But this—" she touched the vellum like a lover "—this doesn't expire. This makes the river mine. Not the water. The *right of way*. Every boat, every dock, every crossing, forever."

"And you need me because?"

"The charter answers to blood or to salvage. My blood is diluted past recognition. So: salvage. First lawful recovery, witnessed." She smiled. "You recovered my chest, Kestrel. Witnessed by your crew. All I need is your testimony, and I'll pay you what you fled from. A house that doesn't float. Land. A name in the registry instead of a hull number. Permanence, Captain. You've been running from stillness your whole life — I'm offering you the good kind."

Kestrel looked at her for a long moment. Behind his faceplate, no one could see his expression, which was a mercy, because it wasn't the expression of a man being tempted.

He thought about the night he stopped moving. The *Larkspur*, anchored in a storm because the harbor master's fee was due and he'd been tired, so tired, and by morning four of his crew were dead and the river had taken the boat's spine. He had learned then that stopping kills. That motion was the only honesty a man like him could afford.

And here was a woman offering him the most expensive stillness in the world.

"No," he said.

Vess shifted. Maren didn't. "You haven't heard the number."

"I don't need to. You want to own the road everyone else walks on. There's no number for that, because there's nothing left after it. You take the river, everyone else gets the banks." He reached out, slowly, and lifted the charter from the case. "And you're wrong about one thing. It wasn't your chest I recovered."

Above them, faint through two hundred years of stone and forty feet of floodwater, the sky went dark.

* * *

The monsoon had been building for three days — Odile had called it, reading pressure in her bad knee and the color of the clouds the way other people read headlines. Now it arrived all at once, the way monsoons do, and the cathedral groaned like a living thing.

Kestrel swam for the rose window with the charter stuffed inside his suit, against his chest, where his heartbeat could reach it. Behind him, lanterns swung wild. He heard Vess shout something into her radio, heard Maren's voice crack its first order of genuine fear: *"Surface. Everyone surface now—"*

The storm surge hit the wreck before they cleared it.

He felt it through the stone — a pressure wave rolling down the nave, and then the great west wall, weakened by two centuries of patient water, finally accepted the river's proposal. Stone sheared. The flood came through sideways, a black fist, and Kestrel let it take him. No fighting it. Fighting it was how you died. He tucked, rode the tumble, and let the river carry him out through the wound it had just torn in its own cathedral.

The charter pressed against his heart the whole way down.

* * *

He surfaced in white chaos, half a mile downstream, and Tam hauled him onto the bank by the collar like a drowned cat. Sefa had the line around her waist already, anchored to a cypress; Odile knelt in the mud with her charts weighted by stones, shouting bearings no one needed anymore. The rain hit sideways. Lightning turned the river into a strobe photograph of itself.

The fleet's gunboats were running. He watched their lights flee downriver, stern-first into the storm, Maren Veymar-Loch's empire of expiring paper abandoning the field. Whatever divers she'd lost in the collapse, whatever the charter had cost her, she'd decided the river wasn't worth dying for. Only people who loved it did that.

Sefa fought her way to him, screaming over the rain. "Tell me you got it! Tell me you have it!"

He did have it. Waterlogged, ink bleeding, seals dissolving into the same brown water that had buried Veymar two hundred years ago. By morning it would be pulp. By afternoon, a story.

He opened his hands and let the river finish the job.

Sefa stared at the emptying vellum shreds, and then at him, and then she started laughing — wild, rain-drowned, furious laughter — and pulled him into a hug hard enough to crack a rib. "You absolute *idiot*. Uncle. You idiot."

"I know," he said.

Tam whooped. Odile sat down heavily in the mud and began, quietly, to redraw a chart that no longer mattered, because it was the only prayer she knew.

* * *

Three days later, the *Magpie's Debt* limped into Corvane Landing under jury rig, engineless, towed the last mile by a ferry captain who took payment in stories. The town was a shelf of tin roofs and rope walks built into the bluff — the kind of place Kestrel had moored at a hundred times and never once stayed overnight.

He walked the length of the deck, checking lines out of habit, and found Sefa repainting the hull number by hand. She'd stenciled it wrong on purpose. It read, in fresh white letters: *MAGPIE'S CREW*.

"That's not a legal name," he said.

"Neither were we, until recently." She blew a strand of hair off her face. "Odile says the ferry office needs a pilot run twice a week to the upper towns. Tam already signed on. Says the diving's honest." She looked at him sideways. "Says the captain's less haunted, too. Her words."

"My niece is gossiping with a stowaway about my soul."

"Our crew," Sefa corrected. "Get it right."

Kestrel looked downriver, where the water ran high and brown and full of everything it had taken and everything it had given back. Somewhere under all that brown was a charter becoming mud, and a nation that had finally, truly, finished dying. The permits would expire. The fleets would move on. The river would belong to whoever kept showing up for it.

He tied off the last line. Then, deliberately, he sat down on the gunwale, in the rain-shadow of the wheelhouse, and did the hardest thing he had done since the *Larkspur* went down.

Nothing.

The boat rocked. The town smelled of woodsmoke and frying fish. Someone — Tam, probably — was singing badly in the galley. Down the dock, the ferry office lamp flickered on for the evening, and a woman leaned out and waved at him like she'd been expecting him for years.

Kestrel Delacroix, who had spent thirty years believing that stopping was dying, sat still on his own deck and let the evening come to him.

"For the record," Sefa said, dropping down beside him with two cups of something hot and illegal, "if you ever try to leave without us, we'll find you. We're a crew now. That's how it works."

"I know how it works," he said, and drank, and stayed.

### Coda — Sefa

The thing nobody tells you about grief is that it keeps a ledger.

My uncle's ledger had four names in it, written so deep into him I doubt he could read anything else anymore. Mine had one: my mother, who chose the shore over the boat, and made me feel like cargo ever since. I signed aboard the *Debt* to settle accounts. To prove the river was a mistake she'd been right about.

Instead I found a disgraced mapmaker who draws coastlines that forgive her. A diver who treats the bottom of the world like a neighborhood. An uncle who traded his engine for my life without hesitating a single stroke of the propeller.

The charter's gone. The fortune's gone. The boat will never make way under power again. And last night, moored at Corvane Landing, I watched my uncle sit still for a whole hour — actually still, not coiled-still, not waiting-for-the-wound-to-open still — and I understood that he hadn't lost the race to the wreck.

He'd won a different one.

We sail Tuesday, pole and sail, upriver to the ferry run. Odile has charted it. Tam has dived it. I have, against every plan I ever made, a home with a hull number that isn't legal and a name that is.

The river keeps what it keeps. Turns out it kept us.
