## 1. The Mood of the Sandbar

The river told you things before it showed them to you, and Edmund Joubert had learned, over nineteen years on the water, to listen with her hands.

She stood at the wheel of the *Meridian* with the engine ticking beneath her feet like a second heartbeat, and she watched the water ahead not as water but as language. There — a long seam of oily smoothness where the current folded under itself, hiding a sandbar that would take the keel like a knife. There — a line of bubbles rising in a lazy diagonal, marking where the channel bent hard to starboard around a point that had eroded since last season. The low water made liars of everything. In the wet months the river was generous, broad-shouldered, forgiving; in the dry months it narrowed its eyes and kept its secrets close, and a captain who trusted her charts more than her instincts would find herself sitting in the mud with her cargo spoiling and her reputation dissolving faster than sugar in rain.

Edmund eased the throttle back a quarter notch. The *Meridian* answered the way an old dog answers — willingly, but with a stiffness in the joints that hadn't been there five years ago. Her hull was sixty years old if she was a day, patched twice below the waterline, her deck planks gone silver-gray from sun and salt and the endless abrasion of rope. She was not a beautiful boat anymore. But she floated, and she turned when Edmund asked her to, and in this country that put her in rare company.

"Bar coming up on the left," Edmund said, mostly to herself, partly to the boat. "Going wide."

She took them wide. The *Meridian* slid past the sandbar's hidden shoulder by less than a boat-length, through water so shallow that the propeller wash stirred up a ghost of silt behind them, and then the channel deepened and the river opened out and Edmund allowed herself one slow breath.

That was the whole art, really. One breath at a time, one reading at a time, for eleven hours a day, alone.

Alone. The word sat differently in her chest than it used to. When she'd bought — no. When the boat had come to her, there had been a crew of four: a cook who sang off-key hymns, a deckhand who could splice wire cable blindfolded, an engineer named Baptiste who understood the old diesel engine the way priests understand scripture. Now it was just Edmund and the engine and the cargo — this run, forty sacks of rice and a crate of roofing tin for the mission at San Cristóbal, nothing worth the trip except that the trip paid, and paying trips were getting harder to find in the dry season when half the landings upriver went unreachable and the traders hoarded their freight until the rains came back.

She passed the drowned church steeple at Kilometer 61 without looking at it directly. Everyone did that now, or almost everyone. The reservoir had taken three villages when they raised the dam gates eight years ago, and what remained above the waterline looked like the tops of heads of people standing very still, waiting. In high water even the steeples went under, and boats crossed over rooftops, and the fishermen said the fish down there were fat and unafraid because they'd never known a hook. Edmund had ferried the last families out herself, one gray dawn after another, their belongings in woven sacks, their faces turned backward toward houses that were already drinking the river. She didn't think about it much. She'd gotten good at not thinking about things.

The morning burned off into heat. By ten o'clock the tin roof of the wheelhouse was radiating like a griddle and Edmund had stripped down to her shirt with the sleeves rolled, sweat making dark maps across her back. She drank watered rum from a chipped enamel cup and ate hard bread with salt fish and kept the boat moving, because moving was easier than stopping, and stopping meant thinking, and thinking meant the letter.

There was always a letter.

* * *

San Cristóbal wasn't much of a town — a crescent of corrugated roofs along a bluff, a market square that emptied by noon, a dock built of pilings that groaned when anything heavier than a dog stepped on them. But it was the end of her run, and it had fuel, and it had ice, and it had the post office, which was the thing Edmund both needed and dreaded with a dull, familiar regularity, the way a man with a bad tooth dreads and needs his own tongue.

She brought the *Meridian* alongside with the ease of long habit, lines out, fenders down, the engine dying in a shudder of black smoke. A dozen hands appeared to take the mooring lines — boys who worked the dock for coins, and old Ferro, the harbormaster, whose belly preceded him like a herald.

"Joubert." Ferro handed up a line and squinted at her through the heat shimmer. "Rice?"

"Rice and tin. Mission's expecting it."

"They're always expecting something, the missions." He wiped his neck with a cloth that had once been white. "You've got mail."

And there it was, the small tightening behind her ribs, the body knowing before the mind admitted it. "From who?"

Ferro shrugged with the elaborate indifference of a man who had learned long ago that knowledge on the waterfront was currency and he wasn't spending any today. "Post office has it. You want the boy to fetch?"

"I'll fetch."

She tied off, checked the lines twice — habit, superstition, call it what you liked — and walked up the dock planks into the town's flat white glare. The post office occupied the corner of the customs house, a room the size of a chapel with a barred window and a ceiling fan that turned with the enthusiasm of a dying insect. The clerk knew her. They all knew her. That was the trouble with staying in one river system your whole life: everyone knew which letters were which.

"Nothing today," the clerk began, and then, seeing her face do whatever it did, softened and reached beneath the counter. "Except this came up on the steamer Tuesday. I held it, thinking you might—" He stopped himself. Held letters were against regulations and they both knew it, and his kindness in breaking the rule was somehow worse than the rule itself.

The envelope was heavy stock, cream-colored, machine-addressed. Not the handwriting of any bank clerk in the river city — those wrote in pencil on cheap paper. This was corporate stationery, the kind that cost more per sheet than Edmund made in a day. MERIDIAN & VALE RECOVERY SERVICES, said the return address, and beneath it, in smaller type, a street in the capital she'd never seen and never wanted to.

She carried it back down to the boat unopened. Some part of her — the superstitious part, the part that read currents — believed that as long as the envelope stayed sealed, the news inside stayed hypothetical. It was a child's logic and she knew it, and she did it anyway, sitting in the wheelhouse with the envelope on the chart table while the dock boys loaded her empty rice sacks and the afternoon stretched out hot and endless.

Finally, because she was fifty-one years old and had survived worse than paper, she opened it.

*Dear Captain Joubert,* it began, in the bloodless voice of all such letters. *We write regarding the outstanding obligation secured against the vessel MERIDIAN (reg. no. 4471), currently held in portfolio by our client. Our records indicate a balance of—*

The number. There it was, printed in figures so neat they looked almost gentle. Eleven thousand, two hundred and forty. Edmund had owed nine thousand once, seven years ago. She had paid faithfully through two good seasons and one disastrous one, and the number had gone up anyway, fed by interest and fees and penalties described in paragraphs of such delicately worded cruelty that she sometimes marveled at the craftsmanship of it. It was like watching a plant grow in fast motion — you never saw it move, but every year it was taller, and its roots were deeper, and by now they were somewhere under the keel, under the engine bed, under the water itself.

*We are instructed to advise,* the letter continued, *that our client is reviewing the disposition of non-performing assets at the close of this fiscal quarter, and that vessels of this class may be subject to repossession proceedings should satisfactory arrangements not be concluded.*

Repossession. The word sat on the page like a snake on a warm rock, patient and certain.

They couldn't take the boat. That was the thing she told herself at night, the rosary she ran through her fingers. The original loan papers were a mess — signed in another life, witnessed by men who were dead, drawn against a company that had since changed its name twice. The debt collectors knew it too, probably, which was why they sent letters instead of lawyers. The letters were cheaper than lawsuits and worked nearly as well, grinding away at a person month after month, year after year, until the fear did the work the courts couldn't.

But the client. That was the part that always caught in her throat like a fishbone. Because the client — the party who held her paper, the party whose money had, nineteen years ago, rebuilt the *Meridian*'s engine and recaulked her hull and put Edmund Joubert on this river as a captain instead of leaving her a widow with nothing — that client was Halvorsen. The Halvorsen estate. Old Henrik Halvorsen, dead these six years, the financier whose name was on half the dams and a third of the trading companies in the valley, the man whose portrait hung in the customs house in the river city with a plaque calling him *Benefactor of the Basin.*

Henrik Halvorsen had lent her the money when no bank would look at her. He had done it, people said, out of respect for her father, whom he'd known in the old days. And Edmund had spent nineteen years paying it back with interest, and the debt had grown instead of shrunk, and now his heirs' agents wrote to her in cream-colored envelopes about *non-performing assets*, and she could not shake the feeling — irrational, shameful, persistent as mold — that the debt was not a mistake. That it was working exactly as designed. That somewhere in a tall building in the capital, her boat was a line in a ledger, and the line was doing precisely what lines in ledgers do, which is hold things still.

She read the letter twice more, then folded it along its creases and put it in the chart drawer, beneath the tide tables, beneath the survey of the upper reaches, beneath everything else she was not currently prepared to think about.

* * *

Evening came down the valley like a lid closing. Edmund cooked rice and onions on the little stove in the wheelhouse and ate standing up, watching the light go amber and then copper on the water. The dock boys had gone home. Ferro had gone home. Down the bluff, the mission's bell rang the hour, and somewhere a radio played a song about a woman who waited, and the river ran on in the dark, patient, enormous, indifferent.

This was the hour that got her. Mornings were fine — mornings were work, and work was a mercy. But evenings, with the boat tied and quiet and the day's earnings counted out in her head against the day's costs, the isolation came up through the deck plates like cold. Nineteen years she'd run this river, and she could count her true friends on one hand without using all the fingers. People respected her. Dockmasters tipped their hats. Traders called her *Capitana* and meant it. But respect was a thing you gave to a function, and a function could be replaced, and Edmund knew — had always known, in the place where she kept the truths she didn't say aloud — that if the *Meridian* sank tomorrow with her aboard, the river would close over the hole in the world within a week, the way water closes over a dropped coin.

She washed her cup. She checked the mooring lines though the boat wasn't going anywhere. She stood at the open wheelhouse door and looked upriver, where the water went black between the far bluffs, toward the drowned villages and the trading posts and the high country beyond, where the mountains stood up against the last light like the backs of sleeping animals.

It was there, in that direction, that the world got strange. The maps thinned out past the third set of rapids. The steamers didn't go. Sefu's people traded down from the trails sometimes, taciturn men with salt and honey and stories they didn't finish. Once, years ago, a survey team had come through asking questions about the headwaters, and Ferro had told them what he told everyone: *Up there, the river forgets its own name.* Edmund had laughed at that, then. She was younger, and the debt was smaller, and the boat felt like hers.

Now she stood in the doorway of a boat that was not quite hers, owned in some unmappable way by a dead man's estate, and listened to the river forget its name in the dark, and felt the loneliness settle over her shoulders like a coat she'd stopped being able to take off.

She was just turning in — checking the lines one final time, because the night watchman at the dock was twelve years old and slept like the dead — when Ferro came back down the gangway with a lantern, moving faster than a man his size moved after sundown.

"Joubert. You still awake? Good." He climbed aboard without asking, which he never did, and stood in the wheelhouse doorway breathing hard, the lantern throwing his shadow huge against the chart house. "There's a man at the customs office asking after you. Arrived on tonight's steamer. Survey office man, by the look of him — city shoes, city hat, sweating through his shirt." Ferro lowered his voice, though there was no one for a mile in any direction to hear. "He says the Halvorsen expedition's gone missing upriver. Eleven days of radio silence. And he says—" Ferro paused, and in the lantern light his face did something complicated, pity and apology tangled together, "—he says they want to hire you to go find them. And Capitana — he says the pay would clear half of what you owe."

The river ran on beneath the dock, black and patient. Edmund stood very still, one hand on the doorframe, and felt the trap — because it was a trap, some deep-water instinct in her knew it was a trap — begin to close around her, soft as current, strong as chain.

"Tell him," she heard herself say, "I'll hear him out at first light."

Ferro nodded and went, and Edmund stood alone in the wheelhouse a long time, watching the lantern bob away up the dock, listening to the water slap the hull in the rhythm of a clock counting down.
