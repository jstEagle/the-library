## 2. Radio Silence

The survey office smelled of ink and wet wool, and Edmund Joubert stood in it the way she stood everywhere that wasn't her boat: like a guest who had already overstayed.

Rain had followed her up from the dock, a thin gray drizzle that had gotten into the seams of her coat and would not come out. The clerk behind the counter — a young man with spectacles fogged at the edges and a ledger open before him as though it were scripture — had asked her name twice. She'd given it twice. Now he was reading back to her the terms of an offer she had not yet agreed to hear, in the tone of a man who had been instructed to assume agreement was a formality.

"—eleven days of silence, Captain. Eleven days since the last scheduled transmission from the *Meridian Star*, out of Halvorsen Station." He said the name with the reverence people reserved for banks and bishops. "The expedition went up past the second gorge on the twenty-third of last month. They were due to report positions weekly. Nothing since."

"And you want me to go look for them." She kept her voice flat. Rain ticked against the office's single window. Behind the clerk, a map of the upper river hung from two nails, one of them bent, so that the whole country above the gorge tilted slightly westward, as if leaning away from itself.

"Not search, precisely. Verify." The clerk adjusted his spectacles. "There's a difference. A search implies we don't know where they are. We know where they were supposed to be. The company wants eyes on the ground — or water, in your case — to confirm whether the silence is mechanical, meteorological, or—" he paused, choosing the word with care, "—otherwise."

"Otherwise," Edmund repeated.

"Desertion. Illness. Interference." He said these the way a man reads a list of possible causes of death at an inquest, none of them his own. "You'd be paid three hundred and forty pounds on completion. Half again if you bring back personnel or evidence of their condition."

Three hundred and forty pounds. Edmund did the arithmetic without wanting to, because the arithmetic did itself now, always, unbidden, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Three hundred and forty was more than half of what remained on the note against the *Vesper*. It was the difference between another four years of hauling cargo for men who haggled over every crate and something like freedom. It was the number her mind reached for first when she woke, before her own name some mornings, and here it was sitting on a government counter between an inkwell and a dead fly.

She looked at the map instead. Above the second gorge the river broke into its braided reaches, drawn in blue lines so fine they seemed less like water than like veins under skin. Beyond the survey's edge the cartographer had simply stopped, leaving a wash of blank paper with the words *unsurveyed — seasonal navigation only* printed across it in letters too small to be read from where she stood. She could have read them anyway. She knew them by heart. That blank space was where her competence ended and her superstition began, and she had kept the two strictly separated for eleven years.

"Captain?" the clerk prompted.

"I'm thinking about my boat," she said, which was true, though not in the way he'd take it. She was thinking about how the *Vesper* rode low at the stern this season, how her port engine coughed on cold starts like an old man clearing his lungs, how every bolt in her hull was a small promise Edmund had made to a debt and intended to keep until the debt released her or drowned her, whichever came first.

"The company would charter the vessel outright," the clerk said. "Fuel, provisions, insurance against loss. You'd risk nothing but time."

Time. As if time were the cheap thing. As if the season weren't already tilting toward low water, toward the weeks when the sandbars moved nightly and the channel she'd known for a decade became a stranger writing letters in a hand she almost recognized. As if the monsoon weren't gathering somewhere out over the sea like a debt of its own, patient, compounding.

"No," she said.

The clerk blinked. In her experience, clerks did not often encounter the word from river people. River people said *maybe* and *depends* and *what's the rate*, because river people lived inside systems of favor and deferral the way fish lived inside water. Edmund had learned flat refusals from her father, along with knotwork and a distrust of offices.

"It's a great deal of money," the clerk said, gently, as though she might not have heard herself refuse it.

"It is." She put both hands flat on the counter, and saw him notice them — the rope-callused palms, the scar across the left knuckle where a winch cable had taken its tax years ago. "And I'm not for sale at any price this month. Ask me again after the rains and I'll tell you no differently."

He wrote something in the ledger. She didn't ask what. She turned for the door, and the rain outside had thickened while she stood there, coming down now in earnest, drumming on the tin awning over the steps, and through it she could see the river running high and brown and indifferent, carrying everything downstream and nothing back.

She was three steps into the wet when the clerk called after her.

"Captain Joubert — there's the matter of the deposit."

She stopped. Rain ran off the brim of her hat. "I didn't take the job."

"Yes, well." He had come to the doorway behind her, holding a slip of paper protected from the weather by his whole body curled around it, a man guarding a document rather than himself. "It appears there's been a credit posted to your account. This morning. Before you arrived."

"A credit."

"From the Halvorsen estate office. One hundred and seventy pounds." He held the slip out under the awning, sheltering it with his cupped hand. "Marked as advance payment for passage and services, vessel *Vesper*, contracted to support the Meridian expedition. Dated six days ago."

Edmund took the paper because her hand acted before her judgment caught up, which was, her father used to say, the defining feature of her character and the reason he'd taught her knots — so that her hands would have honest work when her head went wandering. The slip was official stock, watermarked, stamped. *Received with thanks — H. Vane, agent.* Six days ago. Before anyone had spoken to her. Before the eleven days of silence had even finished counting themselves out.

"This is a mistake," she said.

"Almost certainly," the clerk agreed, with the serene satisfaction of a man watching someone else's problem arrive on schedule. "Clerical error. Wrong Joubert, perhaps. There are Jouberts all down this river; it's a common enough name in the trade." He smiled apologetically. "But the funds have cleared, Captain. And until the error is formally reversed, the account stands as credited. Which means, technically speaking—"

"Technically speaking," Edmund said, "I've already been paid half the fee for a job I refused."

"Half the fee," the clerk confirmed, "for a job you haven't started. The estate office will want the balance reconciled one way or the other. Either you return the advance — and I should mention the reversal fee comes out of the account, there's a schedule — or you complete the engagement and earn the rest." He spread his hands, a small shrug wrapped in an apology. "Bureaucracy, Captain. I don't make it. I only keep it."

She stood in the rain and read the slip again. *H. Vane, agent.* She didn't know the name. But she knew the shape of the thing being done to her, because she had felt its shape before, many times, wearing different clothes: a door closing softly behind her, the click of it arriving a moment after the fact. Someone had decided she was going upriver and had arranged the world accordingly, and had done it with paperwork, which was how powerful men did violence in this country — never with their hands, always with ledgers, so that afterward everyone could agree it had been nobody's fault.

"You could dispute it," the clerk offered. "Petition the estate office. It takes six weeks, generally. Sometimes eight."

Six weeks. By then the pass country would be closing, the blank space on the map filling up with snow, and whatever had gone quiet up there would stay quiet until spring, permanently perhaps, and the money would sit in her account like a stone in her shoe for the whole of it.

"And if I keep the advance and do nothing?"

"Then you're in breach of a contract you didn't sign, which the courts call acceptance by conduct." He said it kindly. That was the worst part. He said it the way you'd tell a woman her house was flooding — sorry, nothing to be done, these things happen.

Edmund folded the slip along its crease and put it inside her coat, next to the letter from the debt collector that had been riding there for nine days, unread past the first line because the first line had been enough. Two pieces of paper now, pressed together over her heart like mismatched wings. She wondered briefly, absurdly, whether they'd mate in there overnight and hatch something.

"Who is H. Vane?" she asked.

"I couldn't say. The authorization came through the estate office. Old money, old accounts." The clerk hesitated, then lowered his voice as though the rain might carry it somewhere. "Between us, Captain — the Halvorsen name has been good for this river for thirty years. Financed half the trade above the gorge, back when financing meant something. If their money says you're going upriver, then as far as every bank and broker between here and the delta is concerned, you're going upriver." He straightened his spectacles. "I'd think about which direction you'd rather that statement be true in."

* * *

The *Vesper* sat at her mooring with rain beading on her deck boards, and Edmund walked her length slowly, fore to aft, doing the thing she did when the land had gotten into her head and needed scrubbing out. Thirty-one feet of riveted iron and stubbornness. Her gunwales worn smooth as driftwood where a decade of hands had gripped them. The wheelhouse windows hazed with condensation, and inside, the compass sat in its binnacle like a small dark eye that had watched her longer than any living person had.

Her father's compass. That was the story she'd always had, and stories, on a river, were load-bearing structures: you built your whole understanding on them, and you did not go poking at the joints.

She ducked into the wheelhouse, wiped a circle in the glass with her sleeve, and looked at the instrument without touching it. Brass case gone soft-edged with handling. The needle steady, pointing its patient north. Engraved around the bezel, worn nearly to nothing, a ring of letters she had never bothered to decipher because her father had told her they were decorative, flourishes, the kind of thing instrument makers added to justify their prices. She had been eleven when he died and had believed him about the compass the way children believe — completely, and then, later, at cost.

Decorative flourishes. She thought of the clerk saying *acceptance by conduct*, and thought: that's what I am. A woman accepted by conduct into a journey she never chose, by a signature she never made.

Tomas appeared at the wheelhouse door without knocking, because he was sixteen and the concept of doors-as-boundaries had not yet taken root in him. He was thin, quick-eyed, all elbows and appetite, and he had been living aboard for five weeks on the strength of a story about an uncle upriver that Edmund neither fully believed nor fully disbelieved, because the boy worked like a man twice his age and lied like one half of it.

"You were gone long," Tomas said. "I stoked the boiler. And the starboard bilge pump is making a noise like it's chewing gravel."

"It always makes that noise."

"It's worse today."

Everything was worse today. She looked at the boy — at the restless intelligence in him, the way his eyes went to the folded papers in her coat the way a gull's eyes go to anything shiny — and felt the day tilt another degree.

"Tomas," she said. "If I told you we were going up past the second gorge, what would you say?"

His face did something complicated and fast, hope and fear crossing each other in opposite directions, and she understood, before he answered, that the uncle was real, and that the boy had been waiting five weeks for exactly this question, and that she had just handed him a gift she wasn't sure she owned.

"I'd say," he managed, "that it's about time."

* * *

Evening came down gray and slow. Edmund sat alone in the wheelhouse with a lamp lit and the rain still working at the roof, and laid her papers out on the chart table like a fortune-teller dealing a hand she already knew was bad.

The debt collector's letter. Nine days old. *Re: outstanding principal and accrued interest, vessel Vesper, per note of assignment.* The usual threats in the usual order, ending with the usual courtesy: *we trust this matter will receive your prompt attention.* She had read it once on the dock and put it away, because the amount at the bottom had a way of standing up off the page and following her around.

The credit slip. Six days old. *H. Vane, agent.*

She set them side by side and looked at them the way she'd look at two floating objects on the current, trying to read from their drift where they'd come from. Different waters, clearly. One flowed from the bank that held her note — and she had never met the holder of that note, not once in eleven years; the debt had changed hands twice through brokers, arriving at her like a piece of cargo nobody claimed. The other flowed from the Halvorsen estate, a name she knew the way everyone on the river knew it: painted on warehouse walls, stamped on barge manifests, spoken by old pilots with a respect that bordered on religion. The family that had opened the upper river. The family whose money lay under half the trade routes she ran.

Two unrelated institutions. Two unrelated papers. Except that both of them, this week, had reached out and taken hold of her boat.

She got up and poured herself the last two fingers of rum from the bottle she kept for emergencies, and decided this qualified, and drank it standing, looking out at the black water.

Eleven days of silence. She turned the phrase over. On a river, silence was information. A boat that failed to report was a boat with a story — engine trouble, weather, sickness, mutiny, or the simple gravitational pull of a place so far up that the maps gave out and men forgot they owed reports to anyone. She had run the reach below the second gorge maybe forty times and never past it, because past it the channel braided into swamp and the swamp rose into foothills and the foothills kept secrets the way the lowlands kept silt. Her father had gone up there once, before she was born. He had come back thinner and quieter and had never spoken of it except once, drunk, when he'd said: *the river doesn't end where the maps stop, girl. That's just where the paying customers turn around.*

Paying customers. She looked at the credit slip again. One hundred and seventy pounds, posted before she was ever asked, binding her by conduct to a course she'd refused. Somewhere upstream, eleven days quiet, an expedition full of somebody's sons and brothers was either dead or found something worth going silent for. And somewhere downstream, in a warm office, a man named Vane had written her name into the middle of it with a fountain pen.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the rain eased at last, and the river made its long sound against the hull, patient as interest accruing.

Edmund gathered the papers, squared their edges, and slid them both into the drawer beneath the chart table — the collector's threat and the stranger's gold, locked in the dark together like cellmates. Then she pulled the chart of the upper river from its tube and weighted its corners with the brass ashtray, the spool of wire, the cup of pencils, and looked for a long time at the blank space beyond the survey's edge.

*Unsurveyed. Seasonal navigation only.*

The season was turning. Whatever window existed up there was closing at exactly the speed of the snowline coming down, and the money in her account was a hook, and hooks did not care whether you bit willingly.

"All right," she said aloud, to the compass, to the river, to the boy asleep in the fo'c'sle who would wake tomorrow grinning and pretend he hadn't heard her talking to instruments. "All right. But I go because I choose to. Write that down."

The compass needle trembled — or didn't; it was impossible to say, at night, with the lamp burning uneven — and settled again on its north, and Edmund Joubert blew out the light and went below to sleep on a decision she had already made, dressed, packed, and provisioned in her mind somewhere between the second finger of rum and now, and would deny having made until the mooring lines came off.

In the morning there would be fuel to buy and a manifest to fake and a boy to disappoint or delight, and somewhere ahead, past the second gorge, eleven days of silence waiting to be explained.

She slept badly, and dreamed of paperwork falling like snow.
