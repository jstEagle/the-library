## 3. Passengers

The *Magpie* took on passengers the way a tired dog takes on fleas — reluctantly, and only because scratching them off cost more than living with them.

Edmund had posted no notice. She had told no one at the survey office that she meant to sail on the morning tide, or that she'd spent half the night below decks with a lantern, tracing the credit entry in her ledger until the ink seemed to blur and reform into something like an accusation. *Halvorsen Expedition — advance payment — received in error.* The clerk's handwriting was neat as a hymnal. Errors that neat were usually made on purpose.

But the money sat in her account like ballast she hadn't asked for, and the debt collector's letter sat in her breast pocket like a stone, and between those two weights Edmund Joubert did what she always did when the current turned against her: she pointed her bow upriver and let the argument happen behind her.

She was coiling the last of the stern line when the woman appeared at the foot of the gangplank.

She was perhaps thirty-five, dressed for travel rather than display — canvas trousers, a coat with pockets deep enough to lose a small nation in, and a leather map case slung across her back the way other women wore shawls. Her hair was dark and cut short in a way that suggested she'd done it herself with a knife and hadn't regretted it. She stood looking at the boat with an expression Edmund recognized from church pews and funeral parlors: the look of someone taking inventory of something they expected to be disappointed by.

"You're the captain," the woman said. Not a question. She'd read the name painted on the wheelhouse — *MAGPIE* — and beneath it, smaller, the letters E. JOUBERT, flaking gold on weathered wood.

"I'm whoever you need me to be if it gets you off my dock."

"Ines Marchetti." The woman climbed the plank without waiting for permission, which Edmund noted and neither approved nor objected to. "I'm told you're going upriver."

"Told by whom?"

"By the way your deck is loaded." Ines nodded at the crates lashed amidships — rice, lamp oil, salt, canvas, the particular cargo of a boat provisioning for distance rather than trade. "You're not selling any of that downstream. And you've taken on water enough for eight people, and there are two of you."

Edmund set down the coil of line slowly. "That's a lot of arithmetic for a stranger on my dock."

"I'm a cartographer. Arithmetic is how I say hello." Ines reached into her coat and produced a folded paper, holding it out. "I was attached to the Halvorsen expedition. Surveying. I came downriver ahead of them to establish base stations along the lower reaches. They were supposed to follow." Her voice stayed level, but Edmund heard the seam in it, the place where the level surface had been stitched over something moving underneath. "Eleven days ago they stopped answering."

"And now you want to go back up after them."

"I want to know where my maps end." Ines looked past Edmund then, upriver, where the water ran brown-green and patient toward the mountains. "A map with a blank in it isn't finished. It's just a lie with edges."

Edmund took the paper. It wasn't a contract; it was a list of coordinates, annotated in a small precise hand, with depths and landmarks she knew by heart and some she didn't. Whoever this woman was, she'd done her homework on the river itself.

"Passage is forty a head, food included, opinions extra," Edmund said. "And I don't carry passengers who give orders at the wheel."

"I don't want the wheel." Ines almost smiled. It transformed her face briefly, the way light through a crack transforms a cellar. "I want the bow. Someone has to watch the water."

They shook on it, and Edmund felt — though she would not have admitted it under torture — the faintest loosening somewhere behind her ribs, as if a knot she'd been carrying had shifted a quarter-turn.

* * *

The stowaway announced himself an hour out, when the town had shrunk to a smudge and the first bend had swallowed it entirely.

Edmund heard him before she saw him: a sneeze, muffled and catastrophic, from inside the hold where the rice sacks were stacked. She stood very still at the wheel, one hand on the brass, and listened. Another sneeze. Then a thump, then a silence so elaborate it could only be a boy trying very hard to be furniture.

She let him sweat through the better part of an hour while the river carried them past drowned willows and the skeletons of old landing stages. Then she handed the wheel to Ines — who took it with a competence that surprised neither of them — went below, and pulled open the hatch.

He was wedged between a rice sack and the hull planking, knees to his chest, maybe fifteen years old, all elbows and hollow cheeks, with hair the color of wet sand and eyes that went wide as coins when the light hit him.

"Don't throw me over," he said immediately.

"That's the plan, is it? Overboard?"

"It's what captains do in stories."

"Stories also have captains who feed stowaways to the engine. Get up."

He got up. He unfolded like a carpenter's rule, taller than he'd seemed crumpled, and stood swaying with the boat's motion, trying to look dignified and achieving instead the look of a heron in a windstorm. His clothes were good once — a jacket with leather buttons, boots resoled twice — but they'd traveled hard, and he had the specific grime of someone who'd slept rough for days without admitting it.

"Name," Edmund said.

"Tomas."

"Tomas what?"

"Tomas Halvorsen."

The name landed in the hold like a dropped tool. Edmund kept her face still, but she felt the word go through her and rattle around. *Halvorsen.* The name on her ledger. The name on the credit entry. The name stitched, whether she liked it or not, into the hull of everything she owned.

"The expedition," Tomas said, reading her silence correctly, the way children and animals read silence. "Daniel Halvorsen is my uncle. My mother's brother. Nobody believes me because we don't have the same name — my father left, and my mother took hers back, and then she died, and—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening, refusing to be a story about dead mothers. "He's my uncle. He taught me knots." He held up his hands, palms out, as if the proof lived there. "Bowline on a bight. Sheepshank. The double fisherman's, for joining lines. He said a knot is a promise you can untie."

Edmund looked at his hands. Thin, cracked at the knuckles, dirt in every crease. But steady. Held out flat, unflinching, the way you offer your hands to someone deciding whether to trust you with rope.

"He's missing," she said. "You know that."

"Everyone knows that. That's why nobody else is going." His chin came up. "Everybody's waiting for him to come back down. Nobody's going up to meet him halfway. So I am."

Ines had appeared at the top of the ladder, drawn by the voices, and was looking down at the boy with an expression Edmund couldn't classify — something between recognition and alarm.

"You're Marius's boy," Ines said quietly. "Tomas. You used to sit outside the survey office and copy the contour maps onto butcher paper."

Tomas blinked at her. "You remember that?"

"I remember thinking the boy had a better sense of scale than half my colleagues." Ines came down the ladder, ducking her head. "Your uncle drew the first reliable chart of the middle gorge. Did you know that? Before anyone paid him to."

"He talked about it." Something moved in the boy's face — pride, quickly buried, like an animal ducking under cover. "He said maps were how you loved a place properly."

Edmund stood there with her arms crossed and did the arithmetic she hated doing: a boy with no family and no ticket, bound for the exact stretch of river where grown men with funding and equipment had stopped answering their radio. Every sensible instinct she owned said put him ashore at the next landing, hand him to the mission sisters, wash her hands.

But she remembered being fifteen. She remembered what it was to have nowhere on earth that wanted you, and a single direction that did.

"You work," she said. "Lines, galley, bilge, whatever needs doing before it's asked. You eat last. And if you sneeze near my cargo again I'll maroon you on a sandbar with the herons."

Tomas grinned — sudden, sunlit, transforming — and Edmund understood she had just made a mistake, and made it anyway.

* * *

Mr. Vane came aboard at Two Rivers Landing, and he came aboard the way a bill comes due: punctually, politely, and with interest accruing.

Edmund saw him first from the wheelhouse — a man standing alone on the landing stage among the fish carts and the idlers, dressed in a grey suit that had no business within fifty miles of this river. Grey suit, grey hat, a leather valise held in both hands like a hymnbook. He was perhaps fifty, lean, with a face designed to be forgotten: pleasant, symmetrical, entirely without handles. When the *Magpie* bumped the stage he stepped forward before the line was even thrown.

"Captain Joubert?" His voice matched his face — smooth, modulated, the accent of someone educated at expense. "My name is Vane. Mr. Ambrose Vane. I'm told you're heading upriver, and I find myself in need of exactly that service."

"Who told you?"

"A clerk at the survey office. A helpful young man." He smiled. It was a well-made smile, load-bearing, deployed often. "I represent certain commercial interests with correspondence pending in the upper valley. Nothing dramatic. Letters, contracts, the ordinary paperwork of enterprise. I'm given to understand the mail steamer isn't running."

"The mail steamer sank in March."

"All the more reason for private initiative." He was already ascending the plank, valise swinging, and Edmund noticed he watched the deck the whole way up — noting the lashings, the depth of freeboard, the state of the planks — with the appraising eye of a man pricing a horse.

She should have refused him. She knew it even as she quoted him triple fare and watched him pay it in crisp notes without blinking, counting nothing, haggling over nothing. Men who didn't haggle weren't paying for passage. They were buying proximity.

Vane settled into the little saloon aft as though he'd been born to it, unpacked a writing case, and began — within the hour — to ask questions.

Not crudely. That was the thing about him. His questions arrived dressed as conversation, wrapped in pleasantries, timed to the pouring of tea. He asked Edmund about the river's moods as if he cared about rivers. He asked about the low-water season, the state of the trails above the last navigable mile, the disposition of the villages, whether the mission at San Rafael still kept its landing clear. Each question, taken alone, was innocent. Strung together, they formed a shape — and the shape was a route. He was mapping her journey the way Ines mapped coastlines: point by point, patiently, toward some destination he declined to name.

By evening Edmund had retreated to the wheelhouse with a cup of tea gone cold in her hand, watching the banks slide past in the amber light, and found Ines already there, leaning against the rail with her arms folded.

"Well?" Edmund said.

"He asked me three questions about the expedition," Ines said. "Where they last reported from. What equipment they carried. Whether they had any local guides." She turned her head, and in the fading light her eyes were sharp as a gull's. "A man carrying letters doesn't need to know what equipment a survey party carries, Captain. A man carrying letters wants them delivered. This man wants to know how far up they went, and how they got there, and who helped them."

"You think he's following them."

"I think he's following *us*, now, because we're the only wake pointing that direction." Ines straightened. "I've been surveyed myself, Captain. By men with clipboards and government letterhead, back home, when my maps showed things they preferred unmapped. I know the temperature of it."

From the deck below came the sound of Tomas hauling the evening line, singing under his breath — some schoolroom song, tuneless, cheerful. Vane's silhouette crossed the saloon window, unhurried, pausing at each pane as he passed, the way a man does when he is looking at reflections rather than views.

Edmund drank her cold tea and thought about clerical errors and crisp banknotes and a boy's hands held out flat, promising knots.

Three passengers. A cartographer whose maps ended in blanks. A stowaway chasing an uncle up a dying river. An agent in a grey suit with questions like fishhooks, each barb polished and baited.

Any one of them alone, she might have managed. Together they filled her small boat the way weather fills a valley — pressure dropping, air going strange, the animals restless before the storm knows its own name.

At dusk she rang down for full steam and took the *Magpie* up through the last of the safe channels as the light failed. Behind them, far downriver, thunder muttered — too early in the season, too heavy in the voice. The monsoon, gathering itself somewhere over the sea, patient as a creditor.

Above the wheel, in its walnut case, the compass swung gently and settled, needle trembling north-northwest, toward the mountains, toward the blank on the map, toward whatever it was that eleven days of silence had swallowed.

Edmund watched it the way she always did — and for the first time in years, it seemed to be watching her back.
