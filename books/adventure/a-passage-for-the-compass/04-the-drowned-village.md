## 4. The Drowned Village

The reservoir began without ceremony.

One moment the river was a river — brown, muscular, shouldering its way between banks it had carved over ten thousand years — and the next it was something else entirely: wide, flat, patient water that held no current worth reading. Edmund Joubert hated it the way a musician hates a room with no echo. Her whole craft lived in the conversation between hull and flow, and here the river had been gagged.

"Dead water," she said to the wheel, because saying it aloud made it more bearable than thinking it.

Behind her, Ines stood at the rail with her notebook open against her thigh, sketching the drowned treeline with quick, economical strokes. Tomas was somewhere below, ostensibly coiling rope and actually doing whatever a fifteen-year-old does when he thinks no one is watching. Vane sat in the shade of the awning with his hat tipped forward and his hands folded, a man who could make even idleness look like an appointment kept early.

Edmund brought the *Mariana* down the middle of the flooded basin at half throttle, watching for the things dead water hid. That was the other reason she hated reservoirs: they didn't erase the world they covered, they just made it invisible. Steeple of a church out there to starboard, she knew, though you'd never see it now except in dry years when the water dropped and the roof broke the surface like a whale's back. A ferry crossing two miles on. An orchard. A schoolyard where children had run races while the surveyors drank sweet tea on the hill and drew lines on maps.

She'd piloted this stretch before the dam closed, back when she was young enough to think the river would always be the river. She remembered the village of San Isidro de las Aguas — a joke of a name even then, a saint of the waters blessing a town that would one day drown beneath them — as a scatter of whitewashed houses on a shelf above the confluence, terraced gardens going up the slope, a bell tower leaning slightly east like a man listening hard.

Now the water stood eleven feet below the eaves of what had been the highest street.

The *Mariana* passed close enough that Edmund could see into the upper windows of the houses along the ridge. Doors stood open. Not broken open — open, the way people leave them when they mean to come back by evening. Curtains still hung in some windows, faded to the color of weak tea. On one porch, impossibly, a chair still faced the view, as if its owner had only stepped inside to fetch something and would be out any minute to watch the boats go past.

Nobody watched the boats go past. Nobody had for six years.

"They left in a hurry," Ines said, coming to stand beside her at the wheel. Her voice was careful, the way people's voices get around graves.

"They left slowly," Edmund corrected. "That's worse. They had time to carry everything down the hill and time to argue about what to leave, and then one morning the water came anyway." She nodded at the chair. "Somebody decided a chair wasn't worth the mule trip. Then couldn't decide anything else after."

Ines looked at her sideways. "You knew this place."

"I ran cargo through it. Before." Before the debt, she didn't say. Before Halvorsen money bought her a boat and chained her to it in the same handshake. "There was a woman here who sold the best guava paste on the river. Doña somebody. Everyone called her Doña Somebody because nobody could ever remember her actual name and she refused to offer it."

"That sounds like a story you invented."

"It sounds like a story I remember," Edmund said, "which may be the same thing."

The compass hung at her breast on its lanyard, as it always did, riding the small sway of her body like a thing alive. Brass case gone soft-edged with handling, glass scratched in one corner where — so the story went — she'd dropped it off the wheelhouse roof when she was nine. Her father's compass. The only inheritance he'd left her that fit in a pocket. It pointed north the way all compasses point north, faithfully and without opinion, and she had never once thought to wonder where it came from beyond *her father*, which is where most things come from until someone asks a better question.

The bell tower rose ahead, leaning east out of the water, its crown furred with dried weed from previous seasons. Below the surface, Edmund knew, the nave stretched away into green murk, pews still ranked, maybe a hymnal still open on a stand if the water hadn't found a way in. Fish would spawn in the transept. She tried not to picture it and pictured it anyway.

"I'm going to put in at the headland," she said. "There's a landing above the flood line. Some of the old families come back on market days, camp on the hill, sell to boat traffic. If we're lucky we can buy vegetables that haven't spent a week in a crate."

"We're never lucky," said Tomas, appearing at the door of the wheelhouse with rope-burned hands and the expression of a boy who has recently discovered that work is repetitive. "Statistically speaking."

"Then we'll be pleasantly surprised instead." She swung the wheel and felt the *Mariana* lean into the turn, obedient even in dead water, bless her.

* * *

The landing above San Isidro was a plank ramp and a shelf of packed earth, and on it, exactly as memory promised, someone had set out tables under an awning of blue tarpaulin. Three women, two old men, a knot of children chasing a dog too dignified to run. Produce in baskets: tomatoes split with ripeness, onions braided into ropes, limes, a heap of guavas filling the air with their particular sweetness that always reminded Edmund of hospitals and holidays at once.

Vane stayed aboard, pleading correspondence, which suited everyone. Tomas came ashore like a dog let off a lead and was absorbed into the children within ninety seconds, his stowaway's wariness dissolving in the universal currency of a stick that could plausibly become a sword. Ines drifted toward the oldest woman at the far table, who was selling dried fish and small clay whistles, because Ines collected information the way other people collected stamps, compulsively and without any clear plan for what to do with it all.

Edmund bought tomatoes. Bought onions. Let herself be talked into guava paste by a round-faced girl of perhaps twelve who had clearly inherited the family talent, and paid too much for it gladly, and did not ask the girl her name because some traditions deserved respect.

It was while she was wrapping the paste in a scrap of newspaper that the old woman from the far table came and stood in front of her.

She was small the way river people got small — compacted, weathered down to essentials, like driftwood. Her hands were enormous relative to the rest of her, knuckles swollen, nails pared to nothing. She wore a man's shirt buttoned to the throat despite the heat and a silver chain at her neck that disappeared into the collar. Her eyes were the pale brown of river water in dry season, and they were not looking at Edmund's face.

They were looking at the compass.

"You," the old woman said. Her voice was gravel poured slow. "Show me that."

Edmund's hand went to the lanyard, an instinct halfway between protection and obedience. "It's not for sale."

"I'm not buying." The old woman held out both hands, palms up, and there was something in the gesture — formal, almost liturgical — that short-circuited refusal. "Show me."

Later, Edmund would try to reconstruct why she unhooked it. There was no good reason. A stranger on a drowned village's market day, asking to handle the one object she'd have fought a man for. But her fingers were already working the clasp, already laying the compass into those huge ruined palms, and the old woman received it the way a priest receives a book — weight first, meaning second.

She turned it over. Ran her thumb across the engraving on the back, the two sets of initials cut deep into the brass, worn nearly smooth by decades of thumbs before hers. Edmund had traced them a thousand times herself without ever wondering why there were two. *E.J.* — her father, Edmund's namesake, obviously. And beside it, in a different, older hand: *A.H.*

The old woman stopped moving altogether.

"Aurelio," she said.

The word dropped into the hot afternoon like a stone into still water. Edmund heard the children stop chasing the dog. Heard the dog stop existing, almost. The round-faced girl with the guava paste had gone very quiet behind her table.

"You know him?" Edmund asked. Her voice came out strange, flattened, as though she were hearing it through the hull.

The old woman didn't answer immediately. She was holding the compass up to the light now, tilting it, reading something in the brass the way Ines read contour lines. "This is the *aguja del pacto*," she said. "The needle of the agreement. My grandmother told me of it. Two men came up the river before the dam, before my mother was married even. One was a surveyor. One was—" she turned the compass so the initials caught the sun— "this one. Aurelio H. They sat in my grandmother's kitchen and drank coffee and promised, and this—" she tapped the compass case with one yellowed nail— "was the token. The pledge-object. You give a thing that points true, my grandmother said, and your word points true also. That is how the old agreements were sealed, before paper meant anything out here."

Edmund's mouth was dry. "That's not possible. This belonged to my father. He carried it his whole life. He gave it to me when I was nine."

"Your father." The old woman looked at her for the first time, really looked, and something in her expression shifted — not recognition exactly, but the neighborhood of it, a house with the lights off but the shape familiar. "What was his name?"

"Edmund Joubert."

"And where did he get the compass?"

"He—" And here Edmund reached for the answer she had carried for thirty years, the comfortable, load-bearing answer, the one that held up the whole structure: *his father's, and his father's before him, a family of river men, the compass handed down like the name.* She reached for it and found that when she actually pressed on it — actually examined the joinery of the memory — she could not say where she'd gotten it. From her father himself? Once, on some evening she ought to be able to picture, some specific kitchen or deck or lamplit cabin? Or from her mother, repeating what she'd been told? Or was it simply a thing Edmund had always known, the way you know the sky is up, knowledge with no birthday?

"I don't know," she said, and the words cost more than she expected. "He always had it. I never asked."

The old woman grunted, unsurprised. "Nobody asks. Things are just *so*, until the water comes." She weighed the compass in her palm once more. "My grandmother said the surveyor's partner was a man who loved the river like a son and betrayed it like a father. Those were her words. *Loved it like a son, betrayed it like a father.* He took the payment for the dam — the company's money, the government's money — and he sat in kitchens up and down this valley with his needle pointing true, and he promised the water would stop at the old ferry crossing. Promised on the compass. And the water did not stop."

"The dam plans were public," Edmund said. Automatic. Defensive. "The elevation figures were published. Everyone knew the height of the reservoir."

"Everyone knew the number," the old woman agreed. "But numbers are not promises. He told them the number was wrong. Told them the engineers had overstated it, that the water would never reach the church, that they could keep their bell tower, keep their high street, keep their dead in the ground above the line. And they believed him because of this." She held the compass up between them, turning slowly, so that its face wheeled through the light. "Because a man who carries his grandfather's needle doesn't lie. That's what my grandmother said. And then the water came to the eaves, and the ones who stayed to move their grandmothers' bones lost everything they hadn't already carried out, and the ones who left early were called fools for a year and wise forever after."

The afternoon had gone very still. Even the dog had given up on the stick. Somewhere below them, under forty feet of green water, a hymnal lay open on a stand in a drowned church, and Edmund found she could not stop seeing it.

"My father wasn't a surveyor," she said. "He was a pilot. He ran cargo. He never—" She stopped. Started again. "He never worked for the dam company."

"No?" The old woman shrugged, a small earthquake of shoulders. "I am telling you what my grandmother said, and my grandmother is forty years in the ground above the line, which is more than I can say for her church. Maybe she embroidered. Old women do." She held the compass out, and Edmund took it back, and it had never felt heavier, not once in thirty years, not even the day it arrived in the mail with the letter from the lawyers and the debt folded inside like a knife in a napkin.

Wait.

The debt. The letter. *Halvorsen Financing Concern,* the letterhead had read, year after year, the name so familiar it had stopped being a word. And the initials under her thumb, worn smooth: *A.H.*

Aurelio H.

The compass in her hand pointed north, faithfully, without opinion, and Edmund Joubert suddenly understood that she had been navigating by an instrument whose history she did not know, and that this might be true of everything else she steered by as well.

"Who was Aurelio H?" she asked. "His full name. Please."

The old woman's pale eyes rested on her a long moment. Behind her, Ines had drifted close without seeming to move, notebook closed, watching with the particular stillness she used for things she intended to keep. Down at the landing, Tomas laughed at something, bright and unbroken, a sound from a different country.

"I don't know the full name," the old woman said at last. "Grandmother only ever said the surname once, and she spat after it, and my mother made her apologize to the Virgin." She gathered her dried fish and her whistles back into their basket, the audience concluded. "But I will tell you this for free, captain, since you bought nothing from me. When the water came, there were men who went through the empty houses before it did. Taking doors, taking roof tiles, taking the saints off the walls. Salvage, they called it. And the man who organized the salvage barges — the one who got rich on our drowning — that man's name began with H also. Draw your own lines on that map."

She walked away, small and straight-backed, leaving Edmund standing in the heat with a compass that had stopped being her father's and not yet become anything else.

* * *

They cast off an hour before dusk. Edmund took the wheel and said little. Ines stood beside her and, mercifully, said less, though Edmund could feel the notebook in her pocket like a held breath.

The drowned village slid past on the port side, doors open, curtains fading, the chair still facing the water. The bell tower leaned east, listening.

"Edmund." Ines's voice, low. "The initials. A.H. — the expedition leader is Daniel Halvorsen. The financing concern that hired us—"

"I know what it spells, Ines."

"And your debt—"

"*I know what it spells.*"

The silence that followed had currents in it. Edmund stared north, where the compass pointed, where it had always pointed, and understood for the first time that a compass doesn't tell you where to go. It only tells you where you are. Everything else — every heading, every course, every safe passage — is a decision you make yourself, based on charts that might be wrong.

Her father had carried this compass his whole life. Had given it to her with his own hands — that much was solid, that much she could see, the wheelhouse of the old *Mariana*, salt-crusted glass, his cracked thumbnail tapping the case: *never steer by anything else, little one.* Never steer by anything else. And all the while, apparently, the thing itself had been steering a story she'd never been told.

Below decks, Tomas was teaching himself knots from a borrowed book, working the rope in the lamplight. Ahead, three days upriver, a missing expedition waited with answers she was beginning to suspect she didn't want. And in her breast pocket, against her heartbeat, the needle of the agreement pointed steadily toward all of it.

That night, unable to sleep, Edmund opened her strongbox and took out the oldest document she owned: the original loan note, signed the year she was twenty-two, and read the signature line properly for the first time in a decade.

The ink had faded, but the name was legible enough.

*A. Halvorsen.*

She sat with it until the lamp burned low, and outside the window the dead water lay flat as a held breath, covering everything.
