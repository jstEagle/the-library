## 5. First Sign

The trading post announced itself before they saw it: a smell of woodsmoke and drying fish, then the sound of a dog barking itself hoarse at the river traffic, and finally the buildings themselves, leaning together like conspirators on the muddy shelf of bank where the current slowed enough to make a landing possible. Ines stood at the rail of the *Magdalena* and sketched it in her notebook without deciding to — the habit of twenty years, the hand moving before the mind gave permission. A long low warehouse with a corrugated roof gone rust-brown. A dock of blackened pilings. A church with no steeple, only a cross nailed crooked above the door as if even God's address here was provisional.

"Kilometer forty-one," she said, and wrote it under the sketch.

Edmund brought the boat in the way she did everything — without apparent effort and without a single wasted motion, reading the slack water where the tributary mouth silted out, swinging the stern wide, kissing the pilings so gently that Ines felt the contact more in the rigging's shiver than in her own body. Tomas appeared from below decks the moment the engine died, materializing on the rail with the boneless ease of the young, his eyes already cataloguing everything: the dog, the crates stacked under the warehouse awning, the two men mending a net who had stopped mending to watch them.

"You said supplies," he said to Edmund.

"I said we'd look for supplies." Edmund killed the engine fully and stood a moment with her hand on the throttle, listening to the silence come back in layers — water first, then insects, then the far complaint of the dog. "This is the last place before the river goes shallow. If the Halvorsen expedition bought anything in the last month, it was here."

"And if they didn't?"

"Then we're looking for ghosts," Edmund said, "and I'm going home."

Ines heard what Tomas heard — that Edmund said it too flatly, too quickly, the way people say things they don't quite believe. She said nothing. She had learned, in cartography and in life, that the most important features on any map were the ones drawn faintest.

* * *

The trading post's proprietor was a woman named Odile, broad as a barrel and twice as weathered, who ran her store, her ledger, and apparently the entire settlement's opinions from behind a counter scarred by decades of machete work and machete sharpening. She knew Edmund — or knew of her; there was a nod between the two women that carried the weight of some older transaction, unexplained. She let them look through her stock without hovering, which Ines recognized as either courtesy or contempt, and possibly both.

It was Tomas who found it. Of course it was. The boy had been drifting along the back wall with his hands in his pockets, performing boredom, while his eyes worked like a customs officer's — and then he stopped, so abruptly that Ines nearly walked into him.

Behind a stack of salt barrels, half-hidden in shadow, sat a wooden crate. Government stencil on one side. Rope handles. And across the top, a pattern of knots.

Tomas went down on his knees in front of it the way other boys went down in front of an altar.

"That's his," he said. His voice came out strange, squeezed. "That's Uncle Anders's knotwork."

Ines crouched beside him. The knots were real — not cargo ties but something deliberate, ornamental almost, a sequence of interlocking loops run along the rope handle with a precision that spoke of practice, of habit, of a man whose hands needed to be doing something even when nothing needed doing. She had seen knotwork like it once before, years ago, in a survey office in the capital, on the personal gear of a cartographer named Halvorsen who drew coastlines the way monks illuminated manuscripts.

"How do you know it's him specifically?" she asked gently. "Lots of sailors tie fancy knots."

"Not like this." Tomas touched one loop without undoing it, reverent. "He taught me this one. He called it the widow's chain. He said—" The boy stopped, swallowed. "He said you tied it on rope you expected to come back for. So you could prove later it was yours."

The words settled over the little storeroom like dust. Ines looked at the crate again and felt the shape of the mystery shift beneath her, the way a river bottom shifts under a hull. Not looted. Not abandoned. *Marked.* Left deliberately, in a place where someone meant to find it.

"Odile," Edmund called toward the front. "Who left this?"

* * *

The story came out in pieces, the way stories do in river country, each teller holding a fragment and none of them the whole. Odile remembered the party well — eleven of them, maybe twelve, foreigners mostly, polite, big appetites, paying in good coin. They'd come upriver on a chartered barge six weeks ago, bought rice and kerosene and quinine, and the tall one with the gray in his beard — Anders, Tomas breathed, *that's my uncle* — had spent an entire evening at her counter drawing maps on butcher paper just for the pleasure of it, maps of places neither of them would ever see.

"They went inland," Odile said. She wiped the counter with a rag that had long since surrendered to its work. "Hired porters from up the valley. Took the mule track past the burn line." She shrugged, a mountain range moving under her blouse. "People go inland. Sometimes they come back."

"When?" Edmund asked.

"Five weeks. Maybe six." Odile frowned, and Ines watched the frown and understood it mattered. "Later than they said. The porters came back without them — well, some of the porters. The ones who came back wouldn't say much. Just that the foreigners wanted to keep going, past where the porters would go, and paid off their contracts proper." Her eyes moved across the three of them — the captain, the mapmaker, the boy kneeling by the crate — and something in her face closed, a shutter drawn against weather. "You're not the first to ask. There was a man here two weeks back. City clothes. Asked the same questions. Paid me to wire a message downriver and not to talk about it."

Ines felt Tomas go still beside her. She kept her own voice level. "Did you talk about it?"

"I'm talking now, aren't I." Odile folded the rag with great deliberation. "Because that man looked at me the way a bailiff looks at furniture. Like I was something to be seized." She spat, with precision, into a spittoon worn silver by use. "Your dead men left that crate with me. Said if anyone came asking after the expedition — anyone who wasn't wearing city shoes — I should give it up. Said the person who came would know what the knots meant."

She nodded at Tomas. "Boy, open it. It's yours by rights, near as I can tell."

* * *

They opened the cache in the warehouse's shade, out of Odile's way and out of the sun, and Ines knelt to inventory it with the same discipline she brought to everything: notebook open, pencil sharp, every item logged before it was lifted. Rice, still sealed. Kerosene, untouched. Quinine tablets, a full tin. A coil of climbing rope. A canvas tent, rolled tight. A brass lamp. And beneath all of it, wrapped in oilcloth, three items that were clearly not supplies.

The first was a journal — Anders Halvorsen's, by the handwriting, though the page Ines turned to was torn out, cleanly, at the spine. Someone had removed a record deliberately. The second was a folding rule, the kind surveyors used, its brass joints green with age. The third was a map.

Not a printed map. A hand-drawn one, in the same confident ink as the journal, showing the river they had just ascended and then — past the trading post, past the last marked settlement — a country of blank space. But not entirely blank. Into that emptiness Anders Halvorsen had penciled a single line of trail, climbing northeast away from the water, annotated in a small careful hand:

*Went this way. Willingly. Do not follow unless you mean it.*

And below that, another line, different ink, bluer, added later:

*If you are reading this, we are alive. We are staying. Forgive us.*

Ines read both lines three times. Around her, the warehouse sounds continued — the dog, the river, Odile's broom somewhere inside — but she had entered that other country, the one made of paper and inference, where she did her truest living.

"He's alive," Tomas said. He was holding the tent roll against his chest like a child holding a blanket. "Uncle Anders is alive. They all are. They *stayed*—"

"Or were made to stay," Vane said.

Ines had forgotten he was there. The financier's agent stood at the edge of the shade with his hands clasped behind him, city shoes immaculate despite the mud of the journey, his face arranged into an expression of reasonable concern that Ines had already learned to distrust on principle. But she made herself consider the words, because she considered everything; that was her discipline and her curse.

"The porters were paid off properly," she said. "The cache was left intact. The note says *willingly*."

"A note can be dictated." Vane smiled mildly. "I don't mean to be grim, Miss Reyes. I mean to be accurate. Eleven people don't simply decide to vanish up a mule track. Something is keeping them. We should determine what before we follow."

"Then we agree," Ines said, rising, brushing off her knees. "We should determine what. That's why we're following."

Something flickered across Vane's face — surprise, perhaps, or recalculation. She had the distinct pleasure of watching him adjust his assessment of her, the way a man adjusts his footing on uncertain ground.

* * *

They walked the edge of the settlement as the light went long and gold, Ines with her notebook, Tomas carrying the crate's rope coil because he refused to let it out of arm's reach, Edmund a few paces ahead saying nothing, which Ines had learned was how the captain thought. At the treeline where the mule track began its climb, they found what Ines had promised herself she'd find, because Anders Halvorsen was the sort of man who always left a sign.

Trail marks. Cut into the bark of a strangler fig at shoulder height: an arrow, and beneath it a number — *11* — and beneath that a symbol she didn't recognize, a spiral with a bar through it, repeated twice.

"Eleven," she said aloud. "The party size. He's counting himself out loud so anyone tracking knows the count hasn't changed." She traced the spiral without touching it. "But this..."

Tomas leaned in. "Widow's chain," he said quietly. "He carved it. Same knot, but in wood."

Ines stood back and looked at the whole message the way she looked at a contour map — letting the shapes resolve into meaning. An arrow. A count. A knot that meant *rope you expect to come back for*. A note that said *we are staying, forgive us.*

"It's not a distress signal," she said slowly. "It's not a plea for rescue. It's a..." She searched for the word and found it, and the finding frightened her a little. "It's an invitation. With a warning attached. *Do not follow unless you mean it.*"

"Mean what?" Tomas asked.

"I don't know yet." Ines closed her notebook. The truth was she did have a hypothesis — she was a cartographer; hypotheses were her native weather — and the hypothesis was that Anders Halvorsen had found something inland that the maps omitted, something worth staying for, something he wanted found only by people who would understand it. But hypotheses were cheap. Evidence was expensive. And the evidence was four days' climb up a mule track into country that grew less mapped with every switchback.

Edmund had stopped at the treeline, looking up the track as it vanished into green shadow. The late light caught the lines of her face and Ines saw, not for the first time, that the captain's competence and her isolation were the same feature viewed from different angles.

"Well, Captain?" Vane's voice was pleasant. "The river ends ahead. The contract says find the expedition. The expedition appears to have found somewhere else. I suggest we consult our respective interests."

Edmund didn't answer him. She looked at the carved fig tree, at the arrow pointing up into the unmapped green, and then — briefly, unexpectedly — at Ines, as if asking a question she couldn't put into words.

Ines opened her notebook to the fresh page and wrote the date, the place, the bearing of the trail, and beneath it, because it was true and because someone ought to write it down:

*They went willingly. We go anyway.*

"We hire mules at first light," Edmund said.

Above the treeline, far up the valley, the evening clouds had begun to build — white towers stacking themselves against the blue with a patience that Ines, who had grown up watching monsoon skies, did not like at all. She said nothing about it. There would be time for weather later. Tonight there was a map to redraw, and for the first time in eleven days, the mystery had a shape she could hold: eleven people, one trail, and a knot that meant *come back for me.*

She sharpened her pencil and began.
