## 6. The Smuggler's Toll

Tomas had learned to count a boat's worth by listening. Not by looking — looking got you caught, and caught stowaways got put off at the next landing with their ears boxed and their pride left bleeding on the dock. But listening was safe. Listening, you could lie flat on the deck planking above the engine room with the heat of the boiler seeping up through the boards like a living thing, and you could hear everything: the captain's boots on the wheelhouse stairs, Ines's pencil scratching at her charts in the saloon, and Mr. Vane's voice, low and even, saying things that never quite added up.

Three days out from the trading post, Tomas had Vane's voice memorized the way other boys memorized football scores. He knew its rhythms the way he knew his uncle's knotwork — by the small signatures no one else bothered to read. And Vane's voice had a signature all right. It smoothed itself flat whenever money came near it, went soft and round as river stones, and it did that now, down in the saloon, while the *Mariana Q* — that was the boat's name, he'd finally pried it out of Ines over dinner, the *Mariana Q*, though the letters on the stern were worn to ghosts — chugged upstream through country that was getting poorer the further they went.

"Your captain's accounts are her own affair," Vane was saying.

"My captain," Ines answered, "is running low on coal, low on food, and low on patience, and you keep asking her questions she doesn't want to answer. I'm asking you instead. What is it you actually want from this trip?"

A pause. Tomas could picture it exactly: Vane's long fingers laced on the table, his hat set square beside his cup, everything about him arranged as neatly as a shop window. He'd seen it often enough through the gap under the saloon door where the planking had shrunk.

"I want what my employer wants," Vane said. "The expedition found. Safe, whole, and accounted for."

"And if they're not found?"

"Then we'll know that too."

It was a nothing answer. It was the kind of answer that sounded reasonable until you held it up to the light and saw there was nothing inside it. Tomas rolled onto his back and looked at the underside of the hurricane deck, at the tarred seams and the rust blooms around the bolts, and thought about how much of the world was like that — built to look solid, hollow underneath.

He'd been aboard six days now. Six days since he'd crawled out of the lifeboat canvas where he'd hidden himself at the landing, since Captain Joubert had hauled him up by the collar and asked him one question — *whose boy are you?* — and he'd said the only true thing he had: *Halvorsen's. My uncle's. Daniel Halvorsen.* She hadn't believed him. He could tell she hadn't; her eyes had gone narrow and measuring, like she was pricing him. But she hadn't put him off either, and every day since, he'd been earning his place the way stowaways do — hauling lines, feeding the fire, learning the boat's moods. The captain didn't thank him. She corrected him. But corrections, he was beginning to understand, were a kind of currency on the *Mariana Q*, maybe the only kind that mattered.

And Mr. Vane watched him. That was the other thing he'd learned. Whenever Tomas carried coal or coiled rope within sight of the saloon windows, he could feel Vane's attention settle on him like a hand on the back of his neck. Not hostile. Worse than hostile — *inventorying*. As if Tomas were a line item being checked against some list.

* * *

The river narrowed that afternoon.

Tomas felt it before he saw it, the way the boat's motion changed — the lazy wallow of open water tightening into something quicker, more nervous, the current grabbing the hull and shoving. He climbed to the main deck rail and looked ahead, and what he saw made the skin crawl up his arms.

The river ran between bluffs now, red clay walls furred with green, and the channel threaded between gravel bars that showed their pale spines above the low water like the backs of sleeping animals. On the left bank, where the bluff stepped down to a shingle beach, stood a collection of structures that had no business calling themselves a village: a long shed roofed in corrugated tin gone orange with rust, two smaller buildings, a signal mast flying a flag Tomas couldn't read at this distance but understood anyway. Every river kid knew what a toll station looked like, even if the ones near home were honest ones, run by the conservancy men with their brass badges and their receipt books.

This wasn't that.

"They call it the Ferry," Sefu-less days ago, Ines had said of this stretch — no, that wasn't right, they hadn't met Sefu yet, that was still ahead of them somewhere in the mountains — Ines had called it something else. She'd spread her chart on the saloon table the night before and tapped a blank space on the left bank below the second rapids. "Here. The chart says *landing, disused*. The chart lies. It's the toll."

"The toll," Edmund had repeated, in a voice with nothing extra in it.

"There's a gang works this reach. They've been here longer than the charts have been wrong. They take payment for passage — cash, cargo, whatever a boat can spare. Mostly boats pay, because mostly boats are smart." Ines had looked up then, at the captain. "How much can we spare?"

Edmund had been quiet a long moment, and Tomas, washing dishes three feet away, had watched her face do something complicated — not fear exactly, but the arithmetic of a woman counting what she had left and finding the column short.

"We'll see when they name their price," she'd said.

Now the price was coming into view. Two longboats lay pulled up on the shingle, each big enough for eight oarsmen, and figures moved among the tin-roofed sheds with the unhurried confidence of men who owned what they surveyed. One of them walked out onto a spit of gravel at the water's edge and raised a lantern — absurd in full daylight, but Tomas understood that too; it wasn't for seeing, it was for signaling. *Stop. Pay. Pass.*

Captain Joubert rang the engine down to half. The *Mariana Q* lost headway and began to drift, and the whole boat seemed to hold its breath along with everyone on it.

Tomas found his place at the rail near the gangway, where he could see without being in the way, and Ines came to stand beside him, her arms folded, her jaw set. Behind them both, at a careful distance, stood Mr. Vane, hands clasped behind his back, watching the shore with an expression of pleasant, unhurried interest, as if the armed men on the beach were a species of bird he'd traveled a long way to observe.

The longboats came out. Eight oars a side, pulling easy, and in the bow of the lead boat a man stood braced against the thwarts — a big man, heavy through the shoulders, wearing a suit jacket that might once have belonged to someone respectable and a red sash knotted at his waist. He carried a shotgun broken open over his forearm, the shells visible in the light like dark candies. Behind him, the second boat followed with men who weren't even pretending not to be armed.

They came alongside with a bump and a scrape, and hooks went over the rail — actual iron hooks, thrown by practiced hands, biting into the *Mariana Q*'s gunwale — and Tomas's stomach dropped through the deck plates, because that was how it started. Hooks first. Then boarding. Then the arithmetic.

"Afternoon, Captain." The big man's voice carried easily across the water, warm as a handshake. He had a broad face and a smile that used all of it, and eyes that used none of it. "Fine old boat. Don't see many of her class anymore. You're a long way from your home waters, eh?"

"I'm wherever the river puts me," Edmund said. She stood at the gangway with her hands loose at her sides, and Tomas marveled at how still she was. "What's the passage fee this season?"

"Ah. Straight to business. I like that in a captain." The man hooked one arm over the rail and hoisted himself halfway up, casual as a man leaning on a fence, and two of his crew came up behind him onto the deck, moving with the loose-limbed ease of people who had done this many times and never once been sorry. "Season rate. Let's see." He rubbed his jaw, drawing it out, making them all stand in the theater of it. "Fuel first. I'll take your spare coal oil — say forty liters. Then provisions for my boys, rice, salt fish, whatever's in your stores. And a passage fee."

"Which is?"

The man smiled wider. "Two hundred."

Tomas heard Ines's breath catch. He didn't know much about money — stowaways didn't carry any — but he knew enough to know that two hundred was not a fee, it was a sentence. He saw it land on Captain Joubert too, saw it hit her somewhere behind the eyes, though her face never moved.

"That's not a fee," she said evenly. "That's a robbery with paperwork."

"It's whatever I say it is, out here." The man said it cheerfully, without heat, the way you'd correct a child on a point of grammar. "You can refuse, of course. Some do." He gestured with his chin toward the shallows downstream, where the ribs of an old barge showed above the waterline, picked clean as a fish skeleton. "That one refused. River keeps what the river takes, eh? Or—" and here his gaze slid past Edmund, past Ines, and settled, deliberate as a hand, on Tomas, "—you can be sensible, and everybody goes home rich except you."

Something cold slid down Tomas's spine. The man knew. Not about him specifically — but he'd marked the youngest person on the deck, the easiest thing to threaten, and he'd done it without breaking his smile, and every adult on that boat had seen him do it, and none of them had flinched yet.

"Forty liters of oil," Edmund said. "Rice and salt fish to the value of thirty. And fifty in notes. That's real, and it's today, and it's more than most boats give you."

"Two hundred."

"Fifty."

The man sighed, a performance of regret so complete it was almost artistry. He turned his broken shotgun lazily toward the sky, then toward the wheelhouse, then let it sag. "Captain. Captain. Look at your boat. Old girl like this, running these waters alone, no escort, no papers worth the paper. Accidents happen on this reach. Boats catch fire. Boats ground out. Boats lose rudders in the narrows just ahead — nasty water, the narrows. I'd hate to read about you in the shipping news." He leaned closer, and his voice dropped into something almost intimate. "I'd hate to read about the boy especially."

Tomas's hands closed on the rail. Beside him, Ines shifted her weight forward, and he heard her say, low and furious, "Touch him and—"

"Ines." Edmund's voice cut clean across hers. Not loud. Just final. Her eyes never left the big man's face. "Fifty is what I have. You can take it or you can take the oil and the food and we part friends, or you can take the whole boat and explain to whoever runs your ledger why you sank a working vessel for spite. Spite doesn't pay your boys. Notes pay your boys."

For a moment — one long, rolling moment, the river slapping the hulls, the oars creaking in their locks — nobody moved, and Tomas understood that he was watching the truest thing he would ever see about the world: that everything, everywhere, came down to somebody counting.

Then a voice spoke from the back of the deck. Mild. Unhurried. Almost apologetic.

"One hundred and eighty."

Everyone turned. Mr. Vane stepped forward out of the shadow of the deckhouse, and he was holding a wallet — no, not a wallet, a folio, a flat leather case of the kind Tomas had only ever seen clutched by bank men and priests — and he opened it with the calm of a man opening a hymnal.

"One hundred and eighty," Vane repeated. "In notes. Plus the forty liters of oil, which I understand is customary. And in exchange, Captain—" he glanced at the big man, and his pleasant face did something Tomas had never seen it do before: it stopped being pleasant. For half a second, the mask slipped sideways, and underneath it was something flat and cold and very old, something that looked at the man in the red sash the way a clerk looks at a misfiled page. "—in exchange, our boat passes unmolested, our persons are untouched, and your men remove their hooks from this vessel's rails. Now, please."

The silence that followed had texture. Tomas could feel it pressing on his eardrums.

The big man looked at Vane. He looked at the folio. And then he laughed — a big, rolling laugh, genuine as far as Tomas could tell — and stuck out a hand. "Where were you hiding this one, Captain? A passenger with manners and money." He took the notes Vane counted out — Tomas counted them too, feverishly, from his place at the rail: ten, twenty, fifty, a hundred, a hundred and eighty, crisp notes, *crisp* notes, the kind of notes that hadn't been folded, hadn't been sweated, hadn't lived a working life — and tucked them away in the red sash. "Friends, then. Friends of the Ferry. You run this reach again, you ask for Bosede. Bosede remembers friends."

"Bosede will find me forgettable," Vane said mildly, and the mask was back, the pleasant agent again, folding away his folio. "Hooks."

The hooks came out of the rails. The longboats backed water. And the *Mariana Q* stood on upriver through the narrows while the toll station fell away astern, its tin roofs flashing in the late sun.

* * *

That night, anchored in a backwater above the rapids with the fire banked low, Tomas couldn't sleep.

He lay in his corner of the deckhouse — his corner now, unspoken but real, a pallet of sacking that had become his by three nights' occupation — and he turned the day over and over like a stone in his hands, looking for what was underneath.

One hundred and eighty. Crisp notes. Plus the oil, plus the food, plus whatever else had changed hands in the smoothness of that transaction that he'd been too young and too far away to see. And Captain Joubert had said, plain as noon, *fifty is what I have.*

So where had a hundred and thirty come from? Where did a financier's *agent* — a man who filed reports and asked questions and carried, supposedly, an expense account — get a folio full of unfaded banknotes and the nerve to spend them like water?

Tomas had grown up around money the way poor kids do, which is to say he'd grown up around its absence and therefore knew its smell better than anyone. Money had a smell. Honest money smelled like work — coal dust and fish scales and the sourness of a note passed hand to hand a hundred times. This money had smelled like nothing. Like paper straight from a press. And nobody carried paper straight from a press unless they were a bank, or a government, or—

Or somebody spending somebody else's fortune.

He got up quietly, eased past the saloon where Ines slept curled in the settee with her chart tube for a pillow, and climbed to the hurricane deck. The night was thick with river sounds — water worrying the anchor chain, frogs stitching the dark together, the far-off complaint of some animal in the reeds. Below him, lamplight showed in the wheelhouse, and through the glass he saw two figures: the captain at the wheel she didn't need to hold at anchor, and Vane standing beside her, and the two of them talking, heads close.

Tomas moved along the deck until he was above the wheelhouse roof, where the tarred canvas would carry sound the way the saloon floorboards did, and he lay down flat, heart thumping, ashamed of himself and doing it anyway.

"—generous today," the captain was saying. Her voice was carefully neutral, which Tomas had learned meant it was anything but. "More generous than your employer's advance covered."

"The advance covered expenses."

"This wasn't an expense. This was a bribe dressed as a toll, and you paid it without blinking, and you paid it in notes I couldn't have earned in a year of running this river honest." A pause. Water slapped the hull. "Who are you carrying money for, Mr. Vane?"

"For the same people who are paying you, Captain." Vane's voice was smooth as poured oil. "My employer wants the Halvorsen party found. I've explained this."

"You've *said* it. Saying isn't explaining. An agent carries expense money — twenty, thirty notes, receipts for everything. You carry a folio like a floating bank branch, and you spent a hundred and thirty surplus notes today like a man buying cigarettes." Another pause, longer. "I've known smugglers who carried less cash than you, and they at least admitted what they were."

"Captain." And here Vane's voice changed — softened, dropped, became the voice of a reasonable man patiently instructing a slower one, and Tomas felt his skin prickle, because he'd heard that voice before, from men on the docks, from the debt collectors who'd come to his mother's door. "You're tired. We're all tired. The money is spent, the boat is safe, the boy is safe — you noticed, I hope, that the boy was the one they marked. I solved your problem today. Perhaps the gracious response is thanks rather than audit."

Silence. Long enough that Tomas's legs cramped and he had to shift his weight, slow as syrup.

"Thanks," Edmund said at last, and her voice was ice over deep water. "Thank you, Mr. Vane. And tomorrow you'll show me the folio. All of it. Because if there's one thing I've learned about debts, it's that they always come due, and I'd like to know the size of yours before it lands on my boat."

Vane laughed softly. "Good night, Captain."

Tomas lay flat on the hot canvas, staring up at a sky with no stars in it, just the brown haze of the river country, and his mind worked like the boat's engine, turning over and over.

Because here was what the adults had missed — what the captain, with all her river-reading cleverness, and Ines, with all her maps and trail marks, had both missed, or maybe just declined to look at straight on:

Vane hadn't saved them today. Vane had *bought* them.

Every note he'd handed over the rail was a link in a chain, and chains didn't have to be made of iron. The toll man had taken the money and called them friends, but it was Vane who owned the transaction now, Vane whose generosity sat in the boat's belly like ballast, Vane who could say, any time he liked, *remember who paid your passage.* The captain felt her debt like a stone in her chest — Tomas had seen it, the way she went quiet whenever money was mentioned, the way she touched the wheelhouse shelf where a compass sat like a saint's relic. She knew what owing did to a person.

And Vane was building her a new one, note by crisp note, and smiling while he laid the bricks.

Tomas thought of his uncle, somewhere upriver or up-mountain, alive or not alive, and of the knotwork he'd recognized at the cache — the double-looped mooring hitch his uncle used, the one he'd taught Tomas himself, years ago, on a dock in another life. His uncle trusted people by their knots. *Show me how a man ties off,* Daniel Halvorsen used to say, *and I'll tell you who he is.* 

Mr. Vane tied everything off neat and hidden. Every knot buried, every end tucked away, nothing left showing that a person could grab and pull.

Below him, the wheelhouse lamp went out. Footsteps crossed the deck — Vane's, measured and unhurried, pausing just beneath Tomas's hiding place for a moment, so close that Tomas stopped breathing altogether — and then continued on to the cabin at the stern, and the door clicked shut with the small, precise sound of a man who locked things.

Tomas exhaled slowly and lay back against the warm canvas.

Tomorrow, he decided, he would start watching Vane properly. Not the way the adults watched him — sideways, politely, giving him the benefit of the doubt because he was useful and well-dressed. Properly. The way you watched water ahead of a boat, reading the surface for the bar hidden underneath.

Because Tomas had seen the surface today, and it had been smooth and pleasant and helpful.

And underneath it, he was nearly sure, there was a bar that could wreck them all.
