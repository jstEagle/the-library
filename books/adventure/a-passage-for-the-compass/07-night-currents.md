## 7. Night Currents

The gang's lanterns were still burning at the toll post when Edmund backed the *Magdalena* away from the bank, and she did not look at them again. Looking was a kind of answer, and she had no answers to give a man with a machete and a ledger of other people's fear.

"South channel," she said to the wheel, more to herself than anyone. "We'll take the south channel."

"The charts show it unimproved since—" Ines began.

"The charts are eleven years old and the river is eleven minutes old." Edmund eased the throttle forward, feeling the old engine answer in its own time, like a mule deciding to be agreeable. "Everything below the second bend is new since the last flood. The river redraws itself every season. The charts just haven't caught up."

Behind her, Tomas crouched low against the rail, still shaking from the sight of Vane laying money on the smuggler's table — too much money, folded wrong, as if he'd never handled cash that wasn't someone else's. Vane himself stood at the stern rail with his hands clasped behind him, watching the toll lights shrink, wearing the expression of a man who found the evening's unpleasantness merely inefficient.

Edmund didn't trust the quiet. Gangs like the one at the toll didn't stay bought; they stayed patient. Sooner or later one of them would remember there was a faster boat, or a grudge, or simply nothing better to do than follow a lone vessel down a dark river and take back what had been paid plus interest.

So she ran dark.

She killed the running lamps first, then the deck lights, until the only illumination aboard was the faint amber of the binnacle hood over her compass and the cigarette-end glow of the engine gauges. The *Magdalena* slid out into the current like something returning to its element, and the river closed over their wake without comment.

"You can't possibly see," Ines said, coming to stand beside her at the wheel. Her voice was low, not accusing — a cartographer's genuine bewilderment.

"I don't see. That's the point." Edmund tilted her head toward the water ahead, black on black. "You listen. You feel. The boat tells you what the river's doing before your eyes could ever catch it. Feel that?" She let the wheel speak through her palms, a faint tremor, a hesitation in the helm. "Shoaling water off the left bank. Current's piling up against something it doesn't want to go around. We'll ease right."

Ines was silent a long moment. Then: "My grandmother read weather in her knees. Bad hip. She always knew rain two days before the barometer did."

"Knees and rivers. Same principle."

"I doubt my grandmother would have appreciated the comparison."

"She sounds like she'd have made a fine pilot."

That earned him — Edmund noted it with surprise — the ghost of a smile from Ines Ferreira, who had spent four days aboard being politely impenetrable, sketching everything and committing to nothing. The smile lasted perhaps two seconds before she turned to the rail again, but Edmund filed it away the way she filed everything about this strange cargo of passengers: the boy who claimed kinship with ghosts, the agent whose questions came dressed as small talk, and now the mapmaker who read rivers with her grandmother's knees.

* * *

The trouble came an hour later, where the river widened and forgot itself.

By day it was a benign stretch, braided channels wandering between gravel bars, easy enough for a pilot who knew which thread held depth after the dry months. By night it was a lie told in darkness. The bars didn't show. The channels didn't announce themselves. And the main current, swollen by the afternoon's rain somewhere upstream, had shifted its weight to a side Edmund's memory insisted was wrong.

She felt it before she understood it: a lightness in the helm, the bow going loose, the sound of the water changing from the deep throaty rush of the channel to a thinner, hollower voice.

"Sandbar," she said, and threw the wheel hard over, and rang the engine down so fast the whole boat shuddered.

Too late, or nearly too late. The *Magdalena* struck the bar's shoulder with a grinding shudder that traveled up through the hull and into everyone's bones. Not a hard strike — not yet — but the boat slewed, listed, and hung there with the current pushing her beam-on across the bar's spine, and Edmund knew with cold clarity exactly how the next thirty seconds would go if she lost them: the current would swing the stern downstream, the bow would dig in, and the river would fold her boat in half like wet paper.

"Vane! Tomas! Weight forward — both of you, get to the bow, now!" She heard them stumble aft-to-forward in the dark, heard Vane's protest cut short by Tomas already moving. "Ines — I need the sky. Can you give me stars?"

Ines didn't ask why. That was the thing Edmund would remember afterward, the thing that began, quietly, to rearrange her opinion of the woman: Ines simply looked up, found a gap in the scudding cloud, and said, "Give me a moment."

Edmund fought the wheel. Full astern port, full ahead starboard, rocking the boat, trying to walk her off the bar the way you'd rock a stuck mule free of mud. The engine screamed. The bar ground along the keel with a sound like teeth. Somewhere below, Tomas was hauling the anchor forward because Edmund had shouted for it without planning to, and the boy actually understood, dropping it ahead into deeper water so they'd have something to pull against.

"Antares," Ines said. "There — south, low, above that notch in the ridge line. And that bright one west of it is — yes. Give me your heading and hold the boat steady for ten seconds."

"Holding steady is the one thing I cannot do."

"Then give me five."

Edmund gave her five. The compass card swung drunkenly under the binnacle hood while the boat rocked, but Ines stood at the rail with her arm raised, sighting along her own fingers like a surveyor's instrument, lips moving through some arithmetic Edmund couldn't follow.

"Your channel runs northeast of us," Ines said. "Twenty degrees off your present heading. The bar shelves away fastest on the starboard side — I can tell from the set of the current against Antares' bearing. Trust me or don't, but decide quickly."

It was madness. It was also the most precise sentence anyone had spoken aboard all day. Edmund hauled the wheel to starboard, rang full ahead, and prayed to whatever presided over drowned sandbars and stubborn women.

The *Magdalena* groaned, slid, caught — and then, with a lurch that threw them all against the rails, came free. The hull dropped off the bar's edge into living water, the current took them like a hand catching a falling child, and suddenly they were moving again, sweeping down the true channel with the black banks streaming past and the engine settling from its scream into a working roar.

Edmund became aware, gradually, that she was shaking.

Not visibly. Pilots learned to hide it; a shaking helmsman shook the crew. But inside, her body was running through the arithmetic of what had almost happened — the folded hull, the drowned cargo, the debt collector inheriting a wreck instead of a boat — and the arithmetic kept refusing to total anything except catastrophe.

"Tomas," she called forward. "That anchor work was textbook. Where did a stowaway learn anchoring?"

"My uncle." The boy's voice came back thin but steady. "He said a boat is just a promise you keep twice — once when you leave and once when you arrive."

"Your uncle sounds like he'd have made a fine pilot too."

Behind her, Ines had not moved from the rail. She was still looking at the sky, and when Edmund glanced over, she saw the mapmaker's face lit faintly by the binnacle glow, and on it an expression Edmund recognized from her own mirror on certain nights: the look of someone who has just been useful in the exact way they were made to be useful, and is quietly astonished by it.

* * *

They anchored at midnight in a backwater eddy behind a willow island, where the current couldn't reach them and the toll gang's boats, if any came looking, would pass in the main channel without a glance into the shadows. Edmund posted no watch — the island did that work for them — and the boat settled into the small sounds of a resting vessel: timbers ticking, water chuckling against the hull, the engine cooling itself down in metallic sighs.

She expected sleep. It didn't come. Instead she found herself in the pilothouse with cold tea, and ten minutes later Ines climbed the ladder with two cups of her own and no apology for intruding.

"Couldn't sleep either?"

"I slept on ships for six years," Ines said, settling onto the chart bench. "You learn to sleep through anything except the feeling of having almost been wrong."

"That bar wasn't on any chart, if that helps."

"It doesn't. I should have seen it anyway." She blew on her tea. "That's the job. Seeing what isn't drawn yet."

Outside, the willows moved in a wind too slight to feel, and somewhere far off an animal cried once and thought better of it.

"Why cartography?" Edmund asked. It was the first personal question she'd asked in four days, and it surprised them both equally.

Ines considered the question with the seriousness she brought to everything, turning her cup slowly. "Because maps are arguments," she said finally. "Every map is somebody insisting the world is a certain way. My father drew cadastral surveys for land companies. His maps helped men in the capital prove that villages didn't exist — that the people in them were squatters on paper that hadn't been filled in yet." A pause. "I draw maps so that things can't be disappeared quite so easily. Or I used to believe that. Some days I still do."

"And the expedition? Halvorsen's expedition — is that disappearing things or finding them?"

"Finding." Ines looked at her directly then, and in the dimness her eyes were very steady. "Daniel Halvorsen believes there's country upriver that every map omits. Not because it's unknown. Because it was omitted. On purpose. There's a difference, Captain, between a blank space and an erasure, and I've spent my career learning to tell them apart."

Edmund felt something shift in her chest, some long-sealed hatch cracking a degree. "My father used to say the river keeps two sets of books," she said. "One for the government, one for the truth."

"He sounds like a man worth listening to."

"He drowned when I was nine. Boating accident, they called it, though my mother never believed it and neither did I. After that it was just me and the boat, and then eventually just me." She heard herself say it and marveled — she had not spoken of her father aloud in years, had not permitted the words into the air where they might be examined. "I inherited the *Magdalena* and a debt I've never fully understood. The Halvorsen family financed her refit when I was nineteen. Paper I signed because I was young and the alternative was losing her entirely. Eleven years I've been paying it. The principal never seems to move."

"Debts like that aren't meant to be paid," Ines said quietly. "They're meant to be kept."

The words landed harder than Ines could have known. Edmund covered it by drinking her tea, which had gone bitter and cold.

"What about you?" Ines asked, mercifully turning the current. "Why does a woman run a river alone at night to escape men she's already paid?"

"Because paying them once doesn't mean they're done. Because I've been alone so long that being alone is the only place I'm not performing." Edmund stared out at the black water sliding past the island. "That's not an answer. Here's an answer: when I'm at the wheel, I know exactly who I am. Everything else is fog. The river doesn't care about my debts or my name or whether I'm a good person. It only cares whether I pay attention. There's a mercy in that."

"And tonight you didn't pay attention."

"One moment. One moment of tired, and the river billed me for it." She laughed, short and without much humor. "Maybe that's why I run at night. Punishment and absolution in the same water."

Ines set down her cup. "For what it's worth — your instinct put us broadside to that bar, but your hands got us off it. The stars only pointed. You steered."

"Stars pointed better."

"Stars had a good teacher." Ines rose, gathering the cups. "My grandmother would have liked you, Captain. She also believed in reading what isn't written down."

She went below, and Edmund stayed in the pilothouse a while longer, watching the willow shadows and thinking about erasures and blank spaces, about debts that were meant to be kept, about a boy asleep in the hold who kept promises twice, and a financier who counted money like a man counting other men.

* * *

They got underway again at first gray light, and the river, in the manner of rivers, pretended nothing had happened. The channel ran wide and honest in the morning sun, the bars plainly visible, apologetic almost, and Edmund hated them a little for it.

Tomas appeared at the pilothouse door mid-morning with a coil of line he was pretending to flem down, and a question he was pretending not to ask. "Captain? Last night — the way you talked the boat off the bar. Was that in a book somewhere? Could I learn it?"

"Books will teach you the words. The rest you learn by nearly dying, preferably in daylight." She watched him absorb this with the terrible earnestness of the young. "You did well with the anchor. Most grown men freeze the first time a hull hits a bar. You moved."

"I was scared."

"Everyone was scared. Moving while scared is the entire profession." She nodded at the coil. "Finish that flem properly and I'll show you the engine room after we clear the bend. If you're going to be crew, you'll learn what keeps us floating."

The boy's face did something complicated and bright, and he vanished down the ladder before she could reconsider.

At the rail, Vane stood watching the banks with his binoculars, and Edmund noticed — because last night had recalibrated something in her attention — that he was not watching the scenery. He was watching the sky. Checking weather, or checking for smoke, or checking for something else entirely. When he felt her gaze he lowered the glasses and smiled his courteous, closed-door smile.

"Fine morning, Captain. The river seems in a forgiving mood."

"Rivers don't forgive. They just forget slower than people think."

"Mm." He turned back to his glasses. "I do hope we make good time today. My employer prefers regular reports, and we're rather past due."

Something cold walked down Edmund's spine, quiet as the current. Reports. To whom, Mr. Vane? Through what wire, from what station, on whose frequency?

She said nothing. But that afternoon, when they stopped for wood and water at a rutted landing with a single telegraph pole leaning over it, Edmund watched Vane climb the bank alone with a leather case under his arm, and she marked the pole, and the case, and the length of time he was gone.

And she remembered the compass in her cabin drawer, its worn brass case, the initials she'd never let herself examine too closely — initials that had been on that instrument longer than her debt had been on her boat, though she'd never asked herself how.

Tonight, she decided. Tonight, with the boat secured and the crew fed, she would open the drawer and look at those letters properly, and find out what her compass had been trying to tell her all these years.

The river carried them on toward the trading post at Cane Landing, and the question rode with them, heavier than any cargo.
