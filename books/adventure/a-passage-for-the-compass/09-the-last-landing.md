## 9. The Last Landing

Sefu had known the river would end before any of them did.

He had been waiting at Kessari Landing for three days when the boat came up through the morning mist — a low, patched thing with a wheelhouse the color of rust and a name half-scraped from her stern. He watched it come because there was nothing else to watch. The river here had gone thin as an old man's wrist, braided into channels that wandered among gravel shoals, and the landing itself was less a place than an argument between water and land: a plank ramp, a leaning shed, a row of mules tied to a rail with the patience of animals who had learned that all journeys end in waiting.

The woman at the wheel brought the boat in the way Sefu respected — no flourish, no wasted motion, reading the shallows by the color of the water and the lie of the current on the surface. She set her bow against the current and let the river slow her, and she touched the ramp so gently the mules didn't even lift their heads.

He counted four figures on deck. A tall woman with ink-stained fingers and a surveyor's case strapped across her back. A boy too young to be traveling alone, trying to look like he wasn't staring at everything. And a man in a good coat that had traveled badly, standing slightly apart from the others the way men stand apart when they believe themselves above their company.

Sefu had carried many travelers up this trail. He knew within a minute which ones would listen and which ones would argue with the mountain. He looked at the man in the good coat and thought: *this one will argue.*

The captain came down the ramp last. She was younger than her hands, and her hands were older than her face — rope-scarred, engine-blackened, the hands of someone who had made her living against the grain of things. She stopped in front of him and looked him over without apology, the way he was looking at her.

"You're the guide," she said. Not a question.

"Sefu." He said nothing else. Names were currency; he spent them slowly.

"Edmund Joubert. I'm told you know the trails past the head of navigation."

"I know them."

"We need to reach the high country. There's an expedition missing up there — or not missing. We don't know yet." She glanced back at the boat, at the woman with the surveyor's case. "My cartographer can show you where they were headed."

"Show me the map," Sefu said.

* * *

The map was spread on a crate in the shade of the shed, weighted at the corners with stones, and Sefu bent over it for a long time without speaking. He could feel them watching him — the captain with her arms crossed, the cartographer leaning close, the boy peering under elbows, the man in the good coat standing back as though maps were beneath him and yet unable to stop looking.

The expedition's last known position was marked in red pencil, up beyond the burn line, toward a fold of mountains that most maps of the region left blank or covered with hachures and the word *unsurveyed*. Sefu knew why they were blank. Not because no one had gone there. Because the people who lived there had made it clear, over a long time, that being surveyed was a form of being taken.

"There," the cartographer said, tapping the red mark. "That's where their last message came from. Eleven days ago."

Sefu studied the routes. There were two ways up. The southern pass was longer — six days instead of three and a half — but it was wide, graded in places, used by traders in dry weather. The northern route went straight up the river gorge, along a trail his grandfather had cut and his father had kept, narrow and steep and honest about what it asked.

"The northern route," he said finally, and put his finger on it. "Three days to the burn line. Four if the weather turns."

The man in the good coat stepped forward then, and Sefu saw how he moved — smoothly, courteously, positioning himself between the map and the decision like a man laying claim to something.

"Mr. — Sefu, is it?" He offered a hand. Sefu looked at it and did not take it. The man withdrew the hand without visible offense, which told Sefu he was practiced at being refused. "Vane. I represent the expedition's financial interests. I wonder if you'd consider the southern route instead. It's longer, certainly, but safer, better provisioned, and—" he smiled, "—I understand the northern trail has a reputation."

"It has a character," Sefu said. "Reputations are what people say about places they haven't been."

"A charming distinction. Still — a party carrying supplies, with a boy along—"

"The boy will do fine," Sefu said, and saw the boy's chin lift. "It's the man in the good coat I'd worry about. The southern route is for people who want the mountain to be something it isn't."

Vane's smile held. But something behind it shifted, the way water shifts when a stone is turned over in a streambed. "I'm sure you know your trails, Mr. Sefu. But I know my obligations. My employer invested considerably in this expedition, and I am responsible for delivering results, not for romantic notions of the difficult way. The southern pass is the prudent choice."

"The southern pass is closed," Sefu said.

That got everyone's attention. Vane's eyebrows rose. "Closed?"

"Snow already in the high section. A trader came down ten days ago — he turned back at the shoulder. Said the drifts were waist-deep and rising. By now, a week later?" Sefu shook his head once. "The mountain is closing its southern door. It does this every year. Some years early, some years late. This year early."

"And the northern route?"

"The northern route holds longer. It's lower until the final climb. But it won't hold forever either." He looked at the sky, at the strange bruised weight of the clouds stacking up in the southwest, where the monsoon was gathering itself like a debt coming due. "And there's other weather behind us. Bad rain. When it comes, these rivers will rise and nothing goes up or down for weeks. Maybe months."

"So we go north, and quickly," the captain said.

"We go north, and quickly," Sefu agreed.

Vane was quiet for a moment. Then, reasonably: "Surely one trader's report isn't grounds to—"

"It isn't one trader." Sefu rolled the map closed with deliberate care. "It's the ice on the morning water. It's the birds. It's forty years of watching this mountain decide. You can hire me or not hire me. But if you hire me, we go my way, because I am not interested in burying anyone."

* * *

They hired him.

The negotiation took less time than Sefu expected, mostly because the captain negotiated like a boat pilot — she named a fair price, stated it once, and waited. He respected that too. The cartographer, Ines, paid half in advance from a canvas roll of notes that had clearly been meant for the expedition's own expenses, and Sefu noted that she did it without asking Vane's permission, and that Vane noticed she hadn't asked, and that neither of them said anything about it aloud. The silences between these people were more informative than their words. Sefu filed them away the way he filed landmarks.

Loading the mules took the rest of the day, and it told Sefu more about each of them than any conversation could have.

The captain loaded her own gear, cinched her own knots, and checked everyone else's without being asked — walking the line of mules at dusk, testing each load with a push of her palm, re-tying two of the boy's knots without comment but also without kindness. The cartographer packed her instruments like relics, wrapped in oilcloth, padded with clothing, and labeled everything in a small precise hand even the boxes that needed no labels. The boy worked hard and pretended not to care whether anyone saw, which fooled no one, least of all the mules, who liked him immediately.

Vane supervised. That was the word for it. He stood near the loading with his hands clasped behind his back and issued small suggestions to no one in particular, and when no one picked them up, he picked up a single crate himself — the lightest one, Sefu observed, and only after making sure he was seen doing it.

But it was what Vane loaded that interested Sefu. Among the general freight — rice, rope, the expedition's recovered cache from the trading post, tents — Vane's personal kit included a strongbox, iron-banded, that he handled himself and would not let out of arm's length. It was heavy for its size. And beneath his bedroll, wrapped in sacking, Sefu caught the shape of something long and rigid that was not a tent pole and not a rifle case, though it was nearly the size of one.

Sefu had carried smugglers before. He knew the shapes of hidden things.

He said nothing. The mountain would say it for him, sooner or later. The mountain always did.

* * *

They left at first light, and the first hour of the trail was the river's last argument.

Below Kessari, the water still ran deep enough to float a boat. Above, within sight of the landing, it simply gave up — spreading into sheets of silver over gravel bars, threading between boulders, going nowhere with great determination. The channel split into five, then eight, then a confusion of channels that no pilot could trust and no keel could forgive.

Edmund Joubert stood at the top of the ramp and looked at it for a long time.

"That's it, then," the boy said beside her. "The end of the river."

"No," she said. "The end of *my* part of it. Rivers don't end. They just change jobs." She said it lightly, but Sefu, walking past with the lead mule, heard what was underneath it — the sound of a person leaving behind the one thing she was certain of. He had seen that look before, on miners coming down from claims that played out, on widows at the edge of towns they'd never chosen. The look of someone whose trade had run out of water.

She turned away from the river and faced uphill, and Sefu watched her do it — watched her make the choice in her body before her mind finished arguing. Then she set her hand on the lead mule's neck, next to his, and said, "Lead on."

So he led.

The trail climbed out of the river basin through country that changed every hour, as if the land couldn't decide what it wanted to be. First the grey shingle of the floodplain, hot and glaring. Then scrub forest, thorny and secretive, full of birds that scolded them from cover. Then grass slopes steep enough that the mules walked like furniture being moved, ears back, hooves finding ledges invisible from below. By afternoon they were in the first pine, and the air smelled of resin and cold stone, and the river below was a bright thread stitched through a valley that suddenly looked very deep and very far away.

Sefu set a pace and did not vary it. This was the first law of the mountains: the pace is the pace. The eager burn out by noon; the proud blister by the second day; only the steady arrive. The boy struggled and hid it. The cartographer struggled and didn't bother hiding it, counting her breaths aloud under her breath like a rosary, which was exactly the right way to do it. The captain walked like a woman who had spent her life keeping her feet on a moving deck, and the mountain, sensing a professional, treated her accordingly.

Vane struggled and resented it.

By mid-afternoon of the first day he had begun to talk. Sefu had learned long ago that suffering men divide into those who go silent and those who narrate, and narration was always a bid for something — sympathy, control, an audience. Vane wanted control.

"Remarkable country," Vane said, drawing level with Sefu, breathing carefully through his nose. "You've walked these trails your whole life, I imagine."

"My family has kept them longer than that."

"Family trails. How lovely. And tell me — do you ever carry passengers south? Traders, officials? Men with business interests in the high valleys?"

"I carry whoever pays and walks."

"Of course. Of course." Vane smiled at the pines. "Only, a man of your experience must hear things. See things. Travelers talk on the trail — it's remarkable what people mention to a guide, thinking you're furniture." He produced, from somewhere, a small leather wallet, and let it sit in his open palm without offering it. "Discretion is a valuable quality in a guide. So is memory. I pay well for both."

Sefu walked ten steps before answering, and he used the steps to decide how much contempt to show. Not all of it. Half.

"What is it you want to know?"

"Nothing specific. Anything. Everything. If the captain mentions her debts, if the cartographer mentions her employers, if the boy mentions his family — I'd be grateful to hear of it. Consider it a retainer." The wallet stayed in his palm, patient as a trap.

Sefu looked at the money. Then he looked at Vane, fully, the way he looked at a river crossing he intended to make — measuring depth, current, the far bank.

"You want me to walk ahead of these people and report behind their backs."

"I want us all to reach our destination safely," Vane said mildly. "Information keeps parties safe."

"In my experience," Sefu said, "it's usually the opposite. The man who wants to know everything is the man planning something." He walked on, and left the wallet hanging in the air between them like a smell.

Vane did not press. That was the most telling thing. A merely curious man would have pressed, would have laughed, would have made a joke to cover the awkwardness. Vane put the wallet away with the unhurried calm of a man crossing an item off a list — *attempted, declined* — and dropped back to walk alone.

Sefu did not like him. But liking was irrelevant. What mattered was that Vane had shown him the shape of his purpose, the way a fish shows the hook by fighting it: Vane was not here to find the expedition. Vane was here to manage what the expedition had found, or to make sure it found nothing, and everyone else on this trail was freight to be delivered or discarded accordingly.

Sefu had taken parties up this trail for thirty years. He had never yet lost one. He intended to keep the record intact, and he began, quietly, to plan around the man in the good coat — which tent he'd sleep near, which mule carried his strongbox, which nights his absence from the fire might mean something.

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow above a spring, under pines old enough to have opinions, and Sefu cooked the evening meal the way his mother had taught him: maize flour and dried meat and wild greens, done in one pot, served without ceremony. Food on the trail was not cuisine. It was fuel with manners.

The boy came to him while the others were settling, carrying both their bowls — his own and the captain's — to be filled, and stood waiting with the particular patience of the young, who have learned that questions are best asked sideways.

"You knew my uncle," the boy said. Not quite a question. "Tomas. The Halvorsen boy. My uncle Daniel led the expedition."

"I knew of him," Sefu said, ladling. "I met him once, two years ago. He came up as far as the burn line with a survey team and turned back."

"What was he like?"

Sefu considered lying kindly and decided against it. Lies, on a mountain, had a way of becoming load-bearing. "Impatient," he said. "Smart, and impatient. He wanted the mountain to answer him quickly. The mountain doesn't do quick." He handed back the bowls. "If he's alive up there, he'll have learned patience. The mountain teaches it free."

The boy — Tomas — took the bowls and lingered. "Do you think he's alive?"

"I think," Sefu said, "that dead men leave different signs than live ones, and the signs I've heard described are live ones. Ordered camps. Cached goods, opened carefully. Dead men aren't tidy." He nodded toward the fire, where Edmund Joubert sat sharpening a knife with long even strokes, and the cartographer lay sketching by lamplight, and Vane sat slightly outside the circle of light with his strongbox between his feet. "Now sleep. Tomorrow the trail gets honest."

The boy went. Sefu banked the fire and took the first watch, as was his right and his habit, and sat with his back against a pine trunk looking down the black funnel of the valley they had climbed out of.

Far to the southwest, lightning flickered inside the cloud mass — silent at this distance, a pulse of veins in a bruise. The monsoon, still days off but coming, fat and slow and certain. Behind them it would fill the rivers and cut the world in two. Ahead of them, higher, the snows were already falling on the southern passes, closing doors.

Between the two weathers was a corridor of days, narrowing. They were in it now, all of them — the captain with her drowned certainty, the cartographer with her careful hope, the boy with his uncle, and Vane with whatever it was he served that wasn't a person.

Sefu listened to the mountain breathe around him, the creak of pine, the trickle of the spring, the mules shifting in their pickets, and he made his calculation the way he made all of them: plainly, without comfort.

Four days, maybe five, before the northern pass began to close. After that, winter owned the high country, and everyone in it belonged to winter.

He hoped the man in the good coat understood arithmetic. But he suspected, watching Vane turn something over in his hands in the dark — a letter, perhaps, or a key, catching the firelight once and going dark again — that Vane understood arithmetic perfectly.

That was what worried him.
