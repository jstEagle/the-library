## 10. Footprints in the Ash

The burn line announced itself before they saw it, a gray seam running down the mountainside like an old scar that had never quite healed. Ines noticed it first from the mule track — she was always noticing things first, though she had learned on this journey to keep some observations to herself. Sefu had opinions about people who narrated every rock and root. But this she said aloud.

"There," she said, pointing with her chin, the way he did. "Fire. Old one."

Sefu reined his mule and studied the slope for a long moment. The burned trees stood in ranks up the hillside, black and silvered, most of them still standing because mountain pines were stubborn that way — killed but unwilling to fall. Between them, the ground had gone over to scrub and fireweed, that pale pink flower that came first after burning, as if the land itself needed something gentle to look at while it recovered.

"Three years," Sefu said. "Maybe four."

"Not five?"

He gave her a look that Ines had learned to read as approval. "Five, the trunks would be soft. You could push them over." He clicked his tongue and the mule moved on. "Fires run uphill here. Whatever burned, it started low. River side."

Ines wrote it down. She wrote everything down now — elevations, bearings, the hour the mist lifted off the gorge each morning, the number of steps between the cairns. It was not only habit. It was the discipline of a cartographer who had spent eleven days not knowing where the people she cared about were, and who had decided, somewhere on the river, that she would never again be the person who couldn't say precisely where she had looked.

Because that was the truth of it, the truth she carried under her ribs like a stone: Anders Halvorsen had taught her to draw maps, and she had failed to draw the one that mattered. When the expedition's last transmission had come through — broken, half-static, Anders laughing about weather coming in — she had been three hundred miles away at the survey office, redrawing a coastline someone else had already drawn better. She could have been there. She should have been there. And so now she measured everything, recorded everything, left nothing to memory, because memory was the thing that had betrayed her into comfort.

They made camp that night below the burn line, in the green timber where there was wood and water, and at dawn began to climb into the ash.

* * *

The ash held the cold of the night longer than the living forest did. Ines felt it through her boots, a chill rising out of the ground as if the fire had left something buried there that had never finished being warm. The burned trees ticked against each other in the wind, a dry wooden knocking, dozens of dead hands applauding slowly.

Edmund walked point. She was different on foot than she was on the river — quieter, watchful, her head turning constantly toward sound. Tomas walked beside Ines with the particular energy of a boy who had slept badly and was too proud to admit it. He had found his uncle's trail marks two days ago at the trading post, and since then he had not stopped looking for more of them, scanning every trunk and stone as if the mountain might simply hand him what he wanted if he stared hard enough.

Sefu brought up the rear with the mules, and Vane — Vane walked in the middle, which Ines noted without comment. A man who kept himself surrounded by other people's bodies. She had begun keeping a separate notebook for observations about Mr. Vane, and the notebook was getting full.

They crossed a streambed gone to gravel and found the first mark.

It was cut into a boulder at the stream's edge: three lines and a circle, shallow, weathered, but unmistakably recent — the lichen around the cuts had died, leaving a pale ghost of the symbol against the stone. Ines knelt and touched it. The rock was smooth inside the grooves. Someone had taken care with this.

"That's the survey mark," Tomas said, crouching beside her. His voice had gone careful, the way voices do when they are afraid of breaking. "Uncle Anders used it. Three lines for the three rivers, the circle for—" He stopped.

"For the source," Ines finished quietly. "For the place the water comes from." She had watched Anders cut that mark a dozen times, quick and neat with his knife, always the same, always taking pride in it. *A map is a promise,* he used to tell her. *Every mark you make, you're promising the next person the land is what you say it is.*

She looked up the slope. Above the boulder, maybe two hundred yards through the standing dead, there was a second mark on another tree. And above that, faintly, the ground leveled into a shelf where the burn thinned and the green came back.

"Camp," she said.

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Edmund said, "We go slow," and Ines loved her a little for it — for understanding, without being told, that you did not run toward a thing you had spent eleven days dreading.

* * *

The camp sat on the shelf above the burn line, tucked against a granite outcrop that would have sheltered it from wind and, from below, hidden it entirely. Ines saw it before she understood what she was seeing: a rectangle of flattened grass, a ring of stones, a lean-to of poles and canvas. Orderly. Neat. Almost tidy.

And empty.

Her legs nearly went out from under her. She didn't let them. She stood very still at the edge of the campsite and made herself look at all of it, piece by piece, the way she would look at a map — because a map did not care what you felt, and neither, right now, could she afford to.

The fire ring held ashes that had been raked flat and doused. Not scattered, not kicked apart by animals — deliberately extinguished, carefully, the way a careful man put out a fire he expected to return to. The lean-to's poles were lashed with proper knots, tensioned, not cut or collapsed. Six bedrolls had been laid out; six impressions remained in the flattened grass, and Ines counted them twice, then a third time, pressing her palm against the ground as if she could take its pulse.

Six. The expedition had been seven. Anders, Daniel, four porters, and old Marta, the botanist. Seven.

She was still counting when Tomas made a sound behind her.

He was standing at the edge of the lean-to, and he was holding a hat.

It was a wide-brimmed felt hat, sun-bleached almost white, the crown creased in the particular way that Anders creased his — pushed in at the front with two fingers, always, a habit from some older expedition he'd never explained. The band had come loose at one side. There was a burn hole near the brim's edge, old, the felt melted and hardened around it, from a campfire years ago that Ines remembered, that she had laughed at, that Anders had sworn about for a week because the hat had belonged to his father before him.

Tomas held it in both hands the way you hold a bird. He didn't cry. He just stood there, and his face did something complicated and terrible, hope and grief pulling in opposite directions across it like weather fronts colliding.

"He wouldn't leave it," Tomas said. "He never leaves it. He wore it in the boat, he wore it in the rain, Mama used to say he'd wear it in the bath if she let him." He turned the hat over. "Someone folded it. Look. Someone set it down here, on the bedroll, and folded the band back in place. Like—"

"Like a message," Edmund said quietly.

Ines crossed to the bedroll. Beneath the hat, weighted with a small stone, lay a page torn from a journal. The paper was good stock, the handwriting slanted and fast — she knew that hand too, had spent two years reading it on shared maps and margin notes. Anders Halvorsen wrote the way he talked, leaning forward, impatient with straight lines.

She picked it up and read it aloud, because Tomas needed to hear it and because she needed to hear her own voice saying it, needed the words to be real and in the air:

*"Third day past the burn line. We have found what we came for, and it is not what the maps say, because the maps say nothing at all. There is a valley above the headwall that no survey has ever recorded — I have checked every sheet, including ours, including my father's. Either everyone who found it chose not to write it down, or no one who found it ever came back to say so.*

*The valley remembers people. That sounds like madness written down, but I have stood in it and I am telling you it is true. There are terraces cut into the hillsides older than any ruin I know, and smoke from fires, and people — people, Ines, living ones — who speak a tongue Marta says belongs to no family she can place. They knew the river before the river had our names on it.*

*We are going in. All of us, by choice — I want that understood. No one is taken, no one is lost. If you are reading this, we stayed longer than we meant to, and that will be our story: not vanished, but unwilling to leave.*

*Do not follow unless you are prepared to have everything you believe about this river rewritten. And bring paper. Bring so much paper."*

The last line was underlined twice, and beside it, small, in the corner, Anders had drawn the survey mark — three lines and a circle — and inside the circle, instead of the usual blank, a tiny flame.

Ines lowered the page. Her eyes were burning. She had told herself on the river that she wanted one thing: to find them, alive or dead, and end the not-knowing. She had imagined both endings, rehearsed them, armored herself against the worst one. She had not imagined this third ending, the one where the answer was not a grave and not a rescue but a door standing open, with the people she loved on the far side of it, waving her in.

"They're alive," Tomas said. He said it the way you test ice — one foot, gently, ready to pull back. "They're alive and they *stayed.*"

"They stayed." Ines heard her own voice crack on the second word and let it. Grief and relief arrived together, tangled so tightly she couldn't tell which was which, and she suspected they would stay tangled for a long time. Eleven days of dread, and the dread had been wrong — but it had also been the only honest thing available, and she honored it by letting herself shake for a moment, hat-page-stone in her arms, before she folded the journal page with mapmaker's precision and put it inside her coat, against her chest.

Edmund had moved to the camp's edge and stood looking up-slope, toward the headwall, where the trees gave way to gray stone and, above that, a notch of pass half-hidden in cloud. The captain's face was doing something Ines hadn't seen it do before — not the river-hard watchfulness, not the wry economy, but something older and more unsettled, as if the valley in the journal page had reached down the mountain and touched a nerve she'd thought was dead.

"You've heard of it," Ines said. Not a question.

"I've heard stories." Edmund's voice was flat, controlled. "My father told them. I thought they were stories." She turned away from the headwall as if it had been staring back. "We should decide before noon. Light's good now. It won't be tomorrow."

Vane cleared his throat from beside the fire ring, where he had been examining the doused ashes with the air of a man appraising merchandise. "I'd counsel caution," he said pleasantly. "A valley that isn't on the maps, that 'remembers people' — forgive me, but that reads like a journal entry written by a man who's been in the mountains too long. Sun, altitude, thin air. It happens to the best expeditions." He smiled his bank-manager smile. "The prudent course is to document the camp, note the direction of travel, and return. Let a properly equipped party—"

"The properly equipped party is standing in it," Ines said.

Vane's smile didn't move, but something behind it did, a shutter closing. "Of course. I only mean to say that Mr. Halvorsen's own letter urges caution. *Do not follow unless—*"

"He also said *bring paper,*" Tomas said, and there was steel in the boy's voice that hadn't been there on the dock, that had been forged somewhere between the drowned village and this ash. "He asked for us. That's not caution. That's an invitation."

Ines looked around the camp once more — the six bedrolls, the raked fire, the hat with its old burn scar, folded and waiting like a letter left on a table. Eleven days she had mourned these people. Now she would walk toward them, and the mourning would turn into something else, something with weight and purpose, and she would carry the journal page against her heart the whole way.

She took out her notebook and drew the camp as it lay: the outcrop, the lean-to, the bearing to the headwall notch, the elevation estimated by barometer. Every mark a promise. Then, beneath the sketch, she wrote the words that had been gathering in her since the trading post, since the knotwork, since the hat:

*Not vanished. Unwilling to leave.*

Sefu was already saddling the mules.
