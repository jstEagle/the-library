## 11. Vane's Ledger

Tomas had learned to count things on this journey. Not coins — his uncle had taught him that kind of counting and it had never stuck — but other things. The number of times Ines checked her star tables before she trusted them (twice, always twice). The number of knots Sefu tied differently from everyone else (four). The number of questions Mr. Vane asked that sounded like politeness and landed like hooks.

That last number was the one that kept growing.

Tomas counted because counting was how he made himself useful. He was fourteen, he had stowed away on a riverboat to find an uncle who might be dead, and he could not pilot a boat or read stars or argue with mule drivers in three languages. But he could watch. He could remember. He could keep a ledger in his head the way his mother kept one in her kitchen — debts and credits, who owed what to whom, and when the bill would come due.

By the fourth night above the last landing, the ledger said: *Mr. Vane owes us an explanation.*

* * *

They had made camp in a fold of hillside where a stream cut down through the scrub toward the river they'd left behind. The mules stood hipshot in the shadows, chewing. Sefu had built the fire small and screened it with stones, the way he did every night now, as though the mountains themselves were watching and might report back to someone. Edmund took first watch without being asked. Ines spread her maps on a flat rock and weighted the corners with pebbles, and by firelight her face went sharp and hungry, a woman reading a letter from someone she loved.

Tomas lay on his bedroll near the edge of the light, not sleeping, doing what he always did at night: turning over the day, sorting it into columns.

The day's column for Vane held three entries.

First: at midday, while the rest of them ate, Vane had walked to the crest of the ridge "to stretch his legs," and stayed there forty minutes. Forty minutes was a long time to stretch legs. Tomas knew because he'd timed it against the mule bells, which rang roughly once a minute when the wind came up the valley.

Second: Vane's leather case, the flat one he slept with under his head like a pillow, had been moved. It used to sit on the left side of his bedroll. Tonight it sat on the right, nearer the slope, nearer the dark. A man who expected to run would put it where his hand could reach it going downhill.

Third — and this was the entry that had finally pulled Tomas out of his bedroll and sent him padding barefoot through the cold grass — Vane was not asleep. Vane was gone.

Tomas found him a hundred yards below camp, in a hollow where the stream ran loud enough to cover voices, crouched behind a thornbush with something in his hands that threw a small green light up onto his face.

A radio.

Not the survey office's clattering box that Edmund kept wrapped in oilcloth and cursed at daily. This was smaller, sleeker, its casing scuffed but expensive, the kind of instrument that cost more than the *Mariana*'s engine. Vane had the earpiece pressed tight and was speaking in a low, even voice, the way men spoke when they were reading numbers off a page.

"—grid reference as marked, elevation twelve hundred, moving northeast along the burn line. Four travelers plus guide, six mules. No, they haven't mentioned the route south. Yes. I understand. I'll keep you informed at each—"

Tomas stepped on a stone. It clicked.

Vane's hand closed over the set like a man smothering a candle, and the green light died, and for a moment there was only the sound of the stream and Tomas's own heart banging against his ribs like a trapped bird.

Then Vane turned, unhurried, and looked at him.

"Couldn't sleep either?" he said.

* * *

Later — much later, after everything — Tomas would try to reconstruct why he didn't run. Partly it was stubbornness; he had crossed half a country on the strength of stubbornness and wasn't going to abandon it now. Partly it was that running would mean waking Edmund, and some animal instinct told him that whatever happened next needed to happen between him and Vane first, quietly, the way you checked a knot before trusting it with weight.

Mostly, though, it was the hat. His uncle's hat, the one he'd found in the abandoned camp two days ago, still sitting in the bottom of his pack with its brim cracked and its sweatband stiff with old salt. Daniel Halvorsen had worn that hat across three countries. If Vane was selling their position to whoever had made Daniel disappear, then the ledger had just changed currencies entirely, and Tomas was done being the boy who watched.

"What are you doing?" he asked.

Vane rose slowly, brushing dirt from his trousers. In the starlight his face was all planes and patience. "Getting air."

"With a radio."

"A man is entitled to correspondence."

"At midnight. Behind a bush. In code." Tomas's voice came out steadier than he felt. "Who were you talking to?"

For a long moment Vane said nothing. Then he did something worse than lying: he sighed, folded the radio into his jacket with the care of a priest putting away relics, and sat down on a boulder as if settling in for a reasonable discussion between reasonable men.

"You're the nephew," he said. "Daniel Halvorsen's sister's boy. Tomas, isn't it?"

"Don't say his name like you know him."

"I've read his file." Vane said it simply, without apology, the way you'd admit to having read a weather report. "I've read everyone's file. It's my job. Sit down, Tomas. You're shivering, and this will go better if your teeth aren't chattering."

"I'm not sitting down."

"Suit yourself." Vane laced his fingers over one knee. "You want to know who I was speaking to. I'll tell you, since you'll only make up something worse otherwise. I was speaking to my employer's office in the river city. My employer financed the Halvorsen expedition — every crate, every mule, every mile of it. Do you know what that costs? More than your uncle will earn in twenty years of drawing maps. And eleven days ago the expedition stopped reporting. So my employer did what any sensible investor does. He sent someone to find out where his money has gone." He tilted his head toward the dark hills. "That's me. That's all this is. I'm not a spy, Tomas. I'm an accountant with good boots."

"You're reporting our position."

"I'm reporting *progress*. There's a difference."

"To whom?"

"To the man whose money is keeping all of us fed and shod." Vane's voice stayed level, almost gentle, and that gentleness frightened Tomas more than anger would have. "Look at me. Am I armed? Have I hurt anyone? Have I once asked you to turn back? All I've done is walk where you walk and ask questions you're too young to find rude. And yes, I note where we are, because somewhere beyond those ridges is a missing expedition and a great deal of invested capital, and when we find them, my employer will want to know exactly where his property is standing."

"Daniel isn't property."

"No?" Something flickered across Vane's face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one. "Everything is property, boy. Boats are. Rivers are. Rivers especially. Your captain's whole vessel is mortgaged down to the rivets; I'd wager she could recite the terms in her sleep. The difference between her and me is that I'm honest about the ledger."

Tomas's hands had curled into fists without his permission. "You don't know anything about her."

"I know her debt paper sits in a drawer in the river city with her name on it. I know what happens to boats whose captains miss payments." Vane stood, unhurried, dusting his knees. "Here's what will happen now, since you've caught me. You can wake Captain Joubert and we'll have an ugly scene in the dark, and tomorrow we'll all trudge on together anyway, because none of us can cross these passes alone. Or you can keep this between us until morning, and I'll explain myself to the adults like a grown man, with facts instead of a child's shouting." He paused at the edge of the hollow, a tall shape against the stars. "But hear me clearly, Tomas, because this is the only part that matters. I am not your enemy. I am not anyone's enemy. I protect investments. And right now, the most valuable investment on this mountain is the truth about where Daniel Halvorsen has gone — and I intend to see it delivered. To my employer. Intact. Controlled. That word again, I know. But think about what it means. Findings that are controlled get published. Findings that are fought over get buried. Which future do you want for your uncle's work?"

He walked back up toward camp, leaving Tomas alone with the stream and the cold and the strange, sick feeling of having been argued with rather than caught — as if the trap had been sprung so smoothly he couldn't even see the bars.

* * *

He woke Edmund anyway, of course. Some things you didn't negotiate.

She listened without interrupting, which was the most frightening thing about her. She sat on her bedroll with the compass in her lap — she held it more and more lately, turning it over like a question — and when Tomas finished she was quiet for so long that Ines stirred and sat up, and then Sefu, silent as ever, materialized at the edge of the firelight with his rifle already slung, though no one had called for it.

"A radio," Edmund said at last. Her voice was flat as river ice. "All this time."

"He says he's protecting an investment," Tomas said. "He says his employer paid for the expedition."

"He told me he was an agent for a trading concern." Ines had come to stand beside Edmund, arms crossed against the cold, her jaw tight. "In the city. He showed me papers."

"Papers can show anything," Sefu said. It was the longest sentence Tomas had heard from him in two days.

They brought Vane to the fire. He came willingly, almost cheerfully, and stood with his hands visible and his voice pitched low so as not to spook the mules, and he told them nearly the same story he'd told Tomas, only longer, padded with figures. The expedition's budget. The salaries. The insurance. The clause — and here he produced a folded document from inside his jacket, holding it out to Edmund between two fingers — giving the financier's representative authority to locate the party and secure its findings in the event of communication failure.

Edmund didn't take the paper. She looked at it the way she looked at a snag floating toward her hull.

"Secure its findings," she repeated.

"Preserve them. Protect them. Words are words, Captain."

"They are. That's why I choose mine carefully." She finally lifted the document, read it top to bottom, and handed it back. "This gives you authority over paper. It doesn't give you authority over people. Daniel Halvorsen signed a contract with your employer. He didn't sign away his sister's boy, or my boat, or Señora Arrieta's right to know whether her colleagues are alive."

"The contract is precisely what makes me legitimate—"

"Legitimate." Edmund laughed, short and hard, with no humor in it anywhere. "You've been transmitting our position for days. To a man I've never met, for purposes I can't verify, while we walk blind through country that could kill us six ways before breakfast. On my boat that's called scuttling. On this mountain I expect it's called something worse."

"And what would you do, Captain?" For the first time, something hard showed through Vane's polish, a glint of ore under river mud. "Turn me out? We're four days' walk from the last mission station, the pass closes soon, and you need every pair of hands. Shoot me? You'd be marooned with a corpse and a clear conscience and no idea what waits beyond the next ridge. No. Whatever you decide tonight, you'll decide it knowing that I am the only one among us who knows what my employer will do when this is over — and believe me, Captain, you very much want to know that."

The fire popped. Somewhere upslope a mule stamped and blew.

It was Ines who broke the silence, and she did it not with anger but with terrible calm, taking the map off the flat rock and folding it away, corner by corner, out of Vane's sight.

"We continue," she said. "Because you're right — we can't spare you, and you can't survive without us, and neither fact makes you trustworthy. But from tonight you walk where we can see you. You eat what we hand you. And you touch nothing of ours — no maps, no notes, no instruments. When we find them, Daniel decides what your employer learns. Not you. Not your drawer full of paper." She looked up, and her eyes in the firelight were dry and bright and absolutely certain. "You wanted control, Mr. Vane. Congratulations. Now you have mine."

Vane considered her for a long moment. Then, to Tomas's astonishment, he inclined his head — a small, precise bow, the bow of a man accepting terms he intended to honor until the hour it profited him not to.

"Fairly done," he said. "Captain?"

Edmund was looking at the fire. When she spoke, her voice had dropped into something quieter and colder than anger, and Tomas would carry the sound of it a long way up the mountain.

"We continue," she said. "And the first time I catch that radio out of your pocket, I drop it in the deepest hole we pass, and you can explain the loss to your employer however you like."

* * *

The camp settled again by degrees — bedrolls shifted, watches reassigned, Sefu drifting back into the dark on his rounds. But Tomas did not sleep. He lay with his uncle's hat beside his head and stared up at the wheeling stars and added the final entries to his mental ledger.

Under *debts*: one financier's agent, owed nothing, trusted less.

Under *credits*: one crew, tested by firelight and found holding.

And under the column he hadn't had a name for until tonight — the column where his mother had written *family*, in her round kitchen handwriting, next to sums that never balanced — he wrote four names, and then, after a long time, a fifth.

He was still writing it when the eastern sky went grey, and Sefu touched his shoulder and pointed north along the burn line, to where the morning wind carried a smell that did not belong to the mountain.

Smoke. Woodsmoke, thin and far and human — rising, Sefu said quietly, from the direction of no map, no trail, and no village that any of them had ever seen.
