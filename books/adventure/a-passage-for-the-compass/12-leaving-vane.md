## 12. Leaving Vane

The mission station appeared out of the morning haze like something remembered rather than seen — a whitewashed chapel with a rust-streaked roof, a bell tower leaning the way old men lean, and behind it a scatter of low buildings half-swallowed by bougainvillea gone feral. Edmund brought the boat alongside the mud landing with the tenderness she reserved for landings that mattered, and killed the engine, and for a long moment nobody moved.

The river here ran slow and brown and patient, as if it too were waiting to see what they would do.

Vane stood at the rail in his ruined good coat, hands folded behind his back like a man admiring the view. He had not slept. Everyone knew it; nobody said it. Since Tomas had caught him at the radio in the grey hour before dawn — since the confrontation on the deck, since his careful confession about investments and employers and *controlling findings* — he had become a thing they carried rather than a person they traveled with. He ate when they ate. He answered when spoken to. Otherwise he was cargo, and cargo of an unpleasant kind.

Edmund looked at him and felt the strangest sensation: recognition. Not of the man himself but of the shape of him. The shape of an obligation you keep feeding because you're afraid of what happens when you stop.

"We'll put ashore here," she said, to all of them, though her eyes stayed on Vane. "Take on water. The sisters keep goats and a decent well."

"The mission is four days off our line," Vane said mildly. "If we're still pretending there's a line."

"There's a line," Sefu said from the bow, not turning around. It was the first thing he'd said all morning.

* * *

The sisters came down to the landing two abreast, small brown women in faded blue habits, moving with the unhurried authority of people who had fed every kind of desperate traveler the river could produce. The elder one — Sister Marta, by the younger's murmured address — took one look at their faces and one look at Vane, and said nothing at all, which told Edmund everything about how many men in ruined coats this landing had seen.

They gave them eggs and cassava bread and bitter coffee in tin cups, and the crew sat in the shade of the chapel wall while Tomas told the sisters an embroidered version of their journey that made Ines snort into her cup twice. Edmund let them have it. She watched Vane instead.

He sat slightly apart, as he had since the night of the radio, eating neatly, thanking Sister Marta precisely, being — and this was the devil of it — *reasonable*. That was Vane's whole genius, Edmund thought. He was never a villain in any scene he stood in. He was always the sensible one, the one pointing out costs, the one who could explain, in a voice like a ledger being balanced, why the terrible thing was actually the prudent thing. She had met a hundred men like him on this river. She had been, in her worst years, half in love with a few of them, because certainty is seductive when your own life feels like a debt you can't service.

"Captain." He had come to stand beside her at the landing while the others filled jerry cans at the well. His voice was pitched for her alone. "A word, professionally."

"Professionally," she said.

"You're going to leave me here." Not a question. He'd read it in the way she'd tied off the lines, probably; in the way she'd counted the food stores last night with Ines whispering figures. Vane missed nothing except, apparently, the point of anything.

"I'm considering my options," Edmund said.

"Let me save us both the theater." He turned so he faced the river, matching her posture, two professionals watching the same brown water. "You think I'm the danger. I understand. But consider: whatever my employer wants with that valley, he will get it. Men like Halvorsen don't fund expeditions out of curiosity, and men like me don't get sent along as ballast. If you strand me here, you buy yourself perhaps ten days. Then someone else comes upriver asking questions, and they won't be polite about it, and you'll have burned your only bridge to the one person who might have softened what happens next."

"And what happens next?"

"That depends entirely on what you bring back." He smiled, thin and correct. "Bring back nothing, and there's nothing to control. Bring back everything, and you make yourself valuable. Valuable people are protected, Captain. I've spent my life making myself valuable. It works."

Edmund looked at him for a long moment. Then she said, quietly, "When Tomas caught you on the radio, you didn't deny it. You explained it. You gave us the whole architecture of it — the investment, the employer, the need to manage findings. Do you know why I believe you?"

"Because I'm honest."

"Because a liar would have lied better." She set down her cup. "You wanted us to know. You wanted us afraid of the same thing you're afraid of. And I've been sitting here all morning trying to work out what that fear is, and I think I finally have it." She turned to face him fully. "You're not afraid of what your employer does to us. You're afraid of what he does to *you*, if you come back having failed. That's why you're still explaining instead of threatening. A man with real power doesn't plead on a mission landing."

Something flickered behind Vane's eyes — quick, quickly mastered, but real. For one unguarded second he looked less like a financier's agent and more like what he probably was underneath: a clever boy from nowhere who had learned that value was the only shelter the world offered, and who had never once been allowed to stop paying for it.

Edmund knew that boy. She had been that boy, on a different river, under a different name of obligation.

It didn't change what she had to do. But it changed how she could do it, and she found she was grateful for that, in a grim and private way.

* * *

She called the crew together in the chapel's side room, where the sisters stored candles and a broken organ and forty years of dust. She laid it out plainly: the route south that Vane kept insisting on, the longer pass, the one he'd argued for since the mule station. She drew it in candle soot on the whitewash — the southern line, wide and safe and slow, passing within signaling distance of three trading posts and a telegraph relay.

Then she drew the northern route. Shorter. Harder. No telegraph. No posts. Nothing between them and the high country but trail.

"He wants us south," she said. "So we go north."

Ines studied the soot map with her head tilted, cartographer's eyes measuring distances her feet hadn't walked yet. "He'll figure it out. He's not stupid."

"No. But he'll figure it out late, and from a mission station with no telegraph, which buys us the difference." Edmund wiped her hand on her trousers, leaving a grey smear. "We leave him supplies. Real ones. Food, medicine, money for passage downriver on the next mission launch. We tell the sisters he's ill — fever, needs rest, shouldn't travel. Sisters don't lie, but they don't ask either, and they'll keep him comfortable and keep their mouths shut out of habit. By the time he can walk to a post and hire a boat, we're over the first ridge."

"And after that?" Tomas asked. He was sitting on the organ bench, knees drawn up, and there was something new in his face these last two days — a hardness setting like cement, the specific hardness of a boy who has caught an adult lying and will never fully unlearn it. "After that he comes after us anyway. With men."

"After that," Edmund said, "we're a problem for the version of us that exists then. I've run rivers my whole life on that principle. You deal with the rapid in front of you. The one below the bend takes care of itself or it doesn't, but you drown just as dead worrying about it early."

Sefu, from the doorway, said: "The northern trail is bad this season."

"It's worse later," Edmund said. "That's the whole argument."

The guide considered this with the gravity he gave everything, then nodded once, which from Sefu was a speech.

Ines lingered after the others filed out. She stood beside the soot map, looking at the two routes, and said, without preamble: "You're doing the arithmetic wrong."

Edmund raised an eyebrow.

"Not the route arithmetic. The other kind." Ines touched the southern line, the safe one, the one Vane wanted. "You're counting what leaving him here costs *him*. Four days, maybe ten. But you should count what keeping him costs *us*. Every mile we carry a man we can't trust, we split the watch, we soften the truth, we spend half our strength guarding against our own company. Out there—" she nodded toward the mountains, invisible past the chapel wall, "—that half-strength is the difference between everyone coming home and not. Leaving him isn't cruelty, Edmund. It's the first honest navigation decision you've made since he boarded."

Edmund looked at her. "You've thought about this."

"I've been thinking about it since the radio." Ines's mouth curved, dry. "I'm a mapper. I notice when someone's position doesn't match their reported course."

* * *

They did it at dusk, because dusk forgave things.

Sister Marta helped without being asked to help — produced a pallet in the infirmary room, boiled water, set out a clay pot of willow-bark tea with the brisk competence of a woman who had manufactured a great many plausible illnesses in her time. If she understood exactly what was happening, and Edmund was certain she did, she gave no sign beyond a single look delivered over Vane's head as they settled him: a look that said *the river brings me every kind of man, and God sorts them, and I only change the dressings.*

"You're making a mistake," Vane said, propped on the pallet, watching them arrange his supplies with the meticulousness of people salving their consciences. Rice and dried fish for two weeks. A coil of good rope. The medical kit's spare quinine. Coins wrapped in oilcloth and tucked into the ration tin. "Not the leaving. The manner of it. Kindness makes it worse, Captain. Now you owe yourself a story about being decent, and stories are heavy cargo."

"Everything's heavy cargo," Edmund said. "I've never once carried a light load up this river. You learn to choose what's worth the weight."

"And what am I worth?"

"Nothing," she said, and watched him flinch, and made herself finish it, because flinching wasn't the point and mercy that stopped at the flinch was just cruelty wearing gloves. "To this journey, you're worth nothing now. But you're a man who'll walk out of here, and find a boat, and go back downriver, and sit across a desk from whoever owns you, and you'll have to decide what to tell them. And I think — I think there's a chance, a small one, that a man who's been left alive and fed and given rope and coins by the people he betrayed might remember what that felt like. So the kindness isn't for you, Mr. Vane. It's a message. Deliver it however you like."

For a moment he said nothing. The bell tower creaked in the evening wind. Somewhere a goat complained about the state of the world.

"You sound like him," Vane said finally, and his voice had lost its polish entirely. "Halvorsen. The father. I sat in his study once, taking instructions, and he talked about obligations the way you talk. Like they were weather. Like you could stand in them." He laughed, short and bitter. "Do you know what he financed? Your boat. Your precious boat. I've read the papers, Captain. I know more about your debt than you do. Ask yourself sometime why a man like that lends money to a river rat with no collateral and no history. Ask yourself what he was buying."

"I have asked myself," Edmund said. "Every day for nine years."

"And?"

"And I'm starting to suspect the answer is worse than the question." She picked up her lamp. "Rest well, Mr. Vane. The sisters make a broth that could raise the dead. You'll want your strength for the story you tell."

At the door she paused. She didn't turn around. "For what it's worth — I hope your employer is everything you believe he is. Because if he's not, if he's just another man buying silence, then you've sold the only clean years you had left for nothing. And I've lived that arithmetic, and I wouldn't wish it on anyone. Even you."

She went out. Behind her, in the little infirmary smelling of carbolic and bougainvillea, Vane said nothing at all, which was the loudest thing he'd said in days.

* * *

They cast off under the first stars, running dark, no lamp lit, Sefu reading the channel by the smell of the banks and the sound of the current against the hull. The mission shrank astern — the leaning tower, the one lit window of the infirmary, a smudge of white in the blue dark — and then the river bent and took it.

Edmund stood at the wheel and felt the strange lightness and the strange grief of it both at once, the way you feel when you set down a pack you've carried so long your spine has grown around it. Nine years she had defined herself by the debt. Every hard choice, every empty season, every night alone on the sandbar — all of it had been *because of the debt*, and the debt had been a fixed star, and she had steered by it without ever once asking whether it was a star or a hook.

Tonight, stranding a man on a mission step with rice and quinine and a lie told gently, she understood something that rearranged her from the inside: some obligations aren't debts at all. They're traps with your own name engraved on the door. The debt had kept her on this river, yes — but it had also kept her from ever choosing it. There was a difference between a mooring and an anchor chain, and she had spent nine years calling the chain a mooring because the alternative was admitting she'd been held.

"Captain." Tomas at her elbow, silent-footed as ever, a boy-shaped shadow with the compass case in his hands. He'd taken to carrying it for her on night runs, an arrangement neither of them had ever discussed and both of them honored. "Course?"

She looked at the dark water, at the black shoulder of the hills rising ahead, at the stars enough visible through the cloud to steer a first leg by. North. Toward the pass, toward the valley the maps omitted, toward a man named Halvorsen who had bought her boat and buried her past in the same transaction.

"North-northeast," she said. "Hold her there."

And far behind them, faint as a memory, the mission bell began to ring — vespers, probably, or maybe just Sister Marta telling the river what she thought of all of them — and Edmund Joubert drove her boat into the dark with a lighter hold on the wheel than she had known in nine years, and heavier thoughts than she had ever carried, toward mountains that were already, even now, gathering their snows.
