## 13. The Ascent Begins

The river gave them up grudgingly.

Ines felt it first as a change in the boat's voice — the deep-throated churn of the paddle wheel going shallow and uncertain, the hull scraping something that was not quite rock and not quite sand, a sound like a man clearing his throat before saying something unwelcome. She had been sketching at the rail, her pencil catching the last of the morning light on the water, when Edmund's hand came down on the throttle and the whole vessel seemed to hold its breath.

"That's it," Edmund said, not turning from the wheel. "That's the end of my river."

Ines looked ahead. The channel narrowed between banks of pale stone, and beyond the last navigable bend she could see the water braiding itself into threads too thin for any keel — a river dissolving into its own beginnings. Above it all, faint as a watermark on old paper, stood the mountains. They had been visible for two days now, a blue suggestion on the horizon, but this morning they had resolved into something actual: white-shouldered, immense, indifferent.

She had spent her adult life making maps, and there was a particular humility in standing before terrain that no map had ever done justice to. The survey sheets in her satchel showed contour lines like fingerprints, neat and confident, and Ines knew — had always known, in the way cartographers know — that those lines were lies told politely. The mountains would not be contained by them.

* * *

They unloaded the boat through the afternoon, and Ines learned what Sefu already knew: that everything they would carry for the next hundred miles had to be chosen with a cruelty that felt like amputation.

The mules arrived at dusk, six of them, led down a goat track by a man who moved as though gravity were a rumor he'd declined to believe. Sefu was not tall, and he was not talkative, and he assessed their expedition with a single slow sweep of his eyes that made Ines feel inventoried — weighed, priced, and found provisionally acceptable. He wore a wool cap despite the heat and carried a staff polished dark by decades of hands.

"You are going to the high pass," he said. It was not a question.

"The valley," Edmund said. "Past the burn line."

Sefu looked at her for a long moment. "There is no past the burn line. There is the pass, and there is what the pass decides to show you." He turned to inspect their pile of gear, prodding the tins with his staff, lifting the rope coils, testing knots with fingers that found flaws Ines couldn't see. "Too much," he announced. "You will leave half."

"We can't leave half," Tomas said. He had appointed himself guardian of the supplies somewhere around the drowned village, and he stood over the pile now with his arms crossed, fifteen years old and ferociously serious. "We don't know how long we'll be up there."

Sefu regarded him without expression. "And you do not know how heavy a mountain is." He tapped a crate of canned meat. "This stays. This—" the tarpaulin, "—stays. Two tents, not three. The sugar stays; the coffee goes." He glanced at Ines. "Except her papers. Those all come. Paper is light, and paper is why you are going."

Ines felt something loosen in her chest. He had understood without being told. Not an adventure, not a rescue — a record. They were carrying the means to write things down, and that, apparently, earned respect even from a man who respected almost nothing else.

By full dark they had halved their mountain of equipment twice, and Ines sat on the boat's deck with her knees drawn up, watching Edmund stand at the rail looking north. The captain had been quiet all day in a way that was different from her usual quiet. Her usual silence was the silence of concentration, of a mind reading water. This was something heavier.

"You'll be all right here?" Ines asked. "Alone with her?"

Edmund's mouth twitched. "The *Mariana* and I have been alone together longer than I've known most people. We understand each other." She paused. "It's strange, though. Leaving her. Every trip I've ever taken, I've been the one who could turn back. Who knew where the deep water was." She nodded toward the black bulk of the mountains. "Up there, I'm just another passenger."

Ines wanted to say something comforting and found she couldn't, because the truth was that she envied it. Ten years of mapping other people's expeditions from behind a desk in the survey office, ten years of drawing coastlines she'd never walked — and now here she was, about to walk into the blank space on her own map, with no idea what she'd find or whether she'd survive finding it. The fear and the wanting were the same sensation. She had never been able to explain that to anyone who hadn't felt it.

"Get some sleep," Edmund said. "Tomorrow we start losing the river."

* * *

They lost it gradually, which somehow made it worse.

The first day out of the basin, the trail followed a tributary still loud and green, and Ines could pretend they were merely walking beside the world they knew. The second day the tributary shrank to a stream they stepped across without wetting their boots, and the air changed — thinner, drier, smelling of dust and some resinous shrub Sefu called *the mountain's soap*. By the third day there was no water sound at all except what they carried, and Ines caught herself listening for it the way you listen for a heartbeat under your own.

The landscape opened as it climbed. Forest gave way to grassland the color of old brass, rolling in long swells toward ridgelines that kept revealing further ridgelines, each one promising a view and delivering instead another horizon. The sky was enormous. Ines had lived her whole life under river skies, skies broken up by cloud and canopy and the smoke haze of towns, and this unbroken dome of blue made her feel like a figure on a map — small, exposed, precisely located.

She sketched constantly. That was her discipline and her defense: when the strangeness pressed too close, she drew it, and drawing it made it hers. She filled page after page with the braided pattern of dry washes, the architecture of termite hills taller than Tomas, the way the light came down the valleys at evening like something poured. Sefu watched her work once, briefly, and said nothing, but that night he pointed out a notch in the western ridge and told her its name in a language she didn't recognize, and waited while she wrote it down phonetically.

"The maps have nothing here," she said. "Not even names."

"The maps have nothing anywhere," Sefu said. "Maps are what people carry so they don't have to ask the mountain anything." He adjusted his cap. "The mountain does not mind being asked. It minds being told."

Tomas, walking behind them, laughed — a short, surprised bark — and Sefu's eyes crinkled very slightly, which Ines was learning to read as open delight.

* * *

On the fifth morning, Sefu stopped the column on a rise and looked south for a long time.

Ines followed his gaze. The sky behind them had changed overnight. Where the southern horizon had been clean and hard-edged for days, there was now a darkness gathering along it — not storm-cloud exactly, or not only, but a bruised, layered massiveness that seemed less like weather than like a wall being built.

"The monsoon?" she asked.

"It is late," Sefu said. "Late and fat. It has been eating the ocean for weeks and now it comes inland to be sick." He spat, ceremonially, to the side of the trail. "When it arrives in the low country, the rivers will rise. All of them. Your riverboat captain knows this better than I."

Ines did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding. The descent alone would take eight days if all went well. The return downriver, three more. If the monsoon broke early — and late monsoons, Sefu said, often broke violently, arriving like a debt collector with interest — the rivers could cut them off from the boat entirely. From the boat, from the mission stations, from everything.

And behind them, above them, the other clock: the snow.

"How long does the pass stay open?" she asked.

Sefu turned north. The high peaks were visible today with terrible clarity, cold and white and utterly silent at this distance. "The old men say the pass closes when the first big snow comes, and the first big snow used to come with the moon of the short grass." He held up a handful of the yellowed stalks at his feet. "That moon is now. Some years the mountain waits. Some years it does not." He let the grass fall from his fingers. "I have crossed in the moon of the short grass eleven times. Twice, the snow came early. Both times, I lost someone."

He said it without drama, the way he said everything, and started walking again, and Ines stood a moment longer doing the new arithmetic: four days of margin, maybe five, between the snow coming down from the north and the water rising from the south. A corridor closing from both ends. Everything they hoped to find in the valley, everything they hoped to bring back, had to fit inside that corridor.

She wrote it in the margin of her sketchbook, because writing it made it real and real things could be planned against: *Two weathers. One road.*

* * *

That night, camped in the lee of a granite shoulder with the fire banked low, Ines sat up late finishing her sketches by lamplight. Around her the others slept: Tomas curled tight as a comma, dreaming, she suspected, of the uncle he hadn't seen in years; Edmund flat on her back with her hat over her face, sleeping the way river pilots sleep, lightly, one ear always on the current; Sefu somewhere out in the dark, keeping the first watch, invisible and total.

She found herself drawing the mountains again, though she'd drawn them three times already. This time she tried to draw what they felt like rather than what they looked like, which was a cartographer's heresy and probably impossible, but she tried anyway: the mass of them, the patience, the way they made every human plan seem like a child's arrangement of pebbles on a riverbank.

Wonder and dread, she thought. Everyone spoke of them as opposites, but up here they were the same thing wearing different clothes. The same vertigo. The same sense of standing at the edge of something that did not care whether you stepped off.

A soft scrape made her look up. Edmund had lifted the brim of her hat and was watching her draw.

"What will you do," Edmund asked quietly, "if the valley isn't there? If the whole thing turns out to be smoke? Eleven days of silence, a burned camp, a story. Maybe that's all it ever was."

Ines considered the question honestly, because Edmund deserved honesty and because she'd been asking herself versions of it since the trading post. "Then I'll map the emptiness," she said. "An honest blank space is worth more than a dishonest fill-in. But it won't be smoke." She touched the pages of her sketchbook — the cache opened carefully, not looted; the trail marks read true; the knotwork Tomas had recognized with his whole body shaking. "Somebody went inland on purpose, Edmund. Somebody left signs for people who could read them. That's not smoke. That's an invitation."

Edmund was quiet a moment. "And if the invitation wasn't meant for us?"

"Then we're crashing a party." Ines smiled. "I've done worse."

Something eased in the older woman's face — not quite a smile, but the ground where a smile might grow. She settled the hat back down. "Sleep, then, cartographer. Tomorrow we climb into the blank space."

Ines snuffed the lamp and lay back, and the last thing she saw before sleep took her was the mountains against the stars, closer tonight than they had been, or seeming so — white shoulders, dark faces, keeping whatever counsel they kept.

Behind them, far to the south, the monsoon gathered like a held breath. Ahead, the pass waited with its snow.

Four days, maybe five. She fell asleep counting them, and dreamed of contour lines that moved.
