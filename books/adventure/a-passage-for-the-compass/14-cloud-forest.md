## 14. Cloud Forest

The river gave them up at dawn.

Edmund felt the parting in her hands before she saw it — the wheel going light, the current loosening its grip on the hull, the *Marianne* settling into still water like a horse brought to the end of its strength. For eleven days the river had carried them, arguing with her, testing her, and now it simply stopped, thinning into a braided shallows where the water ran clear as glass over stones the color of old bruises. Beyond the last navigable bend, the land stood up. Not hills. Land that *stood*, green to the chin and climbing into cloud.

She cut the engine and let the silence come down.

It was a silence she knew from childhood, though she could not have said how — the particular hush of a place where the air itself was heavy, where sound traveled a few feet and then simply drowned. Mist moved through the trees across the water in slow ropes, unraveling and re-forming, and somewhere up in that white blindness a bird called three notes and fell silent, as if embarrassed to have spoken.

"End of the line," Edmund said, mostly to herself.

"End of the line," Sefu agreed from the rail. He had been standing there since first light, saying nothing, which for Sefu meant the country ahead deserved attention. He nodded at the treeline. "The forest takes over from here. It will be wet every hour of every day until we are above it or dead of it."

"Cheerful," said Tomas, coming up the deck with the mule harnesses over his shoulder. He'd grown into the work these past days, his shoulders filling out, his stowaway's wariness burned off by labor. He looked at the wall of green and grinned instead of flinching, and Edmund found herself glad of it in a way she didn't examine too closely.

Ines came last, her map case slung across her back like a rifle. She stood beside Edmund at the wheelhouse and studied the mist without speaking for a long moment. Then: "The expedition's journal mentioned cloud forest. Daniel wrote that they entered it on the ninth day out from the last landing." She checked her notebook against some inner arithmetic. "If their pace matched ours, we should strike their trail within two days' walk."

"And if we don't?"

"Then we're lost in a forest that eats maps," Ines said, "and I'll draw us a new one."

They beached the *Marianne* in a cove Sefu chose — deep water close against a rock shelf, hidden from the channel by a screen of bamboo — and Edmund spent an hour making her fast the way her father had taught her, lines doubled, anchors set fore and aft, everything about the arrangement saying *I am coming back*. The boat rode the gentle swell and watched her go. She felt its eyes between her shoulder blades all afternoon.

* * *

The forest received them the way water receives a stone.

There was no trail at first, only Sefu moving ahead with a machete he used less than expected, reading the ground rather than fighting it. Underfoot the earth was black and soft as bread, giving off a smell of rot and sweetness together, and overhead the canopy closed so completely that Edmund lost track of whether it was morning or afternoon. Light came down in shafts, green-gold, thick enough almost to hold. Everything dripped. Leaves dripped, branches dripped, the air itself seemed to drip; moisture beaded on her eyebrows and ran into her eyes, and after the first hour she stopped wiping it away and let the forest have its way with her.

Tomas loved it immediately. He went along the column pointing things out — a snake banded yellow and black asleep in a fork, fungi growing in shelves like tiny roofs, an orchid growing not from soil but from bare bark, its roots naked in the air. "How does it eat?" he demanded, and Sefu answered, "Slowly," and Tomas laughed as if this were the finest joke ever told him.

Edmund walked behind them all and listened to the forest the way she listened to rivers — not for what it said but for what it failed to say. Rivers lied constantly, in currents and eddies and false channels. This place did something worse. It withheld. Every direction looked identical, green upon green, and the few times she glanced back the way they'd come, the path had already closed behind them like water closing over a dropped coin.

Near midday — she thought it was midday; her watch said so but her body disagreed — Sefu stopped.

He didn't raise a hand or call a halt. He simply stopped walking, and because the others had learned to read him, they stopped too, and the silence deepened around them until Edmund could hear her own pulse.

Ahead, half-swallowed by a tree that had grown around it the way a river grows around a boulder, stood a stone.

Not a large one — waist-high, tilted, furred with moss — but worked. Someone had shaped it, squared its edges, and carved it, and then, centuries ago by the look of the roots gripping it, someone had planted a tree beside it deliberately, so that the living wood would embrace the stone and hold it. Hold it, or hide it. Edmund couldn't decide which, and the not-deciding made her skin prickle.

Sefu approached it slowly. He knelt, took out a cloth, and began, with great gentleness, to clear the moss from the carving.

"What is it?" Tomas whispered.

"Marker," Sefu said. "Boundary stone. My grandmother's people set them — not here, far north of here, but the old trade routes carried the custom." His fingers worked. "The language will be—"

He stopped talking altogether.

The carving emerged under his cloth in shallow, deliberate lines: figures, rows of them, human shapes with arms raised, and below them a band of interlocking patterns that might have been water or might have been rope, and beneath that, larger than all the rest, a single symbol — a spiral, drawn inward, coiling toward a center that had been gouged deep, deeper than any other mark, as if the carver had wanted that one stroke to outlast everything.

Sefu sat back on his heels. He was a man who used words like coins, spending them carefully, and Edmund watched him decide whether to spend any now.

"I know pieces," he said finally. "My grandmother sang parts of this tongue. Lullabies. Nobody living speaks it whole." He touched the spiral without quite touching it. "This says *do not enter*. Or—" his brow furrowed, and he tried again, hunting the memory through layers of half-forgotten song, "*enter changed.*"

"Which?" Ines asked quietly. She already had her sketchbook out.

"That is the problem," Sefu said. "I don't think the people who carved it believed those were different things."

Ines worked while he spoke, her pencil moving in quick sure strokes, copying the figures row by row, the water-rope band, the spiral with its wounded center. She sketched the tree too, and the way its roots gripped the stone, and when she finished she sat back and looked at her own drawing for a moment with an expression Edmund had learned to recognize — the cartographer's hunger, the need to put a thing on paper so it could be understood, warring with the knowledge that some things resisted paper.

"It's a warning," Ines said. "But warnings are also records. Somebody wanted whoever came after to know this place existed, and to know it mattered." She tapped the spiral. "You don't carve *enter changed* unless entering changes you."

"Or unless you want to frighten strangers away from something worth protecting," Edmund said.

All three of them looked at her. She hadn't meant to say it aloud, and the certainty in her own voice startled her more than it startled them. It hadn't sounded like opinion. It had sounded like memory.

"The Halvorsen party passed this way," she said, steadier. "Eleven days ago, give or take. They read this stone or they didn't. Either way they kept going." She shouldered her pack. "So do we."

Sefu rose, but he paused at the marker a moment longer, and murmured something low and rhythmic under his breath — a fragment of lullaby, Edmund guessed, given back to the stone like a toll paid. Then he turned and led on, and the forest folded shut behind them.

* * *

They camped that night on a shelf of ground above a stream whose sound never varied, a constant silver whisper in the dark. No fire — Sefu forbade it, the wet wood being argument enough — so they ate cold food and hung their hammocks and lay in the dripping dark listening to the forest breathe.

Edmund slept, and dreamed.

She was small. Six, perhaps seven, and she was on the river, but not the river she knew as a woman — an older version of it, wider, slower, un-dammed, running brown and easy between banks heavy with trees that no longer stood. Her father was poling a flatboat, and she sat in the bow with her legs dangling through a gap in the cargo netting, watching fish the size of her arm hang motionless in the current below her feet.

Her father was singing. That was the impossible part, because in waking life she could not remember his voice at all — eleven years gone, and the voice had gone with him, worn away like lettering on a coin handled too often. But in the dream she heard it plainly, a low rolling baritone, and he was singing not a working song but something older, a melody in five uneven phrases that circled and came back on itself, coiling inward—

Like the spiral on the stone.

She knew this in the dream the way you know things in dreams, absolutely and uselessly. Her father was singing the shape of the carving. And the banks slid past, and among the trees on the shore stood figures with their arms raised — the same figures, row upon row, people greeting the boat or warning it away, and her father lifted one hand from the pole to them, casually, the way a man waves to neighbors, and they lowered their arms.

*Neighbors,* the dream said. *They were neighbors.*

Then the dream shifted, the way dreams do, without transition or permission, and she was older in it, maybe twelve, and her father was crouched on the deck of a different boat — a fine boat, new-painted, and a heavyset man with a beard was laughing with him over papers spread on a hatch cover. Halvorsen. She knew it was Halvorsen the way children know things, by atmosphere rather than evidence. The two men were not signing anything. They were *drawing* — her father sketching a line of the river with his finger in spilled flour, and Halvorsen following it, nodding, both of them intent, partners over a chart that existed nowhere but between them.

Her father said something she strained to hear, and in the dream she leaned close, and the words were:

"—the valley remembers people, Edda. That's why we keep it off the charts. Some places aren't ours to sell."

And then the dream did what dreams always do at the crucial moment: it dissolved. The river broke into light, the men into shadow, and Edmund surfaced into the wet black night of the cloud forest with her heart hammering and her hands gripping the edges of her hammock like a woman holding a rail in a storm.

She lay awake a long time.

Around her the forest dripped and whispered. Tomas slept with the boneless abandon of the young, one arm hanging down. Ines slept curled tight, efficient even unconscious. Sefu, she suspected, did not sleep at all but merely rested, the way stones rest.

*Edda.* Her father had called her Edda in the dream. No one had called her that in thirty years; she had buried the name so deep she'd half forgotten it was hers. And the valley — *we keep it off the charts* — her father and Halvorsen, partners, keeping a secret together, keeping a valley secret, and then the dam, and the debt, and the drowning, and everything after.

Memory was unreliable cargo. She'd told herself that for years, and it had been a comfort. But tonight the cargo had shifted weight. Tonight it had a shape, and the shape was a spiral, coiling inward, toward a center gouged deep.

Tomorrow they would climb higher. Tomorrow the forest would begin to thin and the pass would open ahead of them, and somewhere beyond it, a valley that remembered people held the son of the man who had financed her boat, carrying letters that might explain what her father had sung to her before she was old enough to understand.

Edmund stared up into the black canopy where no stars reached and did the only useful thing left to her. She hummed the melody from the dream, five uneven phrases, circling, coiling inward — quietly, so as not to wake the others, giving it back to the dark the way Sefu had given his lullaby to the stone.

And from somewhere upslope, faint but unmistakable, something answered.

A bird, probably. A bird calling three notes in the dark, in a rhythm that fit the melody like a key fits a lock.

Probably a bird.

Edmund lay very still, wide awake, and listened to the forest sing her father's song back to her until the grey light came.
