## 15. The Broken Bridge

Tomas heard the river before he saw it — a sound like the whole world tearing cloth, endless and enormous, rising up through the gorge ahead. By the time they reached the overlook where Sefu had stopped, his ears had adjusted to it and it no longer sounded like anything at all, just a pressure in his chest, a constant roar that made thought difficult and speech impossible.

Sefu stood at the edge of the cliff with his hands on his hips, looking down at something below them. The others gathered around him: Edmund with her arms crossed against the spray that rose from the gorge like breath from an open mouth; Ines already unshouldering her satchel of instruments; Daniel Halvorsen, who had been quiet for two days now, ever since the cairn with the letters.

Tomas pushed between them to see.

The bridge was gone. Not destroyed — *gone*, or nearly so. What remained was a single rope anchored into the far side of the gorge, its far end still knotted to a stone post on the opposite cliff, but the near anchorage had sheared away entirely, taking half the span with it. The broken end hung down into the mist, swaying, whipping itself against the rock face in long lazy arcs. Below it, maybe forty meters down, the river ran white and violent through a channel barely wider than a wagon road. Even at this distance Tomas could feel the cold coming off the water, the way you feel cold from an open icehouse door.

"Storm took it," Sefu said. He said it without drama, the way he said everything, as though reporting the color of the sky. "Three days ago, maybe four. You can see where the flood line reached." He pointed to a stain on the rock walls, a band of darker stone running along both sides of the gorge, higher than any of them would have guessed water could climb. "When this river rises, it does not rise politely."

"So we go back?" Vane's voice — but Vane was three days behind them now, stranded at the mission station with supplies and a lie about their route, and the voice belonged instead to one of the valley guides, a lean man named Oren who had joined them after the ambush at the narrows. "Back to the southern pass?"

"The southern pass is closed by now," Sefu said. "Snow came early. I told you."

"You told us the *high* pass closes with snow—"

"And the southern route crosses the same range at lower altitude, which means more rain, which means landslides, which means dead mules and dead men. No." Sefu turned and looked along the cliff face, northward, studying the rock the way Edmund studied water. "There is a traverse. A ledge. It runs along this wall for perhaps six hundred paces, then climbs to a shelf above the old bridge anchorage. From there we can cross on what remains."

"On what remains?" Ines stepped closer to the edge. She did not seem afraid of heights, exactly; she seemed interested in them, the way she was interested in everything, cataloguing. "That rope is hanging loose. There's nothing to cross *on*."

"There is a second rope. Older. Anchored into the wall itself, not the posts. My people used it before the bridge was built." Sefu shrugged. "It has held for sixty years. It will hold for one more afternoon."

*It has held for sixty years.* Tomas turned the sentence over and found no comfort in it anywhere. Sixty years meant it had never once been tested by anyone who mattered to him. Sixty years meant it was older than his uncle, older than Edmund, older than the debt that had eaten her life. Things that old were things that failed while everyone stood around saying *but it held for sixty years.*

Edmund hadn't spoken. She was looking at the ledge, following it with her eyes the way she'd once followed currents, reading the rock for snags and deadfalls the way she read the river for sandbars. Finally she said, "How wide?"

"Half a meter in places. Narrower in one place. There is a corner where you must press your back to the wall and step around a bulge, and there is nothing under your feet for two steps except air."

"Wonderful," Edmund said, and there was something in her voice that wasn't fear at all — something almost like appetite. Tomas had noticed this about her before, on the boat, in the night currents when the sandbar had come out of the dark like a whale's back. Danger didn't shrink her. It sorted her, somehow, into her truest shape.

"We go around the bulge one at a time," Sefu continued. "Rope line from the first anchor to the last. Hands on the guide rope always. If someone falls—" he paused, and the pause was worse than anything he'd actually said, "—the person behind holds the line. That is all anyone can do. Hold the line."

Tomas looked at the ledge again. Half a meter. Two steps of air. The river roaring underneath like some enormous animal that had been waiting sixty years for exactly this.

His hands were shaking. He put them in his pockets before anyone could see.

* * *

They rigged the line while the light was still good. Sefu worked with a speed and economy that made it look easy, which Tomas knew was a lie, because he'd spent enough evenings watching Edmund splice and knot to know that nothing done well ever looked as hard as it was. The anchors went in — iron eyes driven into cracks that Sefu chose by tapping the rock and listening, the way a doctor taps a chest. The guide rope ran taut and humming between them, a dark thread stitched across the face of the gorge.

Ines went first, because she insisted, because she wanted to see the geology of the wall up close, because she was Ines. She moved along the ledge with her left hand on the rope and her right hand trailing along the stone, reading it like a page. When she reached the corner — the place with the two steps of air — she stopped, pressed her back flat against the rock, and stepped around it with a grace that made it look like a dance figure. Then she was beyond it, waving, small against the vastness of the cliff.

Daniel went next. Then Oren and the other guides, moving quickly, easily, men who had grown up on rock the way Tomas had grown up on decks. Then Edmund, who paused at the edge only long enough to check Tomas's harness buckle with two sharp tugs, say "You're second-to-last, behind me, and you'll do fine," and step out onto the ledge as if stepping onto her own deck.

Which left Sefu and Tomas.

"Your turn," Sefu said.

Tomas's mouth had gone dry. He could hear his own breathing, too fast, too shallow. The gorge roared. Somewhere below, the broken end of the old bridge rope lashed against the stone, keeping time.

"I'm fine," he said. "I just — give me a minute."

Sefu gave him a minute. He was good at that, at silence that didn't press. He simply stood there, holding the rope, watching the sky the way he always watched the sky, as if it were a ledger he was auditing.

"It's not the height," Tomas said finally, because saying it seemed better than carrying it. "I'm not scared of falling. I'm scared of freezing. Of getting halfway and my legs just — stopping. Like they don't belong to me anymore."

"That happens," Sefu said.

"It does?"

"To everyone. To me, twice." Sefu tapped his own chest, over the heart. "The body knows when it is somewhere it can die. It votes to stop. That is not cowardice. That is information." He held out the rope. "The trick is not to argue with the fear. The trick is to make each step smaller than the fear. One handhold. Then another. The fear cannot hold all of you at once if you only ever give it one piece."

Tomas took the rope. The fiber was rough and damp and real, and holding it helped, a little.

He stepped onto the ledge.

* * *

For the first hundred paces it was almost bearable. The ledge was wider than it had looked from above — a full arm-span in most places — and the wall rose sheer on his left, close enough to touch, warm from the afternoon sun. He kept his left hand on the guide rope and his right palm flat against the stone and he moved the way Sefu had told him: one handhold, then another, small steps, small pieces.

Below him the river thundered. He tried not to look at it. He looked anyway, once, and immediately regretted it: the water was white all the way across, churning and folding over itself, and a whole tree — a whole *tree*, trunk and branches — went past beneath him, tumbling, swallowed, gone. His stomach dropped. His knees went soft.

Don't look down. Everyone says don't look down, and nobody tells you that the not-looking takes work, that it's a labor all its own, that your eyes keep trying to drag you over the edge like the river itself reaching up.

Ahead of him, Edmund had stopped at the corner. She was waiting, he realized. Waiting for him.

"Talk to me," she called back, not turning around. Her voice carried strangely in the gorge, flattened by the roar. "Tell me something. Anything. What's the first thing you remember?"

"What?"

"You heard me. First thing. Go."

Tomas thought. His feet kept moving because his mind was busy elsewhere, and later he would understand that this was the entire point. "A deck," he said. "My uncle's deck. I was very small. Someone was singing — my mother, I think. And there was tar smell. Tar and oranges. We had oranges aboard, and the tar from the seams, and the two smells together."

"Good. Keep going. What did the song sound like?"

"I don't remember the words. Just that it went up at the end. Every verse, it went up, like a question."

"Like asking the river something," Edmund said. "Keep talking."

The ledge narrowed. He felt it before he saw it, the wall pressing closer, the drop opening wider on his right until his shoulder brushed stone on one side and nothing at all waited on the other. His breath went short. The white noise of the river filled his skull.

Then the corner.

It was worse than Sefu had described it, because Sefu described things plainly and fear described them elaborately. The bulge of rock jutted out over the void like the prow of a ship, and the ledge simply ended, and began again two steps beyond, and between those two steps there was nothing — no stone, no rope low enough to reach, only air and the distant, indifferent white of the river.

Edmund had already come around. She stood on the far side, braced against the wall, watching him.

"Tomas." Her voice cut clean through the roar. "Look at me. Not down. Me."

He looked at her. Her hair was pulled back and the wind off the gorge was pulling strands loose, and there was spray on her jacket, and her eyes were steady in a way that steadied him, the way a fixed star steadies a navigator.

"Now listen. Your left hand stays on the rope. Your chest goes against the rock. You turn your face to the wall — cheek against stone, like you're listening for something inside the mountain. You take one step. Your foot finds the nub — there's a nub, Sefu showed me, about shin height, you'll feel it with your toe before you trust it. Weight on it. Then the second step. Same thing. That's all there is. Two steps. I'll be right here, and I've got the rope, and if anything happens — anything — I've got you. Do you hear me? I have got you."

"My legs won't—"

"They will. They're yours. Tell them whose they are." Something flickered across her face then, quick as a fish under the surface — something old, something that had to do with debts and compasses and fathers — and was gone. "One step, Tomas. Give the fear one piece at a time."

He pressed his cheek to the stone. The rock was gritty and sun-warm and smelled of dust and lichen, and it was the most solid thing he had ever touched. Through it, faintly, he could feel the vibration of the river, the mountain's own pulse.

Left hand on the rope. Chest against the wall.

Step.

His toe found the nub. It was exactly where Sefu had said, worn smooth by decades of other frightened feet, and it held. For one full second Tomas stood with one foot on a knob of stone the size of a fist and nothing beneath the rest of him but forty meters of roaring air, and the world did not end, and the fear did not swallow him, because the fear had only been given one piece and one piece was survivable.

Step.

His second foot came down on the ledge beyond the bulge, and his knee buckled, and Edmund's hand shot out and caught his harness strap and held him until his legs remembered their business. Then she let go, deliberately, so that he knew he was standing on his own.

"There," she said. Not triumphant. Just factual, the way she said *sandbar, port side* or *we make the narrows by dusk*. "There you are."

Tomas discovered he was laughing. It came out of him ragged and shaky, half sob, but it was laughter. "I did it," he said stupidly.

"You did it. Now do it five hundred more times, because we're not even halfway." But she was smiling as she said it, and she fell into step behind him — behind him, he noticed; she'd given him the lead — and they moved along the ledge together, one handhold and then another, small pieces, while the river roared its sixty-year hunger below and got nothing.

* * *

The shelf above the old anchorage was wide enough for all of them to sit, and they sat, passing water skins down the line, letting their arms stop trembling. The far side of the gorge was close now — thirty meters of gap spanned by the ancient rope Sefu had promised, a thick cable sagging in a shallow curve between iron rings set deep in the rock on either side. Beside it, the broken remnant of the newer bridge hung and swayed, its frayed end ticking against the cliff like a slow clock.

Crossing the cable was its own ordeal — you hooked your legs over it and shuffled across hand over hand, upside-down world, the river directly beneath your spine — but by then Tomas had learned the trick of it, the small-pieces trick, and he went across third, behind Ines, and did not freeze, and did not look down except once, deliberately, to prove to himself that he could.

On the far side, Sefu counted them twice, touching each shoulder as he named them. Eleven. All present.

They climbed away from the gorge as the light went long and gold, up through scrub pine and then into shadowed forest, until the roar of the river faded behind them and became, at last, just a sound in Tomas's memory, something that had happened to someone he used to be.

That evening, camped on a bench of ground above the tree line, Tomas found himself sitting beside Edmund at the fire. She was cleaning the compass, as she did most evenings, turning it over in her hands like something alive.

"I froze," he said. "At the corner. Before you talked me across. My legs just stopped."

"I know."

"How do you always know things?"

She was quiet a moment. The fire cracked. Above them the first stars were coming out, hard and cold, and Ines was already lying back against her pack with a notebook on her knees, shooting star fixes by lantern light, calling out faint numbers to no one.

"Because I froze once too," Edmund said. "Somewhere on this same river, years ago. And someone talked me across." She closed the compass and looked at him, firelight moving in her eyes. "That's how it works, Tomas. Nobody gets across alone. Somebody holds the line. Then someday you're the one holding it, and you talk some frozen kid across, and that's how a crew becomes a family. Hand over hand."

Tomas looked into the fire and thought about his uncle, twenty paces away, laughing at something Oren had said — really laughing, for the first time since the cairn, since the letters, since the hat. He thought about how three weeks ago he had been cargo, hidden in a lifeboat locker, a stowaway with a stolen name. He thought about the nub of stone worn smooth by other frightened feet.

"Edmund," he said. "What happens when we find them? My uncle's people. What if they don't want to be found?"

She didn't answer right away. She was looking upriver, toward the pass, toward whatever waited for them in the high snow — and her expression, in the firelight, was the expression of a woman reading water she didn't yet trust.

"Then we'll ask them why," she said at last. "And we'll listen. That's all finding is, in the end. Asking, and listening." She stood, brushing off her trousers, and nodded toward the darkening trail ahead. "Get some sleep. Sefu says the pass holds four days, maybe five. After that, the mountain decides for us."

High above the camp, where the stars met the black ridge line, Tomas thought he saw it — a flicker, brief and pale, gone before he could be sure.

Snow, falling on the summit.

Already.
