## 16. Letters from the Dead

The cairn stood where the burn line ended, at the lip of a saddle of grey stone where the forest gave up trying.

Ines saw it first — she was always seeing things first, a habit from years of reading maps before they existed, of finding the shape of a coastline in a smear of fog. It was not large. Waist-high, perhaps, built of river stones and broken slate stacked with a care that no landslide would imitate. Someone had made it deliberately, and someone had wanted it found: a pole leaned against its crown bore a strip of red cloth, faded to the color of an old wound, snapping in the thin wind that came down off the icefields.

"Mail," Sefu said, when they reached it. He said it the way other men said *snake* or *rockfall* — flatly, with respect for what the word could do. "Expeditions leave cairns like this on the way in. Letters home. If they don't come back, the letters come back instead." He looked at Edmund. "You want to open it?"

Edmund's hand rested on the topmost stone. Ines watched her captain's face do something complicated — hope and dread pulling in opposite directions like two currents meeting, the water between them going strange and smooth.

"We didn't climb this far to leave it standing," Edmund said.

They took it down stone by stone, Tomas handing each rock down the line with the solemnity of an altar boy, until the hollow at the center lay bare: a tin ammunition box, rust freckling its seams, wrapped twice in oilcloth and tied with — Ines felt her breath catch — a bowline finished with a specific doubled hitch, a knot she had seen only once before, drawn in a margin of Halvorsen's field notes. *Tomas's uncle's knotwork.* The boy saw it too. His hands went still over the oilcloth.

"That's Uncle Anders's tie-off," he said quietly. "He taught me that one. Said a knot should tell you who tied it."

"Then he's telling us he's alive," Edmund said, "or he was when he tied it."

Inside the tin: letters. Dozens of them, bundled in rubber bands gone brittle, envelopes soft with damp at their corners but dry at their hearts. Some addressed to cities Ines knew from survey office ledgers — the capital, the port, a university town with a famous geography department. One bundle bound separately, set apart, addressed in a small precise hand to *E. Joubert, care of the Rivermaster's Office, Kilbourn Landing.*

Ines read the name twice before she trusted it.

"Edmund," she said. "This one's yours."

* * *

They made camp early that day, in the lee of the saddle, because some discoveries deserve firelight and none of them pretended otherwise. Sefu took first watch without being asked; Vane sat apart on a boulder with his ledger, writing, though Ines noticed he wrote nothing for long stretches, only turned his pencil over and over, watching them across the flames.

Tomas claimed the letter bundle addressed to his own family and carried it off to the edge of the light like a man carrying water through a drought — carefully, cupped, afraid of spilling. Ines heard him open the first envelope, heard the paper whisper, and then heard nothing at all for a long time, which told her more than any sound could have.

She took the university bundle for herself. That was her right, or close enough to it; she had been hired to reconstruct this expedition from its traces, and letters were traces the way footprints were traces — better, because the feet had written them on purpose.

The handwriting varied by correspondent. A botanist named Okonkwo wrote in a rushing slant, sentences tumbling over each other, complaining cheerfully about the altitude and rhapsodizing about orchids. A geologist named Brandt wrote in block capitals, numbered his observations, and signed off each letter with the same phrase: *All well. Tell my mother the mountains are kind.* A young surveyor named Pell wrote rarely and briefly, as if words cost him money.

Anders Halvorsen — Tomas's uncle, the expedition's second-in-command — wrote often, and warmly, and with a growing strangeness that Ines felt in her chest like a change in barometric pressure.

*Day 9 above the last landing,* one began. *We have found a valley that is not on any map I have ever seen, and I have seen most of them. Daniel says we are to keep its position vague even in these letters, in case they are read by the wrong eyes. So I will say only this: the terraces go back further than the reservoir people claim anyone lived here. Further. Much further. There are walls under the moss older than anything in the capital's museum, and they were not built by people passing through. They were built by people staying.*

Another, later: *Pell swears the birds here sing songs the lowland flocks don't know. I laughed at him until I heard it myself. It's like hearing your grandmother's voice in a stranger's mouth. Daniel has started calling the valley "the archive." I think he means it literally. I think there are things written here that exist nowhere else, and I don't mean on paper.*

And another, the ink blotched as if written in rain or something warmer: *Marta, if you get this, tell Tomas I'm sorry about Christmas. Tell him the mountains are kind. Tell him I'm learning to listen.*

Ines lowered the pages. Across the fire, Tomas sat with a letter pressed flat against his knees, and even in the dark she could see his shoulders shaking, and she understood that somewhere in that trembling paper, a teenage boy was being told by an absent man that he was loved in the language of knots and mountains and postponed Christmases.

She looked away to give him his privacy, and found Edmund watching her.

"You've gone quiet," Edmund said. "Quieter."

"There's a pattern in these," Ines said. She weighed the bundle in her hand. "They all start describing a place. And then, letter by letter, they stop describing it and start defending it. Look — Okonkwo goes from listing specimens to refusing to list them. Brandt stops sending coordinates. By the last letters, half of them are asking their families to burn what they've sent." She paused. "They found something they decided the outside world couldn't be trusted with."

"And you?" Edmund asked. "Do you trust us?"

It was not a small question, dressed in four words. Ines held it a moment, turning it over the way she'd turn an unfamiliar coin, checking its weight against known currency.

"I trust the people at this fire," she said finally. "I trust you because you ran the night current rather than pay the gang twice. I trust Sefu because he warned us about the pass when lying would have been easier. I trust Tomas because he's fourteen and honest, which is the rarest combination there is." She glanced toward the boulder where Vane sat. "I trust the others conditionally. And I trust myself to know the difference between a discovery and a possession. That last part matters. Whoever finds a thing first gets to decide what it becomes."

Edmund nodded slowly, and Ines saw that behind the captain's eyes some parallel arithmetic was running — something about debts, about ownership, about what a person owed versus what a person was made to believe they owed. She had learned to read those calculations on Edmund's face the way she read contour lines: the close spacing meant steep country ahead.

Then Edmund opened her own letter, and stopped doing arithmetic altogether.

* * *

Ines pretended to sort her bundle. She was not proud of it, but she watched.

Edmund broke the seal with her thumbnail, careful, the way you open something that might be load-bearing. The letter inside was several pages, folded in thirds, and the handwriting was the same small precise script that had addressed the envelope. Edmund read the first page quickly. Then slowly. Then she went back to the beginning and read it again, and Ines saw the exact moment the second reading became something else — not reading but *recognition*, the face of a woman finding her own reflection in water she hadn't known was there.

The fire cracked. Somewhere downslope, a bird Ines couldn't name called three notes and got silence back.

"Read it aloud," Ines said gently. "If you want. Sometimes a thing gets smaller when it's shared."

Edmund was silent long enough that Ines thought she'd overstepped. Then, in a voice sanded down to its grain, the captain began.

*"Dear Miss Joubert — or may I call you Edmund, since I suspect we are about to become badly acquainted with each other's patience over the course of this expedition—"*

"It's dated eleven months ago," Edmund said, looking up. "Before they left the river city. Before any of this."

*"I am writing this before we depart, to be cached on the trail, because my father taught me that important things should be said in duplicate — once while they can still be unsaid, and once when they can't. This is the copy that can't.* 

*You will wonder why the leader of an expedition writes to a riverboat captain he has never met. The answer is that I have met you, though you don't know it. I was seventeen the summer my father financed the purchase of your boat, and I stood on the dock at Kilbourn Landing and watched you take delivery. You were younger than I am now. You thanked him with such ferocity — you shook his hand like you were trying to wring the truth out of it — and I remember thinking: she knows something is wrong with this gift. Everyone else saw charity. You saw a chain.*

*I have thought about that handshake for fifteen years.*

*My father is not an evil man. I want to say that plainly, because everything else I have to say will make it hard to believe. He is a man who learned early that money is a language, and he speaks it so fluently he has forgotten it isn't the only one. When he financed the reservoir, he did the arithmetic they gave him — displaced households, submerged acreage, compensation schedules — and the arithmetic balanced, and so in his ledger the thing was clean. What the arithmetic did not contain was the singing. I cannot explain that yet. Ask me again when I am home, if I am home, and I will explain why a valley full of living people does not appear in any column.*

*Here is what I need you to know, Edmund, and why I am telling a stranger instead of my father: the compass he gave you was never payment. It was collateral. It belonged to my grandfather, and my grandfather won it in a card game from a man named Joubert — your father — the year they surveyed this same range together. They were partners. Did you know that? Did he ever tell you? My father says they quarreled over the route and never spoke again, but I found their joint field notes in his safe, and partners do not co-author field notes and merely quarrel.*

*The debt you carry — the boat, the payments, the collector's letters — I have seen the papers. I am my father's son; I read everything. And I am telling you now, in duplicate, in advance, so that it exists somewhere outside his office: whatever that debt says, it is not the whole truth. There are pages missing. There are pages my father removed. When I come down off this mountain, I intend to find them, and I intend to put them in your hands, because a woman who reads a river the way you do should not spend her life paying for a boat that was half hers to begin with.*

*A valley that remembers people. That is what the old survey journals call this place, the ones my grandfather kept and my father locked away. I used to think it was poetry. I am beginning to think it is a warning, or a promise, or both — that some places hold what was done to them, and wait.*

*Wait for me, Edmund. Or don't. But when the snows close the pass and the monsoon cuts the river, remember that somewhere above the last rapids, a man is trying to bring you the rest of your own story.*

*Yours in duplicate,*
*Daniel Halvorsen"*

The wind moved through the saddle. The red cloth on the stripped cairn pole had fallen into the stones, and no one had picked it up.

Edmund folded the letter along its original creases, precisely, and Ines saw that her hands were not quite steady, and that she was holding them the way Tomas had held his bundle — cupped, afraid of spilling.

"My father," Edmund said. It wasn't clear whether she was speaking to Ines or to the fire or to the letter. "He talked about the mountains. Never about a partner. Never about a card game. He died when I was nine, and all I had of him was stories about rivers, and a compass I assumed I'd stolen the family blind to get." She laughed — a short, unsteady sound with no floor under it. "I built my whole life on the assumption that I was the debtor. That the Halvorsens were benefactors. And this boy — this *stranger* — writes me a letter saying the ledger was rigged before I was born."

"The valley remembers people," Ines said softly. "That's what he wrote. And it seems it remembered you."

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned to a red heart and the others slept, Ines took out her sketchbook and worked by ember-light, drawing the cairn's construction, the knot, the tin box, cataloguing the bundles — the discipline of her hands keeping her mind from spinning. Across the camp, Edmund sat awake at the wheel-less helm of nothing, turning the compass over in her palms, and Ines sketched her too, quickly, without permission: a woman holding a small brass object the way you hold a key to a house you've just learned you inherited.

Tomas stirred near the ashes. He had not slept either. He crawled over and sat beside Ines, and for a while neither spoke, which was its own kind of conversation.

"She'll go faster now," Tomas said at last. "Up the mountain. Won't she?"

"Yes."

"Because of the letter?"

"Because of the truth in it," Ines said. "Letters are just the shape truth takes when it has to travel."

Tomas nodded, absorbing this, and then produced from inside his jacket the letter he'd been reading — his uncle's last, by the softness of its folds — and held it out to her. "You should see this part. The others' letters talk about the valley. But Uncle Anders wrote about *us*. About who should come looking, if the expedition stayed too long." He tapped a paragraph near the bottom. "*If Daniel decides to winter in the valley — and he will, he always falls in love with the places everyone else wants to leave — send the girl with the good eyes and the boy with the strong back. Not soldiers. Not officials. Eyes and backs. The valley has survived soldiers and officials. It has never yet survived being forgotten.*"

Ines read it twice. *The girl with the good eyes.* She touched the sentence with one finger, lightly, the way you touch a word in a language you're only learning.

"Tomas," she said. "Your uncle wrote this before we existed."

"He wrote it anyway," Tomas said, with the simple theology of the young. "That's how he is. He aims ahead of the bird."

Ines closed her sketchbook. Above the saddle, past the black teeth of the ridge, the stars wheeled over the pass they would have to cross, and somewhere beyond them — a day's climb, two, no more — a hidden valley held its fires and its walls and its waiting archive, and held also, she now understood, a man named Daniel Halvorsen who had written to a stranger about her own inheritance because he believed debts could be lies and truths could be mailed.

She was still thinking about that — about truth as cargo, about what it cost to carry — when Sefu's whistle cut the dark, two short notes, the signal for *movement below.*

They were on their feet instantly. Down the slope, far off in the black fold of the forest, lanterns were swinging through the trees. Not one. Several. Moving in the disciplined, spaced file of men following a bearing.

Vane was already standing, ledger in hand, and Ines watched his face in the dying firelight and saw that he was not surprised.

"They've found the cairn trail," Sefu murmured, coming down from his watch. "Or they've found us. Either way, captain — we climb tonight, or we explain ourselves tomorrow."

Edmund put the compass in her coat, over her heart, and looked up at the pass.

"Wake everyone," she said. "We climb."
