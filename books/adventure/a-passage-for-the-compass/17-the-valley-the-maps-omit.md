## 17. The Valley the Maps Omit

The ridge had been lying to them all morning.

Edmund had learned long ago that mountains were fluent liars — that a slope which looked like an hour's climb could eat a full day, that a saddle promising a view beyond it might only promise another wall of rock behind it. So she had stopped believing ridgelines the way she'd once stopped believing rivermen who swore a channel was deep enough for her draft. She climbed with her eyes down and her breath counted, one foot after another on the switchback Sefu cut through the scree, and she let the mountain keep its secrets until it chose to spend them.

Ines was ahead of her, moving with the quick economy of a woman who had spent eleven days learning to walk at altitude and hated every minute of it but would never say so. Tomas scrambled above them both, sixteen years old and made of rubber and stubbornness, calling back false alarms about smoke, about roofs, about anything his hungry eyes wanted to be true. Sefu brought up the rear with the eldest guide, an old man named Wendo whose knees had begun to complain on the third day of the ascent and who ignored them with the dignity of a man who had been ignoring worse things longer.

The air had gone thin and clean, scrubbed of the river's wet green smell. Edmund missed it. Eleven days out from the last landing, and she still caught herself listening for water under everything, the way a woman who has lived her whole life aboard listens for the hull's voice even in her sleep.

"Captain." Ines had stopped. She stood very still, the way she did when she was reading something — a chart, a current, a trail mark. "Come here."

Edmund came up beside her, hands on knees, and let her breathing settle before she lifted her eyes.

The world opened.

There was no other way to say it, though she would try many ways in the days that followed, in testimony and in letters and once, late at night, to no one at all. The ridge simply ended its argument, and the land beyond it fell away into a valley so wide and so green that her first thought — absurd, unshakeable — was that she was looking at the river again, some vast drowned arm of it, except that nothing moved down there but light.

Terraces stepped down the valley walls in long pale curves, hundreds of them, thousands, stacked like the rings of a felled tree, like the fingerprints of a hand pressed into the earth and left there. Some were dark with growing things; others lay fallow, grassed over, old as anything she had ever seen. Stone walls threaded between them in lines too regular to be accident and too worn to be new. And at the valley's heart, where any mapmaker would have drawn the river's source or nothing at all, there were ruins — not the sad scattered bones of a place abandoned, but walls with roofs, roofs with smoke, smoke rising straight up in the windless morning air from a dozen chimneys, twenty, more than she could count before her eyes gave up trying.

Tomas shouted something. She didn't hear what. Her ears had filled with a sound like the river at flood, which was strange, because there was no river here, and because the sound was coming from inside her own chest.

*They didn't vanish,* she thought. *They stayed.*

The answer arrived before the understanding. That was how she would describe it later, when Daniel Halvorsen asked her, and she would watch his face do something complicated when she said it, because he knew exactly what she meant. For eleven days they had followed a trail of careful knots and ordered camps and cached mail, building a story out of absence: the expedition went inland voluntarily, the expedition knew where it was going, the expedition is alive. She had believed it the way you believe a channel marker — provisionally, professionally, knowing the river moves markers when it pleases. But belief is a thin thing, a rope across a gorge. What she felt now, looking down at the smoke of inhabited fires in a valley that appeared on none of Ines's maps and none of the survey office's charts and, she suspected now, none of anyone's charts anywhere, was not belief. It was recognition. The way you recognize a face across a crowded dock after twenty years. The understanding would come later, trailing its questions. First came the sheer animal fact of it: *here.*

"They're farming," Ines said quietly. Her voice had gone strange, flattened, the voice of a woman holding herself together with both hands. She had her sketchbook out but wasn't drawing. She was just looking, the pencil hanging forgotten in her fingers. "Look at the terraces — those aren't new. Captain, those terraces are *old.* Centuries. Maybe—" She stopped. Started again. "The maps omit this. All of them. I've mapped this basin from survey photographs and there is *nothing* here. There's a blank. I assumed cloud cover."

"Cloud cover," Edmund repeated.

"In the photographs. A white patch, right here, exactly here." Ines laughed, one short note with no humor in it at all. "I thought it was weather."

Sefu had come up beside them without making a sound, which was his gift. He looked down into the valley for a long time, and his face — usually a closed door, a face that gave away weather reports and little else — did something Edmund had never seen it do. It softened. Then it grieved.

"My grandmother's people," he said, "had a word for a place like this. Not a name. A *word.*" He said the word then, two syllables soft as breath, and Wendo beside him closed his eyes as if the sound had struck him somewhere old. "It means: *the place that keeps itself.* Places that keep themselves don't want finding, Captain. The ones who find them anyway have an obligation."

"I'm tired of obligations," Edmund said, but her voice came out wrong, hushed, and nobody laughed.

Wendo spoke then, slowly, in the trade tongue, choosing each word like a man crossing stones. "When I was young, my father's father told of a valley above the burn line where the first people waited out a great dying. He said the terraces were cut before iron came to the low country. He said the people there kept the river's memory." He looked at her, and his eyes were wet and fierce. "He said it was a story. Old men tell stories. But my father's father also said: if you ever see smoke rising from a valley the maps refuse, do not report it. Report it, and men with paper come. Men with paper come, and the valley stops keeping itself."

Edmund thought of Vane, stranded at the mission station three hundred miles behind them with a lie about their route and a radio he probably hadn't stopped using. She thought of the survey office, of the reservoir gauges ticking upward, of the drowned village with its church tower standing in eight feet of green water, and the old woman who had held her compass and called it by a name Edmund didn't know. *Halvorsen's star-finder,* the woman had called it, and then something else, something older, and Edmund had carried that second name up this whole mountain without letting herself turn it over, the way you carry a letter you're afraid to open.

She turned it over now.

Smoke rose from the valley floor, straight and unhurried, the smoke of cooking fires, of morning bread, of ordinary life going on in a place the modern world had decided did not exist. Somewhere down there were eleven missing people — nine scholars, a photographer, and Daniel Halvorsen, son of the man who owned her boat and her past eleven years of labor. They had walked into this valley in high summer and simply not come out. Not lost. Not dead. *Stayed.* And staying, in Edmund's experience, was never neutral. People stayed for love, or for fear, or for a truth they couldn't bear to carry back down.

She looked at the terraces again, really looked, and saw what awe had blurred: figures moving among the upper fields. Small at this distance, patient, bent to their work. One of them straightened and stood looking up toward the ridge, toward them, for a long moment. Then it raised an arm — not waving, Edmund realized. Pointing. Signaling someone below.

"We've been seen," Sefu said.

"Good," said Tomas, arriving red-faced and radiant at the top of the switchback. "I was starting to think we'd imagined the whole thing. Uncle Anders' hat, the cache, the knots — I half thought I'd made it up because I wanted it true." He stood at the edge of the drop, wind pulling at his shirt, and grinned down at the impossible valley with the uncomplicated joy of a boy who has just been handed proof that the world is bigger than the adults said. "It's real. It's actually real."

"It's real," Edmund agreed. "And being real, it can be hurt. Remember that part, Tomás. Everything real can be hurt."

He glanced at her, and some of the shine went out of his grin and became something better, something steadier. He was learning. God help her, the stowaway was learning.

* * *

They descended the near shoulder of the ridge through pine and then through that dripping, moss-hung forest the guides called the cloud's garden, where the trail vanished into leaf-rot and reappeared under their feet like a rumor. Sefu led. Wendo walked beside him, and the two of them spoke in low voices, sometimes in trade tongue, sometimes in that older language that made Edmund feel like a guest eavesdropping in a house she'd been invited into but not shown around.

The trail, when they found it properly, was the first thing that truly frightened her — more than the gorge, more than the storm-swollen bridge they'd crossed four days ago with Tomas frozen halfway along it. Because this trail was *kept.* Steps were cut into the steep pitches, worn smooth by generations of feet. A fallen tree had been sawn through and the halves stacked neatly aside. At intervals stood posts carved with symbols — the same script they'd found on the warning marker in the cloud forest, the one Sefu could only half read, the one that had said something about the valley and about who might enter and what they owed.

"Somebody maintains this," Ines murmured, running her thumb along a cut step. "Somebody has maintained it for a very long time."

"The expedition?" Tomas asked.

"No." Sefu didn't turn around. "The expedition found it the way we're finding it. The maintenance is older than your uncle, boy. Older than the maps. Older than the maps' grandfathers."

Edmund walked with her hand in her pocket, closed around the compass. She had taken to carrying it against her palm during the hard climbs, a habit she didn't examine. Its brass case was warm from her body, and the needle within swung lazily, uselessly — compasses were poor instruments in these folded mountains, agreeing with no direction for longer than a few hundred yards. But today, as the trail bent and the trees opened, she felt the needle tremble against its pivot and steady, pointing not north but down-valley, into the smoke, and hold there, faithful as a dog.

She took it out and looked at it, and the needle did not waver.

"That's not magnetic," Ines said, stopping beside her. She was watching the needle too, and her cartographer's face had gone carefully blank, the way it did when data misbehaved. "Or it shouldn't be. There's iron in these ridges — that's why our fixes have been drifting since the burn line. Captain, your needle is pointing at the valley."

"Then it finally agrees with me," Edmund said, and put it away, and did not say what she was thinking, which was that the compass had belonged to Halvorsen, and Halvorsen had financed her boat, and the old woman in the drowned village had known it on sight, and somewhere below them Daniel Halvorsen was waiting with answers she suspected would rearrange her life like furniture in a flooded house. The needle pointed at the valley. Fine. Let it. She had spent eleven years letting other people's instruments set her heading. Whatever waited down there, she would look at it with her own eyes first.

* * *

They smelled the valley before they reached it — woodsmoke and bread and something green and crushed, like cut hay, like the fields along the river before the water took them. The trail emerged from the forest onto a lower spur of terrace, and there the expedition found them, or perhaps they found the expedition; afterward Edmund could never sort out who had come upon whom.

A figure was climbing the terraces toward them, taking the risers two at a time with the loose-limbed stride of a man who had been walking these walls for months. Behind him, smaller figures gathered at the edges of the upper fields and stood watching — the valley people, motionless, unreadable, a dozen faces turned up toward the strangers on their stair. And behind them, at the head of the column, six figures in expedition khaki gone soft and sun-bleached, descending in a loose line.

"Tomas!" The shout came down the slope ahead of its owner. "Tomás! Is that—"

"Uncle!" Tomas was already running, scrambling down the terrace steps with no regard for his neck, and Edmund watched the boy and the tall gray-bearded man collide at the third wall down and cling to each other like survivors of a shipwreck, laughing and swearing and pounding each other's backs, and she felt her throat close and looked away, out of courtesy and out of something else, something that ached.

Because the tall man was Daniel Halvorsen. She knew him from a photograph that had hung in her cabin for eleven years — the creditor's family portrait, the son home from university, standing slightly apart from his father with his hands in his pockets and an expression she had always privately read as *get me out of here.* He was thinner now, browner, bearded, with lines around his eyes that the photograph hadn't earned. But she knew him. And as Tomas released him and turned, gesturing wildly up the slope, saying words she couldn't hear over the blood in her ears, she watched Daniel Halvorsen follow the boy's pointing hand and find her.

She saw it happen. Saw the recognition move across his face like weather. Saw him go still — not surprised, not pleased, but stricken, the way a man goes still when a debt he thought was buried surfaces in front of witnesses. His eyes dropped to her hand, to the compass she was holding without quite meaning to be holding, its needle still trembling toward the valley, and whatever color remained in his face left it entirely.

"You," he said. He had come up the last steps without her seeing him move. Up close he smelled of woodsmoke and ink. "You're Joubert. You're — my God. You're the captain."

"Captain Edmund Joubert," she said. Her voice held. She was proud of that. "Your father's boat, Mr. Halvorsen. Or near enough that it's never mattered which."

"It matters," Daniel Halvorsen said. He was staring at the compass, and his voice had dropped to almost nothing, pitched for her alone while the others milled and embraced and the valley people watched from the high terraces like birds deciding whether to fly. "Captain, it matters more than you know. Why did you come? Who sent you? Who *knows* you're here?"

"The survey office sent me to find a missing expedition," Edmund said. "Eleven days silent. Half my debt for the finding." She paused, and let him see that she saw him. "But I'm beginning to think the debt was never the price. I'm beginning to think it was the bait."

Behind him, the smoke rose from the valley fires, straight and calm, and the people of the terraces began, softly, to sing — a low working song, unhurried, the kind sung to make labor lighter, and Edmund's skin prickled from nape to wrist, because she knew the melody. She had heard it once before, hummed by an old woman standing in the shallows of a drowned village, holding a compass and calling it by a name.

Daniel Halvorsen heard it too. He flinched as if the song had touched a burn.

"There are things you need to see," he said. "Before you decide anything. Before you go back down that mountain or send one word downriver. Captain — Edmund — there are things my father paid to drown, and I need you to see them first."
