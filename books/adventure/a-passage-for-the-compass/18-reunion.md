## 18. Reunion

Daniel Halvorsen had learned, in the four months since the expedition went silent, to read the mountain the way his father had taught him to read a river: not by what it showed, but by what it withheld. The smoke from the valley fires told him the wind had turned. The silence of the birds at midmorning told him something was moving on the western trail. And the look on the face of the eldest guide, Anaya, when she came down from the ridge post at a pace no one her age should attempt — that told him the thing moving was people.

"They are six," she said, catching her breath in the cold thin air of the archive terrace. "Five walking, one carried on a litter. They have a guide who walks like a man reading a book — Sefu, I think, though he is grayer than the last time I saw him. And a boy."

Daniel set down the wax stylus he had been using to press characters into the damp clay tablets the valley's archivists still made by hand, the way their ancestors had before the river people had ever heard of paper. "A boy?"

"Small. Quick. He walks beside the tall woman with the ink-stained fingers, and she is teaching him the trail marks as they come to them. He learns them faster than I learned my own letters."

Daniel stood. Around him, the morning work of the expedition went on with the practiced quiet of people who had learned to be careful — Dr. Okonkwo cataloguing seed grain in the longhouse, the two graduate students copying the water-stained ledgers onto fresh linen paper, Marta up on the scaffolding with a brush, clearing eight hundred years of soot from a carved lintel. Four months of silence had made them a household. He had not realized until this moment how much he had been waiting for the sound of other voices from outside.

"Who else?" he asked, and hated how carefully he asked it.

Anaya's eyes, deep-set and unhurried, held his. "A captain," she said. "A woman. She carries a compass at her neck on a cord, the old kind, brass, with a hinged lid. She checks it even here, where the trails are marked in stone." Anaya paused. "She holds it the way you hold yours."

The cold that went through Daniel then had nothing to do with the altitude. He had known, from the first intercepted report Vane's network had let slip downriver — the one that said a riverboat captain named Joubert had been hired to look for them — that this day would come. He had told himself he would be ready for it. He had rehearsed explanations in the dark of the longhouse, lying awake beneath the smoke-hole while the valley breathed around him: *I can tell her the truth. I can tell her most of the truth. I can tell her enough.*

He had not rehearsed the compass.

"Where is she now?" he asked.

"Coming down the switchbacks. An hour, maybe less, if the litter is slow." Anaya looked at him a moment longer. "You have gone white, Daniel Halvorsen."

"I'm fine," he said, which was the first lie he had told in the valley, and it sat in his mouth like river mud.

* * *

He spent the hour badly.

He tried to work. He stood over the ledgers with Okonkwo and heard nothing of what she said about the flood-year records. He climbed to the ridge post and looked down the western trail until the light hurt his eyes, and saw nothing, and came down, and tried to work again. Finally he did what he always did when his mind would not hold still: he went to the terrace where his father's letters were kept, in the oilcloth packet he had carried up the mountain in his breast pocket for eleven days of a journey that was supposed to take four.

He did not open the packet. He knew the letters by heart. He knew the one from his father, written the winter before the old man died, the handwriting already going loose and uncertain, the way his mind had gone:

*Daniel — I have done a thing I cannot undo and I find I cannot confess it to anyone but you, and I cannot even confess it to you plainly, so I will tell it slantwise and trust you to find the shape of it. When we built the dam — when I financed it, when I signed the papers with the ministry men in their white suits — we were told the valley above the reservoir was empty. I paid for the surveys myself. I chose the surveyors. I chose men who would find what I needed them to find, which was nothing. There were people in that valley, Daniel. There had been people there for a thousand years, and I paid to make them into nothing on a map, and the water did the rest. Your mother says a man can be forgiven what he did not know. But I knew. God help me, I knew by the second year, when the canoes started coming downriver with things in them that could only have come from the high valley, and I told the ministry men to stop the patrols, and they stopped, and I told myself that was enough. It was not enough. It will never be enough. If you are reading this and I am gone, I ask only one thing: do not let them finish it. The second phase. They will come for it. They always said they would come back for the upper valley, and I signed that too.*

And beneath it, in a different hand — a smaller, harder hand that Daniel had never been able to place until three weeks ago, when Anaya had looked at the signature and gone still as a heron:

*E.J. — for the passage. You know what it cost. You know what it was for. One day someone will come upriver who deserves to know, and when they do, give them the compass and tell them everything. — E.J.*

The initials had meant nothing to Daniel for years. E.J. His father had known half the river; the initials could have belonged to a dozen people. Then Vane's intercepted report had come up the mountain in a smuggler's packet — *Joubert, E., master of the riverboat* something-or-other, hired by the survey office — and Daniel had lain awake all that night doing arithmetic he did not want to finish.

His father's letters. His father's debt. His father's compass, given to some E.J. who had once, in some way his father could not confess even to his own son, *paid for a passage* — and who had then, apparently, spent years in servitude to the Halvorsen estate for a boat his father's money had built.

And now she was climbing his mountain, wearing the compass at her throat like a question.

"Daniel." Marta's voice, from below the scaffolding. "They're at the cairn. The one on the shoulder of the ridge. You can see them if you come now."

* * *

He watched them come up the last stretch of trail from the shadow of the archive gatehouse, and it was strange, he thought, how a thing you have dreaded can arrive looking like a gift.

They came up out of the cloud line in single file, the way mountain people walk, and the valley light took them one at a time: first Sefu, gray and unhurried, nodding to the ridge sentries as though he had left them only yesterday; then the tall woman with the ink-stained fingers — Ines Marchetti, he realized, his own cartographer, presumed drowned with the rest of them, her face thinner and brown as old wood and her eyes alight with an expression he had never seen her wear in the low country, something between exhaustion and rapture; then a man he did not know, broad-shouldered, watchful, carrying himself with the careful politeness of someone performing a role; then two valley porters with the litter, on which an old man rode with his chin up, taking in the terraces and the smoke and the carved stones with an expression Daniel recognized, because he wore it himself every morning: the look of a man coming home to a place he had never been.

And at the rear, walking beside a boy of maybe fifteen, a woman in a river coat gone soft with years of sun and spray, dark hair cut short and practical, moving with that rolling gait that Daniel had seen on every boat deck from the delta to the last navigable rapids.

She had the compass on a cord at her neck. Even at two hundred yards he could see the brass catch the light when she looked down at it, which she did often, the way a person touches a thing to be sure it is still real.

The boy saw the settlement first — or saw the people, rather, because the settlement was built to be unseen, its roofs sodded with growing grass, its walls the color of the hillside — and Daniel watched him stop dead, and grab the woman's sleeve, and say something, and then break into a run.

"UNCLE MARCUS!"

The name went up the trail like a shot. And from the longhouse doorway, where he had been mending a net with the patience of a man who had learned that mending was most of survival, a big bearded man stood up so fast he knocked over his stool, and looked, and dropped the net, and ran.

Daniel watched his second-in-command — his quiet, competent, grief-hardened Marcus, who had wept only once in four months, the night they burned their own supply cache to keep it from Vane's scouts — meet the boy halfway down the terrace steps and lift him clean off the ground, and hold him, and shake, and say nothing at all, because there was nothing that would hold the weight of four months and one presumed-dead nephew alive in his arms.

The boy was Tomas. Daniel remembered him now — the Halvorsen family's river compound was not so large that a cousin's son could be forgotten, the quiet boy who had sat at the edge of the dock the summer Daniel had loaded the expedition, watching the crates go aboard with an expression of such naked longing that Daniel had almost invited him along and then, with the coward's arithmetic of adults, decided he was too young.

*Too young,* Daniel thought, watching him cling to his uncle. *He was too young, so I left him, and so he lived, while eleven of us went upriver and the world was told we died.* He did not know yet what the boy would think of that arithmetic when he learned the whole of it. He suspected the boy had already done the sum.

The others came up more slowly. Sefu clasped arms with the sentries and began, immediately, a low-voiced conversation about the western trails that had the cadence of men who had argued about these trails for twenty years and would argue about them for twenty more. Ines Marchetti reached the gatehouse, stopped, and looked up — at the terraces, at the lintel Marta had been cleaning, at the archive longhouse with its grass roof and its stone foundation older than any nation on the maps she had spent her life correcting — and Daniel saw her put her hand over her mouth.

"Dr. Marchetti," he said.

"Captain Halvorsen." Her voice came out rough. She took her hand down. "You're supposed to be dead. All of you. We found your cache at the trading post, opened careful, trail marks going inland. I read them as voluntary. I told them—" she gestured back down the trail, "—I told them you'd gone inland on purpose. They thought I was grasping."

"You weren't grasping," Daniel said. "You were right. We went inland on purpose." He heard how it sounded, and made himself say the rest plainly, because he had learned in this valley that the river people had a word for a man who softened the truth until it was easier to swallow, and the word was not a kind one. "All of it was on purpose. Staying was on purpose. The silence was on purpose. I'll explain all of it, but—" and here his eyes went past her, down the trail, to where the woman in the river coat was coming up the last switchback with the litter party, "—there are explanations that can't wait."

Ines turned to follow his gaze, and then looked back at him, and Daniel had the distinct, uncomfortable sensation of being read by a cartographer: of contours and elevations being taken, of his face being mapped.

"That's Edmund Joubert," she said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"She came to find you. She nearly wrecked her boat twice doing it. She carried a boy up a mountain and an old man over scree, and she burned her own debt paper to buy us passage out of a smuggler's toll." Ines's voice was level, but there was iron under it. "Whatever you have to say to her, Captain, you should say it knowing what it cost her to get here."

"I know what it cost her," Daniel said. "Not all of it. But I know more of it than she does."

* * *

She came up through the gatehouse at a quarter past the hour, and the valley received her the way it received everyone: quietly, with the smoke-hole breathing and the terraces holding their green and the carved stones saying nothing, because they had been saying the same thing for eight hundred years and saw no reason to raise their voices.

Up close she was smaller than the reports had made her, and harder, in the way of things that have been knocked against the world a long time and not broken. Her eyes did the same thing Ines's had — took the terraces, the roofs, the smoke, the people — but they took them like a pilot reading water, fast and total, and Daniel understood that this woman had already assessed his settlement's defensibility, its food stores by the look of its drying racks, and its exits, all in the time it had taken her to walk through the gate.

Then her eyes found him, and stopped.

"You're Halvorsen," she said.

"Daniel Halvorsen. Yes." He came forward. Behind him he could feel the whole settlement pretending not to watch — Okonkwo frozen over her seed grain, the graduate students with their pens lifted, Marta on her scaffolding turned fully around. "Captain Joubert. You came a long way."

"Eleven days of radio silence buys a long way." She stopped an arm's length from him. Up close he could see the weather in her face, the fine scarring of sun and spray, the eyes a gray-brown like river water over stone. "Your people are alive. All of them?"

"All but two. We lost men on the ascent, the first winter approach. Cold and a fall. The rest are here, and healthy, and—" he hesitated, "—and not prisoners, whatever you've been told."

"Nobody told me anything. That was rather the problem." Her gaze went past his shoulder, over the terraces, and something in it shifted — not softening, exactly. Recalculation. "This isn't a camp."

"No."

"It's a town."

"It was a town," Daniel said, "for about nine hundred years. It still is. The people you see drying grain on those racks are the descendants of the people who built those terraces, and the archive behind me holds their records going back further than any nation on your maps, and the reservoir project that my father financed was going to drown all of it in the second phase, and that is why we stayed, and that is why we went silent, and that is—" he stopped, because she had raised one hand, not rudely, but with the flat economy of a woman used to cutting through the talk of men.

"Later," she said. "All of that, later. Right now there's a boy over there who hasn't let go of his uncle in ten minutes, and an old man on a litter who crossed a pass to get here, and my crew hasn't eaten since dawn." Her eyes came back to his, and held, and Daniel felt the weight of them like a hand on his chest. "But you should know I have questions, Captain Halvorsen. A lot of them. And one of them I've been carrying since a drowned village, and an old woman there took one look at this—" her hand went to the cord at her neck, "—and called it by a name I didn't know, and told me things about it I've spent my whole life not knowing."

The compass swung free of her collar as her hand closed around it. Brass, hinged, old, the lid worn smooth at the thumb-piece. Daniel had seen it a thousand times in the study of the house on the river — in the photograph on his father's desk, actually, the old man's hand around it, taken the year Daniel was born — and he had told himself, all the way up this mountain, that he was prepared.

He was not prepared. Preparedness, he discovered, is a story you tell yourself in the dark, and the light tells a different one.

"Where did you get that?" His voice came out wrong. He heard it come out wrong, and could not fix it.

"It was in the boat when I got her." Her eyes narrowed, and he watched the pilot in her take his face apart the way she'd taken apart his settlement. "Nineteen years ago. Salvage rights, they called it, and then the debt papers came, and I signed my life into them to keep her. The boat and the compass both. I've carried it ever since. It's the truest thing I own." She turned the compass over in her palm, and Daniel saw the engraving on the back catch the light — the initials his father had cut into it with his own hand, before Daniel was born, before the dam, before everything. "The old woman in the drowned village said it had a name. She said it was called the *Passage*. She said—" and here Edmund Joubert's voice changed, went quieter, went careful, the way a voice goes when it is carrying something fragile across bad water, "—she said it belonged to a man who made a promise on this river, and that the promise wasn't kept, and that the compass would come back upriver one day when the promise was called due."

The valley was very quiet. Somewhere below, the boy Tomas was laughing — actually laughing, a sound Daniel hadn't heard in four months — and the sound of it came up the terraces and broke against the silence between the two of them like a wave against a wall.

"Captain Joubert," Daniel said. "I think we should sit down."

"I don't want to sit down. I want—" She stopped. He watched her master herself, the way he had watched her crew master the river for her, and when she spoke again her voice was level and cold and very clear. "I want you to tell me why a Halvorsen goes white at the sight of a compass he's never seen, and why your father's name has been chained to my boat for nineteen years, and why every answer I've gotten on this river has been a piece of something, and everyone I've met has been holding the piece next to theirs and deciding how much to show me." The compass lay in her open palm between them, brass and old and pointing, as it always pointed, at a north neither of them could see. "I'm done being shown pieces, Mr. Halvorsen. So before I eat your bread or sleep under your roof — before I let one more hour go by — you're going to tell me what your father's initials are doing on my compass, and what he promised, and to whom."

Daniel Halvorsen looked at the woman his father had wronged before she was old enough to be wronged, at the compass that was half confession and half inheritance, at the valley behind her where the smoke of nine hundred years of fires rose straight into the cold blue air — and understood that the moment had come that he had rehearsed in the dark and failed in the light.

"My father," he said, "made a promise to a man named Joubert. And I think, Captain, that the man he made it to was your father — and that what my family owed yours was never money at all."

Edmund's hand closed over the compass, slowly, the way a hand closes over a rail in rising water.

"Then start," she said, "from the beginning."
