## 19. What They Found

Daniel Halvorsen had not slept, Edmund could see it in the way he held his shoulders — like a man carrying water across a long distance without spilling any. He had led them down from the ridge at first light, past terraces cut into the valley's flanks so old that the mountain itself seemed to have grown around them, and now he stood before a low doorway of fitted stone and did not go in.

The smoke from inhabited fires rose behind him, thin and blue against the morning. Somewhere below, children were shouting at each other in a language that had no business still being spoken this far into the century.

"You'll want to sit," he said. "It's a long story, and I've only got the short version."

They sat on a wall of dry-laid stone that had been there longer than any nation Daniel could name. Ines folded herself cross-legged with her sketchbook already open. Tomas perched beside his uncle, close enough to touch, as if he needed confirming that the man was solid. Sefu remained standing, arms crossed, watching the valley with the expression of a man who had been told stories about this place since he was a boy and was now checking them against the facts. Vane was two days behind them somewhere, or ahead of them in every way that mattered, and Edmund found she could not make herself care just now.

Edmund sat apart from the others, the compass in her lap. She hadn't put it away since the reunion. She kept turning it over, reading the worn initials on the plate — *E.H.* — and watching Daniel watch her do it.

"Start with the valley," Ines said. "Start with what it is."

Daniel nodded slowly. "All right. The valley is an archive."

"A library?" Tomas asked.

"Better than a library. Libraries burn." He gestured at the terraces, the ruins, the smoke. "This whole valley is a record. The river people — the ones your maps call extinct, the ones the schoolbooks say vanished when the reservoir went up — they didn't vanish. They came here. Some of them were always here; the high valleys were their winter grounds, their refuge in the bad years. And they brought everything with them. Stones with the year-marks carved on them. Bundles of knotted cord that record harvests and floods and who married whom going back further than writing does in most of the world. Painted bark panels in caves above the snowline. A whole civilization's memory, carried up here on foot, generation after generation, because they knew — they always knew — that one day the low country would be taken from them."

He paused. The wind moved through the terraces and made a sound like breathing.

"And the reservoir project," Edmund said. Her voice came out flat. "Your father financed it."

* * *

Daniel looked at her then, directly, and did not look away, which she gave him credit for.

"My father financed it," he said. "And my father knew."

The words dropped into the group like stones into still water. Nobody moved.

"There are letters in the archive down there — no, not the archive, I mean our own papers, the expedition's papers — engineering reports from twenty years ago. Surveys commissioned before the dam was approved. They mapped the lowland villages. They catalogued what lived there. Not just people — terraces, burial grounds, orchards older than the river towns upstream. And the reports concluded, in language so careful it makes me sick to read it, that the flooding would constitute, quote, 'an acceptable loss of cultural material of limited significance.'"

"Limited significance," Ines repeated. Her pencil had stopped moving.

"They knew the villages weren't empty," Daniel said. "They knew the people hadn't left. There was a relocation plan — you've seen what became of it. A few families moved to the mission, some drifted to the cities, and the rest—" He spread his hands toward the drowned village Edmund had piloted past weeks ago, its rooftops just breaking the surface on low-water days. "The rest stayed until the water came. And then they walked uphill, carrying what they could, and the world wrote them off as dispersed. Assimilated. Gone."

Edmund's hands had gone still on the compass. "The drowned village," she said. "The woman who recognized my compass. She was—"

"One of them. Almost certainly." Daniel crouched so he was nearer her level, and something in his face shifted — the expedition leader's authority giving way to something rawer. "Edmund. There's more, and I'm not going to enjoy saying it, but you deserve it straight."

"Say it."

"The financing for the reservoir came through my father's bank. But the river concessions — the shipping contracts, the salvage rights, the new trade routes the flooded valley opened up — those were parceled out afterward to a handful of partners. Men who took the risk early, who helped push the project through, who kept the surveys quiet." He took a breath. "One of them was your father."

The compass seemed to grow heavy in her lap, hot as a coal.

"My father," Edmund said. "My father was a partner in the dam."

"He was a young man then. Younger than I am now. And I think—" Daniel hesitated. "I think he figured out, too late, what he'd helped do. I think that's why he pulled out. Why the partnership with my family broke. Why the debt paper was drawn up the way it was — not as a loan, Edmund. As a leash. My father didn't want your father talking. And your father took the boat and the debt and the silence, and he went upriver and stayed upriver for the rest of his life, running passengers past the water line, never once telling anyone what he knew."

"That's not possible," Edmund said, but even as she said it, memory began rearranging itself: her father's refusal to ever take the southern channel, the one that ran over the old orchards; the way he'd slow the boat, every single time, passing the drowned village, and stand at the rail with his hat off, and never explain why; the stories he told about the pass, about crossing it once with "a Halvorsen man," and how he'd stop the story whenever she asked what they'd carried over.

"He slowed the boat," she whispered. "Every time. I thought it was the current."

"It was grief," Daniel said. "Or guilt. Probably both."

* * *

Ines broke the silence first, because someone had to. "The letters," she said. "You said the expedition stayed. Eleven days of radio silence to document all this. Why stay? Why not come back down with proof?"

"Because proof gets bought," Daniel said simply. "We sent word down after the first week. One messenger, one report, sealed. Within four days a man was climbing the trail offering us — I'm not exaggerating — a sum that would have funded ten expeditions, in exchange for surrendering our notes and signing a statement that the valley contained ruins of no archaeological value. Ruins. Of no value. They didn't even change the wording from the old survey." His mouth twisted. "We refused. And we understood then that whatever we carried out might never arrive. So we stayed. We're copying everything. Every knot-cord, every panel, every stone — photographed, sketched, transcribed where we have speakers who can read them. The originals stay hidden here, where the mountains can keep them. The copies come out, scattered, in many hands, so no one purchase can bury them again."

"How long?" Sefu asked. His first words in an hour.

"We estimated six more weeks to finish the record properly."

Sefu shook his head once, a small, final motion. "The snow will close the pass in less than half that. And the monsoon is late and fat behind us. When it breaks, the river will rise and nothing goes up or down for months."

"Then we work faster," Daniel said.

"Or someone leaves now," Edmund said.

Everyone turned to her. She stood, and the compass sat in her palm between them like a third party at the negotiation.

"You said it yourself — copies in many hands. Fine. But copies sitting in this valley are copies that can be burned, or buried, or waited out. Someone has to carry the first set down *now*, ahead of the snow and ahead of whatever Vane is doing behind us. Someone with a boat." She looked at Daniel, and her voice did not shake, though she felt the shaking underneath. "My boat. The boat your father's money built and my father's silence paid for. It seems fitting it should carry the truth down instead."

Daniel studied her for a long moment. "You understand what you're agreeing to. If you take those copies downriver, you become the thing they can't buy quietly. They'll have to destroy you openly or lose everything."

"I've been owned by that family's paper for fifteen years," Edmund said. "I'd like to find out what I'm worth free."

Tomas made a sound that was half laugh, half cheer, and clapped his uncle on the shoulder. Ines was already flipping to a fresh page, sketching the valley's layout, listing what should be copied first. Sefu had turned to study the sky over the western peaks, reading it the way Edmund read water.

But Daniel hadn't answered yet. He was looking at the compass in her hand, and his face had gone pale again — paler than at the reunion, paler than when he'd seen the initials.

"Edmund," he said quietly. "Before you decide anything — there's something else in the cairn letters. Something my father wrote to yours, near the end. I think you need to read it tonight. Because the debt you've been carrying all these years—" He stopped, and started again. "It isn't what you think it is. It was never what you thought it was."

Below them, the valley fires burned on, patient as they had been for five hundred years, waiting for someone to finally carry the truth down to the water.
