## 20. The Choice of Staying

The valley did not want them to leave.

Edmund felt it in the way the light lay across the terraces at dusk, in the way the smoke rose straight and unhurried from the hearth-stones below, as though the air itself had agreed to hold still. For eleven days they had been fed, questioned, recorded, and welcomed — and every kindness carried the same unspoken clause beneath it, the way a river carries silt: *stay.*

Daniel Halvorsen made the ask plainly, at last, on the twentieth evening. He did it the way he seemed to do everything — standing, hands loose at his sides, in the long house where the expedition had spread its work across trestle tables like a surgeon laying out instruments. Lamplight moved over the pages. Ines sat among them with her sleeves rolled, sketching a wall of glyphs by the hour, her pencil worn to a stub. Tomas was asleep against a grain sack, his uncle's hat still on his head, tipped down over his eyes like a boy hiding from morning.

"Through the winter," Daniel said. "That's what I'm asking. All of you. The record is half done. Half done, Captain, and the rest of it lives in the heads of people who are old, and in walls that won't survive another generation of rain. If we go now, we come back to less than we have tonight. If we stay, we finish it. We finish it *properly.*"

Edmund stood at the edge of the lamplight with her arms folded, and let the silence do its work before she answered.

"You've been here how long?" she said. "Since the spring?"

"Since the thaw."

"And in all that time, how many boats came up behind you? How many letters came down?"

He didn't answer. He didn't need to. She watched the knowledge move through him like cold water through a hull — slow, then all at once.

"None," she said. "That's the answer. No one came up, and nothing came down. You've been eleven days silent from the river's point of view, and from this valley's point of view you've been the whole world since the ice went out. That's what a winter in here does to a person. It makes the walls of the valley the walls of the world."

"The work—"

"The work will keep." She said it gently, and was surprised to find she meant it gently. She had come up this river expecting to hate this man, or at least to resent him — the son of the family that held her boat in its ledger, the heir to a debt that had eaten eleven years of her life. Instead she had found a man with river mud to his knees and ink to his elbows, who looked at her father's compass like it was a relic from a church that had burned down. Hating him had turned out to be a door that wouldn't open from either side.

"The work will keep," she said again. "The people won't."

She crossed to the table where the valley's own maps lay — the ones Ines had redrawn from the elders' descriptions, the river rendered as it had been before the reservoir, before the drowned village, before the water climbed the hillsides and swallowed the low fields whole. She put her finger on the blue ink at the valley's mouth.

"Monsoon comes late this year. Sefu says the birds are wrong, the river's running cold for the season. When it comes, it'll come heavy — you can smell it in the wind off the pass, even up here. And when it comes, that gorge below us turns into something that doesn't negotiate. I've run that river eleven years. I've seen it take boats with thirty years of river-sense in their hulls. When the flood cuts this valley off, it doesn't cut it off for a week. It cuts it off for the season."

"So we stay, and we work, and in the spring—"

"In the spring, the people down there—" she tapped the terraces, the hearth-smoke, the fields the maps showed in careful brown and green, "—have eaten through whatever they laid in for a winter that was supposed to end with the thaw and a trading run to the low river. Except there's been no trading run, has there? Not since your expedition came up and the route went quiet. Not since Vane's people started asking questions in the river towns and every captain with sense stopped running this water. They planned their winter around a boat that was coming. Your boat. The one that never arrived."

Daniel's face went still. It was the stillness of a man doing arithmetic he didn't want to finish.

"The salt," he said quietly. "The oil. The iron for the tools. The medicine — old Marta's lungs, the children's fevers last month. We've been drawing on their stores since we arrived. We've been *guests*."

"You've been eleven extra mouths for five months, and generous ones, I'll grant you, but mouths all the same. And now you're asking the whole valley to carry a winter's worth of guests through to spring so the record can be finished in comfort." She shook her head. "I didn't come all this way to watch a valley starve politely around a finished archive."

* * *

They took the argument outside, because some arguments are too large for a room.

The night was high and clean and cold at the edges, the way mountain nights are even in the wet season's approach. Stars stood over the ridge in the hard, unwinking way they never managed down at the river, where the haze softened everything. Below, the terraces stepped down into darkness, and here and there a hearth-glow marked a house, a family, a life that had gone on in this hidden fold of the world for longer than either of their families had been keeping ledgers.

"You don't understand what it is we've found," Daniel said. Not angry. Worse — pleading. "My father spent thirty years financing expeditions and never once went up a river himself. He bought other men's courage and put his name on their maps. I came up here to do one thing that was *mine*, and what I found is—" He stopped, started again. "There are tablets in that archive older than any language on the low river. There's a record of a treaty — an actual treaty, signed, witnessed — between the valley peoples and the first river traders, and it says what was promised to them. It says what my father's generation took. Edmund, this valley is the *memory* of the whole river, and it's dying by inches, and I am the first person in a hundred years with the means and the reason to write it down before it goes."

"I know," Edmund said. "I believe you. Every word."

"Then how can you ask me to leave it half-finished?"

"Because I'm not asking you to leave it." She turned to face him fully. "I'm asking you to let some of it go downriver *now*, with me, ahead of the water. Copies. The treaty. The testimony of the eldest. The maps. Enough that if the worst happens — if the archive burns, if the valley's sealed till spring, if anything at all goes wrong the way things go wrong on rivers — the truth is already out. Already in the hands of a judge, a newspaper, someone who can make the reservoir people stop and *think*. You want to protect this valley? The strongest wall you can build for it isn't stone. It's a story too public to bury."

Daniel was quiet a long moment. Below them, a dog barked once, far down the terraces, and fell silent as though embarrassed.

"You'd carry it," he said. "Through the pass. Ahead of the snow and ahead of the flood."

"With the crew I brought up. Ines, Tomas, Sefu. And whatever of your people will come — the ones who can walk fast and carry heavy and don't mind being cold."

"And if the pass closes on you? If the snow catches you in the high country? I lose the record *and* the people who carried it."

"Then you lose them doing the one thing that actually keeps this valley alive," Edmund said. "You're a scholar, Daniel. I'm a boat captain. We're both in the business of reading water — you've just been reading the wrong kind. Downriver, the water that matters is the flood, and the flood has a schedule, and the schedule doesn't care how important your archive is. Somebody has to be on the low river when it opens the trading season. Somebody has to bring the salt up. Somebody has to stand in front of the men who drew the reservoir lines on their maps and say, *I have seen what you drowned, and here are the names of the drowned.* If nobody does that, then in ten years there'll be a beautiful finished archive in a valley with no one left in it, and a reservoir with a new name on it, and everyone will call it progress."

The words came out of her with more heat than she'd planned, and she heard, underneath them, eleven years of her own grievance — the drowned village, the old woman who knew her compass, the boat bought with money that had come, in the end, from the same hand that held the pen that drew the waterline. She had not climbed this mountain to be generous. She had climbed it to be paid. Somewhere between the drowned village and the cairn of letters, the payment had changed currency, and now here she stood, spending it.

Daniel looked at her for a long time.

"My father wrote me a letter once," he said. "The year I told him I was going upriver instead of into the firm. He wrote that a man who owns a boat is a man who owns his own decisions, and that I'd understand that someday when I had something worth deciding about." He laughed, short and without much humor. "I thought it was just him being a banker. But you — you've been deciding with that boat your whole life, haven't you. And now you're deciding against me."

"I'm deciding *for* the valley," Edmund said. "You're deciding for the archive. They're not the same thing, however much we want them to be."

* * *

The crew gathered at first light, because word traveled fast in a valley of forty people and everyone had heard the captains arguing by moonlight.

They met on the flat stone by the expedition's main camp, where the mules were picketed and Sefu stood, as he always stood, slightly apart, watching the sky with the expression of a man reading a document he did not like. Ines came with her satchel already packed — Edmund saw that and felt something in her chest loosen and tighten at once. Tomas came with the hat. Daniel's scholars came in ones and twos, drawn like moths, and the valley people came too, at a respectful distance, the eldest leaning on his staff with two of the young guides flanking him.

"Four days," Sefu said, without preamble, without greeting. He said it the way he said everything, as though reporting the position of a rock in fog. "Maybe five. The pass holds four days, no more. After that, the snow comes down and does not ask permission."

"Then we go tomorrow," Edmund said. "Today, if the copies can be made by noon."

"Made?" Ines was already unrolling her satchel on the flat stone — and there it was, and Edmund could have kissed her for it. Weeks of copying, done in the evenings by lamplight, done without being asked, done because Ines understood, the way Edmund understood currents, that the river always takes back what you fail to carry through. "The treaty, the eldest's testimony, the maps, the glyph transcriptions through the third chamber. Two full sets. One for the judge in the river city, one for the newspaper. Daniel, I can leave you the originals and my field notes."

Daniel stood among them looking like a man watching his own expedition divide into two expeditions, neither of them quite the one he'd planned.

"You'd take the valley guides down too," he said. "Through the pass. In the snow."

"The eldest wants to come," Tomas said. He said it carefully, watching Daniel's face. "He told Sefu last night. He says a testimony that arrives without the man who gave it is worth half. He says—" and here Tomas hesitated, because he was sixteen and repeating an old man's words and knew their weight, "—he says he has been waiting fifty years for someone to carry his voice to the river, and he is not going to let a girl with a boat do it alone."

Edmund looked at the eldest. The old man's eyes were clouded but steady, and when he caught her looking he inclined his head, slow and formal, the gesture of one captain acknowledging another.

"Four days," Sefu said again. "The pass holds four days. After that, the snow decides."

Edmund turned to Daniel one last time. Below them the valley lay in its morning smoke, terraces gold-edged with sun, the whole hidden world holding its breath.

"Stay if you must," she said. "Finish it if you can. But I'm taking the truth downriver before the water takes the road. Someone has to be there when the flood comes — and it won't be the archive. It'll be us."

Daniel looked out over his valley, his archive, his half-finished life's work, and Edmund watched him weigh it — really weigh it, the way she weighed a bar against a falling gauge. Then he straightened, and something in his face settled into a decision she suspected would take him the whole winter to finish making.

"Give me until noon," he said. "And Captain — take my sister's letters with you. If the pass closes on anyone this winter, it will not close on the truth."

Behind them, far down the gorge, the wind changed, and Sefu turned his face into it and frowned, and Edmund felt the first cold edge of it on her own neck — a wind that smelled of snow, arriving four days early.
