## 21. Sefu's Warning

Sefu had learned to read the sky before he could read his own name.

His grandmother taught him on the high pastures above the river basin, when he was small enough to ride a mule and too restless to sit still for the lessons the mission school offered. She would point her chin at the horizon — she never used her hand, hands were for work — and say, *There. What do you see?* And Sefu, squinting into the glare, would see nothing but blue, or nothing but cloud, and say so. And she would click her tongue in disappointment and tell him to look again, look with his whole body, not just his eyes. The sky was not a picture. It was an animal. It breathed. It turned before it moved, the way a man turns his shoulder before he throws.

He stood now at the eastern lip of the valley, where the terraces fell away into morning mist, and looked with his whole body.

The clouds over the pass were wrong.

They had been building for two days — great white towers that rose through the middle of the morning and leaned eastward like men listening at a door. That was monsoon behavior, but it was early for it, weeks early, and the air carried a weight that Sefu did not like. He tasted it at the back of his throat: iron and green rot, the smell of rain that has not yet decided where to fall. And beneath it, thinner, almost shy, another smell. The cold smell. The smell that came down off the glacier fields ahead of snow, the way a rumor comes down off a mountain ahead of trouble.

Two weathers, walking toward each other along the same trail. And between them, four days of open pass. Maybe five if the mountain was generous. The mountain was rarely generous.

Behind him, footsteps on the terrace path — the cartographer woman, Ines, moving with that quick deliberate stride of hers, sketchbook already open against her forearm as though the morning itself might escape before she could record it.

"You're up before the birds," she said.

"The birds are late." He nodded at the eastern wall of the valley, where the peaks showed their heads above the cloud deck, white and indifferent. "Look there. Above the notch."

Ines shaded her eyes. "Cloud?"

"Snow-cloud. It doesn't build the way rain builds. It flattens. It spreads itself thin along the ridge like flour on a board." He let her look. She was one of the few among them who bothered to learn, who asked him questions about the trail and wrote his answers down as though his words were worth ink. "Four days. Maybe five. After that, the pass closes, and it stays closed until the dry season. No one crosses. Not guides. Not goats."

Ines lowered her hand slowly. "You're certain."

"I am never certain." He said it without false modesty; it was simply the truth, and the truth was the only thing he had ever been willing to trade in. "The sky is certain. I am only its translator. And this morning the sky is saying what I have heard it say before, twice, and both times I listened, and both times I lived." He turned to face her fully. "Tell your captain. Tell Halvorsen. Today is not a day for deciding slowly."

* * *

The expedition's council convened in the long house near the central terrace, under a roof of split bamboo and thatch that the valley people had raised for their guests three weeks ago, back when three weeks had seemed like a generous allowance of time.

Sefu sat near the doorway, as he always did. Doorways were for people who needed to leave quickly, and Sefu had made a life's discipline of needing to leave quickly.

Daniel Halvorsen stood at the head of the room beside the great cedar chests that held the archive — the plates, the folios, the wax-cylinder recordings, the bundles of bark-paper documents that the valley's keepers had entrusted to the expedition's care. He was a tall man gone lean from the altitude, with his father's heavy brow and none of his father's heaviness of manner. When he spoke, he spoke softly, and everyone leaned in to hear him, which Sefu suspected was a trick he had learned young.

"We've made copies," Daniel was saying. "Half the archive is duplicated — Ines's photographs, the transcriptions, the sound records. The other half should take another ten days, twelve at most if we all work."

"Twelve days kills you." Edmund Joubert stood opposite him across the fire pit, arms crossed, her voice carrying the flat certainty of someone stating the depth of water over a bar. Sefu had watched her for weeks now, the way he watched all captains, and he had come to respect the economy of her. She wasted nothing — not words, not motion, not worry. But this morning the worry was there anyway, banked like a coal under ash. "I'm telling you what Sefu told me an hour ago, and Sefu reads that sky better than any instrument you brought up here. Four days. Five. Then the pass shuts and every one of you winters here."

"And if we do?" One of the scholars — Okafor, the linguist, a gentle rumpled man who had spent six years learning a language no one else alive spoke — spread his hands. "We winter here. We finish the work properly. Come spring, we carry out everything, complete and undamaged. Isn't that better than racing out with half a record?"

"It would be," Edmund said, "if you were the only ones who had to survive the winter. You'd be eating these people's stores. Their grain, their salt, their dried meat — stores they need themselves, because your financier's reservoir has already drowned half their trading routes downriver. You'd be asking them to feed you through five months of snow so you can finish writing down how their grandparents were betrayed." She let that sit. The fire cracked. "I've run supply boats my whole life. I know what a hungry village looks like in the last month before the boats can reach it. I won't be the reason this one looks like that."

"They've offered," Okafor said quietly.

"They've offered because they are generous, and because they remember what happened the last time outsiders took from them while calling it help." Edmund's jaw tightened. Sefu saw Daniel flinch, very slightly, at that — the leader of the expedition carrying a guilt he had not yet found a way to set down.

Sefu chose that moment to speak, because the argument had reached the place where words begin to harden into positions, and positions were harder to move than stones.

"The captain speaks truly, and so does the scholar," he said. Both heads turned to him. He kept his voice level, unhurried, the voice he used for frightened clients and frightened mules alike. "But you are both arguing about the wrong thing. You argue about days. The days are not yours to argue about." He rose, slowly, his knees announcing their opinions, and walked to the doorway. "Come outside. All of you. If you want to vote on the weather, at least look at the ballot."

* * *

They gathered on the terrace in the strengthening light, twenty-odd souls — the expedition, Edmund's crew, and a handful of the valley's keepers who had drifted over out of curiosity or courtesy. Sefu pointed east with his chin.

"You see the notch between the two high peaks. That is the pass. Yesterday it was clear until midday. Today the cloud sits on it already, and it is the wrong kind of cloud — flat, gray-white, hugging the rock. That is snow-cloud. Behind it, further than you can see, the monsoon is gathering in the low country. I can smell it." He tapped his own chest, below the collarbone. "Here. Rain coming up the river valleys, warm and heavy, and when it meets the cold air off the glaciers—" he clapped his hands once, sharply, and several people started— "the mountain makes its own storm. Wind like you would not believe. Snow sideways. Men die within shouting distance of shelter because they cannot see the shelter."

Tomas, the boy, asked the question the others were too proud to ask: "How do you know? Really know?"

Sefu looked at him and found, as he often did, that the honest answer was the easiest one. "My grandmother taught me, and her mother taught her, and their people crossed this range for two hundred years before anyone drew a map of it. Twice in my life I ignored what I am telling you now. The first time, I lost three mules and nearly lost my brother. The second time, I lost the brother." He said it plainly, without performance, and watched the boy go still. "So. Four days the pass holds. Perhaps five. On the sixth day, I will not lead anyone through it, and no one else will either, because I am the only guide here who knows the goat trails, and I will not go."

That last sentence landed in the group like a stone in a well. Sefu heard it settle. He had thought carefully about saying it, and he did not regret it. A guide who would not swear to his own limits was worth less than no guide at all.

Daniel broke the silence. "If we leave in four days, we leave with half the archive copied."

"If you stay past five," Sefu answered, "you leave in spring with all of it, and the valley spends its winter feeding you, and the river rises behind us and cuts off the supplies these people were promised." He glanced at Edmund. "And the captain is right about one thing more. The monsoon is late and heavy. When it comes up that river, it will come like a fist. Any boat sitting at the landing below will have a bad time of it — or a worse one."

"My boat," Edmund said simply, "is at the landing below."

* * *

The council fractured the way councils do — not cleanly, not along any line a stranger could draw.

Okafor argued for staying, and he was not alone. Two of the younger scholars sided with him, and one of them, a sharp-faced woman named Adeyemi who had given more hours to the archive than anyone, said with real anger: "We did not climb for three weeks and bury a colleague's blistered feet in this valley to walk away with *half*. Half a testimony is no testimony. Half a map is a lie drawn carefully."

Against them stood Edmund, unmoved as a mooring post, repeating the arithmetic of mouths and months. Ines wavered between the camps — Sefu watched her do it, her loyalty pulled toward the work she loved and her judgment pulled toward the woman who had talked her across a cliff-face in a storm. Tomas said little, but Sefu noticed the boy standing closer to Edmund as the argument wore on, the way a nail drifts toward a magnet.

And Daniel Halvorsen stood in the middle of it, being torn in half with great politeness.

It was the valley's eldest who ended the argument, though she did it without appearing to enter it. Mama Adisa — the keepers called her that, and the title had transferred to the visitors by osmosis — came slowly across the terrace on her stick, waited with infinite patience until the arguing voices ran down like clocks, and then spoke in her own language, which Sefu translated haltingly, sentence by sentence, feeling the weight of each one.

She said: *The strangers argue about whether to take the whole pot or half the pot. They do not ask whose pot it is.*

Silence.

She said: *The papers are ours. The songs are ours. We gave them to these guests because our children's children must know what was done, and old women's memories drown with them. If the guests must choose between taking half now and all of it never, then take half. A story that escapes is worth more than a story that is perfect and buried.*

Then she looked at Daniel Halvorsen for a long moment, and added something softer, something Sefu translated with care because he could hear the care in it:

*And she says: the young leader's father promised this valley a road and gave it a flood. Do not become your father, who also meant well, and also stayed too long.*

Daniel closed his eyes. When he opened them, whatever war had been running behind his face had resolved into something quieter and harder.

"Three days," he said. "Not four. We work three days, day and night, rotating crews, and we copy everything that can be copied by hand or plate. On the third dawn we strike camp. Whoever wants to stay and winter can stay — the valley has offered, and I won't order anyone down that pass who isn't willing." His eyes went around the circle. "But I'm going. With whatever fits in the loads. And I'd like every one of you with me."

Adeyemi opened her mouth. Edmund got there first — not loudly, but with the particular authority of a person who has counted lives for a living.

"Three days works," she said. "Barely. Sefu?"

Sefu read the sky once more, with his whole body. The flat cloud on the notch. The iron taste in the wind. Somewhere far south, the monsoon turning over in its sleep.

"Three days," he said, "if we leave at first light on the third, and if the mountain does not change its mind. Mountains change their minds." He picked up his stick. "Now stop talking and start copying. Every hour you spend standing here is a page you will not carry out."

* * *

They worked, after that, the way drowning people swim.

Sefu spent the day doing the work no one thanked him for: preparing. He checked the loads, weighed the food, argued with the mule-men about which animals were sound enough for scree and which would fail at altitude. He walked the first mile of the descent trail and back, reading the ground the way he had read the sky, and came back frowning.

At dusk he found Edmund at the terrace edge, coiling rope with the automatic precision of her trade, and sat beside her on the low wall without asking leave. Captains and guides, he had found, understood each other's silences.

"Your boat," he said.

"What about it?"

"When the monsoon comes up the gorge, the landing below will flood. Not gently. The water comes up thirty feet in a day, sometimes. Debris — whole trees, going down that current like spears." He watched her hands pause on the rope. "If your boat is tied at the landing when that happens, you will not have a boat anymore."

"I know." She resumed coiling. "I've been knowing since before we climbed. There's nothing to be done about it from up here."

"There is one thing." He nodded back toward the long house, where lamplight was beginning to show, where the copying crews bent over their tables. "Your crew. The boy. The cartographer. If the pass closes with all of you on the wrong side of it, they don't lose a boat. They lose a captain, and the valley loses its only voice that speaks river." He paused. "And I lose the only client I have ever had who asks me questions instead of giving me orders."

Edmund looked at him sidelong. "Is that a compliment, Sefu?"

"It is a warning wearing a compliment's clothes. My grandmother's specialty." He stood, knees complaining. "Three days. First light. And captain — when we descend, we descend fast and we do not stop for anything that is not bleeding. Say it back to me."

"Fast," Edmund said. "Nothing that isn't bleeding."

Below them, far down in the dark where the river ran, thunder muttered — the first word of a long sentence the monsoon had begun to speak. Sefu listened to it the way he listened to everything, with his whole body, and did not like the grammar at all.

Three days. He had given his word on three days, and the sky had agreed to it, provisionally, the way the sky agreed to anything.

He only hoped the mountain had been listening too.
