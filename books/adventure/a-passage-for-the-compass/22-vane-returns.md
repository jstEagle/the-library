## 22. Vane Returns

Daniel Halvorsen had learned, in the eleven months since he led his expedition into the valley the maps omitted, to measure time by smoke. The morning fires of the terraced houses rose thin and blue against the eastern cliffs; the midday cooking smoke hung heavier, greasy with maize and river fish; and in the evening the forges of the archive-builders sent up a bitter thread that smelled of hot copper and pine pitch. He had come to love these smokes the way other men loved church bells. They meant the valley was alive. They meant the work continued.

So when he stepped out of the scriptorium on the fourth morning after Sefu's warning and saw no smoke at all rising from the lower terraces — only a gray smear drifting wrong-ways across the river bend, downwind of the gorge mouth — his stomach clenched before his mind caught up.

He found Sefu already standing on the overlook above the corn terraces, one hand shading his eyes, the other resting on the strap of the field glasses slung across his chest.

"You see it," Daniel said. It wasn't a question.

"Three hours ago it was birds." Sefu passed him the glasses without looking away from the bend. "Now it is not birds."

Daniel raised them. The morning light came hard off the water, and for a long moment he could make out nothing but the white glare and the green wall of the forest below the ridge. Then the glare shifted, and he saw them: a line of men moving along the old trade path on the far bank, single file, unhurried, the way men move who have paid for the right to be unhurried. Eight of them. Nine. Porters behind with loads wrapped in oilcloth, and at the head of the file a figure in a pale linen coat that no mountain trail had any business seeing, spotless still, as if the man wearing it refused on principle to sweat.

"He tracked us," Daniel said. "Through the narrows. Through the pass."

"He did not track *us*." Sefu took the glasses back. His voice was flat as slate. "He tracked the radio. I told you the boy was right to break it."

Tomas had broken it — Vane's set, seized from his pack the night they stranded him at the mission — but Daniel had watched Edmund weigh the wrecked thing in her hands and say quietly that a man like Vane did not carry one radio. A man like Vane carried arrangements. And arrangements, once made, did not need wires.

Below them, the valley went about its morning. Women were coming down to the terraces with seed baskets balanced on their heads, their calls to one another echoing off the cliff walls in a language older than any alphabet in the archive. Children ran the irrigation channels, opening and closing the little gates with the ease of habit. An elder sat in the shade of the great fig tree by the stair, teaching two boys the knot-script, their fingers moving through the corded patterns the way Daniel's own fingers had once moved through piano scales — thoughtlessly, beautifully, as if the knowledge lived in the hands before it ever reached the mind.

Eleven months of work. Forty thousand pages photographed, transcribed, argued over. A civilization's whole memory of itself, pulled out of sealed chambers and cedar chests and the recitations of the eldest, and laid down in ink and silver salts so that no flood, no reservoir, no company man with a ledger could ever again claim these people had never existed.

And now a man in a clean linen coat was walking up the trade path toward all of it, carrying what Daniel knew would be an offer.

"We should meet him at the boundary stair," Sefu said. "Not let him past the fig tree. The elders will want to decide how he is received."

"The elders will want to feed him," Daniel said. "That's what hospitality means here. You feed the stranger first and ask his business second."

"And you will tell them not to?"

Daniel didn't answer. He watched the file of men reach the footbridge over the side channel and stop, the porters setting down their loads with the practiced care of men who expected to pick them up again soon. The figure in linen stood apart, consulting something — a watch, a notebook, perhaps nothing at all, merely performing deliberation for the benefit of anyone watching from the heights. Vane had always understood theater. It was, Daniel suspected, the only language he truly spoke fluently.

* * *

They received him at the boundary stair because there was no way not to. By the time Daniel, Sefu, Ines, and three of the valley's elders reached the fig tree, half the settlement had drifted after them, drawn by the same instinct that draws people to a fire or a fight. The eldest herself came last, leaning on the arm of a granddaughter, moving with the terrible slow dignity of a woman who had buried every promise this river had ever been given.

Vane climbed the stair alone. He had left his hired men at the bottom — a calculated courtesy, Daniel noted, or a calculated message: *I don't need them for this part.* Up close, the linen coat was immaculate, the boots beneath it polished to a shine that mocked the mud of the trail, and his face wore the same mild, reasonable expression Daniel remembered from the survey office in the river city, from the contract signing, from every moment Vane had ever stood between two parties holding out his hand as if he were the bridge between them rather than the toll.

"Dr. Halvorsen." He inclined his head. "You're looking well. Mountain air agrees with you."

"Mr. Vane." Daniel kept his voice level. "You've walked a long way to trespass."

"Trespass." Vane smiled as if tasting the word. "On land whose legal status, I assure you, is under active review by three ministries. But we needn't begin with lawyers. May I?" He gestured toward the fig tree, toward the shade, toward the elders watching him with faces carved from patience.

The eldest spoke before anyone could answer — in her own tongue first, then, slowly, in the trade speech she had learned from missionaries sixty years dead. "A guest eats before he speaks. That is the law of this valley." Her eyes, milky at the edges, fixed on Vane with unsettling precision. "But you are not a guest. A guest comes empty-handed or bearing gifts. You come bearing paper. So we will hear your paper, and then you will go."

Something flickered behind Vane's mildness — the briefest recalculation, a man revising a forecast. Then the smile returned, warmer if anything.

"Then I'll be direct, madam, with your leave." He drew a leather folio from inside his coat and held it, unopened, almost reverently. "I represent the Consolidated River and Irrigation Company, and I speak with full authority. Dr. Halvorsen, your expedition was funded by my employer to conduct a survey. That survey has been declared concluded. Its findings — all notes, photographs, maps, and copies thereof — revert to the company under clause nine of your charter, which I have here, witnessed and stamped."

"My charter," Daniel said, "was signed by a man who told me he wanted the upper basin mapped before the dam raised the water. Not erased."

"History is full of generous misunderstandings." Vane opened the folio at last, and Daniel saw the documents within: crisp, typed, ribboned, obscene in their neatness. "But let me not waste the good doctor's time with clauses. Here is the substance." He turned the folio toward the elders as well, including them in the gesture with a showman's grace. "The company offers — and I am authorized beyond anything in your wildest arithmetic — one hundred and twenty thousand for the complete archive. The originals, the copies, the plates, the notebooks. Everything gathered here. In exchange: silence, cooperation, and a future. The company will fund a school. A clinic. A road, eventually, when the terrain permits. Your valley will enter the modern century with patrons instead of enemies."

Ines made a small sound beside Daniel — not quite a laugh. He glanced at her. Her jaw was tight, her sketchbook clutched to her chest like a shield, and her eyes had gone to the porters' oilcloth bundles at the foot of the stair. Daniel followed her gaze and felt the cold arrive in his gut. One of the bundles was the wrong shape for supplies. Too long. Too rigid.

Surveying instruments. Or something worse.

"And if we decline?" Daniel asked.

Vane closed the folio. For a moment — just a moment — the mildness dropped entirely, and Daniel saw the thing underneath: not cruelty exactly, but a vast, patient indifference, the indifference of an accountant watching a column refuse to balance. "Then the company proceeds under its existing mandate. There is a reservoir expansion scheduled — you know this; your captain knows this better than anyone. The surveys required for expansion will, naturally, cover this entire basin. Every terrace, every ruin, every chamber of cedar and cord. And a survey, Dr. Halvorsen, is a funny instrument. It finds what it is instructed to find. A valley can be surveyed as a living community of four hundred souls with an unbroken memory going back a thousand years. Or it can be surveyed as uninhabited seasonal terrain with archaeological residue of negligible significance. The difference between those two reports is a matter of staffing. And staffing—" he spread his clean hands— "is a matter of budget. Which brings us back, circularly, to my offer."

"You're threatening to erase them," Ines said. Her voice shook, but she did not look away. "On paper. You'll write them out of existence and then drown the paper."

"I'm offering," Vane corrected gently, "to do the opposite. Paper is precisely what protects people, Miss Reyes. Ask any nation on a map. The company's paper can make this valley permanent, protected, commemorated — a heritage site with a plaque and a pension fund. Or its absence can make the valley a rounding error. I am, believe it or not, the most honest man who will ever stand in this square and offer you power. Everyone else who comes after me will simply lie."

The eldest had listened to all of it translated in a low murmur by the granddaughter, her face unmoving. Now she stirred, and the crowd quieted, and even Vane turned toward her with the wariness of a man who has met one too many audiences that wouldn't behave.

She spoke in her own tongue, long and deliberate, and the granddaughter's voice trembled as she rendered it:

"She says: sixty years ago, men came with paper also. They promised the water would never touch the high fields. She was a girl; she helped carry the boundary stones they set with their instruments. The stones are underwater now. The high fields are underwater now. The village where she was born is underwater now." The granddaughter paused, steadied herself. "She says: you offer us a plaque. We already have a memory. She says: take your paper and go, before we remember what our grandmothers did to surveyors."

A murmur ran through the assembled valley folk — not anger, exactly. Something older and more frightening than anger: recognition.

Vane absorbed it without visible damage. He tucked the folio away, bowed slightly to the eldest — actually bowed, Daniel noted, a genuine flourish, wasted on an audience that couldn't be bought — and turned back to Daniel with his voice pitched low, private, just for the two of them beneath the fig tree's shade.

"A word between professionals, Doctor. Your father financed this expedition out of sentiment, and sentiment, as you've discovered, makes poor collateral. I hold certain instruments — debts, obligations, a certain boat's title — and I hold testimony from a certain captain regarding events at the mission station that could be construed as abandonment of a contracted agent. The company's lawyers are imaginative. Cooperate, and none of that machinery ever starts. Refuse—" he glanced up at the terraces, at the smoke that had finally begun to rise again from the evening fires, thin and blue and defiant— "and I will return with better instruments than paper. The monsoon is late, gentlemen. Rivers rise. Expeditions get cut off. People get lost. It happens every year, and no one is ever quite to blame."

He straightened his coat. "You have until the water rises. Which, if my meteorologists are correct, is sooner than your guide believes. Consider it my gift: accurate information, freely given. It's the only thing I've offered today that costs me nothing."

* * *

That night the council fire burned in the great house until the small hours, and Daniel sat through it listening to arguments he could not resolve. Some of the younger men wanted to take the money — not for themselves, they insisted, but because a clinic was a clinic, and memory could survive alongside patronage if one were careful. The archivists said careful was a word that belonged to the people who wrote the contracts. Edmund Joubert said nothing for a long time, sitting apart with the compass open in her palm, and when she finally spoke it was only to ask Sefu about the sky.

"Four days," Sefu said, looking up through the smoke hole at stars being steadily eaten by cloud. "Maybe five. After that, the pass belongs to winter, and the river belongs to the rain, and we belong to neither."

Near dawn, Daniel stepped outside into the wet dark and found Tomas awake, sitting on the terrace wall with his uncle's hat — the hat found in the abandoned camp, brim frayed, sweat-stained, unmistakable — turning slowly in his hands.

"He'll come back with the men, won't he," Tomas said. Not a question either. The boy had stopped asking questions sometime around the broken bridge; now he only stated things, and waited to see who would argue.

"Yes."

"Then we should be gone before he does." Tomas looked up, and in the light spilling from the great house Daniel saw that the boy's face had gone hard in a way that no fourteen-year-old's should have to. "All of us. With everything we can carry. My uncle stayed because he thought the valley was safe behind its walls. Nothing's safe, is it? Not even here."

Daniel started to answer — some reassurance, some adult platitude — and found he had none in stock. Because the boy was right, and because even as he opened his mouth, the first fat drops of the late monsoon struck the terrace stones around them, one, then three, then a hundred, hissing on the warm rock like something waking up.

Above the valley, thunder rolled along the cliffs, and the river, far below in the dark, began very quietly to rise.
