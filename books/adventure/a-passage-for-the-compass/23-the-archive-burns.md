## 23. The Archive Burns

Ines had learned to read the valley the way she once read tide tables: by what was absent. The terraces told her where water had been persuaded to go. The empty niches in the archive walls told her where tablets had already been carried away, or hidden, or lost to damp centuries before any of them arrived. Absence had a grammar, and she had spent eleven days in the valley learning to parse it.

So when she woke in the third watch of the night with the taste of smoke on her teeth, she knew — before she opened her eyes, before the shouting started — that something absent was about to become something gone.

The fire was in the long gallery, the eastern hall where the oldest shelves stood, the ones Daniel called the first library because they predated every map Ines had ever drawn of this river. She came out of her sleeping alcove into air that tasted of resin and hot stone, and the corridor ahead was lit orange at its far end, flickering like something breathing.

"Water!" someone was shouting, in the valley tongue and then in Spanish and then in no language at all. "Water, water—"

There was no water. That was the thing she understood as she ran, skirts hauled up over her knees, sandals slapping stone. There was no water in the gallery because there was no water anywhere near the archive; the spring ran on the far side of the terrace complex, and the cisterns were for drinking, hoarded against the dry months, and every clay vessel in the eastern hall was itself an artifact, irreplaceable, two thousand years of river-people's hands pressed into its sides. You could not throw a jar of the archive's own history onto the archive's own burning heart. Whoever set the fire had known that too.

She burst through the doorway into heat that shoved her back a step.

Half the gallery was already lost. The western shelves still stood, black silhouettes against the blaze, but the eastern wall — the wall of the founding records, the king-lists, the river-songs scratched in the old script that Sefu could half-read and the eldest could fully read and no one else alive could — was a sheet of flame from floor to lintel. The cedar shelving had caught like it had been waiting a thousand years for permission. And above it all, impossibly, terribly, the smoke rose through a gap in the roof that had been there for centuries, venting upward into the night sky, so that anyone downriver looking up would see nothing but stars.

Whoever built this place had planned for lightning, for damp, for time. Not for men with lamps and oil.

"Ines!" Daniel's voice, raw, behind her. He caught her arm and pulled her back as a shelf collapsed inward, sending up a geyser of sparks. His face was streaked with soot, his eyes wild. "The copies — the copies are in the west room—"

"I know."

"The originals—" He stopped. He couldn't finish it. Neither of them said *the originals are the point*, though both of them thought it, and the thought sat between them like a third person.

They worked. That was the only word for what happened next, and Ines would remember it afterward not as terror but as work: buckets passing hand to hand down a chain of valley people and scholars, wet blankets beaten against doorframes, sand from the terrace paths shoveled onto the spreading edge of the burn. Edmund appeared out of the dark with her sleeves rolled and her hair tied back and fought the fire the way she did everything — without wasted motion, without complaint, reading the fire's current the way she read a river's, finding the places where it wanted to spread and damming them. Tomas was beside her, soot-black, hauling water, his face set in an expression Ines had never seen on him before: not fear. Fury.

It took until the sky went grey at the edges. When it was over, half the eastern gallery was ash and charcoal and the melted ghosts of clay, and the smell would never fully leave the stone, and nine people had burned hands, and one of the valley elders had breathed too much smoke and lay now in the west room while a woman sang over him in a voice that did not expect an answer.

And Vane's men were gone. All four of them, slipped away in the chaos like oil off water, leaving their camp cold and their mules taken.

* * *

They stood in the ruined gallery in the first true light of morning, and the light made it worse. Fire is honest at night; daylight itemizes.

Ines walked the length of the burn line slowly, cataloguing. It was what she was for. Her whole life had trained her for this single terrible morning without her knowing it: the cartographer's discipline of recording loss, of drawing the drowned village shoreline, of marking on a map the things that will not survive the water. She had always believed that marking was a kind of saving. Standing here, she tested that belief against the ash and found it holding, barely, the way a rope bridge holds — frayed, swaying, but holding.

"The king-lists," Daniel said. He was standing in the middle of the destruction, turning a charred fragment over in his bandaged hands. "Eleven reigns. We had transcribed six."

"The river-songs?"

"Some. The eldest knows forty by heart." He laughed, a small broken sound with no humor in it. "Forty songs in one old man's memory, Ines. That was our backup plan. One old man."

"That was always the real archive," she said. "We just didn't want to admit it."

He looked at her then, really looked, and something passed between them that was not comfort exactly but was adjacent to it — the recognition of two people who had both staked their lives on paper and just watched paper fail.

By midmorning the whole settlement had assembled in the west room, the surviving room, where perhaps a third of the collection remained: the later tablets, the trade records, the correspondence with the lowland kingdoms, the astronomical tables. The eldest sat wrapped in wool despite the warmth, his burned lungs making each breath a negotiation, and he listened while Daniel spoke, and then he said something in the valley tongue that Sefu translated in a low voice:

"He says: they burned the house, not the family. He says the house was always going to burn someday. Stone forgets slower than flesh, but it forgets."

"And then?" Ines asked.

Sefu listened to more of it. His face, usually so closed, showed something almost like awe. "Then he says: copy faster."

* * *

They copied through the day and into the night and into the next night, and it became the strangest and most sacred labor Ines had ever been part of.

Daniel organized it like a military operation, which, Ines realized, watching him, was exactly what it was. Every surviving tablet was assigned. Scholars who could read the old script read aloud; those who couldn't wrote; those who could do neither held lamps steady and ground ink and carried messages between rooms. The valley people joined without being asked — a girl of maybe ten who copied the pictographs with astonishing precision, her hand steadier than any scholar's; two brothers who took dictation of the trade records in tandem, one reading numbers, one writing them. Edmund sat for hours transcribing the astronomical tables, her captain's handwriting neat and unornamented, and when Ines paused beside her near midnight of the second day, Edmund looked up with eyes rimmed red from smoke and fatigue and said simply:

"I used to copy cargo manifests at dawn. This isn't so different. Except nobody ever tried to burn the manifests."

"They'll try again," Ines said. It needed saying. "Vane didn't send four men to steal. He sent them to erase. If we leave with copies, we're carrying the thing he paid to destroy."

Edmund's jaw tightened. "Then we'd best be fast."

But speed was the one thing the work refused. The copying could not be rushed without risking error, and an error in the king-lists or the boundary records would be a smaller, quieter version of the fire itself. So they worked at the pace of accuracy, and Ines learned to live inside that tension, the way she had learned to live inside the tension of a river rising — you cannot make the water slow, you can only make yourself precise.

On the second night, near the smallest hours, she found herself alone in the west room with the eldest. Everyone else had finally succumbed to sleep. He should have been resting, with his lungs, but he had insisted, and now he sat before a tablet older than any nation Ines could name, tracing the grooves of the script with one finger, reading aloud in a whisper — not translating, just voicing, letting the sounds exist again in air.

She sat down across from him and began to draw.

Not the tablets. Him. She hadn't sketched a person in years — maps had eaten that habit alive — but her pencil moved without consulting her, and what emerged on the page was the old man's bowed head, his hands on the stone, the lamplight pooling around him like something poured.

After a while he stopped reading and watched her. Then he spoke, slowly, and gestured, and she fetched Sefu from the doorway where he'd been keeping a sleepless watch.

"He asks what you are writing," Sefu said. "All of you. Day and night. He asks why the words matter so much if they must be carried so far to be safe."

Ines considered the question honestly, because it deserved honesty. "Because words are the only boat big enough," she said at last. "Because a thing written down can be argued with, but it cannot be denied. Because somewhere downriver there are people deciding right now whether this valley exists, and if we arrive with nothing in our hands, the valley loses — politely, quietly, in a filing cabinet somewhere."

Sefu translated. The eldest listened, and then he laughed — a dry rustle, painful, genuine. He said something long.

"He says," Sufu began, corrected himself, "he says the river people made this same argument a thousand years ago, when the lowland kings demanded tribute and the valley hid its records instead of paying. He says the records survived the kings. He says—" and here Sefu paused, and Ines saw him choose to translate exactly rather than soften, "—he says the paper people always think they are rescuing us. But the valley has rescued its own memory many times. He asks only that you carry it truly. Not bravely. Truly."

"Not bravely. Truly." Ines wrote it in the margin of her sketch, beneath the old man's portrait, where she would find it years later and feel this night come back to her whole.

* * *

Dawn of the third day found the copies stacked in oilcloth bundles: ninety-one packets, weighed, numbered, and entered in a ledger Ines kept in duplicate, one copy to travel separately from the other in case either was lost. She had insisted on the duplication, and Daniel had resisted — weight, time, mule capacity — until she laid it out for him flatly: *You watched half your life's work burn in one hour. Tell me again how redundancy is a luxury.*

He hadn't answered. He'd started a second ledger himself.

It was while she was weighing the final bundle on the balance scale — grain weights against oilcloth, the same method the valley's ancient traders had used, a continuity that made her throat tight every time she noticed it — that Tomas came to find her.

He moved differently now than he had on the boat. The gorge traverse had done something permanent to him; he stood square in his shoulders, and he looked at adults directly, waiting for them to finish talking before he decided whether they'd said anything worth hearing. He was fifteen going on whatever comes after fifteen when a boy has crossed a cliff on a rotted rope.

"Ines. Can I ask you something about the copying?"

"Anything."

"You numbered everything. Cross-referenced it. Made two of every list." He hesitated. "My uncle does that with specimens. My mother did it with seeds. I'm trying to learn it — the system. Because it seems like the system is what survives. Not the paper. The system." He flushed, suddenly aware he'd said too much, or something too large. "Is that — is that a real thing? Or am I making it up?"

Ines set down the weights and gave the question the seriousness it deserved, which was complete.

"It's the realest thing there is," she said. "Paper burns. Stone crumbles. But a method — a way of remembering that lives in more than one head, in more than one place — that's the only immortality anyone's ever actually managed. Your uncle knows it. Your mother knew it better. And you've just figured it out at fifteen, which took me until my twenties, so I'd thank you not to show me up further."

He grinned, and for a moment he was just a boy again, and then his expression sobered and he said, quietly, "Edmund wants everyone at the overlook at midmorning. She's been up there since before light with Sefu, reading the sky." A pause. "It's bad, isn't it. Whatever she's reading."

"Probably," Ines said. "With Edmund, it usually is. But she reads it early, Tomas. That's the whole trick of her. Other people get weather. Edmund gets warning."

* * *

She climbed to the overlook after him, past the terraces, to the saddle of rock where the trail left the valley and began its climb toward the pass. Edmund stood at its edge with Sefu, both of them facing north, where the mountains wore a new and unwelcome whiteness above the treeline — not cloud, not mist. Snow. Far too early, far too low.

Behind them, to the south, the sky was the wrong color entirely: a bruised, banded purple-green, towering, silent, the monsoon's advance guard building itself over the lowlands like a second mountain range that moved.

Ines came to stand beside Edmund and followed her gaze from one horizon to the other. Snow closing the pass from above. Flood closing the river from below. And the valley, and its ninety-one bundles, and its one old man with forty songs in his chest, caught precisely between.

"Well?" Edmund said, without turning. Her voice was level, but Ines had spent enough nights at her side on the river to hear the current underneath it.

"Tell me the worst of it," Ines said.

Edmund was quiet a moment. When she spoke, she spoke like a woman reading a depth-sounding.

"Sefu gives the pass four days. Maybe five, if the wind stays kind, which it won't. The monsoon hits the lower river in six, seven at the outside — and when it does, the tributaries rise first, and the boat needs three clear days to run down before the flood makes the channel suicide." She turned then, and her eyes were soot-rimmed and absolutely steady. "Which means we don't have a schedule, Ines. We have a vise. And it's closing from both ends."

Below them, the valley smoked gently from its burned gallery, and the morning bells — the ones the expedition had helped rehang — rang thin and clear across the terraces, calling everyone to the work of copying that could not be finished, by people who would very soon have to leave it.

Ines took out her notebook, opened it to a fresh page, and began, as she had been trained to do, as the eldest had asked her to do, to write it all down truly.

The first thing she wrote was the date. The second was: *Four days.*
