## 24. Edmund's Bargain

The fire had burned itself down to a red heart by the time Edmund found Vane.

He had made himself a camp apart from his hired men, at the edge of the terraces where the valley dropped away into cloud, and he sat before his small tidy flame the way he did everything — precisely, deliberately, as though the wilderness were a drawing room he had agreed to tolerate. His coat was brushed. His boots were clean of ash. Only his hands, folded in his lap, betrayed him: they were not quite still.

Edmund came out of the dark without hurrying. She had learned long ago that a man who heard you coming had time to arrange his face, and she wanted Vane's face unarranged.

It worked, for a moment. He looked up and something crossed it — surprise, then a quick calculation, then the smooth settling of the polite mask she had watched him wear since the first day on her deck. But in that moment before the mask closed, she saw what she had come to see. He was afraid. Not of her, exactly. Of the night behind her, and what it held.

"Captain Joubert." He gestured to the flat stone across the fire. "Please."

She didn't sit. She stood with her back to the drop, so the wind off the snowfields moved at her neck and the smoke drifted between them, and she let the silence do its work. Below them, faintly, came the sound of Ines's lamp still burning in the archive hall, and the scratch of pens — the scholars copying through the night, salvaging what the fire had left. Every scratch was a word pulled back from the flames. Edmund made herself listen to it.

"You should be sleeping," Vane said. "Tomorrow will be difficult enough."

"Tomorrow is why I'm here."

"Ah." He turned his hands toward the fire, palms open, a man warming himself who had no need of warmth. "You've come to negotiate. I confess I wondered which of you would find the courage first. Daniel has been circling me all evening like a dog around a snake. Forgive me — that's unkind to the dog."

"I'm not here to trade insults."

"No," he said. "You're here because you think you have something to sell." He looked up at her, and the firelight made his eyes shallow and bright. "Testimony, isn't it? That's what you whispered to Daniel this afternoon, loud enough for my man Ruiz to hear. Clever. You know I listen. You wanted me to know."

Edmund kept her voice level. "Your employer knew what the reservoir would do. Knew the valley was inhabited. Knew there were people living under that water line when they raised it — people with villages, with graves, with a written memory older than any city your company has ever built. The surveys were falsified. The old woman in the drowned village could have told them that, if anyone had asked her instead of paying her to move. I can testify to what she said. To what the compass says. To what's in that archive. And so can Daniel Halvorsen, whose own father helped pay for the drowning."

Vane listened with his head tilted, courteous as a banker hearing a loan request he intended to refuse.

"And your price?" he said.

"You let us walk out of this valley with the copies. All of them. You let the expedition finish its work and carry it down to a judge. No interference. No more fires. No more men in the narrows."

"And in exchange you hold your tongue about my employer until you're safely downriver." He nodded slowly, as though admiring the architecture of it. "A hostage exchange. The truth for the passage. It's almost elegant, Captain. Almost." He rose, unhurried, and stood opposite her across the coals. "But you've made one error, and it's the error everyone makes when they bargain with what they don't own."

"I own my testimony."

"You own nothing." He said it gently, almost kindly, and that gentleness was worse than any threat. "That has rather been the point of your entire adult life, hasn't it? Let me tell you what I own." He reached into his breast pocket and drew out a leather folio, slim and worn at the corners, and opened it with two fingers like a man displaying a winning card. "Do you know what this is?"

She knew. She had known since the smuggler's toll, since the money that flowed too easily from his pockets, since every question he asked about her boat and her route and her debts. But knowing and seeing were different things, and seeing it — the paper itself, creased and stamped and signed — stopped her breath.

"The mortgage on the *Mariana*," Vane said. "Or rather, the debt instrument secured against her, which is the same thing to a court. Eleven years of interest, compounded faithfully. Principal outstanding: a sum you could not earn in twenty seasons of honest work. Signed by Elias Halvorsen, God rest him, and assigned — transferred, Captain, quite legally — to my employer three years ago." He smiled. "I have been carrying it up this mountain in my breast pocket like a love letter. Do you know why?"

Edmund said nothing. Her hands, at her sides, had gone very quiet. That was the trick of terror, she had learned; it didn't always shake you. Sometimes it stilled you completely, the way a river goes glass-still over a bar that will tear your hull out.

"Because a captain who owns her boat is a free agent," Vane said. "And a captain who doesn't — well. A captain who doesn't can be ruined with a signature. One telegram from the river city and the *Mariana* is seized at her moorings, sold at auction, bought by an interested party for a fair price. Your crew scattered. Your livelihood gone. And your testimony—" he tilted his head— "well. Who believes a ruined woman with a grudge? Ruin is such an efficient disinfectant."

The wind moved. Somewhere below, a pen scratched.

"So," Vane went on, folding the folio closed but not putting it away, holding it instead against his chest like a hymnal, "let us speak plainly, since you came for plainness. Here is my offer, and it is generous, and it is the only one you will get. You and your crew go down the mountain tomorrow. The copies stay. The scholars stop copying tonight. Daniel surrenders whatever he means to carry out, and the valley keeps its secret the way valleys keep everything — quietly, under stone, until the water comes or doesn't, which is no longer anyone's concern. In exchange, I give you this." He tapped the folio. "Burned. Forgiven. Free. You walk away whole, Captain. Boat, name, future. Everything you have rowed toward for eleven years."

*Everything.* The word rang in her like a struck bell. Eleven years of low-water seasons and high-water fears, of taking cargo she despised and passengers she distrusted, of lying awake listening to the river count her debt in the slap of water against the hull. Freedom, offered whole, in a slim folder of paper.

And that was how she knew it was poison. Nothing freely given by this man had ever been free. The toll at the smuggler's crossing had been paid in coin that bought suspicion. The kindnesses had all been invoices.

"And the valley people?" she said. "The ones whose grandmothers' words are burning in that hall right now. What do they get?"

"They get to remain undocumented," Vane said, "which has kept them alive this long. Captain, I am not a monster. I want you to understand that. My employer is not a monster. We are men who believe that some knowledge does more harm loose than contained. The world ran on drowned villages long before you were born, and it will run on them long after. Progress has a wake. Everyone in it gets wet. The only question is who pays for the boat." He spread his hands. "You, at least, are being offered payment. Most aren't."

"My testimony isn't mine to sell," Edmund said slowly. "It never was. That's the difference between us, Vane. You think truth is an asset. It's a debt. And I've spent my whole life learning the difference between a debt you owe and a debt you're *told* you owe."

Something flickered in his face — the first true crack she'd seen in it, a hairline fracture running under the polish. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning I'll take my chances." She stepped forward, into the space between the stones, close enough that the heat of the coals touched her knees. "Here is my counter-offer, and it's the only one. Tomorrow we leave with the copies. All of them. Your men stand aside. And when we reach the river city, I testify — not to buy my boat, not to threaten your employer, but because it's true. You can't stop that with paper. You can't stop it with ruin. A woman with nothing left has nothing left to lose, Vane, and you of all people should understand the arithmetic of that. You've been counting on my having something to lose since the day you boarded my deck. So burn the ledger in your head. I'm not trading."

For a long moment Vane simply looked at her. The mask was gone now; what remained was thinner, older, and tired. When he spoke, his voice had lost its drawing-room smoothness.

"You'd gamble the *Mariana*. Eleven years." He shook his head slowly. "No. You won't. I've watched you, Captain. On the river, at the toll, on the cliff traverse — you calculate. Always. You are the most careful gambler I have ever met, and careful gamblers do not stake the only thing they have on a bluff." He lifted the folio slightly. "Call it, then. Right now. Tell me you'll testify even if I put this paper before a judge within the month. Say it, and mean it, and I'll believe you."

The fire cracked. Below, the pens went on scratching, patient as rain.

Edmund thought of the *Mariana* at her moorings in the river city — the paint peeling at the waterline, the wheel he'd rewrapped twice, the engine that started on the third try and only the third try, as if it too had learned caution from its owner. She thought of the deck where Tomas had slept his first nights as a stowaway, where Ines had spread her charts, where Sefu had stood at the rail saying nothing and seeing everything. Eleven years of her life, nailed and caulked and paid for plank by plank with money she hadn't earned yet.

And she thought of the old woman in the drowned village, holding the compass in both hands, calling it by a name Edmund hadn't known, looking at her with eyes that had already forgiven her for something Edmund hadn't done yet.

"Yes," Edmund said.

The word came out steady. She was almost surprised by it.

"I'll testify with the boat or without her. With a name or without one. You can take the *Mariana*, Vane. Sell her. Scuttle her. Put her in your ledger next to the villages you drowned. But you cannot take what I saw in that archive, and you cannot take what I'll say in front of a judge, and every day you spend trying is a day the copies multiply and the witnesses grow. I'm not bluffing. I'm just done being owned."

Vane looked at her for a long time. Then, very quietly, he laughed — a single breath of a laugh, without humor, aimed mostly at himself.

"Do you know," he said, "I had a speech prepared for this. Two speeches. I've been composing them since the toll station." He closed the folio and slid it back into his breast pocket, and Edmund felt the loss of it like a hand closing around her throat — but she kept her face still, because she had learned that too, on the river, in the white water, in the dark. "You're a harder woman than your file suggested."

"Your file on me is wrong."

"It appears so." He sat back down on his stone, abruptly weary, a man setting down a weight no one else could see. "Go to bed, Captain. Tomorrow will be what tomorrow will be. I make no promises and neither, I think, do you."

She turned to go. At the edge of the firelight she stopped, because there was one thing left, and she had come for it as much as for the bargaining.

"The fire," she said, without turning around. "Ruiz set it. We both know it. But someone told him where the dry stores were kept, and someone told him the scholars would be asleep. Someone who'd been inside the hall." She turned her head, just enough. "Was that you, Vane? Or was that your employer's instruction, sent down the wire before you ever climbed this mountain?"

Behind her, the silence stretched so long that the fire had time to settle twice.

"Goodnight, Captain," Vane said.

Which was, she thought, walking away into the cold with the snow wind at her back and the pens still scratching below like insects determined to outlive the winter — which was not a no.

* * *

She didn't go straight to her bunk. She climbed instead to the archive hall, where the lamplight spilled gold across the terrace stones, and found Ines at the long table with her sleeves rolled and ink to her knuckles, copying a fragment whose edges were charred lace.

Ines looked up. Read her face in the way Ines read everything — quickly, completely, without comment.

"He showed you the paper," she said.

"He showed me the paper."

"And?"

"And I called it." Edmund sat down heavily on the bench beside her, and only then did the shaking start, deep in her arms where no one could see it, the delayed tremor of a woman who had staked everything on a single cast and felt the line go taut. "I told him I'd testify whether he takes the boat or not. I meant it, Ines. God help me, I meant it."

Ines set down her pen. For a moment she said nothing, and Edmund braced for argument — for the arithmetic, for the prudence, for *eleven years, Edmund, eleven years.*

What Ines said was: "Then we'd better make sure the copies are good ones."

She slid a finished page across the table. Edmund looked down at it and felt her breath catch. It was a transcription of a letter — one of the letters from the cairn, Daniel's letter home — but beneath it, in a second hand, older and more careful, someone had copied a passage from the valley's own records. A promise, witnessed and sealed, dated the year the reservoir's first survey party came down the river. *The water will not rise above the old landing,* it said. *This is sworn by the undersigned, who speak for the company.*

Three signatures. She recognized one of them. Elias Halvorsen.

Below it, in different ink, added later, cramped into the margin as if the writer had been ashamed to give it room: *The water rose fourteen feet past the landing. The graves went under in the spring.*

Edmund touched the page once, lightly, the way she'd touch a chart marking a reef that had taken better boats than hers.

"Elias signed the promise," she said. "And then he financed the breaking of it. And then he lent me the money to buy a boat, and I've spent eleven years paying him back for—" She stopped. Started again. "For what? For the *Mariana*? Or for all of it?"

"For none of it," said a voice from the doorway.

Daniel Halvorsen stood there, hollow-eyed, smelling of smoke, a bundle of salvaged papers under his arm. He came into the light and set the bundle down with the care of a man handling bones.

"I've been listening at doorways like a coward all evening," he said. "Forgive me. Edmund — Vane holds the debt paper, yes. But he doesn't hold the only paper." He reached into the bundle and drew out a letter, soft with handling, its seal broken long ago. "My father wrote to me the autumn before he died. I found it in his desk after. I brought it up this mountain because I couldn't decide what to do with it." He laid it on the table between them, face up, but he didn't open it. "There are things in it about the loan. About why he really made it. And about you, Edmund — about your father. Things I think you need to read before tomorrow. Before we go down through Vane's men and whatever else he's arranged."

Edmund looked at the letter. The handwriting on its face was strong and slanted, a businessman's hand, and beside the direction it said *Daniel* in ink gone brown with years.

"Tomorrow," she repeated. "What happens tomorrow?"

Daniel glanced toward the dark window, toward the north where the pass lay under its first dusting of white, and his jaw tightened.

"Sefu came in an hour ago," he said. "He's been reading the sky off the western ridge. Edmund — the snow's not four days out anymore. It's two. Maybe less. And there's smoke moving in the trees below the narrows." He met her eyes. "Vane's men aren't waiting for an answer. They're coming up."
