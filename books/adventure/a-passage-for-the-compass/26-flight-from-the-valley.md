## 26. Flight from the Valley

Tomas had never run from anything in his life until the morning they left the valley, and then he learned that running was not what he had always imagined it to be — a wild thing, a panicked thing, arms and legs and lungs all at war. Running, done properly, was arithmetic. It was Sefu counting paces under his breath, Ines checking her watch against the sun, Edmund standing at the head of the column with the compass open in her palm like a small silver heart that beat north. It was the eldest guide, Amara, refusing to be carried yet, insisting on walking the first mile herself with her chin up as though the mountain were a guest she was seeing off.

They left before dawn, in the blue hour when the terraces were still holding the night's cold in their stone walls. The copies of the archive went into oilcloth and then into two leather satchels and then onto the backs of two of the strongest mules, and Tomas walked beside those mules the whole way out of the valley because Daniel had put him there — not beside his uncle, not beside Edmund, but beside the truth itself, as if to say: *you are one of its keepers now.*

Behind them, smoke still rose from the burned half of the archive house. It rose straight up in the windless morning, a gray thread stitching the valley to the sky, and every time Tomas looked back at it he felt the fire again in his chest, the way you feel a slap long after the hand has withdrawn.

"They will come after us," Sefu had said at the last council, in the flat voice he used for things that were simply true. "Not today. Today they will argue among themselves. But Vane's men know these trails now, or enough of them. Two days, maybe three."

"Then we have two days," Edmund said.

"The pass needs four," said Sefu.

Nobody answered that. Nobody needed to. The silence did the arithmetic for all of them, and the sum was ugly, and they shouldered their packs anyway.

* * *

The trail climbed out of the valley through a country that seemed designed by someone who hated travelers. It switchbacked up a shoulder of black rock where the path was barely wider than a mule's hoof, and below them the valley unspooled green and gold and impossibly far away, the river a bright wire laid along its floor. Tomas made himself stop looking down by looking instead at Amara's hands, which gripped a staff of ironwood and moved over the stone like a spider reading a web.

"You walk well," she said to him once, without turning her head. Her voice was dry as seed husks. "For a boy who spent his life on water."

"I fell in the river before I could walk," Tomas said. "My uncle says I swam before I talked."

"He says many things." She smiled, and it rearranged her whole face into something younger. "He is right about this one, I think. Water people make good climbers. You already trust your feet more than your eyes."

That was true, Tomas realized. On the boat, on Edmund's boat, in the flood, in the narrows — you learned fast that your eyes lied about the water but your feet never did. He tested each stone anyway. The mountain was not the river, but it had the same habit of shifting when you stopped respecting it.

By midmorning they reached the tree line, and the world changed color. Below had been green and brown and the gold of ripe grain; above was gray rock and rust-colored lichen and a sky so large and so close it felt less like weather than like attention. And there — first on the highest stones, then on the trail itself, then dusting the shoulders of Ines's pack like flour sifted through a cloth — snow.

It wasn't real snow yet. It was a promise of snow, a rumor, the mountain clearing its throat. But everyone stopped at the same moment and looked at it, and everyone thought the same thought, and nobody said it aloud, because saying it would make it a fact and facts were heavier than packs.

"Four days," Sefu murmured, mostly to himself, and touched the stone as if greeting an old adversary.

* * *

They ate cold food at noon because fires took time and time was the only currency they had left. Tomas sat with his back against a boulder, chewing dried meat that tasted like salt and regret, and watched Daniel Halvorsen stand apart from the others, studying the backtrail through a battered pair of field glasses.

His uncle had changed since the valley. Tomas kept trying to find the exact shape of the change. When they'd found the expedition's camp, weeks ago now, Daniel had been a name and a hat and a knotwork pattern — a ghost, a story, a reason for a boy to stow away on a stranger's boat. Then he'd been flesh and embrace and the smell of woodsmoke in his coat, and Tomas had cried into his shoulder like the child he'd been pretending not to be. But here, above the tree line, with the burned archive behind him and Vane's money behind that, Daniel had become something else again: a man carrying a weight he refused to set down even to climb.

"Tomas." His uncle lowered the glasses. "Come look."

Tomas crossed the scree. Daniel handed him the glasses and pointed back down the way they'd come, to a notch in the ridge where the trail dipped out of sight.

At first Tomas saw nothing. Then movement resolved itself out of the gray-and-green confusion: figures. Small at this distance, ant-like, but moving with purpose, strung out along the trail like beads on a wire. He counted. Counted again.

"Eight," he said. "Maybe nine."

"Vane kept six men," Daniel said quietly. "These are more."

"Valley men?" The question came out smaller than Tomas wanted. "Some of ours?"

"No." Daniel took the glasses back, and his jaw worked. "Amara says the valley people would not take his coin. These are hired from downriver. Porters and worse." He folded the glasses with a click that sounded final. "Your captain will want to know."

*My* captain. Something turned over warm in Tomas's chest at that — Daniel saying it naturally, without irony, Edmund Joubert's name spoken the way you spoke of family. He ran the word down the slope to where Edmund stood talking with Sefu, and told her, breathless, eight or nine, armed probably, a day behind maybe less.

Edmund didn't curse. That was the thing about her that Tomas had learned to read and still marveled at: bad news landed on her like rain on canvas, it darkened her and rolled off, and underneath she was already calculating. She looked south along the trail, then up at the pass, then at the sky, where the clouds had begun to assemble themselves into something with intent.

"Sefu," she said. "The goat path. The one you wouldn't tell Vane about."

Sefu's face did something complicated. "You remember that."

"I remember everything anyone tells me," Edmund said. "It's how I've stayed alive."

* * *

The goat path was not a path. That became clear within the first hundred yards, and remained clear for the next three hours, which were the longest of Tomas's life until the ones that followed them.

It traversed the mountainside on a shelf of stone no wider than a doorway in places, cut — if it had ever been cut — by hooves and centuries, angling up across the face of the ridge while the main trail curled away below like a lazy snake. To their right the mountain rose sheer; to their left it simply ended, and beyond the ending was air, and beyond the air was a drop that Tomas measured once, briefly, by watching a pebble he dislodged fall and fall and fall without any sound of arrival.

"Single file," Sefu said. "Mules in the middle. No one speaks above a whisper. Sound carries, and we are not the only ones who know this shelf exists."

They went. Tomas went near the front, behind Sefu, because Amara had finally consented to ride one of the mules and the other mule needed a keeper, and because — he admitted it only to himself — being near the front meant he didn't have to watch the others cross the terrifying parts. Ines crossed them like a woman reading a map, eyes always ahead. Edmund crossed them like a woman steering, weight low, hands finding holds the way her hands found wheel-spokes. Daniel crossed them badly, stiff with a fear he didn't hide, and Tomas loved him a little more for not hiding it.

Halfway across the shelf, the snow began again — properly now, fat flakes that came down without wind, almost gentle, almost kind, and utterly merciless. Within twenty minutes the stone underfoot wore a thin white skin. Within forty, the shelf ahead and behind had dissolved into white nothing, and the only thing keeping the column oriented was Sefu's voice, coming back hand to hand like a bucket brigade: *left foot high, keep to the wall side, don't look down, don't stop.*

Tomas's hands went numb inside his gloves. His boots soaked through. The mule behind him pressed its nose against his shoulder blade at the worst corner, breathing hot and afraid, and he found himself talking to it, nonsense words, river words, the same soft litany he used on frightened passengers in rough water, and the mule steadied, and so did he.

When they finally came off the shelf onto a broad saddle of snow-covered grass, the whole party stood gasping in the falling white, and Edmund counted heads twice, and then a third time, and only then let herself lean against a boulder and laugh — one short, sharp bark of a laugh that steamed in the cold.

"That," she said, "is the last easy thing between us and the top."

"She lies," Sefu said placidly, unwinding rope. "There is no easy thing. But yes. That was the last of them."

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of the saddle, tents pitched in a hollow, no fire larger than a cupped flame behind a windbreak of stones. Hot tea passed hand to hand. The snow kept falling, softer now, and the darkness was total except for the faint glow of the cloud deck below them, where somewhere far beneath lay the drowned villages and the trading posts and the mission station where they'd left Vane with a lie about their route — a lie he had evidently chewed through faster than anyone hoped.

Tomas sat his watch second, after midnight, wrapped in every garment he owned, watching the whiteness for movement that never came. What came instead was Daniel, unable to sleep, who sat down beside him and handed over half of a chocolate bar gone gray and bloomed with age, tasting of tin and glory.

"When I was your age," Daniel said, after a while, "I thought adventure was finding things. New valleys, new rivers, new names to put on maps." He turned the empty wrapper over in his fingers. "It took me thirty years and my father's money to learn that adventure is deciding what to do with what you find. Anyone can find a valley. The valley isn't the hard part."

"What's the hard part?" Tomas asked, though he suspected he knew.

"Carrying it out," Daniel said. "Carrying it past everyone who wants it to stay buried." He looked at his son-of-a-brother, or whatever Tomas was to him — nephew, heir, apprentice, boy — and his voice dropped. "If it goes wrong on the pass, if we're separated, you take the satchels and you go with Edmund. Not with me. With Edmund. Promise me."

"Why not you?"

Daniel smiled, and in the dim glow of the shielded lantern his face looked suddenly very old and very young at once. "Because she gets things down mountains. It's what she does. Ask her sometime about a boat called the *Magdalena* and a flooded gorge in '09." He squeezed Tomas's shoulder. "Promise me."

"I promise," Tomas said, and hated the promise, and kept it folded away in the same pocket where he kept his uncle's old hat, which he had carried since the abandoned camp and never once worn, because some things you carry rather than use.

* * *

Dawn came the color of dishwater, and with it came the sound.

Tomas heard it first — he was on watch again, and pride in that would come later, much later, when there was time for pride. A sound below and behind, carried up the still cold air: voices. Many voices. And the flat metallic clink of something that was not a mule bell.

He woke Edmund without touching her, the way she'd taught him in the first week aboard — crouch at the tent flap, say her name once, low, wait. Her eyes opened all the way on the first try, which he'd also learned was rare and meant she'd been sleeping lightly, sleeping the way captains sleep, with one ear on the current.

"How far?" she said.

"Below the saddle. An hour, maybe less. They're moving fast."

She was out of the tent before he finished answering, and the camp came awake around her like a body around a spine — Sefu rolling rope, Ines striking the shelter, Daniel getting Amara up with a gentleness that contradicted the urgency in his hands. No one shouted. Shouting cost time and gave away position. The camp dissolved itself into marching order in eleven minutes, which Tomas knew because Ines timed it, because Ines timed everything now, the way she'd timed star fixes on the night river, because a number was a weapon too.

"They'll see us on the open slope," Sefu said, pointing east, where the main pass trail climbed toward a col between two peaks, visible, exposed, slow. "And they'll catch us on it. We are loaded with satchels and mules and an old woman."

"So we don't take the open slope," Edmund said.

"There is another way." Sefu's voice went strange — reluctant, respectful, afraid. "The narrows. Where the mountain splits. There is a track through it that the valley people used in the old wars. It is steep, and it is exposed to avalanche, and if the snow comes heavy while we are in it—"

"Is it faster?"

"It is a day faster."

Edmund looked at the sky, at the fat gray clouds lowering themselves over the peaks like a lid closing on a box. She looked at Amara, who had come to stand among them leaning on her ironwood staff, snow gathering in the folds of her shawl.

"The narrows," Amara said. "My grandmother hid in the narrows from soldiers. It can be done." A pause, and then, with terrible mildness: "It has also killed everyone who ever hurried in it."

"Then we won't hurry," Edmund said. "We'll move precisely. Sefu leads. Single file. Mules lashed tight. Nobody stops in the narrows for anything short of a broken leg." She turned and found Tomas in the little huddle of faces, and her eyes held his for a moment, and what passed between them had no words in it, only the understanding that the next day would ask everything and pay in advance for nothing.

"Mr. Halvorsen," she added, turning to Daniel, "you carry the second satchel key. If we're split, the archive goes down the mountain even if we don't."

"And you?" Daniel asked.

"I," said Edmund Joubert, pulling her collar up against the rising wind, "am going to get us through the narrows before the mountain closes. I've run floods. I've run ice. I have never yet been caught by weather I saw coming." She glanced up once more at the sky, and for just a moment — a flicker, quickly banked — Tomas saw something else in her face, something that was not confidence at all.

The snow thickened as they climbed toward the mouth of the narrows, flakes coming fatter and faster now, erasing the trail behind them step by step, which was mercy and menace in equal measure. At the entrance to the split — two walls of black stone rising so high their tops vanished into the boiling cloud — Sefu paused and looked back down the way they'd come.

Far below, tiny with distance, dark against the white, the line of pursuers had emerged onto the open slope. Even at that range Tomas could see them pause, could imagine the glasses passing from hand to hand, the pointing.

"They've seen us," he said.

"Yes," said Sefu. "Now they will hurry." He settled his pack, spat into the snow, and stepped into the narrows. "And so must we."
