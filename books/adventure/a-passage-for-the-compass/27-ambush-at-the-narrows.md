## 27. Ambush at the Narrows

Sefu had been listening to the mountain since before dawn, and the mountain had told him a lie.

That was how he thought of it afterward — not that he had failed to hear, but that the mountain had chosen to keep its counsel. The wind had come down the valley all night from the north, steady as breathing, carrying nothing but cold and the smell of stone. No smoke on it. No voices. If men had been moving along the high trail in the dark, they had moved the way snowmelt moves, without sound, without scent, patient as water finding its level.

He had walked point for thirty years. He had carried salt over passes that killed his father's mules, and he had learned the first law of the mountains before he learned his own name: *the trail tells you what walks it.* A goat leaves its droppings in the same place every morning. A mule leaves the print of an unshod hoof and the sweet smell of its sweat in the cold. Men leave fire-scar and boot-mark and the particular silence of people trying not to be heard. The narrows ahead — that throat of rock where the old trail pinched between two walls of granite with the gorge roaring forty fathoms below — was the one place on the whole route where no one could pass unseen. It was also the one place where anyone could be made to stop.

So when the wind shifted at midmorning, Sefu stopped walking.

It was a small thing at first. The north wind had died sometime in the night, and now the air came up-valley instead, warm and wet off the lower slopes, smelling of pine resin and wet earth. And under it, faint as a thread pulled through cloth, woodsmoke. Not valley smoke — the valley's fires burned dung and damp hardwood, and their smell was sweetish, familiar as his own hearth. This was resin-smoke, pitch-fed, the kind men burned who had no time to gather dry wood.

He raised his hand and the column behind him stopped. Twenty-two souls on the trail: Edmund Joubert at the rear with her compass out and her jaw set like something carved; Ines with the archive copies wrapped in oilcloth against her chest; Tomas carrying the eldest's pack along with his own; Daniel Halvorsen and his scholars; four valley guides; and the eldest himself, old Mekonnen, walking with two sticks and a patience that seemed older than the mountain. Above them the sky had gone the color of pewter, and the first flakes had begun to fall an hour ago — small, dry, almost shy, the mountain clearing its throat before it spoke.

Sefu crouched and put his palm flat against the trail. Cold stone, but not frozen. Then he looked up at the walls of the narrows, three hundred paces ahead, and saw what the wind had finally given him: a thread of smoke rising from behind the eastern wall, thin and vertical, too disciplined to be a traveler's fire. Travelers' fires lean and wander. That one stood straight as a plumb line.

"Someone is in the narrows," he said quietly, in the valley tongue, and then again in trade speech so the river people would understand. "Waiting."

Edmund came forward at once, moving low along the trailside, and crouched beside him. She smelled of woodsmoke herself, of the boat, of the river — a smell Sefu had come to trust more than most men's words. She studied the smoke the way she must have studied currents, reading what stood still.

"How many?" she asked.

"Cannot say. Two fires would make two threads. I see one." He turned his hand flat, weighing it. "One fire means few men or careful men. Careful men are worse."

"Vane's?"

"Who else climbs a pass in the first snow?" Sefu let the question sit. "His hired men are lowland men. They will hate this cold. They will be slow and loud by tomorrow. But today, in the narrows, they need only stand there and hold their ground, and we cannot pass them without dead men on both sides."

Daniel Halvorsen had come up quietly, and now he knelt with them, his breath steaming. "Could we go around? Over the top?"

Sefu shook his head slowly. "Over the top is two days above the snowline. With the elders and the scholars, four. The snow will take the high ground tonight or tomorrow. We would die beautifully and be found in spring, very tidy, very white."

Tomas had drifted forward too — the boy could not help himself; he moved toward trouble the way iron moves toward a lodestone — and Ines came behind him with the eldest leaning on her arm. Old Mekonnen lowered himself onto a boulder with the care of a man folding up a tent he had owned a long time, and looked at the narrows with eyes that had watched this valley for eighty years.

"There is another way," Mekonnen said.

Everyone turned. The old man smiled, and it was a terrible smile, full of teeth and memory. "There is always another way. That is what the lowlanders never learn. They see one trail on their maps and believe the mountain has only one mouth." He tapped his stick against the stone. "The goat path. My grandfather's grandfather drove goats across the cliff face above the narrows when the war took the bridge below. It is not a trail. It is a habit the goats have, and men have followed it because goats are wiser than soldiers."

Edmund looked at the cliff face. So did Sefu. It rose from the trail's edge in shelves and ledges, white with old lichen, stitched here and there with the pale threads of goat tracks — visible now that you knew to look, invisible five minutes ago. It climbed perhaps two hundred feet above the narrows, traversed the face, and came down beyond the granite wall into a hanging meadow Sefu knew of but had never needed.

"It can be done," Sefu said, because it was true, and because someone had to say the hard thing plainly. "It can be done with twenty-two people, in falling snow, carrying the copies and the packs. But not fast. And not silently. And if any one of us freezes on the face—" he did not finish it. On a cliff, an unfinished sentence is a mercy.

"The copies go across," Edmund said. It was not a question and not quite an order; it was the voice of a woman who had spent weeks learning which things were hers to decide. "The people go across. Everything else is negotiable."

They left the packs of the scholars buried in a cache beneath a leaning slab, marked with stones in the valley fashion, and took only food, rope, the oilcloth bundles, and the eldest. Mekonnen refused, at first, to be carried or helped across anything. "I have walked this valley longer than your fathers have been alive," he told Sefu, and Sefu bowed and said nothing, because the argument was true and useless, which is the worst kind.

The goat path began well enough. For the first hundred paces it was merely steep — a staircase of tilted slabs where the footing was good if you trusted the mountain and fatal if you hesitated. Sefu went first, testing each hold, calling back the pattern of it in a low chant that the guides behind him passed down the line: *left foot high, right foot wide, hand on the grey stone, never the white.* The snow thickened as they climbed, settling into the ledges, softening the edges of everything until the cliff looked less like stone than like the bones of some enormous animal under a sheet.

Below and behind them, the narrows fell silent. Whatever waited there had not seen them yet — or had seen them and understood, and was already climbing.

Halfway across the face, the path narrowed to a ledge barely wider than a man's shoulders, with the drop opening below like a held breath. Here Sefu fixed the rope they'd brought, looping it through an iron-hard root of juniper that had grown out of the crack for a century, and the line of them crossed one at a time, pressed to the stone, breath ragged, snow gathering on shoulders and lashes. Ines crossed with the copies strapped tight, her face bloodless, her eyes fixed on the rock an arm's length away and nowhere else. Daniel crossed carrying a scholar's pack and his own guilt, which weighed more. Tomas crossed last of the young ones, light as a bird, and Sefu allowed himself one moment of pride, quickly swallowed, because pride on a cliff is a stone in your boot.

Then it was Mekonnen's turn.

The old man stood at the base of the ledge with his two sticks and looked up at it for a long time. Snow gathered on his wool cap. When he spoke, it was to Sefu alone, in the valley tongue, and his voice was quiet and clear as water under ice.

"I crossed this face at nineteen," he said. "With the goats, in the drought year. My knees were young then. Now they are liars. They tell me they will hold, and then they do not."

"We can build a litter," Sefu said. "Rope and poles. Four of us can bear you."

"And slow everyone to a crawl, and be caught on this face in the dark when the snow closes." The old man shook his head. "No. I will walk it. Pride is a heavy load, but I have carried heavier."

He should not have walked it. Sefu knew it even as he gave the order to let him try, and he has carried the knowing ever since, the way a man carries a stone in his chest that no doctor can find. But you do not tell an elder of the valley that his legs are finished. You let the mountain tell him, gently, if it must.

Mekonnen made it halfway along the ledge. Sefu was ten paces ahead, paying out slack, watching the old man's feet — left foot high, right foot wide, hand on the grey stone — and the feet were obedient, the feet were fine. It was the right knee that lied. Sefu heard it before he saw it: a small wet sound, a click like a lock turning, and then Mekonnen's weight went sideways off the ledge and the world contracted to a single rope and a single falling body.

The old man did not cry out. That was the worst of it, Sefu thought later — that he fell in silence, as though even in falling he refused to burden the others with fear. The rope snapped taut. Sefu threw his weight backward against it, boots skidding on snow-slick stone, and felt his shoulder wrench nearly out of its socket, and held. Below him, two of the younger guides had anchored the line around a horn of rock, and the four of them together took the old man's weight as he swung against the cliff face, his sticks clattering away into the gorge, lost to the same depths that had taken the bridge and the village and so much else.

They hauled him up by inches. His face was grey as the lichen, and his breath came in short, torn pieces, and his right leg hung wrong below the knee. But his eyes, when they found Sefu's, were furious and alive.

"Well," Mekonnen said, through his teeth. "Now you may carry me. I release you from politeness."

* * *

They built the litter from juniper poles and the spare rope and the oilcloth that had wrapped Ines's copies, and they lashed the old man in with a skill born of long practice at carrying wounded goats down from the high pasture — though no one said so aloud. Four carriers, rotating every quarter hour, because a litter on broken ground eats strength the way fire eats dry grass. The pace dropped to half. The snow thickened. Behind them, somewhere below the cliff, Sefu heard — or believed he heard, and after that day never doubted it — a shout in a lowland accent, thin with distance and rage, and knew the narrows had emptied and the hunt had changed shape.

"They'll cut for the meadow trail," Edmund said, coming alongside the litter, her breath smoking. "How far ahead are we?"

"An hour," Sefu said. "Perhaps two, if the snow slows them more than it slows us. Lowland men walk on snow like drunk men on ice."

"Then we don't stop."

They did not stop. Through the afternoon the column wound along the hanging meadow and up into the scrub pine, the litter swaying between its bearers, the eldest silent inside it except once, when a ptarmigan burst from the snow near the trail and he laughed — a dry rustle of a laugh — and said, "Even the birds flee the lowlanders."

At dusk the snow stopped pretending to be gentle. It came down in earnest then, fat and wind-driven, erasing the trail behind them within minutes, which was mercy and menace both: mercy, because it hid them; menace, because it hid the trail. Sefu navigated now by memory and by the feel of the slope under his boots, counting paces, reading the lean of the trees, doing the work he had done since boyhood while his shoulder throbbed and his hands went numb inside their wraps.

It was full dark when Tomas came stumbling forward through the snow to the head of the column, his face wild under its rime of white.

"He's fevered," the boy said. "The eldest. His leg — Ines bound it, but he's burning, and he keeps saying names. Old names. People from the drowned villages."

Sefu closed his eyes for one breath. Ahead, the pass still rose — another day's climb to the saddle, maybe more in this weather, and behind them the lowland men were somewhere in the storm, and ahead of them the snows were coming down like a curtain being drawn across the world.

"Then we make shelter," he said. "No one is left behind. Not on my trail. Not ever."

And far below them, faint through the roar of the wind, he heard the thing he had been dreading all day: the small, sharp report of a signal flare, blooming red through the snow-gloom behind them, marking their position for whoever climbed in the dark.

The mountain had kept its secrets all day. Now it was giving them away.
