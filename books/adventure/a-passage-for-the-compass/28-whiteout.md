## 28. Whiteout

The snow did not fall so much as arrive.

One moment Edmund was watching the trail climb through grey scree under a sky the color of pewter, and the next the world had been erased — wiped clean like slate before a lesson. There was no horizon. There was no up. The wind came off the heights in long white gusts that erased Sefu's back ten paces ahead and left nothing but the rope between them and the sound of their own breathing.

"Rope tight!" Sefu bellowed against the wind, and Edmund checked the line at her waist, then turned to check Ines behind her, then Tomas behind Ines, then the two valley guides and old Maren, the eldest, lashed into a litter of poles and hide that two men carried between them like something sacred. Which, Edmund thought, tightening the knot with numb fingers, it was.

The pass had closed four days early. Sefu had read the sky and said *maybe four more days*, and the mountain had laughed at him and given them one and a half.

They had been climbing since dawn, if dawn could be said to exist in a place where light came from everywhere and nowhere at once. Edmund had run rivers in fog before — had felt that particular vertigo of steering into a wall of grey with nothing but memory and nerve — but this was worse, because here there was no current to feel through the hull, no vibration in the wheel telling her what the water was doing beneath her. Here there was only rock and wind and the compass in her coat pocket, and a set of stories she had half-believed since she was six years old.

*Keep moving,* she told herself. *Moving is warm. Stopping is dying.*

She leaned into the wind and counted her steps, because counting was something a pilot understood. A hundred steps, check the rope. A hundred more, call the count down the line so Ines could mark it against her watch. They were making perhaps a mile in three hours. The shelter Sefu spoke of — a cave below the saddle, a place he'd used twice in his life and both times unhappily — was still hours away, and every hour the snow deepened and the light flattened further, until Edmund could no longer tell whether they were walking on a slope or across the level face of the world.

It was Ines who first said what they were all thinking.

"We're drifting." Her voice came muffled through the scarf wrapped around her face, close at Edmund's shoulder. "The wind's pushing us. I can't get a fix — I haven't seen the sun since morning, and even if I could—"

"I know."

"The compass—"

Edmund already had it out. She'd had it out for an hour, cupped in both hands inside the lee of her body, watching the needle tremble in its bowl of glass and brass like a small frightened animal. It was pointing somewhere. That was the trouble. In a whiteout on a switchbacking trail, north was not the same as forward, and a compass would lead you faithfully off a cliff while telling you the truth the whole way down.

Except.

She stopped, and the rope went taut behind her, and Sefu's voice floated back: *"Why are we stopping?"*

Edmund didn't answer. She was looking at the compass, and she was hearing her father's voice, which was strange, because she hadn't heard it clearly in years — it had gone the way of all dead voices, worn smooth by repetition until it was less a sound than a shape. But now it came back whole, sharp-edged, as if the cold had preserved it.

*The needle lies on a mountain, little fish,* he used to say, turning his own compass — this compass, though she hadn't known it then — in his brown fingers. *Iron in the rock, the height, the storm. But the mountain remembers the way even when the needle forgets. You hold it flat. You walk slow. And you listen for the story underneath.*

She had thought it was just talk. Riverboat talk, the kind of talk men made over cards and rum, stories that began *when I crossed the high pass with Halvorsen* and ended in laughter nobody outside the cabin ever quite got the joke of. She had been a child at the rail, mending net or coiling line, drinking the stories in without believing they were anything but entertainment. Her father had never been above the river in his life, as far as she knew. He was a boatman. Boatmen stayed on the water.

But the compass in her hands had Halvorsen's initials engraved under the plate, and Daniel had gone pale when he saw it, and the old woman in the drowned village had called it by a name Edmund didn't know, and piece by piece, over these weeks on the trail, the stories had stopped being stories. Her father had crossed this pass. With Halvorsen. Before the dam, before the debt, before whatever it was that had divided two families who had once been — what? Partners? Friends?

And if he had crossed it, he had crossed it in weather, because no one crossed this pass in clear weather by choice, and he had come down the other side and told the tale a hundred times in a smoky cabin while his daughter listened from the shadows.

*Listen for the story underneath.*

"Sefu!" she called. "How far to the cave?"

"Three hours. Maybe four, in this."

"And after the saddle — does the trail bend east or west?"

Sefu's silhouette resolved briefly out of the white, a dark shape shaking its head. "West. Then south along the ridge. Why?"

"Because we're not going west." Edmund turned to face the line, hunching against the wind. "We've drifted. The wind's been walking us sideways for an hour. If we keep bearing as we bear, we'll come off the ridge on the wrong side and none of us will know it until the ground disappears."

"You can't know that," one of the valley guides said — Karim, the younger one, whose patience had been fraying since the ambush at the narrows. "No one can navigate in this. Not even Sefu. We should stop, dig in, wait—"

"If we stop, we freeze," Sefu said flatly. "Maren cannot spend a night in open snow."

"Then we need the right direction," Edmund said. "And I think I have it."

They looked at her — the shapes of them, half-guessed through the driving white, faces reduced to eyes and frost. She held up the compass.

"This belonged to Halvorsen. Before it belonged to me. And my father crossed this pass with him — I know how that sounds, but Daniel knows it too, and I'll tell you the rest when we're under stone. Right now you need to know that my father spent twenty years telling anyone who'd buy him a drink exactly how this pass runs. Every bend. Every false ridge. Where the iron in the rock pulls the needle wrong." She looked down at the trembling card. "He talked about it so much I hated him for it. Turns out he was leaving me a chart."

Ines stepped closer, snow crusting her lashes. "You memorized it? As a child?"

"I memorized everything as a child. It was the only book I had." Edmund almost smiled. "He said the trail bends west after the saddle, then south. He said — God, I can't believe I'm saying this — he said *where the red stone shoulders out of the white, the needle swings drunk, and there you trust your feet and not the glass.* Red stone, Sefu. Is there red stone past the saddle?"

For a long moment Sefu said nothing. Snow filled the silence between them. Then, slowly, the old guide's head tilted, the way a man's head tilts when he hears a tune he half-remembers.

"There is red stone," he said. "A shoulder of it, on the south side, where the wind has stripped it bare. The porters used to curse it. It is the only color on the whole ridge." His eyes, dark in his frost-rimed face, fixed on hers. "Your father came this way."

"My father came this way," Edmund agreed, and found that saying it aloud did something to her chest that had nothing to do with the cold. "With Halvorsen. Before any of us were born. And he left the route in my head without either of us knowing it."

* * *

They moved.

It was not better, exactly — the whiteout did not care about charts or inheritances — but it was different, and different mattered. Edmund went first now, beside Sefu, the compass open in her gloved hands, calling the bearings her father had sung into her thirty years ago. *Bear northwest by the glass until the ground rises left. Then the drift will take you and let it — the drift is going where you want to go anyway.* It was uncanny, following instructions given as a joke in a river cabin, and it worked, and each time it worked Edmund felt another plank of her old certainty knock loose and float free.

Her whole life she had built herself on a simple foundation: her father was a boatman, the boat was bought with borrowed money, and the debt was the truest fact of her existence, more real than the river itself. She had steered by that debt the way other people steered by stars. Everything she'd refused, everything she'd endured, every hard bargain and bitter winter — all of it arranged around that single fixed point.

And now the fixed point was moving. The debt was a forgery, Vane's collection a crime, the loan forgiven before her father died. The boat was not bought with borrowed money; it was bought with partnership, with friendship, with whatever her father and old Halvorsen had been to each other before the dam came between them. And the man who had taught her to read water had also walked this knife-edge of the world with the very family she believed she owed.

What else had she misread? What else in her life was a clerical error dressed as fate?

"Step!" she shouted, dropping to her knee, and the line froze behind her. Ahead, where the slope should have continued rising, there was nothing — a seam of air where the ridge fell away into cloud. Another two steps in the old bearing and Tomas would have walked off the edge of the world.

They edged along the cornice, roped at arm's length, and found the trail again on the far side, and climbed.

* * *

The saddle, when they reached it, was not a saddle at all but a suggestion — a brief flattening of the wind, a moment where the roar dropped enough that they could hear each other without shouting. Sefu held up his fist and they clustered together, a knot of animals in a white sea.

"Cave is below us, south side," Sefu said. "An hour. Maybe less, going down."

"Maren?" Ines asked.

The eldest's face was grey inside her hood, her lips bloodless, but her eyes were open and clear, and when Edmund bent close she caught the faintest movement of the old woman's hand — two fingers lifting, falling, lifting again. Walking on. Edmund squeezed the hand through the mittens. *Hold on. We're nearly there.*

They started down, and within a hundred paces Edmund saw it: a shoulder of rock thrusting out of the whiteness, dark and rust-red, streaked with ice. Red stone. Her father's marker, standing exactly where he'd said it stood, patient as a lighthouse, waiting thirty years for his daughter to come read it.

She stopped in front of it. The others crowded in behind her, using the rock as a windbreak, and for a moment they simply breathed, five travelers and two guides and one dying elder, huddled against a stone her father had named before any of them existed.

"He stood here," Edmund said quietly. Nobody asked who. "Him and Halvorsen. Right here. Probably cursing the cold the same way we are."

"Do you think—" Tomas began, and stopped, and tried again. "Do you think they knew? About the valley? About what it was?"

"I don't know." Edmund turned the compass over in her hands. Under the repaired plate, Daniel had found the second set of initials — her father's, beside Halvorsen's, two names cut into the same brass. Two families, one instrument. "I think they came up here together when the world was simpler, and then the world got complicated, and maybe they chose different sides of it. Or maybe the sides chose them." She looked up at the white void where the summit should have been. "Either way, he walked me through this pass every night of my childhood and I never once believed him. That's the thing about inheritance. It doesn't wait for you to accept it. It just sits there, being yours, until you're ready."

Ines touched her arm. "Edmund. Your feet. Look."

Edmund looked down. The wind, funneling through the notch beside the red stone, had scoured a patch of the slope bare — and in the exposed ice, faint but unmistakable, ran a line of old cut steps, hacked into the rock generations ago, half-healed with lichen. A porter's stair. Older than Sefu's memory. Older, perhaps, than the maps.

"Somebody's carried loads over this pass for a long time," Sefu murmured, crouching to touch the cuts. "Longer than my grandfather's grandfather."

"The river people," Tomas said softly. "Before the reservoir. This was their road, wasn't it? Up to trade, up to—" He glanced at the litter where Maren lay. "Up to the valley."

Nobody answered, because the answer was obvious, and because the wind chose that moment to return with renewed fury, erasing the moment for them, hurrying them on. They took the old steps down through the murk, Edmund calling the turns from a dead man's memory, and the mountain gave them each one grudgingly, like a toll paid in an old coin.

* * *

The cave announced itself as a change in the sound of the wind — a hollow note, a draft with warmth in it — and then they were through the mouth of it and into black, blessed stillness, and Edmund's knees nearly folded with relief.

Sefu struck a flame shielded in his jacket and lit a candle stub, and the cave grew slowly out of the darkness: a chamber tall enough to stand in, dry-floored, with old ash at the back and walls that gleamed faintly where generations of hands had polished the stone. Somebody had sheltered here many times. Many somebodies, over many years.

They got Maren out of the litter and onto the driest ground, wrapped in every spare layer they owned, and fed her melted snow and the last of the sugar. Ines checked frostbite — Tomas's cheek, Karim's fingertips, Edmund's nose, which had gone waxy-white without her noticing — and Sefu got a small fire going at the mouth of the cave where the smoke could draw, and for the first time since morning, since the world had been wiped blank, Edmund sat down and let herself shake.

It was the good shaking, the aftermath shaking, but it wouldn't stop, and she let it run its course while the fire grew and the others settled around it. Tomas was asleep almost instantly, curled against his uncle's pack like a boy half his age. Ines was sketching by candlelight — she was always sketching, the journal filling now with the old cut steps, the red stone, the shape of the pass as Edmund had called it. Building the chart her father had never drawn.

Edmund took out the compass and opened it one more time, though there was nothing new to see. The needle swung, steadied, pointed its faithful, useless, truthful point north. On the underside, hidden until Daniel lifted the plate, two sets of initials shared the same brass, cut by the same hand, in the same hour, by the look of them.

*J.H. E.J.*

Joubert. Her name. Her father's name. Cut beside Halvorsen's like two men who had owned something together — or been owned by it, or simply belonged, the way crew belongs to a boat and a boat belongs to the water.

"You knew," she said to the compass, quietly, so the others wouldn't hear. "Both of you knew. You put your names in the same case and then you let it end up with me, and neither of you ever told me why."

The needle trembled, as needles do, and pointed nowhere in particular, and everywhere.

Across the fire, Maren stirred. The eldest's eyes had opened, and they were fixed on Edmund — or rather, Edmund realized with a small cold shock, on the compass in her hands. The old woman's lips moved. Edmund leaned close, and the voice that came out was thin as paper but perfectly clear, in the valley tongue that Sefu half-knew and translated in a low murmur:

"She says — *you carry the two names. Now you must hear what the names did.*"

The fire popped. Outside, the storm screamed past the mouth of the cave like a freight of white water, and Edmund found she was holding her breath.

"She says she knew your father," Sefu added softly. "Or men like him. She says she has been waiting a long time to tell this story — and that the storm has given us the night to hear it."

Maren raised one hand, palm up, an invitation older than any of them.

Edmund closed the compass, set it gently on the stone between them, and leaned into the firelight to listen.
