## 30. Tomas's Turn

The mountain had been grinding them down for two days, and on the third morning it stopped pretending to be kind.

Tomas woke with frost in his eyelashes and the taste of iron in his mouth from breathing too fast at altitude, and the first thing he heard was Sefu standing over the fire, saying the word *scree* the way other men said *landslide*.

"Below the notch," Sefu said, pointing with his chin at the saddle of grey stone that barred the pass ahead. "Four hundred meters of it. Loose rock the size of a man's fist, the size of a man's head, the size of a man. It moves when you step on it. It moves when the wind breathes on it. It moves because it has been waiting since the mountain was born to come down, and it is in no hurry, but it will come down."

The eldest, wrapped in three layers of valley wool with a fourth that Ines had sewn around her overnight, sat propped against a boulder with her hands folded in her lap. She was ninety-one years old, or she said she was; her people counted years differently, by floods rather than winters, and by that count she was older than the river itself, which was clearly impossible and which nobody had ever thought to argue with. Her name was Ama Suri, and she had insisted on coming with them out of the valley, and she had walked every step of the trail so far with a patience that made the young guides ashamed of their own complaining.

Now she looked at the scree field with the calm of a woman reading a letter she had expected for a long time.

"I will slow you," she said. Not a question. Not an apology. A fact, laid out like goods at market.

"You will not slow us," said her grandson, one of the two valley guides who had come with them, a wiry man named Kel who had not slept properly since they left the valley. "We will carry you."

"Carry me over *that*?" Ama Suri gestured at the slope, and her laugh was a dry rustle, like wind through the archive ashes. "You will carry me halfway and then we will both go down together, and the mountain will keep us, and that is a fine way to be kept, but it is not the way I intended."

Nobody laughed. The fire crackled. Somewhere above them, a rock came loose on its own schedule and clattered down the slope with a sound like a bone breaking.

It was at this point, Tomas later understood, that his life divided itself into before and after. Not with any ceremony. Not with any words. Just with the fact that while the adults were looking at the mountain, he was looking at the old woman, and he saw what none of them wanted to see: that she had already decided to tell them to leave her behind, and that she would say it soon, gently, the way she said everything, and that if someone did not act she would win the argument because she always won the argument, because she was Ama Suri and she had outlived everyone who had ever argued with her.

"I'll carry her," Tomas said.

The fire went quiet. Edmund turned. His uncle turned. Even Sefu's chin came down from its habitual altitude to look at him directly, which Tomas suspected happened perhaps twice a year.

"She's small," Tomas said, and immediately wished he had found better words, because Ama Suri was now looking at him with an expression of vast, delighted interest, the way a cat looks at a bird that has just volunteered.

"You are what, fourteen?" she said.

"Fifteen in the spring."

"Fifteen." She said it the way Ines said coordinates, placing it exactly. "And you have carried what, in your life? Firewood? A little sister? A grudge?"

"Grain sacks," Tomas said. "Two at a time. Fifty kilos each."

"Ah." She nodded slowly. "Grain sacks do not hold on to you when they are frightened. They do not grab your collar when the ground moves. They do not whisper in your ear that they are sorry to be such a burden, which is the heaviest thing a person can carry." She unfolded her hands and looked at them, old hands, hands that had copied the archive pages by lamplight in the weeks after the fire, ink-stained to the second knuckle. "But perhaps you are right. Perhaps you are exactly what is needed. The young carry the old across the hard places. That is the whole of the law, if you boil it down. Everything else is commentary."

"Two at a time," Daniel said quietly. He was looking at Tomas in a way that Tomas could not read, or could read too many ways at once.

"The grain sacks," Tomas said, "were heavier."

* * *

They roped up in the grey light before the sun cleared the eastern ridge, because Sefu wanted them across the worst of it while the frost still held the stones together. "Frozen scree is honest scree," he said. "It holds its tongue. When the sun comes out and the ice lets go, every stone becomes a liar. Move like the mountain is asleep. Because it is. And you do not wake a sleeping thing."

The plan was simple, which Tomas knew meant it was the kind of plan that only worked if every person did exactly what they were supposed to do, which meant it was the kind of plan that never worked. Sefu would lead, reading the slope the way Edmund read the river, finding the invisible seams where the scree ran in long stable sheets instead of loose chaos. Kel and the other guide, Roshan, would carry the loads. Edmund would bring up the rear with Ines, and Daniel would walk beside his uncle's son, and Tomas would carry Ama Suri on his back in a harness that Kel wove from rope and pack straps in twenty minutes of silent, practiced work, because the valley people had been carrying their old ones over mountains for longer than there had been maps.

They had carried her over mountains before. That was the thing that steadied him, when she was strapped against his back and her arms came around his shoulders and her chin settled near his ear: this was not a new thing under the sun. Somewhere in the archive that had burned, there were probably carvings of this exact act, some ancestor of his carrying some ancestor of hers across some slope exactly like this one, four hundred meters of waiting stone, and both of them had made it, or both of them had not, and either way the carrying had gone on.

"Listen to me," Ama Suri said into his ear as they took the first step, and the slope shifted under it, a long dry sigh of settling stone, and his heart tried to climb out through his throat. "You will want to hurry. Do not hurry. You will want to stop. Do not stop. The middle is the worst. Everyone knows the middle is the worst, and everyone is surprised anyway. And when your legs begin to shake — and they will begin to shake, boy, it is not weakness, it is the mountain asking you a question — you will answer with your breath. In through the nose, out through the mouth. My people's runners used to say: the breath carries what the legs cannot."

"Did your people have runners?" Tomas asked, to have something to say besides *I'm afraid*.

"We had everything," she said, "once. Runners. Weavers. A calendar that read the river's moods better than your survey office reads its instruments. We had songs for carrying, did you know that? Songs with a step built into them, so that the carrier's feet found the rhythm without being told." And she began, very softly, to sing.

It was not a song in any language he knew. It was low and repetitive, a handful of notes that circled back on themselves like a path through fog, and after a dozen steps he realized what she was doing: she was giving him a beat. Left foot on the low note. Right foot on the high one. The song did the counting so his mind was free to do the other work, the work of watching the slope and choosing stones and feeling through his boot soles whether a rock was asleep or only pretending.

He climbed.

* * *

The first hundred meters were almost easy, and he distrusted them completely.

The frost held. The stones clicked under his boots with a sound like crockery, and held. Ama Suri weighed almost nothing, or his fear had burned off everything else and left only the carrying, which is a different kind of lightness. Behind him he could hear the others: Daniel's steady breathing, Kel's occasional low word to Roshan, the hiss of displaced rock. Once he heard Edmund say something to Ines and Ines laugh, a small compressed laugh, the kind you make at four hundred meters of scree, and the laugh went through him like warm water. They were still there. He was still tied to them by rope and by something the rope stood for.

At a hundred and fifty meters the sun cleared the ridge.

He felt it before he understood it: a change in the sound under his feet, the crockery-click going duller, wetter, the frost beginning to let go of its grip stone by stone. Ahead of him Sefu stopped, crouched, put his palm flat on the slope like a man taking a pulse.

"Faster now," Sefu said, and his voice was the same flat calm it always was, which was how Tomas knew it was bad. "Not running. Quicker. The mountain is waking up. We move while it stretches."

The song changed. Ama Suri sang it quicker, the circling notes tightening, and Tomas understood that she had done this her whole life, or her people had — that the song had a fast version and a slow version the way a river had a dry season and a flood, and that she had been carrying the fast version inside her for ninety-one years, waiting for a morning like this one.

At two hundred meters, the slope moved.

Not a slide, not yet. A shudder, a long muscular ripple that ran down the scree field below them like a shiver down a horse's flank, and the sound was enormous, a grinding roar that was almost too low to hear, felt in the teeth and the sternum more than the ears. A boulder the size of a cart came loose somewhere above and to their left and went down the mountain in long bounding arcs, and where it landed it started other things moving, a fan of smaller stones hissing down like water.

"Rope team, hold!" Sefu's voice cracked across the slope. "Weight down! Everyone still!"

Tomas stood with Ama Suri on his back and the mountain shivering underneath him and did the hardest thing he had ever done, which was nothing. His legs wanted to run. His whole body was one clenched instruction to run. But the rope ran from his harness back to Daniel and Edmund and the others, and if he ran he would pull them off their footing, and if they fell the rope would pull him, and so he stood in the roar and the shudder and sang the song in his head, left foot, right foot, breath in through the nose, and he felt Ama Suri's arms tighten slightly around his shoulders, not from fear, he understood, but the way you tighten your arms around something you are proud of.

The shudder passed. The mountain settled, grumbling. A haze of dust hung over the lower slope.

"It was testing us," Ama Suri said in his ear. "It does that. Everything old tests the young. Now it knows you are serious, and it will let you pass — if you are quick, and if you are humble, and if you do not look down."

He looked down. He couldn't help it. Four hundred meters of grey stone fanned out below him and kept going, down and down, into the cloud that filled the valley floor, and the cloud was the color of nothing, and the nothing went all the way down.

"Ah," Ama Suri said. "You looked."

"You said not to."

"I say many things. The trick is knowing which ones are law and which are only weather." He felt her shift her weight, settling herself more securely, her hands finding new grip on his collar. "The middle now, boy. This is the part I told you about. Everyone is surprised anyway."

* * *

The middle was the worst.

The frost died by degrees, and the stones began to tell lies. A slab that looked solid would shift under his weight with a sound like a door opening. A patch of loose gravel would hold like concrete, and then the patch beside it, identical, would pour away downslope. He learned to read it the only way it could be read, with his feet, with the soles of his boots, with the small animal intelligence that lives below thought: step, listen, feel, decide, and if the decision was wrong, correct in the air, in the half-second before the wrongness became a fall.

His legs began to shake at three hundred meters.

It was exactly as she had said. Not weakness — the mountain asking a question. His thighs were two columns of fire, his shoulders burned where the harness straps cut, and Ama Suri weighed nothing and everything, and his breath came in ragged white plumes, and the song in his head had begun to fray at the edges.

*In through the nose,* she said in his ear, and he realized she had been singing again, louder now, her old voice cracked and thin and absolutely steady, the circling notes pulling his feet back into rhythm. *Out through the mouth. The breath carries what the legs cannot.*

"I've got her," he said out loud, and didn't know who he was saying it to. "I've got her, I've got her, I've got her," and it became part of the song, a third line under her two, and he climbed.

At three hundred and fifty meters a stone the size of his head turned under his left boot and his whole world tilted, and his right foot found nothing, and for one full second — he would remember the length of it his whole life — he was falling, they were falling, the old woman on his back and the boy under her, going down the grey fan together into the cloud.

Then his left hand closed on a boulder that had been sitting in that spot since the mountain was born, and held.

He hung there. One hand, one boot wedged in a crack, the rope taut behind him, Ama Suri's weight swinging gently against his back like a pendulum finding its rest. Below his dangling right foot, a stone he had dislodged went down and down and down and did not make a sound for a long time, and when it did, the sound was very small, and that was the worst part, how small it was, how far.

"Boy." Ama Suri's voice, in his ear, calm as a lake at dawn. "You have her. The stone. Now the foot. There is no hurry. There is all the hurry in the world, and there is no hurry at all. Both are true. Find the foot."

He found the foot. He pulled. The rope behind him came taut and then mercifully slack — Daniel paying it out, giving him line instead of hauling him, trusting him to climb it himself — and he climbed, and the slope rose, and the song went on, and at three hundred and ninety meters his boot came down on stone that did not move, and at four hundred meters it came down on stone that did not move again, and then there was grass, actual grass, frost-stiff and thin as wire but grass, rooted, committed, holding.

He walked ten more steps, because Sefu had said ten steps past the edge before you stop, and then he went down on his knees in the grass with the old woman still on his back, because he could not, in that moment, figure out how to put her down without putting himself down too.

Ama Suri untied the harness herself, her old fingers quick and sure, and slid off his back and stood beside him in the frost and put her hand on his head, on his hair, the way you touch something you are returning to the world.

"There," she said. "That is the whole of the law. Everything else is commentary."

* * *

He didn't cry, which surprised him. He had expected to, had braced for it the way you brace for cold water, but what came instead was worse and better: a stillness, a huge quiet inside him, as if the shaking of his legs had been the shaking of everything and now everything had stopped. He sat in the grass while the others came off the slope one by one — Kel, Roshan, Ines with her sketchbook strapped to her pack, Edmund moving with that river-walkers' economy that made even scree look like deck plating — and he sat and breathed and watched the cloud below the pass and let the mountain be behind him.

It was Daniel who came and sat beside him.

His uncle said nothing at first. That was Daniel's way; Tomas had learned it over the weeks on the river and the trail, that Daniel Halvorsen was a man who believed silence was a form of respect and not, as Tomas's mother had believed, a form of ambush. They sat in the frost-stiff grass, and below them the scree field lay in the sun, innocent, a grey fan of stone that had nearly killed them and was now just geology.

"Your mother carried you over a flood when you were three," Daniel said finally. "Did she ever tell you?"

Tomas shook his head. His mother did not tell stories about before. Before was a country she had emigrated from and rarely mentioned.

"Two meters of water in the streets, the year the levee broke at San Miguel. She waded the whole district with you on her shoulders. Everyone told her to wait for the boats. She said the boats had other people to save." Daniel turned a stone over in his fingers, absently, the way he did when he was thinking. "I've thought about that story for fifteen years. I've told it at dinners. I've told it to Ines, to Sefu, to anyone who'd listen. And this morning, watching you take Ama Suri on your back, I realized I've been telling it wrong my whole life. It isn't a story about your mother's courage. It's a story about where you come from. I just never met the person it was about until now."

Tomas looked at him. His uncle's face was weather-burned and frost-cracked and there was a grey stubble on his jaw that no one had shaved in weeks, and his eyes were wet, and he was not hiding it, and that — more than the scree, more than the fall, more than the hand on the boulder — was the thing that made Tomas's chest hurt.

"You saw me," Tomas said. It came out before he could dress it up. "On the river. You saw me. In the galley, the night I came out of the cargo hold. You looked right through me."

"I know." Daniel didn't flinch from it. "I saw a stowaway. A problem to be fed and managed. I was so busy carrying the expedition — the money, the letters, the archive, all of it — that I looked at a boy carrying nothing and saw nothing." He put the stone down. "And then I watched you hang off a mountainside with ninety-one years of the valley's memory on your back, and you didn't drop her, and you didn't drop yourself, and you sang." His voice caught, just slightly. "You made up your own line to her song. Do you know what that is? That's what my father did. That's what the scholars in the valley do. You don't just carry the old thing. You add to it. That's how a thing survives."

Tomas didn't know what to say to that, so he said the truest thing, which was small: "My legs are still shaking."

"Good," Daniel said. "Let them. Shaking means you were honest with the mountain." He stood, and put out his hand — not to help Tomas up, Tomas noticed, but the other way: he waited, hand extended, for Tomas to take it and rise on his own strength, and when Tomas did, his uncle gripped his forearm the way Sefu gripped the arms of the men he trusted on a rope. Crewman's grip. Not uncle to boy.

"Apprentice," Daniel said. "When we're down off this mountain, we'll make it formal. If you'll have us."

Tomas's throat closed. He nodded, because nodding was all there was, and over Daniel's shoulder he saw Ama Suri watching them from beside the fire Sefu was building, and she raised one hand — not waving, blessing, or something older than either — and then she turned to Kel and said something in her own language, and Kel laughed, and Tomas caught only the last word of it, the one she'd said twice now, the one that meant, he was fairly sure, *commentary*.

* * *

They came down off the pass shoulder in the long gold light of late afternoon, and the world below them changed the way the world does on the far side of mountains: the air thickened, the frost gave way to wet green, and far below, threading through the valley floor, Tomas saw a river again — brown, swollen, fast, running the wrong way, running *down*, running toward everything they had left behind.

Edmund stopped at the trail's edge and looked at it for a long time. Tomas watched her read it the way she read everything, and he saw her face do the thing it did when the river told her bad news.

"That's not snowmelt," she said quietly, to Sefu, to all of them. "That's early. That's the monsoon's edge, and it's a week ahead of itself."

Sefu looked at the brown water, then at the sky, where the clean high blue of the pass was already bruising at the southern horizon with a color that had nothing innocent in it.

"Then we are done resting," Sefu said.

Behind them, far up the trail they had come by, a sound drifted down on the wind — faint, rhythmic, almost like the song Ama Suri had sung on the scree. Almost. But wrong. Too regular. Too many voices.

Tomas turned, hand shading his eyes, and counted the figures moving along the ridge they had crossed that morning.

They were not alone on the mountain anymore.
