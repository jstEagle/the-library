## 32. The Descent

The snowline announced itself the way a border announces itself: not with a wall, but with a change in what lived there.

Ines noticed it first as color. For eleven days above the pass they had traveled through a world reduced to three shades — white, gray, and the bruised blue of shadow — and then, quite suddenly, on the morning after the cave, there was green. Not the green of the lowlands, lush and careless, but a thin, determined green: lichen spreading across wet stone, moss furring the north faces of boulders, a single stunted pine leaning out of a crack in the cliff like a man testing ice with his foot.

She stopped on the trail and breathed, and the breath itself was different. Warmer. Heavier. It lay on her tongue like something with weight to it, and she understood before anyone said it aloud.

"The monsoon," she said.

Sefu, ahead of her with the eldest guide's arm over his shoulder, did not break stride. "Smell it. That's not mountain air. That's river air that's been lifted up and carried two hundred miles and dropped here still holding the river in it."

Edmund came up behind them, and Ines watched her turn her face into the wind the way a sailor does — eyes half-closed, reading something invisible. The captain had been quiet since the cave, since the eldest's story had settled over all of them like ash. Quiet, but not hollow. There was a new economy to her movements, as if some weight she'd carried for years had been set down and her body hadn't yet learned its own lightness.

"How far below us is the river?" Edmund asked.

"Two days' walk if the trails hold," Sefu said. "Three if they don't."

"And the flood?"

Sefu was silent for a moment. Above them the sky was still white, but lower down, where the peaks stepped down toward the basin, the clouds had gone a color Ines knew from her survey work — that particular yellow-green bruise that meant water was being gathered somewhere in enormous quantities and would be spent without mercy.

"The rivers come down first," Sefu said at last. "Before the rain reaches us down here, the headwaters are already drowning. Everything we cross today will be higher tomorrow."

Ines got out her notebook and wrote that down, because writing things down was how she kept fear in its proper compartment. *Everything we cross today will be higher tomorrow.* Beneath it she sketched the profile of the descent as Sefu described it: three river crossings, each fed by valleys they could not see, then the long shelf of forest above the gorge, then the mission road, then — if the boat still floated, if the sisters still guarded her, if Vane's men had not found her first — the *Mariana* waiting at the last navigable landing.

They walked down into the warm air, and the mountain gave way around them like a held breath being released.

* * *

The first river had been, on Sefu's map of memory, a stream you crossed without wetting your knees.

It was now a brown muscle thirty feet across, moving with that smooth, terrible speed that deep water has — no whitecaps, no drama, just power sliding past them with a sound like continuous applause. Debris rode the current: branches, a whole tree traveling crown-first, a section of bamboo fencing that must have belonged to some farm already underwater upstream.

The valley guides went along the bank, reading it the way Edmund read currents, until the eldest raised his hand and pointed to a place where the water spread wide and shallow over a gravel bar.

"There," he said. "While it is still a bar. By evening it will be a channel."

They crossed in a chain, roped together, the strongest positioned to break the current for the weakest. Ines went fourth, and the water took her to mid-thigh and pushed with a force that made her understand, viscerally, the arithmetic of drowning: it wasn't depth that killed you in water like this, it was the simple multiplication of speed against your own small weight. She fixed her eyes on Tomas's back ahead of her — the boy had insisted on position three, beside Sefu, and no one had argued with him anymore, not since the scree — and counted her steps, and came out the other side shaking, laughing a little, alive.

Daniel Halvorsen crossed last with the archive copies strapped to his chest in oilcloth, the way a man carries an infant across a flooded street. When he reached the bank he stood dripping and looked back the way they'd come, up toward the pass, which was already hidden behind cloud.

"Snow up there," he said quietly. "Rain down here. And between them, everything we're carrying." He touched the oilcloth bundle once, lightly, the way you'd touch a wound to check it was still closed.

Ines opened her notebook against her knee. "How much survived?" she asked. "Of the archive. Honestly."

Daniel was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Around them the expedition was repacking loads, wringing out rope, redistributing weight; the eldest sat on a stone being tended by one of the younger guides, his fallen leg wrapped and splinted, his face composed into the patience of a man who had decided not to be a burden and was paying daily for the decision.

"Half," Daniel said finally. "Maybe less than half. The fire took the oldest chambers first — the ones cut into the dry hillside. The paper records, the bark codices, most of the genealogy cords." He looked at his hands. "My father financed the reservoir that will drown what's left. I led an expedition to document a living culture, and I will carry out copies of a dead man's library and call it rescue." His voice didn't rise. That was the worst of it, Ines thought. Grief that shouted was grief that still believed someone might listen. This was quieter than that.

"It isn't nothing," she said. "Copies aren't ashes. What you're carrying can be recopied, translated, argued over in courtrooms. Ashes can't testify."

Daniel looked at her then, really looked, and something in his face shifted — not hope exactly, but the recognition of a job that could still be done. "You sound like my father's letters," he said. "The ones in the cairn. He wrote like that. Like every fact was a small act of defiance."

"I know," Ines said. "I read them."

* * *

The second crossing nearly killed the eldest.

The trail down to it had become a stream itself, water sheeting across the switchbacks, and by the time they reached the river it had swallowed the gravel bar entirely and was eating at the near bank in great soft bites, undercutting the roots of trees that leaned out over the current like drunks over a railing. Sefu stood at the edge for a long time, saying nothing, and Ines had learned enough of him to know that his silences were measurements.

"We don't cross here," he said finally. "We go up. Two hours upstream there's a gorge where the banks pinch together. Narrower water, faster — but rock underneath instead of this mud, and a fallen trunk wedged across it. My father used it when I was a boy. If it's still wedged."

Two hours upstream meant two hours of trail that climbed away from their descent and then dropped again, and the eldest could not do it unaided, and everyone knew it, and no one said it, which in Ines's experience was how groups told the truth to themselves.

It was Tomas who solved it, or rather Tomas who refused to let it remain unsolved. "Then we build a litter," he said. "Poles and the spare rope and the oilcloth. Six carriers, rotating. I've carried Uncle Anders's packs since I was nine; I know how to balance a load that argues with you."

"You're fourteen," said one of the expedition men, not unkindly.

"I'm crew," Tomas said, and the word landed in the group like a coin dropped in a quiet room, and Ines saw Edmund, standing apart with the compass in her hand, close her fingers around it slowly, as if the boy had said something that belonged to her too.

They built the litter in forty minutes. It was ugly and it worked. They took turns — six carriers at a time, Sefu calling the rotation, the eldest protesting the whole way with a dignity so complete it became almost comic, insisting on directing his own transport like a general reviewing troops from an undignified sedan chair. Ines carried her turns and counted them, and between turns she sketched: the litter, the trail, the wedge of the gorge ahead, the faces of people doing hard things well. Her notebooks were filling. She had begun, somewhere above the snowline, to understand that she was not recording an expedition anymore. She was recording a passage — of people, of a truth, of a debt being paid in a currency no ledger recognized.

The fallen trunk was still wedged.

It spanned the gorge at perhaps chest height, slick black with spray, the water beneath it running fast and green-white through a gap barely twelve feet wide. Sefu went first with a rope, anchored it to a root mass on the far side, and came back. Then the loads went across, then the guides, then the others, one at a time, sidestepping along the trunk with hands on the rope.

The eldest refused the litter across the log. "I have crossed worse on narrower things," he said, and he did — slowly, with Sefu's hand under his elbow and the whole party holding its collective breath, and when he stepped off onto the far bank he turned and looked back at the gorge and said something in his own language that the young guide beside Ines translated in a whisper: *"The river remembers being small. We remember being many."*

Ines wrote it down immediately, verbatim, and marked it with three lines in the margin. Some sentences were load-bearing. You learned to feel them.

* * *

They camped that night on a shelf above the third river, and the rain found them.

Not the mountain's cold sleet but true rain, warm and vertical and vast, arriving not as weather but as a condition — the monsoon's leading edge reaching even here, soaking through canvas, turning the fire into a hissing argument. They huddled under an overhang, twenty-three people and the oilcloth bundles, and Ines sat with her back against stone and listened to the dark make noise.

Below them, faint through the rain, they could hear the third river.

It did not sound like a river anymore. It sounded like freight — a continuous rolling roar, deeper than hearing, felt in the stone under her spine. Sefu had gone down in the dark with a lantern and come back with water shining to his thighs and an expression Ines had never seen on him: something close to respect for an enemy.

"The ford is gone," he reported. "Gone meaning the ford doesn't exist. Gone meaning the river has taken the approach trail on both sides." He crouched by the ruined fire. "We wait for it to drop or we find another way down to the mission road. Waiting costs a day. Maybe two."

"A day," Edmund said, "is the boat."

No one answered that, because everyone had done the same arithmetic. The *Mariana* at her landing, the sisters guarding her if they could, the flood coming down the main river behind them like a tide with a grudge. If the monsoon crested before they reached her, the landings would drown, the moorings would drown, and the whole journey — the pass, the ambush, the burned archive, the eldest carried over gorges — would end stranded on a mountainside, safe and useless, while the truth they carried lost its window.

Ines found Edmund at the edge of the overhang, watching the rain fall through the space where the stars should have been.

"You're going to suggest running it," Ines said. "Whatever Sefu finds tomorrow. You're going to look at something no sane pilot would attempt and start calculating."

Edmund didn't deny it. Rain ran off the brim of her hat and she let it. "I ran the Sandbar at low water with a hole in my hull and a debt collector's letter in my pocket," she said. "That was a woman with nothing to lose trying to prove she had something. This is different." She turned the compass over in her fingers — Ines saw the new plate Daniel had fitted, bright metal against old brass. "This is a woman with too much to lose, going carefully."

"Carefully," Ines repeated. "That's a new word in your vocabulary. Should I mark the date?"

Edmund smiled — a real one, small and tired. "Mark it in pencil," she said. "I might need to erase it tomorrow."

* * *

Sefu found the other way at dawn, and it was worse than the ford, which somehow made it better, because at least it was possible.

A goat path, he called it — a ledge traversing the gorge wall fifty feet above the flood, wide enough for one loaded person in most places and for faith in all of them. It had been used by salt traders once, he said, before the road; his grandfather had carried salt along it. The eldest listened to the description, examined the sky, and delivered his verdict with the gravity of a court: "The mountain is washing its face. We should not stand on its cheekbone. But we have no other cheekbones. Go."

They went, single file, roped in sections, the litter broken down and the eldest supported between two guides with his splinted leg held clear of the rock. Ines went in the middle of the line, where she could neither lead nor delay anyone, and kept her eyes on the placement of her feet and her mind on the work of not looking down — except that she did look down, once, deliberately, because she was who she was.

The river filled the gorge floor like a brown sea moving fast. Whole trees went by beneath her, traveling at the speed of trains. Where tributaries entered from side ravines, the water stood up in ridges and collided, throwing spray fifty feet into the air, and the spray drifted across the path in warm gusts that tasted of earth and drowned forests.

And yet — this was the thing she stopped to write later, braced against the rock with rain hammering her notebook — *and yet the air is kind.* After weeks of cold, warmth itself felt like forgiveness. Below the snowline the world was running green again: ferns exploding from every crack, orchids hanging from the wet branches, birds calling through the rain in voices so loud and various it sounded like celebration. The mountain was not dying as it gave way to the lowlands. It was being born into a different shape.

Relief and urgency, she wrote. Both at once, all day. The body relaxes; the clock accelerates.

By late afternoon the path descended to meet the mission road — an actual road, rutted and streaming, but a road — and the party came down onto it like divers surfacing, blinking, laughing shakily, slapping each other's shoulders. The eldest was set down gently and demanded to stand, and stood, leaning on Sefu, looking down the long green corridor of the valley toward the distant smudge of the river plain.

"How far to the landing?" Edmund asked Sefu.

"Half a day. Less if the road holds." He paused. "More if the river's taken the ferry crossing at Kessu. Then it's a full day around."

Ines saw Edmund do the arithmetic again — Ines was learning to see it happen, a flicker behind the captain's eyes, the pilot's constant silent ledger of water against time — and saw the flicker stop, and sharpen.

"Then we don't sleep," Edmund said. "We eat walking. Anyone who can't keep up says so now, and I'll say something kind, and then we'll keep up anyway." She looked down the road, at the rain, at the country between them and the boat. "The *Mariana* has waited for me through worse than this. But she shouldn't have to wait through a flood."

They moved. And Ines, falling into the rhythm of the march with her notebook zipped inside her coat against the rain, let herself feel it for one full minute — the pure animal relief of descending, of warmth, of green, of a road that went somewhere instead of up — before she made herself take it back out and look at it honestly.

Because relief was a liar. Relief said *the hard part is over*, and the hard part was not over; the hard part had simply changed clothes. Up in the snow it had worn white and silence. Down here it wore water and time. Somewhere behind them the monsoon was cresting over the high valleys and pouring itself into every river that fed the one they needed to reach, and somewhere ahead the *Mariana* rode at her mooring on a river that would rise all night, and somewhere between those two facts was half a day of muddy road and twenty-three exhausted people carrying the memory of a civilization in oilcloth on their backs.

Tomas fell into step beside her, soaked to the skin, mud to the eyebrows, grinning like the rain was a personal gift.

"You're smiling," Ines said. "Explain yourself."

"I'm thinking about her face," Tomas said. "The boat's. When she sees us coming. Uncle says boats know their people." He shrugged, suddenly shy about it. "Is that stupid?"

Ines looked at him — at the boy who had stowed away and frozen on a bridge and carried an old man over scree, who was becoming, mile by mile, someone the journey had made on purpose — and she found she couldn't give him the cartographer's answer, the careful qualified one.

"No," she said. "I think it's the truest navigation there is. Everything else is just instruments."

Ahead of them, Edmund laughed at something Sefu said — actually laughed, a sound Ines filed away as rare — and lengthened her stride, and the whole column lengthened with her, drawn forward the way water is drawn by a fall it cannot yet see.

They came around the last shoulder of the ridge as the light was failing, and the road dropped away before them through terraced fields gone wild with rain, and there, far below, where the valley narrowed to the river —

Smoke.

A thin thread of it rising from the bend where the landing ought to be. Too much smoke for a cookfire. Too steady for a signal fire. And the wrong color — not the gray of wood, but the darker, oilier gray of something that had been burning for a long time and been fed.

Ines heard Edmund stop breathing for one full second.

Then the captain was running, and the whole descent began to run with her, down through the rain and the failing light, toward the smoke and the river and whatever was left at the landing of the boat that had brought them all this way.
