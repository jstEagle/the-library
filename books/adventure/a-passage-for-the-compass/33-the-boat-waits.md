## 33. The Boat Waits

The river announced itself before they saw it.

For two days they had been descending through country that changed by the hour — the high cold grass giving way to bamboo, the bamboo to broad-leafed green, the air thickening like broth until every breath felt like drinking. Sefu had gone quiet the way he always did when the land began to speak to him, and the eldest guide, carried now on a litter of lashed poles that Tomas and two of the valley men refused to put down, had begun to hum something low and repetitive, a traveling song, Edmund guessed, sung by people who had always known the way home even when home was taken from them.

Then, around a shoulder of the mountain, the sound reached them: not the polite trickle of the upper streams but the full-throated roar of the river in monsoon, brown and swollen, running fast with the rain of a thousand unseen hills.

Edmund stopped on the trail and stood listening, and something in her chest that had been clenched since the pass — since the narrows, since the ambush, since the fire, since Vane's face in the snow — began, very slowly, to unclench.

"There she is," Tomas said beside her. He was soaked to the skin, mud to his knees, carrying more of the archive copies in a waterproofed roll on his back than any boy his size should have been able to bear, and he was grinning like a fool.

"She," Edmund said. "You've met her once."

"I've heard enough about her to last a lifetime." He shifted the roll on his shoulders. "Besides, Ines says a captain always knows."

Ines, a few paces back with Sefu, said nothing, but Edmund caught the look that passed between them — the one they thought she didn't see, the one that said *let her have the moment, she's earned it* — and Edmund turned away before they could see what it did to her face.

The trail dropped steeply through the last stand of forest, and then the trees opened, and there was the landing.

She knew it before she knew why she knew it. A shelf of packed earth above a gravel bar, a leaning fig tree whose roots had grown around an old mooring post, the post itself scarred with the rope-grooves of fifty years of boats. Her father's stories lived here. She had stood on this bank as a child — or she had stood on a bank like it, on a hundred banks like it, and the memory of one had worn the grooves of all of them. The river ran wide and high and fast beyond it, the color of strong tea, carrying branches and whole trees in its current, and the rain came down in the soft steady way of the monsoon's edge, not the violence of the storm but the patience of it, the sense that the sky had all the time in the world.

And there, at the mooring post, riding at her lines with her deck awash in puddles and her paint gone dull as old pewter — the *Marianne*.

Edmund did not run. She wanted to. Her legs wanted to, her whole body wanted to, the way a body wants to run toward a light in a dark house. But she walked, because captains walk, and because some part of her was already counting what the flood could have done, what looters could have done, what three months of absence could do to a boat left alone on a wild river, and she did not want to arrive at the answer running.

The others came down behind her, and she heard them stop and go quiet, the way people go quiet at a graveside or a birth.

The boat had been looted. She saw it from thirty paces. The cabin door hung open on one hinge. The canvas over the forward hold had been cut away and was gone. The rail brass was stripped — she could see the pale scars where it had been pried loose — and the wheelhouse windows were broken, and something dark had been burned in a small fire on her foredeck, right where the paint was newest, right where she had knelt with a brush only a week before she'd taken the Halvorsen commission.

Tomas made a sound beside her, an angry animal sound, and started forward, and Edmund put out an arm and stopped him.

"Wait."

"Captain—"

"Wait." She was looking past the boat, at the bank. "We're not alone."

Two figures had come out of the tree line above the landing. Women, in the grey habits and white wimples of the mission order Edmund knew from the river towns — the sisters who ran the dispensary at San Cristóbal, who vaccinated the river children and buried the river dead and asked nothing of anyone. One was old, small and straight-backed, her face a network of fine lines like a dried riverbed. The other was young, maybe Tomas's age, carrying a shotgun broken open over her arm with the ease of someone who had learned it out of necessity.

The old sister came down to the landing at her own pace, unhurried, and stopped an arm's length from Edmund, and looked her over — the mud, the frost-cracked lips, the bandage on Sefu's arm, the litter with the eldest — with the frank appraisal of a woman who had seen every kind of arrival a river could deliver.

"Edmund Joubert," she said.

"Sister Marguerite." Edmund heard her own voice come out rough. "You know me."

"You ferried us up from San Cristóbal two years ago, and the year before that, and the year before that. Vaccines, and vestments, and once a piano, God forgive us, in the wet season." The old woman's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "You never took a coin. You said the mission had paid enough into this river already."

"I said that?"

"You did." Sister Marguerite's eyes went past her, to the others, to the litter, and softened. "And now look what the river has brought back to you."

* * *

They got the eldest aboard first, of course. That was not a decision; it was simply what happened, the way water finds its level. The sisters had a stretcher, and the valley men would not surrender their burden to strangers, and so it took six people and a great deal of careful, wordless cooperation to bring the old man down the bank and through the broken cabin door and onto the bunk that had been Edmund's own, in the stern, where the ride was kindest.

The eldest had not spoken since the cave — since the story he had told them by firelight, the story of the promise and the betrayal, the story that now traveled with them in Ines's copies and in his own memoried voice, the last living version of a thing the archive had recorded in a hundred pages that had burned. He was exhausted past speech, but his eyes were open, and as they settled him he looked around the little cabin — at the chart table, at the brass fittings long since stripped, at the water-stained photograph of a riverboat Edmund's father had captained before her — and he said something soft in his own language to the young sister, who answered him in the same tongue, and both of them seemed satisfied.

Edmund stood in the doorway and watched, and did not understand a word, and understood all of it.

"You speak it," she said to the young sister, when the eldest was sleeping.

"Enough." The girl — Sister Lucia, the older one called her — racked the broken shotgun in a corner of the wheelhouse with the tenderness other people gave to instruments. "He taught me some, on the nights he couldn't sleep. He's been here two months." She glanced at Edmund. "You didn't know?"

"I didn't know anything. I left the boat at this landing with a note for a man who was supposed to watch her, and the note said I'd be back inside the season."

"The man came," Sister Lucia said. "Twice. Then the flood came, and the looters came with it — they always come with the flood, they know what the high water covers and what it uncovers — and after the second visit the man did not come again." She said it without accusation, which was somehow worse. "We heard the boat was yours. We moved what we could carry — your charts, your tools, your father's things from the cabin. They're at the mission, half a day downriver. We could not carry everything. We could not stop them from everything." She looked at the scarred foredeck, the burned patch. "But we could stay. Sister Marguerite says a thing that is guarded is not the same as a thing that is lost."

* * *

They worked through what remained of the day, and Edmund learned the full inventory of her losses the way you learn a wound — by touching it, all of it, at once.

The engine was intact. That was the mercy at the center of it, the mercy she kept returning to like a tongue to a broken tooth: the looters had been river people, opportunists, not engineers. The brass was gone, the windows, the canvas, the small boat that had hung on davits astern, every coil of spare line, the ship's bell — the bell, her father's bell, and that one hurt worse than she expected and she filed the hurt away to be opened later, alone — and the fuel had been siphoned down to fumes. But the engine was whole. Sefu, who turned out to know engines the way he knew trails, had the intake open within the hour and pronounced it sound, needing only cleaning and fuel and faith.

The fuel was the problem. The mission had kerosene for lamps, and there was a trading post two bends downriver that might have diesel if the flood hadn't cut it off, and the flood had almost certainly cut it off. Edmund stood in the wheelhouse at dusk, over the broken glass she had swept into a bucket, and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic did not come out in her favor.

"We'll make it," Ines said from the doorway. She had been copying all day — she and two of the expedition scholars, drying the salvaged pages by the cabin stove, re-inking the water-blurred ones, the archive copies passing through their hands like patients through a field hospital. Her fingers were stained black to the second knuckle. "You always make it. It's becoming tedious."

"The river's up four meters and running at flood speed. I've got maybe eight hours of fuel, a stripped boat, a crew of eleven, and a passenger who can't be jostled. I don't have a route. I have a prayer."

"You have the sisters."

Edmund looked at her.

"Sister Marguerite has been on this river longer than you've been alive," Ines said. "And she stayed with your boat for three months against armed men with a shotgun and a girl. Ask her what she knows about the flood channels. Ask her what this bank was before it was a bank." Ines came into the wheelhouse and put her stained hand flat on the chart table, on the space where the charts used to be. "You keep thinking you're alone with this. You have been wrong about that since the sandbar."

* * *

Sister Marguerite came aboard after dark, in the rain, and Edmund fed her tea from the mission's own kettle, borrowed back along with everything else, and the old woman sat in the wheelhouse on the one intact chair and looked out at the black flooded river with the expression of someone reading a familiar letter.

"You want to know how to run her in this," she said.

"I want to know if it can be done."

"Everything can be done. The question is what it costs." She drank. "The flood channels change every year, but the old ones remember. Your father knew that. He ran this river with a man who used to say the water has a long memory and no forgiveness, and the only thing you can do is have a longer memory than the water."

Edmund's hands went still around her cup.

"Who said that?"

"A river pilot. Dead now, I should think. He and your father used to argue about it at this very landing, back when the mission was a tent and I was young enough to be embarrassed by my own opinions." She looked at Edmund over the rim of her cup, and her eyes were old and kind and pitiless. "Halvorsen. That was the man's name. You'd know the family."

The rain came down on the wheelhouse roof, and the river roared past in the dark, and Edmund sat with the name in her lap like something heavy that had been handed to her without warning.

"Yes," she said finally. "I'd know the family."

"Mm." Sister Marguerite set down the cup. "Then you know more than you did a month ago, and less than you will. That's how the river works. It gives things back a piece at a time." She rose, joints cracking, and gathered her shawl. "There's a channel on the far side, past the fig tree, that the looters don't know and the flood hasn't closed. It joins the main river below the worst of the current. Your father used it. He called it the sisters' passage, because the mission women showed it to him one year when the flood had him trapped above this landing for six weeks." She paused at the wheelhouse door. "He never wrote it on any chart. He said some things you keep in your body or you lose them. Come to the mission tomorrow and I'll walk you along the bank as far as the channel mouth. After that, Captain, it's your body."

* * *

That night Edmund slept in the wheelhouse, on a pallet the sisters had left, with the broken window boarded over and the rain drumming on the boards like fingers.

She woke once, near morning, to a sound she couldn't place — not the river, not the rain. She came up off the pallet with her heart going and stood at the dark doorway, and found Tomas on the foredeck, sitting against the wheelhouse wall out of the rain, with the archive roll across his knees and the eldest's blanket around his shoulders, keeping watch.

"You're supposed to be asleep," she said.

"So are you."

"I'm the captain."

"You're a person who's been running on fear for a month," Tomas said. It was not disrespectful. It was simply a fact, delivered the way he delivered facts now, in the flat sure voice of someone who had crossed a gorge and carried an old man over scree and was no longer anyone's stowaway. "Sister Lucia took the first watch. I took this one. Ines has the next. Sefu wouldn't sleep at all until we made a roster, so we made a roster." He looked up at her. "You don't have to stand over everything anymore. That's what a crew is. That's what we are."

Edmund stood in the doorway a long moment, rain-smell and river-smell and the sound of the water in the dark, and felt something shift in her that she had no chart for — the same feeling, she realized, as the moment in the whiteout when the compass had steadied under her hand. Not certainty. Direction.

"Four hours," she said. "Then you wake Ines."

"Four hours," Tomas agreed.

She went back inside and lay down, and this time she slept like the drowned sleep, deep and dreamless, and if the boat moved beneath her in the current, tugging at her lines like a living thing impatient to be gone, that was only the river. That was only the river, and the flood behind them, and the mission half a day downriver where her father's charts waited in the sisters' keeping, and beyond that the long run to the delta with everything they carried — the copies, the testimony, the eldest, the truth — and a river rising all the way.

In the grey of the morning she stood at the broken wheelhouse window with tea going cold in her hands and watched Sister Marguerite come down the bank through the rain toward the landing, walking with the unhurried certainty of a woman who had promised to show her a passage her father had never written down, and Edmund thought: *the world is not only debts.*

The world was also this. A boat guarded three months by women she had never paid. A channel kept in a body for thirty years, waiting to be given back. A boy on watch who had made a roster so an old guide could sleep.

"Captain," Sister Marguerite called up, when she reached the mooring post, and there was something in her voice now that hadn't been there last night — something quick, something that had crossed her face like a shadow crossing water. "You'll want to come and see this. We found it on the bank after the last flood, wedged in the fig roots, and I wasn't sure whose it was. But seeing your boat—" She held up her hand, and in it, dripping rainwater, turning slowly so the early light caught the metal, was the ship's bell.

Her father's bell. And stamped into the bronze beside the maker's mark, where Edmund had looked ten thousand times and never thought to look closely, was a second mark, small and worn: two letters, joined, the way partners join their names.

The bell swung gently in the old woman's grip, and Edmund came down the bank to meet it.
