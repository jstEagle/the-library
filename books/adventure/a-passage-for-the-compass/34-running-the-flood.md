## 34. Running the Flood

The river met them a mile below the mission, and it did not look like the river Edmund had known for twenty years.

It looked like something being born.

Where the channel had run narrow and brown-green between sandbars she could name like old friends, there was now a single broad muscle of water, swollen taut against both banks, carrying whole trees end-over-end like matchsticks. The current came at them in long rolling seams, and where those seams crossed — where two currents argued over who owned the deeper water — the river stood up in waves that had no business existing on anything but open sea.

Edmund stood at the wheel with her hands light on the spokes and her weight forward on her toes, the way her father had taught her before she was tall enough to see over the brass rail. *Don't grip. Grip is fear wearing gloves.* The *Marta Celia* — looted of her cargo, stripped of her spare lines, her wheelhouse glass cracked by whatever hands had gone through her while Edmund was in the mountains — sat low in the water with thirty-one souls aboard and a tonne of oilcloth-wrapped archive lashed to her deck.

She had never been so heavy. She had never been so full.

"Starboard bank's eroding past the old ferry landing," Sefu said from beside her. He had not left the wheelhouse since they cast off. He stood with his arms folded, watching the water the way other men watched fire. "There'll be snags coming off it. Whole banks let go in flood."

"I see it." She did. A pale scar of raw earth along the right-hand shore, and beneath it, just under the surface, the ghost of something long and dark turning slowly in the current. "Ines — log that. When this is over I want every bar and snag we pass marked, because if the valley route opens, someone else will be running this water behind us."

Ines sat wedged in the corner of the wheelhouse with the chart board across her knees, pencil moving without looking up. "Marked. You realize half my fixes are useless now. The bars have moved."

"Then we make new ones." Edmund swung the wheel two spokes to port, easing the boat toward the seam where the current ran smoothest, threading between a drowned fencepost and a floating mass of cut cane. "That's all navigation ever was. Making new ones."

* * *

The first day was the hardest because it was the strangest.

Everything Edmund knew about this stretch of river was wrong. The bend below Kessel's Point, where she'd always hugged the inside line, now had its inside buried under ten feet of churning brown; the safe line was outside, hard against the willows, where the eddy sucked backward along the bank. The narrows above the drowned village — the village whose old woman had called her compass by a name Edmund didn't know — were no longer narrow at all. They were a lake with a current through it, and somewhere beneath that current lay the rooftops she remembered, and the church bell tower standing to its eaves in gray water like a man up to his chin.

Tomas spotted it first. He was up on the cargo hatch with Daniel's field glasses, keeping watch ahead as he'd appointed himself to do within an hour of casting off, and his voice came down the ladder tight with something between awe and grief.

"The village. Captain — you can see the village."

They all looked. Even Sefu, who had never known the village above water, looked. The bell tower rose out of the flood, and around it the current broke into a long V of white water where the submerged streets turned the flow aside, and for a moment nobody said anything, because the reservoir project had drowned this place on paper, in ledgers, in a meeting room in the river city — but here it was, drowned again by weather, and the difference between the two drownings seemed suddenly very small.

"That's where she lived," Edmund said quietly. "The woman with the compass."

"She knew the name," Ines said. "She said it belonged to the river before it belonged to anyone."

"Well." Edmund put the wheel over and gave the tower a wide, respectful berth, the way you'd pass a grave. "Now it belongs to the flood. Let's not argue with it."

They ran until the light went. That was the first lesson of flood-running: you did not stop when you were tired, you stopped when you could no longer read the water, because reading the water was the whole of the work. At dusk they tied off to a stand of ironwood on high ground above a side channel — Sefu chose the trees, testing each trunk with his shoulder — and ate rice and dried fish in shifts, half the crew always awake, watching the mooring lines and the rising marks Sefu had cut into the bark.

Daniel found Edmund at the rail near midnight. Below them the river slid past black and immense, carrying its freight of broken trees, and the sound of it was so large it made speech feel private even at a whisper.

"My father ran boats," Daniel said. "Before the dam money. Before all of it. He used to say a river in flood tells you exactly what it thinks of you."

"What does this one think?"

"That we're late." Daniel smiled, but it didn't hold. "Edmund. If we don't make the city before the monsoon peaks — the copies are all that's left. Half the archive burned. What's on this deck is what the world gets."

Edmund watched a whole fig tree go by, roots first, patient as a funeral barge.

"Then the world gets it," she said. "Get some sleep, Daniel. I need your eyes fresh tomorrow. You read country better than anyone aboard except Sefu, and Sefu can't be everywhere."

He went. She stayed at the rail a while longer, one hand resting on the wheelhouse doorframe, feeling through the wood the small tremor of the river working against her hull, patient, enormous, unimpressed by everything the people aboard carried — letters, maps, testimony, guilt, forgiveness. The river had carried worse and kept less.

* * *

The second day, the river tried to kill them twice.

The first time was the bridge.

There had been a footbridge at Marrow Cut — a rope-and-plank affair the mission sisters used, sagging, picturesque, forty feet above the dry-season water. Edmund had passed under it two hundred times. Now the flood stood only twelve feet below the anchor stumps, and the bridge itself was gone from its anchors but not from the river: it came at them around the bend like a sea serpent, sixty feet of tangled rope and plank riding the current's spine, planks cartwheeling, one end dipping and rising.

"Tomas!" Edmund's voice cracked across the deck before her mind finished forming the order. "Port bow, floating hazard — take the horn, three blasts, then keep sounding till it's past!"

Tomas hammered the horn. Daniel and two of the valley guides got the passengers down flat behind the hatch coaming. Ines braced the chart board and shouted depths from the lead line, which she'd insisted on rigging at dawn — *you can't fix what you can't measure, Captain* — and Edmund took the *Marta Celia* hard to starboard, toward the willow bank, gambling on the back-eddy there being strong enough to slow her while the bridge swept past downstream.

For eleven seconds nothing in the world existed but the wheel, the water, and the swinging wreck. The eddy caught them. The boat's head came round sluggish, heavy as she was, and the bridge went by off the port side close enough that Edmund could hear the ropes hiss, could have spat and hit it. One trailing plank clipped the rail aft and tore loose a stanchion with a crack like a rifle shot.

Then it was behind them, dwindling, and the river closed over the place where it had been as though nothing had happened.

"Stanchion," Sefu said. He hadn't moved from the wheelhouse. "I'll splice it."

"Log the bridge," Edmund said. Her hands, she noticed, were perfectly steady. Twenty years ago they wouldn't have been. "And log that I owe this boat an apology for the rail."

"You owe the boat nothing," Sefu said. "You owe yourself sleep. Tonight I take the middle watch at the wheel. You taught me enough on the lake crossing."

"You hate steering."

"Yes." His face didn't change. "Tonight I steer."

The second time was after dark, and it was worse, because it was quiet.

They were running the last hour of readable light, Edmund alone at the wheel, everyone else lashed and fed and dozing, when she felt it through her boots before she saw anything: a wrongness in the boat's motion, a stutter in the way the hull answered the water, like a word missing from a sentence she'd been reading aloud all day.

She throttled down. Not stopped — you did not stop in flood water, stopping was dying — but slowed, and swung the wheel gently, testing, and the stern answered a half-beat late.

"Sefu. Wake Ines. Sound the channel under us — something's down there."

What was down there, when Ines's lead line found it, was a sandbar that had grown a hundred yards overnight, fed by the eroding bank upstream, and its downstream tail curled directly across their line with barely four feet of water over it. Behind them the current pushed. Ahead of them the bar waited. And on either side, the channel edges were invisible in the dark, marked only by the different sound the water made over deep versus shallow — a sound Edmund's father had spent her childhood teaching her to hear.

"Kill the lamp," she said. "Everyone silent. I need to listen."

So they stood in the dark on a flooded river, thirty-one people making no sound at all, while Edmund Joubert listened to the water the way the old woman had listened to the name of a compass. Deep water spoke low and even, a held note. Shallow water broke its note, frayed it, added a hiss of sand in suspension. She turned the wheel a spoke at a time, following the deep note east, then south, feeling the bar slide past under the keel with six inches to spare, invisible, enormous, indifferent.

When they were clear, Ines exhaled beside her and said, "I couldn't hear anything."

"You weren't raised on it." Edmund realized she was smiling. "My father used to say the river talks to everyone, but it uses the language you learned as a child. His was this river. Mine too, I suppose. I just spent twenty years pretending I'd learned a different one."

"And now?"

"Now I'm going home down it," Edmund said, "at flood stage, with a library on my deck. Ask me tomorrow."

* * *

The third night the monsoon arrived properly, and the river and the sky became the same thing.

Rain came in sheets that erased the far bank, then the near bank, then the bow. Lightning walked the ridgelines upstream, and in each flash Edmund saw the river higher than the last flash had shown her — brown, muscular, crowned with foam, carrying the entire watershed downhill at once. The wind came in gusts that laid the rain flat and shoved the boat sideways like a hand on her shoulder.

Below the last gorge the valley opened, and with the opening the wind grew teeth. There was nothing to break it. The *Marta Celia*, overloaded, top-heavy with lashed crates, wallowed and shipped green water over the cargo hatch, and Edmund made the call she'd been dreading since the mission: they could not wait out the peak. Waiting meant the side channels would flood and the main channel would become unnavigable even for her; waiting meant the archive soaked, the guides stranded, the testimony late. And late, she thought of Vane in the city with his lawyers and his immunity and his employer's patience, meant lost.

"We run it," she told them, gathered dripping under the awning aft. "Three hours of open water to the city landing. It'll be the worst three hours this boat has ever seen. Some of you should be angry with me for asking."

Nobody looked angry. Tomas looked terrified and determined in equal measure, which Edmund privately considered the correct expression for the situation. Daniel looked at her steadily and said, "You got us up a mountain. Get us down a river."

They prepared the way she'd drilled them, without being asked, and that — more than the spliced stanchion, more than the soundings logged in Ines's careful hand, more than the archive wrapped in oilcloth and tarps triple-lashed to the hatch — was the thing that made her throat tighten. Sefu took the bow with a lantern shuttered to a slit, calling depth and debris. Ines knelt over the chart board under an oilskin canopy, calling the line. Daniel and the guides manned the lashings and the bail. Tomas worked the horn and the throttle telegraph, relaying her orders, his voice cracking only once in three hours and never on the important words.

The city landing appeared out of the rain the way salvation always does: later than you hoped, sooner than you feared, smaller than it had any right to be.

The last mile was the worst water of the whole run — the river spreading wide where the delta began, currents braiding and warring, whirlpools spinning off the channel edges like slow dark eyes. Edmund flew her by feel and memory and Ines's voice in her ear, and the *Marta Celia* groaned and rolled and drove through it, and when the landing pilings finally loomed out of the rain, Sefu threw the heaving line first try, and twenty hands hauled her in against the current, and the boat kissed the fenders with a bump so gentle that Edmund laughed out loud, once, a sound she didn't recognize as coming from herself.

They stood on the flooded landing in the hammering rain, thirty-one people and a library, alive.

Ines found Edmund at the gangway. Rain ran off the captain's hat brim in a solid curtain, and underneath it she was grinning like a girl.

"Three days," Ines said. "You said three days and nights, and it was three days and nights."

"I've been wrong before." Edmund looked back at her boat — battered, leaking at one seam, her rail short a stanchion, her decks crowded with sleeping guides and oilcloth-wrapped truth — and felt something settle in her chest that had been unsettled for twenty years. "Get the archive covered double. Nothing touches that canvas until a judge does."

Down the landing, through the rain, lanterns were already moving. Figures under umbrellas, hurrying — a newspaper man who'd ridden the last ferry out on rumor, a court clerk who never slept, and, standing apart beneath the customs shed roof where the lamplight reached, a thin figure in a dry coat, watching them come, holding a telegram in one gloved hand.

Vane.

Edmund's grin faded slowly into something else. She looked at the oilcloth bundles being carried carefully down the gangway, at Daniel walking beside them with his father's letters in an inner pocket, at Tomas standing guard over the stack with his uncle's hat pulled down against the rain.

"Well," she said to Ines, stepping onto the planks. "Let's go tell him what the river thinks."
