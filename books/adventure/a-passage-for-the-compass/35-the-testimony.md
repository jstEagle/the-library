## 35. The Testimony

Daniel Halvorsen had imagined this day a hundred times on the mountain, and in none of those imaginings did the courthouse smell of mildew and fried plantains.

The river city's Hall of Justice was a colonial relic gone soft at the joints, its marble steps worn into shallow bowls by a century of feet, its shutters open to let the monsoon air move through in slow, wet currents. A ceiling fan turned with the indifference of something that had outlived every argument ever conducted beneath it. Daniel sat in the second row with his hands folded, wearing the one clean shirt he owned, and watched the copies of the archive being carried into the chamber in oilcloth bundles — forty-one of them, wrapped by Ines's own hands, each labeled in her small precise script. They looked less like evidence than like relics. In a way, he supposed they were.

The judge was a woman named Ondine Ferreiro, sixty or thereabouts, with reading glasses on a chain and the unhurried manner of someone who had learned that speed was a kind of surrender. She had agreed to hear the petition on a Tuesday morning between two land disputes, as if the drowning of a valley were a matter to be slotted between fences.

"State your name for the record," her clerk said, when Daniel was called.

"Daniel Halvorsen. Leader of the Halvorsen expedition."

"You were reported missing," Judge Ferreiro observed, not looking up from her papers. "Eleven days, then eleven weeks. There was talk of a memorial service in the capital. Your father attended, I'm told."

"My father is dead, Your Honor."

"I know." She looked up then, and her eyes were shrewd and tired. "I knew him. He sat where you're sitting once, twenty years ago, arguing about water rights. He lost." A pause. "Perhaps he learned something from losing. Proceed."

* * *

They had come down off the mountain nine days ago, and Daniel still hadn't stopped being surprised by flat ground.

The descent had been everything the ascent was not: fast, loud, green going gold going brown as the snowline gave way to cloud forest gave way to the drowned basin, until at last the river opened before them, swollen and brown and running fast with the monsoon's first full fury, and Edmund Joubert stood on the muddy bank looking at her boat and said nothing at all for a long time.

The boat had been looted — the brass fittings stripped, the cabin door hanging by one hinge, the engine cover pried open and left like a ribcage. But she floated. And she was guarded: three mission sisters in grey habits had taken turns sleeping aboard since the day Edmund failed to return, armed with a shotgun older than any of them and a conviction that Captain Joubert would come back, because Captain Joubert always came back, because some debts were paid in kindness rather than currency.

Sister Marta had fed them all rice and beans until they could barely stand, and refused thanks with a wave of her spoon. "You ferried our medicine upriver three seasons for nothing," she said. "This is arithmetic, not charity."

Edmund had wept, briefly and privately, standing at the rail while the sisters' boat pulled away. Daniel had seen it and pretended he hadn't. Some things you owed people the courtesy of not witnessing.

Then came three days and nights of flood-running that Daniel would remember until he died — Edmund at the wheel with her sleeves rolled and her eyes half-closed, reading the river the way scholars read manuscripts, calling depths and channels in a low voice while Tomas worked the winch, Ines called soundings, Sefu stood at the bow with a pole like a figurehead, and the valley guides clung to the cargo and sang, softly, songs about water. The boat groaned and shipped water and survived. On the third night, running dark through the delta's braided channels, Edmund had laughed — actually laughed — when a floating tree missed them by an arm's length, and Daniel understood that she was not afraid anymore, and that this frightened her more than fear had.

Now they were here, in the city, in the courtroom, and the archive lay in bundles on the clerk's table, and the truth was about to be spoken aloud in a room with a fan in it.

* * *

Ines testified first.

She had prepared maps — of course she had; Daniel had watched her work on them by lamplight for six nights straight, redrawing the valley from memory and survey notes, terraces and aqueducts and the great stepped archive-house with its roof of fired clay. When she unrolled them across the judge's bench, a murmur went through the gallery. The official survey maps showed forest and blank space. Ines's maps showed a civilization.

"The reservoir's authorized expansion," she said, her voice steady but her knuckles white against the map's edge, "would raise the waterline by thirty-one meters. This elevation—" she tapped the archive-house, the terraces, the lowest of the inhabited ruins, "—would be submerged. Completely. Permanently."

"And the existing reservoir?" Judge Ferreiro asked.

"Already drowned eleven villages." Ines didn't flinch. "Your Honor, I have walked the old lake bed in dry season. I have seen church roofs under four feet of water. I have interviewed survivors who were told their relocation was temporary. What I am showing you is not a mistake that happened once. It is a method."

A lawyer for the Water Authority rose to object — the maps were unsanctioned, the witnesses self-interested, the whole proceeding irregular — and Judge Ferreiro overruled him with a single raised finger, which seemed, Daniel thought, to be how most things got done in this country.

Then the valley guides spoke. The eldest, old Mateo, who had been carried down the mountain on a litter and had insisted on walking into the courtroom upright, told the story in his own language while a translator stumbled after him. He told of the promises made when the dam was surveyed — compensation, relocation, a school, a clinic. He told of the men who took signatures from people who could not read what they signed. He told of the archive itself, kept by his grandmother's grandmother's generation, a record of names and boundaries and songs, "so that when they erase us, there will be a place where we are written."

He did not cry. Daniel, watching from the second row, thought that this was the most terrible thing in the whole testimony — the absolute dryness of the old man's eyes, the patience of a grief so old it had become geological.

* * *

Vane testified on the third day.

He arrived in a clean suit with a lawyer of his own, thinner than Daniel remembered, his face carrying the grey cast of a man who had spent two weeks in the mountains and not enjoyed it. Under the immunity agreement — negotiated in advance, in writing, witnessed — he would answer questions fully and truthfully regarding the knowledge and conduct of his employer, Meridian Development Consortium, in exchange for exemption from prosecution for obstruction, tampering, and "related offenses."

Related offenses. Daniel turned the phrase over. It covered a great deal. It covered the fire.

He had not been in the valley when it happened — none of them had planned for Vane's return, and the fire had taken the eastern wing of the archive-house in under an hour, fed by dry stored reeds and forty years of accumulated paper. One of Vane's hired men, a heavy-shouldered man named Corbeau, had set it with lamp oil and fled down the goat path, and by the time the smoke was seen it was too late to save anything but what could be carried. Half the collection. Gone. Names, boundaries, songs — a place where they were written, and now half of it ash.

Corbeau had been caught at the narrows, abandoned by his own employer, and had talked freely enough. But it was Vane who had brought the order down the mountain in his breast pocket, and Vane who now sat in the witness box with his hands flat on the rail, and answered.

"Mr. Vane," the judge said, "you have reviewed the immunity terms?"

"I have, Your Honor."

"Do you understand them?"

"Entirely."

"Then tell the court what your employer knew about the valley, and when."

And Vane — polite, precise, exhausted Vane — told them.

He told them about the preliminary surveys commissioned eight years ago, the ones marked *internal only*, which had identified the stepped structures and recommended "cultural mitigation assessment prior to phase two inundation." He told them the assessment had been commissioned, completed, and suppressed. He told them the report concluded — and here he recited it from memory, which meant he had read it many times, which meant it lived behind his eyes now the way the fire did — *"the valley contains an intact vernacular archive of the pre-reservoir river cultures, of significant historical value, whose loss would be irreversible and, given the availability of documented alternatives, indefensible."*

"Indefensible," Judge Ferreiro repeated.

"That was the finding, Your Honor."

"And what did the Consortium do with this finding?"

"They filed it." Vane's voice did not waver, but his hands had begun, very slightly, to tremble. "And proceeded with phase two planning."

Daniel felt Edmund shift beside him. She had sat through three days of testimony with the stillness of a woman at a wheel in bad water, but at the word *filed* something moved through her, a current under calm.

"Who made the decision to proceed?"

"The executive committee. Chaired by—" Vane named the director, a name Daniel knew from the letterheads of his childhood, from the paper his father's company bought and the banks that held his father's loans. The name landed in the courtroom like a stone in still water.

"And did the Consortium take any further action regarding the valley's inhabitants or records?"

Vane was quiet for a moment. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped its polish.

"They instructed me to recover or destroy the expedition's documentation. The wording of the instruction was 'control the narrative.' I interpreted—"

"Did you set the fire yourself?"

"No, Your Honor. But I delivered the order, and I hired the men, and I chose them knowing what they were for." He looked, for the first time, at the gallery — at Mateo, at the guides, at Ines with her ruined maps, at Daniel. His gaze found Daniel's and held it, and there was no apology in it, only a kind of bleak accounting. "I believed I was protecting an investment. I am a man who has spent his life believing that. It turns out I am simply a man who burns things for other men who never get smoke on their hands."

Judge Ferreiro wrote for a long time.

* * *

The newspaper ran it on the fourth morning, across five columns, under a headline Daniel read standing in the street outside the printing house with ink still sharp on the page: **THE VALLEY THE MAPS OMITTED**. Below it, Ines's map reproduced in fine detail, and beside it a photograph of Mateo's hands holding a fragment of salvaged archive — scorched at the edges, the writing intact.

By noon there were people outside the courthouse who had not been summoned by anyone: students, priests, a delegation of fishermen from the lower villages, an old woman Daniel recognized with a start as the seer from the drowned village who had named Edmund's compass years ago — she had traveled two days on a mail barge to be present, and stood in the crowd with her hands folded, watching the doors.

Edmund stood apart from all of them, as she tended to, arms crossed, hat brim low. Daniel went and stood beside her.

"You should be inside," she said. "It's your family's mess."

"It's my family's mess," he agreed, "which is why I can't watch it being cleaned from the front row." He paused. "My father financed that dam, Edmund. Not just the boat. The survey. The roads. He sat in rooms where these decisions were made, and he came home and taught me to love rivers without ever telling me what he'd done to this one."

"He also forgave your debt," Edmund said. "Years ago. And put his initials next to my father's in a compass he gave away instead of selling." She said it without softness and without accusation — a fact laid on the table like a chart. "Men are not one thing, Halvorsen. You of all people should know. You led an expedition and hid in a valley."

"Yes," Daniel said. "I did." He had stopped defending himself somewhere on the mountain, around the time Tomas had carried Mateo over the scree. "We stayed to finish the record. That was true. But part of why we stayed was that coming home meant saying this out loud, in a room like that one, and I wasn't—" He gestured vaguely at the courthouse, the crowd, the heat. "I thought the truth could wait for winter. Edmund, the archive burned because men like me wait."

She considered this with the patience she gave to rivers.

"Truth does its work slow," she said finally. "Like water. Doesn't mean you stop opening the gates."

Inside, faintly, they could hear the session resuming — the clerk's voice, the rustle of papers, the fan turning its indifferent circles above it all.

* * *

The ruling came on the sixth day, and it took Judge Ferreiro eleven minutes to read.

She suspended the phase-two expansion authorization pending a full cultural and archaeological review, ordered the Consortium's internal surveys entered into the public record, referred the suppression of the mitigation report to the capital's prosecutor, and — in a paragraph that would be quoted for years — wrote:

*"A nation that drowns its own memory has not developed; it has amputated. The court finds that the people of the valley were promised, and betrayed, by men who signed their names to both acts. The expansion is suspended. The record will be preserved. Let the record show that preservation is not charity rendered to the dead, but a debt owed to the living."*

Debt. Daniel heard the word and looked sideways at Edmund, who was staring straight ahead at the judge with an expression he couldn't read — something between release and vertigo, the look of a woman hearing a word she had carried so long she'd forgotten it was a word.

The courtroom did not erupt. That was the thing that surprised him most, afterward, trying to describe it to Ines. There was no cheering, no weeping, none of the triumph he had rehearsed on the mountain during the worst nights. Mateo bowed to the judge, slowly, the way one bows to a river that has risen — respect, not gratitude, and a warning underneath. The crowd outside dispersed quietly, back to their boats and stalls and lives. Somewhere a bell rang the hour, and the fan went on turning.

Justice, Daniel thought, was not a fire. It was a flood: slow, unglamorous, moving more earth than any explosion, arriving long after anyone had stopped watching the water line.

Ines found him on the courthouse steps. Her eyes were red-rimmed — she had cried, privately, the way Edmund wept, in places no one was meant to see — but her voice was level.

"Half of it's ash," she said. "Half of it will never be read by anyone now. And they gave him immunity. Vane walks out of there a free man, testifying for the prosecution next month, probably getting paid for it." She shook her head. "It doesn't feel like winning, Daniel."

"It isn't," he said. "Winning would be the archive whole and the valley left alone and my father alive to answer for it. This is just — the water rising a little. Enough to float a boat."

Ines almost smiled at that. Then her expression changed, and she touched his arm.

"There's a man asking for you," she said. "At the dock. Says he has business with the Halvorsen expedition." A beat. "He says he has business with Edmund Joubert, too. He's carrying a commission folder with a government seal on it, and he won't give it to anyone else."

Daniel looked past her toward the river, where the masts swayed in the afternoon haze and the smell of the flood-water hung thick and brown and alive. At the far end of the dock, a figure in a grey suit stood waiting beside a stack of sealed papers, patient as tide.

Edmund was already walking toward him.
