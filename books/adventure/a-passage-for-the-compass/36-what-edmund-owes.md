## 36. What Edmund Owes

The river city smelled of wet stone and frying plantains and printer's ink, and for the first time in her life Edmund Joubert woke up owing nothing to anyone.

She lay in the narrow berth of her own boat—the *Mariana's Wake*, patched at the waterline, looted of half her stores, but afloat, hers—staring at the deck beams above her head and waiting for the old arithmetic to start. It always had before. The interest accruing by the hour. The quarterly payment like a stone in her chest. The number that followed her down every reach of the river, that sat across from her at meals, that slept beside her in the dark: four thousand two hundred, then three thousand nine hundred, then less, always less, never fast enough.

She waited for it.

Nothing came.

The silence where the debt had been was not peace. It was a room with the furniture gone, and she kept reaching for chairs that weren't there.

* * *

They had delivered the copies on Tuesday—to Judge Okafor, gray-faced and careful behind his desk; to the offices of the *River Gazette*, where a young reporter named Adaeze read the first pages of Ines's transcriptions and then stopped reading and started shaking. By Friday the expansion of the reservoir was suspended pending inquiry. By Saturday the story was everywhere: the valley, the archive, the drowned villages that had been drowned twice over—once by water, once by paper. Vane's testimony, given under immunity in a courtroom so quiet you could hear the ceiling fans turn, had done its slow, ugly work. He had named names. His employer's name among them. He had not looked at Edmund once, and she had not needed him to.

Daniel Halvorsen had stood beside her through all of it, tall and rumpled, looking less like an explorer than a man who had been awake too long and would be awake longer still. The expedition's scholars were already talking about returning upriver when the routes reopened. Tomas had not left his uncle's side since the descent, except to come find Edmund each evening and sit with her on the foredeck without saying much, which was its own kind of conversation now.

And this morning—a Monday, ordinary as any other—there had been a letter.

Edmund held it now, standing at the wheelhouse window while the river slid past below the dock, brown and swollen still with the tail of the monsoon, carrying branches and whole trees south toward the delta. The letter bore the seal of the provincial survey office. She knew the hand; it was the same clerk who had hired her eleven days' silence ago, a lifetime ago, back when the pay was going to clear half her debt and she had refused the job out of pride and taken it anyway because fate dressed itself as clerical error.

*Captain Joubert,* it began. *In light of your recent service to the province, and upon recommendation of Mr. D. Halvorsen and the office of Judge Okafor, the Survey is prepared to offer you a standing commission: provision and transport of supplies to the valley settlement, via the reopened mountain route, at contracted rates, for a term of five years.*

Five years. Steady work. Wages that would keep the *Wake* in paint and rope and coal for the rest of her working life. A route only she knew well enough to run safely, in a season only she could read. It was everything she had spent fifteen years believing she wanted: to matter to the river, to be indispensable, to be paid what she was worth instead of what desperation allowed.

She set the letter on the chart table and found that her hands were trembling, and could not say why.

* * *

"You haven't signed it."

Ines stood in the wheelhouse doorway with two cups of coffee, which was how Ines said good morning. She had cut her hair short in the river city—Edmund wasn't sure whether it was practicality or grief or celebration, possibly all three—and it made her look younger and harder at once, like a coin freshly struck.

"I'm reading it," Edmund said.

"You've been reading it for two hours." Ines handed her a cup and leaned against the doorframe, watching her with those cartographer's eyes that took bearings on people the way they did on coastlines. "You'd have signed it in a heartbeat three months ago."

"Three months ago I owed four thousand."

"And now?"

Edmund looked at the letter. The commission. The term. Five years of being needed, of being useful, of waking with a purpose bolted to the floor of her like ballast.

"Now I don't know what I am," she said, and heard how it sounded, and laughed shortly. "That's a foolish thing to say. A woman with a boat and a crew and a commission on the table, and she doesn't know who she is."

"It's not foolish." Ines blew on her coffee. "I've mapped coastlines my whole life. You know what happens when the sea reclaims land? The charts go wrong. Not because the surveyor made mistakes—because the ground itself changed. Every bearing you trusted is measured from a point that isn't there anymore." She sipped. "Your debt was your datum, Edmund. Everything about you was measured from it. Take it away and every number you have is wrong until you fix new ones."

Edmund turned the cup in her hands. Outside, a barge went downstream loaded with timber, its pilot giving her boat a friendly blast of the horn. She raised two fingers in reply, automatic, and hated how automatic it was—how even now, unowned, she performed the part of Captain Joubert, debtor, survivor, the woman who ran the low water because no one else would and couldn't afford to refuse anything.

"When I was nineteen," she said, "my father died and left me a boat I couldn't fuel and a debt I didn't know he'd taken. Halvorsen senior bought the note. Everyone said it was charity. I told myself it was charity too, right up until the first payment came due." She stared at the river. "Fifteen years I told myself the story: my father was proud, he gambled on the boat, he lost, I inherited the losing. It explained everything. Why I worked the dangerous runs. Why I never married, never settled, never went past the delta. Why I kept the compass in a drawer instead of using it—it was collateral, you don't use your collateral." Her jaw tightened. "It was a lie someone built for me, and I moved into it and furnished it and called it home. And now the lie's burned down and I'm standing in the ashes asking the commission letter to build me another house."

"What kind of house do you want?" Ines asked quietly.

"That's just it." Edmund set down the cup. "I don't know. I only ever wanted what the debt allowed."

* * *

She found Daniel that afternoon on the mission station's porch, where the sisters had let the expedition spread its salvaged papers across long tables to dry and sort. He sat hunched over a fragment of the burned archive, copying by lamplight though it was barely midafternoon, because Daniel Halvorsen had learned in the valley that documents could die overnight and he no longer trusted darkness to wait.

He looked up when her boots hit the boards, and something in her face must have shown, because he set down his pen.

"The commission," he said.

"You recommended me."

"I did. And I'd recommend it again. But you haven't signed."

"How do you know that?"

"Because if you had, you'd be upriver already, and instead you're walking the docks like a woman checking the lines on a boat that isn't going anywhere." He pushed back his chair. "Walk with me."

They walked along the levee, past the fish market winding down for the day, past children diving off the pilings into the brown flood-water. The mountains were invisible from here, weeks away, but Edmund felt them at her back the way you feel weather coming.

"My father financed your boat," Daniel said, when the market noise had fallen behind them. "You know that now. What you may not know is why."

"He wanted collateral. He wanted leverage. He wanted—" She stopped.

"He wanted a partner." Daniel's voice was gentle, and that gentleness almost undid her more than accusation would have. "I found the letters, Edmund. After the valley, after everything, I went through his correspondence—all of it, every box. There's a file with your father's name on it. Do you know what's in it? Charts. Your father's charts, of the upper river, of the pass, of approaches to the valley that our own maps get wrong. Correspondence between them going back twenty years. They were planning something together—an expedition, a trading route, a way to bring the valley's people into contact with the cities on terms the valley set, not the cities. My father put up the money because he believed in the plan. Your father put up the knowledge because he believed in the man." Daniel stopped walking. "Then the reservoir project came. My father sat on the board that approved it. He was told—there are minutes, Edmund, I have the minutes—that the valley was uninhabited. That the drowned villages were empty. He chose to believe it, because believing it was profitable. And your father found out it wasn't true, and confronted him, and my father—ashamed, cornered, a man who had built his whole life on being a benefactor—called the loan. Called it in full, immediately, knowing your father couldn't pay, knowing it would take the boat and break him. Six months later your father was dead, and the compass—the one thing he asked to keep, the one thing that mattered—was left to you, and my father let it go, because to him it was a trinket. Because he never understood what it was."

Edmund stood very still. The river breathed against the levee stones.

"He never told me any of that," she managed.

"No. I think he was protecting you from what he'd learned about his friend. Or protecting himself from having to say it aloud." Daniel looked out at the water. "I'm telling you because you deserve the true ledger. And because there's one more thing in the file. A letter my father wrote after yours died, and never sent. It says: *The compass was always meant for her. Tell her, when she's old enough, that it was her grandfather's design and my father's craft, and that the two families made it together, and that whatever I have done, the instrument does not carry my debt.*"

The world tilted slightly, the way it did when a current shifted under a hull. "His—"

"Your grandfather and mine were friends before either of us was born. The compass was a joint work. Halvorsen money, your family's hand. It was never collateral. It was inheritance." Daniel turned to face her fully. "You've been carrying a debt that was forgiven before you ever touched the wheel, and an heirloom you thought was a lien. No wonder you don't know who you are. You've been navigating from the wrong datum your whole life."

Edmund's eyes stung. She looked hard at the river until the burning passed, because captains did not cry on their own decks, and this levee was close enough.

"So what do I owe?" she asked finally. It came out rougher than she intended. "If the debt was never real—if the paper was fraud and the forgiveness was real—what do I owe him? Your father? The dead? Anyone?"

Daniel considered it with the seriousness he gave everything, the seriousness that had kept forty people alive in a hidden valley for a year.

"Nothing," he said. "That's the terrible answer, Edmund. You owe nothing. Nobody owes anybody. The valley doesn't owe my father for financing the expedition that found it. I don't owe you for my father's sins. You don't owe the commission board for their contract." He smiled, tired and crooked. "Everything that matters was never a debt. It was a gift, freely given, or it was nothing at all. The question isn't what you owe. It's what you want to give."

* * *

Evening came down on the river city in layers of gold and violet, and Edmund climbed to the wheelhouse alone and opened the drawer where the compass lived.

She had carried it fifteen years without trusting it. Now she lifted it out and set it on the chart table under the lamp, brass worn bright at the edges where two generations of thumbs had rubbed it, the needle swinging lazily north-northwest, toward the mountains, toward the valley, toward everything.

Her father's initials were supposed to be inside it—under the plate, Daniel had said, where the maker's mark hid. She hadn't looked yet. She wasn't ready, or she was saving it, she couldn't tell which. Some things you opened only once.

Instead she picked up the commission letter and read it again, slowly, and for the first time saw it plainly—not as salvation, not as identity, but as what it was: a job. Good work, honest work, work only she could do. And beneath it, the question Ines had planted that morning, growing roots.

What kind of house did she want?

Not the debt's house. Not the furnished lie of Captain Joubert, debtor, running dangerous water because refusal cost more than risk. Something else. Something with a crew in it—Ines with her new-cut hair and her star fixes; Tomas, who had crossed a gorge on fear and carried an old man over scree and was becoming someone before her eyes; Sefu, who would guide for them or for no one; Daniel, who had gone pale at the sight of a compass and spent a year in the mountains paying down a debt that was never his either. A family, if she was honest, though the word still fit strangely, like a boot not yet broken in.

And the valley. The valley with its terraces and its fires and its eldest who told stories the archive could only half hold. The valley needed supply lines more than it needed scholars now. It needed someone who could read the monsoon and the snowline both, who could run the flooded river and time the pass, who could carry rice and lamp oil and medicine up a route everyone else called impossible.

The commission offered exactly that. Five years of it, contracted, sealed.

But contracts, she was learning, were just debts wearing better clothes.

Edmund dipped a pen. Then she set it down again, and took a fresh sheet instead, and began to write—not an acceptance, but a counteroffer, in her own hand, in her own terms: seasonal runs timed to the weathers, valley representatives holding veto over cargo, wages shared with the guides who made the route possible. A partnership, in other words. The thing her father and Halvorsen senior had tried to build and broken instead. She would build it properly, or not at all.

When she finished, the lamp had burned low and the needle of the compass had settled, steady and certain, pointing at the mountains.

A knock at the wheelhouse door. Tomas, holding a lantern, trying to look casual and failing completely.

"Ines says come to the mission. Daniel got word—the survey's reopening the upper route next month, and there's a delegation going up to meet the valley elders, and—" he broke off, grinning, unable to hold the news in any longer, "—they want a captain. And Daniel says nobody else is crazy enough."

Edmund looked at the boy—at the young man—who had stowed away on her deck a lifetime ago, and felt the strange new weight settle onto her shoulders, heavier than any debt and easier to bear: the weight of being chosen, and of choosing back.

"Tell them," she said, "the captain will give them her answer in the morning."

Tomas ran. Edmund turned back to the compass, and beneath the lamp, with a small screwdriver from the chart drawer, she began, carefully, to lift the plate.
