## 37. The Naming

The river city smelled of wet stone and frying plantains and the particular green rot of a monsoon just finished with it. Tomas had lived his whole life in towns that smelled of something — sawdust, diesel, his uncle's pipe tobacco — but this was the first place he'd ever stood where the smell seemed to belong to him too, as if the three days of flood he'd helped run had soaked into his skin and made him part of the water's story.

He stood on the deck of the *Mariana Q* — still the *Mariana Q*, though Edmund had threatened twice to rename her something less sentimental — and watched Ines carry a rolled sheaf of paper up the gangplank like a bride carrying flowers she intended to throw away.

"You're staring," Sefu said beside him.

"I'm observing."

"Observing is staring with excuses." The old guide adjusted the strap of his pack, which he wore even ashore, even at rest, as if the mountains might call him back at any moment and he refused to be caught unready. "She's been offered the post. The survey office wants her to map the valley properly. Every ridge, every tributary, every village the old maps left blank."

"She said no last night."

"She said *not yet* last night." Sefu smiled the way he did when he knew something about weather or people before anyone else did. "There is a difference. With Ines there is always a difference."

Tomas looked downriver, toward the delta he'd never seen, and upriver, toward the pass that was by now surely white to its throat. Somewhere beyond both was the valley, holding its smoke and its terraces and its archive — half of it ash now, the other half copied out in Ines's small fierce handwriting through two nights without sleep. And somewhere in this city was a judge's chambers where Daniel Halvorsen was, at this hour, laying those copies on a table and telling the truth slowly enough that it could be believed.

Eleven weeks ago Tomas had been a stowaway under a tarpaulin, counting his heartbeats and praying the captain wouldn't find him. Now the captain's crew was arguing about whether he counted as family, and nobody had asked him to leave yet.

* * *

They gathered that evening in the boat's saloon, which was really just the cabin with the table cleared and Edmund's one good lamp lit. Edmund sat at the head because she always sat at the head, though she claimed it was only because the bench there didn't wobble. Daniel Halvorsen sat across from her, and between them lay the space where the debt used to be — a space Tomas could feel the way you feel a missing tooth, everyone's tongue drifting toward it.

Ines came last, still damp from the rain, and set her roll of maps on the table.

"The survey office made the offer formal this morning," she said, without preamble, because Ines never used preamble; she treated words like cargo, loaded only what was needed. "Principal cartographer for the upper basin. Full funding. A team of four. Two years."

"And?" Edmund said.

"And I told them I would take it on one condition." Ines unrolled the topmost sheet. It was the valley — their valley — drawn from memory and from the sketches she'd made in the cloud forest, the terraces rendered in fine cross-hatching, the river's true course running through it like a spine. "That I map it *with* them, not *for* us. That the first complete map belongs to the people who live there. Their names for things. Their boundaries. We put our instruments at their service, not the other way around."

Daniel was quiet a moment. "They'll agree. They have no choice, after the testimony. It would look—" He stopped himself.

"It would look bad," Ines finished. "Yes. Let them agree for the wrong reason. The map will be right regardless."

Edmund leaned back against the bulkhead and looked at Ines the way she looked at a difficult stretch of current — reading it, respecting it, deciding to trust it. "Two years," she said. "You'll be based out of the valley?"

"Out of the valley and this boat, both. If the boat will have me." Ines's eyes went to Edmund, and something passed between the two women that Tomas recognized but couldn't have named: the thing that had been born in the dark on the river, running blind by starlight, when Ines had called the fixes and Edmund had steered to them as if they were gospel.

"The boat will have you," Edmund said gruffly. "Someone has to tell me where I'm going. God knows the compass only tells me where I've been."

* * *

Sefu's ceremony, if you could call it that, happened the next morning on the dock, and it consisted almost entirely of a piece of paper and a great deal of silence.

The mission sisters had connections — everything in the river city ran through the mission sisters, Tomas was learning, including apparently the government office that licensed guides — and so it was that Sefu stood before a clerk's desk at eight o'clock sharp, wearing his good coat over his trail clothes, and signed his name to a document making him Guide of Record for the reopened route to the valley.

"That's it?" Tomas whispered.

"That's it," Sefu said. But afterward, walking back along the dock, he stopped at the rail where the brown water slid past and said, quietly, "Twenty-two years I have walked these trails. Twenty-two years, and every time I crossed onto someone's land I was either a trespasser or a favor. Now I am a record." He tapped the folded license against his palm. "A piece of paper says what I always was. It should not matter. It matters."

Tomas thought about saying something wise and decided against it. Instead he said, "Will you teach me the goat path above the narrows? Properly, I mean. Not just how we climbed it with men shooting at us."

Sefu looked at him for a long moment, and then he laughed — a rare sound, dry as gravel, real as rain. "Yes," he said. "I will teach you. But not because you ask well. Because you carried the eldest over the scree when your legs were shaking worse than hers." He started walking again. "A guide is not someone who knows the way. Everyone knows the way, eventually. A guide is someone who keeps walking when the way disappears. You kept walking."

Tomas walked beside him and said nothing at all, because his throat had closed up, and some lessons you don't answer, you just carry.

* * *

His own turn came on Sunday, and he didn't know about it until it was already happening, which he suspected was deliberate.

They held it on deck, in the late afternoon light, with the river running gold below the rail. Edmund had anchored midstream — "No docks," she'd said. "Docks are where people leave from. Midstream is where people are." — and the whole company was there: Daniel and the two expedition scholars who'd come down with the copies, Ines with ink-stained fingers, Sefu standing at ease like a man attending a coronation he found excessive, and the eldest sister from the mission, Sister Amara, whom Tomas had ferried supplies for once, years ago, when he was small enough that she'd given him a sweet and told him God sees stowaways too.

Edmund stood at the wheel housing with a piece of paper she clearly wished she didn't have to read from.

"There's no form for this," she began. "I checked. There are forms for apprenticeships in surveying, forms for guides, forms for boats, forms for everything except what we're actually doing, which is—" She paused, and Tomas watched her decide to say the true thing instead of the easy one. "—which is deciding whose people we are. So we made our own form."

Daniel stepped forward. He'd shaved, and he wore the jacket his father's letters had been posted in, folded flat, over his shirt — a strange relic, but Tomas understood it. You wore the dead close when they'd finally told you the truth.

"Tomas Reyes," Daniel said. "Your uncle Mateo entered this expedition eleven months ago as a field recorder. When we stayed in the valley, he wrote to your mother asking her blessing to bring you into the work when you were older. She wrote back. Her letter is in the cairn we never reached." He held up an envelope, soft with travel. "We sent for it. It came yesterday."

Tomas's heart did something complicated. "You went back for the mail?"

"We went back for the mail," Daniel said simply. "An expedition that doesn't honor its dead and its letters isn't worth the name."

He opened the envelope and read aloud, and Tomas heard his mother's voice in every line — the careful schoolroom Spanish, the pride she hid under practicality. *If my brother thinks the boy is ready, then the boy is ready. Tell him to write to me. Tell him a person can be lost and found in the same year and be better for both.*

Then Daniel took out a small brass pin — the expedition's mark, a stylized contour line forming a V — and pinned it to Tomas's collar, and said, "Apprentice field recorder, Halvorsen Expedition, by consent of the leader and the blessing of your mother. Do you accept?"

Tomas meant to say something dignified. What came out was, "Does it pay?"

The deck erupted. Even Edmund laughed, a short bark like a pistol shot, and Sister Amara crossed herself and smiled, and Sefu said gravely, "It pays in blisters," which was true, and which was somehow the best wage Tomas had ever been promised.

"Yes," he said then, properly. "I accept."

* * *

Afterward, when the others had gone below and the lamp in the saloon cast its warm square on the water, Tomas found Edmund alone at the rail, smoking one of the cigarettes she claimed she'd given up in chapter — in some earlier month, anyway.

"You didn't say anything during the ceremony," he said. "About me."

"I signed the witness line. That's saying plenty."

"You're the one who pulled me out from under the tarpaulin. You should have said something."

Edmund exhaled smoke at the river. For a while she didn't answer, and Tomas had learned that this was not rudeness with her; it was navigation. She was finding the channel through whatever she wanted to say.

"When I found you," she said finally, "I was going to put you off at the next landing. Did you know that?"

"I suspected."

"I had a speech ready. Something about how a boat is not a charity and a river is not a place for children." She flicked ash into the water. "And then you got up at four in the morning and coiled lines without being asked, and you learned the sounder, and you asked questions at exactly the right moments, and somewhere around the drowned village I realized I wasn't keeping you aboard out of kindness." She looked at him sideways. "I was keeping you because a crew is just a family that admits what it is. And I hadn't had one since I was your age."

Tomas felt his ears go hot. "So the speech—"

"The speech died. Good riddance. It was a terrible speech." She stubbed out the cigarette against the rail and dropped the butt in her pocket, because Edmund Joubert did not litter rivers she loved. "You'll go up with Daniel's party when the passes open. You'll write to your mother every week. And when you come back down—" she pointed at him, and her voice roughened in a way she probably thought he wouldn't notice, "—you come back to *this boat*. Not any boat. This one."

"Yes, Captain."

"Don't 'Captain' me when I'm being sincere. It's cheating."

But she was smiling when she said it, and Tomas grinned all the way below decks.

* * *

He couldn't sleep. Eleven weeks of habit — sleeping light, listening for pursuit, listening for weather — didn't dissolve in one warm night in a safe harbor. So near midnight he climbed back up and found the deck already occupied: Ines, sitting cross-legged on the hatch cover with her maps spread around her in the lamplight, working.

"Couldn't sleep either?" he asked.

"I slept. I woke up wanting to work. There's a difference." She shifted to make room. "Look at this."

He knelt beside her. It was the valley map, but changed — new names inked along the river and the ridges in a second hand, careful and unfamiliar. Valley names. The names the people themselves used.

"They gave them to me the last week," Ines said. "Before we came down. Every place on the map has two names now — ours and theirs. When I publish, theirs goes on top." She traced a line of the script. "This one means *where the river remembers*. That ridge — *the shoulder that watches*. And here—" her finger stopped at a bend downstream of the valley, a bend that looked, Tomas realized, very much like the sandbar where Edmund had been idling when the survey office letter found her, "—this one means *the waiting place*. Where travelers stop before they know why."

Tomas looked at it a long time. "Do you believe maps can mean things? Really mean them? Or is it just pretty?"

Ines considered the question with the seriousness she gave all questions. "I believe a map is a promise," she said. "Every line on it says: someone will come back here, and this is what they'll find. Most maps are promises other people make about places that never agreed to anything." She tapped the valley. "This one is a promise the place itself helped write. That's the difference. That's the whole profession, honestly. Everything else is just drawing."

Above them the sky was clearing, the monsoon breaking apart into scattered clouds, and through a gap Tomas could see stars — the same stars Ines had shot fixes by on the night run, the ones that had saved them from the hidden bar. He thought about how a person could learn to read the sky until the sky became a kind of home.

"Ines," he said. "When you go up for the two years. Will you need an apprentice cartographer? Because I'm told I'm an apprentice now, and I hear apprentices get assigned."

She looked at him over the lamplight, and something in her face softened that rarely softened. "I hear that too," she said. "Ask me again when you can draw a contour line that doesn't look like a frightened snake."

"That could take years."

"Good," said Ines. "The valley isn't going anywhere. Neither, I suspect, are we."

* * *

On the third morning, the summons came.

A boy in mission gray ran down the dock waving a telegram, shouting Edmund's name before he was halfway along the planks, and the whole boat turned to watch him arrive. Edmund took the flimsy yellow slip, read it once, and Tomas — watching her face the way he'd learned to watch the river, for the small changes that meant big water underneath — saw her go very still.

"Well?" Daniel asked.

Edmund read it again, slower. Then she looked up, and there was something in her expression Tomas had never seen there before: not dread, not relief, but a kind of stunned, dawning recognition, like a woman hearing her own name spoken in a language she thought she'd forgotten.

"It's from the north," she said. "From the delta archives. A records clerk." She swallowed. "They've been cataloguing old shipping partnerships — pre-dam registrations, everything the reservoir project tried to bury. And they found one." She held up the telegram. "A joint registry. Two boats, one license, filed forty-one years ago. The *Halvorsen Line* and the *Joubert Run*, registered as partners." Her voice caught and steadied. "Under both names. My father's and Halvorsen's. Filed together, sealed together, and the seal was broken this week because a judge ordered every old paper opened."

Nobody spoke. The river ran past, brown and patient, carrying everything toward the sea.

"My father didn't just know Halvorsen," Edmund said softly, mostly to herself, mostly to the water. "They built something. Together. Before the dam, before the debt, before—" She stopped, pressed the telegram flat against the rail as if it might blow away, as if the truth might blow away. "There's more in the delta. The original file. Whatever they were to each other, the proof of it is sitting in a basement two hundred miles downriver, waiting for somebody to come read it."

Daniel stepped forward. "Edmund—"

She was already turning toward the wheelhouse, already reaching for the telegraph key to notify the harbor office, and over her shoulder she said the words that made Tomas's chest tighten with a joy he had no form for:

"Get your things. We sail with the morning tide."
