## 38. The Valley Opens

The river had changed its mind about them.

Edmund felt it before she could name it—the way she had always felt things about water, in her wrists and the soles of her feet before her head caught up. The channel that had run shallow and mean through the dry season now held the monsoon's gift in its banks, deep and slow and generous, as though the river had spent the whole of her life testing her and had finally decided to let her pass.

*The Compass Rose* rode high and eager under her hands, loaded to her marks with rice and salt, lamp oil and nails, bolts of cloth and a crate of school slates, of all the improbable things. Daniel's scholars crowded her deck like starlings—six of them, young, loud, delighted by everything, hanging off the rail to watch kingfishers detonate out of the reeds. Ines had appointed herself their unofficial quartermaster and was already regretting it; she stood at the cargo hatch with her ledger open, saying *no* in three languages to a young man who wanted to open a crate of ink before they had even cleared the second bend.

Tomas was at the bow with the sounding pole, and he had stopped needing to be told where to place it. That was the thing about the boy that kept catching Edmund sideways, like a root underfoot: he had arrived on her boat a stowaway with a lie in his mouth, and somewhere between the drowned village and the pass he had become the kind of person who stood at the bow of a loaded boat and read the water without being asked. His uncle's hat sat on his head, too big, the brim pinned with a brass pin Sefu had given him. He wore it like a rank.

"Sandbar, port side, past the dead fig," Tomas called back, and Edmund eased her over to starboard without looking, because she had already seen it.

"Show-off," she said, but she said it the way you say a prayer.

Sefu sat amidships with his back against the wheelhouse, arms folded, doing the thing he did on every voyage now, which was nothing at all, magnificently. He had come as guide of record, which mostly meant he sat and listened to the river and occasionally said one word—*left*, *wait*, *now*—and the word was always right. The valley's eldest had insisted he come. "You carry the road in you," she had told him, in the cave, in the whiteout. "Roads need carriers or they die."

The route was open now. That was the phrase Daniel used, and the newspapers had borrowed it, and Edmund had heard it so often in the last two months that she had almost stopped hearing what it meant. The reservoir's expansion was suspended. The survey teams had been recalled. The pass had a recorded route, mapped by Ines in her careful hand, with water sources and camps and the goat path marked where Sefu had carried the eldest on his back. And the river—the river that had been a drowned country's coffin lid for thirty years—was now a road again.

Edmund was the one who ran it. First supply run. Her name on the manifest.

She had not known, until the manifest was printed, that a name could feel like a key.

* * *

They made the drowned village by late afternoon, because Edmund had timed it that way, though she would not have admitted to anyone that she had timed it at all.

The water stood high enough now that the rooftops were islands, and the church tower rose out of the flood like a candle in a dark room, and the dead fig tree they used as a landmark spread its drowned arms over the whole of it, dripping and patient. In the dry season you could see the streets. In this light, with the water lying gold and still between the roofs, it looked less drowned than sleeping—a town gone down for a long nap, waiting for someone to call it home.

Edmund cut the engine and let the boat drift.

"Captain?" Ines came forward, pencil still in hand. "Are we—"

"I want to show you something," Edmund said. "All of you. Come here."

They gathered at the rail, the scholars and Tomas and Sefu, and Edmund stood at the wheel with her compass open in her hand, the one that had started everything, the one with two sets of initials under the plate now—Halvorsen's and, beneath it, in her father's cramped engraver's hand, *E.J.*—and she told them about the old woman.

"She lived on the hill above here, when I was a girl. Before the water came up. She used to trade with my father—fish for flour, reeds for rope. The last time I saw her, she was standing on the shore watching the reservoir rise over the rooftops, and she was singing. I thought she'd gone mad with grief." Edmund looked down at the compass, at the brass worn bright where her thumb had polished it for thirty years. "She wasn't mad. She was singing a song the water hadn't learned yet."

She turned the boat in a slow circle, and the scholars leaned over the rail, and the drowned village turned beneath them, street by street, roof by roof.

"Her name was Ama Sefa," Edmund said. "She called this compass by a name I didn't know. *The road that remembers.* I thought she was an old woman talking nonsense. I spent most of my life thinking the things she said were nonsense." She looked up. "They're going to hear her song again. That's why we're here."

Ines had stopped writing. That, more than anything, told Edmund how it landed—Ines, who wrote everything down, who believed that a thing unrecorded was a thing already lost, stood with her pencil capped and her ledger closed, and watched the water.

* * *

They anchored that night in the lee of the church tower, and in the morning the valley came down to meet them.

Edmund heard it before she saw it: a sound on the water, thin at first, the way sound comes over a lake at dawn. She thought it was birds. Then it resolved into voices, many voices, and she stood at the rail with her heart going strange in her chest as the canoes came out of the morning mist—eight of them, ten, paddles dipping in unison, and the people in them singing.

It was a rowing song. She knew that much from the shape of it, the call and answer, the lead voice rising and the others falling in beneath like water finding its level. And she knew it. That was the thing that undid her. She did not know the words—the words were in the valley's old tongue, the tongue the archive had preserved, the tongue the scholars had spent a winter learning—but the *shape* of it, the rise and fall, the places where the singers leaned on a note the way you lean on a pole in a current—she had heard that shape before, from a woman on a shore, thirty years ago, singing at the water as it swallowed her town.

The old woman had been singing this. Or its grandmother. Or its great-grandmother. The song had come down out of the valley, along the river, into the drowned village, and it had survived the drowning, carried in one old throat, and now it was coming back up the river to meet the boat.

The canoes came alongside, and the singing did not stop, and hands reached up—not for the cargo, not yet, just hands, touching the hull, touching the rail, touching the scholars' arms as they leaned down—and Edmund understood that this was not a greeting. It was a claiming. The boat was being sung back into a country that remembered it.

Tomas was crying and pretending he wasn't. Sefu stood at the rail with his hand flat on the gunwale, and he was singing—low, under his breath, in the valley tongue, and Edmund stared at him, and he shrugged.

"My grandmother's people were river people," he said. "Before the mountains took us in." A pause. "The eldest says every family in the valley has someone who drowned down here. Now they have someone who came back up."

The lead singer in the nearest canoe was a broad-shouldered woman with grey in her braids, and when the song ended she looked up at Edmund and said something in the valley tongue, and then, in the trade tongue they all shared now, the way you offer a gift in a wrapping the other person can open:

"We know this boat's grandmother. The boats that came before the water. You have her shape."

"I have her shape," Edmund agreed, and her voice came out rough, and she did not try to smooth it.

* * *

The unloading took two days, because the valley had come down to help and the helping kept turning into teaching and the teaching kept turning into songs. The scholars worked like people possessed, cataloguing, asking, filling notebooks; one of them, the ink-hungry young man from the hatch, turned out to have a memory like a net—he could hear a song twice and have it whole—and the valley singers adopted him on the spot, dragging him into canoes to teach him paddling songs and grinding songs and a lullaby that made even Sefu look away and study the hills.

Edmund watched it all from her wheelhouse, mostly, doing the captain's work: the manifests, the waterline, the balance of the load. But she watched the rest of it too, the thing that was happening on her deck, which was harder work than any of it.

A woman from the valley sat with Ines for a whole afternoon, drawing the drowned village from memory—every house, every family, who had lived where, which roof had belonged to the midwife—and Ines mapped it, street by street, and when she was done the woman touched the drawing and said, in the trade tongue, "Now the map remembers too," and Ines had to go stand alone by the rail for a while, and Edmund pretended not to see, because that was the courtesy Ines would have wanted.

Tomas spent both days in the canoes. He came back the second evening with his arms burned brown, a new song stuck in his throat that he hummed while he coiled lines, and a shell necklace a valley child had put on him, and he wore it over his uncle's hat brim and his pinned collar like the whole outfit was a uniform he'd been issued. His uncle would see him in three days, when the boat reached the valley landing, and Edmund thought: *he won't recognize him. Good. Let him meet his nephew for the first time, properly, the way the rest of us did.*

And Edmund herself—Edmund stood her watches and ran her boat and slept badly and well, both, the way you do when something in you is being rearranged.

On the second night, anchored below the drowned village with the last of the cargo gone ashore, she took the compass up onto the roof of the wheelhouse and sat with it under the stars, and she did the thing she had been avoiding since the first supply run was proposed.

She thought about the old woman. Ama Sefa. Dead now—two years gone, the mission sisters had told her, in her sleep, on the hill above the water, with the drowned town below her window. Edmund had missed her by a season. She had spent thirty years running this river above that woman's rooftops, carrying cargo for the men who had drowned them, and she had never once stopped. She had never known to stop. But she had never *tried* to know, either, and there was a difference, and the difference had a name, and the name was the debt. The debt had kept her eyes forward and her hatches shut for thirty years. The debt had been very convenient that way.

"You would have liked my boat," she said aloud to the dark water. "You'd have told me everything she was. You'd have known her grandmother."

The river said nothing, the way rivers do, and the way mothers do, and the way the dead do when they have already said everything and are waiting for you to catch up.

But the current, in the morning, was kind.

* * *

They left the drowned village astern on the third day and turned up the river proper, into the water that had never been dammed, and the country changed around them the way it always did—banks rising, forest closing, the air going green and cool—and the scholars hung off the rails pointing at things Ines had already mapped, and Edmund ran her boat through water she now knew like a face.

Because that was the other thing. She had made this run twice now, and the third time the river had stopped being a route and become a road, and a road has people on it. Word had traveled ahead of them, the way word does in the valley country, on foot and by drum and by the simple fact that everyone's cousin was everyone's cousin. At the second landing, people were waiting on the bank with goods to ship—honey, dried fish, woven mats, a crate of the valley's small hard apples that the mission sisters had declared the best in the territory—and at the third, a schoolteacher with a letter for the city, and at the fourth, an old man who wanted only to ride as far as the bend and look at the water from a boat again, one time, before he died, and who wept the whole way and apologized for it and was told by everyone on deck, in chorus, that he had nothing to apologize for.

The boat was full. Not just of cargo. Edmund stood at the wheel on the fourth morning with the sun coming up behind her and the river running silver ahead, and she did the arithmetic she had been avoiding since the delta: the boat was carrying more than she had ever carried, and it was riding *lighter*.

*The Compass Rose* had been built for freight. Rice, salt, timber, fuel—the dead weight of a country's commerce, run up and down a drowned river for a company's profit. That was what she had been. A boat for hauling what could be weighed.

Now her hold was honey and letters and school slates and an old man's last wish, and her deck was full of songs being written down, and every mile she made was a mile of road being opened behind her, and Edmund Joubert, who had spent her whole adult life as a debtor running a creditor's river, stood at the wheel of a boat that was becoming something else entirely, something she did not have a word for yet, and did not need one, because the valley people had given her one.

The broad-shouldered singer had said it at the parting, pressing a woven band into her hand, a captain's band, river-woven, the kind that had not been made in thirty years:

"*The road that remembers,*" she said, touching the compass at Edmund's belt, and then the boat, and then Edmund's chest. "*All one road now.*"

* * *

They sighted the valley on the sixth day, in the last gold of the afternoon, and Edmund brought her in slow, the way you come in when you know the whole shore is watching.

The landing was rebuilt—Sefu's cousins had done it, timber and stone, and it stood solid over the water with steps going up into the terraces, and the terraces were green, and the smoke rose from the fires, and the whole slope of it was crowded. Not with an audience. With a family. That was the difference, and Edmund felt it in her chest: a crowd watches; a family comes down to the water.

Daniel Halvorsen stood at the head of the steps, and beside him the eldest, wrapped in her red wool, and beside them the scholars who had wintered there, and the valley people in their dozens, and the drums had started before the boat was even in sight of the bend.

Tomas stood at the bow, and his hands had gone still on the line he was coiling, and Edmund watched him watch the shore, and watched him find his uncle's face, and watched the boy stand there for a long moment with the wind coming off the water and his uncle's hat on his head.

Then he took the hat off. Held it. And put it back on—straightened, pinned, *his own now*—and raised his hand, and the whole shore raised theirs, and the drums and the singing came out over the water and took the boat the rest of the way in.

Edmund brought her alongside gentle as a breath, and Sefu made the lines fast, and the gangplank went down, and the valley came aboard.

Daniel reached the deck first. He stood in front of her, and for a moment neither of them said anything, because there was too much to say and they had both learned, over the winter and the pass and the testimony and the slow months since, that some things are better said by waiting.

"You brought the roof beams," he said finally, nodding at the last crates going ashore. For the new schoolhouse. For the archive's second home, up out of the flood country, where paper could sleep safe.

"And the slates," Edmund said. "And a schoolteacher with a letter, and an old man who wanted one last ride, and about forty songs that a young man with a net for a memory is going to write down before he sleeps tonight."

Daniel laughed—the real laugh, the one that had come back to him slowly, the way rivers come back. Then he went quiet, and looked at her, really looked, the way he had at the reunion, when he'd seen the compass and gone pale.

"My father used to say the river would forgive us someday," he said. "I never believed him. I thought forgiveness was the kind of thing that had drowned with everything else." He put his hand on the rail, on the old wood, on the boat's worn shoulder. "Then you came up the river with a boatload of rice and my father's compass, and I watched the valley sing to you, and I thought: *there it is. It didn't drown. It was just waiting for someone to carry it back up.*"

Edmund looked past him, up the terraces, at the fires and the green and the people, and down at the water, which held the whole of it, sky and valley and boat, in its long patient memory.

"The old woman at the drowned village used to say this compass was the road that remembers," she said. "I finally understand her. It doesn't point north, Daniel. It never did. It points *back*. And everything we've been doing since the pass—" she touched the brass at her belt "—is just following it home."

Daniel smiled and turned to help the scholars with the crates, and the singing rose, and Tomas was embraced by his uncle on the planks with the whole valley watching and pretending not to.

Edmund stayed at the wheel a moment longer. Below her, in the clear evening water, she could see it: the drowned village's church tower, far downstream now, days behind her, but present somehow, the way the dead are present in a family's songs. Ama Sefa's rooftops. Ama Sefa's song, come all the way home.

"First run's done," she said to the river, to the woman, to the compass. "There'll be more."

And the river, high and kind and full of the monsoon's memory, carried her answer downstream toward the delta, where the newspapers were waiting, and the judges, and the world that had drowned a valley and was learning, slowly, its own name.

Behind her, on the shore, the valley began to sing the rowing song again—and this time, Edmund realized, they were singing it for the boat's departure, not its arrival. A road song. A song for going and coming both.

She had never heard a song for a road before. She stood at the wheel and learned it.
