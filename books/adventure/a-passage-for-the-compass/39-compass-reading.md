## 39. Compass Reading

The morning came up gold through the mission's shutters, and Edmund woke with her hand already closed around the compass, as if she had been holding it in her sleep.

She lay a while in the narrow bed the sisters had given her—the same bed, she suspected, they had given her eleven years ago on the night she first ran this river alone—and listened to the building wake around her. Water in the courtyard cistern. Tomas's voice, too loud for dawn, arguing happily with Sefu about knots. The groan of her boat at the dock below, riding high now that the flood had spent itself, complaining the way old boats complain when they are happy.

Daniel had returned the compass to her two nights ago, after the testimony, after the judge's clerk had sealed the copies into evidence and the newspaper man had filled three pages with words that would do their slow work whether or not anyone thanked them for it. He had brought it to her on the dock wrapped in a scrap of oilcloth, and he had said, *I repaired the hinge while we were in the valley. It was loose. I hope you don't mind that I opened it.*

She hadn't minded. She hadn't thought about it much at all, truthfully—there had been testimony to give, and Vane's confession to sit through without satisfaction, and Ines's maps to unroll across the mission table until midnight, and the strange hollow business of being debt-free, which turned out to feel less like setting down a weight than like setting down a limb she'd been walking on so long she'd forgotten it wasn't part of her.

But this morning there was nothing urgent anywhere in the world, and her hand was closed around the compass, and she remembered what Daniel had said about opening it.

* * *

She sat up and turned it over in the light from the shutter cracks.

It was a good instrument. She had always known that, in the wordless way a pilot knows a thing by how it sits in the hand—the weight of it, brass-cased, older than her boat, older probably than her father. The glass was slightly convex, slightly clouded at the edges, and under it the needle swung with a patience no modern instrument had ever shown her. On the underside, where the case met the back plate, there was a seam she had never once examined in thirty years of carrying the thing. Why would she? A compass is not a box. A compass is a needle and a direction and nothing else.

Except that Daniel had said *hinge*, and hinges imply doors.

She found her thumbnail in the seam and pressed. For a long moment nothing happened, and she felt foolish—a grown woman prying at an heirloom in a mission bedroom like a child with a locket—then something gave with a small click she felt more than heard, and the back plate lifted away on a sliver of hidden metal, thin as a fingernail moon.

Beneath it, engraved into the inner face of the case where no one would ever see it unless they knew to look: two sets of initials, side by side, joined by an ampersand.

**E.J. & A.H.**

Edmund Joubert. She read her own name first, of course. Her father's name—Edmund Joubert senior, the man who had taught her to read water before she could read letters, who had died owing money and left her a boat and a compass and a debt that had eaten twenty years of her life. And beside him, cut by the same hand, in the same hour, by the look of the strokes:

A.H. Aurel Halvorsen.

*Partners.*

The word arrived in her mind without permission and then refused to leave. Not creditor and debtor. Not benefactor and beggar. Partners. Engraved together inside a compass that had been carried against her heart through drowned villages and smugglers' tolls and a whiteout on the pass, pointing north the whole time, pointing home the whole time, and she had never once thought to ask what else it might be carrying.

* * *

She dressed without noticing she was dressing and went down through the mission corridors with the compass open in her palm, past the sisters sweeping the courtyard, out onto the dock where the morning mist was lifting off the river in ropes.

Ines was there, sitting on the bollard with her boots off and her feet nearly touching the water, sketchbook on her knees, doing what Ines did every morning since the valley: drawing the river as if to make sure it would still be there when she looked up. She glanced at Edmund's face and put her pencil down.

"What is it?"

Edmund held out the compass. "Look under the plate."

Ines took it with the care she took everything—maps, fragments of burned archive, other people's grief—and tilted it to the light. Edmund watched her read the initials, watched her lips move, watched the moment land.

"Your father," Ines said slowly, "and Halvorsen."

"Together." Edmund heard her own voice come out flat and strange. "Not one man lending to another. Together. Like—" She stopped, because the next word was *family*, and she wasn't ready for it.

"Like partners," Ines finished. She looked up. "Edmund. Do you know what this means?"

"I know what it means for every story I've told myself." Edmund sat down heavily on the dock planks, and the river moved beneath them, patient, brown-gold, going about its business. "I built my whole life on a story. My father borrowed from Halvorsen. My father died owing. The debt passed to me like a fever, and I paid it because that's what you do—you pay what you owe, you keep your boat, you keep your mouth shut and your bow pointed upriver. Twenty years I steered by that story."

"And now?"

"And now the story has a door in the bottom of it, and behind the door are two men who were friends. Who were *partners*. Who put their names inside a compass together like—" She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Like two boys carving their names in a tree."

"Not boys," Ines said quietly. "Men who trusted each other enough to hide their trust where only they could find it." She turned the compass over once more, then closed the plate gently and handed it back. "You should talk to Daniel."

"I know."

"He'll be at the survey office. He's been there since six."

"I know that too." Edmund stood. Her knees ached in the way they did after long descents, though the descent had been days ago. "Ines—when you were copying those fragments, the ones we saved from the fire. Did any of them mention my father?"

Ines was quiet a moment, and Edmund saw her deciding something. "There was a ledger page. Half burned. It listed contributions to the reservoir survey—money, equipment, boats." She met Edmund's eyes. "Two boats were chartered for the original survey, Edmund. Thirty years ago. The *Mariana* and the *Halcyon*. The Mariana was captained by E. Joubert."

The river went on beneath them. Somewhere upstream a kingfisher hit the water and came up flashing.

"The Halcyon," Edmund said. "That was—"

"Aurel Halvorsen's boat. Yes." Ines picked up her boots but didn't put them on. "Your father didn't borrow from Halvorsen, Edmund. They surveyed this river together. Whatever happened after—the debt, the dam, all of it—it happened between partners. Between friends."

* * *

The survey office smelled of ink and wet paper and the particular staleness of rooms where important decisions are made by tired men. Daniel Halvorsen sat at a corner desk with his sleeves rolled, copying figures in his careful expedition hand, and when he looked up and saw Edmund's face he knew—she could see him know—before she said a word.

"You opened it," he said.

"You repaired it." She set the compass on his desk between the inkwell and a cold cup of coffee. "You knew what was under the plate."

"Yes."

"For how long?"

He leaned back. Up close, in honest light, he looked less like his father than he had on the ridge above the valley—tired, sunburned, human. "Since I was a boy. My father showed me once. He'd had too much wine at Christmas and he took the compass down from the shelf—I didn't even know he kept it, it was supposed to have gone with the boat—and he showed me the initials and he said, *This belongs with the other half.* And then the next day he pretended it hadn't happened, and a year later he was dead, and the debt papers came to your family, and I was fourteen and afraid of my mother and I said nothing." He rubbed his eyes. "For twenty years I've been waiting for someone to bring that compass to me. When I saw it on your belt at the reunion, Edmund, I nearly fell off my feet."

"Why didn't you tell me then?"

"Because you'd just climbed a mountain to save my expedition, and because some things shouldn't be said standing up in the wind." He gestured at the chair across from him. "Sit down. Please."

She sat. Outside the window, the river city went about its morning—carts, gulls, the bell of the customs house—and none of it mattered at all.

"Tell me about them," Edmund said. "My father. Yours. Tell me the true version."

Daniel folded his hands on the desk. "They met young, running cargo on this river. Your father was the better pilot; mine was the better with numbers. They bought the survey contract together—thirty years ago, when the government first started talking about damming the upper basin. Two boats, two captains, one contract. They spent four seasons mapping every channel, every village landing, every family that drew water from that river." His voice caught slightly. "The archive in the valley—the oldest parts of it, the parts that burned—they weren't just valley records. Some of those pages were copies of my father's and your father's own field notes. They gave them to the valley people for safekeeping because they didn't trust the survey office. They were right not to."

"The reservoir," Edmund said. "The expansion. The one your father financed."

Daniel nodded slowly. "Here's what I've pieced together, and what the letters confirm. The survey found what we found—that the basin wasn't empty wilderness. It was lived in, cultivated, remembered. A whole culture with its roots in that valley. My father reported it. And then men with more money than either of them ever saw explained to him that the report could be filed differently, and that there was a great deal of financing available for a man who filed it differently." He looked down at his hands. "He took the money. He financed the dam. And then he couldn't live with it, and he tried to buy back his conscience the only way he understood—which was to lend, endlessly, generously, to everyone the project had harmed. Including, when your father refused to stay silent about what the survey had really found and lost his charter because of it, including your father. The loan that became your debt was my father trying to keep his partner's family fed after he'd destroyed his partner's livelihood."

Edmund sat very still. Through the window, the river glittered, indifferent and beautiful, carrying everything downstream the way it always had.

"So the debt was never a debt."

"It was guilt wearing debt's clothes." Daniel's jaw tightened. "And when he died, the paper passed to lawyers, and lawyers don't carry guilt, they carry balances. If my letters had reached you—if Vane hadn't been intercepting correspondence between my father's estate and this river for years—we could have ended this a decade ago. That's on me. That's on all of us who let paper stand in for truth."

"My father," Edmund said, and stopped, and started again. "Did he hate him? After?"

"I don't think a man engraves his partner's name beside his own inside a compass if he hates him." Daniel reached across the desk and touched the brass case, lightly, the way you touch a sleeping animal. "I think he kept it because it was half of something. And I think he gave it to you because he wanted you to have the half that still pointed somewhere true."

* * *

She walked back down to the dock slowly, taking the long way along the waterfront, and the city parted around her the way cities do when you're carrying something heavy that no one can see.

Twenty years. Twenty years of steering by a story that had a lie in its keel. She thought of the old woman in the drowned village, calling the compass by a name she hadn't known—*the river's eye*, the woman had called it—and how Edmund had laughed politely and tucked it away. The river's eye. Of course. Her father had helped draw the maps that named every bend of it. The compass had never been a payment for a loan. It had been a partnership made small enough to hold in one hand, and she had carried it like a ballast stone when it was meant to be a key.

At the dock, Tomas was re-coiling lines with the exaggerated competence of a boy who had recently become crew and wanted everyone to notice. Sefu sat on the rail mending a net, watching the boy with an expression he probably believed was neutral. The boat rode the gentle post-flood current, scarred, looted, patched, hers.

Tomas looked up. "Captain? You've got a face on you."

"Do I."

"Like you found something you lost a long time ago and it's heavier than you remembered."

Edmund almost laughed. "Something like that." She sat down on the rail beside Sefu and opened the compass plate again, and showed them both, and told them—all of it, the initials, the two boats, the survey, the guilt dressed as a loan. Sefu listened the way Sefu listened, entirely, saying nothing, and when she finished he tied off his knot and said only:

"Now the needle makes sense."

"The needle?"

"A compass points north," Sefu said. "But yours always pointed a little wrong. I noticed it on the mountain, in the whiteout. You corrected for it without thinking. Every pilot corrects for their instrument's error." He looked at her with his level dark gaze. "It wasn't error. It was pulling toward the valley. Toward where it was made. Where both their names were carved into the country."

"That's not how compasses work," Tomas began, and then stopped, because he'd seen the whiteout too, and he'd seen what the compass and Edmund's memory had done together in it, and he wasn't sure anymore where instruments ended and inheritance began.

Ines came down the dock with her sketchbook under her arm and her boots in her hand, and she didn't say anything at all, just sat down among them, and the five of them—captain, cartographer, apprentice, guide, and the boat herself groaning softly against the pilings—sat in the strengthening sun while the river ran gold-brown toward the delta.

* * *

Near noon, Daniel came down the dock carrying a flat parcel wrapped in canvas, and Edmund knew before he reached her what it was, because some things arrive announced by their own weight.

"My father's field journals," he said. "The real ones—not the copies in the archive. These came back with me from the valley. I've been reading them all night." He set the parcel on the deck beside her. "There's a letter in the last one. Dated the year you were born. It's addressed to your father, and it was never sent. I think—he wrote it during the last season they worked together, and then everything happened, and he kept it, and I think he kept it because he couldn't find the courage to send it and couldn't bear to burn it."

Edmund looked at the parcel a long time.

"You read it?" she asked.

"No." Daniel shook his head. "It's not mine. It was never mine. It's the missing half of whatever's under that plate." He stepped back. "Whenever you're ready. There's no hurry. We sail when you sail."

He walked back up the dock, and Edmund sat with the parcel on her knees, and the river waited with her.

Around her, without being asked, her crew began to work. Tomas coiled the last line and stowed it. Ines went below to her charts. Sefu tested the current with two fingers and a length of string, reading the river the old way. Nobody spoke. Everybody stayed.

And Edmund Joubert, debt-free, story-free, holding a compass that had finally told her the whole truth and a letter she hadn't yet dared to open, understood that the parcel in her lap was not the end of anything. It was the beginning of the passage home.

She untied the first knot.
