## 1. The Map of Closed Lines

The rain had been falling on Puerto Frío since Tuesday, and by Friday the town smelled of wet coal and old rope, of the harbor rotting gently into itself and the mountains behind it pretending not to notice. Ada Quintana noticed. She noticed everything about weather now — it was a professional deformity, like a doctor who couldn't stop looking at hands. Rain on the coast meant snow on the cordillera, and snow on the cordillera meant the northern lines were dying a little further, rusting in their sleep beneath drifts that would not melt until spring, if they melted at all.

She stood at the window of the rail office on the Calle del Muelle and watched a boy lead three mules through standing water that reflected the customs house in broken pieces, as if the building itself were coming apart. The office behind her was warm the way government offices are warm — reluctantly, grudgingly, the stove fed just enough to keep the clerks from complaining. A clock ticked somewhere above the filing cabinets with the patient insolence of a thing that has outlasted everyone who wound it.

"You may sit, señora Quintana," said the man from the ministry. He had said it three times already, in the tone of a man who believes repetition is a form of hospitality.

"I'll stand."

He was a small, neat man with a ministerial pallor, the color of a man who had never been rained on in his life. His name was Ferrán and his card said something with many words in it — *Delegación de Obras y Caminos, Subsecretaría de — * — but what he was, really, was a messenger, and messengers always dressed better than the news they carried.

"You understand," Ferrán said, arranging papers on the table between them with the delicacy of a man laying out surgical instruments, "that this is not an accusation of anything. The ministry wishes to be clear about that. There has been no accusation."

"Of course not," Ada said. "There's been a disappearance. Accusation would require someone to accuse."

Ferrán's pen hovered. He had the look of a man who wrote things down for a living and had just realized that some things are better left unwritten. Outside, the rain thickened, drumming on the tin awning over the freight dock, and somewhere down the line a locomotive that should not have been running — the coastal engine, the only one left with a working boiler — let go a long, low whistle, the sound of a living thing calling into a country that had stopped answering.

The northern lines had been closed for twenty years. Everyone in Puerto Frío said it the way you'd say a man had drowned, or a woman had gone mad — as a fact of geography, a thing the land had done, as if the mountains themselves had simply reached down and taken the rails back. Eleven hundred kilometers of track, three tunnels through the cordillera, forty-one bridges, and the pass above the Río Blanco that the old men still called the Puerta del Viento, though no train had breathed there in two decades. Ada had been nine years old when the last engine came down. She remembered standing on the platform of Puerto Frío with her mother's hand heavy on her shoulder and her father's face in the window of the cab, and she remembered that he had not looked at her. He had looked north, up the line, into the rain, with an expression she was too young to read and had spent her whole life learning to read.

Now the ministry wanted the north read again.

"The survey team," Ferrán said, "left San Gabriel de Arriba on the fourth of March. Six men. Engineer Delgado, two instrument men, a geologist, a topographer, and a cook. They were contracted to assess the condition of the abandoned lines — bridges, tunnels, the high route — with a view toward eventual rehabilitation." He said *rehabilitation* the way a priest says *resurrection*: with hope he did not entirely own. "They were to report by the end of April. They did not report."

"Someone must have gone up after them."

"Someone did." Ferrán's pen made a small, apologetic note. "A party from San Gabriel went as far as the second bridge over the Río Blanco. They found the trail abandoned — mules, gear, everything in order, they said. And then they found nothing, which frightened them, and they came back down. The ministry has no one qualified to go further. The passes—" he gestured vaguely at the window, at the gray wall of the cordillera where the rain ended and the weather became something older, "—the passes are closing. We are told there is a window. A matter of days, perhaps a week, when the high route is passable. After that—"

"After that, winter," Ada said. "And winter up there doesn't end. It just gets interrupted."

"Exactly so." He seemed relieved to have the weather explained to him by someone who sounded as if she'd argued with it before. "Which is why the ministry requires someone with—" he consulted his papers, though Ada was certain he had memorized them, "—specific experience. You surveyed the coastal realignment in '09. You rebuilt the viaduct at Quebrada Honda after the slide. And your father—" the pause was so small a less attentive ear would have missed it, "—your father, Señor Emilio Quintana, surveyed the northern lines originally. There is no one living who knows that country as your family does."

*No one living.* Ada turned it over and found the edges sharp. Her father had been dead eleven years, and the mountains had taken the habit of killing Quintanas, apparently, and now the government wished to offer the family a third turn.

"What are they paying?" she asked, because the question was easier than the one underneath it.

Ferrán named a figure. It was a good figure. It was, in fact, an embarrassingly good figure, the kind of number a government produces when it is frightened and wants the fright to look like generosity. Ada had negotiated contracts for eleven years and she knew what a number meant by its shape, and this number had the shape of a door being closed behind her.

"And if I find them?"

"Then you find them." Ferrán folded his hands. "And if you find what happened to them, you report it. To the ministry. To me." He said it twice, and Ada heard the second time as the real one. *To me.* Not to the newspapers in the capital, not to the families in San Gabriel, not to anyone with a press or a pulpit. *To me.*

"Mr. Ferrán," she said, "I've been a railroad engineer for fifteen years. I've been offered bribes by three governors and a bishop. When a man tells me twice who to report to, I start wondering what's up the line he doesn't want reported."

Something flickered behind the ministerial pallor — respect, or fear, or the particular exhaustion of a small man carrying a large secret. "Señora Quintana," he said quietly, "I am a clerk. I am the least important man in this building, and I was chosen for that reason. What is up that line, I do not know. But I know that six men are missing, and I know that the money for this contract came from an account that does not appear in the ministry's ledgers, and I know that when I asked about that account I was told to ask nothing further." He pushed the contract across the table. "I would rather it were you. That is all I am permitted to say, and more than I should have."

The rain came harder. Down at the freight dock, a crane swung a crate over the water and the boy with the mules watched it pass with his mouth open, and the coastal locomotive whistled again, and Ada Quintana stood in a warm room in a wet town and felt the north open inside her like a wound that had only ever been bandaged, never closed.

She signed.

* * *

Her office above the chandlery was one room with a south-facing window and a drafting table that had belonged to her father before it belonged to her, and it was there, in the last gray hour of the afternoon, that she took down the map.

She did not take it down often. She told herself it was because she had no need of it — the closed lines were closed, the northern country was a memory, and an engineer who kept a dead man's map over her desk the way other men kept a photograph of a sweetheart would be pitied, or worse, indulged. But the truth was simpler and she knew it: the map was heavy. Not in the hand — in the chest. It was drawn on linen-backed paper in her father's own ink, in his own hand, and his hand had been a beautiful hand, the hand of a man who believed that a line drawn honestly was a kind of promise, and every time she unrolled it she heard his voice in her ear saying the thing he had said to her the last time they stood together on a survey stake above the Río Blanco, when she was nineteen and apprenticing and full of her own competence:

*"A map is a promise, Ada. The country keeps its side. Make sure you keep yours."*

She spread it on the drafting table and weighted the corners with the brass objects that lived for this purpose: a plumb bob, a broken compass, a paperweight shaped like a locomotive that Tomás had given her when they were children and still spoke to each other without arithmetic.

The map hummed.

She knew it didn't, of course. Linen and ink and iron-gall do not hum. But there was a frequency to it, a standing wave, the way a bridge hums under a train at speed — the sense of enormous weight moving across a structure that holds. Eleven hundred kilometers of line ran across that paper, and every kilometer of it was drawn twice: once in the confident black of the original survey, and once in red, in a different, later hand — a government hand, a hand that had never stood in the rain at Quebrada Honda — and the red lines were the closures. *Line suspended. Line suspended. Tunnel sealed. Bridge condemned. Line suspended.*

Her father had drawn the black. The ministry had drawn the red. And between the two, in the margins, in his small, fierce annotations — *bridge sound, do not let them condemn this*; *tunnel dry, timber intact, closure unnecessary*; *the pass holds, I have held it, I have measured it* — her father had spent the last years of his life arguing with the red ink, and the red ink had won, and then her father had died on the last survey he ever walked, alone, above the Río Blanco, and the ministry had called it an accident of weather and everyone had believed it because it was easier to believe than the alternative, which was that the mountains had wanted him.

Or that someone had wanted him to stop surveying.

She had never let herself think that sentence all the way through. Eleven years, and she had kept it in the same place she kept the map — rolled up, weighted, in the dark. But Ferrán's contract lay on the table beside it now, and the two documents seemed to be in conversation, and the map was winning.

She traced the line north with one finger. Puerto Frío. San Gabriel de Arriba. The second bridge over the Río Blanco, where the rescue party had turned back. Then the country that came after — the country of the closed lines, the country of the red ink: the Quebrada del Cóndor, the tunnel at Kilometer 214, the high route over the pass itself, the Puerta del Viento, drawn in her father's hand with a delicacy that was almost tenderness, contour lines stacked like the rings of a tree, and beside the pass, in the margin, the annotation she had read ten thousand times and never understood until this afternoon, when it seemed suddenly less like a note and more like a message left in a bottle:

*The pass is real. Whatever they tell you, the pass is real.*

*They.* Her father had never said who *they* were. She had assumed, as a girl, that he meant the government, the ministry, the men with the red ink. But red ink is cheap and governments are many, and a man does not write *whatever they tell you* in the margin of his life's work unless he is afraid of something specific, something with a name, something he has seen up close.

Ada stood for a long time in the failing light with her finger on the pass, and the rain came down on the town, and the stove ticked as it cooled, and the harbor bell rang the half hour with its flat, drowned voice, and she thought about the figure Ferrán had named, and the account that was not in the ledgers, and six men walking north into a country that had already eaten her father, and she thought about the window — the calving window, the few days each spring when the glacier above the high route let go of its winter and opened the corridor through the ice, a door that stood ajar for forty-eight hours and then shut for another year.

She could refuse. She could send Ferrán back down the coast with his contract and his frightened money and let the north keep its dead, all of them, her father among them, and live out her life repairing coastal viaducts and never again standing where the air went thin and the silence had a texture to it, a pressure, like the silence inside a tunnel before the rock moves.

But the map was open on the table, and the map was a promise, and her father had kept his side of it, and the question that had been bandaged for eleven years was now standing in the room with her, dripping, asking to be looked at:

*What did he find up there that they closed the lines to hide?*

She began, then, in the last of the light, to pack. Instruments first — theodolite, levels, the aneroid her father had carried, its brass worn bright at the case by his thumb and now by hers. Rope. The good boots. She worked the way she did everything, in order, without hurry, an engineer assembling a structure that would have to hold, and she was halfway through when she stopped, because she understood that she could not do this alone.

Not because the country required more hands — it did, but she could hire hands. It was the other thing. The map lay between her and the north like a locked door, and there was one other person alive who had grown up with that map, who had learned its contours before he learned his letters, who had once been the better draftsman of the two of them, before the drink and the disgrace and the eleven years of silence that had a name she did not say and a shape she could not stop tracing.

Tomás was in Villarrica, the border town, three days south. She knew this because she knew everything about Tomás that could be known without speaking to him, which was the way they had loved each other for a decade now — at a distance, through intermediaries, like two survey parties working opposite sides of the same unclimbable ridge, each mapping the other's shadow.

Ada rolled up the map. She did it carefully, along the old creases, and she felt the hum of it go quiet as it closed, the way a struck bell goes quiet, and in the quiet she heard the rain, and under the rain, far off, so far it might have been her own blood in her own ears, she heard the north.

Six men were up there. Or the place where six men stopped being men. And the snow was already falling on the high passes, and the window was opening, and her father's last annotation was waiting in the margin like a hand held out across eleven years.

She put the map in its oilcloth sleeve, and the sleeve in her pack, and she wrote a telegram to Villarrica in her father's beautiful hand, four words, and paid the boy double to run it to the telegraph office before it closed:

*COME NORTH. BRING YOUR EYES.*
