## 2. Gear Without Tracks

The mule track gave out two hours past the last telegraph post, and after that the mountain made its own rules.

Ada Quintana rode with her father's map folded against her ribs, inside the oilskin where she kept the things she could not afford to lose and could not bear to look at. She had not unfolded it since Puerto Frío. She did not need to. Twenty years of sleeping beside that paper had printed it into her: every closed line drawn in her father's careful hand, every station name crossed through — *Vado Seco, Cerrada 1903* — every tunnel he had walked into and measured and never come back to explain. The government man in his office smelling of ink and coffee had slid the contract across the desk as if he were handing her a shovel, and she had signed because refusing would have meant admitting how much she still wanted to go.

Now she was going. The Cordillera del Viento rose ahead of them in tiers of granite and cloud, and the river ran beside the trail the way rivers do in narrow valleys — close enough to hear, too fast to trust. It was the same river her father's map was drawn for. She tried not to think of it as a companion. Rivers on this side of the divide had a habit of taking things and giving nothing back but silt.

Río Salazar rode point, the way he had ridden point for her father on a survey no one had ever finished. He had not spoken in an hour. That was Río's way; silence from him was not absence but attention, and she had learned long ago to read the set of his shoulders the way other people read weather. Right now his shoulders said *nothing behind us, nothing above us yet*, and she let herself believe him for another hour.

Tomás brought up the rear with the pack mules, and the sound of him — the creak of leather, the occasional muttered curse when a mule tested his authority — was the only proof she had that her brother had actually come. In Puerto Frío he had said yes so quickly it frightened her, and then spent three days drinking as though to take the edge off having agreed. She had not asked him why he'd said yes. Some questions you save for altitude, where there is less air for lying.

The campsite announced itself by smell before they saw it: woodsmoke gone cold and sweet, the particular staleness of a fire that died mid-life rather than at its end.

Río raised a fist and they stopped. The trail bent around a shoulder of rock, and below it, tucked against the treeline where the alerce forest began its last stand before the scree, lay the Delgado survey camp.

Ada dismounted and stood very still.

She had prepared herself for wreckage. That was what the government man's face had prepared her for, all those careful questions about bandits and weather and *how far upriver exactly*. She had imagined torn canvas, scattered instruments, the ordinary archaeology of disaster. What she saw instead was a camp that looked like its owners had stepped out to check a reading and meant to come back before dark.

Three tents stood pitched and guyed, taut as the day they were raised. The cook fire had been banked, not abandoned — ash raked over coals in the neat spiral of someone who expected to return and breathe it back to life. A kettle sat on a flat stone. A shirt hung drying on a line between two trees, collar turned out, sleeves pinned by twigs. It had been rained on and dried and rained on again until the fabric had gone soft and grey as lichen, but no one had taken it down.

"Nobody leaves a shirt," Tomás said behind her. His voice came out flat, stripped of the irony he wore like armor in the low country. "Nobody leaves a shirt unless they're coming back."

"Or unless they were stopped from coming back," Río said.

Ada walked into the camp the way she would walk into a machine room whose rhythm had gone wrong — slowly, listening with more than her ears.

The instruments were what undid her first. In the largest tent, the theodolite stood on its tripod under a canvas fly, and when she lifted the fly and put her eye to the eyepiece, the crosshairs found the ridgeline instantly, cleanly, the instrument still leveled, still zeroed on a sightline someone had established days or weeks ago. Beside it, the barograph — she knew the make, had serviced a dozen of them on the coastal lines — ticked away on its drum, the ink trace rising and falling with each pressure change like a pulse. Someone had wound it. Someone had been winding it. The trace on the drum was unbroken, seven full days of atmosphere recorded in a fine black seam, and no hand had disturbed it.

"They didn't pack out," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her, too calm. "You don't abandon a barograph. You don't leave a theodolite leveled. These are the most expensive things in the camp and they're sitting here like furniture."

"Bandits don't work this way either," Río said. He had moved to the food cache, a platform lashed between two trees, greased canvas over the top. He untied it with two fingers and looked inside for a long moment. "Flour. Salt pork. Dried peaches. Sugar." He held up a sack of hardtack, turned it over. "Unopened. No sign of animals getting in, which means someone's been keeping it sealed." He set it down with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down something that might go off. "Six men eat through a cache like this in ten days if they're hungry. This looks barely touched."

"So they left," Tomás said. "Walked out light."

"In which direction?" Ada said. "Show me their tracks."

No one answered, which was itself an answer, but she made them show her anyway. They circled the camp together, three pairs of eyes reading ground — Río best, then her, then Tomás, who had spent his disgrace years mapping places he could describe only from other men's notes and had lost some of the ground-reading he'd been born to. The soil here was a mix of pine duff and glacial silt, soft enough to hold a print for weeks in the dry cold of the high valley. There were tracks everywhere inside the camp: boot prints, mule prints, the scuffed crescents where men had stood at the fire, the double-line drag of a sledge hauled toward the tent. Six men, maybe seven. Weeks old, hardened at the edges, softened by rain.

The tracks simply stopped.

Not faded. Not washed out. Stopped — at a line perhaps forty meters beyond the last tent, where the alerce forest broke and the ground tilted upward into open scree. Ada knelt at the boundary and put her fingers in the dirt. Beyond that invisible line the ground was untouched. No prints. No dung. No broken branches at knee height where men push through. The scree above lay in its natural order, stones settled against stones the way they settle when nothing has walked on them since the last freeze.

It was as if six men had walked to the edge of the trees and been lifted off the earth.

"The wind," Tomás started, and stopped himself, because they both knew the wind did not erase selectively. Wind erased in fans and sheets. It did not draw a clean border at the treeline and respect it.

"Snowmelt?" she offered, just to hear how hollow it sounded.

"It hasn't melted up there in a month." Río crouched beside her. He was close enough that she could smell woodsmoke and the resin he chewed, and beneath both, faintly, the river. "I guided your father through this valley twice. I know what ground tells you here. This ground is telling us a lie, or telling us something we don't want to hear."

Ada stood and looked at the camp again, and now she saw it differently, the way a bridge engineer sees a span differently once she knows the load path has failed somewhere hidden. Everything intact. Everything running. Everything *maintained* — the barograph wound, the cache sealed against animals, the fire banked. Someone had been caring for this camp. Or something wanted it to look cared for.

That thought arrived quietly and then refused to leave.

She went through the tents methodically while the men watched the treeline without saying they were watching it. Clothing, folded. Blankets, rolled. A tin of pencils. Delgado's spare glasses case — empty, so the man himself had his spectacles, wherever he was. A letter half-written in Spanish, home to a wife in Valdivia, cheerful in the way letters are when the writer believes he'll finish them tomorrow: *the pass shows better than the old maps claim, we may be able to give the Republic a route the rail men only dreamed of.* Ada read that sentence twice and felt it go into her like a splinter. *The old maps.* Somewhere in a drawer in Puerto Frío, her father's survey of this same pass sat crossed-out and officially dead, and this stranger had looked at the same mountains and seen a living route.

In the smallest tent she found the logbook.

It was a standard survey diary, canvas-bound, and the last entry was eleven days old. Routine readings. Barometric figures. A note about a lame mule. And then, in Delgado's hand, smaller than the rest, pressed hard into the page as though written quickly or angrily:

*Saw lights on the north ridge after midnight. Not stars. Three points, moving west to east, regular spacing. V. says it will be the company's prospecting party out of Puerto Frío Norte, but the company has no concession on this side of the divide. Nobody does. We will move up to the saddle tomorrow and have a look.*

There was no entry for the next day. There was no next day.

Ada closed the book and held it against the map under her coat, one document finding the other through the layers of cloth, and she understood that she was holding the beginning of a story whose middle was missing and whose end was standing right here in the shape of three empty tents and a shirt no one took down.

* * *

They made camp downstream of the site, out of sight of it, because none of them said aloud that they didn't want to sleep among the ghosts' belongings. Río built a small fire in a hollow and screened it with a rock. The river talked to itself in the dark, that high-country mutter of water working stone, and Ada sat apart from the others with the logbook open on her knees, reading it again from the beginning, letting Delgado's voice build itself in her head: methodical, dry-humored, increasingly puzzled.

The entries told a clean professional story for most of the book. Then, in the final week, the handwriting tightened. Readings taken at odd hours. A note about instruments drifting — *theodolite off true by four minutes this morning, re-leveled, cannot account* — which to any surveyor is a sentence that raises the hair on the arms, because a theodolite does not drift. A note about a sound in the night, *like blasting, but wrong, too deep, felt in the feet before heard in the ears.* And then the lights on the ridge, and the decision to go up, and nothing.

Blasting. Ada turned the word over. There were mines in these ranges, old ones, dead ones, and new ones that paid the government for the courtesy of being ignored. But the letter in the tent had said it plainly: nobody held a concession on this side of the divide. So who blasted in the dark, deep enough to feel through bedrock, on land where no one was supposed to be?

She looked up. Tomás sat near the fire sharpening a knife he did not need to sharpen, the blade whispering over the stone in long even strokes, his eyes on the middle distance where his thoughts usually lived. Río had taken first watch and stood at the edge of the firelight facing upstream, motionless as one of the stones he grew up among.

"Tomás." He didn't startle; he never startled; he just turned his head. "The consortiums working the northern cordillera. When you were drawing for them. Did any of them hold ground past the divide?"

The knife stopped. For a moment the only sound was the river.

"You've been saving that question for altitude," he said.

"I'm asking it anyway."

He laid the knife down carefully, aligning it with the stone, a cartographer's tidiness even in exile. "Consorcio Andrade holds everything on the eastern slope. Copper, mostly, and whatever they tell the ministry it is. They bought their concessions cheap because everyone believed there was nothing past the divide worth the rails to reach." He paused. "Everyone believed it because the surveys said so. Because our father's survey said so. Closed lines. Dead routes. Nothing up there but ice and wind."

"And you drew for them."

"I drew what they paid me to draw." He said it without flinching, which was worse than flinching. "That's what I've been doing for fifteen years, Ada. Drawing what people pay for. It's why my license lives in a drawer in a cantina in Los Arrayanes." He picked the knife up again. "Andrade doesn't send prospecting parties onto land they don't own. They send crews to make sure no one else finds out what's there. If Delgado saw lights on that ridge—"

"He saw a crew that isn't supposed to exist."

"I'd bet the license I lost on it."

Ada looked into the fire. The flames bent sideways in a gust off the glacier, and for a heartbeat the shadows of the three of them leaned east together, one shape.

Below the fire's crackle, beneath the river's voice, she thought she caught — or imagined she caught — the sound Río had described from his years here: something deep, percussive, felt in the soles of the feet. She waited. It did not come again. The mountain kept its counsel.

"We go up at first light," she said. "To the saddle. We follow Delgado's route and we find out what's on that ridge."

"And if the crew that isn't supposed to exist is still there?" Tomás asked.

"Then they'll have the same problem Delgado's team had," Ada said, and heard her own voice come out level and cold and certain, the voice of her father rather than of the woman who had spent twenty years avoiding this valley. "They'll be dealing with someone who reads ground."

Río, at the edge of the light, had not moved. But when she glanced at him, he was looking at her with an expression she remembered from another lifetime — the look of a man watching someone decide to become who they were always going to be. He touched two fingers to his hat brim, the guide's acknowledgment, and turned back to the dark.

Above the treeline, the scree fields climbed silver-grey into shadow, unmarked, waiting. Somewhere up there, six men had walked out of the world leaving their shirts hanging and their clocks ticking. Tomorrow she would walk up into the place that had swallowed them, carrying her father's map against her ribs like a second heart.

The wind shifted. From far up the valley, faint but unmistakable, carried down the river's corridor of cold air, came a sound like thunder with no storm attached — one deep concussion, then another, patient, rhythmic, like the mountain clearing its throat.

Río's head came up. Across the fire, Tomás's knife went silent.

"That," Río said quietly, "is not calving. Wrong season. Wrong glacier." He listened a moment longer, and when he spoke again his voice had changed, gone flat and hard as the scree above them. "That's blasting. And it's coming from the saddle."
