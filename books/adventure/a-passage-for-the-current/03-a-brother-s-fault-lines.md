## 3. A Brother's Fault Lines

The border town had a name — Puerto Ceniza, they called it, ash port — but Tomás Quintana thought of it as the place where maps went to die. He lived above a cantina called El Farol, in a room with one window that looked out on the freight yard where trains idled all night like sick animals, breathing diesel into the dark. The room contained a bed, a chair, a table scarred by rings from a hundred glasses, and a trunk he had not opened in six years. The trunk held his instruments: the brass alidade, the plane table with its cracked varnish, the rolls of vellum gone soft at the edges like old skin. A cartographer's tools are a confession, his father used to say. They show every lie you've ever drawn.

Tomás did not open the trunk. He drank instead, downstairs, in the amber light of El Farol, where the ceiling fan turned with the slow resignation of a thing that has forgotten why it spins.

It was raining the evening she came for him. He heard her before he saw her — that was always true of Ada, even as children; she moved through the world like weather announcing itself — boots on the stairs, unhurried, each step placed with an engineer's certainty. He was sitting at the corner table with a glass of aguardiente he'd been nursing for an hour because nursing was all he could afford, and when he looked up she was standing over him with rain beaded on her shoulders and her father's eyes looking out of her face like lamps in a window of a house everyone believed empty.

"Tomás."

He made himself finish the swallow before answering. "You're wet."

"It's raining."

"In Puerto Frío too?"

She pulled out the chair across from him without being invited, which was also like her. She sat the way she stood, spine straight, hands flat on the table as if it might try to leave. Up close he could see what the years had done to her — the fine lines at the corners of her mouth, the gray coming in early at her temples, the calluses on her fingers that spoke of wrenches and calipers rather than pens. She had become their father. That was the cruelty of it. He had spent six years trying to stop becoming him, and Ada had spent those same years becoming him so completely that sometimes, when Tomás dreamed, it was her face on the body in the river.

"You got my letters," he said. It wasn't a question. He'd written three, over the years, and mailed them drunk enough to be brave and sober enough to mean them, and none had come back marked undeliverable, which meant she had read them and chosen silence.

"I got your letters." She turned her glass when the barman set one down — she hadn't ordered; he had, reflexively, remembering she took water, only water, never anything that could dull the instruments of her mind — and she looked at the water as if it were a document requiring interpretation. "I didn't answer because I didn't have anything to say that wouldn't start a fight."

"And now?"

"Now there's work." She reached inside her coat and laid something on the table between them, and Tomás's stomach dropped through the floorboards of the cantina, through the earth, down to whatever cold place holds the things we bury deepest.

It was the map. Not a copy. The original. Their father's map of the closed northern lines, drawn in that small severe hand Tomás could reproduce from memory better than his own signature — the hand that had taught him letterforms at this very kind of table, guiding his fingers over the strokes, *a map is a promise, mijo, every line you draw is a promise to someone who will walk it in the dark.* The paper was foxed with age, soft at the folds, and the ink had faded to the brown of dried blood, and there it all was: the Cordillera del Viento, the abandoned surveys, the pass that officially did not exist, the route their father had walked the summer before the river took him.

Tomás did not touch it. He kept his hands around his glass and studied it the way you study a snake you once loved.

"A government contract," Ada said. "A survey team went missing up there. Delgado's people. Six of them, gone two months. They want someone to find out what happened before the snows close everything."

"They want you." Not a question either. The Quintanas had a genius for stating terrible things plainly.

"The government wants me. I want—" She stopped. Started again, and he watched her rebuild the sentence like a bridge with a span knocked out. "I can't read the old lines alone. The northern surveys were done in two hands. His and—" She didn't say *yours*. She didn't have to. "There are annotations in the margins. Shorthand. Yours."

"My shorthand," Tomás said, and laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, rusty, like a gate on a line no train had run in twenty years. "So now my hand is good for something."

"Tomás."

"Don't." He put the glass down harder than he meant to. The barman glanced over; the fan turned; somewhere a dog complained at the rain. "Don't say my name like that. Like we're twelve and I've broken something in the shop."

"Weren't you?"

The words landed exactly where she aimed them, which was always her gift and her sin — Ada never missed, not with a transit, not with a truth. Tomás looked at the map again. In the lower margin, half worn away, was the little mark their father used for water under stone: a wave over a solid line. *The river keeps what the mountain lends,* he used to say. *Draw your bridges for the flood year, not the dry one.* And then the river had kept him. Taken him the way the current takes a rail tie, casually, completely, leaving the family to stand on the bank and count what was lost.

Six years ago. Six years since the constable in Puerto Frío had come to the door with his hat in his hands. Six years since Tomás had stood at the funeral beside his sister and felt nothing but the enormous fraudulent silence of the mountains to the north, the mountains that had eaten their father and given back a body too broken to identify except by the surveyor's ring fused to his finger. Six years since Ada had said, at the graveside, in a voice like iron cooling, *the river took him* — and Tomás had said nothing, said absolutely nothing, because saying anything would have meant telling her what he knew, and what he knew would have broken her, and he had chosen, coward that he was, to let her keep her clean grief rather than give her the filthy truth.

That was the wedge. Not the death. The silence after it.

"I'll pay you," Ada said into the quiet. "Not much. Expenses and a wage. It's government money, which means it arrives late and apologizes for itself, but it arrives."

"I don't need money."

"You need something. Everyone in this town needs something." Her eyes went briefly, mercilessly, to his glass, to the rings on the table, to the stairway leading up to the room where his license to practice cartography hung face-down against the wall because he could not bear to look at it and could not bear to throw it away. "I'm not here to judge how you've lived, Tomás. I'm here because you're the best field topographer I ever worked with, and because the northern lines were drawn in your hand, and because—"

"Because I owe him," Tomás said.

Ada's jaw tightened. "Because he trusted you."

"He trusted me." Tomás heard his own voice go strange, flattened, the voice of a man reading coordinates aloud. "Yes. He did. That was his mistake, wasn't it? Trusting us. Trusting the mountains to behave. Trusting the river to keep its bargains." He pushed back from the table. The chair legs shrieked on the boards. "Find another draftsman, Ada. There are boys in the capital hungry for work. They'll draw your lines straight and they won't ask what happened to the man who drew them crooked."

He was three steps toward the stairs when she spoke, and she didn't raise her voice, which was worse.

"What do you know about how he died?"

The rain filled the pause. Rain on the tin roof of El Farol, rain in the freight yard, rain coming down off the cordillera a hundred miles north where it would turn to snow and seal the passes and keep all its secrets until spring, if secrets could be kept that long, if anything up there could be kept at all.

Tomás turned around slowly. Ada hadn't moved. She sat with her father's map between her hands and her father's patience in her face, and Tomás understood suddenly, with the clarity that only comes at the bottom of a fall, that she had not come for a draftsman. She had come because she suspected. Because engineers notice load paths, and something in the story of their father's death had never borne weight properly for her — the timing, the route, the fact that a man who knew that river like his own pulse had drowned in it on a clear day in the dry season.

And she had come to him first. Before anyone. Before the government, before the archives, before the truth. His sister, whom he had not written to in four years, had carried his name into her calculations like a fixed point she refused to triangulate away.

"There's a guide," Tomás said. His voice came out rough. "For the high route. If you're going past the treeline you'll need one, and there's only one man alive who knows the glacier windows." He came back to the table. He didn't sit. He put one finger on the edge of the map, the first time he had touched it in six years, and the paper was cool and slightly damp from the rain on her coat, and touching it was like touching a wound that has finally stopped bleeding and started itching. "Salazar. Río Salazar. He ran the last surveys with Papá. Ask him about the calving season. Ask him what week the ice opens the corridor."

"I know about Salazar."

Of course she did. Tomás almost smiled. Of course she had already found the guide, already priced the mules, already calculated the tonnage and the rations and the window of weather down to the hour. She had probably known Río Salazar's rates before she'd bought her ticket. What she didn't know — couldn't know — was that Río had loved her since before she was old enough to see it, and that Río had been the last man to speak with their father alive, and that Tomás had drunk with Río exactly once, in this same cantina, four years ago, and neither of them had managed to say the thing they both carried, and Río had left before dawn and Tomás had finished the bottle alone.

"Then you don't need me," Tomás said quietly.

"I need someone who can read these lines." She turned the map toward him. "Look at the eastern fork. Look at what he wrote."

Tomás leaned over it. The lamplight of El Farol fell yellow across the faded ink, and there, in the eastern fork of the dead river, in their father's small severe hand, a single annotation he had never seen before, added after the last official survey, after the closure, after everything:

*They will come for this. Keep it moving.*

Below it, the wave-over-line mark. Water under stone. The river keeps what the mountain lends.

Tomás stood very still. Somewhere behind the bar a radio was playing a waltz from the coast, thin and tinny, a music for weddings and departures. He thought about the trunk upstairs, the alidade wrapped in oilcloth, the vellum waiting. He thought about the thing he had sworn at the graveside, silently, kneeling in the mud while the priest droned — *never again, never again up there, never again a pencil in my hand* — and how oaths made in grief are like lines drawn in sand, and rivers run through everything eventually.

"If I come," he said, and stopped. Cleared his throat. Started again, slower, because some sentences must be surveyed before they're crossed. "If I come, we do it my way in the field. My projections, my datum. You run the engineering, I run the mapping, and nobody overrides anybody. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"And when it's done — whatever we find, whoever sent Delgado up there, whatever the government thinks it's buying — the map comes home with us. Both of us. Together. No more of this—" he gestured vaguely, at the six years, at the letters unanswered, at the whole geography of their estrangement, "—this distance. I'm done drawing distances, Ada. I falsified enough of them."

Her eyebrows moved, a fraction. *Falsified.* The word hung there, and she was too good a surveyor not to fix on it, and he saw her file it away in that relentless ledger she kept behind her eyes, the entry to be reconciled later, on the ice, in the dark, at the worst possible hour. But she only nodded and held out her hand, and he took it, and her grip was a wrench-grip, a grip that had tightened bolts on engines in the rain, and it hurt, and he was glad it hurt.

"I leave tomorrow," she said. "First boat upriver. There's a mule driver named Fuentes who knows the lower trail."

"I know Fuentes. He waters his stock at my expense." Tomás released her hand. He picked up the aguardiente, considered it, and poured it slowly onto the floorboards, watching the amber spread and soak into wood that had swallowed a thousand such libations. Then he set the empty glass upside down on the table, next to their father's map, like a marker on a closed line. "Tomorrow, then."

Ada stood. At the foot of the stairs she paused, her back to him, rain still dripping from her coat, and for a moment Tomás saw her not as the engineer, not as the executor of their father's ghost, but as his little sister, eight years old, standing in the doorway of the survey shed asking to be taught the letters, and he felt the longing hit him sideways like a slide on a scree slope — sudden, total, carrying everything loose in him with it.

"Tomás. Why did you say yes?"

He looked at the map. At the annotation. *Keep it moving.* At the wave over the line.

"Because he asked me to," he said. "In his way. He's been asking for six years."

She held his gaze a moment longer — searching, cataloguing, unconvinced — and then she pushed open the door of El Farol and went out into the rain, and Tomás Quintana climbed the stairs to his room, took down the license that hung face-to-the-wall, and set it on the table facing outward, and opened the trunk for the first time in six years.

The oilcloth smelled of camphor and old winters. Under the alidade, folded twice, was a letter he had never sent — addressed to Ada, dated the month after the funeral, four pages in his smallest hand. He had written it the night he learned the truth, and he had never had the courage to post it, and now it would travel north with him into the cordillera like unexploded ordnance in his pack.

He weighed it in his palm. Outside, the freight yard groaned; a train was moving at last, heading north, into the mountains, into the weather.

Two days, he thought. Two days before she learns what I know. Maybe the river will be merciful. The river had never once been merciful to a Quintana, but a man could hope, the way a man could hope for a dry crossing, right up until the water took the bridge.

He packed the letter with the instruments, blew out the lamp, and lay down to listen to the rain, which sounded, all night long, like a river deciding.
