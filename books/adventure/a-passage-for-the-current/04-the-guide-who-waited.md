## 4. The Guide Who Waited

The river told Río Salazar they were coming two days before they arrived.

He had learned its language the way other men learned scripture — slowly, at his mother's knee, then all at once in the year his father drowned and the water became the only parent left who spoke. The Río Blanco did not gossip. It kept its counsel in gravel and cold. But it changed its voice when strangers walked its banks, a new hollowness in the riffles, a stutter where the current should have run clean, and for two mornings Río had stood on the flat rock below his house with his coffee going cold in his hands, listening to the water say: *someone is walking upstream, and they are not from here.*

He knew who it was before he saw her. He had known since the letter came, three weeks ago, forwarded twice and smelling of the border towns it had passed through — Ada Quintana's handwriting, narrower than it used to be, the loops strangled the way handwriting gets when a person has spent years writing things she does not believe. *Río. I need a guide up the Blanco to the high country. I need one who will not ask me why. I will pay what the government pays. I know what the government pays. Come anyway. — A.*

He had read it eleven times. On the twelfth reading he finally understood that she had written *come anyway* the way a person throws a rope across a gap — not expecting it to hold, only needing to have tried.

So when she came around the bend below his house on the fourth morning, he was waiting on the flat rock, and he did not stand, and he let her do the walking while he did the watching.

She had her father's shoulders. That was the first thing, and it went through him like a fishhook. Old Quintana had carried himself with that same forward lean, that same economy, as if the body were a tool kept sharp for the work it was made for. Ada wore a canvas jacket gone grey at the elbows and a pack that sat on her like it belonged there, and her hair was tied back with what he recognized — and this was worse, this was the hook setting deep — as one of her father's old surveyor's flags, the orange cloth faded to the color of a healing wound.

Behind her came a man Río did not know at first, and then did, all at once, the way you recognize a scar. Tomás. Thinner. Softer in the middle in the way of men who had stopped walking anywhere on purpose. His eyes went everywhere except at Río, which told Río everything about what Tomás had been doing with his years, and what he thought Río might say about it.

"You got my letter," Ada said. Not a question. Her voice was lower than he remembered, sanded down.

"I got your letter." He stood then, finally, because there were limits to what a man could make a woman do on flat ground. "You're two days behind the pace you'd need."

"Then we're two days behind."

"The river's up." He nodded at the water, brown-green and muscular with the last of the melt. "It'll drop in four, maybe five. After that the fords are honest. Before that they'll lie to you."

"You always talked like that."

"You always listened like that. You've gotten worse at it."

Something moved behind her eyes — not anger. Recognition, maybe, of an old argument she had thought was buried and found still breathing. "Río. I don't have time for fords. I need to be at the treeline in nine days."

He looked at her for a long moment. The river ran loud beneath them both, saying what it always said, which was nothing and everything.

"Then you need to hear what I'm about to tell you," he said, "and you need to believe it the first time, because I won't enjoy saying it twice."

* * *

He fed them first. This was not kindness; it was strategy. He had watched too many expeditions die in the high country with full bellies on the first day and empty heads on the last, and he wanted their heads full when he told them what the mountain would demand. He made trout in a pan with wild onions and the last of his flour fried hard, and Tomás ate like a man who had forgotten food was anything but fuel, and Ada ate slowly, looking around the single room of his house — at the coiled ropes, the brass instruments on their shelf, the photograph of his father and hers standing hip-deep in the Blanco in 1971, young men laughing at something the camera couldn't hear.

She stopped at the photograph. He let her.

"You kept this," she said.

"I kept everything of his I could carry." He said it plainly, because there was no other way to say it. "Your father was the only man I ever guided twice for free, and the only man I ever guided three times at all."

"He paid you."

"He paid me what I asked. I asked for nothing the third time." He turned the flatbread on the iron. "He was going to show me the pass, Ada. That was the arrangement. He'd found something up there — he wouldn't say what, only that it was worth the walk. He said, *when I come down, I'll show you where the mountain keeps its door.*" The pan hissed. He watched it instead of her. "He never came down."

The silence that followed had weight to it, the kind of silence that has been sitting in a room for eleven years, patient, waiting for someone strong enough to lift it. Ada set the photograph down with both hands, carefully, the way you set down something that might still be alive.

"That's why I'm here," she said. "The Delgado team. The government wants them found."

"The government," Río said, "wants a story it can file."

"You don't know that."

"I know that your father worked for the government his whole life, and when the government needed him to have died in an accident, he died in an accident. I know that the men who came asking me about his last route wore boots that had never seen mud." He looked up at her then, finally, and held her eyes the way you hold a live wire. "I'll take you upriver. I'll take you to the treeline and past it if the season allows. But I need you to hear the terms, and the terms are the mountain's, not mine, and I have never once negotiated them down."

* * *

He took them out to the woodshed after, where the map hung.

It was not a map anyone official had ever drawn. It was his own — forty years of the Blanco and its tributaries inked on stitched hides and butcher paper, corrected, annotated, cursed at, loved. He watched Tomás look at it and saw the cartographer wake up in the man's ruined face, the way a lamp comes on in a window you'd thought long dark. Tomás drifted along it like a man reading letters from the dead.

"The high route," Río said, and put his finger on the glacier — the Viento, the white spine of the range, the thing that had killed his father and nearly killed him and had taken Old Quintana and, if the stories were true, seven men of the Delgado team. "Everyone wants to go this way because it's short. It is short. It is also a door that opens maybe six days a year."

"Calving," Ada said.

"Calving." He said the word and felt the cold of it in his teeth, the way you feel it every time, memory living in the jaw. "The glacier hangs over the corridor like a wall that's deciding whether to fall. Most of the year it's fused — the ice bridges the whole throat of the pass, and there is no way through, not for a man, not for a spider. But in the deep warm of the season, the face lets go. It calves. Chunks the size of churches come off that wall, and when they come, they take the ice bridge with them, and for a few days — I have seen it as few as three, never more than nine — there is a corridor. Open water, open scree, open sky. And then the ice moves back in and the door shuts for another year."

"And the window?"

"Opens with the first real thaw. This year the thaw is early — the river told me that too, it's been running high and angry since the equinox. My guess is the calving starts in eleven days. Maybe twelve." He turned to face her. "Your nine days to the treeline is fine. The treeline is easy. It's the four days above the treeline that will eat your margin alive, and if you reach the glacier face on day thirteen and the window opened on day eleven and is already closing—" He shook his head. "The mountain does not wait. I have buried men who thought it would."

Ada was quiet. He watched her do the arithmetic — he could see it moving behind her face, the engineer's mind, the same mind her father had, the mind that loved a schedule because a schedule was a promise you could keep. And he watched her arrive, as he knew she would, at the truth underneath the numbers.

"Then we leave tomorrow," she said. "Before the fords are honest."

"The fords will lie to you."

"Then I'll ask them nicely."

Despite everything — despite the eleven years, despite the letter, despite the photograph on the wall — Río laughed. It came out of him rough, like something dislodged. Ada looked at him with an expression he remembered from a summer when they were both young and the river was low and everything was still possible, and then the expression closed, the way doors close in houses where someone has died.

"I'll take you," he said. "On my terms. We move when I say we move. We camp where I say we camp. If I say turn around, we turn around, and you can hate me the whole way down but you'll be alive to do it. And—" he hesitated, and hated the hesitation, "—you tell me the truth about what happened to your father's map, and about who else is looking for whatever Delgado found. Because someone is. The river's been telling me that for a week, and the river doesn't lie about strangers."

He saw it — the flicker, the half-second where she decided how much of herself to spend. He had watched her do it her whole life. Even at nineteen, even in the summer that he had loved her with the whole dumb open heart of a young guide and she had loved him back in the way of someone already halfway out the door, she had rationed herself like a woman crossing a desert.

"There are men on the mountain," she said finally. "Maybe. We saw sign at the last camp — boot prints that weren't surveyors'. I'll tell you what I know when I know it's true."

"Ada—"

"That's the truth I have, Río. It's not much, but it's all of it, and I'm not going to dress it up to make it easier to hand over." Her chin came up, and there she was — not the girl, not the ghost of her father, but Ada, the actual woman, stubborn as bedrock. "Come with me or don't. But don't ask me to be less careful with the truth than you'd be with a rope."

He held her gaze. Outside, the river ran, high and early and angry, saying its one long sentence.

"I'll come," he said. "Not for the government. Not for Delgado." He picked up the photograph of the two young men in the water, laughing, and set it face-down on the shelf, gently, the way you close a book you can't finish. "Your father promised to show me a door. I've spent eleven years wondering what was behind it. Whatever's up there — whatever your consortium and your missing surveyors and your closed lines are all circling — I want to stand in front of it once before the ice takes it back."

Ada nodded. It was not gratitude, exactly. It was something older and more tired than that, an agreement between two people who had both been standing at the same grave for a decade, facing opposite directions.

They left at first light. The fords lied to them, exactly as he'd promised — the second one took the mule to its chest and Ada's breath with it, and he pulled her horse's bridle across the current with one hand while she fought the water with both, and afterward she didn't thank him and he didn't want her to, because between them thanks had always been a currency neither could afford.

They made the treeline in seven days, not nine, moving hard through country that got more beautiful and less forgiving with every mile — the river narrowing to a green throat, the peaks coming up around them like witnesses. On the seventh evening they camped in the last of the stunted lenga trees, and Río stood apart from the fire and looked up at the glacier's white shoulder catching the last light, and did the arithmetic he had been refusing to do since the letter.

The thaw was early. The calving would start soon — two days, three. And they were not the only ones climbing.

He had seen them that afternoon, far across the valley: a line of men moving with the wrong discipline, the wrong silence, no smoke where smoke should be. He said nothing. Not yet. Tomorrow he would tell Ada what he'd seen, and watch her face, and learn from it whether the danger behind them was worse than the ice ahead.

Above the treeline, in the blue dark, the glacier groaned — a long, deep sound, like the mountain clearing its throat before it spoke.
