## 5. What the River Keeps

The river had a voice, and by the third day I had learned its grammar.

Río Salazar had taught me to listen the first morning out of the last known camp, standing on the gravel bar with his head cocked like a heron's, one hand raised to still us. *The water tells you what's coming,* he said. *A shallow run chatters. A deep pool goes quiet. When the voice changes without a reason, something changed the riverbed — and up here, that something is usually stone, or ice, or weather.* He said it the way a man repeats a lesson he gave once before, years ago, to a girl who hadn't listened then. I had been that girl. I was trying to be someone else now.

So I listened. The Río Blanco — my father had named it in his map margins, *Blanco, for the milk of the glacier at its source* — spoke in a low continuous syllable as we walked its northern bank, and I learned to hear the pauses in it. The wide bends went soft and murmurous. The gorges roared. And twice on the second day, the voice dropped without warning into a strange, hollow hush, and both times Río had already stopped walking, and both times we found the reason: fresh slides, raw scars of rock and mud on the far bank, the river's bed rearranged by forces we could not see.

"Mountains are talking to each other," Tomás said the second time, and there was something in his voice I didn't like — not fear exactly, but recognition. He had spent his disgrace in country like this, I reminded myself. He knew its moods. That was supposed to be a comfort. It wasn't.

We made our camps in the lee of the bank, below the treeline where the alerce still stood thick and dark, and every night I laid my father's map open on my knees inside the tent and read it by candle stub, the way other people read scripture. The northern lines ran up this valley like veins on the back of a hand. He had drawn them in 1931, in ink that had gone brown as old blood: the surveyed grade, the proposed tunnel stations, the little marginal notes in his cramped, beautiful hand. *Good ballast gravel, left bank.* *Water plentiful year-round.* *Pass negotiable — see profile, sheet 7.* He had believed in this route. He had died believing in it, or so the official record said — killed when his survey trestle failed in a spring flood, an accident, a tragedy, a closed file.

I had spent twenty years believing that file. I was beginning to understand how much a person can believe a thing simply because the alternative has no shape yet.

* * *

We found the notebook on the morning of the fourth day, wedged in the stones of a rapids where the river pinched between two granite shoulders and ran white and furious.

Tomás saw it first. He had gone down to fill the water skins — he did small chores without being asked now, a penance he performed silently, and I let him — and I heard him stop, and then I heard him call my name in a voice stripped of everything casual.

The notebook was caught in a cleft of rock at the river's edge, wedged so precisely it might have been placed there, though the water had clearly worked at it: the leather cover was swollen and dark, the pages fanned like the gills of a fish. Tomás pried it loose with his knife and held it dripping in both hands, and for a moment none of us touched it, the way you don't touch a thing that has been waiting for you.

"Delgado," Río said quietly, reading the name stamped into the cover before the water had eaten it. "Emiliano Delgado. Chief of the government survey."

The government survey. The four men and one woman who had walked into this valley in late summer and never walked out. The reason a ministry clerk in Puerto Frío had put a contract in front of me with a sum of money on it that was either generous or insulting, depending on how you priced a life.

We carried it up to the fire and dried it page by page, Tomás holding the leaves toward the flame with a care I had not seen in his hands since he was a boy mounting specimens in his first atlas. The early entries were dry as a ledger. Elevations. Bearings. Notes on grade and substrate in a surveyor's shorthand Tomás could read like a priest reads Latin — he translated aloud in a low voice, and I watched the professional in him surface through the drink-roughened skin, the old precision rising in him like a trout to a hatch.

"August ninth. *Established base camp at the confluence. Timber good, water good. Morale excellent. Vargas sings badly but constantly.*" Tomás almost smiled. Then he turned the page and stopped smiling.

The handwriting began to change.

It was gradual at first — the neat surveyor's hand loosening, the letters leaning as if pushed by wind. The entries grew shorter. Then they grew stranger.

"*August 14. Men on the ridge. Not ours.*"

"*August 17. Flagged a line we did not flag. Orange paint, no stencil. Vargas says hunters. Hunters do not run instrument lines.*"

"*August 19. Found one of our cairns dismantled and rebuilt wrong. Deliberately wrong. Someone is correcting our map.*"

I felt the hair rise on my arms, though the fire was warm. Río had gone very still, the way he went still when he heard a change in the river's voice. "Someone didn't want their survey to be accurate," he said.

"Someone wanted it to be *their* survey," Tomás said. "Or wanted there to be no survey at all." He turned another page, and the paper crackled, still damp at the spine. "*August 21. Consorcio Andrade. The name is on a crate at the abandoned mining camp below the moraine. We went in. The tunnel was blown from the inside — charges set to collapse it, not to open it. Andrade isn't mining this valley. Andrade is making sure no one else ever can.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere across the river, a branch cracked — a deer, or the cold settling in the timber, or nothing. All three of us looked up at the same moment, and all three of us looked away again, and nobody said what we were all thinking.

Delgado had found it. Whatever my father had found in 1931 — whatever had killed him, if it had not been the river — Delgado had found it too, twenty years later, and had written it down in a hand that grew more frantic with every page. The last entries were nearly illegible, the pen pressed so hard it had torn the paper in places.

"*August 23. They know we know. Moving at night now.*"

"*August 24. Sent Ruiz and Chávez downriver with copies. If we don't come out, the copies will.*"

"*August 25. No fire tonight.*"

And then nothing. The notebook ended on the twenty-fifth of August with the words *no fire tonight*, and beneath them, a final line in a different, hurried script — someone else's hand, pressed in haste:

"*They are in the valley.*"

* * *

We did not speak for a long time. The river spoke for us, running its white argument over the stones below the camp.

"Ruiz and Chávez," I said at last. "The copies. Did they make it out?"

"The ministry would have told you," Tomás said. "If copies had reached Puerto Frío, there'd have been an inquiry, not a missing-persons contract."

"Then they didn't make it out." I looked north, up the valley, where the river came down out of a haze of cold air and the mountains stood in ranks like a jury. "And neither did Delgado. And whoever took the copies knew about the notebook — knew it existed, knew it was evidence — and let the river have it rather than be seen searching for it. That's why it's still here. They couldn't be caught looking."

"Or they never found it," Río said. "The river keeps what it keeps. But Ada's right about the rest." He fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "There were six people in that survey party. There is a company in this valley that makes surveys disappear. And we are four people with a map, walking the same river at the same speed they walked it."

"Slower," Tomás said. "We have a mule."

"We have a mule," Río agreed, and for a moment the absurdity of it hung there — the mule, Sombra, dozing under an alerce with her ears at half-mast, oblivious to the weight of the conversation happening near her — and then the moment passed and the cold came back into it.

I opened my father's map and laid it beside the notebook, and the two documents looked at each other across the canvas of my knees. My father's ink from 1931. Delgado's frantic hand from last summer. Twenty years apart, and the same valley, the same pass, the same silence closing over both of them like water over a stone. My father's marginal note — *pass negotiable* — had been the last confident thing written about this route. Everything after it had been erasure.

"He refused them," I said. It came out of me before I knew I was going to say it, a thing I had carried so long it had worn smooth, like a stone in the pocket. "My father. Whatever Andrade offered him — and they would have offered, a route like this is worth a fortune — he turned it in. Complete survey, honest grades, the pass documented for the nation. That's why the file says accident. That's why I've never quite believed the file."

Tomás's face did something complicated and then went carefully blank, and I was too full of the notebook to read it properly. Later I would remember that blankness. Later it would mean everything. That night I only saw my brother, tired and sober for five days running, looking at the fire as if it were a document he was trying to decipher.

"Then we finish what they started," he said finally. "Delgado wrote it down so it would survive him. We're what survived him."

* * *

We broke camp before dawn the next day, because Río said the light on the water would carry our shapes to the far bank if we moved in daylight, and because none of us wanted to admit aloud that we were now moving like hunted things.

The valley narrowed as we went north. The alerce gave way to ñire scrub, the scrub to stone, and the river's voice deepened and multiplied until it seemed to come from everywhere, from under the earth itself. My father's map showed a survey camp at the mouth of the second gorge — *good shelter, overhang, dry* — and we aimed for it, and found it, and it was the overhang he had promised, a slab of granite leaning over a shelf of gravel above the high-water line. Someone had camped there before us. The ashes in the fire ring were old, a season old at least, and scattered among them Tomás found a boot heel, military pattern, and a brass cartridge casing that he turned over and over in his fingers without saying anything at all.

We camped anyway. There was nowhere else. But we built no fire — Delgado's last entry sat in all of our minds like a stone — and we ate cold food in the blue dusk, and Río set watches in pairs, and we lay in our bedrolls listening to the river and to the dark above it.

I took the middle watch with Río, as I had the night before, because the watches had arranged themselves that way and neither of us had remarked on it. We sat with our backs against the granite and our breath smoking, and for a long time neither of us spoke. Below us the river ran black and silver, and beyond it the far wall of the valley rose in terraces of shadow, and above everything the stars were hard and close as nailheads.

"Your father sang," Río said, out of the dark. "On the trail. Badly. Constantly. Like Vargas, according to his notebook." He nodded at the notebook, which lay wrapped in oilcloth against my chest. "He sang the same three songs, and one of them wasn't even a song, it was just the names of rivers. He'd sing the rivers he'd surveyed, in order, like a rosary."

"I didn't know that."

"There's a lot you don't know about him. That isn't an accusation. It's just the shape of things — he kept his two worlds apart, the valley and the family, and neither one ever got the whole of him." He turned his head, and in the starlight his face was all planes and patience. "I'm telling you because you're walking his route and carrying his map, and you should know he was afraid up here too. He sang to cover it. He was the bravest man I ever guided and he sang the river rosary every night above the treeline."

The words went into me slowly, like warmth into frozen ground. I had built my father out of ink and memory — the marginal notes, the beautiful hand, the closed file — and I had made him into a monument, and monuments don't sing badly. "Thank you," I said, and meant it in a way that had nothing to do with manners.

We sat on. The river ran. And then Río's hand closed on my arm — not hard, but absolutely, the grip of a man who had spent his life reading water and had just heard it lie.

"Downstream," he breathed. "Listen."

I listened. The river's voice came up the gorge as it always did, the long white syllable of it — and inside the syllable, faint and wrong, something that did not belong. A rhythm. A regular, metallic knock, like stone on stone, like a boot heel on rock, spaced and deliberate. Not an animal. Not water. Not wind.

*One. Two. Three.* A pause. *One. Two. Three.*

"Signal," Río whispered. "Carried up the gorge. Sound travels strange in these narrows — they could be a kilometer off, or two, bouncing it off the walls." He was already moving, waking Tomás with a touch, gathering the map and the notebook and the food with hands that made no sound at all. "We're leaving. Now, in the dark, by the river. They'll expect us to run from the sound — to break north or climb. We do the opposite. We go *toward* it, along the water, where the noise covers us. The river that kept Delgado's notebook will keep us too."

Tomás was up and saddling Sombra in the dark with a competence that surprised us both. "How far?" he whispered.

"Close enough to signal. Far enough to not have come in the dark. They'll move at first light." Río looked at me, and in his eyes there was no tenderness now, only the cold bright attention of a man in country he respected and did not trust. "They know we're here, Ada. They've known since the rapids, I'd wager — since the notebook. Nobody lets evidence sit in a river unless they're watching who comes to take it out."

I wrapped my father's map against my chest, over the notebook, over the two dead men's words, and looked up the gorge to where the signal was still knocking its patient three-count off the stone. *One. Two. Three.* Somewhere in that dark, men who made surveys disappear were talking to each other about us.

"Then we give them a river to watch," I said, and stepped down into the noise of the water, and the current took the sound of us and carried it away north.
