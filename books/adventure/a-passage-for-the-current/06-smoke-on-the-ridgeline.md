## 6. Smoke on the Ridgeline

The smoke came at dusk, and it came wrong.

Tomás saw it first — not because his eyes were better than Ada's or Río's, but because he had spent three years teaching himself to read a landscape for the things that did not belong in it. That had been his trade, once, before the trade became a shame: the falsified contour, the river bent a degree eastward on paper, the seam of ore erased from a map so that no one would ever come looking. He had learned that a lie drawn carefully enough will hold for years, but the world itself always tells the truth eventually — in the angle of light on a ridgeline, in the behavior of birds, in the color of smoke.

Campfire smoke should rise thin and blue-gray, ragged at the top, dissolving into the evening air the way water dissolves into sand. This smoke rose in a column. It was dense, dark, deliberate — the smoke of green wood burned hot and fast, or of a stove fed by men who wanted heat without light and had calculated exactly how much smoke they could afford to show. It stood against the last copper light on the western ridge like a signature on a document.

"Río," Tomás said, and his voice came out lower than he intended, as though the smoke could hear him across the valley.

Río was already looking. He had a way of going still that Tomás had noticed on the first day out — not the stillness of a man listening, but of a man who had grown up in a country where stillness was survival. He crouched now beside the cold ashes of their own fire, which they had buried at dawn as Río always insisted, and he watched the far ridge with his head tilted slightly, the way he watched water.

"One fire," Río said. "Maybe two, close together. They're banking it for the night, not cooking. Cooking smoke comes in pulses." He glanced at Ada, who stood with her hand on the pannier where Delgado's notebook rode wrapped in oilcloth. "Cooking smoke is hungry smoke. That's disciplined smoke."

"How far?" Ada asked.

"Four, five kilometers east of us. Parallel. They've been holding our line since at least this morning — I thought I heard a mule bell at the second bend, but I told myself it was ice shifting in the gorge." His jaw tightened. "I shouldn't have told myself that."

Tomás said nothing. He was looking at the smoke and feeling something move in his chest that he recognized the way you recognize a face across a crowded plaza — with a lurch, with the certainty that you know it, and with the immediate wish that you didn't.

* * *

They did not light a fire that night. They ate cold beans and hard bread in the blue dark under a leaning granite overhang, and Río took first watch, and Ada sat with the notebook open on her knees even though the light was gone, as if she could read it by will alone. Tomás lay on his bedroll with his back to the rock and watched the eastern ridge, where the smoke had long since faded but where, now that he knew to look, other small wrongnesses declared themselves.

A light, moving laterally. Then gone. Then two lights, closer together, moving the same direction — the same direction they themselves had traveled all day, north along the river, toward the high country.

Lanterns, or the filtered glow of them through tent canvas. A camp being struck or shifted, quietly, at night, in country where no one had any honest reason to be.

He thought: *they're pacing us.* And then, because his mind was the kind of mind it was — the cartographer's mind, the mind that had once been paid to make the world say things it didn't mean — he thought: *no. They're not pacing us. They're herding. A pace that matches is a survey line. They're running their own line parallel to ours, and when the two lines converge—*

He made himself stop. He rolled onto his side. Sleep did not come, but something worse than sleeplessness came, which was memory, arriving on its own schedule the way weather does, indifferent to what you had planned for the day.

* * *

It had been six years ago, in a drafting office in the lowlands that smelled of ink and cigarette smoke and, underneath, faintly, of the river — the office sat above a chandlery on the waterfront, and the smell of the water came up through the floorboards like a rumor. Tomás had been twenty-six and brilliant and broke, and his license was still three years from its disgrace, and a man named Señor Ferrán had come to him with a commission that was dressed, at the time, in perfectly respectable clothes.

Ferrán had been soft-spoken and precise, with clean fingernails and a way of placing documents on the table squared to its edges, and he had represented — so the letterhead said — a land and minerals concern called Consorcio Andrade. The work was simple, Ferrán said. A set of field surveys from the Cordillera del Viento needed to be redrawn for a client's internal use. The original surveyor had been careless. Elevations were off. Drainage basins were mislabeled. The client needed clean copies, and Tomás Quintana had the cleanest hand in the province.

Tomás had redrawn them. It had taken him eleven days. He had been paid in cash, in an envelope, and the money had been beautiful, and he had told himself a story about it the way he would later tell himself stories about the second commission and the third — that he was a technician, not an author; that the map was only paper; that the mountains did not care what men wrote about them.

He had known, even then, in the place where a man keeps the knowledge he cannot afford to look at directly, that the "errors" in the original surveys were not errors. He had known it because the errors all ran in the same direction. Every stream that fed the Río Blanco had been drawn a hundred meters too far west. Every elevation along the northern wall of the valley had been shaved down, softened, made unremarkable. And one entire feature — a notch in the high ridge, a saddle that the original surveyor had marked with three careful crosses and the word *paso* — had simply been omitted. Not redrawn. Omitted. As if a pass that could take a road, a rail line, a fortune in freight across the spine of the range had never existed at all.

Someone had paid him to make a door disappear from the face of the earth. And he had done it, and he had taken the money, and the envelope had been beautiful.

He learned later — much later, when the scandal came, when his license was revoked, when the word *falsificación* followed him into every border town saloon like a dog — that the original surveyor whose careless work he had been hired to correct was a man named Quintana. His own father. The commission had not been an accident. They had chosen him precisely because of whose lines he was erasing, because a son's hand over a father's map was the one forgery no auditor would ever think to question.

He had never told Ada. He had never told anyone. It was the kind of knowledge that, once spoken, would require him to become a different person, and he had not been able to afford the exchange.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and cold, and with it came the survey marks.

They found the first one an hour after breaking camp, on a boulder at the edge of the river terrace: a chiseled symbol the size of a coin, fresh enough that the rock dust still sat in the grooves unweathered. A triangle with a bar through its base, and beneath it a small numeral.

Tomás stopped walking so abruptly that Río, on the rope behind him, had to check his stride.

"What is it?" Ada said.

He didn't answer at once. He crouched, and his fingers went to the mark without his quite deciding to touch it, the way a man's hand goes to an old scar.

"Triangle and bar," Río said, reading it over his shoulder. "Boundary mark? Mine claim?"

"It's a datum," Tomás said. His voice sounded strange to him, flattened, like a voice heard through a wall. "A vertical control point. It means someone is running a proper instrument survey — theodolite, stadia rod, the whole liturgy. Not prospectors. Prospectors scratch and dig. This is—" He made himself say it. "This is Consorcio Andrade's mark. Triangle and bar. I'd know it anywhere. I drew the maps that went with these marks for two years."

Ada had gone very still. "You *what*?"

The word was out and he could not take it back, and he stood there with the river running loud beside them and his sister looking at him with an expression he had spent six years dreading — not anger, not yet, but the terrible patience of someone rearranging everything they knew about you to make room for a new and uglier fact.

"Not now," he said. "Please. Not here, not with them—" he tipped his head toward the eastern ridge, "—four hours behind us. I'll tell you all of it, I swear it on Papa's name, but if I tell you now we'll stand here until dark, and they'll close the gap, and—"

"How long have you carried it?" Ada said quietly.

"Six years."

Something moved behind her eyes, and was put away, deliberately, like a document squared to the edge of a table. He knew that gesture. He had invented it. "Then it will keep six more hours," she said, and turned and walked on, and the fact that she turned and walked on was worse than any blow, because it meant she had decided he was still useful, and being useful to Ada while she decided what you were worth was the coldest place Tomás had ever stood.

* * *

They saw the column itself at midday.

The river bent west around a shoulder of rock, and the trail climbed a moraine spine with open ground on both sides, and from the crest of that spine the whole eastern valley lay exposed below them — a long trough of gray scree and stunted ñire scrub, and crossing it, in single file, small with distance but unmistakable, eleven men and a mule train.

They flew no flag. That was the thing Tomás's eye seized on first and could not release. Every expedition he had ever seen or read about — government, academic, commercial, even the poachers and the gold-mad — flew something. A company pennant, a national color, a university crest, a red rag on a stick. Men in the wilderness wanted to belong to something; the flag was the smallest possible country you could carry. But these men flew nothing. Their gear was uniform and dark. Their mules moved at an even, uncomplaining pace, which meant good hands and good feed and a long budget. And they were not following the river the way a traveler follows a river — loosely, choosing the easy bank, drifting. They were holding a line. A surveyed line. Tomás could see it in the way they moved, the way a draftsman can see a ruled edge under a freehand stroke: dead straight, corrected at intervals, as though an invisible thread ran across the whole valley and they were beads strung on it.

And the last two men in the file were not walking like porters. They walked with their weight back, heads up, hands free, and even at this distance Tomás could see the long shapes slung across their shoulders and know them for what they were.

"Rifles," Río said. He had unfolded the field glasses, and his voice had the flat calm of deep water. "Two, maybe three. The rest are crew. They're not hunting us, Ada. Not yet. You don't bring a survey crew and a cook to a killing."

"Then what are they doing?"

"Redoing it," Tomás said.

They both looked at him.

"They're re-surveying the pass," he said, and the words came faster now, the way a slide starts slowly and then is simply falling. "That's what the datum marks are. That's why they're holding a line parallel to ours. They're not chasing Delgado's team — they're *replacing* them. Delgado found something the consortium paid to have erased, and now the consortium is up here making a new set of maps, their own maps, with the seam buried and the pass closed, so that when the government asks what happened to its survey team, there's a clean paper record waiting. A record that says there was never anything here worth a man's life." He heard his own voice shake. "I know because it's the same job I did for them from a desk. They're doing it in the field now. They're doing it *properly*."

The wind came up the moraine and flattened the scrub and carried the smell of ice off the high country, and for a long moment none of them spoke. Below them the file of men went on walking their invisible thread, patient as ink drying.

Ada was looking at the column through the glasses, and her face, when she lowered them, had changed. It was not fear. Tomás knew his sister's fear; he had watched it all their childhood, at their father's departures, at the telegrams. This was something older and harder. This was the face she had worn at the funeral, standing at the graveside in the rain, sixteen years old and refusing an umbrella.

"They knew Delgado was here," she said. "Before we did. Before the government did. They've known all along, and they've been up here cleaning behind them." She looked east, at the empty ridge where the smoke had been, and then north, up the valley, toward the white wall of the high country that was even now, somewhere above the clouds, holding a glacier and a window of days. "And they know we're here too."

"They've known since the smoke last night," Río said. "Probably since the second bend. They're not hurrying." He folded the glasses with a small, terrible precision. "Men who aren't hurrying are men who already know where you're going. There's only one route north past the gorge. They don't need to catch us. They just need to be standing where the valley narrows when we get there."

"Then we don't take the valley," Ada said.

"The valley is the only way," Río said. "Until the calving window opens the high route. And the window—"

"Opens when it opens," Ada said. "So we move faster than their survey line. We move tonight. We move in the dark."

"Ada—" Tomás began.

"Six years, Tomás." She was already tightening the straps on her pack, not looking at him, her hands quick and hard. "You carried it six years, and I carried Papa's map twenty, and Delgado's people carried whatever they carried right up until they vanished off the face of the earth, and those men down there have been carrying it longest of all. Nobody in this valley is putting their burden down today. So we walk." She swung the pack up onto her shoulders. "And tonight, by whatever light we can spare, you're going to tell me exactly what you drew for them, and where, and what it erased. Because if I'm going to outrun the men you used to work for, I need to know what they know — and you're the only map of them I have."

She turned north, into the wind, and started down the moraine, and Río fell in behind her without a word, the way a man falls in behind a decision already made.

Tomás stood one moment longer on the spine, looking down at the file of men moving along their invisible thread, at the mules, at the rifles carried with the easy care of tools rather than weapons, at the whole patient machinery of erasure that he had once, with the cleanest hand in the province, served from a desk above the smell of the river.

Then the wind shifted, and from far up the eastern ridge, faint but unmistakable, came a sound he had heard once before, six years ago, in an office doorway, as Ferrán squared his papers to the edge of the table and smiled: a whistle. Two notes, low, then high.

A signal. Passed hand to hand along the column below, man to man, until it died in the wind.

They had seen them. And they had stopped hurrying altogether.
