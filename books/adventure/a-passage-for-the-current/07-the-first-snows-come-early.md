## 7. The First Snows Come Early

The mountain told me first.

Not the sky — the sky had been lying all week, holding its blue like a man holds a smile he doesn't mean. It was the river that spoke the truth, the way it always does if you've lived beside one long enough to learn its grammar. For three days the water had been running low and clear, glacial milk thinned to almost nothing, and on the morning we broke camp above the last estancia it came up loud and gray-green, thick with fresh-ground flour from a glacier I couldn't yet see. Somewhere above us, in country none of us had entered, the ice was being chewed open by weather that hadn't reached us yet but was coming down the valley like a debt collector.

I stood at the bank with my hands in the cold spray and let the river tell me what it knew, and what it knew was this: winter had already started without us.

"Río." Ada's voice behind me, level as always, though I'd learned by now — learned again, after all these years — to hear the wire under the level. "You've been staring at the water since before light."

"It's dirty," I said. "Glacier flour. New melt."

She came to stand beside me. She didn't ask what new melt meant. She was her father's daughter; she'd grown up on survey lines and snowpack tables the way other children grew up on lullabies. But she waited anyway, because she wanted to hear me say it, or maybe because she needed to hear someone say it out loud before she'd let herself believe it.

"The high passes are closing," I said. "The ones below us will be shut within four days. Five if we're lucky, and the mountain stopped being lucky for Quintanas a long time ago."

Behind us, Tomás dropped the pannier he'd been cinching and swore softly, a word their father would have boxed his ears for. The mules shifted in their pickets, breath steaming, sensing — animals always sense it first — the change in the air's weight, the iron smell that comes before snow.

"So we don't take the low passes," Ada said.

"No," I agreed. "We take the glacier."

* * *

We spent that morning doing arithmetic, because that is what you do when fear arrives: you make it sit at the table and count with you until it becomes something useful. Fear that won't count is just panic wearing boots.

I laid it out on the flat top of a supply crate while the wind began to test the tent lines. "The calving window opens when the summer melt undercuts the serac field at the head of the corridor. The ice lets go, the passage clears, and for a short time — days, not weeks, I told you, and I meant it — the high route stands open. After that, the corridor fills with debris and new ice, and nothing crosses until next season. Nothing human, anyway."

"And if we miss it?" Tomás asked. He'd been quiet all morning, which for Tomás was itself a kind of weather report. Since the notebook, since Delgado's handwriting had turned frantic in those last pages, he'd been carrying something besides his pack, and it sat on him heavier than canvas and rope ever could.

"If we miss it," I said, "we winter here. There are caves below the moraine. We could survive — people have. But we'd come down in spring with no provisions, no animals, and Vásquez's men knowing exactly where to find us. Or we go back the way we came, through passes that will be closed, and the mountain decides whether it wants to keep us." I looked at each of them. "Or we commit. Today. Tomorrow at the latest. We climb to the icefield and we're standing at the corridor mouth when the calving starts."

Ada was already repacking. That was the thing about her that had undone me at nineteen and undid me still: she never wasted a moment between decision and motion. Other people mourned their choices. Ada simply became them.

"How long to the icefield?" she asked.

"Two days hard climbing. Three if the weather argues."

"The weather will argue," Tomás said.

"The weather always argues," I said. "The trick is not answering."

* * *

We climbed through the afternoon into country that kept changing its mind about what it was. Forest gave way to krummholz, the trees bent and gnarled into shapes like old men arguing with the wind, and then the trees gave out entirely and there was only scree and sky and the thin whistle of air through stone. The light went strange and yellowish, the way it does when the sun is trying to burn through cloud that hasn't arrived yet but has been invited.

I led, because they were paying me to lead and because I needed to be in front where I could worry privately instead of infecting them. Behind me came the mule train — three animals now, the fourth having gone lame two days back and been given to the mountain with more ceremony than any of us felt. Ahead of the mules came Ada, walking with that surveyor's stride, reading the ground the way Tomás read maps and I read water. And behind her, Tomás, who complained about his feet every hour on the hour and never once actually slowed down.

By late afternoon the first flakes came. Not snow, not really — spindrift, the mountain shaking out its blankets, testing. They came sideways on a wind that had teeth in it, and they melted on contact with skin, and every one of them felt like a message.

I called a halt at a shelf of granite above a streambed, sheltered enough to pitch both tents, and while Tomás saw to the animals I walked the perimeter the way my grandfather taught me, looking up as much as around. In this country you check the slopes above camp or you wake up dead. The rock held. The wind did not.

At supper — cold oats and dried meat, because fuel was arithmetic too now — Ada spread her father's map between us, weighted with stones at the corners. I still couldn't look at it directly for too long. I had guided Emilio Quintana along these very contours eleven years ago, and I had come down alone, and there was not a contour line on that paper that didn't have a memory attached to it like a burr.

"The corridor mouth," Ada said, tapping a place where the map's ink went uncertain, her father's hand trailing off into guesswork. "How close did he get?"

"To here." I touched a point below the serac field. "He wanted to go farther. I wouldn't take him. The ice was moving wrong that year — groaning all night, like something turning over in its sleep." I paused. "He was angry with me for it. He didn't speak to me the whole way down."

Ada's eyes came up. "You never told me that."

"There wasn't much either of us told anyone, in those years." I folded my hands around my tin cup, feeling the heat bleed away. "I've thought since that if I had taken him up, he might have died on the ice instead of wherever he died. Or he might have found whatever he was looking for and come home safe, satisfied, an old man telling stories. You can't know. The mountain doesn't offer refunds on the roads not taken."

Tomás snorted, but there was no contempt in it. "That's the most honest thing anyone's said on this trip."

"Then it's downhill from here," I said, and Ada almost smiled, and for a moment the tent held something like warmth that had nothing to do with temperature.

* * *

The storm found us in the night.

It came down the valley the way I'd known it would, announced first by sound — a rising hum in the rocks, the stream below us going suddenly loud — and then by pressure, the tents snapping taut, and then by snow, real snow now, fat and wet and endless, falling so thick the lantern made only a dim coin of light two arm-spans wide.

I lay awake listening to it and doing the arithmetic again, because the numbers had changed. Two days to the icefield had become three, maybe four, in this. Every hour of storm was an hour subtracted from the calving window at the far end, which did not care about our delays and would open and close on its own schedule, indifferent as tide. And somewhere behind us — I could feel it the way you feel a hand approaching the back of your neck — Vásquez's party was making the same calculations, and they had more men, more food, and no reason to hurry except murder, which is the most patient of motives.

Near dawn the wind dropped as if cut, and I crawled out into a world erased. The scree was white. The krummholz was white. The sky was a lid of gray so low I felt I could press a palm against it. Everything soft-edged, silent, beautiful, and lying. Fresh snow like this is a promise the mountain makes right before it takes something from you.

"We move now," I said, kicking snow off the tent fly. "While it's calm. This is the eye of it or the end of it, and either way the trail won't be easier later."

* * *

It was the third day of the climb that the mountain took its price.

We had come up past the treeline into a world of gray stone and white ice, following a route I half-remembered from another lifetime and half-invented on the spot, because routes in this country are less paths than negotiations. The mules were failing. I could see it in their ears, in the way they placed their hooves with exaggerated care, conserving something they didn't have enough of. The lead animal — a dun mare named Soledad, because Tomás had named her and Tomás named things after loneliness — was carrying the bulk of the provisions, and she was tired in a way that goes past tiredness into something else.

The crossing was a slope of talus below a cliff band, maybe two hundred meters of broken rock fanged with ice, with a drop on the left side that went down and down into a gorge full of river-sound. I crossed it first, testing, and waved them across one at a time, spacing them out because that is the discipline, that is the whole religion of mountains: never put two lives on the same patch of uncertainty.

Soledad went second-to-last, loaded heavy, and she was halfway across when the slope moved.

It wasn't dramatic. That's the thing people who've never been in the high country don't understand — the mountain rarely announces its violence. A slab of frozen scree simply let go beneath her hindquarters, a patch no bigger than a door, and slid, and she went with it, sideways, scrambling, eyes rolling white, and the panniers swung and caught on a spur of rock and held for one impossible second while she kicked against empty air.

Tomás lunged for the rope. I shouted him back — one word, *no*, thrown like a stone — because I could see what he couldn't: the rock under his own feet was part of the same failing sheet, and his weight would have taken him down with her.

The panniers tore free. Soledad fell.

She fell perhaps forty meters, turning slowly, terribly, and landed on a shelf of ice in the gorge below, and did not get up. The sound she made — I will not write what sound she made. Some things belong to the mountain.

The provisions that survived the fall were scattered down the slope, torn sacks bleeding rice and flour into the snow, and half of everything was simply gone, down in the gorge with her, unrecoverable. We stood at the edge in the wind and looked at what remained, and the arithmetic redid itself in all our heads at once, and the answer was ugly.

"Half," Tomás said finally. His voice was scraped raw. "Half of everything. Rations, fuel, feed."

"Then we eat half as much and burn half as much and move twice as fast," Ada said.

I looked at her. Her face was pale and set, and there was grief in it — she'd liked that mule, fed her from her hand each night — but the grief was already being converted, already becoming fuel, the way her father's death had become fuel, the way everything in that woman's life got burned rather than buried. It was magnificent and it frightened me, because I have seen what happens to people who convert all their grief to fuel. Eventually the tank runs dry, and there's nothing underneath.

"No," I said. "We eat the same and carry the same and rest properly. Tired climbers make mistakes, and mistakes up here cost more than mules." I met her eyes. "Discipline isn't speed, Ada. Discipline is refusing to let the mountain rush you. It wants us panicked. Panic is how it collects."

For a long moment she stared at me, and I braced for the fury — I remembered her fury from twenty years ago, from a girl on a station platform — but what came instead was something quieter and harder. She nodded once, picked up the salvageable sacks, and began redistributing the load with the precision of a woman balancing a bridge.

* * *

That night we camped in the lee of a moraine, huddled closer than comfort allowed because warmth was now a shared resource. Above us, catching the last violet light, the icefield showed itself for the first time — a vast white shoulder of the world, crevassed blue at its edges, and beyond it, hidden still, the corridor and the serac field that would decide everything.

I heard it before the others did. A deep, distant concussion, rolling down from the heights like a door slamming in an enormous house. Then another. Then silence.

"What was that?" Tomás whispered.

"The glacier talking to itself," I said. "Calving has started early. Upstream of the corridor, the ice is letting go." I pulled my blanket tighter and looked at the two of them, firelit, frost-rimed, alive. "Which means the window may open sooner than I promised. Or the whole route may collapse before we reach it. The mountain has stopped keeping schedules."

Ada was watching the icefield with an expression I recognized from photographs of her father — the look of a person measuring themselves against something immense and refusing to blink. "Then we'd better be early," she said.

Above us, the mountain answered with thunder.
