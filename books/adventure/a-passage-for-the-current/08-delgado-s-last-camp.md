## 8. Delgado's Last Camp

We smelled the camp before we saw it.

Not smoke — the cold had eaten any smoke days ago — but the particular sourness of abandoned things, canvas gone to mildew, kerosene spilled and frozen into the scree, the sweet-rot ghost of food left to spoil at altitude where nothing spoils quickly and everything spoils strangely. Río stopped at the head of the moraine and lifted one hand, and we stopped with him, the four of us and the two surviving mules breathing plumes into the thin air, and for a long moment none of us said anything, because the camp said it first.

It sat in a shallow bowl below the glacier's snout, where Delgado's team had chosen well — sheltered from the wind that came screaming down the corridor, close to running water, on ground solid enough to pitch steel poles. They had been good surveyors. I could see that even from a hundred meters out, in the geometry of the thing: the tents aligned to true north, the instrument tripod still standing at the center of a cleared circle, the theodolite's brass housing catching the afternoon light like a small sun that had forgotten how to burn. They had been good surveyors, and they had been interrupted.

"Nobody's moved the tripod," Tomás said beside me, and his voice had gone flat the way it did when he was working hard not to feel something. "You don't leave a theodolite standing. Not in weather like this. It's the first thing you break down."

"Unless you didn't get the chance," Río said.

We came down into the bowl in a loose line, and the camp opened around us like a wound.

The tents were still up. Three of them, expedition-grade, the kind the ministry bought in lots and stamped with inventory numbers, and all three had been opened — not torn, not cut, but unfastened, deliberately, the flaps folded back the way you'd fold back a sheet to check on a sleeping child. Inside the nearest one, sleeping bags lay rolled and strapped. Mess kits hung from a ridge line in neat, patient rows. A pair of boots stood side by side at the tent's mouth, laces tucked in, the way my father had taught me to leave boots you expected to come back to.

Nobody had come back to them.

I stood in the doorway of that tent longer than I should have. The canvas moved around me in small, nervous breaths of wind. On the floor, half beneath a rolled sleeping bag, someone had left a tin cup with tea gone to ice in the bottom, and the ice had sublimated over the weeks until it looked like a small gray ghost of itself, a memory of tea, the idea of a morning that had never finished happening.

"They walked out," Tomás said from behind me. "Or were made to walk out."

"Made," Río said. He was crouched at the fire ring, turning over stones with his one good habit of touching everything and reading it. "No shell casings. No blood in the snow — and look, the snow's been undisturbed since it fell, three weeks at least, maybe four. If there had been shooting, the weather would have covered it by now, but there'd be other signs. Scorch. Bone. Vultures, if we were lower." He looked up at me, and his eyes were the color of the glacier lake below us, green-gray and holding things at the bottom. "This wasn't violence, Ada. This was erasure. There's a difference. Violence leaves a body. Erasure leaves a shape where a body should be."

I knew what he was doing. He was giving me the words for it because he knew I'd been circling them since the first camp, since the intact tents and the running instruments and the treeline with no prints beyond it. Since the moment I'd understood that we were not looking for the dead. We were looking for the disappeared, which is worse, because the disappeared keep a door open in you that never quite closes.

* * *

We spent the rest of the light going through the camp the way you sift ashes — carefully, respectfully, looking for what the fire missed.

The ransacking, when we found its traces, was surgical. Drawers opened and emptied and closed. Trunk lids set back down. The survey records — the field books, the level notes, the photographic plates — gone, every sheet of them, but the instruments left, the expensive brass and glass left standing in the open air like offerings. Whoever had come had wanted paper and nothing else. Paper is the only thing a mountain can't hold for you. Paper is the only thing that can travel down to a city and speak.

"They took the records," Tomás said. He'd found the records chest — a steel case, ministry-stenciled, pried open with something flat and patient — and he was kneeling over it the way he used to kneel over his own drafting table, before. "But Delgado was twenty years in the service. He knew what a records chest meant to men like the ones who'd come for it. He knew they'd take it."

"So he let them," I said.

"So he let them," Tomás said, "and he put the real record somewhere else."

We looked at each other across the emptied chest, and for one strange, suspended second it was like being young again, like being twelve and nine in our father's study with a puzzle of contour lines between us, two minds turning on the same axle. Then the moment closed, the way those moments had been closing for fifteen years, and Tomás looked away first.

It was Río who found the crevasse.

Not by searching — by listening. He had walked out beyond the tents to where the glacier's edge pushed its blue tongue against the moraine, and he stood there with his head tilted, the way he did when he was reading water under ice, and then he said, quietly, "Ada. Bring the lamp."

The crevasse mouth opened in the glacier's flank like a held breath — a slot no wider than a shoulder, running down into blue so deep and so clean it looked less like ice than like the idea of depth. Río went first, on the rope, and I followed, and the world above shrank to a ragged coin of sky while the cold rose up around us, the old cold, the cold that lives in glaciers the way memory lives in houses.

Ten meters down, on a shelf of ice where the crevasse widened into a chamber, a lantern hung from a piton.

It was unlit. But it was hung — deliberately, carefully, at the height of a man's hand — and beneath it, wrapped in a groundsheet and lashed with surveyor's cord, was a cache.

Río's frost-nipped fingers worked the knots. I held the lamp, and the light trembled a little, and I told my hands to be steady, because whatever this was, it had waited weeks in the dark for someone to come, and it deserved steady hands.

Inside the groundsheet: a canvas satchel. Inside the satchel: a leather notebook with a rubber band around it, gone brittle with cold. A pistol, unfired, its action frozen stiff. A tin of hardtack, a block of pemmican, a spirit stove with fuel enough for one man to live perhaps four days in this cold — and beside the food, wrapped in oilcloth with a care that told me everything before I unwrapped it, a packet the size of a prayer book.

And a note, written in pencil on the back of a level sheet, in handwriting that started strong and deteriorated line by line into a scrawl:

*If you are reading this, you came up the river, and you are either the ministry or you are brave or you are both, and I pray you are both. They took the rest on the fourteenth. Six of them, armed, no flag, led by a man who showed papers from Consorcio Andrade and called it a security escort. They marched my team down the corridor. I do not know to where. I hid because I am old and slow and I have seen this before, in the north, in '09, and I know what happens to witnesses who descend in the custody of security escorts. The records chest they took was decoy paper — my own hand, three nights of it, wrong elevations, wrong bearings. The true record is here. Take it down. Take it to the tribunals if they still exist. Take it to a newspaper if they do not. My name is Emilio Delgado and I am the last of my team and I am writing this by a light that is nearly out.*

There was no more. The pencil line ran off the edge of the sheet as if the hand had simply stopped being able.

I read it twice. The second time my lips moved, though no sound came out, because the air in the crevasse was too thin and too cold to waste on breath that wasn't work.

"Ada." Río's voice came to me from very far away and very close at once. "Ada, sit down before you fall down. There's a shelf. Sit."

I sat. The ice took my weight without complaint. Somewhere above us, Tomás was calling down, his voice bouncing off the blue walls and arriving broken — *what is it, what did you find, Ada* — and I could not answer yet, because I was unwrapping the oilcloth packet, and my hands were steady after all, steady the way they are on the worst days of an engineer's life, when the bridge is singing a note it shouldn't sing.

* * *

Photographs. Eleven of them, printed on field-stock paper, each one captioned in Delgado's careful hand with date, grid reference, and bearing. The cold had kept them perfectly. They might have been printed that morning.

The first showed a tunnel mouth.

I knew the tunnel. Every child of the northern lines knew the tunnel — the Túnel del Cóndor, the great bore through the shoulder of the Cordillera, the engineering pride of my father's generation, sealed by government decree nineteen years ago and written into every map since as *CERRADO — INTRANSITABLE*. I had grown up on my father's knee hearing about the rock conditions in that bore, the granite that fought the drills for eleven months, the day they broke through and the whole crew wept in the dust. It had been closed, the official record said, for structural failure. For safety. For the mountain's own good.

The photograph showed the tunnel mouth blown open from the inside.

The caption read: *Interior face, station 4+200. Charge pattern: industrial, commercial explosive, placed in a ring — not a collapse pattern. This was demolition, not failure. Note cut faces of seam exposure at lower left.*

I moved the lamp closer. At the lower left of the photograph, where the blast had peeled the tunnel wall open like the rind of a fruit, the rock did not look like granite anymore. It looked like a vein. A seam of ore, wide as a rail car, running into the mountain at the exact depth and on the exact line that the tunnel had been driven through — because my father's generation had driven the tunnel *through* it without knowing, or perhaps knowing, or perhaps the seam had been found and measured and then the tunnel had been closed and the seam buried under a decree and a legend of structural failure.

The second photograph was a survey plat. Delgado's own drafting — I recognized the hand, the same hand as the note, the same beautiful, disciplined lettering — showing the seam's extent in plan and section, traced from the tunnel exposures, with tonnage estimates penciled in the margins. The numbers were enormous. They were the kind of numbers that buy governments.

The third through the ninth photographs documented the rest: core samples stacked and tagged; a company survey marker — the same mark I'd seen on the ridgeline stones, the same mark Tomás had gone white looking at — driven into the seam face itself; a page from a Consorcio Andrade internal memorandum, photographed flat on a camp table, its letterhead crisp, its language the language of men discussing ore bodies and *access constraints* and the *desirability of maintaining the current regulatory posture regarding the northern corridor*. The current regulatory posture. The closed tunnel. The closed pass. The closed lines my father had surveyed and been told to forget.

The tenth photograph was of men.

It had been taken from distance, through a long lens, grainy with enlargement: a column of figures on the glacier below the tunnel mouth, hauling crates, and among them a man in a good coat pointing with a stick at the tunnel face. The caption, in Delgado's hand, was one line: *Andrade party at the bore, third day of the demolition. The man directing is identified to me as V. Andrade himself.*

And the eleventh photograph — the last one, the one I held longest, the one I finally had to set face-down on my knee because looking at it was like looking into a well — was of a tunnel wall with a date and a set of initials drilled into the rock in surveyor's lettering, the way crews signed their breakthroughs.

*E.Q. — R.Q. — 19—*

My father's initials. My father's crew. The breakthrough he had told me about, the day the mountain opened and the men wept in the dust. The tunnel had been *theirs*. They had driven it through the mountain with their hands and their lives, and then someone had come with commercial explosives and closed it, not because it had failed, but because it had *succeeded* — because it had found the seam, because the seam was worth more sealed than open, because a pass that carried trains carried witnesses and a pass that carried nothing carried only ore, waiting, like money in a vault.

"Ada." Tomás's voice came down the crevasse, thin with cold and something else. "The light's going. What did you find?"

I looked at the photographs in my lap. I looked at the note, at the line where the pencil ran off the edge of the page. Somewhere out in the darkening corridor of the glacier, an old man named Emilio Delgado was either dead or walking, and either way he had done the one thing left to him: he had wrapped the truth in oilcloth and hung a lantern in the ice and waited for the river to bring someone.

Vindication rose in me like water in a closed pipe — my father had been right, the lines had been murdered, the pass had been killed in its cradle — and dread came with it, inseparable, the same water, because I understood now what we were carrying, and I understood what men had already done to keep it from descending, and I understood that the six of them had marched out of this camp and that no one had marched back, and that the men who had marched them out were somewhere on this mountain, or below it, or ahead of us.

"We found him," I called up, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "We found what he left. Tomás — get the rope. We're getting this out of the ice."

* * *

We climbed out of the crevasse into the last violet light, and the cold had teeth now, and the first stars were out over the corridor like nails holding the sky down.

Río took the cache and divided it without being asked — the food into the common stores, the pistol into his own pack, the notebook and the photographs wrapped again in oilcloth and distributed, half to my coat, half to his, so that no single fall, no single theft, no single river could take all of it. He did it the way he did everything, without ceremony, and when he was done he stood looking north up the corridor, where the glacier ran white and blue into the dark, and he said, quietly, mostly to himself:

"Four days of fuel. He meant to walk out. Old man, alone, four days of fuel." He shook his head slowly. "The river should have brought us sooner."

"The river brought us when the river brought us," I said, and heard my father's own cadence in the words, and could not take them back.

Tomás had not spoken since the crevasse. He stood apart from us at the edge of the dead camp, his back to the tents, and when I came to him he was holding the steel records chest lid in his hands, turning it over, looking at the ministry stencil as if it were a face he was trying to memorize.

"Ada," he said, and stopped. He did this twice more, starting and stopping, and I watched my brother — my brilliant, ruined brother, who could read a landscape the way priests read scripture and who had sold that gift once and hated himself for it every day since — wrestle with something behind his teeth.

"What?"

"Nothing." He set the lid down. "It's nothing. Let's get off the ice before full dark."

But his hands were shaking, and it was not the cold, and I filed it away with everything else — with the second expedition on the ridgeline, with the smoke we'd seen two nights past, with the feeling, growing in me like a splinter working toward the skin, that everyone on this mountain was carrying a piece of the truth and that the pieces were about to start cutting.

We struck camp in the dark and moved a kilometer east, off the glacier's flank, to a bench of rock Río knew, and we did not light a fire, and we ate cold food, and above us the glacier groaned and settled in the dark like a ship at anchor, and I lay with the photographs against my chest inside my coat, over my heart, where they were coldest and where I could feel them, every one, like a second heartbeat.

Eleven photographs. A dead man's lantern in the ice. And somewhere below us, coming up the corridor we had come down, or waiting ahead of us at whatever gate the mountain kept, the men who had marched six surveyors into disappearance.

I slept in pieces, and between the pieces I dreamed of my father's hands drilling his initials into breakthrough rock, and in the dream the dust of it never settled, and the mountain never closed its mouth.

We broke camp before first light. Río stood at the edge of the bench, looking north, and his whole body had gone still in the way that meant he was listening to something we couldn't hear yet.

"Smoke," he said. "Ahead of us. And fresh."
