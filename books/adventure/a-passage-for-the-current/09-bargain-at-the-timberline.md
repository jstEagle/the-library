## 9. Bargain at the Timberline

The trees gave out the way a voice gives out at the end of a long speech — not all at once, but thinning, faltering, until there was nothing left to say. One morning we were walking through stunted ñire bent like old men against the wind, and the next morning there was only stone, and the stone went up forever.

Tomás Quintana knew timberlines. He had drawn them on a hundred maps, the neat dotted line where the cartographer's world ended and the surveyor's began, and he had never once thought about what it meant to *cross* one. Now he understood. Below the trees there was cover, fuel, the possibility of hiding. Above them there was only exposure, and everything exposed could be seen.

And they were being seen.

Río had spotted the smoke two days earlier — a gray smudge on the ridgeline to the east, too regular to be a storm, too persistent to be a campfire. Since then the smudge had kept pace with them, paralleling their route up the valley like a shadow that had learned to walk. Ada said nothing about it, but Tomás watched her check the ridgeline every hour, the way a man checks a wound he knows is festering. Río doubled back each evening to read their own trail, and each evening he came back with his jaw set tighter.

"They're not trying to hide," he said the second night, crouched over the little spirit stove while the wind worried the tent fly. "A man who doesn't want to be found doesn't light fires on a skyline. They want us to know they're here. It's a message."

"What kind of message?" Ada asked.

"The kind you send before the other kind."

Tomás sat with his back against his pack and his father's map rolled in its oilcloth tube between his knees, and he thought about how a mountain looks from a map room. How easy it is, from a thousand kilometers away, to make a dotted line cross a dotted line and call it a route. He had done it for money once. He was beginning to suspect he might do it again for something cheaper — for the simple, animal want of being warm and low and alive.

* * *

The timberline caught them at noon on the fourth day, in a bowl of granite where the last trees stood in a ragged, wind-flattened fringe, and the men came out of that fringe the way water comes out of a broken dam.

There was no shouting. That was the worst of it. Tomás had expected shouting, rifles leveled, the theater of violence. Instead eight figures simply stood up among the twisted ñire, eight rifles already shouldered, and the valley went quiet in the particular way a valley goes quiet when it understands what is in it. The mules stopped. Even the wind seemed to pause to take inventory.

"Rope," Río said, very low. His hand had gone to the machete at his hip and then, deliberately, away from it. "Nobody moves. Nobody talks. Let me."

But the men weren't interested in Río. They parted, and a tall man came through the middle of them with the unhurried gait of someone arriving at an appointment he had scheduled himself. He was fifty, maybe, gray at the temples, dressed in better gear than any of his men — canvas the color of bone, boots that had been resoled in a city, a rifle slung across his back like a piece of luggage he had no intention of using. His face was long and reasonable, the face of a man who had spent his life explaining things to people who then did what he wanted.

"Señora Quintana," he said, and his voice carried in the thin air without effort. "I'd hoped to make your acquaintance under a roof. The mountain is a poor host. My name is Vásquez."

Ada stood very still. Tomás watched her take the measure of the man the way she took the measure of a bridge — load paths, failure points, the question of what would give first.

"You know my name," she said.

"I know all three of your names." Vásquez's gaze touched Tomás and lingered there a half-second too long, and Tomás felt the old shame rise in his throat like bile. "And your guide's. And I know what you're carrying."

"The government hired us to find the Delgado team."

"The government hired you to find a rumor." Vásquez smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, the smile of a man who had talked his way across three provinces. "The Delgado team was reassigned. There was a clerical error. They're in Coyhaique, all of them, fat and happy and drawing salaries. You've been walking north after a ghost, señora, and winter is coming down that valley behind you faster than any of us would like. I'm offering you a way off this mountain."

"And what does it cost?"

"Nothing you'll miss." He spread his hands. "The map your father drew. The closed lines, the old survey. It's government property, technically — a document of record. It was never meant to circulate. You return it, we all walk down together, warm and fed, and in a month you're in Puerto Frío reading about it in the papers. Everyone lives. Everyone is paid. It's the simplest bargain you'll ever be offered."

The wind came down the glacier and flattened the last trees, and Tomás looked at his sister and saw her looking at Vásquez, and he understood — with the clarity that only comes to men who have already lost things — that she was going to refuse. He could read her the way he could read a contour line. The set of her shoulders. The hand drifting, not toward the map, but toward the ice axe at her hip. Ada Quintana did not carry a weapon to use it. She carried it to remind the world she was not afraid, and it never worked, because she never was.

"You dynamited the tunnel," she said. "At Quebrada Honda. You killed six men and buried the pass to hide what's under it."

Something moved behind Vásquez's reasonable face — not anger, exactly. Recalculation. "You've been reading other people's mail."

"Delgado's notebook. His photographs. The cache in the crevasse."

"Ah." Vásquez nodded slowly, as if she had confirmed a reservation. "Then I'll be plainer, since you're a woman who prefers plain. The map is worth a great deal to people who cannot come up here themselves. They cannot come up here because the pass is closed, and the pass must stay closed, and the map is the only document that proves it was ever open. Give it to me, and you are a woman who got lost. Keep it, and you are a witness. I don't have to tell you what happens to witnesses in this country. You've read the papers too."

"You're threatening her," Tomás said.

The word was out of his mouth before he knew he had spent it, and Vásquez turned to him with the slow, pleased attention of a man who has found the loose board in a floor.

"Señor Quintana." The warmth in his voice was unbearable, because it was genuine. "I wondered when you'd speak. You and I have met before, in a manner of speaking. Lima, was it? The Andrade coastal office? You were younger. The work was smaller." He let that sit in the cold air like a body. "You did good work, they said. Careful work. A man who understands that a map is a tool, not a truth. Tools serve whoever holds them."

"Tomás." Ada's voice, quiet, without turning her head.

"I'm just remembering," Tomás said, and his own voice sounded far away to him, "that I was paid well."

* * *

They let them keep their camp. That was the obscenity of it. The men withdrew fifty meters to the treeline and made their own fire, and the two fires burned in the granite bowl all afternoon like an argument neither side would end, and Tomás sat apart from his sister and Río and did the arithmetic he had spent eleven years refusing to do.

Eight rifles against one machete and two ice axes. A storm front building over the western peaks — Río had read the clouds at midday and said nothing, which was its own sentence. The calving window that would not open for days yet, that might not open at all if the season turned. And on the other side of the scale: a valley. A warm room. A train south. Money, even — Vásquez had not named a figure, but men like Vásquez always had a figure, and Tomás had learned in Lima that the figure was always enough, that enough was the most dangerous number in the world.

He had been twenty-six in Lima, and the money had been enough, and he had redrawn the coastal survey to swallow three fishing villages into a concession boundary, and he had told himself for eleven years that he hadn't known what would happen to the villages. He had told himself it so many times the telling had worn a channel, the way water wears stone, and he had flowed down that channel every day since, and it had delivered him here: to a granite bowl at the top of the world, calculating the price of his sister's spine.

She was across the fire, mending a torn pack strap by lantern light, her father's map in its tube propped against her knee like a sleeping child. Río sat beside her, saying nothing, the way he always sat beside her, and Tomás hated him a little for it — hated the patience, the earned place, the way Río's silence said *I have already chosen and do not need to choose again.*

Vásquez came alone after dark.

No rifles. No escort. Just the tall man and the lantern he carried, and he crouched at the edge of their fire circle like a neighbor come to borrow salt, and he spoke low, and he spoke only to Tomás.

"Your sister is her father's daughter," he said. "She'll take that map to the summit or die on the ice, and she'll take the two of you with her, because that's what conviction costs when it's other people paying. You've seen the icefall. You know what's ahead. I've crossed that glacier four times, señor. I know what it does to ropes and to the men holding them." He set something on the ground between them — a canvas wallet, heavy, the shape of a decision. "Passage down. Provisions. This. And no one will ever know what you drew in Lima. Your sister included. Especially your sister."

Tomás looked at the wallet. He looked at it for a long time, and the fire cracked, and somewhere up-valley the glacier spoke in its sleep, a long groan like a door in an empty house.

"You knew about the tunnel," he said. "Before it happened. You drew the survey that justified it."

"I drew a survey," Vásquez said. "What other men did with it is between them and God."

"That's what I told myself."

"And how has that served you?" The question was gentle, and that was the cruelest thing about it. "Eleven years, señor. I know what eleven years of that sentence does to a man. I'm offering you the only commutation on the market. Take your sister down the mountain. She'll curse you. She'll curse you for ten years. And then one winter night she'll be old and tired and she'll pour herself a glass of something and she'll be glad her brother was a coward, because cowards are the ones still alive to be cursed." He stood, and left the wallet on the ground, and looked down at Tomás with something that was almost, horribly, kindness. "The offer stands until dawn. After dawn, señor, we stop being neighbors."

He walked back to his own fire, and the dark took him the way the dark takes everything up here — completely, without residue.

* * *

Tomás did not touch the wallet. He did not touch it for an hour, and then he did, and its weight in his hands was the weight of every year since Lima, and he sat with it while the fire burned down and the stars wheeled over the col and his sister slept — or seemed to sleep — with her back against the map tube.

He thought about the channel worn in the stone. He thought about how a man flows downhill, always downhill, and how the lowest ground is the grave.

Then he thought about his father's hands. Not the map — the hands. Big, cracked at the knuckles, always smelling of graphite and kerosene. He had been six years old, sitting on a survey bench in a rail cut, watching those hands draw a line across a blank page, and he had asked his father what the line was, and his father had said, *that's where the train will go, mijo, and everything the line touches will change,* and Tomás had asked, *is that good?* and his father had been quiet for a long time, and then he had said, *it's true. That's the first thing a line has to be. Everything else is a choice.*

A line has to be true. Everything else is a choice.

Tomás looked at the wallet. Then he stood, crossed the frozen ground to the edge of the firelight, and dropped it — not into the fire, not into his pack, but into the rocks at the bowl's rim, where it lay like a stone among stones, and he stood over it shivering until his legs would hold him no longer.

"Tomás." Ada's voice, from the dark. Awake, then. Had been awake all along. "What did he say to you?"

He came back to the fire. His sister was sitting up, and Río was sitting up, and both of them were looking at him, and the lantern light made their faces into something out of an old photograph — the kind of photograph that outlives the people in it.

"He offered me what I've wanted for eleven years," Tomás said. "To stop being what I am." His voice broke on it, and he let it break, because he was done wearing the smooth face for them. "And I nearly took it. I want you to know that. I want you to know exactly what I nearly did, because you're going to rope yourselves to me on the ice tomorrow, and you should rope yourselves to me knowing the rope is tied to a man who had his hand on the door."

The wind moved through the last trees below them, and far up the valley the glacier groaned again, longer this time, like something turning over in its sleep.

Ada looked at him for a long moment. Then she reached over and took the map tube from against her pack and held it out to him — held it out, an offering, a weight, a sentence.

"Then carry it," she said. "If your hands are going to shake anyway, let them shake holding something true."

He took it. It was heavier than paper and oilcloth had any right to be.

And it was Río, standing then, turning east toward the treeline, who said the thing that ended the night — said it quietly, the way he said everything, but with a blade in it:

"Dawn's coming. And we're not the only ones who know the icefall freezes hardest at first light." He looked at the fifty meters of dark where Vásquez's fire had burned low. "They'll want to move before we do. So we move first — and we move onto the ice."
