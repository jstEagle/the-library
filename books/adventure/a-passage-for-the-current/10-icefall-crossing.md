## 10. Icefall Crossing

We left camp in the dark, because the dark was the only honest hour the glacier had left to give.

By three in the morning the cold had a texture — not the dry bite of the high desert I had grown up in, but a wet cold, a cold that seemed to be thinking about you, weighing you, deciding what it wanted to take. I moved through it the way my grandfather taught me to move through anything dangerous: slowly, listening more than looking, letting the mountain make the first argument.

Behind me came the sounds of the others. Ada's boots on the frozen moraine, steady as a metronome — she walked like an engineer, every step placed on purpose, her weight committed only when the ground had answered for the last one. Tomás was quieter, but not in a good way; his silence had the ragged edge of a man conserving himself, body and spirit both, the way you ration water on a long dry stretch. And behind him, the mules — what was left of them, one mule and half our winter, gone down into a crevasse two days back with a sound I still heard when I closed my eyes: the bray cut short, the rope singing, then nothing.

The icefall waited above us like a held breath.

I had crossed it twice in my life, both times with Ada's father, both times in calving season, when the frost did its nightly work of welding the broken ice back into something that would hold a man's weight until the sun came to undo it. The icefall was a river that had fallen and broken and refused to finish falling — a chaos of seracs, those leaning towers of blue ice, some of them the height of a church, all of them leaning, all of them waiting for the season to give them permission to come down. In the day, they shed and groaned and shrugged off their own weight. At dawn, in the hard frost, they held still. That was the bargain the mountain offered: cross in the dark, before the sun, and the ice will pretend, for a few hours, to be a road.

"You're sure about the hour," Tomás said at my shoulder. Not a question. A man asking a question he already knew the answer to, so he could hear it said by someone else.

"The frost sets at two. It holds until the sun clears the eastern ridge — six hours, maybe five if the clouds come up from the valley." I checked the knot at his harness without asking permission. He let me. "We cross in the dark or we don't cross at all."

"And if the sun comes early?"

"Then the mountain decides, and we find out what it decided."

He said nothing to that. Above us the seracs stood against the stars like the shoulders of sleeping animals, and the wind came down off the col with that high thin whistle that meant clear air and cold deep enough to kill a man's fingers before he felt them go.

Ada came up last, coiling rope with hands that didn't fumble, and I watched her the way I have been watching her since we were both too young to know what watching was. There was frost in her eyebrows. Her breath plumed and tore away. She had not slept more than an hour — none of us had — but she stood at the foot of the icefall the way she had stood at the foot of every closed line her father ever surveyed, with her chin up and her jaw set, as if the obstacle were a discourtesy she intended to correct.

"Order?" she said.

"You first. Then Tomás. Then me." I had thought about the order all night, in the shallow scrap of sleep I allowed myself. Ada first because she moved best on ice and would set the pace the others could follow. Tomás second because I wanted him where I could reach him. I did not say that last part aloud. Some debts you carry quietly or they stop being debts and start being demands.

"And the rope?"

"Twelve meters between us. When I call *frozen*, you stop and plant. When I call *move*, you move. Nobody speaks otherwise." I looked at each of them in turn, and the starlight was enough to see their faces by, barely. "The icefall is not a place for conversation. It is a place for obedience. Whoever disobeys it doesn't get a second lesson."

Ada almost smiled. "You sound like him."

I felt that go through me like a spike of cold. "I sound like someone who has buried men on this ice," I said. "Come."

* * *

The first hour was the easy lie.

The lower icefall was broken but honest — crevasses you could see, bridges that showed their thickness, a route that wound between the seracs like a path between houses. I had it in my body from twenty years ago, and my body remembered it even as my mind kept insisting it must have changed. Icefalls change every season; some of them change every night. But the great blocks move slowly, and the route I had drawn in my memory held. We crossed the first bridges with the rope taut and singing faintly, and beneath us, through the ice, we could hear the glacier's inner voice — that deep, slow percussion of a living river of ice, shifting, settling, dreaming.

Tomás crossed well. I will give him that. Whatever he had been drinking away in that border town, it had not taken his mountain legs. He moved with his weight low and his axe planted like a man who had done this before and been punished for doing it badly, and I wondered — not for the first time, and not for the last — what exactly the consortium had made him do in those years he would not speak of, and where they had sent him to do it.

The trouble began where the easy lie ended.

Above the mid-shelf the icefall stood up. The route turned from a path into a wall — a ramp of blue ice pitched steep enough that we climbed it facing in, front-pointing, axes swinging, the rope between us hanging free and then tightening and then hanging free again as each of us in turn became the other's anchor. The seracs here leaned over the route like conspirators. I could feel them above me the way you feel a stranger standing too close in a dark room — not seeing them, knowing them.

And then the light began.

It came up out of the east the way it always does at altitude — not a sunrise but a rumor of one, a gray stain, then a bruise of rose along the serrated ridge, then a thin gold wire drawn along the highest serac, and the whole icefall lit from within like a lantern of blue glass. It was, as it had always been, the most beautiful thing I ever expected to die looking at.

"Faster," I said. "The frost is dying. Move when I move, rest when I rest, and *move*."

We moved. The ice began to talk — small sounds, at first, the tick and creak of expansion, a serac shedding a skin of rime somewhere above us with a soft hiss like sand poured on a drum. Then a crack, deep and long, rolled through the glacier's bones, and I planted my axe and called *frozen* and we all stood like herons, one-legged, waiting, while the mountain considered us and found us, for the moment, beneath its notice.

It was in that stillness that I heard it — not with my ears first but with my feet, a tremor, a shiver in the ramp itself — and then with my eyes: high on our right, a serac the size of a chapel leaning a degree further than it had leaned a minute ago.

"*Move*," I said, and my voice came out flat and hard and did not sound like mine. "Move now, all of you, do not wait — *move*."

Ada went like water. Tomás went like a man running from a fire. The serac let go with a sound like the world's largest door being torn off the world's largest hinge, and the air filled with ice-dust, white and roaring, and I felt the rope behind me snap taut and then — snap.

Not the rope. The word. The rope *parted*.

I have thought about that rope every day since. Twelve meters of eight-millimeter line, new that season, and it parted not at a knot, not at an edge, but clean across, as if the mountain had simply reached down with a pair of scissors and cut the argument short. Later I would find the break and understand it — a burr of ice, a sawing, a hundred small frictions — but in that moment there was no later. There was only the sound of Tomás going, the scrape of his boots losing the ramp, his axe ringing off the blue ice and cartwheeling away into the white, and his voice — one word, my name, nothing else, as if in the last moment of his life all the bitterness and the silence and the years of not-speaking had burned away and left only that.

I threw myself down.

I threw myself down the way you throw yourself between a child and a falling wall — without thought, without decision, with the whole body answering a question the mind never got to ask. My axe bit. My boots bit. The rope's broken end was in my left hand — I had been carrying the slack coiled there, an old habit, a guide's habit, the habit of a man who has spent his life holding other people's lives in his fists — and the weight of Tomás came down that rope like the weight of the whole mountain, and I held it.

The burn went up my arm to the shoulder. The rope smoked through my grip. I could feel the skin going, feel the cold of the ice beneath me and the fire of the rope above me meeting somewhere in my palm and shaking hands. Somewhere above and behind me the serac's debris came down in a long white thunder, and the ice-dust swallowed the light, and I shouted — I don't know what, some word, some animal sound — and planted my axe deeper, and *held*.

"Ada!" I roared. "Anchor! *Anchor!*"

She was already moving. I heard her before I saw her — the flat slap of her axe driving home, twice, three times, the engineer's economy of her, no motion wasted — and then the rope between us went taut and my weight halved and halved again, and together, the two of us, we held the broken line while Tomás swung below us over the blue void like a bell that had rung its last and now only hung.

"Tomás." My voice was a rasp. My left hand would not open. "Tomás, listen to my voice. Feet to the wall. Find the ice with your feet."

"I can't—" His voice came up small, stripped. "Río, I can't feel—"

"You can. Your feet are smarter than your fear. Feet to the wall. *Now.*"

And he did it. I heard him do it — the scrape of his boots finding the ramp, the small gasp of a man discovering that his body still belongs to him — and then he was climbing, hand over hand up the broken rope's lower end toward my voice, and Ada was hauling, and I was hauling with my right hand and my shoulder and my teeth if it came to that, and slowly, terribly, the three of us folded back up onto the ramp like a single creature pulling its own limb out of a trap.

We lay on the ice and breathed. The ice-dust settled around us in a fine white veil, and through it the sun came fully up, and the icefall, having made its point, went quiet.

* * *

It was Ada who saw the hand first.

We had gotten Tomás over the lip of the ramp, onto the shelf, and he was on his knees, breathing in long shuddering draws, and I was trying to open my left hand and discovering that it had stopped taking instructions from me.

"Río." Ada's voice. Close. Careful. "Río, let me see it."

I let her see it. I already knew what she would find, because I could feel it — or rather, I could not feel it, and that was the message. The hand was white. Not pale — white, the white of the ice itself, the fingers waxy and stiff and slightly wrong, like a sculptor's first attempt at a hand by someone who had only ever seen one from a distance. The rope had taken the skin off the palm in a long burn, and the cold had taken everything else.

"Frostbite," she said. "Deep. We need to get you down to warmth. We need to—"

"No." I said it quietly, and the quiet made them both stop. "No warmth for days. You know what's above us, Ada. You know what's behind us." I flexed the fingers. Two of them answered, slow and grudging. The rest had resigned. "It will keep. Hands keep. Men don't, on this ice, if we stop."

"You could lose it."

"I could lose worse." I looked at her, and then at Tomás, kneeling in the snow with his harness still half-cut where we'd freed him, looking at me with an expression I had never seen on his face in thirty years of knowing him — not gratitude exactly, and not shame, but the place where those two things meet and cannot decide which one to become.

"You held on," he said.

"It's what the rope was for."

"The rope broke."

"Then it's what I was for." I said it without weight, the way you state a fact about the weather, and I watched the words go into him like water into dry ground. This was the man who had falsified maps for Andrade. This was the man who had nearly taken Vásquez's bargain at the timberline. I knew all the shapes of him, the way I knew the shapes of the icefall — and I knew, too, that a man is not his worst crossing. My grandfather had a saying: *the river does not remember the stone it carried, only that it carried it.* I had carried him. That was all. That was everything.

Tomás stood up. He took the broken rope's end from my frozen left hand — gently, prying my fingers with his own, and his hands were shaking, and he did not pretend they weren't — and he coiled it, and he slung it across his shoulder where I would have carried it, and he said, "I'll take the lead rope from here. You'll climb with your right. And when we're down off this ice, Río, you and I are going to have a conversation that's twenty years overdue."

"Tomás—" Ada began.

"Not now." He said it to her, but he was looking at me. "Not on the ice. But it's owed. All of it."

I looked at my hand, white as the glacier, and at the sun climbing the eastern ridge, and at the route above us — the last ramp, the col, the high corridor beyond it where, if the season held and the glacier did what glaciers do, the calving would soon tear open a passage that had been shut for twenty years.

The frost was dying. The seracs had begun, softly, one by one, to shed.

"We have an hour of ice left," I said. "Maybe less. After that, the mountain stops lending the road." I got my feet under me, my axe in my right hand, my ruined left cradled against my chest like something I intended to bring home. "So we spend it moving. All of it. Every minute."

Ada looked east, at the sun, at the shrinking margin of it, and I saw her do the arithmetic she could not help doing — the same arithmetic her father used to do, the same arithmetic that had gotten him killed — and when she looked back at me her face had gone still and certain.

"Then we move," she said.

And far above us, at the head of the icefall, deep in the blue heart of the glacier, something vast shifted its weight — and began, with a groan that rose through the ice and up through our boots and into our bones, to let go.
