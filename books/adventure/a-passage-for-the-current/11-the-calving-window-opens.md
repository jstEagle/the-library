## 11. The Calving Window Opens

The mountain spoke first.

It was not a sound so much as a change in the air itself — a pressure behind the ears, a hum in the fillings of the teeth, the way the river beneath the ice seemed to hold its breath. Ada had been awake for an hour already, lying in the blue dark of the tent with her father's map rolled against her chest like a sleeping child, listening to the glacier groan through its long winter throat. But this was different. This was the sound of something letting go.

"Río," she said, and her voice came out smaller than she intended.

He was already outside. She heard him moving, heard Tomás's muffled protest as Río shook him awake, and then all three of them stood at the edge of their camp on the lateral moraine, breath smoking in the starlight, watching the face of the glacier two kilometers across the valley begin to die.

It started small — a crack like a rifle shot, then another, overlapping, a stutter of reports echoing off the cirque walls. Then a section of the ice cliff the size of a church leaned forward, ponderously, almost politely, and fell.

The thunder arrived seconds later, rolling up the valley and through the ground and into the soles of Ada's boots. The calving front collapsed inward in a slow white avalanche, and where it had stood there opened a gap — a corridor, dark and clean-edged, running up into the heart of the range like a door kicked open in a wall that had stood for centuries.

"Madre de Dios," Tomás whispered.

Río said nothing. He was counting. Ada could see his lips moving, see his eyes tracking the falling ice the way an engineer's eyes track a bridge under load — measuring, timing, reading the rhythm of collapse the way she herself read the rhythm of rail joints flashing past a locomotive window.

"How long?" she asked.

"Forty-eight hours." His voice was flat with awe and something close to grief. "Maybe less if the storm comes early. After that—" he nodded at the calving front, where new ice was already grinding forward to seal the gap, "—the mountain closes its mouth."

Ada looked at the corridor. Even from this distance, even in starlight, she could see what it meant: a passage through the icefall that no living person had walked since her father surveyed it twenty years ago. A route drawn in ink on paper that was currently pressed against her heartbeat. *The pass exists,* the map said. *I walked it. I measured it. It is real.*

And somewhere behind them, down the valley they had climbed for six days, Vásquez and his armed men were climbing too.

* * *

They broke camp in the dark, by habit now rather than necessity. Six days on the ice had taught them a choreography: Tomás striking the tents while Río cut the mule's picket — the one surviving mule, gray-faced and patient, carrying what remained of their provisions — while Ada studied the route ahead with the map unrolled across her knees and a headlamp clamped between her teeth.

The corridor was everything her father's ink lines promised and nothing her imagination had prepared her for.

They reached the calving front as the first gray light came up, and Ada stopped, momentarily unable to move. The walls of ice rose thirty meters on either side of them, sculpted by meltwater into flutes and buttresses, glowing from within with a blue so deep it seemed less like color than like depth — the blue of deep water, of deep time. The floor of the corridor was old ice, polished smooth by ancient flow, veined with bubbles that held air from before anyone had thought to build a railroad anywhere.

Her father had stood here. She knew it the way she knew her own hands. Somewhere along these walls he had set his instruments, shot his bearings, written in his careful surveyor's hand: *Pass negotiable during calving window only. Corridor stable approximately 48 hours. Recommend permanent marking.*

*Recommend permanent marking.* And then he had died, and the marking had never come, and the consortium had made certain the map itself would never be seen.

"Stay center," Río said quietly. "Never near the walls. Ice sheds without warning." He glanced upward, at seracs the size of houses balanced in impossible attitudes above them. "And keep moving. We don't stop inside."

They moved.

For three hours they walked through the blue heart of the glacier, and Ada forgot, in intervals, to be afraid. It was the same forgetting she found on a good run of track, when the machine beneath her sang and the country unspooled and she was briefly nothing but motion and attention. The corridor bent and widened and narrowed. Meltwater ran beside them in channels of unbelievable clarity, singing over ice the way water sings over stone but higher, stranger, a music with no weather in it. Once a weasel — or something like one, some small white hunter — crossed the corridor ahead of them and vanished into a crevasse mouth, and Tomás laughed out loud, a startled bark of joy, as if the animal were proof they'd been expected.

Then the mountain reminded them who was host.

A crack ran overhead, sharp as a whip, and a block of ice the size of a cart horse sheared off the eastern wall and smashed down fifty meters behind them. The concussion knocked Ada to her knees. Shards whistled past her shoulder, stinging through her parka, and for a moment the whole corridor rang like a struck bell.

"No stopping," Río repeated, hauling her up. His voice hadn't changed at all. That was the thing about Río — fear moved through him and left no sediment. "That was the wall saying hello. Next time it won't announce itself."

They went faster after that.

* * *

They emerged from the lower corridor onto a shelf of rock at midday — actual rock, brown and gritty and blessedly still — and ate cold food with numb fingers while the mule dozed against a boulder. Below them the icefall they had crossed at dawn lay like a frozen waterfall, beautiful now from above, its violence all memory. Above them the corridor continued, climbing in great switchbacks toward the high col that the maps called Paso del Viento and the government files called, in bureaucratic shorthand, *Route 7-North: status closed.*

Ada spread the map on the stone and weighted its corners. Her father's line climbed exactly the way the terrain did — she checked it twice, three times, because some part of her still needed to be sure, still needed the man on the paper to be true. The line was true. It switchbacked up the shelf, crossed the upper corridor, gained the col through a notch he had marked *ice axe required* in a margin note so small and so familiar that her throat closed around it.

"He drew this from memory," Tomás said suddenly. He was crouched over the map, and his expression was strange — not the bitterness Ada had carried across half a country to find, but something rawer. "Or from notes. Look — here, and here. Elevations to the meter. You don't get that from a single traverse. He came back. He must have come back at least twice." He looked up at her. "We thought he was in Puerto Frío those winters. Mama thought—"

"I know what Mama thought." Ada's voice came out harder than she wanted. Twenty years of her mother waiting at windows, and here was the arithmetic of it: her father had been climbing toward this pass while his family counted his absences like debts. "He chose this over us. Whatever else happened, Tomás, he chose it."

Tomás held her gaze for a long moment, and there was something in his face she couldn't read — something that looked almost like pity, which made no sense, and which frightened her more than the icefall had.

"You don't know what he chose," he said quietly. "Not yet."

Before she could answer, Río's hand closed on both their shoulders.

"Down-valley," he said. "Look."

* * *

Through the gap they had come through — the calving front, still grinding slowly inward, the door already swinging shut behind them — Ada could see the lower glacier, and on the lower glacier, movement.

Four figures. Small at this distance, ant-like, roped together, moving with the deliberate speed of men who knew they were being watched. Behind them, further down, two more. And at the tail of the column, a glint that could only be glass — binoculars, or a scope — catching the sun and throwing it back up the valley like a signal flare.

"They're on the icefall," Tomás breathed. "In daylight. In the afternoon sun. They'll lose someone."

"Or they know something we don't about the crossing," Río said. His jaw was tight. "Vásquez doesn't waste men. If he's sending them up the icefall at the worst hour, it's because he believes the corridor will hold longer than I told you it would." He turned to Ada, and his eyes were steady and terrible. "Which means our forty-eight hours may be thirty-six. Or less."

Ada looked at the figures below, then at the map, then up at the col — still hours away, still wrapped in the high cloud that was thickening even as she watched, sliding down from the summit peaks like smoke poured from an invisible fire.

The exhilaration of the morning — the door opening, the pass real, her father's lines walking true beneath her boots — curdled slowly into arithmetic. Hours against meters. Weather against distance. Six of them against seven armed men who did not need to reach the pass themselves; they only needed to reach *her*, and they had rifles that would make distance irrelevant long before the ice did.

"We rest ten minutes," she said. "No more. Then we climb until dark, and we're on the col by tomorrow noon."

"And if the storm comes early?" Tomás asked.

Ada began rolling the map, carefully, along its old creases, the way her father must have rolled it a hundred times.

"Then we're on the col when it comes," she said. "Because down is no longer a direction we own."

Above them, faint but unmistakable, the glacier cracked its knuckles — and the first flakes of the new snow came sifting down through the blue light, fine as ash, falling on the map, on the corridor, on the men climbing patiently out of the south.
