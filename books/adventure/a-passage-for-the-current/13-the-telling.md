## 13. The Telling

The storm shelter was older than any of us — a dry-stone bothy built by muleteers a century back, its walls furred with frost that glittered like crushed glass when Río's lantern swung across them. We had gained it at dusk, half-blind from wind, and now the three of us sat pressed close around the small spirit stove while outside the temperature fell through numbers I didn't want to name.

Tomás hadn't spoken since we came in. That alone should have warned me. My brother could talk through anything — through grief, through hangovers, through the long silence of our father's funeral when he'd filled every pause with logistics and train schedules because neither of us could bear the actual words. Silence was not his native country. When he went quiet, he was crossing a border into somewhere dangerous.

He sat with his back against the east wall, our father's journal open on his knees. He'd found it that morning wedged behind a shelf of stone in the shelter's far corner, wrapped in oilcloth, the leather stiff as bark. He'd read it through the day's march without telling me what it held. I'd seen him reading it by lantern light at the last halt, his lips moving, his face doing something I couldn't read — or wouldn't.

"Give it to me," I said.

He didn't move. The stove hissed. Outside, the wind found some flaw in the mountain and played it like a reed, a low moaning note that rose and fell and rose again.

"Tomás."

"I will," he said. "I'm going to. But you have to let me say it first, Ada. All of it. Because if you take this book out of my hands right now, you'll read it cold, and there are things in here that need — " He stopped. Started again. His voice had gone rough, the voice of a man walking on ice he already knows is thin. "There are things in here about me."

Río set down the cup he'd been filling and looked up sharply. In the lantern light his face was all planes and shadow, watchful, the face of a man who has guided enough parties to know when the weather inside a group is turning before the weather outside does.

I held out my hand anyway. It was shaking, and I hated that he could see it.

* * *

He began, as I would learn later, at the end, because the beginning was too far away for him to reach in one night.

"The seam," he said. "You know about the seam. Father found it in '09, running his survey up the northern lines. Copper, maybe more — the samples he sent south came back rich enough that Andrade's people opened an office in Puerto Frío within the month. You remember the men who came to the house. The ones with the good coats."

I remembered. I'd been nineteen. They'd brought brandy and flattery and a contract written in language designed to be signed rather than understood, and our father had served them coffee in mother's cups and thanked them kindly and shown them the door.

"What you don't know," Tomás said, "is that they came back. Not to the house. To me."

The wind moaned. The stove hissed. Somewhere under us, deep in the glacier's body, ice settled with a sound like a door closing in another room.

"It was after the funeral," he said. "After they told us the bridge cable failed. After the inquiry that found no fault in anything except weather. I was twenty-three and drinking more than eating, and a man named Ferrán came to my rooms with a folder of survey sheets and asked me to correct them. Just correct them. Adjust the elevations on the eastern approaches. Show the pass route as unstable. Show the tunnel as compromised. He said it was for safety. He said the consortium didn't want anyone else going up there and dying the way our father died." Tomás laughed then, one short terrible note with nothing in it. "And I believed him. God help me, Ada, I wanted to believe him, because believing him meant Father's death was an accident, and if it was an accident, then I hadn't spent the whole year before it not answering his letters for nothing."

"Stop," I said.

"No." His voice cracked but held. "No, you don't get to stop me. I did the work. Fourteen sheets. I redrew the elevations so smoothly even I couldn't see the lie after a week. Andrade filed them, and the ministry used them, and the northern lines were closed for engineering reasons that never existed, and the valley above the pass — the farms, the muleteers, the whole river country — lost their only road south. And I took the money. I took it and I drank it and I told myself every night for six years that it was grief, that I wasn't myself, that the maps weren't really mine anymore once they were filed." He looked at his hands, turned them over as though checking them for stains. "They were mine. Every line."

Río had gone very still. I felt him beside me the way you feel a change in air pressure before a storm — not movement, exactly, but readiness.

"And Father?" I said. My voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "What does the journal say about Father?"

Tomás closed his eyes. "Read it yourself," he whispered, and pushed the book across the frozen floor toward me.

* * *

Our father's handwriting was beautiful. I had forgotten that — or perhaps I had never known it properly, having left home at seventeen to study rails and grades and load tolerances while he stayed with his instruments and his careful letters. The early entries were what I expected: barometric readings, sketch-maps, notes on serac movement along the calving face, a small drawing of a ptarmigan he'd watched for an hour because, he wrote, *it is good practice to look at something that owes you nothing.*

Then the entries changed.

*Ferrán returned today. Third time. He no longer pretends the corrections are for safety. He offered me a number with four zeroes and said the alternative was that the mountain becomes unsafe for surveyors generally. I told him what I told him the first time: the seam is real, the pass is sound, and I will file what I measured or file nothing.*

*I have hidden the master map. If anything happens to me, whoever finds these pages should know: the map is true. Every line of it. I drew it for the river, not for them.*

*They followed me up from the lower camp today. Two men. I made camp short of the icefall and watched their fire on the ridge. I am not a young man and I am not a fool. If I do not come down, it was not the bridge.*

The next entry was dated two days before he died.

*I have decided to give Ferrán nothing. Not the map, not the silence. Tomorrow I begin the descent and I will go to the ministry myself, whatever it costs. Ada will understand. She always understood the difference between a line drawn honestly and one drawn to please.*

Ada will understand.

I read that sentence four times. The lantern flame bent in some draft I couldn't find, and the shadows on the wall leaned toward me like figures at a bedside.

"He knew," I said. "He knew what they were going to do."

"Yes," Tomás said.

"And you've known since you found this book this morning. You walked beside me all day knowing, and you said nothing."

"I was trying to find a way — "

"There isn't one!" The word came out of me with force I didn't know I was carrying, and it seemed to fill the whole stone room, to press against the walls and come back at us doubled. I was on my feet. I didn't remember standing. "There is no way to tell someone that, Tomás. There is no gentle version. There is only the truth and how long you sit on it, and you sat on it for twelve years. Twelve years I mourned a bridge failure. Twelve years I thought the mountain took him, and I loved the mountain anyway, I gave my whole life to these lines because they were his, because finishing his survey was the only way I knew to talk to him — and it was men. It was money. It was a man named Ferrán with good boots and a rope, and my brother helped bury it."

"I didn't know they'd kill him," Tomás said, and his voice broke clean through. "Not then. I swear it on — " He stopped, because there was nothing left to swear on. Our father. Our mother. The house. "I found out after. A year after. Ferrán got drunk in Valparaíso and told a man in front of me that the old surveyor had been *difficult right up to the end.* And I understood. And I did nothing. I drank, Ada. That was my answer. I drank for six years and let them close the pass over his grave."

Río moved then — not between us, exactly, but into the space where the argument might have become something worse. He put himself near the stove, near the door, near the rope coil, a guide managing a party in falling weather. "Sit down, Ada," he said quietly. "Both of you. The temperature's dropping fast and anger burns fuel we can't spare."

"Let her be angry," Tomás said. "She's earned it."

"She's earned survival," Río said. "Angry hands freeze faster. Sit."

I sat. Or my legs decided for me. The journal lay open on the floor between us, our father's last sentence facing the ceiling — *she always understood the difference* — and I looked at it and felt something in my chest tear along a line I hadn't known was drawn there.

"You falsified the maps," I said. It wasn't a question. I needed to hear it in my own voice, to make it real by saying it. "The same crime you're helping me expose. The same paper, the same pen. If we get down off this ice and put Vásquez's masters in front of a court, they'll pull your fourteen sheets in the first hour. Everything we're carrying — Delgado's photographs, Father's journal, everything — it convicts you too."

"I know."

"You could have taken Vásquez's deal at the timberline. Safe passage down, no questions. Instead you're freezing on a glacier carrying evidence against yourself."

"I know what I'm carrying." He wiped his face with the back of his glove, and I saw that his eyes were wet and freezing at the lashes. "I'm not asking you to forgive me tonight. I'm not asking ever, maybe. I'm asking you to finish this. Because Father drew that map for the river, and Delgado died for those photographs, and I have spent twelve years being the reason the truth stayed buried, and this — " his hand closed over the journal, over the oilcloth, over the whole weight of it — "this is the only penance there is. Not forgiveness. Penance. There's a difference and I've had a long time to learn it."

* * *

We didn't speak for a long time after that. The stove burned low. Río fed it measured splinters of the driftwood stacked by some muleteer dead fifty years, each piece rationed like medicine, and the light moved on the walls and the wind kept up its one long note, and I sat with my brother's confession spread out in front of me like a survey sheet I didn't know how to read.

Because here was the thing I could not say aloud, the thing that frightened me more than Vásquez or the cornice or the closing window of the ice: part of me understood him. Part of me, in the dark hour of some other night, might have signed those fourteen sheets myself if someone had promised me my father's death was weather. Grief makes cartographers of us all. We redraw the world until it holds what we can carry.

And that understanding was its own betrayal. I wanted to hate him cleanly. Hatred was a straight line; love was the contour mess that had gotten us both lost.

"Ada." Río's voice, low. "The storm's building. We move at first light whether you've finished this conversation or not."

"We haven't started it," I said.

"Then start it walking." He met my eyes across the stove, and in his face was something patient and unyielding, the face of a man who had waited years at a trailhead and knew the value of simply continuing. "The mountain doesn't care what's between you. It'll kill you together or apart, and together you at least die warm."

Outside, the wind changed key. Lower. Heavier. The sound of snow beginning to fall in earnest, the first fat flakes ticking against the stones like fingers testing a lock.

Tomás stood, folded the journal into its oilcloth, and held it out to me with both hands, the way you return something borrowed, the way you surrender a weapon.

I took it. I didn't thank him. I don't know if I ever will.

But I slept that night with the book inside my coat, against my ribs, and when the first grey light came and Río pulled the door open onto a world gone white and silent and utterly transformed, I saw, beyond the shelter's mouth, fresh tracks on the new snow below us — many tracks, moving fast, climbing.

Vásquez had not waited out the storm.

"Rope up," Río said, and his voice had gone hard as the ice underfoot. "Now."
