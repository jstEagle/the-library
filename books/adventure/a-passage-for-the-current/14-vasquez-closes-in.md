## 14. Vásquez Closes In

The corridor spoke to Río before the men did.

He had learned its language over three days of hard travel — the groan of settling ice, the tick of meltwater finding new channels, the deep arterial rush of the river running beneath them, patient as a heartbeat that had outlived every man who ever listened for it. He knew which sounds were ordinary and which were warnings. So when the echo came back wrong on the second morning in the high passage — a footfall answering a half-beat late, doubled by some trick of the walls — he stopped and let the others walk ahead of him, and he listened the way his grandmother had taught him to listen for puma: not with his ears but with the whole skin of the silence.

There. Behind them, perhaps a kilometer back, where the corridor bent around a shoulder of blue ice. Boots. Many boots. Moving without care for the noise they made, because they no longer needed to hide.

"Ada," he said quietly.

She turned. The wind off the glacier pulled at her hood, and in the flat white light her face looked carved from something older than tiredness. Tomás was thirty meters ahead, testing a snow bridge with his pole, and she read Río's stillness before she read his eyes.

"How many?"

"Six. Maybe seven." He tilted his head, letting the sound come again. "They've stopped pretending."

* * *

They had been pretending for two days. That was what unsettled Río most — not the pursuit itself, which he had expected since the cornice took Vásquez's men, but the *quality* of it. A hunter who wanted you dead shot to kill. These men did neither. Twice now, shots had cracked down the corridor from long range, and twice the bullets had struck stone and ice a deliberate arm's length from where Ada walked — close enough to herd, far enough to miss. The third shot had taken the heel off Tomás's pack frame, splintering birchwood an inch from his spine, and Tomás had gone white and silent and had not spoken of it since.

"They're shooting like men who've been given rules," Ada said that night in the lee of a serac, her voice low so it wouldn't carry up the corridor. "Why not simply kill us?"

Río fed a twig into the alcohol stove and watched the flame stand straight in the windless pocket of blue shadow. He knew why. He had known since the first warning shot, because he had spent enough years around men who worked for companies to understand the arithmetic they carried in their chests.

"Because dead men leave questions," he said. "A body found in the pass — even in spring, even months from now — brings investigators, families, newspapers. But three climbers lost in a storm, in calving season, in a corridor that seals itself behind them?" He looked up. "That is not murder. That is weather. They want us gone cleanly, Ada. No bodies, no blood, no martyrs. Just absence."

Tomás sat with his back against the ice, turning his ruined pack strap over in his hands. "Andrade's people buried my career the same way," he said. "No accusation, no trial. Just a map that was wrong, and a license that quietly expired." He laughed, and there was nothing in it. "They're consistent. I'll give them that."

Ada stared into the flame. "Then we don't give them a clean disappearance."

"No," Río agreed. "We give them a dirty one."

* * *

He went out alone while the others slept, as he had gone out alone on the first night and would go out alone on any night this work required. This was the part of guiding no client ever saw: the hours after midnight when the mountain became a thing you could negotiate with, if you knew its grammar. His hand ached inside its mitten — the frostbitten fingers from the icefall had passed through their black week and were beginning, the doctor's letters in his memory promised, to come back — but the ache was useful now. It kept him honest about what his body could still do.

The corridor here ran between walls of ice forty meters high, and a kilometer ahead it narrowed where an old tributary glacier had once pushed its snout against the main flow. Río climbed the lateral moraine in the dark, moving on the rock spine where his boots wouldn't print, and studied the ground beyond the narrows by the light that snow gives back even under starless cloud.

It was good ground. It was very good ground, and that was the trouble with it.

Beyond the narrows the corridor widened into a shelf, and the shelf ran level for two hundred meters beneath the flank of the peak on the eastern side. And along that flank, curving out over the shelf like a lip over a cup, ran a cornice — a ridge of wind-built snow, cantilevered over emptiness, thick as a house at its root and thin as a plank at its edge. He had marked it on the way up, the way he marked every cornice, the way his father had taught him: *the mountain builds traps and then forgets where it put them.* Under fresh snow, under cloud, under the flat grey light that made shadows die, a cornice line was invisible. A party hurrying through a narrowing, watching the walls for snipers, feeling the breath of pursuers on their necks — such a party would take the shelf. Such a party would always take the shelf. It was the natural line, the easy line, the line a tired mind chose without choosing.

And beneath the lip of the cornice, the mountainside fell away four hundred meters into a blue crevasse field that the glacier would fold shut again by June.

Río crouched on the moraine and felt the old cold move through him — not the cold of the ice but the cold of what he was considering. He had led men through mountains for twenty years, and in all that time he had held one rule above the others, the rule that separated a guide from everything darker: *you never spend another man's life to save your own.* What he was shaping in his mind was not quite that. And it was exactly that. The men behind them had rifles and orders and the patience of institutions. If Río did nothing, they would be run down in the corridor within a day, maybe two, and Ada and Tomás would vanish into weather, and the valley below would keep its poison another generation. If he did this thing, the mountain might do his killing for him — and the mountain, unlike Vásquez, killed without malice and without witnesses and left the conscience entirely unburdened.

Except it didn't. He knew that too. He would carry whatever happened on that shelf for the rest of his life, however justly it fell.

He thought of his father, who had guided on these same glaciers and died in a whiteout doing it, and who had told him once, drunk on aguardiente and honesty: *the mountain will always offer you a way out that costs someone else. That is how it tests whether you deserve to walk on it.*

But then he thought of Delgado's notebook, wedged in the river stones, the handwriting going frantic page by page. *They have closed the tunnel. They have closed the tunnel and they are closing the road.* He thought of the photographs in the crevasse cache — the dynamited rail mouth, the gleam of ore in the cut faces, the survey team's faces in the last picture, sunburnt and laughing, none of them knowing they were already being subtracted from the world. He thought of Ada's father, whose death was not an accident, though Ada still believed it was, though Tomás carried the truth like a stone he could neither swallow nor set down.

The mountain offers you a way out that costs someone else.

"Yes," Río said aloud, to the dark, to the cornice, to his father. "But sometimes everyone on it has already been spent."

* * *

He built the false trail in the hour before dawn, working fast and working carefully, because a false trail that looks like a false trail is worse than no trail at all.

First he woke the others and moved them — not forward along the shelf, as any pursuer reading their tracks would expect, but *up*, onto the true route, a steep chute on the western wall that climbed past the narrows through broken ice. Hard climbing, slow, exposed to falling seracs for one bad hundred meters. But it kept them off the shelf entirely, and it let him do what needed doing below.

Then he went back down and lied to the snow.

He broke trail along the shelf himself, wearing Ada's spare snowshoes so the prints would carry her stride-length, dragging the empty supply sled so the furrow would read as weight, as cargo, as the whole party committed to the easy line. He wove the track convincingly: a pause where a party would pause, a boot-print angled toward the view, the small honest litter of travel — a scrap of wrapper, a dropped cord, the kind of things tired people shed without knowing. He laid it all the way along the shelf, directly beneath the cornice, and then, at the far end where the ground rose toward the next bend, he made the tracks stop being careful. He lengthened the stride. He let the sled slew sideways. He made six people who had been walking for days finally break into the stumbling urgency of flight — because that was what Vásquez's men would want to see, what they would *need* to see, confirmation that their quarry was rattled and running blind.

At the end of the shelf he turned the trail up a shallow gully, followed it fifty meters, and then — stepping only on bare rock, where no print could live — he descended again and erased himself out of his own story.

Last, the invitation. He climbed to the base of the cornice itself, where the wind had built a smooth ramp of spindrift up against the snow-wall, and he cut steps into it. Six steps. Shallow, hurried-looking, the kind a fleeing party cuts when the open shelf suddenly seems too exposed to snipers and the sheltering wall seems safer. Steps that said: *we left the shelf, we hugged the wall, we are just above you, we are close.*

No one would climb those steps. That was the point. The steps were a sentence, and the sentence ended at the cornice line, and the cornice line was invisible under the night's fresh snowfall, and somewhere above it hung four hundred meters of gravity waiting to be introduced to six armed men.

Río stood at the top of his own trap and looked at what he'd made, and his stomach turned over slowly, like ice shifting in a bergschrund.

"You're a guide," he said to himself. "Remember what you are."

But the wind came down the corridor carrying, faint and far, the sound of boots again — closer now, much closer, an hour maybe — and the sound settled the argument the way arguments on mountains are always settled: by the mountain itself.

* * *

They climbed the western chute in the grey beginning of morning, roped, silent, Ada leading because her engineering eye read ice better than either of them. Halfway up, where the route crossed beneath a leaning serac, she stopped and looked back down at the shelf, at the false trail lying in the new snow like a story told in a language only hunters read.

"They'll follow it," she said. Not a question.

"They'll follow it fast," Río said. "That's the design. Men who think they're catching you don't test the ground."

Tomás, gray-faced, gripping the rope, said: "And when the cornice goes?"

Río watched the light strengthen across the crevasse field below, blue shadows filling with white. "Then the mountain closes another account," he said. "And we don't look back. Whatever we hear — we don't look back."

They climbed on. An hour later, from the height of the chute, they heard the pursuers enter the narrows behind them — voices, the ring of a rifle butt on ice, the confident rhythm of men who believed the hunt was nearly over. Río pressed himself against the wall and counted the sounds across the distance, and beside him Ada's breath came slow and controlled, and Tomás whispered something that might have been a prayer or a curse.

Then the voices reached the shelf. Then the boots found the trail — his trail, his lie, his six strangers' strides and a dragged sled and a scatter of honest litter — and quickened.

Río closed his eyes and gave the mountain its instructions the only way he knew: by listening.

The first rifle shot cracked up the chute and sparked off rock ten meters above Ada's head, herding, hurrying them, driving them upward and away — and that was the mistake, Río realized with a lurch, *their* mistake, because the shot meant the men on the shelf had stopped caring about stealth, had spread out, had stepped off the neat line of the trail—

The sound, when it came, was not loud. That was the terrible thing. After all the thunder of the calving glacier, after the booming of the icefall, the cornice let go almost politely — a soft whump of snow releasing its hold on itself, a long sighing slide — and then the shelf simply ceased to exist, taking its six footsteps and its dragged sled and everything standing on it down into the blue.

The roar arrived afterward, rolling up the chute like a door slamming in the belly of the world. Snow smoke boiled up out of the crevasse field and swallowed the lower corridor whole.

For a moment nobody moved. Then, faint through the settling white, from somewhere on the shelf's upper edge — from ground that had not fallen — a voice. One voice, screaming a name, screaming it over and over into the smoke.

Vásquez had not been on the cornice.

Vásquez was still alive.
