## 15. The Cornice Gives Way

The mountain lied to us about the time.

I had learned to read its hours the way my father taught me — by the color of the ice, by the pitch of the wind coming down the corridor, by the small complaints of the glacier as it settled into itself like an old man into a chair. By those measures we had most of a day left in the calving window. The light was still high and hard. The seracs above the eastern wall stood in their places like a jury that had not yet decided.

But Río kept looking north, toward the low gray smudge on the horizon that I had taken for cloud, and his face was doing arithmetic he did not like.

"That's not weather," he said quietly, when Tomás asked. "That's weather *arriving*."

We did not stop to argue. Behind us — perhaps two kilometers back along the corridor, hidden now behind a shoulder of blue ice — Vásquez's men were following the false trail Río had cut through the night: boot prints and sledge-marks angled up and away from our true line, climbing toward the corniced ridge on the western flank where the snow hung out over nothing like a held breath. He had built the deception with a guide's patience, laying each print twice, dragging the empty sledge so its runners scored convincingly, even scattering a strip of torn canvas where a careless party might have snagged it. It was good work. It was the kind of work that keeps people alive.

I hated how good it was. Because I understood, standing there with my breath freezing in the wool of my balaclava, exactly what we were asking the mountain to do with those men. We were not leading them astray. We were leading them onto a ledge that had no business holding anything heavier than snowlight, and we were hoping — I was hoping; I will not pretend otherwise — that the mountain would finish what we had started.

The morning passed in the rhythm of rope and step. Río led, probing the floor of the corridor with his pole, listening through it the way a doctor listens through a stethoscope. His right hand was still wrapped in its filthy bandage from the icefall, three fingers stiff as candle wax, but he managed the pole anyway, tucking it under the ruined hand and driving it home with the good one. He had not once complained. He had not once looked at the hand. When Tomás tried to help him coil rope, Río took it back without a word and coiled it one-handed, better than Tomás could have done with two.

Tomás walked behind him. My brother had been quiet since the shelter, since the journal, since the telling that had nearly opened a crevasse between us wider than any the glacier owned. He had said everything there was to say — the seam, Andrade's money, our father's refusal, the tunnel, the years of silence — and I had said things back that I meant then and regretted hourly, and now there was only this: the two of us roped together on a river of ice, unable to be more than four meters apart or less than alive.

"Ada." His voice came flat over the wind. "Look at the ridge."

I looked. The cornice on the western shoulder caught the sun and threw it back in a long white blade. Against it, small as punctuation, moved figures. Four of them. Then five. They had found the false trail, and they were trusting it, climbing steadily toward the overhang with the confidence of men who believed they were closing on fugitives instead of walking out onto a lie.

"They'll see the trail ends," Río said. "They'll turn back."

"Do you believe that?" Tomás asked.

Río was silent for a moment. Below us, deep under the ice, the river made its sound — that vast, patient rushing, like the mountain's own heartbeat, like the pulse I had been navigating by since the whiteout took the world. "No," he said. "Vásquez doesn't turn back. That's the thing about him. It's also the thing that's going to kill him."

* * *

It happened at midday, when the sun stood highest and the snow was weakest, and it happened the way avalanches always happen: too fast to stop and too slow to escape.

I heard it before I saw it. A sound like a door opening in the world — a deep exhalation from beneath the ridge, the sound of several tons of overhanging snow deciding, all at once, that it had held long enough. Then the cornice went.

It did not fall so much as *leave*, the whole white lip of the ridge peeling away in a single curving slab, taking the air with it, taking the light. For one suspended second the men on the trail were silhouetted against the sky, absurdly upright, arms out, like figures on a wedding cake. Then the slab tilted beneath them and the mountain swallowed the scene whole.

The report came up the corridor a full ten seconds later — a boom that I felt in my teeth, in the soles of my boots, in the old scar tissue over my heart where my father's death lived. The echo ran up the eastern wall, struck the seracs, and came back changed, doubled, multiplied, until the whole Cordillera seemed to be repeating the news to itself in a hundred voices.

Then: silence. The terrible, ringing silence after.

None of us spoke. We stood roped in a row on the blue ice, three small dark stitches in all that white, and listened to the place where two men had stopped existing. Snow-dust drifted across the corridor in a slow, glittering curtain, settling on our shoulders like ash.

"God," Tomás whispered. It was not prayer. It was horror looking for a word and finding only that one.

"We should keep moving," Río said. But his voice had no iron in it. Even he had not fully believed they would die. You build a trap to turn an animal aside, and when it kills instead, something in you flinches whether you meant it to or not.

I made myself look at the slope. At this distance I could see nothing but the raw scar of the slide, blue ice exposed where the cornice had torn free, and below it a churned field of debris running down into shadow. Two men. They had names, probably. Families, probably. Men who had shot at us, who had hunted us through the high country on behalf of a company that dynamited tunnels full of sleeping workers — but men. The mountain did not care about the difference. That was the lesson my father never wrote down, though he died of it: the mountain does not care about anyone's reasons.

"Vásquez," I said. "Where was Vásquez?"

Río shaded his eyes with his bandaged hand. "Back," he said at last. "He wasn't on the cornice. He was below, at the base of the ridge." A pause. "He watched it happen."

And then, faint across the ice, carried on that strange acoustic channel the corridor made of itself, we heard shouting. One voice. Furious, cracked, human. Not words — just rage, stripped of language by distance, bouncing off the walls of the pass.

"He knows," Tomás said. "He knows it was deliberate."

"Of course he knows," Río said. "That's why he sent them up first. He suspected the trail was bait and he let his own men test it." He said it without contempt, which somehow made it worse. "Now he has nothing left to lose and no reason to pretend. Get ready."

* * *

We moved. There was nothing else. Grief and guilt are luxuries that require a horizon wider than a corridor of closing ice, and ours was narrowing by the hour.

Because the storm front had arrived while we stood mourning. It came down the throat of the pass like smoke poured into a bottle — not clouds so much as a *condition*, a graying of the air, a flattening of every shadow. Within twenty minutes the seracs that had stood all morning like a jury had become suggestions of themselves. Within forty, the far wall of the corridor was a rumor. The temperature fell so fast that the moisture of our breath froze in our eyelashes and the ropes grew stiff and creaking, like rigging on a ship in a killing sea.

"The window," I said to Río. We had stopped in the lee of a pressure ridge, huddled close enough that our words didn't have to fight the wind. "How long?"

He looked north, where the exit chute lay — the lower corridor, the descent, the way out. Then he looked south, back the way we'd come, where somewhere in the failing light a man with rifles and nothing left to lose was deciding what he was willing to walk into.

"With this front? Hours. Maybe six. Maybe less." He pulled his balaclava down and his voice came muffled and strange. "The calving season gave us forty-eight. The storm is taking most of them back."

Behind us, faint but unmistakable, came the crack of a rifle. Not aimed — a signal shot, or a threat, or simply a man announcing to the mountains that he was still alive and still coming. The sound rolled up the corridor and came back wrong, directionless, so that none of us could say where it had been fired from. That was the worst of it. In this whiteness, Vásquez could be anywhere. He could be everywhere.

"He'll follow our real trail now," Tomás said. "There's no false one left to give him."

"No," Río agreed. "There isn't."

I looked at my brother. Somewhere in the last days something had shifted between us — not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever, but a kind of shared weight-bearing, the way two people under one fallen beam learn to lift together or die together. "Then we make the ice decide," I said. "Same as it decided for them."

Tomás flinched. "Ada—"

"I'm not proud of it." The wind tore my words sideways. "But I'm done pretending we're not in a war. Andrade started it when they put dynamite in a tunnel with men sleeping inside it. Vásquez continued it on the timberline. If the mountain takes a side, I'm not going to apologize for which side it takes."

Río said nothing, but he adjusted the rope coil on his shoulder and began walking, and I understood that in his language, that was agreement.

* * *

The corridor narrowed as we went north, the walls leaning in, the ceiling of ice groaning overhead with a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat. The calving window was not merely closing; it was *sealing*. I could hear it happening — the deep internal shifts, the booms from the upper glacier as new ice slumped into the spaces the season had opened, the river beneath us running louder now, fuller, as if it knew it would soon be the only road left.

We traveled by sound and rope-length. The whiteout came down around us like a lid. Ten meters of visibility became five became three, until Tomás, on the middle of the rope, was a gray ghost and Río at the lead was only a voice calling "Step — step — hold," in the cadence of a man reading scripture. Once I stumbled into a hidden hollow and the rope snapped tight and Tomás's arm hauled me back from whatever waited below, and neither of us said anything about it afterward, because there was nothing to say that the rope hadn't already said.

Somewhere behind us, another shot. Closer. Or maybe not closer — maybe just the acoustics lying again. In the corridor, sound came from everywhere. The mountain was playing both sides against each other now, giving our position to Vásquez and his to us, impartial as gravity.

Then Río stopped so suddenly that I walked into him.

Ahead, where the corridor should have opened into the lower chute, there was — nothing. Not darkness. *Whiteness.* A wall of fresh avalanche debris, smooth and featureless, plugging the passage from wall to wall like a cork in a bottle. New snow, minutes old, still steaming faintly with the friction of its own arrival.

The exit was gone.

For a moment nobody moved. The wind screamed thinly over the top of the plug. Under my boots, patient as ever, the buried river went on rushing toward a valley I could no longer see.

"It'll open," Río said at last, and his voice was steady in the way that cost him something. "The river's cutting under it. Give it an hour, maybe two, and it'll undermine the plug and drop it into the chute. I've seen it before." He turned. Through the slit of his balaclava his eyes found mine. "But an hour is exactly what we don't have. Because Vásquez doesn't need the chute. He only needs us to be here when it opens — or he needs us frozen, which is faster."

I pressed my mittened hand against the avalanche plug. It was warm-ish, almost alive, still settling, still deciding what it wanted to become. Somewhere beyond it, downhill, was survival. Somewhere behind us, uphill, was a man with a rifle and a grief of his own now, two dead men heavy in his wake, and no more pretense between us.

The rifle spoke again. This time there was no mistaking it. The bullet struck the ice wall beside us and threw up a fan of glittering shards that rang off my goggles like thrown gravel.

"Down!" Río roared, and we dropped behind the pressure ridge as the second shot cracked past, and the third, methodical, unhurried, a man who had all the time in the world because he intended to take ours.

I flattened myself against the ice with my brother's breath ragged beside me and Río's ruined hand fumbling for his axe, and above us the storm closed the last of the light, and behind us the footsteps came on — crunch, crunch, crunch — slow as a clock counting down the calving window, and I understood with terrible clarity that the mountain had finished choosing sides.

It had chosen the river. And the river was still an hour away.
