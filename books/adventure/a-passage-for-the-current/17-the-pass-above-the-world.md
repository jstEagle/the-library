## 17. The Pass Above the World

The corridor opened without warning.

One moment they were in the blue throat of the glacier, walls of ancient ice pressing close enough that Río's pack scraped both sides, and the next the light changed — not brighter, exactly, but *wider*, as though the sky had leaned down to look at them. Ada stopped walking. Ahead, the chute tilted upward at an angle that made her engineer's eye calculate grades and loads and the sheer improbability of what they were about to do, and above that angle the ice simply ended, cut clean as a survey line, and beyond the cut was nothing but air and altitude and a light so thin and silver it seemed to come from inside her own skull.

"The exit chute," Río said behind her. His voice was rough. He had not spoken more than a dozen words since the whiteout, since the three of them had come through the dark roped blind and listening, and his frostbitten hand was bound in a sling of bandage and canvas that Tomás had fashioned from a tent fly. "Fifteen minutes. Then we're above the world."

Ada looked back at her brother. Tomás's face was gray with cold and something else, something he had carried out of the frozen shelter and through the storm and still had not set down. He met her eyes and held them, and she understood that he was waiting for her to say *go*, that whatever else lay broken between them, he would not take another step on this mountain without her word.

"Go," she said, and it came out steady, and she was grateful for that, because inside her chest there was a sound like a train crossing a high trestle, iron and wind and the long vibration of something that could not be taken back.

They climbed.

The chute was steep enough that they kicked steps, front-pointing in the old style Río had taught her father, and the ice here was different — older, denser, shot through with veins of mineral blue so dark they looked like writing. Ada found herself reading them without meaning to, the way she read rail gauges and bridge loads, the way her father had read mountains: as texts, as arguments, as promises the earth had made and then, for its own reasons, sealed away. *This is why they want it closed,* she thought, driving her axe into the blue. *Not because of what's up here. Because of what up here proves.*

The last twenty meters were a ramp of wind-carved snow. She crested it on her knees, breath tearing, and then the wind hit her full in the face and she stood up into it and forgot, entirely, how to breathe.

The summit shelf was not a peak. It was a room — a vast, level terrace of wind-scoured ice and bare gray stone, perhaps two hundred meters across, cradled between walls of rock that rose on three sides like the ribs of some buried cathedral. And open to the south, open to everything, the world fell away in terraces of cloud and distance: the valley, the river, the dark green stitch of the old rail line she could trace by memory even at this distance, running north toward the closed tunnels and the closed towns and the twenty years of silence her father's map had tried to end.

But it was not the valley that stopped her heart. It was the range.

The Cordillera del Viento did not end at the pass. It *began* there. Range beyond range unfolded to the north and west, peak after unnamed peak, their faces glazed with ice, their valleys holding lakes the color of hammered tin, and not one of them — not one — appeared on any map Ada had ever seen. The government charts showed blank space. The consortium maps showed blank space. Even her father's map, the folded ghost riding against her chest inside its oilcloth, showed only the pass and the line of the river, a single thread through a country it had only glimpsed.

The whole secret range. Standing in the wind with her axe still in her hand, Ada understood that she was looking at the largest blank space left on the continent, and that she was the first person in twenty years to stand here and see it, and that the reason no one else had stood here was written in dynamite and money and her father's blood.

"He stood here." Her voice was torn sideways by the wind, and she said it again, louder, not because anyone needed to hear but because the mountain should. "He stood *here*."

She found the proof without looking for it. Near the eastern wall, half-drifted, a cairn of stones — small, deliberate, the work of one pair of hands. She crossed to it and knelt and lifted the top stones away, and beneath them, flat and gray and fused with frost, was a survey plate. Brass. Her father's brass, with the initials E.Q. stamped at the rim where he had stamped everything, where he had stamped her lunchbox and her first compass and the lid of the box where he kept the maps of closed lines. The plate was set in the stone as if the stone had grown around it, twenty years of freeze and thaw gripping it like a fist.

He had stood on this exact square of the world, driven his marker into it, and looked out at exactly what she was looking at. And then he had gone down the mountain and died in a tunnel collapse that was not a collapse, and no one had ever come up to take the reading he had left waiting.

Ada pressed her bare hand flat against the brass until the cold burned, until she could feel her own pulse hammering against his initials, and she did not say anything else, because there was nothing left to say that the plate did not already know.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the eastern wall, in a hollow of stone that Río said had sheltered her father's party and would shelter them, and while Tomás worked the stove with his one good hand and his teeth, Ada walked the shelf.

It was the light that did it. At this altitude the afternoon came in low and long, and every surface — the ice walls, the stone, the fields of wind-carved snow — caught it and threw it back doubled, so that walking the shelf felt like walking through the inside of a lantern. She counted what there was to count, because counting was how she prayed. Eleven frozen lakes visible in the northern valleys, holding the light like coins. Three ridgelines of virgin rock, unclimbed, unnamed. The river, far below, a bright wire stitched through the valley floor, running from the glacier's snout all the way down to the sea — the river her father's map had been drawn for, the river she had promised herself, in the dark of the icefall and the white of the storm, would carry that map home.

And below — below and south and close, terribly close — the staging camp.

She saw it before she understood it: a scatter of rectangles in the lower bowl where the glacier met the valley moraine, too regular for rock, too organized for ruin. She raised Tomás's binoculars, the ones he had carried through his disgrace and his drinking and his years of exile the way she carried the map, and the camp leapt into focus.

Quonset huts. Fuel bladders, drab and swollen. A tracked vehicle under tarpaulin, and beside it the long straight scar of a graded road that ended at the mountain's knee, built halfway and abandoned, waiting. Generator exhaust feathered from one hut. Figures moved between the buildings — she counted nine, then lost count — and on a pole at the camp's center hung a flag she could not read at this distance and did not need to. No government flew a flag over a camp that was not on any government's chart. No government graded a road to nowhere and left it half-built, patient as a trap, waiting for spring.

Waiting for the calving season to end. Waiting for the pass to seal. Waiting for three exhausted climbers on the summit shelf to become a rumor, and then a mistake, and then nothing at all.

"They're not retreating," Tomás said at her shoulder. She had not heard him cross the ice; his boots made no sound on the wind-scoured stone. He took the binoculars when she offered them and studied the camp for a long time, and when he lowered them his face had gone to the gray of the rock itself. "That's a two-year camp. Maybe three. Fuel, timber, a road bed. They're not chasing us, Ada. They're *moving in*. The chase is just — housekeeping."

"Housekeeping." The word tasted like iron filings.

"Vásquez's men are behind us in the corridor. Those men are below us with vehicles and a radio. And we are standing on the only flat ground for fifty miles, at the top of a dead end, with one working hand between the three of us." He said it flatly, the way he read contours, the way he had once falsified contours for money, and she heard under the flatness the thing he was not saying: *I have done their arithmetic before. I know how it ends.*

Ada turned back to the view, because she could not look at her brother and the camp at the same time and keep her hands steady.

The valley lay below her in the long gold light, and it was not empty. Smoke rose from the town of Vado Seco, thin and domestic. Fields stitched the river terraces. She could see, or believed she could see, the schoolhouse roof, the church, the little bridge where she had once fished with her father while he talked about the northern line the way other men talked about God — *someday, Ada, someday the trains will run up that valley and no one will have to be poor to live somewhere beautiful.* The valley did not know what was coming for it. The valley thought the blank space on the map was empty, and blank space was safe, and spring was just spring.

The map rode against her chest. She could feel it there, the oilcloth slick and cold, the paper inside holding every line her father had ever drawn: the pass, the grade, the tunnel alignments, the mineral seam he had found and refused to sell, the route that would bring the trains north and the truth with them — or, in other hands, the route that would bring the consortium, the road, the dynamite, the end of every blank space left in the world.

One document. One folded square of paper, older than her grief. It could save the valley. It could also, in the wrong courtroom or the wrong newspaper or the wrong pair of hands, sign the death warrants of everyone who had helped them — Río's village, the muleteers, the boy who had sold them grain at the last settlement — because a map was not just a proof, a map was an *invitation*, and invitations could be accepted by anyone.

Or she could burn it. Here, on the summit where her father had stood, feed it to the stove flame and let the secret range stay secret, let the consortium's road die unfinished, let the mountains keep what the mountains had taken. And Delgado's photographs — the proof of the dynamited tunnel, the proof of murder — would stay buried in a crevasse cache, and her father would stay a man who died in an accident, and Tomás would stay a liar, and she would stay a woman who came down off the mountain with nothing but frostbite and a story no one could print.

Save the valley, or save the truth. Carry the map down, or let the mountain have it.

"You're doing the thing," Tomás said quietly.

"What thing."

"Where you decide everything alone and then tell us afterward." He did not look at her. He watched the camp below, and the wind pulled at his collar. "I did that for fifteen years. I know the weight of it. You don't have to carry it the way he did."

"He didn't carry it alone," Ada said. "That was the problem. He tried to hand it to Andrade and Andrade handed him back a collapsed tunnel." She turned to face her brother, and the low light caught them both, and for a moment she saw him as he had been at nineteen, hunched over a drawing table, brilliant and hungry and so easily bought. "I don't know what to do with it, Tomás. That's the truth. I've known since the icefall that I don't know. Every road off this shelf ends in someone's grave."

Below them, the light was failing. The shadows of the western walls crept across the shelf like a closing hand, and the temperature fell so fast she could hear it — the ice singing its thin night song in the walls around them, a sound like struck glass, like a held breath. In the staging camp, lamps were coming on. Nine lights. Then twelve. A radio mast she hadn't seen before stood up black against the last of the sky.

And then, from the southern corridor — the corridor they had come through, the corridor the calving had opened for them — a sound.

Not ice. Not wind. A crack, flat and small and human, carried up the throat of the glacier and out onto the shelf, and it was followed by another, and then by a voice, faint and distorted by the stone, shouting orders in a cadence Ada had heard her whole life on rail crews and work gangs and every site where men moved heavy things on a schedule.

Río came up from the hollow at a limping run, his bandaged hand pressed to his chest, and his face in the dying light was the face of a man reading a river he did not like.

"They're in the corridor," he said. "Three of them, maybe four, moving fast. They'll reach the shelf by first light — the ice is bonded at night, it'll hold them." He stopped, and his breath came out in a long silver flag, and he looked from Ada to the camp below to the map at her chest, and he did not ask the question. He had guided her father. He knew what a surveyor's last plate looked like, and he knew what it cost, and he knew that whatever she was going to do, she was going to do it before the sun came up.

Ada looked north, at the blank ranges going violet in the dusk, at the lakes holding the last light like a hand holds water. She looked south, at the corridor where the voices were climbing. She put her hand over the map, over the oilcloth, over her father's folded twenty years, and the wind came across the shelf from the open south and pushed against her like a current pushing against a bridge pier, and she stood into it the way she had been taught, the way she had been born, reading the load.

"Then we don't wait for first light," she said. "Get the rope. We're moving."
