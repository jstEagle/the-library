## 18. Terms of the Descent

The descent chute opened below us like a throat.

I had known this mountain for thirty years, and I had never seen it from here — no one had, not in living memory, because the pass above the world kept its own counsel and only opened its mouth during calving season, and even then it swallowed more than it gave back. But there it was: a corridor of blue ice narrowing toward a shelf of scree, and beyond the scree, faint as a promise, the grey-green smudge of timberline. The valley. The world of men.

Ada stood at the lip with her father's map pressed flat against her chest, both arms wrapped around it like a mother holding a child against wind. Tomás was on one knee, retying his bootlace for the third time without tightening anything — his hands had been shaking since the icefall, though he'd say it was the cold. Behind us, down the corridor we'd crossed, the glacier groaned and settled, still digesting what the calving had torn loose.

And below us, spread across the scree shelf in a loose crescent, were nine men with rifles.

They had come up through the corridor behind us faster than I thought possible. Vásquez drove them the way a drover drives cattle — with fear behind and hunger ahead — and they had gained on us through the night while we slept in the frozen shelter, believing ourselves ahead of the storm instead of racing it. Now they stood between us and the descent, breath rising in white columns, boots planted on stone that would be under forty feet of new ice by spring.

"Señor Salazar." Vásquez's voice carried up the slope without effort, pitched the way a man pitches his voice when he has done this before, in other places, to other cornered people. "You remember me."

"I remember your kind," I called back. "You all sound the same at altitude."

A ripple of laughter went through his men, quickly strangled. Vásquez didn't smile. He climbed halfway up the slope alone, rifle slung across his back, hands open and empty, and stopped where the ice began — a careful man, my grandfather would have said, who knew exactly where solid ground ended.

"Engineer Quintana," he said, and now his voice changed, softened into something almost gentle, which was worse. "You've been through hell. All of you. The guide's hand is frostbitten black under those wrappings — don't look surprised, I can see how he holds it. Your brother can barely stand. You have maybe six hours before that window closes, and if it closes with you inside this corridor, no one will ever find so much as a button." He spread his hands. "I am offering you the only way down that doesn't end in the ice."

"You're offering nothing," Ada said. "You're standing in the way and calling it a road."

"I'm offering terms." He took a folded paper from inside his coat, and even from twenty meters I could see it was written out fair, in ink, signed — a document, prepared in advance, which meant he had always expected to find us alive. That told me something. Men who expect corpses don't carry contracts. "Surrender the map and everything Delgado's team recovered. In exchange: safe passage down, employment if you want it, silence guaranteed by the same paper that guarantees your lives. There is money attached. Enough money that your brother never has to draw another map for anyone but himself."

Tomás flinched. I saw it go through him like a current through wire.

"And if she refuses?" I asked.

Vásquez turned his head and looked at me for the first time, and I understood why men followed him into places like this. His eyes were patient. Patience is the most frightening thing a violent man can have.

"Then we wait," he said simply. "We are nine. You are three, and one of you is half-frozen. The window closes. We have tents, fuel, food. We will stand here very politely until the mountain does our work for us, and then we will walk down through the corridor after it seals, and report — truthfully, every word — that we found three surveyors lost above the calving line. A tragedy. The Cordillera is famous for them." He paused. "Or. The map comes to me, and everyone walks down together, rich and alive, and in ten years none of us remembers this slope."

* * *

The wind came up the chute then, carrying the smell of deep ice — that mineral coldness, older than any nation, older than any company. Below us the scree ticked and shifted as the day's warmth let go of it. Somewhere beneath our feet, muffled by a hundred meters of glacier, the river ran. I put my good hand flat against the ice and felt it: the pulse of it, the push. The water didn't care about contracts. It never had.

My grandfather guided men through these mountains before there were rails or republics worth the name, and he taught me one lesson above all others: when a man corners you, he has already told you what he fears. You only have to listen.

Vásquez feared the season.

He had said it himself, standing there with his open hands and his prepared contract — *the window closes, the corridor seals.* Nine armed men, and every one of them glancing up-slope every few minutes, the way you glance at a dog that has bitten once. They had raced the calving to catch us, and now they were caught in the same hourglass, and the sand was falling faster for them than for us, because they had to go down through territory they did not know, on a glacier that was coming apart at the seams, in the dark, with a storm front building behind the western peaks that I could smell the way an old horse smells rain.

And there was one thing more. One thing Vásquez could not know, because no map showed it and no survey had ever recorded it, because it lived only in the memory of men who had guided this range before the maps came.

The lower chute — the one they meant to descend tomorrow, the easy way, the way their staging camp below expected them — did not exist anymore. Not as a route. Three nights ago, in the first great calving, the whole eastern face of the glacier had let go, and what remained was a wall of rotten seracs leaning over the chute like a held breath. Any party descending it in the next forty-eight hours would bring that breath down on their heads. I had seen it from the ridge. I had said nothing, because at the time I thought only we might live long enough to need the knowledge.

Now I looked at Vásquez, and I decided how much of it to spend.

"You're right about one thing," I said, walking forward until I stood beside Ada, close enough that she could hear me low, under the wind. "The window closes. For all of us." I raised my voice. "But you've made an error in your arithmetic, señor. You're counting on the lower chute. The route your camp below is watching. The one your maps show."

Something moved behind Vásquez's eyes. Small. Careful men hate being told they've made errors.

"That chute is closed," I said. "It died three nights ago, in the first calving. The east face came down and left the whole approach hanging in rotten ice. You send nine men down it, you'll bury them all, and your consortium will spend next summer digging for its guns." I let that sit. "There is one way off this mountain tonight, and it's the way I take them. Down the western rib, over the shoulder, into the valley by dawn — if we start now, all of us, and if nobody wastes an hour on bargaining."

"You expect me to believe a guide's word over my own surveyors' maps."

"Your surveyors' maps are why Delgado is dead," I said. "Ask yourself why the government team came this way at all. Ask yourself what they found that was worth killing for. And then ask yourself whether men who falsify a map once will hesitate to falsify it twice." I nodded down-slope, toward his waiting rifles. "You want the map because the map is true. That's the whole of it, señor. Everything else you've said on this slope is weather."

Vásquez stood very still. Above us, far up the corridor, the glacier spoke — a long, deep concussion, like a door closing in a house the size of the sky. Every man on the scree flinched except Vásquez. But his men looked at each other, and I watched the doubt move through them the way wind moves through wheat. They had heard the chute described. They knew what rotten ice sounded like when it lets go. And they had families, most of them; you could see it in the way they stood, the way they kept their weight ready to run.

"The western rib is suicide," Vásquez said, but his voice had lost its floor.

"The western rib is steep," I answered. "Suicide is staying here arguing with me while the mountain makes up its mind." I pulled my knife and cut a strip from the spare rope at my belt and held it up where he could see it. "Here are my terms, since we're trading. You keep your distance — fifty meters, no closer, all the way down. Rifles slung. If anyone fires, anyone, I stop walking, and I tell you plainly: I would rather die on this ice than lead your men one meter further, and dead guides give very bad directions. You follow my voice or you follow the storm. Those are the choices. There is no third."

"And the map?"

"The map goes where the engineer decides," I said. "That was not part of any bargain, and it never will be. You can have her silence, her brother's testimony buried, your names clean — she'll give you that, won't you, Ada? Because she wants to live too. But the paper her father drew stays hers until this mountain is behind us. After that—" I shrugged, and the shrug cost me, my frostbitten hand screaming inside its wrappings, and I let them see the cost. "After that, negotiate with her. She's better at it than I am."

Ada's eyes met mine. There was a question in them — *can you do this, can you hold this line* — and I gave her back the answer I have given very few people in my life, which was yes.

Vásquez looked at his men. His men looked at the glacier. Up-corridor, the ice boomed again, longer this time, and a thread of powder snow lifted off the eastern wall and hung in the still air like smoke from a fuse.

"Forty meters," Vásquez said at last. "Rifles slung. We follow." He almost smiled, and it was the ugliest thing I saw that day. "But understand me, Salazar. When we reach the valley, this conversation resumes. Nothing on this mountain is settled. Nothing is forgiven."

"Nothing on this mountain is ever settled," I agreed. "That's what it's for."

* * *

We started down within the hour, and the mountain began closing behind us before we had gone a kilometer.

I led with my good hand on the rope, calling the route in a low carrying voice — *left of the pressure ridge, mind the crevasse bridge, step where I step* — and behind me came Ada, then Tomás, then the long ragged line of Vásquez's men moving like penitents, rifles slung, heads down, fifty meters back exactly, because fear keeps distances better than discipline does. The light was going copper and then violet. The storm front I had smelled all afternoon was now visible, a bruise-colored wall shouldering over the western peaks, and it was coming down the corridor faster than any of us.

Twice the glacier calved while we descended — great thunderclaps somewhere above and behind, the mountain shedding its skin, and each time the whole column stopped and leaned into the slope and waited for the vibration to pass through the ice beneath our boots. The second time, a section of the corridor wall we had walked past an hour earlier simply wasn't there anymore. Tomás stared at the fresh scar of it, blue ice gleaming wet and raw, and said nothing, but I saw him do the arithmetic every man in that line was doing: *that could have been us; that will be someone.*

Near dusk we reached the shoulder of the western rib, and below us, for one clear moment before the storm ate it, lay the valley — black-green forest going blue with evening, a thread of woodsmoke, the far glint of the river running south toward Puerto Frío like a wire of hammered tin. Home. The world. I heard Tomás make a small sound, half laugh, half sob, and I understood him completely.

Then the front arrived, and the world went white, and the temperature fell so fast the air itself seemed to crackle.

"Fifty meters becomes nothing!" I shouted back through the wind, to Vásquez, to all of them. "In this, we rope together or we die separately! Your choice, señor — but choose fast!"

For a long moment there was only the storm roaring, and I thought: *he'll refuse, pride will kill them all, the mountain will take its tithe.* Then, out of the white, his voice came, stripped of everything smooth, just a man's voice now, cold and ordinary and afraid:

"Rope us to your people, Salazar. And God help you if this is a trick."

"It's not a trick," I said, tying the first knot with fingers I could no longer feel. "It's a mountain. Tricks are beneath both of us."

We tied the two parties into one line — enemies sharing a single rope, which is either the beginning of peace or the perfect shape of a trap, depending on the hour and the heart. Ada came last, the map sealed in oilcloth against her body, and as she knotted herself onto the line she leaned close to me, her lips at my ear, her words nearly torn away:

"Río. The river. Where does the map go — if it goes to the water?"

I looked down into the storm, where beneath a hundred meters of ice the meltwater torrent was already gathering strength for spring, already pulling at the roots of the glacier, already running — patient, ancient, unbuyable — toward the sea.

"To the sea," I said. "And no one has ever bought the sea."

She nodded once, and stepped off the shoulder into the white, and the mountain took all of us — the guilty and the grieving, the armed and the unarmed, the map and the men who wanted it — down into the storm together, and the glacier behind us calved again, sealing the high pass forever, like a book closing on its final page.
