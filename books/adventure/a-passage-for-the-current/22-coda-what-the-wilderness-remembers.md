## 22. Coda: What the Wilderness Remembers

The girl arrives in Puerto Frío in the last week of February, when the river runs high and brown with snowmelt and the town smells of wet creosote and cut timber. Río sees her from the doorway of the stationmaster's office, standing on the platform with a rucksack too large for her shoulders and a brass theodolite case held against her chest the way other people hold infants. She is twenty-three, maybe twenty-four. She has the particular stillness of someone who has already learned that the mountains do not care how well you have prepared, and she has come anyway.

Her name is Inés Valderrama. She has a letter of introduction from the Ministry, and she has questions.

Río has been guiding for thirty-one years. He has taken geologists up the green valleys of the south, taken birdwatchers to see the condors ride the thermals off the eastern scree, taken a widow once, all the way to the lake where her husband drowned, because she asked him to and he could not find it in himself to refuse. He is sixty now. His right hand aches in cold weather — three fingers that curl a half-degree short of straight, that will never again feel the difference between silk and rope. He has stopped explaining the hand to strangers. He lets them assume frostbite, which is true, and lets them assume accident, which is also true, and lets them assume nothing deeper, because the deeper thing is not for sale and never was.

But Inés Valderrama has questions, and her questions have a shape he recognizes. He saw that shape once in a woman standing in a rail office in Puerto Frío, holding a government contract in one hand and her father's map in the other, and he knows that the shape does not go away. It only waits.

* * *

They leave on a Tuesday, upriver, the way Río has gone a thousand times and will go a thousand more. The mules are named for saints, as his mules always are, and the young one — Tomás, because Tomás had named it, laughing, the year before he finally stopped drinking — carries the tents and the survey gear and does not complain, which makes it better company than its namesake ever was at that age.

The valley opens the way it always opens: slowly, then all at once, the walls falling back to reveal the meadows where the horses of the old estancia went feral and stayed feral, the stands of lenga turning gold at the edges of the season, the river running beside them the whole way like a conversation neither of them can leave.

Inés talks. Río lets her. She is writing a thesis, she says, on the historical hydrology of the northern cordillera — how the rivers were mapped, who mapped them, what the maps got wrong. She has found references in the Ministry archive to a survey from thirty years ago, a team under one Delgado, whose final report was never filed. There is a gap in the record, she says. A clean gap. The kind of gap that someone has swept.

Río listens with his eyes on the trail.

"You were there," she says, on the third day. Not a question. She has done her reading. "Your name is in the supply manifests. Guide, R. Salazar. And then nothing. The whole expedition just — stops."

"Rivers stop," Río says. "At lakes. At falls. At the sea." He adjusts the pack on the lead mule, though it does not need adjusting. "Then they start again somewhere else."

She is quiet for a long time after that. She is learning, he thinks. Learning that the mountains give answers at their own pace, and that a guide who has walked this valley for thirty years is not being cruel when he says nothing. He is being kind. There are things he could tell her that would save her years of searching, and there are things he could tell her that would take the searching away entirely, and the second kind is a theft worse than the first.

* * *

On the fifth day they come up out of the treeline, and Río stops, and Inés stops beside him, and neither of them speaks for a while, because the Cordillera del Viento is there in front of them the way it is always there — sudden, total, indifferent — a wall of ice and black rock running north to south as far as the eye can follow, its summits wrapped in the long white banners of cloud that never quite leave.

The pass is not visible. That is the first thing everyone looks for and the first thing no one finds. There is no pass. There is only the wall, seamless as the day the world was made, and the glacier at its foot, blue-green in its crevasses, holding everything it has ever taken.

"It's sealed," Inés says. She has read the geological reports. She knows the calving event of thirty years ago restructured the whole face of the ice, closed the corridor, dropped a hundred thousand tonnes of serac across the high route. "Permanently, they say. No viable crossing. The surveys all agree."

"The surveys agree," Río says, and lets it sit.

He does not tell her that surveys can be made to agree with anything, if the money is right. He does not tell her that he once stood on the summit shelf above the world and looked down into a valley no map has ever carried, and that he watched a woman kneel at the edge of a meltwater torrent and let a piece of paper — her father's whole life, drawn in ink and grief — go into the current, and that the river took it the way the river takes everything, without malice, without ceremony, the way a mother takes a child who has finally come home.

He does not tell her that the consortium's men came down the mountain two days later with nothing, and that Vásquez — if that was even his name — stood at the trailhead in the rain and looked at Río for a long moment, and that Río looked back, and that neither of them said anything, because there was nothing left to negotiate. The mountain had ruled. The mountain always rules.

He does not tell her that Ada Quintana is alive, that she lives now in the capital and signs her letters with the old railroad seal, that she and her brother speak every Sunday by telephone, that Tomás Quintana's sworn testimony reopened the investigation and put three men in prison and buried a company and put a plaque on a wall in Puerto Frío that Inés has certainly walked past without reading. All of that is public. All of that is hers to find. But the rest —

The rest belongs to the river.

* * *

They make camp that night in the shelter of a moraine, and Río builds the fire small, the way he always does above the treeline, and Inés sits across from him with her notebook and does not write in it, which he takes as a kind of respect.

"My grandfather worked the northern lines," she says, after a while. "Before they closed. He used to say the railroad was the only honest thing the government ever built up here. Then the lines closed, and nobody would say why, and he went quiet about it for the rest of his life." She looks at the fire. "He died when I was nineteen. I found his old pay stubs in a tin box. There was a bonus payment, the year before the closures. From a company I've never been able to trace."

"Consorcio Andrade," Río says.

Her head comes up.

"It doesn't exist anymore," he says. "It stopped existing the same year the survey stopped existing. Companies are like rivers that way too." He feeds a stick to the fire. "Your grandfather took their money. A lot of men took their money. It wasn't always a sin. Sometimes it was just winter coming, and a man has to eat."

"Did you?"

"No." He says it without pride, because there is no pride in it. He simply had nothing they wanted, back then, except the trail knowledge in his head, and they had tried to buy that too, and he had said no, and the saying of it had cost him a hand and very nearly cost him everything else. "But I knew men who did. And I knew one man who didn't, and the difference between them is under that ice." He nods north, toward the wall, toward the glacier, toward the place where Sebastián Quintana's bones lie in the crevasse where the consortium left him, in the tunnel they dynamited, in the dark they thought was final.

Inés watches him. She is smart — smarter than the Ministry letter suggests, smarter than her careful questions have let on. He can see her assembling things. He can see the gap in the record opening in her mind like a crevasse of its own.

"Señor Salazar," she says. "What happened to the Delgado team?"

And Río looks at her — at the firelight on her young face, at the theodolite case beside her, at the whole long hunger of her — and he tells her the only thing he can tell her, the thing the river allows.

"The river happened to them," he says. "And the river kept them. And some things, Inés Valderrama, are kept better than they are found."

* * *

She does not sleep well that night, and neither does he, though his sleeplessness is the old familiar kind, the kind that comes when the mountain is near. He lies in his bag and listens to the glacier talking to itself in the dark — that deep, grinding, patient language, older than any country, older than any company — and he thinks about the map.

Thirty years, and he still thinks about the map.

He has never told anyone what it looked like going into the water. How the current took it flat at first, holding it up, letting it shine there for one impossible second — the closed lines, the dotted routes, the handwriting of a dead man — and then folded it under, and rolled it, and pulled it down into the green, and carried it away into the mountain's own plumbing, into the melt-channels and the silt and the long cold dark where the river is born.

Ada had cried. He remembered that. She had not made a sound, but the crying had been in her shoulders, in the way her whole body shook once, hard, like a horse shivering off rain. And then she had stood up, and wiped her face with the back of her glove, and turned to face Vásquez and his rifles with nothing in her hands at all, and she had said — Río has never forgotten the words, has carried them thirty years the way the glacier carries its stones —

"You can't buy what's already spent."

The ice had failed under Vásquez four minutes later. The river had ruled. The river always rules.

And somewhere under all that ice — this is the thought that comes to him now, as it comes every year on this night, in this place — somewhere under the seracs and the blue pressure and the hundred metres of accumulating snow, the map is still moving. Slower now. Slower every year, as the deep ice stiffens toward the bedrock. But moving. The whole glacier moves, has always moved, a river of a different kind, and everything it takes it carries, and everything it carries it delivers, in its own century, in its own way, to whatever sea the ice eventually becomes.

Maybe in a hundred years the front of the glacier will calve into some lake that does not yet exist, and the map will come out of the ice face in a block of blue glass, and sink, and lie on the bottom, and some fish will shelter in its folded shadow. Maybe the ink will have faded to nothing. Maybe the paper will have become part of the ice, and the ice part of the water, and the water part of everything.

Maybe that is what a map is for, finally. Not to be read. To be become.

* * *

In the morning they turn back. There is nothing above the treeline for a surveyor of rivers but ice, and Río has told her that plainly, and she has accepted it with a grace that reminds him painfully of Ada — the same way of folding a disappointment and putting it away, to be opened later, in private, and examined.

They come down through the lenga forest in the gold of late afternoon, and the river is beside them again, running high and loud with the season's melt, and Inés walks a little apart, and he knows she is thinking. He lets her.

At the ford, where the trail crosses a side-channel, she stops. She crouches at the water's edge, and dips her hand in — the way surveyors do, the way her whole profession does, testing the thing it measures — and the cold of it makes her breath catch, and she laughs, surprised, and shakes her hand dry.

"It's fast," she says. "Where does all of it go?"

Río stands beside her and looks up the valley, at the wall of the cordillera going pink and then violet in the last light, at the glacier at its foot holding its long silence, at the pass that is not there and the people who are in it and the paper that is under it, travelling.

"Down," he says. "Eventually. Everything goes down, and everything arrives." He turns his horse toward the trail. "The river knows the way. It's the only map that's never been wrong."

She follows him across the ford, and the water rises white around the mules' legs, and on the far bank the trail leads south, toward Puerto Frío, toward the trains, toward the lowlands where the records wait and the questions wait and the long slow work of truth goes on the way it always goes on — not in revelations, but in pay stubs, in testimony, in the patient testimony of a brother who finally told everything, in a plaque on a wall that a young surveyor will one day stop and read.

Río does not look back at the mountain. He never looks back. The mountain does not need his looking. It keeps what it keeps — the tunnel, the seam, the bones, the ink, the whole buried argument between greed and the world — and it keeps it the way only the wilderness knows how to keep things: completely, without malice, until the season comes when keeping ends and the water carries everything, everything, down.

Behind them, very far away, deep in the wall of ice, something settles with a sound like a door closing — or like a door opening, in a house where no one is left to tell the difference.

Inés turns at the sound. "What was that?"

Río rides on, and the river answers for him, all the way down.
