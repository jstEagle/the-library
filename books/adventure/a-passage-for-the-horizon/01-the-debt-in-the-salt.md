## 1. The Debt in the Salt

The thief's tracks told me everything I needed to know, and one thing I didn't want to.

Wren Corvane ran light. That was the first thing. Even hauling a satchel heavy enough to hang a man for, the prints barely dented the salt crust — a heel-strike here, a toe-drag there, the signature of someone who'd learned to move across bad ground by treating it as a partner instead of an enemy. I'd been reading tracks on these flats for eleven years, since my father handed me his compass and told me the desert keeps honest books if you know how to read them. Wren Corvane kept dishonest books. The stride lengthened and shortened without rhythm, doubled back twice over bare pan where no print would show, once cut a wide arc through a field of shattered crust just to cross a stretch of soft mud that would hold a print like wax holds a seal.

Clever. But clever leaves marks too, if you're patient enough to find them.

I wasn't patient. I was angry. There's a difference, and the flats punish it.

Three days out of Meridian Station, chasing a warrant with my name inked at the bottom and a bounty notice flapping in my saddlebag, I'd let the anger set my pace instead of the sun. By noon the light went flat and white and killed every shadow on the pan, and I lost the trail twice in an hour, and each time I found it again I was running harder, not smarter. My horse — a rangy dun named Tern who deserved better than me — lathered up and started refusing the broken ground where the crust sounded hollow underfoot. I gave her water from my own canteen and hated the thief a little more for it.

The warrant said theft of survey materials from the Territorial Office. Charts, mostly. Sealed folios. The clerk who'd filed the report had used the word *irreplaceable* three times, which in my experience meant either genuinely priceless or bureaucratically embarrassing, and I hadn't yet decided which. What I knew was that Wren Corvane had walked into the Office on a supply day, smiled at the right people, and walked out with a canvas case that weighed less than a rifle and was worth, apparently, more than the whole eastern district's annual budget.

And what I knew beyond that — what nobody at the Office knew, because I'd never told them — was that I recognized the name. Corvane. It had surfaced once before, years back, in my father's letters. A name he wrote carefully, the way you write about fire.

By late afternoon the horizon began to do something wrong.

I noticed it before I understood it. The light ahead had a sheen to it, a wet silver that didn't belong to salt. Then the smell reached me — brine and iodine and something older, something like the inside of a shell — and I crested a low rise of crusted sand and stopped Tern so hard she sidestepped.

The sea was there.

Not the sea itself — I'd never seen the sea, not this far inland, nobody had — but its ghost. A vast shallow sheet of water lying across the lowest reach of the flats, stretching to a horizon that shimmered and broke like heat-haze made liquid. Except it wasn't heat-haze. It moved. Slow, tidal, breathing against the shore of salt with a sound like a sleeping animal turning over.

The maps said there was no water within two hundred miles. The maps, I was beginning to understand, were old in ways nobody had bothered to check.

And then the water began to leave.

There was no drama to it. No wave, no roar. The sea simply drew back — steadily, hugely, the way a held breath releases — peeling off the flats in widening ribbons of gleaming mud. Fish flopped in the shrinking pools. The horizon came toward me, closer and closer, as if the whole ocean had decided to walk away and take its reflection with it. Within the hour the waterline had retreated farther than any tide I'd ever heard described, farther than any tide should go, and the seabed lay naked under the lowering sun: ribbed sand, dead coral in gray thickets, the ribs of some vessel too large to be anything but a ship, half-buried and black with age.

Tern wanted no part of it. I left her hobbled on the hardpan above the tide line with most of the water, and I went down alone, because the tracks did.

Wren's prints led straight onto the seabed. Fresh ones. And beside them, pressed deep and recent, other prints — many others, boots of different sizes, a dragging heel, the stagger-pattern of someone carrying weight they couldn't afford to carry. A whole expedition's worth of feet, all funneling toward the same point.

A cave mouth opened in the base of the headland where the seabed met the cliffs — revealed now, exposed for the first time in who knew how long, a black arch maybe twelve feet high, its rim carved with lines that resolved, as I got closer, into lettering.

Old script. Older than the Territory, older than the maps. But I'd seen the alphabet before, in my father's things, in a book he kept locked in a drawer I'd never been allowed to open. I read the warning slowly, sounding it out the way you sound out a language you dreamed in as a child:

*WHEN THE SEA FORGETS ITS ROAD, LET NO ONE FOLLOW IT HOME.*

My father had owed somebody a debt. He'd never said who. He'd only said, near the end, when the sickness had him and his hands shook too badly to hold a pen: *If anyone ever comes asking, Ansel — if anyone ever says my name and the word debt in the same breath — you listen. You listen even if it costs you.*

I stood at the mouth of that cave with the sea gone impossibly far behind me and thought about turning around. I want that recorded honestly. Every instinct I had as a ranger said: lost trail, hostile terrain, incoming weather on the western horizon stacking up black, and whatever's in that cave is none of your warrant. Go back. Report the water. Let the Territory send people with authority and equipment.

But the tracks went in. And my father's voice was in the carving somehow, or I was tired enough to hear it there.

I went in.

* * *

The dark inside tasted of salt and iron.

I gave my eyes time, then lit my lamp low and followed the passage down — smooth-walled, man-smoothed in places, though the work was ancient and half-swallowed by mineral bloom. The floor sloped gently, and the sound changed around the second bend, the cave opening its throat into larger spaces. I heard dripping. I heard wind moving through some deeper channel with a low moan like a held note.

Then I heard a woman's voice, cracked and hoarse, saying: "Don't shoot. We've got nothing worth shooting us for."

She was wrong about that, but I didn't know it yet.

The passage opened into a chamber the size of a chapel, and the lamp found them one at a time, the way lamplight does — a boot, a bedroll, a face. Six people. Maybe seven; one shape in the shadows might have been a body or a bedroll, and I couldn't tell. They looked like an expedition that had been wrecked twice: first by whatever had happened to their gear — crates split open, rope cut, a lantern smashed against the wall — and then by waiting. Their faces had the gray patience of people who'd stopped expecting rescue and started rationing hope.

A big man with a frostbitten-looking beard sat binding a splint with shaking hands. A young woman — sharp-eyed, mud to the knees, a chart tube strapped across her back like a rifle — had a knife half-drawn before she saw the ranger's badge and forced herself to put it away. Two others huddled near a seep in the wall where fresh water trickled, drinking from cupped hands.

And in the center, propped against a crate with a wadded shirt pressed to his side, lay a dying man.

He was older than the rest, sixty at least, with a face built for command and currently losing the argument with pain. Blood had soaked through the shirt, through the blanket beneath him, and pooled in a seam of the floor. He'd been bleeding a long time. He looked at me with eyes that were still clear, which was worse than if they hadn't been.

"Ranger," he said. His voice was gravel underwater. "Territory badge?"

"Ansel Nakamura. Out of Meridian Station." I kept the lamp high. "I'm tracking a thief. Wren Corvane. Stole survey charts from the Territorial Office three days ago."

Something moved through the group — a flinch, a glance exchanged between the bearded man and the young woman with the knife. I marked it. Filed it.

"Corvane," the dying man repeated, and laughed, and the laugh cost him visibly. "Of course. Of course it was Corvane."

"You know him?"

"I know everyone worth knowing, ranger. It's my curse." He tried to sit up, failed, and settled for lifting his chin. "Halvard. Halvard Reyes. This is my expedition, such as it remains. And you—" He stopped. His breath caught. When it came back, it came back changed, quieter, aimed straight at me like something thrown. "Nakamura. Your father was Tomas Nakamura."

It wasn't a question. The floor tilted slightly under my boots.

"He died four years ago."

"I know. I sent money for the doctor. It arrived too late — I know that too." Halvard closed his eyes. Opened them. "Your father pulled me out of a collapse in the Kestrel workings when we were young men, and he never once asked me for anything in return. Not once in forty years. Do you understand what that does to a man? It sits on you. It grows." His hand found mine before I could decide whether to offer it — cold fingers, terrible grip strength for a dying man. "I'm asking now. For both of us."

"Sir—"

"Two days," he said. "Maybe three, if the bleeding slows. The tide took our boats, the storm will seal this entrance, and the charts your thief stole are half of what we found. The other half is under your feet." He pressed something into my palm — small, metal, warm from his skin. A compass. Old brass, worn smooth, a hairline crack across the crystal.

My father's compass. The one I carried every day of my working life was already on my belt. This was its twin.

"He wanted you to have it when the debt came due," Halvard said. "This is it coming due."

Behind me, at the mouth of the passage, the wind changed pitch — dropped suddenly, the way air does when a door closes somewhere. The bearded man went to look and came back pale.

"Storm's rolling off the sea wall," he said. "We're sealed in."

Halvard's grip tightened on my hand, and he smiled like a man handing over a live coal.

"Welcome to the expedition, ranger."
