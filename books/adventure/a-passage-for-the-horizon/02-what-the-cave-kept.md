## 2. What the Cave Kept

The cave breathed cold.

Ansel felt it before they saw anything — a draft rolling out of the dark like the exhale of something enormous and patient, carrying the smell of wet stone and old salt. Behind them, the flats stretched white to the horizon, stripped bare by a tide that had pulled back farther than any tide had a right to go. Ahead, the cave mouth gaped in the cliff face, its rim carved with warnings in three scripts, two of them dead.

*The sea remembers what men forget.* That was the one Ansel could read. Their father had taught them the old coastal script from a primer he'd kept wrapped in oilcloth, though he'd never said why a desert ranger needed it.

Wren Corvane's tracks ended here. Of course they did.

Ansel stood at the threshold a long moment, hand on their sidearm, listening. Water dripped somewhere deep inside. Wind moaned across the cave mouth, low and tonal, like a struck bottle. No footsteps. No breathing but their own.

They went in anyway. Chasing a thief into a hole in the world was bad judgment. Letting the thief get away with whatever was in that satchel was worse.

The passage widened after fifty paces, and Ansel clicked on their lamp — and stopped.

Bones first. Not animal. A row of them laid along the wall with care, skulls facing inward, some kind of offering or warning or both. Then lanterns — real ones, brass and glass, hung on iron hooks rusted to lace. And then people.

Six of them, maybe seven, huddled around a camp of tarps and crates in a chamber wide as a chapel. They came up off the ground at the light, and two of them had rifles, and for a long ugly moment everyone aimed at everyone.

"Ranger," said a woman's voice, hoarse. "You're a ranger."

Ansel kept the lamp high, off their eyes. "Ansel Nakamura. Salt District. Who's in charge here?"

A laugh answered — wet, broken. From the deepest shadow, a man raised one hand, and even before the light found his face, Ansel knew the voice.

Halvard Reyes.

Older than the last time Ansel had seen him, which was at their father's funeral, standing at the back in a coat too fine for the salt country, saying nothing. Now he lay against a crate with his abdomen bound in strips of shirt gone brown and stiff, and his face was the color of the tallow candle guttering beside him.

"Nakamura's kid," Halvard said. "Come to collect."

"I came chasing a thief." Ansel lowered the rifle a fraction. "Satchel. Tall, quick hands, walks like the ground owes them money. Came through here?"

Something moved through the survivors — a flinch, exchanged glances, a woman by the far wall going very still.

"You just missed them," Halvard said, and smiled with bloodless lips. "Or you didn't. Depends how you count."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," Halvard said, "that your father owed me a debt, and I've come to this hole in the earth to spend it. Sit down, ranger. The storm's coming, and we have days, not hours, and I'd rather not die mid-sentence."

Outside, as if summoned, the wind changed its mind and began to scream.

* * *

Ilsa Varga had counted the bullets twice and lied about the number once.

She sat near the entrance now, where the air still moved, watching the ranger — Nakamura's child, all sharp angles and watchfulness, exactly as Halvard had promised years ago when he'd first spoken of the debt. *If it ever comes to it,* he'd said, *the Nakamura will come. It's in the blood. They pay.*

She hadn't believed him. She was beginning to believe him.

The storm hit the coast an hour after the ranger arrived, and it hit like a verdict. Wind drove sand and spume against the cliff hard enough to sing, and by midnight the cave mouth was a solid gray roar, sealed as thoroughly as a vault door. Ilsa had watched the last light die in it and felt the old dread settle back over her shoulders like a wet coat: *days, not hours*. The tide would not return until the moon swung around, and the moon was in no hurry.

Seven of them left. Eight, counting the ranger. Nine, counting the dead man under the tarp by the charts.

"We should tell them," she said to Halvard, quietly, kneeling beside him. His fever had come up with the storm. His skin burned; his eyes were clear, which was worse. Dying men with clear eyes made decisions that got other people killed.

"They'll know soon enough."

"They'll know faster if we don't insult them."

Halvard laughed, and it cost him. "You always did argue like your mother. Fine. Fetch the lamp. Let's show the ranger what we bled for."

What they bled for lay forty paces deeper in, past the bones, down a natural stair worn smooth by ten thousand years of water that no longer existed.

The chamber at the bottom was round as a held breath. And in its center, half-buried in concreted sand and salt, lay a ship.

Not a wreck — a ship. Intact, keel to rail, preserved in the mineral crust like a fly in amber. A hundred feet of her, maybe more, her hull scarred with barnacles fossilized to stone, her mast snapped but present, her name still legible along the stern in letters of applied bronze:

*Meridian's Daughter*.

"She sailed," Ilsa said, because she said it every time, because saying it made it true. "Two hundred years ago, when the maps say there was nothing here but dunes. She sailed this whole basin, bow to stern. There were ports. There were *cities*, ranger. And somebody drowned them."

Nakamura stood very still, lamp raised, light sliding along the hull like water. "The charts."

"In Halvard's case. Hand-drawn copies of the originals we found in her hold. Soundings, routes, harbor marks. Proof the desert was a sea lane — proof the border treaties, the water rights, the whole map of three nations is drawn over a lie." Ilsa hugged her arms against the cold. "There are people who very much prefer the lie. One of them followed us in."

The ranger turned. "Corvane."

"Wren joined us eight months ago," Ilsa said. "Good climber. Better liar. We trusted them with the survey lines and the supply ledgers, and three weeks ago our radio died, our spare batteries vanished, and Petrov ended up under a rockfall nobody heard." Her voice caught on the name and she let it. "Then two nights ago Wren took the original charts and went out the low passage toward the sea caves. Halvard went after them alone. He got the satchel back." She nodded upward, toward the camp, toward the dying man. "It cost him what it cost."

"So Corvane's still—"

"Here," Ilsa said. "Sealed in with us. Eating our food. Smiling at us. Waiting."

The lamp light trembled slightly. Not the ranger's hands — Ilsa realized — the flame itself, bending toward the inner passages, where the air moved.

"Which way does that draft come from?" Nakamura asked.

"The flooded galleries. Why?"

"Because if there's moving air, there's another way out," the ranger said. "And if there's another way out, so does Wren."

Above them, faint through forty feet of stone, they heard it: the scrape of a crate being dragged.

* * *

By the time they climbed back to the main chamber, the supplies had been opened like wounds.

Rations slit and scattered. Water cans drained onto stone, the puddles already evaporating into the dry dark. The medicine kit gutted — Halvard's morphia gone, the antibiotics gone, everything gone but the gauze, which Wren had left in a neat folded square atop the empty case. A message. A courtesy. A taunt.

And pinned beneath the gauze, a page torn from a notebook, written in a hand Ansel recognized from a hundred forged waybills across four counties:

*Debt's paid either way, ranger. Question is who pays it. — W*

Ansel read it twice and felt the old chase-heat rise in their chest, the thing that had carried them across sixty miles of salt flat on a rumor and a footprint. But the heat cooled fast, because the arithmetic on the floor was simple: seven people, five days of short rations, no medicine, a dying man, and a betrayer loose in the dark with a head start and nothing left to steal but time.

Ilsa crouched over the ruined stores, saying nothing, her jaw working. One of the others — a young surveyor named Dek, hands shaking — started to gather the scattered food, then stopped, then started again.

Halvard had propped himself up against the crate, gray-faced, watching Ansel with those too-clear eyes.

"Well, ranger," he said. "Your father's debt comes due. I need you to do what you do best."

"Chase thieves?"

"No." Halvard's breath rattled. "Find doors. Get my people out. The rest—" he pressed the satchel of copied charts weakly against Ansel's knee, "—the rest is yours to carry. However you choose."

Ansel looked at the satchel. At the scraped-clean water cans. At the black seam of the inner passages, where the air moved cold and steady, and somewhere inside it, quick hands were already at work.

The storm screamed against the stone. The tide was days away.

Ansel slung the satchel across their chest and picked up their lamp.

"Then we move tonight," they said. "Before Wren decides we're the next thing worth taking."
