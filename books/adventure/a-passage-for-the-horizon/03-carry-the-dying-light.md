## 3. Carry the Dying Light

Halvard stopped speaking in sentences on the second day.

He still had words — he spent them like a man spending his last coins, one at a time, choosing carefully. *Water. Ilsa. The debt.* He said Ansel's name once, and the ranger crossed the camp to kneel beside him, and whatever passed between them was private even in a cave full of strangers.

Ilsa kept the vigil. She had been Halvard's cartographer for six years, and she knew every line of his face the way she knew every line of his maps, and she could read the deterioration in both. The wound in his side had gone the color of old rust. The fever came in waves now, and between them he slept with his jaw clenched, as if even unconscious he was holding something shut.

The others had stopped pretending they weren't afraid. There were nine of them left of twenty-two. They sat in the lamplight among the crates and the half-buried hull of the vessel — that impossible, ancient thing in the rock — and they listened to the storm hammering the sealed entrance, and they waited for someone to tell them what happened next.

Ansel told them. That was the strange part, Ilsa thought. The ranger who had come chasing a thief had become the spine of the expedition without ever being asked.

"There's a second way out," Ansel said, unrolling the survey sketches Ilsa had made during the first desperate hours. "Inner passages. They run north under the ridge. My father's generation used them — smugglers' routes, before the tide changed." A pause. "They'll be flooded. Partway."

"Flooded how?" asked Okafor, the engineer, whose hands hadn't stopped shaking since the collapse.

"Knee-deep if we're lucky. Chest-deep if we're not. Cold enough to kill anyone already weak." Ansel's eyes went to Halvard, then away. "We carry him or we leave him. Those are the choices."

"We carry him," Ilsa said, and her voice came out harder than she intended, and nobody argued.

* * *

They moved at first light, or what passed for it — the cave kept its own hours, lit by lamps and by the faint phosphorescence that clung to the wet stone like a memory of the sea that had made it.

Ilsa walked third in the line, behind Ansel and Okafor, with Halvard lashed to a stretcher of tent poles and sailcloth, borne by four of the strongest. The water found them within the hour. It came up through the dark in absolute silence, black and glass-still, and when Ilsa's boots broke its surface the cold climbed her legs like something alive.

"Keep moving," Ansel said ahead of her, voice flat and steady. "Cold is a liar. It tells you to stop. Don't listen."

The passage narrowed. The ceiling came down to meet the water until they were wading bent double, lamps held high, breath fogging. Somewhere behind them, a crate shifted; someone swore; the sound echoed down the tunnel and came back wrong, doubled, as if the cave were repeating it to itself.

It was an hour later, at the first junction, that Ilsa discovered the charts were gone.

She carried them herself. She always carried them herself — the master sheets, the ones that proved the desert had been a sea lane, that showed harbors where there was only salt, that would redraw every border from the coast to the mountains. She had slept with them strapped against her ribs since the ambush.

The strap was still there. The oilskin packet inside it was gone.

She stood in the freezing water and felt the bottom drop out of her. Then she did the arithmetic she didn't want to do: who had been close to her in the night watches. Who had volunteered for rear guard, always rear guard, where no one looked. Who had been the last to sleep and the first to wake.

"Wren," she said aloud, and the name fell into the black water.

* * *

Ansel took the news the way stone takes rain.

"When?"

"Sometime in the night. They cut the strap and re-tied it. I wouldn't have known until—" Ilsa's voice cracked and she hated it. "Until it mattered."

"It matters now." Ansel turned to the group, huddled on a shelf of dry rock above the floodline, and Ilsa watched the ranger weigh what to say and decide to say all of it. "Wren Corvane has the charts. Wren Corvane also cut the supply lines two nights back — the spoiled rations weren't rot, they were sabotage. And Wren Corvane is somewhere ahead of us, because this passage is the only way out."

A murmur ran through the survivors. Okafor said, "Then we're trapped between a thief and a flood."

"No," Ansel said. "We're ahead of schedule. Wren doesn't know these tunnels. I do." Something moved behind the ranger's eyes, something old and cold. "And I've been chasing them across three hundred miles of salt. I know exactly how they think."

"And when you catch them?" Ilsa asked. She meant it as a real question. She watched Ansel hear it as one.

Ansel didn't answer. That was the answer.

* * *

They caught up to Wren at dusk, at the place where the passage split around a drowned chamber and rejoined at a low arch — the second exit, breathing cold storm-air, a gray thread of daylight visible beyond it.

Wren stood on the arch itself, silhouetted, the oilskin packet held up in one hand over the black water below. Not fleeing. Waiting.

"You're slow," Wren called down. Their voice echoed off the wet stone, and Ilsa thought: *they don't sound like a thief. They sound like someone reading a verdict.*

"Give me the charts," Ansel said, climbing.

"Why? So you can bury them again?" Wren laughed, and it was a terrible sound, thin and bright. "You've seen what's back there, ranger. You've seen the ship under the mountain. Do you know what your government does with things like that? What *any* government does? They seal it. They bury it. They kill everyone who's seen it." The hand holding the packet swayed out over the water. "Halvard found the truth and sat on it for two years. Two years! While the villages downstream drowned one flood at a time, because the maps that could have warned them were locked in a strongbox."

"That's not why you stole them," Ilsa shouted. "You sold out the expedition. You brought the raiders down on us. Twenty-two people, Wren. Thirteen dead."

"I brought the *buyers*," Wren said, and for just a moment the voice wavered. "The raiders were supposed to take the charts and go. Nobody was supposed to—" They stopped. Reset. "It doesn't matter. Drop your weapons, all of you, or these go into the water. Every harbor. Every border. Gone."

The chamber went silent except for the drip of water and the far-off thunder of the storm.

Ansel climbed the last few feet and stood on the arch, ten paces from Wren, and Ilsa saw the rifle come up — saw the whole geometry of it, the angle, the distance, the shot that would end everything. Wren saw it too, and smiled, and let the packet tilt another degree toward the black water.

"Go on," Wren said softly. "Shoot. And spend the rest of your life knowing the truth died with me, in the dark, and the floods kept coming."

Ilsa held her breath.

Ansel lowered the rifle.

"I chased you three hundred miles to arrest you," the ranger said. "That was the debt. That was the job." A breath. "But my father owed Halvard a debt, and Halvard just spent his last coin telling me the truth costs more than silence. So here's mine."

And Ansel turned — turned away from Wren entirely, turned to face the huddled survivors on the rocks, and raised a voice cracked raw by smoke and cold water:

"The desert was a sea! There are charts proving it — harbors under the salt, a trade route older than any border on any map! And the same waters that carved them will drown the lowland villages downstream when this storm breaks, and the maps that could save those people are in the hands of a thief standing right there!" A ragged breath. "So take it from me, or take it from them, but *take it* — because I'm done keeping secrets for dead men!"

The echo rolled through the chamber and came back changed, multiplied, carried down the flooded passage into the deep of the cave, as if the sea itself had heard and agreed.

Wren stood frozen on the arch, the packet still in hand, and Ilsa watched something break in the thief's face — not defeat, exactly. Something more like vertigo. Like a person who had planned for every outcome except being believed.

"You fool," Wren whispered. "Do you know what they'll do to all of you now?"

"They'll have to find us first," Ansel said, and started walking toward the gray light of the exit.

* * *

They came out of the mountain into the aftermath of the storm, into a world scoured and silvered, the sky torn open to a hard clean blue. Below them the salt flats stretched to the horizon, and far off, glinting, the flooded lowlands — and beyond them, small as toys, the rooftops of the village that might yet live.

Behind them, at the mouth of the cave, Wren Corvane stood holding the charts, neither running nor following, watching Ansel Nakamura walk down into the light with nothing left to protect but the truth.

"Halvard's failing," Ilsa said quietly, coming up beside the ranger. "He's asking for you."

Ansel nodded once, eyes fixed on the distant water.

"Then we'd better hurry," the ranger said. "There's a debt to settle."
