## 1. The Mood of the Sandbar

The Vell River had moods, and Beatrix Ashgrove had learned to read them the way other people read faces. This morning it was sullen — low water, a green-brown gleam like an old coin, the fog sitting on it in patches that would burn off by midmorning and leave the banks exposed and honest. She stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel* with one hand on the brass and the other around a tin cup of coffee gone cold an hour ago, and she listened.

Not with her ears. With the hull.

The *Kestrel* was forty years old and told her everything. A shudder through the wheel meant the current had found the wing dam below Marrow Light. A change in the engine's note — that deep, patient chuff of the boiler Odile Voss hadn't built and Beatrix couldn't yet afford to replace — meant the fireman had thrown on green wood, and she'd have to go down and say something about it. And a certain hollowness underfoot, a feeling like walking on a drum skin, meant sand moving beneath the keel, the river rearranging its furniture in the dark.

This morning the hull said: *careful, below the bend. Something's changed.*

She'd felt it since dawn. The channel markers were where they should be — three staves with tin reflectors, leaning the way they always leaned after a hard rain — but the water around them was wrong. Too smooth. Water that smooth over a sandbar meant the bar had grown, and water that smooth where it should be choppy meant something had been dredged and the channel had been moved without anyone bothering to tell the river's working boats.

Beatrix set down her cup, rang the engine telegraph to slow, and swung the wheel two points to starboard, out toward the deeper water near the far bank. It cost her time. Time was money on the Vell, and money was the thing she had least of and thought about least, in that order. But the hull had spoken, and she had survived ten years on this river by treating its opinions as law.

Half a mile on, she passed the place where she would have been.

The sandbar lay where the old chart said six feet of channel lay, a long yellow tongue of it just breaking the surface, and beyond it, wedged onto the bar's shoulder like a beetle on its back, was the *Merry Adeline*.

* * *

Beatrix brought the *Kestrel* around in a long, unhurried arc and let the current carry her alongside, close enough to read the name on the stern, far enough not to foul her own wheel on the wreck's gear. The *Adeline* was a sternwheeler, smaller than the *Kestrel*, newer by twenty years, and now listing hard to port with her paddlewheel half out of the water, dripping. Her deck cargo — crates stenciled with a Grayhollow grocer's mark — had shifted and was sliding, one crate at a time, into the river with a soft, obscene *gulp* each time.

Two men stood on the roof of the pilot house. One was waving his coat over his head like a man signaling a train. The other was just standing, very still, the way men stand when they've recently understood they might die and haven't yet decided what to do with the information.

"Ahoy the *Adeline*," Beatrix called. Her voice carried flat and clear across the water, the way she'd trained it to. "Anyone else aboard?"

"Engineer's below — he's caught — the deck's flooding—" The coat-waver's voice cracked on the last word. "The bar came out of nowhere. There was no marker, no warning—"

"There's always a warning," Beatrix said. "You weren't listening."

She was already moving. She rang the telegraph to half ahead, walked the *Kestrel* up past the wreck, and dropped her stern anchor with a precision she'd have denied feeling any pride in. The boat swung and settled broadside above the bar, in the slack water behind the wreck, close enough that a thrown line would reach. Then she went down to the deck herself, because she didn't trust crew work to crew, and because the *Kestrel*'s crew that season was a part-timer named Sallow who was, at this moment, asleep in the galley.

"Line!" she called up.

The still man — the mate, by the look of his coat — threw it. His first throw fell short. His second caught her rope rail, and Beatrix made it fast with two turns and a hitch, then hauled the boats together until the fenders screamed.

"Listen to me," she said, looking up at both of them. "One at a time. Down the line, hand over hand, feet on the hull. Don't jump. If you jump, you'll go under my guard rail and the river will take you at the stern, and I won't be able to stop it. Do you understand?"

The mate nodded. The other one — the pilot, she guessed, from the wheel-shaped burnish on his coat where his hands had rested for years — was still waving his coat.

"Stop that," Beatrix said. "You look like a fool and you'll tangle the line. You. Pilot. You first, because you're the one panicking, and panicking men go second and push the calm ones into the water."

That got his attention. He came down the line badly, all arms and no legs, and she caught him by the collar as his feet found her deck, held him upright until his knees agreed to work, and then pointed at the deckhouse. "Sit there. Don't touch anything."

The mate came down better. He was younger, with a rope-scarred palm and the flat, grateful eyes of a man who has just been handed back his life and doesn't know where to put it.

"Your engineer," Beatrix said. "Where below?"

"Fire room. The hull's stove on the port bow — the bar went right up through us. He's got water over the plates. He wouldn't leave the boiler. He kept saying if the fire hits the water wrong—" The mate's voice went again.

"Then we're going to get him before it does." Beatrix was already uncoiling a second line, tying a bowline around her own chest and under her arms, the way she'd done perhaps two hundred times and hoped to do two hundred more. "Sallow!" she bellowed at the galley, and was rewarded — after a scandalous pause — by the appearance of her fireman, blinking, suspenders hanging. "Steam pressure. Hold her steady. If I'm not back in ten minutes, you're captain, and God help the river."

She went over the side.

* * *

The *Adeline* below decks was a drowning animal. Water came through the stove planking in a gray-green rush, waist-high already and rising, carrying deck debris, a broom, a hat, the small floating wreckage of other men's livelihoods. The air was steam and coal smoke and the sweet iron stink of a boiler working too hard against a failing hull. Beatrix waded, then swam the last few feet, and found the engineer in the fire room, up on the plates with his feet in the water, feeding the fire with one hand and holding a wrench with the other as though the wrench were a weapon and the boiler a thing worth defending.

"Out," Beatrix said.

"Can't." He was an old man, gray to the eyebrows, with burns gone shiny on his forearms. "Pressure's still up. If I leave the fire, she'll—"

"She'll do what she does whether you're here or not." Beatrix took the shovel out of his hand. "The boat's dead. You're not. Up you go."

"I built that engine," he said, and there was such grief in it, such naked, unhidden grief, that Beatrix paused for half a second — half a second longer than she should have, with water climbing her ribs.

"I know," she said. "I'm sorry. Now go."

She got him to the line. He fought her the whole way, not with his body but with his head, turning back to look at the fire room, at the engine he'd built, until the water took his footing and Beatrix simply hauled, hand over hand, both their weights on the line and the river pulling the other way, and the *Kestrel*'s rail came up to meet them and hands — Sallow's, then the mate's — dragged them over into the daylight.

Behind them, the *Adeline*'s boiler let go with a muffled whump, not an explosion but an exhale, a white column of steam standing up out of the wreck like a soul deciding whether to leave. The fire was drowned. The engine was dead. The *Merry Adeline* settled a few inches deeper and began, with the patience of all rivers and all wrecks, to become part of the river.

* * *

They sat in the *Kestrel*'s deckhouse and drank Sallow's coffee, which was worse than Beatrix's, which was saying something. The engineer had a blanket around his shoulders and was shaking, though the morning was warm. The pilot had found his voice and was using it.

"We'll pay you," he said. "Whatever you ask. The Company insures us — there'll be a settlement, salvage, all of it. You'll be owed a share, captain, a real share—"

"No."

The word landed flat. The pilot blinked. The mate looked up from his coffee.

"No salvage claim," Beatrix said. "No share. I'll put you ashore at Grayhollow landing and you'll tell the insurance men the *Kestrel* happened to be passing. That's all that happened."

"But you went below for him." The mate's voice was quiet. "You tied a line around yourself and went into a flooding hull. That's not passing."

"It is, actually. It's exactly what passing looks like." Beatrix stood, and the deckhouse felt suddenly smaller with her in it, the way it always did. "There's a bar where your chart says channel. Someone dredged a cut above Marrow Light and moved the current without marking it. You should know that, for the report. And you should know the Company doesn't pay salvage shares on acts of God, and if you tell them a private boat assisted, they'll spend a year deciding whether that makes her liable. I don't want their money and I especially don't want their attention."

The pilot's mouth opened, closed, opened again. "Then let us at least know your name. For the report. For — for our own—"

"No," Beatrix said again, and this time she softened it by half a degree, which for her was a great deal. "You'll forget my boat and my face and you'll be better off. You'll run the Vell another thirty years and you'll never see me again, and that's the best thing I can give you."

She went out to the wheel and left them to their coffee and their confusion.

Sallow appeared at her elbow a few minutes later, chewing something. "They'll talk," he said. "Men always talk. You pulled three of them out of the river. That's a story."

"Stories fade," Beatrix said. "Names don't. That's why I don't give mine."

Sallow chewed a while longer. He was a good fireman and a poor philosopher, and he knew when a conversation was over. But at the door he paused. "That dredge cut," he said. "Ain't Company work, is it? They'd have marked it, even them."

Beatrix looked downriver, toward the smooth water and the drowned boat and the long yellow tongue of sand.

"Company work gets marked," she agreed. "Which means somebody wanted a channel there that they didn't want anyone asking about. Somebody moving a lot of water, quiet." She rang the telegraph to slow ahead. "Or somebody moving a lot of something else."

* * *

She put them ashore at Grayhollow at dusk.

The landing was lamplit and loud, all cargo hooks and fish stalls and the smell of tar and frying onions, and the three survivors of the *Merry Adeline* stood on the planks looking up at her like men at a church door. The engineer had stopped shaking. He raised one burned hand — not a wave, exactly. Something older than a wave. A blessing, maybe, or the beginning of one.

Beatrix turned away from it and looked at the wheel.

She didn't want their gratitude. Gratitude was a debt, and debts were hooks, and hooks were how people found you. Ten years on the Vell and she had a rule as fixed as any channel marker: *help, then move on.* She'd pulled drowning men out of the current at Sorrow Bend, off the flats below Kettering, once out of a flooded quarry pit where a boy had gone in after a dog. She'd never once stayed to be thanked. Thanks required a name. A name required a past. And her past was a thing she had paid a great deal to leave on the far side of the water, and she did not intend to let three half-drowned boatmen row it back to her.

The *Kestrel* took the current and Grayhollow slid away behind, lamplit and loud and mercifully anonymous.

But that night, lying in her bunk with the river talking to the hull the way it always did — that low, patient, endless conversation — Beatrix found herself turning one thing over. Not the wreck. Not the unmarked cut, though that was worth turning over too, and she'd be a fool not to wonder what the Company was dredging above Marrow Light and why.

It was the engineer's face. That grief, naked and unhidden, for a machine. For a thing he'd built with his hands and loved without apology and lost in an afternoon. She had spent ten years making sure she had nothing that could be lost like that. No engine she'd built. No name she'd answer to. No one aboard who could drown in her place.

It had kept her alive. She was almost sure it had kept her alive.

The hull spoke to her, the river spoke to her, and she spoke to neither, and the *Kestrel* ran on down the dark water toward the morning.

* * *

Grayhollow again, three days later, on the return run — because the river was a loop and even a woman running from her past had to travel it in circles.

She took on cargo at the same landing where she'd put the *Adeline*'s people ashore, and she kept her eyes on the manifests and her back to the planks, and she almost made it. Almost.

"Captain."

The voice was wrong. Not the mate's, not the pilot's. Thinner. Rougher. Like a voice that had been coughing for a long time and had learned to spend itself carefully.

She turned. The man on the planks was gaunt as a heron, gray-faced under a gray hat, with a prospector's canvas coat gone soft with wear and a pack that looked like it weighed more than he did. His eyes were the problem. They were too clear for the rest of him, the way water is clear just before it goes over a falls.

"You're the one who pulled the *Adeline*'s people out," he said. "Heard it at the fish stall. They wouldn't say your name. Said you wouldn't give it." He smiled, and the smile cost him something; she watched him pay for it. "A captain who doesn't give her name. That's a captain who'll take a passenger and ask no questions."

"I don't take passengers," Beatrix said.

"Upstream," he said, as if she hadn't spoken. "Past the locks. As far as you'll go." He drew an envelope from his coat — fat, sealed, held the way a man holds the last of something. "I can pay."

Beatrix looked at the envelope, and at the man's hands, which had a fine tremor in them that had nothing to do with the cold, and at his mouth, which was pressed tight around something he was keeping down.

"I don't take passengers," she said again. But she heard it, even as she said it — the half-degree of softness, the crack in the rule, the sound a door makes just before it opens.

The gaunt man heard it too. He stepped up onto the *Kestrel*'s deck, uninvited, and stood there swaying slightly with the river's motion, like a man who had finally, after a very long walk, found a boat that was moving the direction he needed to die in.

"Hollis," he said. "My name's Hollis. And I'll pay you whatever's in here, captain, and I promise you won't have to ask me a single question."

Beatrix looked at him for a long moment. The river talked to the hull. The fog was burning off the banks, exposing everything, honest and plain.

"Deckhouse is aft," she said. "Don't touch anything."
