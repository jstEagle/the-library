## 2. A Stranger at the Dock

Grayhollow came out of the morning mist the way it always did: all at once, and none too honestly.

Beatrix had made the approach a hundred times, and the town never improved. It clung to the east bank of the Vell where the river bent northward, a huddle of tar-paper roofs and leaning wharves, its skyline a forest of masts and crane-arms and the crooked iron stack of the smelting works bleeding its perpetual bruise of smoke into the low clouds. The air changed a mile out. Anyone who ran the river long enough could smell Grayhollow before they saw it — coal smoke, creosote, fish offal, and beneath it all the sweetish mineral tang of the assay houses, where men crushed stone all day trying to coax fortune out of the mountains' bones.

She brought the Kestrel in against the current with the engine at half, one hand on the wheel and one on the throttle, reading the water the way other people read a page. The fog was burning off in rags. Sunlight came through in slants and made the river surface look like hammered pewter. A barge train was tied up three deep at the Company wharf, grey hulls, grey canvas, grey men in grey coats moving cargo with the joyless efficiency of ants. She gave that wharf a wide berth and slid instead toward the public landing downstream, where the pilings were rotten and the boards were sprung and nobody important ever looked.

"Coming in," she called down the deck, though there was no one to hear it but herself and the boat. Ten years she'd run the Kestrel alone — cargo when she could get it, passengers when she had to, and never more than she needed of either. A boat with a small wake left fewer questions behind it.

The landing took her lines without complaint. She made fast fore and aft, checked the fenders, and only then let herself look up at the town.

Grayhollow's dock street ran parallel to the water like a scar. There was the chandlery with its faded awning, the ferry office with its board of tariffs gone grey with weather, the saloon called the Drowned Rat that had burned down twice and been rebuilt both times out of pure spite. And there, at the rail's end where the planking met the mud of the shore path, stood a man who had clearly been waiting for her.

She knew the type before she knew the man. Prospector. Upland boots worn through at the heel and re-soled with harness leather. A coat that had been good twenty years ago and had been living on memory since. A pack at his feet that was mostly straps and hope. Gaunt as a winter branch, with a beard gone to iron-grey and a face the mountains had been chewing on for a long while. He stood very still, the way men do when standing still is all they have left, and his eyes were fixed on her boat with an intensity that had nothing casual in it.

Beatrix had a rule about eyes like that. The rule was: don't.

She coiled her lines with deliberate slowness, giving the man time to approach or not, and he took the time. He came down the dock at a walk that was trying very hard to be brisk and not managing it, and stopped a respectful six feet from the gangplank, and took off a hat that had once been black.

"Captain." His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "Kestrel, is it?"

"It's a boat."

"Kestrel," he said again, as if the name mattered to him, as if he'd asked after it. "I'm told you run upstream. As far as the locks, and past them if the price is right."

"I'm told a lot of things."

"Beatrix Ashgrove." He said the name carefully, like a man handling something that might be hot. "That's the name on the ferry office board, anyhow. Cargo, passengers, no questions."

"No questions is the service," she said. "The name's just what's left over."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. It might have been a smile trying to remember how. "Hollis," he said. "Ezra Hollis. And I've got a question, Captain, and coin behind it, and I'll ask plain if you'll hear me plain."

She set down the coiled line. "Passengers going up to the locks is two silver. Past the locks, I don't go."

"Past the locks," he said. "Above Sorrow Bend. To the high water."

"Then your coin's no good to me."

"It's good enough." He reached into his coat, and she watched his hands the whole way — old habit, older than the river — and what came out was not the greasy fold of small bills she'd expected. It was a purse. A real one, soft with age, and when he loosened the drawstring the morning light found gold in it. Not dust. Not flakes. Coin, minted and heavy, the kind that rang when it landed.

He poured a measure into his palm — she counted without meaning to, eleven pieces — and held it out over the gap between them.

"Passage," he said. "Upstream. As far as the river goes."

Beatrix looked at the gold, and looked at the man, and did the arithmetic she always did. Eleven coins was a month of cargo. It was a new stern gland and a coil of good rope. It was coal and grease and the small, dull dignity of solvency. It was also, very plainly, trouble wearing a hat. Men didn't pay gold for passage. Men paid gold to be carried away from something, or toward something, and both kinds left marks on a boat.

"I don't run the high water," she said. "It's Company water above the locks. They don't like traffic they haven't sold the ticket for."

"The Company," Hollis said, "doesn't like a great many things." He coughed then, sudden and deep, doubling at the waist, and turned his face away into a handkerchief with the practiced motion of a man who'd been doing it long enough to have manners about it.

She saw what he thought she didn't. She was standing six feet away with the sun full on him and she saw it plain: the handkerchief came away spotted, dark red going brown at the edges, and he folded it closed with a quickness that was itself a kind of confession, and tucked it away, and straightened up as if nothing had happened, and his eyes were wet and bright with the effort of pretending.

The lung fever, then. Or worse. The kind of worse that the upland camps had names for and no cures for. She'd seen men work a claim with it, coughing themselves thin over a hole in the ground, because the alternative was to stop and die in a place with no name on the deed.

"Captain," he said, when he had his breath back. "I haven't got a season left in me. Maybe not a month. I know what that looks like — you saw it, no use pretending otherwise. I'm not asking you to carry a sick man out of charity. I'm asking you to carry a fare. Gold is gold, whether the hand that pays it shakes or not."

The river moved beneath the hull, patient and indifferent. Somewhere behind them a crane groaned, swinging a net of ore sacks over the Company wharf. Beatrix looked at the gold in his palm a long moment.

"I take you to the locks," she said. "That's the farthest I've gone in ten years and the farthest I mean to go."

"Take me to the locks, then." He didn't argue, and something in the way he didn't argue made her wary, because men with a real destination argue. Men with a real destination wear you down. This one just poured the eleven coins into her hand — they were heavier than they looked, all of them — and shouldered his pack, and stood waiting for her to say yes.

She should have said no. She knew it the way you know weather coming. Ten years of no had kept the Kestrel afloat and Beatrix Ashgrove unremarkable and alive, and every instinct she owned stood in a line and said *no, no, no.*

"Deck passage," she said. "You sleep in the cargo hold. You keep your gear lashed down. You don't touch the wheel, the engine, or the lines, and you don't ask me where I've been or where I'm going, because I won't answer."

"Fair terms."

"And if you die aboard, I put you ashore at the next landing and I don't write anybody."

Something flickered across his face at that — quick, there and gone, like a fish under the surface. Something that wasn't fear and wasn't quite amusement. "Fair terms," he said again, quieter. "Captain Ashgrove, you're everything they said."

"Who said?"

"River talk." He stepped onto the gangplank before she could press it, and the plank dipped under his weight, and she saw how little weight there was to dip. He crossed to her deck and stood there looking around at the Kestrel with an appraiser's eye — the patched bulwark, the boiler housing, the wheel with its worn brass — and whatever he concluded, he kept it behind his teeth.

* * *

They cast off within the hour.

She'd meant to take on coal and be gone by noon, but the coal chandlery was slow and the chandler was slower, and by the time the Kestrel backed off the public landing the light was going long and amber across the water. Grayhollow slid away astern, its smoke smeared into the western sky, and Beatrix felt the familiar loosening in her chest that came whenever a town let go of her. Out here there was just the river, and the river never asked a man his name.

Hollis sat on a crate amidships with his pack between his knees and his hands folded on it, and he watched the water. Not the banks — the water. She noticed that. Passengers watched the scenery, the cliffs and the birds and the other boats. This man watched the current itself, the way it curled off the pilings and the color of it where it ran deep, reading it, and she thought: *he's been on rivers before. Upland rivers. He knows what he's looking at.*

She didn't like that either.

She ran the boat until full dark, tied up at a willow bank twenty miles above town where the Company's eyes didn't reach, and cooked a plain supper of beans and salt pork on the little deck stove. She set out one bowl and one spoon, then, after a moment she'd deny later, a second bowl and a second spoon.

Hollis ate like a man who'd forgotten food could be warm. He ate slowly, though, making it last, and when he'd finished he washed both bowls in the river without being asked and set them to dry, and sat back down on his crate, and looked at the fire.

"Captain," he said. "Can I ask you something that isn't a question?"

"That's a new one."

"Where I come from, up past Sorrow Bend, there's a kind of person the river makes. The kind who helps and moves on. Never moors twice at the same landing. Never lets a town learn their face." He poked the fire with a stick, careful, economical. "I've met a few. Good people, mostly. Tired people. I always wondered what it was they were rowing away from, or if it was just the rowing they loved."

"You wondered that from a camp up the river."

"I wondered it for a long time." He looked at her across the small fire, and his eyes in the firelight were clear and dark and terribly tired. "Myself, I spent thirty years digging holes in the ground looking for the shiny stuff, and you know what I found at the bottom of every one? Dirt. It's always dirt, Captain. All the way down. The gold's never in the ground. It's in what you'll do to keep digging."

Beatrix fed a stick to the fire and said nothing.

"I'm not asking what you're rowing from," Hollis said. "Terms are terms. I only wanted to say — a man can tell, watching you handle her. You didn't learn that boat on safe water." He nodded upstream, into the dark where the current made its low continuous voice against the hull. "There's water up there no map knows. When we get past the locks, you'll want to trust the boat and not the charts. She'll tell you the truth. Charts only tell you what a man measured."

"We're not going past the locks."

"No," he agreed, mildly. "I heard you."

The fire cracked. Somewhere across the river a night bird called, three notes, and got no answer. Beatrix found she was gripping the stick too hard and made herself let go.

"Sleep," she said. "I cast off before light."

He gathered his bedroll from the hold and made it up on deck, near the stove, and she watched him lie down on his side facing the fire like an old dog, and within minutes his breathing went long and rough, with a whistle in it that she didn't like at all.

She sat up alone after that, long past when she should have turned in. The Kestrel swung gently at her lines, and the willows hissed on the bank, and the gold coins sat in her coat pocket against her hip, eleven small heavy weights that had no business feeling the way they felt. She'd been paid fairly for a simple fare. That was all it was. A fare, and a fee, and a sick old man going up to the locks to die where he'd dug, which was more sentiment than most men got.

She told herself all of it, in order, twice.

Then she got up, quiet so as not to wake him, and went to the hold where his pack sat lashed where he'd left it, and she stood over it for a long moment with her hand half raised.

She didn't touch it. That wasn't the service. No questions, no pockets, no prying — that was the whole architecture of the life she'd built, and it had held for ten years, and she turned and climbed back into the night air and stood at the rail and breathed the cold river in.

But she'd seen the shape of what was in there, when he'd slung it aboard. A flat case, long and rigid, riding against the canvas roll. The shape of a map case. And she'd seen, when he thought no one was looking, the way his hand went to it — once at the dock, once when the boat lurched casting off — the way a man touches the one thing he cannot lose.

Beatrix Ashgrove stood at the rail in the dark and admitted, to the river and no one else, that she had taken his gold knowing it, and that the gold had felt less like payment than like a door being opened somewhere below decks, slowly, and that she had no idea what was coming up through it.

Above the willows the stars were hard and cold. The current pulled at the hull, patient, insistent, always upstream, always toward the high water she had sworn off a decade ago.

She went below, at last, and did not sleep.
