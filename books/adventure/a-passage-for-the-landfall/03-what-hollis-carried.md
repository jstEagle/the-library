## 3. What Hollis Carried

The fog came up off the Vell like something alive, the way it always did in the hour before dawn, rolling down from the high country in slow grey tongues that swallowed the banks and the willows and finally the river itself, until there was nothing in the world but the deck beneath my feet, the rail under my hand, and the sound of the Kestrel's engine turning over beneath us like the heartbeat of some patient animal.

I stood at the stern rail with my blanket around my shoulders and watched Beatrix Ashgrove work.

She didn't know I was watching. Or she did, and she'd decided I wasn't worth minding, which amounted to the same thing with a woman like her. She stood at the wheel with her coat collar turned up and her hands bare on the spokes, and she steered by something other than her eyes. I'd been on the river long enough to know what I was seeing. Most pilots watched the water. She listened to it. When the fog thickened and the current swung wide around a shoal that no chart in Grayhollow marked properly, she felt it come through the wheel a half-second before it showed, and she answered it, easy as breathing, and the Kestrel slid through the white like a knife through cloth.

Forty years I'd walked mountains. I'd crossed scree fields at altitude with my lungs full of blood and my hands frozen to a pick handle, and I'd never once been afraid the way a man is afraid on water, because stone doesn't lie to you. Stone is what it is. But a river lies every minute of every day. It shows you a smooth face over a drowned tree. It hums you to sleep and then takes your boat apart. And here was this woman reading its lies like a letter from an old friend.

I coughed into my fist, careful, careful, and checked the handkerchief after by feel alone, in the dark, the way I'd learned to. Red, but not the worst it had been. The fever sat behind my eyes like a coal that wouldn't go out, and my chest made a sound when I breathed that I chose not to listen to.

"You should be below," she said, without turning her head. Her voice carried flat across the water, not raised at all. "Deck's wet. Air's cold."

"So it is."

"I'm not saying it twice."

"I heard you the first time, Captain." I came forward along the rail, slow, because slow was all I had left, and took up a place at her elbow where a pilot can tolerate a passenger — close enough to talk, far enough not to crowd the wheel. "I've slept enough in my life to last. I'd rather see this."

"See what? Fog?"

"The river from the inside of a boat." I said it lightly, and it was even mostly true. "Thirty years prospecting the headwaters and I always came at the water from the banks. Crossed it when I had to. Never rode it."

"Then you've been sensible." She adjusted the wheel a finger's width. Somewhere off to port, invisible, the current muttered over gravel, and I watched her hear it and answer before my own ears had even found the sound. "Riding it gets people killed."

"So I've heard. There was a boat went down below Grayhollow last month, wasn't there? Dredge channel cut across the fairway where no one had any call to put one." I kept my voice idle. Idle was a skill, like reading water. "Company work, they say. Unmarked."

Her hands went very still on the spokes. Not a flinch. A stillness, deliberate, the way a animal goes still when it scents something on the wind and hasn't decided yet whether to run or fight.

"Company work," she agreed, in a voice that agreed with nothing at all.

"They say the pilot of that boat was a good one. Pulled two of her crew out of the water after, and wouldn't so much as take a drink with them after."

"Did they say that." Not a question. She turned the wheel and the Kestrel leaned, gentle, and the fog closed around us again.

I let it lie there between us. That was the trick of it — you don't pull on a knot to see if it holds. You leave it be and come back tomorrow. I'd spent a lifetime reading men who didn't want to be read, claim-jumpers and claim-holders, survey men with soft voices and hard ledgers, and the ones you had to fear were never the ones who talked. They were the ones who went still.

She was still now.

* * *

The fog burned off by midmorning into a thin, high, washed-out light, and the Vell showed itself: brown-green and muscular, sliding between hills that grew steeper as we climbed upstream, the banks giving way from willow flat to cut stone, the current thickening under us like something waking up. We passed a Meridian Company barge lashed to a mooring buoy, grey hull, grey tarp, grey men who watched us pass without waving, and I saw Beatrix watch them back with an expression I filed away with the rest.

I slept in the afternoon — or lay in the bunk in the bow cabin pretending to, which for a dying man amounts to much the same — and I thought about what I was doing.

Here is what I had, and I laid it out on the bunk beside me in my mind the way I'd lay out tools on a canvas sheet: a map, wax-sealed, that had cost more to make than any map I ever drew. A cache at the source of the Vell that no living soul but me knew the way to. Papers in that cache that would hang the Meridian Company from its own yardarm if they ever saw daylight. And a daughter in Callow's Rest who believed I was dead in every way that mattered, and had believed it so long that the belief had grown bones and a spine and could stand on its own.

And a body that was failing faster than the river rose.

I'd watched Beatrix Ashgrove for one day now, and here is what a lifetime of reading men told me: she helped, and then she moved on. The two survivors from the wrecked boat — I'd heard their story in a Grayhollow tavern before I ever saw her, heard how she'd pulled them from the water and vanished before they'd finished thanking her — that wasn't coldness. I knew coldness. I'd been cold myself for twenty years, cold enough to walk away from a girl of nine and never send word. What I saw in Ashgrove was a woman who had decided that being owed nothing was the only safe way to live. Help, then move on, before the helping turns into a thread, and the thread into a rope, and the rope into something a man can use to hold you.

I'd been that man once. I'd held on to nothing so hard that I'd let go of the only thing worth holding.

The fever climbed through the afternoon. I knew the signs the way you know weather in your own valley: the coal behind the eyes banking hotter, the sweat that came cold, the way sounds went distant and then too near. I got up on deck at dusk because I wanted to see her face in the last light, and because I was testing something, and I am not too proud a man to write down that I was testing her, even then, even with what little time I had.

"You know this water well," I said.

"Well enough."

"Better than well. You read it like scripture." I leaned on the rail beside her, and the hills were going purple and the first star was out over the ridge to the east, and I said, casual as asking the time: "You have family, Captain? People who'd know where you are tonight?"

The silence stretched. I counted it. Five seconds. Six. A woman with nothing to hide answers in two.

"No," she said.

"No one at all?"

"Did you buy passage or a conversation, Hollis?"

"Both, if the rate's fair." I smiled, and my smile used to work on people, and I saw it not work on her at all. "I ask because I'm a man with a long road behind him and not much road ahead, and a man in my position starts to wonder what the point of it was. Whether anything he carried is going to arrive anywhere. Whether anyone's waiting."

"Then I'd say that's a question for a priest, not a riverboat captain."

"Priests want confessions. I'm looking for something simpler. I'm looking to know whether promises mean anything on this river."

She looked at me then. First time she'd given me her whole face since Grayhollow, and I'll set down what I saw, because it matters: I saw a woman younger than her hands. Her eyes had done forty years of work and the rest of her hadn't caught up. And behind the wariness there was something else, quick and quickly buried — a hunger, the kind you see in people who have decided they're not allowed to want anything.

"Promises mean what they cost," she said. "That's all they ever mean. A promise that costs you nothing is worth nothing."

"And if it costs everything?"

"Then you'd better be sure you want to pay." She turned back to the wheel. "Get below, Hollis. The air's turning."

I went. But I went smiling, because I had my answer, or the shape of one. She hadn't said promises were worthless. A woman who believed that would have said it fast and easy, the way you say a thing you've never examined. She'd said promises cost. Which meant she'd paid for one, somewhere, sometime, and knew the price to the last copper.

You can build on ground like that.

* * *

The fever broke loose in the night.

I knew it was coming the way you know a storm is coming — the pressure, the taste of it — and I'd made my preparations the way I made all my preparations, methodical, a man who'd spent his life alone in country where a mistake meant death. The oilcloth packet was in my coat, against my chest, where it had been since Grayhollow. The map inside it. The letter to Wren, written and rewritten eleven times and still wrong. The little brass locket with the curl of hair I'd carried since she was small enough to sleep in the crook of my arm.

The fever took me around midnight, and it took me properly.

I won't write down what I saw. Fever dreams are private country and I've poached in enough of other people's. But I remember surfacing once, more than once, and hearing sounds that didn't belong to my dream: boots on deck boards. A voice, low and level, giving orders to no one — to the river, maybe, or to herself. The creak of the wheel. And once, a hand on my forehead, cool and dry and brief, gone before I could open my eyes, and a voice saying, not unkindly, in the flat tone of a woman who would deny it under oath:

"Stubborn old man."

I surfaced again near dawn, in that grey hour when the fog comes up off the water, and I knew two things with the clarity the fever leaves you like a gift: I knew that I was dying, and I knew that I had found the one.

Not the one for love — I'd had my love and buried it, and I'd made my bed of stone after and slept in it twenty years, and that was my doing and my debt. The one for the carrying. The one who would help, and then want to move on, and who would have to choose, at the end, between the moving on and the thing I was about to ask of her. Because that was the only kind of person you could trust with a thing like mine. Not a believer — believers sell out for the next belief. Not a hero — heroes die of the wanting to be one. A woman with armor, and a crack in the armor, and something alive behind the crack that she couldn't quite kill no matter how she tried.

I'd watched her pull two strangers out of a flooded channel and refuse their gratitude. I'd watched her listen to the river through a wooden wheel. I'd watched her ask a dying stranger if he was warm, in a voice that pretended she hadn't asked.

I lay in the bunk in the grey light and listened to her boots on the deck above, steady as a heartbeat, and I thought: *Wren, my girl, I have found her. I have found the woman who is going to carry my ghost up the river, and she doesn't know it yet, and she is going to hate me for it, and she is going to do it anyway, because she is the kind of woman who helps and then tries to leave and can't.*

The cough came then, the deep one, the one that brought the copper taste and left me shaking, and I pressed the handkerchief to my mouth and waited it out, and when it passed I lay looking at the beams of the cabin roof and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since Grayhollow.

Days. Not weeks. The river was rising with the melt and the Kestrel was making good time, but the fever was making better.

Above me, the boots crossed the deck and stopped. A knock at the cabin door — two, brief, no ceremony.

"You alive down there?" Beatrix Ashgrove's voice, flat as the water at dawn.

"Barely," I said. "Come in and see, if you like. I make a fine corpse. Very lifelike."

The door opened and she stood in it, backlit by the grey, and she looked at me the way she looked at the river — reading, listening, answering something I hadn't said. Her eyes went to the handkerchief in my fist. I hadn't hidden it fast enough. I watched her see the red on it, and I watched her decide not to have seen it, and I understood that we were going to lie to each other for a while yet, she and I, both of us too proud and too careful, circling like two animals at a waterhole, each waiting for the other to drink first.

"Breakfast in ten minutes," she said. "Eat it. All of it."

"Yes, Captain."

"And Hollis." She paused in the doorway, and the fog moved behind her, and for a moment her face was unreadable as deep water. "Whatever it is you're carrying in that coat, that you think I don't know about—"

My heart stopped. I'd swear to it. In that moment it stopped dead in my chest, and the fever and the dying and the river all went away, and there was only her voice, level and low.

"—keep it dry," she said. "This boat leaks."

Then she was gone, and the deck took her boots, and I lay back in the bunk and laughed until the laughing turned to coughing, and the coughing left me weak and grinning at the beams like a madman.

She knew. Of course she knew. She'd known since Grayhollow, maybe — the way I'd held the coat, the way I checked it, the way a man guards a thing he's dying over. She'd known and she'd said nothing and she'd taken my money anyway and pointed her boat upstream, into water no cargo run needed, and I understood that I had been read, thorough as a chart, by a woman who pretended she couldn't be read at all.

Two of a kind, then. That was the whole of it. Two liars on a leaky boat, each certain they were the only one aboard who could keep a secret.

I closed my eyes and let the fever have me again, and for the first time since the mountains, I wasn't afraid of what I'd see when it took me. Because whatever came, I'd woken to the sound of her boots every time. Steady on the deck. Steady as a heartbeat. Steady as a promise that costs.

The river climbed, and the fog held, and somewhere ahead of us, up where the charts went blank and the water ran cold and clean, my daughter was waking in a town she'd never leave, hating a father she thought she'd never see again.

*Hold on, Wren girl*, I thought, as the fever folded over me like dark water. *Hold on. Help is coming. It just doesn't know it yet.*
