## 4. The Trade

The fog came off the Vell that night like something poured, thick and white and smelling of cold iron, and Beatrix Ashgrove stood at the wheel of the Kestrel with her hands light on the spokes and her mind on nothing at all — which was the way she liked it. Nothing at all. The river ahead, the river behind, the slow thump of the paddle wheel turning over like a patient heart.

She had been running the night shift alone since Grayhollow, three days back. The Kestrel carried a hold full of salt pork, nails, and lamp oil for the upriver landings, cargo dull enough to keep the Company's clerks from looking twice at her manifests, and she wanted it delivered and herself gone before the first hard frost turned the shallows to glass. That was the whole of her ambition that autumn: deliver, be paid, move on. It had been the whole of her ambition for ten years, and it had kept her alive when grander ambitions had gotten other people drowned.

Behind her, in the deckhouse that served as passenger cabin, Hollis coughed.

She heard it through the bulkhead the way you hear weather coming — not all at once but in waves, long dry rattles followed by a wet, tearing sound that made her jaw tighten. He'd been coughing since Grayhollow. He'd been bleeding longer than that, though he still believed she didn't know. She had found the handkerchief once, folded small on the deck outside his cabin door where it had blown, rust-brown blooms soaked through the linen like leaves pressed in an old book. She'd hung it on his rail without a word and never mentioned it, and he had taken it in without a word, and between them they had built a small architecture of silence that both of them pretended was privacy rather than cowardice.

The fog thickened until the lanterns on the texas roof made only soft gray smears of themselves. Beatrix rang down to Finch — no, not Finch yet; the stoker she had then was a sullen Cardolan man named Brack who drank — and slowed the wheel. There was no point driving blind into water she couldn't read. She let the Kestrel drift with the current, engine ticking over at half, and listened to the river feel its own way down through the dark.

That was when Hollis came up onto the pilothouse deck.

She heard him before she saw him — the shuffle of boots gone loose at the heels, the hand finding the doorframe. When he stepped into the lantern light she saw what three days of fever had done to him. He had boarded at Grayhollow looking like a man built out of fence wire and bad luck, gaunt as a November tree, but there had been something upright in him then, some iron pin holding the whole lean structure together. The pin was pulling free now. His skin had gone the color of tallow, gray-yellow, and his eyes burned in it like coals raked over too late in the season. He wore his coat buttoned wrong.

"Captain Ashgrove." His voice was gravel rolled in wool. "You're drifting."

"I'm reading the fog." She didn't turn from the window. "You should be lying down."

"I've been lying down for three days." He came to stand beside her at the wheel, close enough that she could hear the whistle in each breath, the fine bubbling sound underneath it that she knew, that she hated knowing. Water in the lungs. Or worse. "I want to talk while I can still stand up to do it."

"Talk about what?"

"Promises."

The word sat in the air between them. Beatrix adjusted the wheel a half-spoke, unnecessary, just to have something to do with her hands. Below them the river slid by unseen, and somewhere off the port bow a night bird called once, a raw descending note, and did not call again.

"I don't deal in promises," she said. "I deal in freight. You paid your passage. When we make Callow's Rest landing, you get off, and that's the end of our business."

"That's not the end of it." He laughed, or tried to, and the laugh turned into a cough that bent him double against the wheel housing. She reached for him without deciding to, one arm braced across his chest, and held him up until the spasm passed. He weighed almost nothing. That frightened her more than the blood had. A man could hide a great deal behind a coat, but not weight.

When he straightened, there was fresh color on his lips and something new in his face — a kind of settled calm that she recognized and distrusted, because she had seen it before, years ago, on another river, in another life, on the faces of people who had stopped fighting and started arranging.

"You asked me nothing at Grayhollow," he said. "I offered coin, you took it, you never asked why I was going upstream or what I was carrying. Most captains would have asked."

"Most captains talk too much."

"Most captains are alive because they ask questions." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, casually, as if she wouldn't see, as if the whole boat hadn't heard him dying for three days. "I'm going to ask you something now, Captain, and I'd take it kindly if you'd let me finish before you answer. Because I don't think you'll answer yes right away, and I don't have time to watch you come around gradual."

"Hollis—"

"The map," he said.

The word went through her like a fishhook. She kept her face still. Ten years of practice kept her face still.

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"You do." No anger in it. Almost gentle. "You searched my duffel the second night. While I slept. You were quick about it, I'll grant — I only woke at the end — but you looked, and whatever you found, you put it back exact, even the knot on the drawstring, which was a sailor's knot and not yours. A woman who re-ties a man's knots so he won't know she's been looking is a woman who understands what papers are worth." He leaned closer, and the fever-heat came off him like heat off a stove door. "So don't insult us both."

Beatrix said nothing. The paddle wheel thumped. The fog pressed against the windows like a living thing trying to get in.

* * *

He told her sitting down, because standing had become more than he could manage. She got him into the pilothouse chair — the good one, hers, with the horsehair stuffing gone flat — and wrapped him in the wool blanket she kept for the winter runs, and he talked with his eyes half-closed, watching the fog instead of her, which she understood. Some things were easier said to a window.

He had been a surveyor once, before he was a prospector. Company-trained, Company-sworn, twelve years in their service mapping the upper Vell and the tributaries that fed it, back when the headwaters country was still open ground and the maps were drawn in ink instead of ledgers and lawyers. He had walked every mile of river above Sorrow Bend. He had named half the rapids himself, small immortalities that nobody would ever attach to his name now.

"And then I found what I wasn't sent to find," he said.

"Which was?"

"Sick water." His eyes closed. Opened again. "Upstream of the Company mines, the water ran clean as glass. Downstream, it ran the color of old brass, and everything that drank it died — fish first, then the livestock the miners' families kept, then slow, the miners themselves. I sampled it. Properly, the way I was trained. Sealed vials, chain of custody, affidavits from men who worked the adits and knew what was being dumped and where. Twelve affidavits. Eleven of those men are dead now. The twelfth is me, and I'm working on it."

Beatrix felt the cold come up through the deck plates into her boots. Not fear, exactly. Recognition. She had spent ten years learning what companies did with inconvenient paper, and the learning had cost her things she did not name even to herself.

"What did you do with it?" she asked quietly.

"Tried to give it to the magistrate at Fort Meridian. He thanked me, shook my hand, and had my samples confiscated within the hour. Tried a newspaper man in Copper Landing. Newspaper man sold me to the Company for enough to buy his press twice over. After that I stopped giving it to people and started hiding it." A ghost of a smile moved under the gray skin. "Surveyor's habit. I hid it where only someone who could read the river would ever find it. And I drew the map."

"In your duffel."

"In my duffel." He turned his head and looked at her directly for the first time, and his eyes were terribly clear, the fever having burned away everything except the last thing he intended to do. "Wax-sealed. Never opened by anyone but me. It shows the source of the Vell — the true source, above the last lock, above anything your charts carry — and the cache. Samples, ledgers, affidavits, letters. Enough paper to hang the Meridian Company from its own signboard, if anyone ever has the spine to use it."

"Why tell me this?"

"Because I'm dying, Captain, and dead men can't walk to Callow's Rest."

The name landed in the pilothouse like a dropped tool. Callow's Rest. Her next delivery stop. She had it written on her manifest in her own neat hand.

"You have a daughter there," Beatrix said. It wasn't a question. He'd been circling toward it for days, oblique questions over supper — *do you have family, captain; do you believe a person can owe a debt to someone they've never met* — and she had deflected every one, and now the circling ended.

"Wren." Saying the name seemed to cost him, and also seemed to steady him, the way gripping a rail steadies a man on a pitching deck. "Nineteen years old. Runs an assay office her uncle left her, barely. Hates me, and has earned the right to. I left when she was nine. Told myself it was to keep the Company's eyes off her. Told myself a lot of things." He coughed, softly this time, and pressed the blanket to his mouth, and when he lowered it Beatrix looked away, deliberately, granting him that much. "I'm not asking you to make her love me. I'm not fool enough to ask that. I'm asking you to put what I found in her hands. What she does with it is hers."

"No," Beatrix said.

It came out fast, before thought, the way a flinch comes out. She stood up from the wheel housing and moved to the window because she needed the glass in front of her, needed the fog and the black water and the simple problem of a river that only wanted to drown her honestly.

"Captain—"

"No. You listen to me, old man." Her voice was low and hard. "You know what you're handing me? You're handing me a reason. People with reasons go places they shouldn't and do things they can't undo, and companies burn boats over reasons like yours. I've seen it. I've—" She stopped herself at the edge of it, ten years deep, and turned around. "I run freight. Salt pork and nails. I help people when helping costs nothing, and then I move on. That's how I've stayed alive, and I don't break the rule for dying strangers with beautiful maps."

Hollis watched her from the chair, wrapped in wool, dying by inches, and did not look afraid of her, which was somehow the worst part.

"That's a fine rule," he said. "How's it been keeping you?"

The question went in under her ribs like a splinter of ice. She stared at him and he stared back, mild and merciless, a man with nothing left to lose spending his last coin on the truth.

"Here's my offer, and it's the only one I'll make," he went on. "Not coin. I know you won't take coin for this; you'd smell the trap in it. I ask for a promise. One promise, sworn proper, the way river people swear — on the water you steer by. Bring what I found to Wren Hollis in Callow's Rest. That's all. Put it in her hands. Then your rule resumes, and you can run to the ends of the earth, and I'll have no claim on you beyond that."

"And if I say no?"

"Then when we make the Callow's Rest landing, I'll crawl off this boat on my hands and knees and die in the street, and the map will go into the river with me, and the eleven dead men will stay dead for nothing, and the Company will go on poisoning the water every soul on this river drinks." He said it without drama, an inventory, a man reading a bill of lading. "And you'll go on running freight, Captain. Alive. Comfortable. Help-then-move-on. How's that been keeping you?"

Beatrix turned back to the window. The fog was thinning — she could see the first pale suggestion of the eastern bank, the black teeth of deadfalls, the water sliding silver-gray beneath it all, indifferent and honest and going somewhere whether she went or not.

She thought about the handkerchief on the rail. She thought about a girl of nineteen running a failing assay office in a mountain town, waiting for a father who was never coming, learning to stop waiting. She thought about a different girl, ten years and a lifetime ago, who had learned what happened when you stood still, and had built a whole religion out of moving.

"I'd want to say no," she said finally, to the glass. "You should know that. Everything in me wants to say no."

"I know," Hollis said. "That's why I trust you. A woman who says yes easy says yes cheap."

Silence, except the wheel. Except the river.

"How do I swear it?" she asked. "Proper. River-way."

He made her open the pilothouse window, made her dip two fingers in the rain barrel kept on the deck for fire, made her touch the wet fingers to the wood of the wheel itself, the old oak worn smooth and dark by forty years of hands.

"Say it after me. On this water I steer by, and every water it feeds."

"On this water I steer by, and every water it feeds."

"I swear to carry what Hollis found to Wren Hollis, in Callow's Rest, into her hands and no other's."

"I swear to carry what Hollis found to Wren Hollis, in Callow's Rest, into her hands and no other's."

"So sworn."

"So sworn."

The words hung in the cold air of the pilothouse, and Beatrix felt them settle onto her like ballast — heavy, permanent, changing the trim of the boat. She had sworn oaths before, in another life, and knew exactly what it cost to keep them and what it cost to break them, and she had promised herself never again, and here she was, hand wet with river water on an old oak wheel, bound.

Hollis exhaled, long and slow, and something in him seemed to finish. Not die — finish. Like a sentence reaching its period.

"There's a copy of the key pattern in the map margin," he murmured, already sinking. "She'll understand. She used to solve my ciphers for sport, when she was small. Cleverer than me, always." His eyes drifted shut. "Thank you, Captain Ashgrove. Whatever your name was before."

Her blood went cold. But he was already past seeing her face, sunk deep in the blanket, breath whistling, and she stood at the wheel in the graying dawn-light and did not correct him, because there was nothing left to protect by correcting him.

* * *

He lasted until dawn.

She stayed with him through the last hours, in the deckhouse cabin, because she could not have said why and did not try to say why. Somewhere past midnight the fever broke — not upward into health but downward, burning itself out like a lamp running dry — and he surfaced once, near the end, and looked at her with terrible clarity.

"It's not gold," he said. "In case you wondered. Men always wonder. It's not gold, Captain. It's better than gold. It's true."

"I know," she said, and found that she meant it.

"Good," he whispered. "Then you'll do."

Near dawn she dozed in the chair by his bunk, and woke to a silence that was different from sleeping-silence, a silence with edges to it. The river sounds came through the bulkhead — water, water, always water — and Hollis lay still with his hands crossed on his chest, arranged by no one, and his face in the gray morning light looked ten years younger and completely untroubled, a man who had set down a load he'd carried too far.

Beatrix sat for a long time without moving.

Then she went to the duffel, and untied the sailor's knot the way he'd shown her without showing her, and took out the map — oilcloth-wrapped, wax-sealed, lighter than it had any right to be, the way a thing is light when it weighs more than lead. She held it a moment. Through the porthole, the Vell ran north toward the mountains, toward Callow's Rest, toward a daughter who didn't know yet that she was about to inherit a dead man's war.

Downriver, far behind them, faint on the morning air, came a sound she had been listening for without admitting it: the low horn of a Company packet boat, making way upstream, two locks back and closing.

Beatrix wrapped the map inside her coat, against her skin, and went up to ring the engine room to full ahead.
