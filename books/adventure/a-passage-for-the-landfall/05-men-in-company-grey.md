## 5. Men in Company Grey

The lock at Tarnmoor sat in the river like a held breath.

Beatrix saw it from two miles out — the grey smoke of the Company compound smearing the morning sky, the long stone arms of the lock chamber reaching into the current, and the flag above the gatehouse hanging limp in windless air. She had passed through Tarnmoor eleven times in ten years. She knew the lockkeeper's name, knew the toll, knew which cleat was rotten and which line would hold. She had never once seen the Company's flag flown there. The Meridian did not bother with small water. The Meridian bothered with ore barges and gunboats and the slow, patient acquisition of everything that floated, but a cargo steamer the size of the *Kestrel*, hauling timber ties and tinned peaches, was beneath their notice.

Beneath their notice. She said it to herself as she brought the boat in, and the words tasted like a lie told too many times.

Hollis lay below, in the bunk behind her cabin, wrapped in blankets that had stopped warming him hours ago. The fever had broken sometime in the night — broken the way ice breaks, all at once and for the worse — and now he slept a sleep that was too still, his breath coming in shallow pulls, his hands folded on his chest as if he had already begun arranging himself for whatever came next. The map was in the floor of her cabin, under a board she'd pried up with a chisel at four in the morning, wrapped in oilcloth, sealed in wax the color of old blood. She had not looked at it again. She did not need to. She could feel it the way you feel a stone in your boot — a small hard weight that changed the shape of every step.

"Captain." Finch appeared at her elbow, all elbows and soot, their cap pulled low. The kid had a genius for materializing exactly when Beatrix wanted solitude. "Smoke. Lots of it. That's not the lock's boiler, is it?"

"No."

"Company?"

"Probably just their tug."

Finch squinted at the grey smear on the sky, then at her, with the particular flat skepticism of the young, who have not yet learned to pretend. "You're doing the thing where you lie calm."

"I don't lie calm. I am calm."

"You lie calm," Finch said, and went forward to handle the bow line, which was the kid's way of ending an argument they intended to win later.

Beatrix took the *Kestrel* into the lock approach at half speed, watching the water. The channel was dredged and marked, the pilings fresh-tarred, and none of that was normal either. Tarnmoor's approach had been a shoal-ridden mess for a decade; she'd scraped her keel here twice in the early years. Someone had spent money on this water recently, and money spent on water meant something under it, or something meant to come through it.

The lock gates stood open. Waiting.

She brought the boat in, and the gates closed behind her with a sound like a coffin lid drawn gently shut, and that was when the men in grey came down the lock wall.

* * *

There were eight of them. Not the usual toll clerk with his ledger and his bored arithmetic — eight men in the slate-grey uniform of the Meridian Company's river service, rifles slung, boots ringing on the wet stone in a cadence too neat to be accidental. They spread along the lock wall with the unhurried precision of men who had done this many times and been paid well for it. At their center came a tall man in a better coat.

Beatrix stood at the rail and made her face into the thing it had learned to be over ten years: pleasant, forgettable, a working captain with working hands and nothing behind her eyes worth the trouble of reading.

"Morning," she called down. "Toll's in the wheelhouse, same as always. Timber ties and tinned peaches, manifest's on the clipboard."

The tall man did not answer immediately. He stood at the edge of the lock wall and looked at the *Kestrel* the way an assayer looks at ore — not at the surface, but through it, estimating what might be inside. He was perhaps forty, lean without being thin, with a face that had been handsome once and had since been sanded down to something more useful. His hair was iron-grey at the temples and cut with military shortness. His eyes were the color of river ice, and they did not move quickly, and when they stopped moving they stopped completely, and Beatrix had been on the water long enough to know that stillness in a man was worse than any amount of noise.

"Captain Ashgrove," he said.

Her name. Her river name, the one she'd built out of driftwood and ten years of small honest cargoes — and hearing it in his mouth felt like a hand on the back of her neck.

"You have me at a disadvantage," she said, and made it light.

"Sergeant Corvane. Meridian Company, Office of River Claims." He produced a folded paper but did not open it, did not offer it, merely held it as a man holds a thing he does not yet need. "We're conducting an inspection. Routine."

"Routine," Beatrix repeated. "Eight rifles for a routine."

"Rivers are dangerous places, Captain." Corvane's voice was quiet, unhurried, the voice of a man who had never once needed to raise it because everyone around him did the raising for him. "Drownings. Fires. Men dying of fevers far from home. We find a great many bodies in this river." A pause, thin as wire. "And a great many papers."

The word went through her like a fishhook. She kept her hands loose on the rail. She kept her breathing even. Somewhere below her feet, behind a floorboard, a dead man's map lay wrapped in oilcloth, and it seemed to Beatrix that she could hear it — that the whole boat had gone silent around the fact of it, the engine, the water against the hull, Finch's breathing in the bow, all of it hushed as though the river itself were listening.

"Paper doesn't do well on the water," she said. "Damp gets into everything."

"It does," Corvane agreed. "That's why we're thorough." He turned his head slightly. "Sergeant Vess. Board her."

* * *

They went through the *Kestrel* the way a tide goes through a wreck — patient, total, indifferent.

Beatrix stood at the rail and let them. That was the first discipline of it: to stand still while strangers put their hands on everything you owned. Two of them went through the cargo deck, pulling timber ties and tossing them aside, opening crates of tinned peaches with bayonets and not apologizing for the dents. One went through the wheelhouse and read her logs with his finger moving under each line like a schoolmaster's. Two more went below.

She watched them descend the ladder to her cabin and made herself count the rivets on the lock wall. Forty-one. Forty-two. If they pried up the floorboard — if they thought to pry up the floorboard — then everything ended here, in a lock chamber at Tarnmoor, and Hollis's daughter would never know her father had thought of her at the end, and Beatrix would spend whatever years they left her learning what the Company did with people who carried things.

She had buried the map under the bilge stringer instead. No — she corrected herself, forcing the memory to be precise, because precision was all she had — she had buried it under the floorboard by the stove, where the boards were warped with heat and would take a crowbar and a curse to lift. She had done it at four in the morning. She had done it well. She had done it in perhaps ninety seconds, and ninety seconds was not enough time, and she knew it, and there was nothing to be done about it now except to stand at the rail with her hands loose and her face forgettable and count rivets.

Below her, muffled by the deck, she heard a man say, "Who's in this bunk?"

She was moving before she decided to move. That was the trouble with the *Kestrel* — she had built the boat so that everything in it answered to her hands without consultation, and now her hands were taking her down the ladder into her own cabin where three men in Company grey stood around the bunk where Hollis lay.

Hollis looked dead.

She had seen enough dead men to know the difference, and Hollis had found, in his fever, a stillness that was very nearly the real thing — breath so shallow it hid in the folds of the blanket, face grey as the lock stone, hands folded. The Company men stood over him the way men stand over something they have been sent to find, and Beatrix understood in one cold drop of clarity that this was not a search. This was a retrieval. They knew a man had come down the river. They knew he was dying or dead. They were looking for the body and the papers, and they had found the body.

"That's my passenger," she said. "Dying. Not that it's any business of the Company's."

"Everything on this river is the Company's business, Captain." The one who'd spoken — Vess, presumably — was younger, with a sergeant's stripes and a sergeant's appetite for using them. "Name?"

"Hollis."

"First name?"

"Didn't ask. Men who cough like that don't want to talk."

Vess looked at Hollis a long moment. Then, deliberately, he reached down and took the blanket at the old man's shoulder and pulled it back.

Beatrix did not flinch. She had spent ten years learning not to flinch, and the learning held, and she stood there while a stranger in grey turned a dying man over like merchandise — checked his pockets, ran his hands along the seams of his coat, lifted the pillow, and found nothing, because Beatrix had already taken everything, because she had gone through his coat herself on the second night when he slept, and found the wax seal and the folded papers and nothing else, because Hollis had carried his treasure in one place and one place only, and that place was now beneath a warped floorboard by a stove.

"Empty," Vess said, and there was disappointment in it, the flat disappointment of a man who will be asked questions later.

"Men that sick don't carry much," Beatrix said. "Sickness carries them."

Vess straightened and looked at her, and for a moment she thought he would push — she saw it in him, the itch of it — and then Corvane's voice came down the ladder, unhurried, quiet, and stopped the whole cabin like a hand laid flat on a table.

"Captain. A word."

* * *

Corvane had not boarded. He stood at the rail where she'd left him, hands clasped behind his back, watching the lock water with the mild interest of a man watching something he already owned. When she came up the ladder he did not turn at once. He let her stand. He was good at that — at making waiting into a small punishment, at letting silence do the work that other men needed rank for.

"Your passenger," Corvane said. "Hollis. A prospector."

"Like I said, I didn't ask."

"Prospectors are a superstitious trade. They carry things. Samples, mostly. Ore in little bottles, notes in little books. They carry them for years." He turned then, and the ice-colored eyes came to rest on her, and stayed. "A prospector came down from the upper Vell six weeks ago. He was seen at Ferrow's Landing. He was seen at the Company store at Coldbank, where he sold nothing and bought nothing and asked questions about the headwaters. And then he was not seen anywhere at all, until now, dying, on a cargo boat out of Grayhollow." He paused. "You can see how a man in my position might find the shape of that story interesting."

"I can see how a man in your position finds everything interesting. It's the job."

Something moved at the corner of Corvane's mouth. It was not quite a smile, but it had a smile's architecture, and Beatrix understood that she was being measured — that every word she gave him was a weight on a scale, and he was reading the balance.

"We're looking for papers, Captain. A dead man's papers. Notes, maps, correspondence — anything relating to his work upstream. If such papers came aboard your boat, knowingly or otherwise, the Company would look on their return very favorably. There is a standing reward." He named a figure. It was more than the *Kestrel* had earned in three good years. He named it the way a man names the price of nails.

"I haul timber and peaches," Beatrix said. "If your prospector left papers on my boat, they're welcome to them. You've seen my boat. You've been through my boat. There's no papers."

"No," Corvane agreed softly. "There don't appear to be." He looked past her, down the length of the deck, at the *Kestrel* — at the patched rail, the soot-dark stack, the worn wheelhouse glass — and his gaze had the quality of a man memorizing. "You've worked this river ten years, Captain Ashgrove. Alone. No crew to speak of, no partner, no family listed at any port office. A woman who owns her boat outright and owes no one. Do you know how rare that is? On any river?"

"I know how rare it is. I worked for it."

"I imagine you did." He unfolded the paper he had been holding — the one he had never needed — and glanced at it without any appearance of reading it. "Grayhollow. Ten years. And before Grayhollow, nothing. A woman appears out of nowhere with a boat and a clean ledger, and the river accepts her, and no one asks where she came from. Rivers are forgiving that way." He folded the paper again, unhurried. "The Company is less forgiving. The Company remembers."

The lock chamber was very quiet. Water seeped through the great gates in thin silver threads. Somewhere above, a gull cried once and thought better of it.

"I don't know what you're implying," Beatrix said, "but I'd thank you to say it plain or not at all."

"I'm not implying anything, Captain. I'm introducing myself." Corvane stepped closer, and up close she could see the seams around his eyes, the fine map of a face that had spent decades watching other men. "Because I believe your passenger carried something off the upper Vell that belongs to the Company. I believe it will surface — papers always do; they're like bodies that way, they rise when the water warms. And when it surfaces, it will surface near someone, and I would prefer that someone be a sensible woman with a boat and no attachments." He let that sit. "Sensible women do well on this river, Captain. They grow old. They keep their boats. They keep their names exactly where they left them — unexamined."

There it was. He had not said her old name. He had not needed to. He had laid his hand flat on the water over the thing she had drowned ten years ago, and pressed, gently, once, and let her feel the shape of it underneath.

Beatrix's heart was going hard against her ribs, hard enough that she felt certain he could see it, and she made her voice come out level anyway, made it come out bored, made it the voice of a woman who hauled peaches.

"Are you threatening me, Sergeant?"

"Of course not." Corvane smiled then, fully, and it was the worst thing yet, because it was warm. "I'm offering you a future. The other thing — the thing I'd be doing if I were the kind of man you're imagining — that would look nothing like a conversation at a lock." He stepped back. "We're done. You'll be through the gates in a quarter hour. Vess will return your boat to the condition he found it." A pause. "Give my regards to your passenger. I do hope he recovers. Dead men can't answer questions, but they can't sign affidavits either, and the Company does so prefer signatures."

He went up the lock wall without hurry, his men folding around him, and Beatrix stood at the rail of her own boat and watched the grey coats diminish along the stone, and only when the last of them was gone did she discover that her hands, loose on the rail all this while, had closed hard enough on the wood to ache.

* * *

The gates opened. The *Kestrel* slid out of Tarnmoor into the long brown morning of the river, and Beatrix took her up-current with the engine at a pace just short of suspicious.

Finch found her at the wheel within a mile. The kid didn't ask anything at first — just stood at the rail beside her, watching the banks go by, working a twist of tarred string between their fingers.

"They tore up your peaches," Finch said finally.

"I know."

"They tore up the whole forward crate. Bayonets. For peaches." Finch's voice had a hard little edge on it. "That's not looking for papers. That's looking for an excuse."

Beatrix said nothing. The river ran. Ahead, the valley narrowed into the first low shoulders of the high country, blue and far off, the direction Hollis had come from, the direction the map went, the direction of everything she had sworn on a dying man's breath to do.

"Captain," Finch said. "What's on this boat?"

She looked at the kid — at the cap pulled low, the burned-looking knuckles, the stubborn set of a jaw that reminded her, painfully, of a girl she had once been on a different river with a different name — and she weighed the truth against the danger of it, and found, to her own surprise, that she was tired of carrying it alone.

"A map," she said. "To the source of the Vell. And a dead man's promise that I'm going to keep."

Finch absorbed this with the unblinking stillness of the young. "And those men back there—"

"Want it. And know it exists. And know it came down the river on somebody's boat." Beatrix turned the wheel a degree, easing her toward the faster water in the channel's heart. "They didn't find it today. But their sergeant looked at me the way a man looks at a lock he intends to come back through."

"So we go faster."

"So we go faster," Beatrix agreed, "and we get hands. Real hands. People I can trust with a boat that's about to become worth killing for." She was already running the list in her head — every dock, every name, every drunk genius and desperate kid between here and the high water — and hating that she needed one, and knowing that she did.

Below, behind a warped floorboard, the map rode in the dark. Ahead, the river narrowed and the mountains came on.

She opened the throttle, and the *Kestrel* leaned into the current like a creature finally running in the right direction.
