## 6. Crew of Strays

Beatrix had always believed a boat told you what it needed, if you were willing to listen. The Kestrel was telling her plenty these days: a rudder pintle worn to a knife's edge, a boiler that coughed like a consumptive, and — most damning of all — water ahead that no single pair of hands could survive. She stood at the rail in Grayhollow's morning drizzle, Hollis's map sewn into the lining of her coat where it pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and made herself say the truth out loud.

"I can't run her alone."

The words tasted strange. For ten years she had run alone, and alone had been the whole point.

Grayhollow smelled of coal smoke and fish guts and the particular sourness of a town that lived off the river and resented it. Down the dock, past the tarpaulin stalls and the men mending nets with fingers gone white from cold, a hand-painted sign hung crooked over a workshop door: *Vasseur & Daughters — Marine Engineering*. Someone had scratched out *Daughters* and beneath it, in a different hand, someone else had written *Daughter* back in again. Beatrix looked at that small act of stubbornness for a long moment before she knocked.

* * *

Odile Vasseur heard the knock through the hiss of her forge and knew before she opened the door that it would be trouble. Trouble always found her now; she had stopped being surprised by it around the time they took her certification away.

She was not what the woman on the step expected. That much Odile could read in the flicker of the stranger's eyes — the quick inventory, the recalculation. A big woman, broad through the shoulders, forearms scarred with old burns and new ones, hair cropped short for practicality rather than fashion, standing in a doorway full of steam and iron filings wearing an apron that had once been white in some other decade.

"You're the Kestrel," the woman said. Not a question. "I watched you take the lock at Millrace last week. You came through on a falling gauge with your paddle wheel half out of the water."

"Someone should have told me that was difficult." Odile wiped her hands on the apron. "What do you want?"

"A head engineer. Dangerous water upstream. Pay is poor, conditions are worse, and there's a chance we'll all be shot at."

Most applicants would have laughed. This one didn't laugh either. She just studied Odile with eyes the grey-green of river water over silt, and said, "You're Vasseur. The one who built the *Coriolan*'s engines."

The name landed like a dropped wrench. Odile felt it in her chest, that familiar hot weight — the inquiry, the board of examiners, the words *gross negligence* read aloud in a room full of men who had never gotten grease under their nails in their lives. Eleven dead when the *Coriolan*'s boiler let go. Never mind that she'd signed three letters warning the owners about the cracked crown sheet. Never mind that they'd sailed her warnings and sailed anyway, because schedules were money and money talked louder than engineers. Her name went into the record. Theirs went into the settlement.

"That's me," Odile said flatly. "The one whose engines fail."

"The one whose engines were failed *by*," the woman corrected. "I read the inquiry transcript. It's public record. I make a habit of reading about boats before I trust them to anyone."

Something in Odile's chest shifted, small and painful, like a seized bearing beginning, against all odds, to turn.

"And you are?"

"Ashgrove. Captain Ashgrove." The woman hesitated, just barely, on the word captain, as if it were a coat that hadn't fit in years but still buttoned. "Well? Do you want the work or not?"

Odile looked back into her shop — at the cold forge, the unpaid rent notice pinned to the beam, the little brass engine she'd been building for no client at all, just to keep her hands honest. She thought about how a person could drown in a place this dry.

"What's wrong with her boiler?" she asked, which was as good as yes.

"Coughs under load. Loses pressure above six knots."

"Cracked firebox seam, probably. Or your feedwater's fouled." Odile untied the apron. "I'll need parts. And I don't work for anyone who calls me *the engineer* like it's an apology."

Ashgrove almost smiled. Almost. "I'd never insult you that way."

* * *

Finch saw the Kestrel first from the top of the coal heap behind Harrow & Sons' chandlery, where Finch spent six days a week shoveling black into other people's boats and dreaming, in the wordless way of the very young and very trapped, of being anywhere else. The boat sat low in the gray water, patched and patient, and there was a woman at her rail who moved like the river belonged to her — which was ridiculous, obviously, rivers didn't belong to anybody, but this woman moved like she'd been granted an exception.

Finch slid down the coal heap, leaving a black streak down the whole face of it, and was at the dock before any sensible part of themselves could raise an objection.

"You're hiring," Finch said. It came out as a statement, which was a gamble, because it wasn't one yet.

Captain Ashgrove turned. Up close she was younger than her posture suggested and older than her face, if that made sense, and Finch decided it did. Her eyes went over Finch the way Finch imagined she went over charts: thorough, unsentimental, noting every hazard.

"I'm hiring an experienced stoker," she said. "Not a child."

"I'm not a child." Fourteen going on fifteen, though Finch kept the exact number loose even in their own head, the way you keep a knife loose in its sheath. "And I'm not inexperienced. I've fired boilers since I was eleven. Harrow's, the *Marten*, the *Two Sisters* before she burned—"

"Burned?" Something moved behind the captain's eyes. "Were you aboard?"

"No." The lie came smooth as spilled oil. Finch had practiced it so long it had worn a groove. "But I know what a boiler does when someone feeds it wrong. I know what a firebox sounds like before it goes. Most stokers your age—" Finch caught it, the slip, and barreled through, "—most stokers *my age* only know how to shovel. I know how to listen."

The captain was quiet a moment. Rain ticked on the deck boards between them. Somewhere down the dock a gull screamed its one opinion about everything.

"Why do you want to leave Grayhollow?" Ashgrove asked.

It was the wrong question, or the right one asked too soon, and Finch felt the answer rise up their throat like bile — *because my mother looks at me like I set the fire, because I did set the fire, because Tam was in the loft and the roof came down and nobody will say my name in this town except to spit it* — and Finch swallowed all of it and gave the captain the coin-version instead.

"Same reason everyone wants to leave. Nothing here for me."

Ashgrove held their gaze a moment longer, and Finch had the horrible, vertiginous feeling of being *read* — of every locked door inside them being tested, gently, by someone who knew locks. Then the captain looked away, out at the river, and something in her face closed like a ledger.

"Deckhand and stoker," she said. "Room and board, wages when there's cargo money. You work hard, you don't steal, you don't ask questions about my business. Break those rules and I put you ashore wherever we happen to be, however far from anywhere that is."

"Yes, Captain."

"Don't thank me yet. Ask me again after Sorrow Bend."

Finch didn't know what Sorrow Bend was. They climbed aboard anyway, with their one bag and their one secret, and stood on the deck of the Kestrel feeling the river move underneath them like a pulse, and thought: *this is the farthest from home I have ever been.* It was thirty yards. It might as well have been the moon.

* * *

The trouble started, as Odile had predicted it would, within the hour.

The boy — Odile refused to think of Finch as anything else until proven otherwise, and even then warily — had gotten into her engine room without wiping their boots, had leaned on her clean rag pile with coal-black hands, and had pronounced, with the magnificent confidence available only to the young and ignorant, that the Kestrel's injector "looked funny."

"It is a Kestrel-pattern injector," Odile said, without looking up from the firebox seam she was chipping clean, "and it has been in service longer than you have been alive. Touch it and lose the hand."

"It's clogged. Look at the scaling on the cone—"

"I said—"

"If it's clogged it won't draw when you need it, and when you need it is going to be *Sorrow Bend*, isn't it, that's why she hired us, that's the dangerous water—" The boy was already elbow-deep, already pulling the cone free, already holding it up to the lantern light where the mineral crust showed white as bone. "See? Told you."

Odile opened her mouth to explain, slowly and with great precision, exactly how many ways the child was wrong — and then stopped, because the child was not wrong. The scaling was bad. Worse than she'd have guessed from outside. Which meant the feedwater was fouled, which meant—

"Where did you learn to see that?" she asked.

Finch shrugged, suddenly shy, the confidence draining out of them like steam from a cracked line. "Boilers talk. If you listen."

Odile took the cone from their grimy hands and inspected it properly. "Clean it. With the wire brush, not your shirt. And wipe your boots next time."

The boy grinned — a quick, unguarded flash that made them look about nine years old — and Odile turned back to her chisel before the grin could do whatever it was trying to do to her.

Above them, on deck, they heard the captain's voice, dry as kindling: "If you two are finished bonding, we cast off in twenty minutes."

"We're not bonding," they said together, and then glared at each other for having said it together.

* * *

Beatrix watched them from the pilothouse and felt something in her chest she refused to name.

It was the way they moved, that was the thing. Odile worked the engine room like a surgeon who'd been exiled to a butcher shop — precise, furious, tender when she thought no one was looking. Finch worked like a creature escaping a trap, all speed and hunger, and then sometimes, in the gaps, like a kid: humming tunelessly, stealing biscuits, asking questions about the river with naked wonder and then pretending they hadn't.

And both of them lied, fluently, about things that mattered. Beatrix recognized the accent. She'd spoken it herself for ten years.

The Kestrel backed off the Grayhollow dock into a rain gone soft as breath, and the town fell away behind them — the chandlery, the coal heaps, the crooked sign with *Daughter* written back in — and Beatrix turned her face upstream toward country she'd never run, carrying a dead man's map and two strangers who were already, against her explicit instructions, becoming something else.

Hollis's voice came back to her, that last night, thin as paper: *A boat's just wood and iron, girl. It's the people on it that make it go somewhere.*

He'd been talking about more than boats. Dying men usually were.

"Captain?" Finch appeared at the pilothouse ladder, holding two tin mugs of coffee that smelled like it had been brewed from rope. "Odile says the pressure'll hold to seven knots if we don't ask for eight. Also she says to tell you the injector's fixed, and also she says she fixed it, and also—" the kid squinted, consulting memory, "—she says if you tell anyone she let a fourteen-year-old touch her injector she'll deny everything."

"How old does she think you are?"

"Sixteen," Finch said, with the serene certainty of a professional.

Beatrix took the coffee. It was terrible. She drank it anyway, and steered the Kestrel into the fog above Grayhollow, and did not say aloud what she was already thinking: that a crew was a debt you took on, that every person aboard was another thing the river could take from her, that she had spent ten years learning to owe nothing to anyone and had just, in the space of one rainy morning, broken the rule entire.

Ahead, the fog thickened. Somewhere beyond it lay the locks, and the patrols, and the water called Sorrow Bend, and whatever the Meridian Company sent down the river looking for a dead man's papers.

"Steady as she goes," Beatrix said, to no one, to everyone, and the Kestrel went.
