## 7. Rapids at Sorrow Bend

The river told you things before it showed them to you. Beatrix had learned that much in ten years of running the Vell — that the water spoke first in the soles of your feet, a tremor through the deck planks like a word forming in a throat, and if you were patient and paid attention, you could hear what it meant to say.

She heard it now, standing at the wheel in the gray hour before dawn, her boots bare against the cold boards because she'd come up without them, drawn from her bunk by a sound she couldn't name until she was already climbing the stairs. The Kestrel's hull was humming. Not the engine hum — Odile's patched-together contraption had its own vocabulary, a stuttering arrhythmic complaint Beatrix had learned to read like weather — but something underneath that. A deeper note. The river was gathering itself somewhere ahead, the way a dog gathers before it barks.

Sorrow Bend lay six miles downstream. She'd run it twice in her life, both times alone, both times at low water with the whole day ahead of her and no one aboard but herself to drown. It wasn't a stretch of river you ran for pleasure. The guidebooks called it unnavigable; the pilots who worked the lower Vell called it a graveyard with a current; the old men who drank at the Grayhollow docks called it nothing at all, because the men who'd named it hadn't come back to do any naming.

And here she was, running toward it in the dark, with a broken engineer and a burned-out stoker and a dead man's map sewn into the lining of her coat.

Because behind her, three miles back and closing, was a Company patrol boat with a searchlight on its bow and a name painted on its hull in letters neat enough to be an insult: *Vigilant*. Corvane's boat. Corvane's water. The whole Vell from the locks down to the delta belonged to the Meridian Company now, or so their charts claimed, and they had begun enforcing the claim the way companies enforce everything — with paperwork first and gunboats after.

Beatrix had seen the searchlight sweep the bend above her anchorage an hour ago, a pale blade cutting through the fog, and she had done the arithmetic every fugitive does: how fast, how far, which way. The Vigilant drew nine feet loaded. Sorrow Bend drew four, in places two, in places less than that where the granite shoulders of the gorge pinched the river down to a throat full of teeth. No Company pilot would take a gunboat through there. No sane pilot would take anything through there.

Which left her, standing at the wheel with her heart going like a trip-hammer and her hands perfectly steady, doing the thing she had sworn off doing: gambling other people's lives on her own skill.

"Odile," she said quietly, not turning. The engineer had appeared at the foot of the stairway without being summoned, drawn by the same change in the river's voice, her gray hair unbound and her eyes already reading the gauge board over Beatrix's shoulder.

"I know," Odile said. "I felt it." She came up the last two steps and stood at the rail, looking downstream into the dark, and Beatrix watched her do the same arithmetic and arrive at the same ugly sum. "You're thinking about the Bend."

"I'm thinking we don't have a choice."

"There's always a choice. Sometimes they're just all bad." Odile rubbed her jaw, and Beatrix heard the rasp of stubble under her palm, and waited. That was the thing about Odile — she never wasted motion, not with her hands and not with her words. Ten years ago she'd been chief engineer on a Company packet, the *Meridian Rose*, proudest ship on the upper river, until a boiler inspection she'd flagged as unsafe got buried by men in better coats, and the boiler did exactly what she'd said it would do. Eleven dead. Her name on the report, theirs nowhere. She'd been drinking her way downriver ever since, repairing dredges and sawmill engines, until Beatrix found her in a Grayhollow machine shop arguing with a foreman and recognized, in the set of her shoulders, another woman who'd stopped expecting the world to be fair.

"The patch will hold," Odile said at last. "Probably."

"*Probably* is my favorite word this morning."

"It shouldn't be." Odile turned and went below without another word, and Beatrix heard her wake the engine with a wrench-tap on the pipe casing, the old signal, *stand ready*, and heard Finch scramble out of their bunk in the stokehold answering it.

Finch. Seventeen years old, maybe sixteen, wouldn't say. All elbows and soot and stubbornness, with a way of going silent when you asked about home that Beatrix recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting. She'd hired them off a coal dock three days ago because they could read a firebox and didn't ask questions, and because watching them haul scuttle with that fierce, furious competence had been like looking at a photograph she'd never taken of herself.

Now that fierce competence was about to matter more than either of them knew.

* * *

Fog on the Vell at dawn was not like fog anywhere else. It came up off the water in ropes and sheets, thick enough to chew, and it muffled the world down to essentials: the engine's heartbeat, the hiss of water along the hull, the creak of the wheel ropes in Beatrix's grip. She ran by sound and by the feel of the current against the rudder, reading the river through the wood like a blind woman reading a face.

The Vigilant's searchlight swept the fog behind them, a diffuse glow like a false sunrise, and Beatrix kept the Kestrel hard against the eastern bank where the current ran slack and the echo of her own engine would tangle with the cliff wall and lie to anyone listening. Behind her, Wren Hollis slept in the saloon, or pretended to — the prospector's daughter had boarded at Callow's Rest with her father's map and a face like a locked door, and she had said perhaps forty words since, most of them to tell Beatrix she didn't believe in captains, or promises, or rivers that gave anything back.

Ahead, the gorge opened its mouth.

You felt Sorrow Bend before you saw it. The air changed first — cooler, wetter, tasting of stone — and then the sound arrived, a long rolling thunder that lived below hearing as much as above it, and the deck began to shiver underfoot like a horse sensing a snake. Beatrix rang the engine bell twice: half ahead. Then, after a breath, once more: slow. You entered the Bend slow or you didn't enter it at all.

Odile's head appeared at the engine room hatch. "Patch pressure's holding at eighty. I don't like asking eighty of it."

"Don't ask. Tell it."

"That's not how boilers work."

"That's not how *you* work. Make it believe."

Something that was almost a smile crossed Odile's lined face and was gone. She dropped below, and Beatrix steered into the fog-wall at the gorge mouth, and the world ended and began again on the other side.

The Bend revealed itself in pieces, the way a story reveals itself to someone determined to misunderstand it. First the green water going suddenly glassy — the lying calm, the river holding its breath — then the drop, invisible in the fog but loud as a waterfall, because it was one, a twelve-foot fall where the whole Vell poured over a shelf of granite into a churning basin, and then the basin itself, and then the channel beyond it: a quarter mile of white water threaded between black rocks, none of it wider than the Kestrel was long, all of it moving faster than anything had a right to move.

There was a line through it. There was always a line. Beatrix had run it twice and remembered it the way you remember a scar — not the pain, but the shape. Enter the falls bow-on, slightly left of center where the tongue of smooth water slid over the shelf. Punch through the trough at the bottom before the recirculating current could catch the stern and swing her broadside. Then hard right around the Sentinel — the great leaning rock the old pilots had named for a man who'd drowned against it — and thread the gap between the Sawteeth, two ledges that tore open hulls the way a boot tears open ice, and then ride the chute below, steering nothing, trusting everything, until the river spat you out into the long pool past the Bend and forgave you for coming.

Twice she'd done it alone. Now there were four souls aboard and a boat that was held together partly by rivets and partly by Odile's blasphemies, and behind her a gunboat whose captain was even now deciding whether to follow.

He wouldn't. But he'd telegraph ahead. And there were other boats below the Bend, and other bends after this one, and the map in her coat lining weighed more every mile.

"Finch," Beatrix said into the voice tube to the stokehold. "Fire hot and steady. When I ring three bells, give me everything the boiler has and don't ask what it costs."

A pause, then Finch's voice, thin through the brass: "Everything it has. Aye, Captain." They still stumbled over the word *Captain*, saying it too carefully, like a coin they weren't sure was real.

Beatrix centered the wheel. The falls filled the fog ahead, a white roar with a hole in the middle of it, and she thought — not for the first time, but with new weight — of Hollis coughing blood into his handkerchief in her cabin, telling her the source was worth dying for, and her answering that nothing was, and meaning it, and being wrong.

She put the wheel over two spokes and drove the Kestrel at the falling edge of the world.

* * *

The drop took four seconds and lasted a year.

Bow-up, then the sickening float of the crest, then the plunge — the whole river landing on the hull like a collapsed building, green water bursting over the pilothouse windows, the deck plates screaming, and Beatrix riding the wheel with her whole body, feeling the rudder go light as the screws lifted half out of the foam, feeling the current seize the stern and begin the swing that would put them broadside to the Sawteeth and end everything.

"Now!" she shouted into the tube, and rang three bells, and the Kestrel answered.

What Odile's patch did in those next thirty seconds, Beatrix would never fully know, because she was fighting the wheel and the river at once and had no eyes for gauges. She knew the engine note climbed from a laboring growl to a full-throated roar that had no business existing, knew the screws bit and hauled, knew the stern swung true and the bow came around and the black shoulder of the Sentinel went by close enough to read the moss on it. She knew the boat was shaking in a way boats are not built to shake, a deep structural shudder like a living thing in terror, and she knew — in the way you know things in rapids, instantly and completely — that the patch was failing anyway, that somewhere below her feet metal was tearing, because the engine note developed a hitch, a skipped beat, a dying-man's rhythm.

Then the whistle blew. Not the Kestrel's whistle — the boiler's safety valve, shrieking, and beneath it a sound Beatrix had heard once before on a different boat in a different life and had hoped never to hear again: the high, thin hiss of steam escaping where steam should not be.

"Finch!" she screamed into the tube. "Get out of the stokehold!"

No answer. Just the hiss, and the roar, and the Sawteeth rising white ahead.

She threaded them. Later, she could not reconstruct how — a hard left, a harder right, the hull grinding something that shrieked along her keel for one endless second, Odile's voice from below swearing with the fluency of a woman who had learned profanity in eleven engine rooms — and then the chute took them and the steering died and the river did the driving, hurling the Kestrel down the last quarter mile in a chaos of spray and thunder, and finally, mercifully, threw her out into the long green pool below the Bend like a cat discarding a toy.

The silence afterward was enormous. The engine ticked and knocked. Steam sighed from somewhere aft. Fog closed around the hull, and Beatrix realized her hands were welded to the wheel and her arms were shaking and she was breathing in gasps, and that the Vigilant, somewhere upstream, was turning away from a graveyard it wanted no part of.

Then she remembered the stokehold, and the silence from the voice tube, and the shaking stopped mattering.

* * *

She found them in the aft passage outside the stokehold door, which was hanging open on a scene lit hellish orange by the firebox: Finch on their knees on the deck plates, cradling both hands against their chest, and Odile crouched over the open firebox door with a wet sack in one hand and a shut-off wrench in the other, having already done whatever needed doing, because Odile always did it first and explained later.

"What happened," Beatrix said. It wasn't a question. It barely had a shape.

"Steam joint let go," Odile said, not looking up. "The patch. Feed line fitting sheared when the hull flexed — I told you, eighty was too much. Live steam across the front of the firebox." She jerked her chin at Finch. "Kid had their arms in the firebox door clearing a clinker when it blew. Should've taken it in the face. Instead they—" For the first time since Beatrix had known her, Odile seemed to lose hold of a sentence. She looked at Finch, and finished differently. "They shut the damper and killed the blower first. Burned hands instead of a boiled face. That's the right order. Most grown engineers get that order wrong, once."

Finch knelt in the orange light, breathing in small careful sips, and Beatrix saw the hands for the first time. The gloves had fused to the skin in places. The backs of both hands were an angry, blistered red, the fingers already swelling, and Finch was holding them the way you hold something precious and broken, and not making a sound. Not one sound. Seventeen years old, maybe sixteen, and not one sound.

"We need cool water," Beatrix said. "Now. Saloon. Move."

"I can walk," Finch said, and tried to stand, and swayed, and Beatrix caught them under the arm and felt how light they were, how the bones of a teenager moved under her hand, and something in her chest that she had spent ten years bricking over cracked straight down the middle.

"You'll walk with help," she said, "or you'll be carried, and I promise you the carried version is worse," and Finch, who argued about everything, who fought her on watch rotations and coal quality and the proper way to fold a chart, went quiet and leaned into her side and let her steer them forward through the dim passage while Odile secured the firebox behind them.

In the saloon, Wren was already awake — of course she was; the whole boat had screamed — standing by the galley door with a kettle in her hand and her father's stubborn jaw set hard. She said nothing about captains or promises. She pumped cold river water into the basin, and when Beatrix eased Finch's ruined gloves off — slowly, Odile directing, scissors for the worst parts — Wren was the one who held the burned hands under the stream, gently, steadily, murmuring something too low to hear, some rhythm, maybe just counting, while Finch's breath came ragged and their eyes fixed on the middle distance with the particular courage of someone enduring being cared for.

"They'll scar," Odile said, wrapping the hands in clean linen soaked in the last of the carbolic. "Blisters across both palms and backs. Two weeks before they can hold a shovel. Six before they should." She tied off the bandage and sat back on her heels, and looked older than Beatrix had ever seen her. "Could've been worse. Because of the kid, it was only worse for the kid."

"Finch," Beatrix said.

The boy — girl — whoever Finch was today lifted their head. Sweat-plastered hair, soot-streaked face, eyes too old for the face they lived in. "Captain."

"You saved the boiler."

"Saved the boat," Finch corrected, with the ghost of their usual mutiny. "Boiler's scrap. Odile says the patch is done for good. We're running on what's left till it isn't." A pause. The ghost faded. "I heard the hiss and I knew. You learn the sounds. My—" They stopped. The word *father* or *brother* or *home* stood behind the sentence, unopened, and they stepped around it the way they always did. "You learn the sounds or you die learning them."

Beatrix crouched down to their level, which she almost never did, and looked at the bandaged hands resting in Wren's careful grip, and said the thing she had not planned to say and could not stop herself from saying.

"When we make Callow's Rest — when this is done — I'm getting you to a real doctor. Not a dockside one. A real one. That's not an order. That's a promise."

Finch studied her with those old eyes, weighing the word *promise* the way they weighed everything, testing whether it would bear load. Somewhere in the walls the damaged engine ticked and knocked, wounded but alive. Outside, the fog was lifting off the long pool, and the sun was coming up over the gorge rim, and the river below the Bend ran smooth and deep and forgiving, carrying them onward whether they deserved it or not.

"Okay," Finch said at last. Just that. But they said it the way sailors answer a bell — acknowledged, understood, logged — and Beatrix rose, and her knees cracked, and she went to the window.

Downstream, far off where the pool narrowed toward the next reach, she saw it: a smudge of smoke against the morning haze, too steady to be a campfire, too deliberate. A boat, anchored or patrolling, waiting where the river funneled and no pilot could avoid passing.

Corvane had telegraphed ahead after all.

Behind her, Odile followed her gaze and read the smoke and said, very quietly, "How bad?"

Beatrix watched the smudge and did the arithmetic again, the fugitive's arithmetic, and found the sums getting worse.

"Worse than the Bend," she said. "But the Bend didn't kill us. Get me an hour on that engine, Odile. Whatever's left of it — I need to know what it can do."

"And if it can't do enough?"

Beatrix touched the coat pocket where the map rode against her ribs, Hollis's wax seal crackling faintly under her palm like a small dry heartbeat.

"Then we find out what *we* can do," she said, and turned to wake Wren, because the daughter of the man who'd found the truth was about to learn what it cost to carry it.
