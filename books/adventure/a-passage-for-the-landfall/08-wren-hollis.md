## 8. Wren Hollis

Callow's Rest sat in the mountains the way a tired man sits in a chair — slumped, but not yet fallen. The town clung to a shelf of granite above the river, its streets crooked with frost-heave, its buildings wearing the grey of wood that had given up arguing with the weather. From the deck of the *Kestrel*, tied off at a rickety landing that groaned like an old man kneeling, Finch could see the assay office before anyone pointed it out. It was the building with the sign that had lost half its letters: **HOLLIS & SON ASSAY** — except someone had scratched out SON and never bothered to paint anything in its place.

"That's her," Beatrix said. She stood at the rail with her arms crossed, watching the town with the flat attention she gave everything, as if it might turn on her. "Wren Hollis."

Finch looked. A woman was sweeping the boardwalk in front of the office, though there was hardly anything left to sweep — just mountain dust that blew back the moment the broom passed. She swept anyway. That told Finch something about her before they'd exchanged a single word.

"She doesn't know we're coming," Odile said from the engine hatch, wiping grease off her hands with a rag that had been white once, in some other life. "You're sure about this, Captain? Walking up to a stranger and telling her her father's dead?"

"I'm sure about the promise," Beatrix said. "I'm not sure about anything else."

Finch hugged the map case a little tighter against their chest. They'd been carrying it since Grayhollow, ever since Beatrix had handed it over without explanation — *you're small, you're quick, nobody watches somebody your size* — and Finch had understood without being told what it meant to be trusted with a thing worth killing for. It was the first thing anybody had trusted them with in two years. They carried it the way other people carried a candle through wind.

"Maybe I should do the talking," Finch said.

Beatrix looked down at them. Her eyes were the colour of river water over slate, and they missed nothing. "Why?"

"Because I know how to tell somebody bad news without making them want to hit me." Finch shrugged, aiming for casual and probably missing. "I've had a lot of practice being the one people are angry at."

Something moved behind Beatrix's face — quick, gone again, like a fish under ice. "All right," she said finally. "But I'm standing next to you."

* * *

The climb from the landing to the assay office took ten minutes and cost Finch most of the breath they had. The air up here was thin and cold and smelled of pine resin and stone, nothing like the river's wet green breath. Finch hadn't realized until that moment how much they'd already gotten used to the river — how it had gotten into them, into the way they breathed.

The woman with the broom watched them come the whole way. She didn't stop sweeping. She didn't smile. She was maybe thirty, with dark hair pulled back so tight it made her look severe, and hands that were red and cracked from work and cold. When they reached the bottom of the office steps, she leaned the broom against the rail and said, "If you're selling something, we don't buy. If you're collecting for something, we don't pay. If you're lost, the road back down is behind you."

"We're not selling," Finch said. Their voice came out smaller than they wanted. They cleared their throat and tried again. "Are you Wren Hollis?"

The woman's whole body changed. It wasn't much — a stillness, a settling, like water going glassy before a storm — but Finch saw it, because Finch had spent years learning to read exactly those small movements in adults. The difference between a person who was about to listen and a person who was about to swing.

"Who's asking?"

"My name's Finch. This is Captain Ashgrove." Beatrix inclined her head a fraction, which from her was practically a bow. "We came upstream from Grayhollow. We — knew your father."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for Finch to count four separate things wrong with it: the way Wren's jaw tightened, the way her eyes went to the road as if measuring the distance to somewhere else, the way her hand found the broom handle again, and the way she laughed — one short, hard note with no humour anywhere in it.

"Knew him," she repeated. "Past tense."

"Yes," Beatrix said quietly.

"Well." Wren picked up the broom and started sweeping again, right past them, as if they were furniture. "Then you can save yourselves the trip to the undertaker and the trip to my door both. Whatever he left, leave it with the church. Whatever he owed, forgive it, because he's beyond collection now. And whatever he *said*—" the broom stopped mid-stroke, and her knuckles went white around the handle— "he said a lot of things, over the years. None of them were true."

"He wasn't lying about this," Finch said.

"You don't know what he lied about. You knew him a week. I knew him nineteen years, and then I knew the inside of his empty chair for eleven more." Wren turned, and her eyes were bright and furious and wet all at once, grief and anger tangled so tight Finch couldn't have said where one ended and the other began. "He walked out of this town when I was nineteen years old. He kissed my mother's headstone, shook my hand like I was a business associate, and told me he had one more strike to make and then he'd come home rich enough to fix the roof. Eleven years, Captain. Not one letter. Not one coin. Not one word. And now two strangers walk up my steps and tell me he's dead, and I'm supposed to what? Cry? Thank them?" She jabbed the broom toward the street. "Get off my boardwalk."

Finch should have gone. Any sensible person would have gone. But Finch had spent two years running from doors, and they knew what it was to stand outside one wanting desperately for somebody to make you come in.

"He coughed blood into a handkerchief," Finch said. "For three days straight. And every time you came up — in his talking, I mean — he'd get quiet and look out the window like the answer was floating by on the river. He talked about a girl who could read ore samples better than grown men twice her age. He talked about teaching somebody to pan when she was six, and she cried because the gold looked like dirt and she thought she'd failed." Finch swallowed. "He said she hadn't failed. He said she'd been the only one who ever got it right."

The broom handle creaked in Wren's grip.

"He remembered wrong," she said, but her voice had cracked down the middle, and they all heard it. "Or he's lying. Or you're lying. It doesn't matter. He's dead, and dead men don't get to come home."

"No," Beatrix said. "They don't. But they get to send things ahead." She nodded at Finch, and Finch untied the oilcloth wrapping with fingers that suddenly felt clumsy.

* * *

Inside, the assay office smelled of acid and paper and cold iron. Scales sat on the counters under dusty glass cloches. Ledgers lined the walls, most of them thin — too thin, Finch guessed, for a business that was surviving. Wren lit a lamp with hands that weren't quite steady and then pretended they were, arranging herself behind the counter like a fortress wall.

Beatrix set the map case on the counter between them.

"This is what he traded us passage for," she said. "Not money. Well — money too. But this is what mattered. He made me promise to bring what he found to his daughter, in Callow's Rest. I don't break promises. So here I am, whether you want me or not."

Wren stared at the case like it was a snake. "I don't want it."

"Then don't take it. Look at it. That's all I'm asking."

For a long moment nothing happened. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, a dog barked twice and gave up. Then Wren reached out — slowly, the way you reach toward a thing that might bite — and lifted the case's lid.

The map unrolled across the counter, wax seal broken, covered edge to edge in fine, cramped handwriting. Elevations. Soundings. Notes in margins. Little sketches of rock strata. Numbers running down the sides like rain.

Finch watched Wren's face and saw the exact moment it happened.

It started in her eyes. Something flickered there — recognition, raw and involuntary, the kind you can't fake and can't stop. Her hand rose without her permission and hovered over the page, not touching, trembling.

"That's—" She stopped. Started again. "That's his hand."

She said it like an accusation. Like the handwriting had personally betrayed her.

"He wrote like a doctor having a seizure," she went on, and her voice was climbing, cracking, anger rushing in to fill the space where grief had broken through. "My mother used to say you needed a shovel and a prayer to read his field notes. He *knew* that. He knew nobody could read them but—" 

She stopped.

"But me," she finished, very quietly.

The lamp hissed. Wren Hollis stood over her dead father's handwriting, and Finch watched eleven years of fury collide head-on with the simple, terrible fact that he had written to her after all — that every cramped line on that map was a letter he'd never sent, addressed in the only language he'd ever trusted himself to speak.

"He drew the little mine symbols the same way," Wren said. Her voice had gone strange, flat and thick at once. "Triangle with the tail. He used to draw them on my school slates instead of doing sums with me. Said arithmetic was for clerks." A sound came out of her that was almost a laugh and absolutely wasn't. "I hated him for leaving. Do you understand? I built the hating like a house. I lived in it. I *furnished* it." Her finger traced the air above the map, following the river's line upward, toward the source, toward the place marked in his hand with that little tailed triangle. "And now he goes and dies, and leaves me his handwriting, and I can read every word of it, and I hate him for that too."

Finch didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say. But they slid the second item out of the case — the folded pages beneath the map, the letters, the ones whose contents they hadn't fully understood yet — and set them beside it.

Wren looked at the top page. Her lips moved as she read. Then they stopped moving.

"What is it?" Beatrix asked.

Wren didn't answer. She turned the page. Turned another. Her breathing had gone shallow and fast, and when she finally looked up, all the anger had drained out of her face and left something underneath that was far worse to see — a kind of dawning horror, and beneath the horror, a terrible, unwilling purpose, like a pilot light catching in a cold furnace.

"These are affidavits," she said. "Sworn statements. Miners' names — I know some of these names, they worked the Meridian upper camps, they—" She flipped faster now. "Sample results. Water assays. Arsenic readings at levels that would kill a herd of cattle, taken *below* the Company's tailings runoff, dated over three years—" She looked up at Beatrix, and her eyes were blazing now, but it was a different fire than before. "Where did he get these?"

"He didn't say," Beatrix said. "He was dying. He said he found proof. He said the Company buried it, along with some of the men who collected it. He said his daughter would know what to do with it."

"My father," Wren said slowly, "was a prospector. A good one, a stubborn one, but a prospector. He chased gold his whole life and never held onto any of it." Her hand flattened on the papers. "And the one time he finds something worth more than a mountain of gold, he sends it to me."

"Can you read the rest of it?" Finch asked. "The map, I mean. The notes. Where it leads?"

Wren was already bent over the map, and something in her posture had changed — the bitterness burned away, or at least set aside, replaced by the fierce concentration of a woman doing the one thing in the world she was best at. Her finger moved along the river's course, up past the last marked lock, into the blank space where the charts ran out and only her father's handwriting remained.

"The source," she murmured. "He's marked a cache at the source itself. An old adit — here, this symbol, this is the Alderman workings, they flooded out twenty years ago, everybody thinks they're gone." She looked up, and for the first time she looked directly at Finch, and then at Beatrix, and Finch saw that the house of hate she'd built had just lost its roof, and she didn't yet know what she was going to live in instead. "There's a problem."

"There's always a problem," Beatrix said.

"The Meridian Company has men on this river. Armed ones. My father's notes say they killed to keep this quiet, and your faces tell me you already believe that." Wren squared the papers, tapped them into a neat stack with the crisp efficiency of someone who had handled ten thousand documents and trusted none of them. "So before I decide anything, you're going to tell me everything. From the beginning. Who you are, why he chose you, and what a riverboat captain, a child, and a disgraced engineer think they're doing carrying the Meridian Company's worst secret up a river full of guns."

Finch opened their mouth to answer — and stopped, because through the dusty front window of the assay office, they saw it.

A man in a grey coat, standing across the street. Just standing. Not shopping, not walking, not doing anything a man in a mountain town did. Watching the assay office windows with the patience of somebody being paid by the hour.

And further down the street, leaning against a hitching post, a second one. Same coat. Same stillness.

"Captain," Finch said softly. "Company grey. Two of them. Maybe more."

Beatrix moved to the window without seeming to hurry, and Finch watched her expression go from watchful to grim in the space of a heartbeat.

"They followed us up from the lock," she said. "Or they telegraphed ahead. Either way—" she turned back to Wren, and her voice was low and even and absolutely certain— "it seems your decision's been made for you, Miss Hollis. The only question left is whether you make it standing in this office, or on the *Kestrel*'s deck with the rest of us."

Wren Hollis looked at the map. At the papers. At the scratched-out word on her own sign, visible through the window, *HOLLIS & ~~SON~~*.

"Give me five minutes to close the ledger," she said.
