## 9. What Hollis Found

The assay office smelled of her father.

That was the first cruelty of the morning, before the light had even finished climbing through the frost-furred window — that a room she had kept for six years, swept and ordered and made entirely her own, should suddenly reek of him. Pipe smoke and river mud and that particular sourness of a man who had slept in his coat too many nights running. It clung to the oilskin packet on her desk as though it had soaked through the wax, through the canvas, into the very grain of the wood beneath it.

Wren Hollis sat with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and her hands flat on either side of the packet, and did not touch it.

Outside, Callow's Rest was waking in its usual way: the mill wheel groaning awake under its skin of ice, a dog complaining at nothing, the smith's hammer starting up like a slow heart. Ordinary sounds. Sounds that belonged to a town where nothing had ever happened and nothing ever would, which was precisely what she'd told herself she wanted when she'd come here with two trunks and a grudge and a license bought with the last of her mother's money.

The Kestrel's captain had left an hour ago, saying something about cargo and tide and not looking back when she went out the door. *I'll be at the dock until sundown,* she'd said. *After that, I'm gone, and you can do whatever you like with it.* As if it were a parcel of dried fish. As if it hadn't cost a man his life and four strangers their blood to carry it this far.

Wren touched the wax seal at last. It cracked under her thumbnail with a sound like a knuckle popping.

Inside: a ledger bound in oiled hide, swollen with damp; a map folded so many times its creases had gone soft as cloth; three glass vials nested in wool, stoppered with cork and sealed with pitch; and a bundle of letters tied with fishing line, each one addressed in a hand she knew better than her own.

She knew it better than her own because she had learned to write by copying it. Ten years old, tongue between her teeth, tracing the tall ascenders of his H's while he laughed and said she'd have a finer hand than any clerk in Grayhollow someday. He'd been right about the hand. He'd been wrong about everything else.

*Don't,* she told herself, the way she'd been telling herself since the boat came up through the fog with its patched engine and its burned boy and its captain who watched everything and gave away nothing. *Don't make him a ghost worth grieving. He chose the river over you. The river won.*

But grief has never once obeyed instruction, and neither, apparently, had she. She opened the ledger.

* * *

The first forty pages were assay notes. She read them the way she read anything technical — fast, hungry, with the particular pleasure of competence recognized. Her father had been a good assayer. That was the bitter joke of her whole childhood: he could read ore the way priests read scripture, could hold a crushed sample to the light and tell you what the mountain was hiding, but he couldn't read a daughter's face across a supper table to save his life.

The notes were meticulous. Dates going back eleven years. Locations marked by bearing and landmark rather than claim number — she understood why later, and the understanding sat in her stomach like swallowed stone. Sample after sample from the headwater streams above the Company mines: Vell-water taken at the confluence below the Corrick adit, at the tailings outfall, at the spring seeps along the north bank. And beside each entry, in columns ruled straight as fence wire, the numbers.

Wren had spent six years telling miners' wives why their well water tasted of iron. She knew what those numbers meant before her mind finished translating them.

Arsenic. Copper sulfate in concentrations that would blister a trout's gills at fifty yards downstream. Mercury salts — the old amalgamation process, still in use, still dumping its waste straight into the current because containment cost money and the Company did not spend money it could spend on men instead. The readings worsened year over year, season over season, each page a little deader than the last. In the earliest entries there were marginalia about mayflies, about the white caddis hatch coming off the water at dusk. By the third year the margins said only *no surface life observed.* By the fifth year he had stopped writing about the insects altogether, and the silence where they had been was louder than anything else in the book.

She turned pages until her fingers ached from the cold, and the office around her dimmed, the morning sliding past unremarked. The mill wheel groaned. The dog complained. Somewhere a woman called a child home, and the child's feet slapped the frozen mud, and none of them knew — how could they know? — that the water they drank had been dying for a decade, quietly, from the top down, and that every official report filed by the Meridian Company said otherwise.

Because then came the second section, and the second section was not science.

* * *

It began with a letter, tucked between the ledger's back cover and its final gathering of pages, addressed to no one. A draft, she realized. Her father's draft of a report he had evidently intended to file with the territorial survey office, dated four years past, never sent.

*I have completed analysis of forty-one samples taken between the Corrick and Dunmore workings,* it read, in that tall-handed script she hated. *The findings are unambiguous. Effluent from the amalgamation sheds is entering the Vell headwaters untreated. Contamination is detectable at Grayhollow, two hundred miles downstream. I have reason to believe the Company's own internal surveys reached identical conclusions no later than three years ago, and were suppressed.*

Below that, crossed out and rewritten twice, a single sentence that stopped her breath:

*I have spoken to the men who worked the sheds. They will sign affidavits if they can be assured of reaching print alive.*

*If they can be assured of reaching print alive.* Not *if payment can be arranged.* Not *if anonymity is guaranteed.* Alive. Her father, who had never once used a word he didn't mean, had written *alive*, and then he had crossed the sentence out — not because it was untrue, she saw immediately, but because he had realized no such assurance existed. No assurance could exist. Not while the Company owned the magistrates and the newspapers and, as she would learn within the hour, the telegraph lines.

The affidavits themselves followed. Eleven of them, each sworn before a justice of the peace whose name she looked up afterward in the county rolls and found had died — all of them, every last witnessing magistrate — within eighteen months of signing. Fever, the records said. Accidents. One drowning in a shallow creek, which the coroner's report described without comment, as though men drowned themselves in knee-deep water every day of the week.

Eleven voices. She read them all, standing up at some point without noticing she'd risen, reading aloud in a whisper because silence had become unbearable.

A stamper named Aldous Petrie describing the sheds where mercury was wrung from cloth by hand, no gloves issued, the floor drain running open to the creek. A woman — a woman, Ellery Voss, cook at the Dunmore camp — testifying that workers who complained were blacklisted across three counties, that one man who threatened to write to the survey office had been found at the base of the tramway with his lantern still burning beside him. A foreman, God help her, a Company foreman named Bracken, swearing that he had been ordered to falsify the monthly effluent logs for six years running, and producing the originals, which were sewn into the affidavit itself in a pocket of oilcloth.

*They know we're dying,* Petrie had written, in a laborious schoolroom hand. *They count us. There is a column for it.*

Wren set that one down very carefully, as though it might bruise.

There was a column for it. She thought of the ledger again, of her father's neat figures, and understood at last what she was holding. This was not treasure. Treasure could be spent, divided, hidden under a floor. This was a verdict — assembled sample by sample, signature by signature, by a dying man who had known exactly what it would cost to gather and had gathered it anyway. Every vial of poisoned water was a witness that could not be bought or buried. Every affidavit was a name the Company had failed to erase completely, though God knew they'd tried.

And the map — she unfolded it now, smoothing it against the desk with both palms — the map was where the rest of it lived. Because these eleven were only what her father had carried out. His notes spoke of more: a full duplicate archive, ledgers and samples and the original effluent logs, cached somewhere the Company's reach couldn't follow. He had written the location nowhere, of course. He had drawn it.

She bent over the map and let the assayer in her take over, because this part, at least, was work she knew how to do.

* * *

The map showed the upper Vell above the last surveyed lock — country no chart bothered with, rendered in her father's confident strokes. But it was wrong. That was her first impression, and her second, and it took her the better part of an hour and two pots of tea to understand that it was wrong *on purpose.* Elevations transposed. A tributary drawn flowing uphill. Distances that didn't reconcile with any bearing she could construct.

Then she remembered being nine years old, sitting on his knee in a camp much like this one, watching him draw a map for a client and deliberately shift a landmark half a mile east. *Any fool can copy a map, birdie,* he'd said. *Only the maker knows where the truth lives.* It had seemed like a game then. It was a lock now, and the key was the thing he had taught her without ever meaning to: how to read country.

She started with the tributary that flowed uphill. Impossible — unless it wasn't a tributary at all but a ridge line, drawn as water to mislead anyone who read the map without knowing the ground. Flip it, and the elevations reconciled. Transpose the two false peaks, and the distances closed. An hour past noon, the map opened like a hand, and there it was, in the fold of country above the source: a mine adit, abandoned, marked with no symbol at all except a small circle of dots that meant nothing on any legend she knew — except that she had seen her father dot circles like that around every cache site he'd ever trusted. Supply drops. Emergency stashes. The places a careful man put things he could not afford to lose.

At the source. The man had hidden the truth of the poisoned river at the river's pure beginning, where the water came out of the mountain clean and unknowing, before it ran down through all that ruin. If there was poetry in it, she was certain he'd never noticed. If there was defiance, he'd noticed that plenty.

Wren sat back. The light had gone long and amber across the desk. Her tea was cold, her eyes were hot, and the papers lay before her like a body on a slab — something dead that demanded to be identified by someone who loved it.

He had found it. He had proven it. He had carried it alone for years, coughing his lungs loose in one flophouse after another, hunted by men in grey, and he had never once sent for her. Never written. Never said *birdie, I need you, this is bigger than my pride.* He had hired a stranger instead. Paid a stranger with coin he must have scraped from the bottom of his last purse, and made her swear a promise, and died at dawn on a boat full of people who owed him nothing.

Anger rose in her, old and familiar, wearing its usual clothes — and then, for the first time in ten years, the clothes didn't fit. Because she could see it now, laid out in ink and arsenic readings. He hadn't stayed away out of indifference. He had stayed away the way you keep a lamp behind shuttered glass: because everything near it was in danger of catching. Eleven witnesses dead. Magistrates dead. A foreman dead with his lantern burning. What would the Company have done to a daughter in Grayhollow? To a wife? He had chosen the coldest, loneliest protection he knew, and he had done it badly and silently and without ever once explaining himself, because explanation was a door and doors let the danger through.

It didn't forgive him. She held onto that. Nothing forgave him; forgiveness was hers to give and she wasn't ready and might never be. But it *explained* him, and explanation, she discovered, hurt worse. Anger had been a wall she could lean her whole weight against. Understanding was air.

She looked down at the map, at the little circle of dots above the source, and felt the weight of it settle onto her shoulders — not gently, not like a mantle, but like a yoke, like something harnessed. Her father's purpose, inherited the way you inherit a debt. The Company had killed to keep this quiet. They would kill to keep it quiet still. Carrying it to print meant carrying it through them, upstream, into country no map knew, with a captain who lied beautifully and a crew of strays who had already bled a little for a dead man's papers.

She could burn it. That was always there, the honest option: feed the ledger to the stove, pour the vials down the drain, tell the captain at sundown that she wanted none of it, and go back to telling miners' wives about iron in their wells — one household at a time, one truth at a time, small and safe and useless.

*There is a column for it.*

Wren stood, wrapped the oilskin packet tight, and blew out the lamp though the day wasn't done with the window yet. Outside, the street ran downhill toward the river, and the river ran silver and oblivious toward Grayhollow and every mouth that drank from it.

She was halfway down the hill, boots striking frost-hard mud, before she admitted what she was doing: not deciding. Deciding had happened somewhere among the affidavits, quiet as a trap springing. All that remained was delivery.

At the dock, the Kestrel sat low in the brown water, steam breathing from her pipe, and the captain stood at the rail watching the far bank with the patience of a woman who had already counted the minutes to sundown. When she saw Wren coming she didn't smile. She just stepped back from the gangway and held out one hand, palm up, as if to say: *well?*

Wren climbed aboard with the packet hugged to her chest, and had opened her mouth to say *I'm coming with it* when the captain's gaze lifted past her shoulder, down the street, and went flat and hard as river ice.

Three men in Company grey had stopped at the head of the dock. And the tallest of them was smiling.
