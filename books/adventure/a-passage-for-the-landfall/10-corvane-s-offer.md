## 10. Corvane's Offer

The town of Marrow's Landing clung to the riverbank the way barnacles cling to a hull — stubbornly, gracelessly, and with no intention of going anywhere. Beatrix had been there twice before in ten years, and both times she'd kept her visits short, her transactions cash, her face angled away from the lamplight. It was that kind of town. The kind where a man could vanish into a card game and a woman could vanish into a crowd, and neither would be missed by morning.

She came ashore alone. That was the first mistake, though she wouldn't know it until later. Odile had begged off to re-pack the starboard bearing — it had been singing a thin, metallic note since Sorrow Bend, like a teakettle deciding whether to scream — and Finch was asleep in the coal bunkers, curled around a wrench the way other children curled around blankets. Wren Hollis was still aboard too, shut in the cabin with her father's notes spread across the table like the pieces of a disassembled clock, trying to make them tell time again.

So Beatrix walked up the plank and into Marrow's Landing with nothing but a list of supplies and the comfortable weight of a decade's practice at being no one.

The evening market smelled of tar and frying onions and the green rot of the river at low water. She bought lamp oil from a woman with a cleft lip who didn't look at her twice. She bought salt pork and a coil of tarred twine and, after a moment's weakness, a paper sack of candied ginger that she told herself was for Finch, because the boy burned his hands at Sorrow Bend and hadn't complained once, which was its own kind of alarming. She haggled over the price of a new gasket out of habit rather than need. She let the noise of the market wash over her — the barking of fishmongers, the argument of two drovers over a lame mule, a fiddle somewhere tuning itself toward a tune it never quite found.

It was almost pleasant. That should have warned her.

* * *

She noticed him on her third pass through the square, which meant he had let her notice him. That was the second thing she understood, and understanding it made the back of her neck prickle like the air before lightning.

He stood beneath the awning of a closed chandlery, out of the lantern light but not hiding in the dark of it either — occupying the shadow the way a man occupies a doorway, deliberately, as a matter of architecture. Company grey, but not the field grey of the men who'd boarded her at the lock outside Grayhollow. This grey was better cut. The coat fit across the shoulders the way coats only fit when they were made for the man wearing them, or when the man had been wearing them long enough that they'd learned. He was tall without being broad, silver at the temples, and his hands were folded loosely in front of him like a clergyman's between services.

Sergeant Corvane. Though *sergeant* sat on him the way *deckhand* would sit on an admiral.

Beatrix considered her options with the cold arithmetic she applied to rapids: run, fight, or read the water. Running through a market in a river town announced everything. Fighting was absurd. So she read the water — adjusted her course a few degrees, not toward him and not away, the neutral line — and let him make the next move.

He made it without hurry. He fell into step beside her as naturally as if they'd arranged to meet, and for three full stalls' worth of walking he said nothing at all, just matched her pace through the crowd while the fiddle finally found its tune somewhere behind them.

"Captain Ashgrove," he said at last. The name landed wrong. Not because it wasn't hers — it was, these days, the only one she had — but because of how he said it. Carefully. Like a man handling something wrapped.

"That's me." She didn't stop walking. "If you're here about your dredge channel, you can take it up with the harbor office. If you're here about the dead man's papers, I already told your men at the lock: dead men don't leave papers with me."

"No," Corvane agreed. "They generally don't. Dead men are famously unreliable correspondents." He smiled faintly, and the smile did not reach anything above his cheekbones. "I'm not here about papers, Captain. I'm here about a boat. Yours specifically. The *Kestrel*, stern-wheeler, forty-one feet, patched boiler, engine work that I'm told is frankly illegal in three jurisdictions. A remarkable vessel. Remarkable crew, too. A disgraced engineer. A child stoker. And now—" he paused, delicately, "—a prospector's daughter."

Beatrix kept her eyes forward. "You keep good books."

"The Meridian Company keeps excellent books, Captain. It's what we're for." He gestured ahead of them, toward a narrow lane between a cooperage and a shuttered assay office, where a lantern hung above a door marked with the Company's sigil — the mountain-and-river, the circle that swallowed both. "You'll forgive the theater. But some conversations are better held indoors, and this one is better held once."

"I can walk and listen."

"You can. But you'll want to sit down for the part about your father."

* * *

That stopped her. Not visibly — she'd spent ten years learning to stop things invisibly, to swallow flinches whole — but something in her chest went tight and cold, like a valve seating under pressure. Her father had been dead twenty-two years, drowned in the flood year, and the only people alive who knew that name — her real name, the one before Ashgrove — were buried deeper than he was.

Corvane watched her not-react with the appreciation of a craftsman watching another craftsman's work. "There it is," he said softly. "You have a very good face, Captain. Truly. But everyone has a seam."

"I don't know what you think you know—"

"Indoors," he said again, gently. "Please. I've walked a long way to say this properly, and my knees are fifty-four years old."

Against every instinct she owned, Beatrix went indoors.

* * *

The room beyond the door was a Company way-station: bare plank floor, a desk bolted down, a map of the Vell watershed pinned to the wall with brass pins, the upper reaches marked in red ink that looked dried-blood brown in the lamplight. There was a decanter of brandy on the desk and two glasses, and the fact of the two glasses — set out before she arrived, waiting — told her more about the evening's planning than any speech could have.

Corvane poured without asking. She didn't touch hers. He drank a finger of his own, set it down, and folded his hands again, and for a moment he simply looked at her with an expression she couldn't immediately classify. It took her a heartbeat to place it, and when she did, it frightened her more than menace would have.

It was respect. Genuine, unhurried, professional respect.

"Do you know what I do, Captain Ashgrove?" he asked.

"Company man."

"I solve problems. Quietly, where possible. Expensively, where necessary." He turned the glass a quarter-turn on the desk. "In eleven years with Meridian I have never once raised my voice. I've never needed to. Violence is what happens when negotiation fails, and I am very, very good at negotiation. Do you follow?"

"I follow."

"Good. Then understand that everything I say tonight is an offer, and none of it is a threat, and the difference will matter to you before we're done." He reached into his coat and produced a slim ledger, bound in grey leather, and laid it on the desk between them without opening it. "Before we discuss the map, I want to discuss you. Because you've been a fascinating problem, Captain. You see, most people are legible. They want money, or safety, or revenge, and you find their lever and you pull. But you—" he tapped the ledger once, lightly, "—you have been illegible for ten years. No debts. No registered home port. No family on any manifest. A woman who pays cash, tips generously, rescues strangers from drowning, and refuses their gratitude. I've read the reports. The sandbar rescue last spring. The pilot at Tarn Ford you pulled out of the water and then left before he woke. You help people and then you erase yourself from the story. Why is that, I wonder."

"Maybe I just don't like thanks."

"Maybe." He opened the ledger.

And then he said her name.

Not *Ashgrove*. The other one. The old one, spoken in a quiet room in a river town four hundred miles from anywhere she'd ever been known by it, and hearing it was like hearing a floorboard creak in an empty house — small, almost gentle, and absolutely damning. The room tilted a degree and righted itself. She kept her hands flat on her knees. She breathed the way she breathed when the boat went sideways in heavy water: slow, deep, mechanical, letting the body work while the mind caught up.

"There she is," Corvane murmured, watching her. "Ten years, Captain. You built quite the vessel to sail away in. And you sailed it well. But the Company has archives. The Company has clerks with long memories and longer ledgers, and the Company remembers a young woman who testified — briefly, before she recanted — in the inquiry after the *Alderney* disaster. Before certain documents went missing. Before certain names were protected and one unprotected name was left holding the blame." He closed the ledger. "We know what happened. More to the point, we know what was done *to* you. And we know you've spent a decade making sure no one else ever learns it."

The lamplight buzzed. Somewhere outside, the fiddle had stopped, and the silence of the town pressed against the window like a hand.

"What do you want," Beatrix said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, in a distant, disconnected way, the way a drowning woman might be proud of her posture.

"The same thing I wanted at the lock. The map. The papers. Whatever Hollis carried out of those hills." He leaned back. "And here is my offer, and I ask you to weigh it honestly, because I mean every word. Sell it to us, and the ledger closes. Not burns — closes. Your old name stays buried. The *Alderney* stays a story with the ending we wrote for it. You keep your boat, your crew, your anonymity, your life. In addition, the Company will pay you eight thousand crowns — more than that boat will earn you in thirty years of honest freight. You could moor somewhere warm. You could buy a different name entirely, a better one, with paperwork attached. You could disappear upward instead of sideways, for once."

"And if I don't sell?"

Corvane sighed, and it was almost tender. "Then the ledger opens. Names get checked. Questions get asked in ports you frequent. And one day, Captain — not soon, I'm a patient man, but one day — someone in some tavern says your true name aloud, and the past comes down the river after you like weather. You cannot outrun weather. You of all people know that."

* * *

Beatrix looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked at the map of the watershed on the wall — at the red ink climbing the upper Vell, marking the Company's mines, its claims, its appetite — and she thought about Hollis coughing blood into his handkerchief in her wheelhouse, and about a promise sworn to a dying man against every instinct she had, and about Wren sitting in her cabin right now, four hundred yards away, piecing together her father's handwriting by lamplight.

Eight thousand crowns. A closed ledger. Anonymity forever — the thing she had traded half her life to build, the armor she'd worn so long she'd forgotten it was armor and started calling it skin.

All she had to do was stay invisible. All she had to do was remain no one.

"Let me ask you something," she said, and her voice sounded strange to her own ears, steadier than she felt. "Hollis. The prospector. Did your Company kill him?"

Corvane's expression didn't change, which was itself an answer of a kind. "The Company doesn't kill prospectors, Captain. Prospectors die. Of exposure, of bad water, of lung fever. The hills are full of ways to die." He paused. "Though I'll note that a man who finds what Hollis found does tend to have a shortened life expectancy. The finding is the dangerous part. Not the dying."

"And the miners whose affidavits he carried. The ones your mines poisoned upstream. Did they die of exposure too?"

Something flickered behind Corvane's eyes — not guilt; she doubted the word was in his vocabulary — but a kind of weary acknowledgment, the look of a man who wished the conversation could have stayed on the level of money. "Captain. Every enterprise leaves a wake. Rivers drown pilots. Mines bury miners. The question is never whether there's a wake. The question is whether you're standing in it when it arrives." He slid the ledger aside and produced instead a bank draft, already made out, the figure written in a clerk's careful hand: 8,000. He set it on the desk between them like a card played face-up. "This is valid until noon tomorrow. After that, my instructions change, and I will no longer be able to protect you from what follows. I genuinely hope you take the money. You strike me as a woman who has paid enough."

"I have," Beatrix said. She stood. "That's rather the point."

She picked up the draft. Corvane's face eased into something like relief — and then she tore it in half, and in half again, and let the pieces fall onto his desk like molted feathers.

"You foolish woman," he said quietly. Not angry. Almost sorrowful. "Do you know what you're spending? Ten years, Captain. Ten years of running, and you're about to spend them all on a dead man's paper."

"They're not his papers anymore." She was at the door before she answered, hand on the latch, and the words came out of her with a certainty that surprised her, that felt less like decision than like arrival — like a boat coming around a bend she'd been steering toward without admitting it. "Nothing's only yours once you promise it to somebody else. You'd know that, if you'd ever promised anyone anything."

She stepped out into the night. Behind her, she heard Corvane move — unhurried, methodical — and heard him speak to someone in the lane, low and brief, and the tone of it told her everything the offer's politeness had concealed: the negotiation was over. What followed would not be negotiation.

* * *

She walked back to the dock fast, not running, running being an admission, and the whole way her heart hammered out the same cold refrain: *he knows your name, he knows your name, he knows the name.* Ten years of current between her and the *Alderney*, and one grey-coated man had crossed it in an afternoon with a ledger and a soft voice. The armor had a seam. Of course it had a seam. Everything sewn by human hands had a seam, and she had been sewing it herself, alone, stitch by stitch, for a decade, and it had taken him minutes to find the loose thread and pull.

At the head of the dock she stopped and made herself breathe the river air — mud and diesel and the sweet rot of the shallows — until her hands stopped shaking. Then she boarded the *Kestrel*, hung the supply sacks on their hooks, and stood a moment in the companionway listening to the sounds of her strange little crew: Odile's wrench tapping somewhere below, rhythmic as a pulse; Finch snoring in the bunkers; the scratch of Wren's pencil through the thin cabin door, patient as rain.

Her family, though she'd never once used the word. Her witnesses, though they didn't know yet what they were witnessing.

Corvane had offered her a fortune to remain nobody, and she had torn it up, and tomorrow at noon the price of that would start arriving, installment by installment, the way all debts on this river arrived. She looked at the map case lashed beneath her wheelhouse bench — wax-sealed, oilcloth-wrapped, heavier than lead and lighter than a lie — and understood, finally and completely, that there was no downstream route out of this anymore. Only through.

"Odile," she called down the engine hatch, keeping her voice even. "How soon can you make steam?"

A pause. A tap of the wrench. "Depends who's asking, Captain, and why they're asking like that."

"Because at first light," Beatrix said, "we're going to need to be gone before anyone expects us to be."
