## 11. Night Departure

The warning came wrapped in oilcloth and bad news.

Odile Vasseur found it tucked under the Kestrel's engine-room hatch when she went down at dusk to check the packing glands — a folded scrap of paper, no signature, the handwriting hurried and slanting. *Corvane has requisitioned two gunboats out of Grayhollow. They'll be at the Callow's Rest reach by tomorrow's ebb. Move tonight or don't move at all.*

She read it twice by the light of a single storm lantern hooded with her coat, then held the corner of it to the flame and watched it curl into ash that she ground beneath her boot heel. Old habit. There had been a time — she could still find it in herself if she pressed on the right bruise — when Odile Vasseur had believed that paper was the most dangerous thing on a river. Not water. Not fire. Paper. A requisition order. An inspection report. A letter with an engineer's seal on it, saying what she had signed and what she had known and when she had known it, all of it true, all of it damning, none of it hers to argue with once the ink was dry.

She had spent six years learning that lesson the expensive way, and here it was again, delivered anonymously to her engine room like a bill come due.

Above her, through the deck plating, she heard the sounds of the boat settling into evening: Finch's boots crossing the foredeck on their endless rounds, the creak of Wren Hollis re-tying something in the cargo hold for the third time because her hands needed work the way other people's hands needed rest, Beatrix Ashgrove standing at the rail the way she always stood at railings — like a woman keeping an appointment with the horizon. And somewhere beyond the mooring pilings, out in the dark where the Company's reach ran thick as silt, two gunboats loading coal and gunpowder and men who did not ask questions.

Odile wiped her hands on a rag that had been black longer than it had been cloth, climbed the ladder, and went to find her captain.

* * *

The cabin smelled of lamp oil and river damp. Beatrix sat at the little table with the chart of the middle reach spread out under a weight of brass, and she didn't look up when Odile came in, which meant she already knew. News traveled down a boat faster than water traveled downhill; Odile had learned that too, though from her it had been less a lesson than a sentence handed down.

"You got one as well," Beatrix said.

"Under my hatch." Odile lowered herself onto the bench across the table, which groaned the way everything on the Kestrel groaned, a language of complaint she'd come to find honest. "Whoever's feeding us is spending their own neck doing it."

"Or they want us exactly where Corvane wants us, and this is how you herd a boat." Beatrix traced the channel line with one finger, not quite touching the paper. "I've considered it."

"And?"

"And I keep coming back to the fact that Corvane doesn't need to herd us. He needs only to wait. We're the ones in a hurry now." She looked up then, and Odile saw the thing she'd seen on the first day she'd signed aboard this rust-bucket with a reputation and no references: a woman doing arithmetic she hated. "Midnight. We run dark. No lamps, no fires above decks, banked boiler low enough to hold steam but not show smoke. We take the old cut below the toll reach instead of the main channel."

"The old cut hasn't been dredged since before the war."

"No. It has snags, and it has shallows, and it has a bend where the current will try to put us on the point bar." Beatrix rolled the chart closed. "It also doesn't have gunboats, because gunboats draw too much to follow us in. That's the whole trade. I'm asking if your engine can make it."

And there it was, laid flat between them like a card turned face up. *Your engine.* As if the patched, welded, cursed, resurrected machine in the bilge were hers alone — which, in every way that mattered, it was. Odile had rebuilt that engine twice since Sorrow Bend, once properly and once with parts that had no business agreeing to work together, and both times she had done it with the particular tenderness of a woman mending the only thing standing between her and the open water where the past could catch up.

She thought about saying no. It would have been the sane answer. Stay moored, stay small, let the gunboats pass in the morning looking for a boat that wasn't moving, slip away later at leisure. Every instinct she'd polished over six years of disgrace said *wait, hide, survive.*

But waiting meant Corvane's men walking the docks. Waiting meant questions asked of harbor clerks, and harbor clerks kept books, and books were paper, and paper was the most dangerous thing on a river. Waiting meant Wren Hollis sitting in this cabin with a dead man's map and a living man's vendetta closing around her like a lock gate.

"It can make it," Odile said. "If I nurse it. Which means I don't sleep, and Finch doesn't sleep, and you don't ask me what I've fed it or where the parts came from."

"I never ask you those things anyway."

"That," said Odile, rising, "is why I stay."

She was almost to the door when Beatrix spoke again, quieter. "You could go, you know. Any port between here and the delta would take you. Your name carries further than mine does — different reasons, same weight. One good reference from a Company yard and the disgrace comes off you like paint thinner."

Odile stood with her hand on the doorframe and let herself feel the full shape of the offer, the way you test a sore tooth. A clean name. A yard with proper lathes. Drawings that weren't lies, signatures that weren't confessions. Six years she had wanted it so badly she'd stopped letting herself say it aloud, the way you stop touching a burn.

"Thinner takes the paint off," she said finally. "Doesn't fix what's underneath. Good night, Captain."

* * *

The engine room at midnight was a cathedral built by someone with no reverence and excellent hands.

Odile moved through it by touch and memory, dousing the last of the running lights, checking each gauge by the match-strike glow of a shuttered lantern she allowed herself three seconds at a time. The boiler held its banked heat like a held breath. She'd fed it the good coal — the small hoard she'd been saving against some emergency she hadn't been able to imagine, and now the emergency had arrived wearing grey uniforms, and there was nothing left to save it for but this.

Finch came down the ladder feet-first, the way they always did, landing soft as a cat that had learned to be quiet in houses where quiet mattered. Fifteen years old, maybe sixteen, wrists like kindling and eyes that catalogued everything. They carried the thermos of chicory Odile had left brewing and poured it without being asked, and the two of them drank in silence while the boat breathed around them.

"You're scared," Finch said. Not a question. Finch didn't waste questions on things that were obvious.

"I'm always scared. Fear keeps an engineer honest. You check the valve twice because you're afraid of what happens if you don't." Odile turned the pressure gauge toward the faint light. "What I am tonight is tired. There's a difference. Fear makes you quick. Tired makes you careful. Tonight I need careful."

"Captain says the old cut has snags big as churches."

"The captain says a lot of things in a flat voice that are worse than they sound. It's a gift." Odile looked at the kid sidelong. "You could still get off. Slip over the rail at the moorings, walk into Callow's Rest, be nobody by morning. Nobody's hunting a stoker."

Finch was quiet long enough that Odile heard the river against the hull, patient as a creditor. When the kid finally spoke, the voice was steady in the way that cost something.

"When I ran, I promised myself I'd never be somewhere I couldn't leave." A shrug that tried to be careless and didn't manage it. "But leaving's just another way of being somewhere. You're still you when you arrive. I figured that out about four towns ago." Finch drained the chicory and set the cup down with exaggerated care. "Besides. If I jump ship every time it gets hard, I'm just my brother's fire with legs. I'd rather be something else."

Odile said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't have cracked her own voice along its oldest seam, and the engine needed her voice whole.

At half past midnight, Beatrix's knock came — two soft raps on the hull plating, the signal — and Odile opened the throttle a fraction, easing the Kestrel off the moorings on a breath of steam, silent as a held secret sliding out of a pocket.

* * *

Running dark was a discipline Odile knew from the wrong side of her history.

In her first year as a junior engineer, bright and certain and twenty-four, she had worked the Meridian yards at Corvane's own district — before Corvane, before anyone knew the name, back when he'd been just another clerk with clean cuffs and cold eyes. She had helped design the pump systems for the dredge fleet, elegant machines, efficient, and she had signed the drawings without reading the second appendix, the one that showed where the pumps drew from and where they discharged. Upstream intake at the mine tailings. Downstream discharge into the channels the fishing villages used. She had signed her name to plumbing that moved poison, and when the affidavits started surfacing — workers' statements, suppressed, buried — her signature had been on the drawings that made the burying possible.

They had taken her license, not her freedom. The Company preferred ruin to martyrdom; a ruined engineer walking around was a warning to everyone else. She had spent six years proving the warning true, drinking in river towns, taking work under false names on boats whose owners didn't look closely, until the night in Grayhollow when a lean, hard-eyed captain had watched her rebuild a seized cylinder head in nine hours with borrowed tools and offered her a job without asking a single question about her past.

*I don't care where you've been,* Beatrix Ashgrove had said. *I care whether the engine runs.*

And the engine ran. And then Sorrow Bend had happened, and the engine had nearly died, and Odile had lain in the bilge with scalded knuckles and a cracked rib holding the patch together with wire and will, and somewhere in that long dark hour she had understood the thing she'd been circling for six years: that she had never actually left the Company's employ. She had just changed the direction of her labor. Every weld she made now unmade a little of what she'd built then. It wasn't redemption — she didn't believe in redemption, it was a word men used in sentencing — but it was direction. Upstream, against the current, toward the source of the poison, carrying the proof of it in a wax-sealed case that a dying man had trusted to a stranger.

The Kestrel slid into the old cut and the darkness closed over them like water over a stone.

There were no lights anywhere. Beatrix stood forward reading the water by ear and by the pale ghost-glow of stars on the current, calling depth changes to the wheel in a murmur pitched to carry ten feet and no further. Wren crouched at the bow with a boathook, feeling for snags, her face a smudge of concentration. Finch fed the boiler in measured shovelfuls, keeping the fire just breathing. And Odile stood at her engine with her hand flat against the cylinder head, reading its temperature through her palm the way a midwife reads a pulse, coaxing, restraining, listening.

The cut was everything the chart had promised and worse. Snags rose out of the black water like the ribs of drowned churches. Once, a deadfall swept past close enough that Odile heard leaves brush the paddle housing, a sound like whispering, and nobody breathed for a hundred yards after. The current took them at the bend exactly as Beatrix had predicted, shoving the stern toward the point bar, and Odile gave the engine everything the boiler could give without screaming, and the Kestrel clawed across the current by the width of a coffin, and then the channel deepened and the river forgave them, the way rivers do — temporarily, and without meaning it.

It was near the end of the cut, in the hour before dawn when even fear gets tired, that Odile understood why she stayed, really stayed, and it had nothing to do with redemption or direction or any of the words she used to make her choices sound principled.

She stayed because of the sound the boat made when all four of them were working. The muffled calls forward. Finch's shovel finding its rhythm. Wren's hook tapping snag and clearing. Beatrix's voice, low and sure, steering them through a channel no map remembered. Underneath it all, her own engine turning over steady and strong, a machine that should have died three rapids ago running on stubbornness and skill and the refusal of four people to quit. She had spent her career in rooms full of engineers better credentialed than she was, and not one of those rooms had ever sounded like this. This was not competence. This was something with no name in any of her textbooks. It sounded, if she was honest, the way she had always imagined family ought to sound, back when she'd been young enough to imagine such things: everyone in their place, everyone needed, the work shared and the danger shared and no one left standing alone on a dock watching a boat they built carry someone else downstream.

The Company had taught her that a signature was the most dangerous thing on a river. They'd been wrong. The most dangerous thing on a river was deciding, in the dark, with gunboats behind you and snags ahead, that these people were yours — because after that, every choice you ever made had to account for them, and accounting for people was the one engineering problem with no clean solution.

Dawn found them through the cut and into the lower reach, the old channel opening into grey water and grey mist, the Kestrel still dark, still silent, still unseen. Odile killed the last of the banked fire's excess pressure and allowed herself, for the first time in eleven hours, to sit down on her own tool chest.

That was when Finch came down the ladder too fast, eyes wide, and pointed up through the engine-room skylight.

Far astern, back where the cut met the main channel, two shapes were moving through the morning fog. Long, low, black-hulled, funnels breathing thin grey smoke. Gunboats, running slow, sweeping the water with searchlights that the dawn hadn't yet made useless — quartering the reach, methodical as fingers feeling through drawers.

Looking for them. Behind them, yes — but the fog lay thin ahead, and the next lock was Company-manned, and the telegraph lines ran alongside the river like stitches holding the whole valley shut.

Odile put her hand back on the cylinder head. It was warm, and steady, and hers.

"Hold on to something," she called up the ladder. "We're going to need speed."
