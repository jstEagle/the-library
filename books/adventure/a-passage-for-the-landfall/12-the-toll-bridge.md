## 12. The Toll Bridge

The river told them about the bridge long before they saw it.

Beatrix felt it first in the water — a flattening, a hesitation, the current slowing as though the Vell itself had been asked to show its papers. Then came the sound: a low industrial groan carried upstream on the morning wind, iron against iron, the great chains being cranked taut across the channel. By the time they rounded the bend at Marrow Point and the bridge stood revealed in the grey light, she had already been doing arithmetic for an hour.

Two stone towers rose from midstream, squat and windowless as tombstones, joined by a timber span high overhead. Between them hung the boom — a lattice of blackened chain and tarred logs that could be raised for honest traffic and dropped, when it dropped, like a guillotine. On the near tower flew no flag at all, which was worse than any flag. Unmarked meant Company. Everything unmarked meant Company now.

"Chain's up," Odile said from the engine hatch, wiping her hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime around Sorrow Bend. "They're letting boats through."

"They're letting boats they've looked through," Beatrix said. She stood at the wheel with her weight forward, reading the water the way other people read letters. The channel funneled hard between the towers; there was a boil of confused current off the western abutment where the river resented being squeezed, and a mooring line of waiting barges along the eastern bank, their masters sitting idle on deck with the particular patience of men who had learned that impatience cost money here. "How much cargo are we still carrying?"

Finch appeared at the rail, soot-smudged, counting under their breath. They'd taken to keeping the manifest in their head since the Narrows, since the hole in the hull and the feverish days in the quarry cove, as if numbers were something solid to hold onto. "Forty barrels of pitch resin. Twelve of salt pork. The dry goods from Grayhollow nobody claimed. And the crates of millstone grit nobody wants at all."

"And all of it bought with money we don't have to spare," Wren said. She sat on the cargo hatch with the map case strapped across her body like a bandolier, a habit she'd developed after the ambush and refused to be teased out of. Her arm was still bound where the splinter had torn it; she moved it stiffly, guarding it. "You're not thinking what I think you're thinking."

"I'm always thinking six things," Beatrix said. "Which one frightens you?"

"The one where you give away our cargo."

Beatrix didn't answer. She watched the bridge instead. A skiff was putting out from the tower toward them, one oarsman and two figures standing in the stern in coats the color of wet slate. Company grey. One of them held a ledger board; the other held nothing at all, which somehow was more ominous. Behind them, on the span above, she could make out the small dark shape of a telegraph shed straddling the bridge like a tick on a dog's back, its wire running away eastward along the gorge, a thread of spider silk strung between this place and every other place the Meridian Company wanted to know about.

Every mile has a price, her father used to say, back when she'd had a father and a name and a boat with a different name entirely. Some you pay in coin. Some you pay in blood. The trick is knowing which toll is which before the collector asks.

"Odile," she said quietly. "If I tell you to put everything into reversing, can you do it without tearing the guts out of her?"

Odile considered. "I can do it without tearing *all* the guts out of her."

"That'll have to do."

* * *

The skiff came alongside with a bump and a scrape, and the man with the empty hands climbed aboard first, moving with the unhurried confidence of someone who had never once been told no by anything smaller than a government. He was broad, red-faced, with a clerk's ink stains on his fingers and a soldier's set to his shoulders, and he wore his Company badge the way some men wore wedding rings — as proof of ownership, though it was never quite clear who owned whom.

"Toll station at Vellgate Crossing," he announced, to no one in particular and everyone in general. "All vessels subject to inspection. Papers, cargo manifest, and passenger list." His eyes did the work his voice didn't bother with: the patched hull planking below the waterline, the jury-rigged exhaust stack, the girl with the bandaged arm, the boy with the burned hands, the woman with the leather case strapped to her chest. Beatrix watched him catalog them and knew exactly which entry he would write down first.

"Captain Ashgrove," she said, and gave him the name like a coin tossed to a beggar — worthless, but it kept him busy. "Cargo run out of Grayhollow. Resin, salt pork, millstone grit. Bound for the upper mills."

"Upper mills closed in the spring." He said it mildly, almost kindly, the way a man sets a trap and then holds the door open. "Company order. You'd know that if your papers were current."

"My papers were current when the mills were open."

"Then they're not current now." He smiled. It was a good smile, well maintained, like the rest of him. "Sergeant Corvane sends his regards to traffic on this stretch of the river. He asks that we be thorough. There's a dead man's papers somewhere on this water, Captain, and until they're found, thoroughness is the order of the day." He let that sit. Downriver, behind them, the current went on speaking its endless indifferent language against the hull. "So. We'll start with the hold."

There it was. Beatrix had known it was coming since the boil off the western abutment, and knowing hadn't made it easier. An inspection meant hands in the hold, and hands in the hold meant the false-bottomed locker beneath the pilothouse stair, and the locker meant the map case, or rather meant the thing she'd built to *look* like a map case — oilcloth, wax seals, Hollis's actual handwriting copied out in Wren's careful forgery over three sleepless nights in the quarry cove — while the real case rode flat against Wren's ribs under her coat, warm as a second heartbeat.

The decoy was good. Wren had inherited her father's hand down to the impatient crossbar on his H's. But good wasn't certain, and certain was the only currency the Company accepted.

"Inspection will take time," Beatrix said. "Time is money, and my crew hasn't eaten a proper meal since Grayhollow. Let's talk about the toll instead."

"The toll is fixed."

"The toll is fixed for boats with nothing to hide," Beatrix said, "and ruinous for boats with something worth finding. I know how this works. I've paid bridges from here to the delta." She stepped closer, close enough that the clerk's smile flickered, recalculating. "Here's my offer. The cargo. All of it. Resin, pork, grit, the lot — forty-odd barrels and every crate, delivered to your stores on the eastern bank, signed over clean, no questions asked. In exchange, you wave us through with a receipt that says *inspected and cleared*, and neither of us wastes the morning."

The clerk's eyebrows climbed. Behind him, the oarsman shifted, interested despite himself. On the Kestrel's deck, Beatrix heard Finch's breath catch and Odile go very still by the engine hatch, and Wren — Wren said nothing at all, which was the loudest sound aboard.

"You're offering me your entire cargo," the clerk said slowly, "to avoid an inspection."

"I'm offering you an easy morning. Inspections generate paperwork. Paperwork generates signatures. Signatures generate questions from men above you about why a routine toll stop took four hours and produced a report." She shrugged, letting the motion carry all the weariness of ten years of rivers. "Or you can search us, find nothing, and explain to Sergeant Corvane why the dead man's papers weren't on the one boat you delayed till noon. Your choice. I've made mine."

It was a bluff stacked on a gamble stacked on a prayer, and she knew it. The cargo was worth perhaps sixty crowns — real money to a crew that had been eating salt pork and river trout for a month. But the alternative was the locker, and the locker was a coin flip with a hangman. Every mile of this river had a price, and the bridge was simply the first collector honest enough to stand in the open and ask.

The clerk studied her. She let him. She had learned long ago that silence was a load-bearing wall; most men couldn't bear its weight longer than ten seconds, and this one managed eight.

"Resin's worth something," he said at last, half to himself. "Pitch prices are up since the fire at the Corrick works."

"Everything's worth something to somebody," Beatrix agreed. "That's the whole trick of trade."

He turned to the oarsman. "Get Master Quill from the tower. Tell him we have a voluntary surrender of goods, section nine." Then, back to Beatrix, with the smile reassembled: "You understand I'll need to look in the hold anyway, Captain. Form's a form. But it'll be quick, and it'll be just me, and whatever I find or don't find stays between professionals."

"Professionals," Beatrix repeated, and found she could almost smile too. "Aye. That word will do."

* * *

Master Quill turned out to be the lockmaster — a narrow, grey man in a narrow, grey office at the top of the eastern tower, surrounded by ledgers and the constant dry rattle of the telegraph key in the adjoining room, worked by a boy who never seemed to blink. Quill received the deed of surrender, read it twice, and pressed his seal into the wax with the tenderness of a man kissing a relic.

"Sixty-one crowns' declared value," he murmured. "Generous of you, Captain."

"Charity keeps the wheels turning."

"Mm." Quill's eyes lifted, pale and patient, and for a moment Beatrix felt the peculiar sensation of being read rather than reading — of standing in a current she hadn't charted. "You came through Sorrow Bend last week. Running the rapids instead of taking the portage road. That was talked about. Boats don't run Sorrow Bend for economy, Captain. They run it to avoid something." He set down the seal. "I won't ask what. Asking is above my station. But the wire runs both ways on this bridge, and I only control what goes out of it, not what comes in." He slid the clearance chit across the desk. "Cleared and inspected. Go with God, or whoever's listening."

In the hold below, while the clerk made his brief and theatrical circuit — kicking a barrel, lifting a tarpaulin, glancing at the decoy locker without opening it because the paperwork upstairs was already signed and men like him never reopened what they'd already profited from — Beatrix stood on her own deck and watched the telegraph shed and hated it with a cold, specific hatred.

She couldn't hear the key from here. She didn't need to. She could see the boy's shoulders working, the little machine's rhythm, and she knew — the way she knew a snag under brown water, by the wrongness of the surface — that the message going east had nothing to do with sixty-one crowns of resin. *Voluntary surrender, vessel Kestrel, master Ashgrove, bound upstream, crew of four.* Somewhere down that wire, in some office smelling of lamp oil and ambition, a sergeant named Corvane was going to read those words over his morning coffee, and he was going to learn two things: that she was still on the river, and that she had paid a bridge rather than open a locker — which was, to a man like Corvane, the same as signing a confession.

"There," Finch whispered beside her, barely a breath. "That's it, isn't it? That's him knowing."

"That's him knowing," Beatrix said.

"Is that bad?"

She thought about the Narrows, about the gunboat's wake, about Wren's blood on the deck planks that even scrubbing hadn't fully lifted. "Bad is a word for things that might happen, Finch. This already happened. Now it's just weather coming. And we've sailed through weather before."

Below, the clerk emerged blinking into the daylight, brushed his knees, and touched his hat brim. "Clean ship, Captain. Pleasure doing business." The boom-chains began to rise with their groaning song, link by link, and the gap between the towers opened onto the grey-green water beyond, and the river ran on northward toward Callow's Rest country and everything waiting there.

Beatrix took the wheel. The Kestrel gathered herself and slid through the gap, under the span, past the telegraph shed where the boy's fingers never stopped talking, and out into the free water on the far side — free, and followed.

Behind them the chains came down again with a boom she felt in her boots. Ahead, the sky over the northern gorge had gone the color of bruised pewter, and the wind had turned, and Odile stuck her head out of the hatch and frowned at it with the expression of a woman reading bad news in a language she'd hoped to forget.

"Barometer's falling," she called. "We want anchorage before dark. There's a cut-out below Raven Rock, if it's still there."

"Take us there," Beatrix said, and steered into the rising wind, and tried not to count what the morning had cost, because the total kept changing, and the river, as always, was only getting started.
