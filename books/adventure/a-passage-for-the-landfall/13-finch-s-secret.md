## 13. Finch's Secret

The storm came down the valley like something with a grudge.

Finch felt it before they saw it — a pressure in the ears, a greenish wrongness to the light on the water, the river going flat and oily between gusts. Odile called it from the engine room, her voice pitched over the clatter: "Barometer's falling like a drunk down a stairwell, Captain. Whatever's coming, it's coming fast."

Beatrix didn't argue with the sky. She never did. She took one long look upstream, then another at the cutbank ahead — a shelf of grey stone with a notch in it, half-hidden by willows — and swung the wheel hard over.

"Storm anchorage," she said. "That cove behind the point. Finch, get forward and watch for the snag line. I've run past this hole a dozen times but never in weather."

The Kestrel nosed into the notch just as the first rain hit, fat cold drops that turned within a minute to a wall of water. The cove was small and deep, ringed by pines that bent and hissed, and Beatrix brought the boat in so gently that Finch only felt the bottom take the keel as a soft shudder, like a dog settling down.

Then there was nothing to do. That was the trouble with storms, Finch had learned. All the terror of them happened in the waiting.

They double-lined the moorings. Odile lashed the boiler hatch and muttered calculations about steam pressure that Finch understood maybe a third of. Wren sat in the cabin with Hollis's map case strapped across her body even indoors, even asleep, like a woman wearing a bandage she didn't trust yet. And Beatrix stood at the wheelhouse window with her arms folded, watching the rain hammer the river into white noise, and said almost nothing at all.

* * *

Supper was beans and salt pork and the last of the hard bread, eaten by lamplight while the storm threw itself against the trees. Nobody talked much. The wind found every gap in the old boat's seams and played them like a bad orchestra, and twice the whole hull groaned — a long wooden complaint that made Finch's teeth ache.

It was after supper that Finch couldn't stand the quiet anymore.

They found Beatrix in the wheelhouse, alone, a cup of coffee gone cold in her hand. Rain streamed down the glass. Beyond it the world was black water and blacker trees, and lightning walked along the ridge far off, silent, like something searching.

"Captain," Finch said. "Can I — I mean. Do you need anything?"

"I need this storm to be over," Beatrix said, but not unkindly. "Sit down, Finch. You're hovering like a moth."

Finch sat. The wheelhouse bench was narrow and smelled of tar and old varnish. For a while neither of them spoke, and the rain filled the silence so completely that speaking into it felt almost private — like the storm had built them a room no one else could enter.

"I had a brother," Finch said.

The words came out before they'd decided to let them. That was how it worked, Finch was learning. Things you swallowed long enough started swallowing you back.

Beatrix didn't turn. "You mentioned a home once. You didn't mention a brother."

"Tobin." The name tasted like smoke. "He was older. Fourteen when — " Finch stopped. Started again. "There was a fire. At our place, outside Marrowick. The drying shed."

Lightning flickered. Beatrix's face in its brief glare was still and listening.

"It was my fault." Finch's voice went flat, reciting, because flat was the only way to say it. "I used to sneak out there nights. There was a lamp I wasn't supposed to have — I'd saved up for it, a proper brass one, and Da said I'd burn the whole county down, he said it as a joke, mostly. I'd go read in the shed. Tobin caught me at it one night and instead of telling on me he — he stayed. He liked the stories I made up about the boats on the river. He'd sit on the flour sacks and listen till midnight."

The rain drummed. Somewhere upriver thunder rolled its barrel down the valley.

"The lamp leaked. Or I knocked it, I don't know, I've gone over that night ten thousand times and I honestly don't know. But the shed went up fast — it was full of dry flax — and Tobin didn't come out." Finch's hands had closed into fists without permission. "I got out. I always get out. That's the whole of what I'm good at."

"You were a child," Beatrix said.

"I ran." Finch's throat was tight. "That's the part nobody forgives, Captain, and I don't blame them. I didn't stay for the burying. I heard Ma saying my name and the word *fire* in the same sentence and I was gone before dawn. They'll have buried him thinking — " The sentence broke. "Everyone in Marrowick believes I killed him. Maybe they're right. Maybe running is the same as confessing."

Beatrix set down the cold coffee. When she finally turned, her eyes in the lamplight were the colour of the river in winter — grey, and moving under the grey.

"Do you know why I don't use my name?" she asked.

Finch blinked. In two months aboard the Kestrel, that door had never so much as rattled. "I figured you just liked being Captain."

A sound escaped her that might have been a laugh in a better life. "Nobody likes being nobody, Finch. You do it because the alternative costs more." She looked back at the streaming glass. "I had a different name once. A town, a family, a whole life with my name stitched on everything. And then there was a night — a fire, too, if you can believe the river's sense of humor — and people died, and I was the one who walked away from it. Whether I was guilty or not depended on who told the story, and the ones telling it loudest had money. So I put that name down like a heavy coat and I walked into the water, and I've been Beatrix Ashgrove ever since. Ten years."

Finch stared. "You never — Odile doesn't even know?"

"Odile knows enough not to ask. That's why she's here." Beatrix's jaw tightened. "I'm not telling you this to make us even. I'm telling you because you said maybe running is the same as confessing, and I've had ten years to test that theory, and I can tell you it isn't true. Running is just fear with your legs working. What you did or didn't do that night doesn't change because you left. And it wouldn't have changed if you'd stayed, either — except you'd have been sixteen and grieving in front of people who needed someone to blame more than they needed the truth."

"They needed *someone*."

"They needed their son," Beatrix said quietly. "And no one could give them that. So they took the next thing available." She shook her head slowly. "One day, Finch — not now, maybe not for years — you go back to Marrowick and you say your name out loud in that town, and you find out whether the truth weighs less than the running has. My bet is it does. My bet is it always does."

The storm slammed a gust against the wheelhouse hard enough to rattle the lamp chimney. Neither of them flinched. Out on the black water a willow thrashed like a drowned thing fighting the current.

"What was your name?" Finch asked. "Your real one?"

Beatrix was silent so long that Finch thought the question had broken something. Then she said, "Not yet. When I tell it, I want to be telling it somewhere it can't be taken back. You'll hear it someday. I think — " and here she paused, and something moved across her face that Finch had never seen there, something almost like hope, "— I think you'll all hear it sooner than I planned."

Finch nodded, and found their eyes burning, and didn't hide it. Above the Vell the thunder moved off downstream, grumbling, and the rain softened from a hammering to a steady wash, and in the little wheelhouse of the Kestrel two runaways sat in the lamplight, each carrying a fire, each discovering the other's matched their own.

"Captain," Finch said at last. "Why are you doing all this? The map, Wren, Corvane — you could've put Hollis ashore at Grayhollow and never thought of him again."

Beatrix looked out at the river a long moment.

"Because a dying man trusted me," she said, "and I'm tired of being someone who's only ever trusted by accident." She rose, and squeezed Finch's shoulder once — briefly, awkwardly, like a woman handling something fragile she hadn't handled in years. "Get some sleep, stoker. Storm'll break by morning, and we've got Company water ahead."

Finch went aft through the rocking dark, past Odile snoring in the engine room with a wrench still in her hand, past Wren curled around the map case like a hen over one egg, and climbed into their bunk in the bow. The boat breathed around them — timbers ticking, lines creaking, the river muttering against the hull — and for the first time since Marrowick, since Tobin, since the lamp and the flame and the road out of town, Finch fell asleep without checking where the exits were.

They woke near dawn to Odile pounding up the deck plates, shouting, and the sound — faint but unmistakable, carried flat across the calm morning water from far downstream — of a steam whistle blowing three short blasts.

Company signal. *Vessel to heave to.*

Ahead of them, around the bend, the river waited.
