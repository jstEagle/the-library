## 14. Ambush at the Narrows

The Narrows announced themselves an hour before they arrived.

Beatrix felt it first in the water — a quickening beneath the hull, the Vell gathering itself like a runner at the blocks — and then in the banks, which had been leaning closer all morning and now stood shoulder to shoulder, granite walls furred with moss, so that the river ran between them like a wire pulled taut. The sky shrank to a ribbon of grey overhead. Sound changed too: every word on deck came back doubled off the stone, so that when Finch called down from the engine hatch, *pressure's good*, it sounded as though two boys were saying it, one from either side of the world.

"Half a mile of this," Odile said, standing at Beatrix's elbow with her ruined hands tucked into her armpits the way she always did now, as if she were holding them prisoner. "Maybe less. Chart says forty feet wide at the throat."

"The chart also says this stretch was blasted clear in 'sixty-one." Beatrix turned the wheel a spoke, feeling for the current's temper through the wood. "Rivers don't honor charts."

"No," Odile said. "They don't."

Wren came up from below with the map case slung across her body on its strap, the way she'd taken to carrying it since Callow's Rest, since she'd spent three nights hunched over Hollis's notes by lamplight until the letters gave up their meaning. She hadn't let it out of arm's reach since. It hung against her chest like a breastplate, or a wound.

"I could take it below," Wren said. "Bolt it under the floorboards."

"And if we take water below?" Beatrix kept her eyes forward. The walls were closing. Ahead, the ribbon of sky bent hard to starboard around a shoulder of rock, and she couldn't see what waited beyond the bend, which was the whole problem with narrows: you learned their secrets only by giving yourself to them.

"Then I'll swim with it," Wren said, and there was no joke in her face at all.

Beatrix looked at her — at the set jaw, the ink stains still ghosting her fingers from all those nights of deciphering, the strap cutting into the wool of her coat — and understood that arguing would cost more than it bought. "Stay near the rail," she said. "If anything happens, you go over the side toward the north bank. The current eddies there. Odile showed you."

"She showed me," Wren said. "I didn't much listen."

"Listen next time."

It was then that Finch's voice came down out of the engine hatch, thin and strange against the doubled stone: "Smoke. Aft. Real smoke, not ours."

Beatrix turned. Behind them, folded into the grey of the gorge, hung a smudge of darker grey — coal smoke, moving fast, riding low. Even as she watched it thickened, took shape, resolved into a bow wave and a hull and the blunt superstructure of a boat built not to carry but to catch.

Company grey.

* * *

"Odile." Beatrix heard her own voice go flat and calm, the voice she used for weather. "How fast can she make in this channel?"

"In this channel? Eleven knots before we start painting the rocks with our bottom."

"And them?"

Odile squinted aft. The gunboat was maybe six hundred yards back now, close enough that Beatrix could pick out details she wished she couldn't: the swivel gun bolted to its foredeck, canvas-covered; the men along the rail, four, five, six of them, rifles slung; the wake it threw, white and arrogant, eating the distance between them like a debt collector counting interest.

"Fifteen, sixteen, easy. She's a Company packet — new boiler, no cargo, nothing aboard but malice and coal." Odile spat over the rail. "We cannot outrun her in a bottle this narrow."

"Then we don't run." Beatrix spun the wheel, bringing the Kestrel's nose around the rock shoulder, and the Narrows opened ahead of them — and closed. Forty feet wide, just as the chart promised, and worse than narrow: choked. The flood of early spring had done its work while the Company's blasting crews did theirs elsewhere, and the river had answered by dragging half a mountainside into the channel. Logs lay across the current in a black lattice, some sunk to within a hand's breadth of the surface, some riding high, turning slowly, grinding against each other with a sound like teeth. The water piled against them and tore through the gaps in white ropes, and beyond the jam, perhaps two hundred yards on, the channel widened again into a pool where the current slackened — where a gunboat could come alongside and board, and everything they carried, everyone they loved, would be forfeit.

"There," Beatrix said. "That's our door."

"That's a graveyard," Odile said.

"It's a graveyard for them too, if we do this right." Beatrix's mind was already running the river ahead of the boat, the way it did, the way it had always done since she was small enough to be afraid of water instead of people. The jam sat across the channel like a gate. But gates had hinges. There — where the main current drove hardest against the northern wall — a gap between two great pine trunks, wide enough for the Kestrel if Odile threaded the needle, and behind the jam, on the southern side, a pocket of slack water where the logs had bunched and matted into something almost solid. Something a boat could be driven against. Something that would hold.

"You want to ram it," Odile said slowly. "You want to ram the logjam."

"I want to put the Kestrel through the gap, swing her hard to port, and put our stern into that mat of logs behind us. Hard enough to crack a plank or two. Then we kill the engine, drift into the pool like wreckage, and let them find a channel blocked behind us and a derelict ahead of them." Beatrix glanced at Odile. "Can you thread it?"

Odile looked at the gap. Looked at her bandaged hands. Beatrix saw her do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic she'd been doing since Sorrow Bend, since the day she'd signed aboard a condemned woman's boat because there was nowhere else for a disgraced engineer to stand.

"If I miss," Odile said, "we splinter on the pines and the river keeps whatever's left."

"If you miss, none of us will know enough to be embarrassed." Beatrix put her hand briefly on the older woman's shoulder. "Thread it."

* * *

What followed lived afterward in Beatrix's memory not as minutes but as sensations, stripped of sequence, the way shipwrecks are remembered.

The gunboat's whistle, one long blast — *heave to* — bouncing off the canyon walls until it seemed to come from everywhere, from inside her own skull.

Finch coming up out of the hatch with his sleeves scorched to the elbow, seeing the logs, understanding instantly, and throwing himself at the throttle linkage without being told, feeding steam the way Odile screamed for it through the speaking tube.

Wren at the rail with the map case strapped tight, braced against the wheelhouse, watching the pines grow.

The gap.

God, the gap. It looked half the width of the Kestrel and it wasn't, it was a boat's width and a prayer, and the current funneled through it with such violence that the water stood up in the middle, ridged and trembling, and Odile took them into it at eleven knots with her eyes fixed and her lips moving — Beatrix never learned whether in prayer or profanity — and the Kestrel went through the way a needle goes through cloth, drawn by some sure invisible hand, the pine trunks close enough on either side that Beatrix heard bark scrape paint along sixty feet of hull, a long tearing whisper, like the river taking a receipt.

And then the swing. Hard to port, wheel over, engine reversed, the whole boat pivoting in the slack pocket behind the jam, and Odile opened the throttle one last time and drove the Kestrel's stern into the log mat with a crash that Beatrix felt in her spine, in her teeth, in the fillings of her soul.

Wood shrieked. Something below decks let go with a bang. The Kestrel bucked, settled, bucked again, and lay still, wedged against the matting like a fox gone to ground, hidden from the downstream approach by the very wall of timber they'd just made thicker.

"Engine off," Beatrix said. "All hands flat. Nobody moves."

They flattened. Beatrix stretched along the wheelhouse deck with her cheek against cold planking and watched through the rail stanchions as the gunboat came around the bend.

It came in fast, too fast for the water, its bow wave slapping the granite on both sides, and its pilot sawed at his wheel when he saw the jam — but he'd committed himself to the channel's throat, and the throat was full of trees. Beatrix watched the mathematics fail him. Watched him reverse, too late, the current already holding his bow and walking it sideways toward the pines. The gunboat struck the outer edge of the jam with a boom that echoed off the cliffs like a cannon shot, slewed, ground along the trunks with a horrible prolonged groaning, and swung broadside, pinned, its rail dipping toward the water as the current leaned its whole weight against the hull.

Men shouted. Rifles cracked — wild shots, snapping off the rocks above them, none finding flesh. One ball starred the wheelhouse window and Beatrix didn't flinch, because flinching cost time and time was the only cargo she had left.

"They're hung up," Odile breathed beside her. "Sweet mercy, they're hung up like a fly in amber."

"Not for long. They've got steam and leverage. Ten minutes, maybe fifteen, and they'll back off that jam and come around the north gap." Beatrix's eyes moved along the pinned boat, cataloguing. "Which means we have ten minutes to become something they aren't looking for."

"A wreck," Wren said.

"A wreck," Beatrix agreed. "Odile, how bad is the stern?"

Odile had already gone below. Her answer came up the tube a moment later, grim as a sermon: "Two planks sprung at the third frame. We're shipping water. Not fast — but steady. I can keep ahead of it with the pump if the pump holds, and the pump holds if Finch holds, and Finch is currently holding it together with his shirt."

"Then we drift," Beatrix said. "Dead slow, dead quiet, no smoke. We look like exactly what we claim to be — a holed boat drifting to her death — and we drift upriver into the fog bank past the pool, and by the time they're free we're a rumor."

She raised her head to check the wind, and that was when the rifleman found his range.

* * *

The shot came from the gunboat's foredeck, from a man kneeling on the canvas cover of the swivel gun, and it was not a wild shot. Beatrix knew that even before she heard the crack — knew it from the angle, from the patience of the muzzle tracking along their rail, picking targets. Picking the figure standing at the rail with a leather case strapped across her chest.

"Wren — *down*—"

But Wren had seen it too, seen the kneeling man, and instead of dropping she turned — turned her back to the shooter and wrapped both arms around the map case, curling over it the way a mother curls over a child, the way Hollis must once have curled over the papers themselves in some mine office where men died for knowing what he knew.

The ball took her high in the left shoulder, through the meat above the strap, and spun her against the rail.

Beatrix crossed the deck in three strides that she would never remember making. She caught Wren before the rail did, and the two of them went down together onto the planking, and the blood was already spreading through the grey wool of the coat, shockingly bright, shockingly fast.

"I'm fine," Wren said, which was the most transparent lie Beatrix had ever heard spoken through gritted teeth. "The case is fine. Check the case."

"The case can drown for all I—" Beatrix stopped. Breathed. Tore the coat open at the shoulder. The ball had gone through clean, front to back, high enough to miss the joint, low enough to miss the collarbone — a killing shot everywhere else on earth, and here merely ruinous. "Through and through. Odile! Needle, thread, and the good brandy, and tell Finch to keep us dead in the water!"

The gunboat was working free behind the jam — she could hear the strain of its engine reversing, the grind of hull on timber — but her hands were busy now, and her fear had to wait its turn. She packed the wounds, front and back, with Odile kneeling opposite, sewing with those ruined hands more gently than Beatrix would have believed possible, while Wren lay white-lipped on the deck and refused, absolutely refused, to surrender the map case, so that finally Beatrix had to work the strap over her good arm herself.

"You should have dropped," Beatrix said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "A case, Wren. Leather and paper. You're a person."

"It's not a case." Wren's eyes were fever-bright, fixed on Beatrix with a ferocity that had nothing to do with pain. "It's thirty men who drowned in the Millbrook adit. It's the affidavits they buried with them. My father carried it ten years and it killed him and nobody ever read it." She coughed, winced. "Nobody reads it, they died twice. So no, Captain. I don't drop it."

Beatrix had no answer for that. She tied off the last knot, gave Wren brandy until she sputtered, and got her below onto the bunk in the tiny cabin, propped and pale and still gripping the case like a drowning woman grips driftwood.

Behind them, the gunboat tore loose from the jam with a final rending groan.

* * *

They drifted.

Kestrel went upriver on the slack of the pool and into the fog that pooled in the gorge's upper reaches, cold and thick as milk, and Beatrix steered by memory and by the sound of water against stone, dead slow, funnel half-drawn, no smoke, no lights, no sound above a whisper. The hole in her stern talked to her the whole way — a soft rhythmic sloshing below the deck, water coming aboard in the dark, patient as a creditor. Finch worked the pump in shifts with Odile, and the pump clanked and gulped, and between its strokes Beatrix could hear the river getting in, and getting in, and getting in.

An hour passed. Two. Somewhere behind them, faint through the fog, a whistle sounded — searching, frustrated, doubling off the cliffs — and faded, and did not come again.

Only then did Beatrix allow herself to lean against the wheel and shake.

Not much. Just her hands. Just for a moment, where no one could see, in the wheelhouse dark, with the fog pressing at the glass like something wanting in.

Because she had counted, in the hour of drifting, and the count was this: one crew wounded, one hull breached, one pump failing by inches, one gunboat behind them that would report their heading within the day, and Corvane — Corvane, who did not send one boat when two would do, who did not warn when he could ambush — waiting somewhere upstream with whatever came next. And ahead of them, days of water no chart honored, and after the water, a mountain, and after the mountain, a city full of magistrates who might or might not care what thirty drowned miners had to say.

Survival, she thought, was starting to feel like a debt. Every mile they stole from the river, from the Company, from the arithmetic of guns and hulls, went onto a ledger somewhere, and ledgers came due. Hollis had paid with his life. The Kestrel was paying in planks. Wren had paid in blood for a leather case, and would have paid again, and called it fair.

And Beatrix — Beatrix had spent ten years believing that the trick was to owe nothing, to help and move on, to touch no shore long enough to leave a print. She looked down at her hands on the wheel, steady now, and understood that the ledger had her name in it too, written in a hand she almost recognized. Every kindness was a lien. Every person aboard was collateral. And the interest compounded daily, and there was no current in any river that could carry her out from under it.

The cabin door opened. Wren stood in it, grey-faced, one-armed, the map case still slung across her body, having climbed out of her bunk against all instruction and all sense.

"You should be lying down," Beatrix said.

"So should this boat, probably." Wren lowered herself onto the bench across the wheelhouse, breathing carefully. For a while neither of them spoke. The fog drifted past the glass. The pump clanked below, faithful and doomed.

"He'll have gunboats at the next lock," Wren said at last. "Corvane. He'll have guessed the fog, the drift, all of it. He plays three moves deep."

"I know."

"And the hull—"

"I know."

Wren looked at her then, and there was something in her face that hadn't been there at Callow's Rest, something that grief had worn like a coat for years and had finally begun to shed. "Then say it," she said quietly. "Whatever you're calculating behind those eyes, Captain. Say it out loud. Because Finch is down there pumping water with his skin burned off, and Odile hasn't slept in two days, and I've got a hole in me that matches the one in the boat. And if you've decided we're finished — if you're already looking for the bend in the river where you can put us ashore and disappear the way you disappeared from wherever it was you came from — then say it now, and let us decide what we do with the truth, the way my father should have let me decide ten years ago."

The fog pressed at the windows. The pump clanked. Somewhere below, Finch's voice rose in a cracked, defiant fragment of a song about a girl who robbed a mail coach, sung to keep his hands from shaking.

Beatrix opened her mouth to deflect — the old reflex, smooth as oiled wood — and found, to her horror, that it wouldn't come. That the deflection had drowned somewhere between the logjam and this bench, between the shot and the blood, and what remained was only a tired woman at a wheel with a leaking boat and a promise she couldn't outrun.

"We're not finished," she said. "And I'm not putting you ashore."

"But you've thought about it."

Every second of every day since Grayhollow. "Yes," Beatrix said. "I've thought about it."

Wren nodded slowly, as if Beatrix had passed some test whose questions she'd never seen. "Good," she said. "Honesty. That's a start." She eased back against the bulkhead, wincing, and closed her eyes. "Wake me when the fog lifts. And Captain — the pump won't hold to the source. Whatever Odile tells you otherwise, she's lying to be kind. So before the river decides for us, you'd best decide where we careen this boat — and pray to whatever you pray to that there's a cove ahead that wants us."

Beatrix turned back to the wheel, to the fog, to the blind water sliding past the glass, and began, silently, to pray for a cove.
