## 15. The Price of Going On

The quarry cove had been drowned a century ago and never forgiven for it.

Wren felt that the moment the Kestrel nosed through the breach in the old stone quay — a gap where the wall had crumbled into the water like a jaw missing teeth. Below the surface, forty feet down, the flooded terraces of the mine still showed in the clear green light, steps cut for men long dead, a rusted crane arm reaching up as if it had been trying to climb out ever since. The water was cold enough to ache. It smelled of wet granite and old iron and something faintly chemical, like a struck match that never quite went out.

They came in at dusk, the hull grinding once against submerged masonry, and Beatrix brought her alongside a shelf of rock where the old loading dock still stood above the waterline, its timber bleached silver, its iron rings furred with moss. No one spoke until the lines were made fast. No one had spoken much since the Narrows.

The boat was dying. That was the truth of it, and Wren could see it the way she could read an assay — the numbers didn't lie, they just waited for someone honest enough to look. The shell wound below the waterline was the worst of it: a gouge where the gunboat's wake had driven them onto a snag, planking sprung along two feet of seam, the frame behind it cracked like a wishbone. Odile had plugged it with sailcloth, tallow, and what Finch swore was an entire tin of their emergency pemmican, and the patch was holding the way a held breath holds.

Holding. Not fixed.

Wren stood on the loading dock with her arms crossed against the cold and watched the light die off the flooded terraces. Somewhere below the green water, the mine her father's generation had worked was still down there, still full of its tools and its timbers and, if the stories were true, at least one man they'd never bothered to bring up. Her father had grown up within ten miles of this cove. She hadn't known that until last night, when she'd found the notation in the margin of his notes — *quarry water tastes of the seam, don't drink it* — written in that cramped, slanted hand she was learning to read the way you learn a dead language: too late, and obsessively.

*Don't drink it.* He'd been here. He'd stood on this dock, maybe, and looked down at the same drowned terraces, and he'd written a note about water and said nothing to her, ever, about any of it.

She heard the others coming up the rock path — Odile's uneven tread, Finch's quick scuffle, Beatrix's boots striking stone with the metronome steadiness Wren had come to hate and rely on in equal measure. They carried lanterns and tools and the last of the bread. Behind them, the Kestrel sat low on her lines in the cove, her running lights dark, her smoke thin, looking less like a boat than like a wounded animal that had crawled into the reeds to decide something.

"Water's clear enough to work by morning," Odile said, setting her lantern on the dock. "If I can get her over on her beam against the shelf, I can get at the seam. Two days. Maybe three."

"With what?" Finch asked. "We've got half a keg of pitch and my good hand."

"Your good hand and my bad temper. That's been enough so far."

"It hasn't been *enough*, it's been barely—" Finch stopped. Wren watched them swallow the rest of it, the way they'd learned to, the way children of hard houses learn. Their burned hand was wrapped in a rag gone grey with boiler grease. They were sixteen years old and they'd saved the boiler at Sorrow Bend with that hand and hadn't once complained, and Wren found that more unbearable than the wound in the hull.

Beatrix said nothing. She stood at the edge of the dock, lantern in one hand, looking down at the water with the expression she wore when she was navigating by instinct — that far-off focus, as though the river ran somewhere behind her eyes. Wren had watched her do it for weeks now. She was beginning to understand that Beatrix Ashgrove went away like that whenever a question was asked that she didn't want to answer.

"Two days," Beatrix said at last. "Then we decide."

"Decide what?" Wren said.

Beatrix looked at her. The lantern light did something unkind to her face, hollowing the cheeks, deepening the scar through one brow that Wren had never asked about. "Whether we go on."

"We go on." Wren heard her own voice come out flat and hard, her assay-office voice, the one she used on prospectors trying to salt a claim. "That's not a question. We have the evidence. We have the map. We have—" she gestured at the boat, at all of them, at the whole improbable arrangement of their lives — "this. We go on."

"Up what?" Odile said quietly. She'd lowered herself onto the dock's edge, legs dangling over the black water, and she spoke to the river rather than to any of them. "Above the last lock there's no channel marked. No lock at all. No Company water, which is the only reason we're alive, but also no towpath, no telegraph, no help. The river up there is a rumor. I've seen the old charts. Half of them end with a note that says *unrunnable*."

"Charts are drawn by men who didn't try," Finch said.

"Charts are drawn by men who *did* try," Odile said, "and whose boats are still down there. I've seen them. At the falls above Marrow Lock there's a sternwheel sticking out of the water like a rib. Somebody tried."

"Then we don't run the falls," Wren said. "We portage. We find a way."

"A way," Odile repeated. She wasn't mocking. That was somehow worse. She turned her lantern up so the light caught her face — the ruined career written in it, the disgrace, the years of it. "Miss Hollis. I have spent my whole life finding ways, and I will tell you what I've learned: a way is just a price you haven't paid yet. Every mile of this river has cost us something. Sorrow Bend cost Finch's hand. The Narrows cost the hull, and nearly cost you your life, and did cost us—" she stopped, and Wren saw her choose not to say *the boat's soul* or something like it, and settle for — "a great deal. The next mile costs more. The one after that costs more still. There is a price at which going on stops being courage and starts being arithmetic, and I am asking us, as the person who has to keep the arithmetic, to know where that line is *before* we cross it."

The water lapped the drowned terraces. Far below, Wren imagined, the old crane arm went on reaching.

"Turn back, then," Wren said. "Go downstream, past the toll bridge where the clerk telegraphed ahead, past the locks where Corvane's people know our faces, in a boat with a hole in her, carrying evidence the Company will kill us for. That's your arithmetic?"

"Turn back to *what*?" Finch said, and then, quickly, as if they'd said too much: "I mean — there's nothing back there. For any of us."

"There's nothing up there either," Odile said. "That's my whole point, child. There's nothing anywhere but what we make, and I'd like us to survive the making."

"It isn't nothing," Wren said. "It's the truth. It's forty men's affidavits and a ledger of what the Company buried at the headwaters and water samples that prove every claim between here and the delta is poisoned. My father died carrying it. He traded his last breath for a promise from a stranger because he couldn't trust anyone else in the world to—"

She stopped. The words had gone somewhere she hadn't meant them to, and the silence that followed had a shape, and the shape was Beatrix.

Wren turned and looked at her. Really looked. The captain stood at the dock's edge with the lantern held a little too still, the way people hold things still when they're afraid the trembling will show, and Wren thought: *she's already halfway down the river.* She'd seen it before, now that she knew to look. At Callow's Rest, when Wren had first refused the map. In the Narrows, when the gunboat's whistle had sounded — one heartbeat, no more, of something that wasn't fear exactly but was fear's older sibling, something that had been living in this woman for ten years and knew all the exits.

"You're going to run," Wren said.

The words came out of her with all the grief wearing anger's clothes that she carried, all of it, the whole wardrobe, and she didn't try to take any of it back.

Beatrix didn't move. "Wren."

"You are. You've been planning it since the Narrows. Don't think I can't see it. You look at the map and you look at the water and you're already choosing your route *out*. When the hull's patched enough to move, you'll find a side channel, some backwater, and you'll put me and this—" she kicked the map case, the brass-cornered case that had taken a splinter meant for her heart — "ashore somewhere safe, somewhere *anonymous*, and the Kestrel will be gone by morning and you'll be a nameless captain on a nameless river again, and that's what you want. That's all you've ever wanted. A decade of running, and you're *good* at it, and you hate that we've made running expensive."

The lantern in Beatrix's hand trembled, just once, just enough.

"Say I'm wrong," Wren said.

The cove held its breath. Finch had gone very still by the tool chest. Odile had turned on the dock's edge to watch, and her face was carefully, professionally blank, the face of an engineer observing a stress test.

"Wren." Beatrix's voice was low and even. "You're not wrong."

Wren had prepared for denial. She had a whole speech loaded for denial, all the evidence, the sidelong glances, the way Beatrix checked the downstream current every morning like a woman checking an escape route. The speech collapsed in her chest and left something rawer behind.

"Say I'm wrong," she said again, quieter, and hated how it sounded. Like begging.

"I can't." Beatrix set the lantern down on the dock, carefully, the way she did everything, as if even her surrender had to be done with a steady hand. "I won't lie to you. Lying is what the Company does, and I've spent ten years being better at it than they are, and I'm done doing it to this crew. So. You want the truth? Here it is. Every night since the Narrows I have lain awake and worked the problem, and the problem is not the gunboats. The problem is that there is a version of me — ten years old and very well practiced — who knows exactly how to disappear from this story. I know the channels. I know the backwaters. I know how to be no one, and I am *good* at it, and every instinct I have is telling me that the smart move, the survivable move, the move that has kept me alive for a decade, is to put you all ashore and vanish up some water no map knows."

"And?" Wren's voice cracked on it.

"And I hate that I can't tell you I wouldn't." Beatrix looked out at the dark water, at the drowned terraces, at the crane arm reaching. "I can't promise it. I can't stand here and swear that when the price gets high enough, the part of me that runs won't win. That's the truth. I've been running so long I don't know if I *can* stop. I don't know if there's anything underneath the running. That's what you've caught me at, Wren, and I'm sorry, because you deserve better than a captain who has to discover her own answer in the middle of the river."

The silence afterward was enormous. Somewhere in the quarry walls, water dripped with the patience of geology.

Wren found she was shaking — not with rage now, something else, something that had been waiting under the rage since a gaunt man had coughed blood into a handkerchief on a dock in Grayhollow and asked a stranger for a promise. Her father had run too. That was the thing she'd never said aloud, the thing the map had made undeniable: he'd run from her, from Callow's Rest, from the whole shape of a life, into the hills and the headwaters, and he'd only turned around when he was dying, and by then all he had to give her was paper.

*Running men,* she thought. *The river is full of them.*

"I watched my father do it," she said. "Run. Ten years, same as you. And I swore I'd never understand it, and then I watched you check the downstream current every morning, and I understood it perfectly, and I hate that too." She stepped closer, close enough to see that Beatrix's eyes weren't hard at all; they were exhausted, the way water is exhausted at the bottom of a long fall. "So here's what I want. Not a promise. I don't trust promises; I've got a dead man's promise sitting in a brass case and it's the heaviest thing on this boat. I want you to stay *tonight*. Just tonight. Patch the hull with us. Get your hands in the pitch. And tomorrow, if you still want to run — if the running is all there is under there — then take the Kestrel and go, and I'll walk the evidence out over the mountains alone, and I'll think of you every time I drink clean water for the rest of my life. But stay tonight."

Beatrix looked at her for a long moment. Then she looked past her — at Finch, standing rigid by the tools with their bandaged hand curled like a dead leaf; at Odile, who had said nothing more but whose face had lost its professional blankness and become something older and more tired and more hopeful.

"Finch," Beatrix said. "Your hand. Can you hold a caulking iron?"

Finch blinked. "I can hold it left-handed. Ugly, but it'll hold."

"Then we work tonight." Beatrix picked the lantern back up, and her voice had shifted into the register Wren knew from the rapids, the one that meant a decision had been made even if no one knew what it was. "Odile, I want her rolled onto the beam against the shelf before full dark. Wren — there's pitch in the hold under the forward bench. And Wren."

Wren waited.

"I'm not promising tomorrow," Beatrix said. "I'm telling you where I'll be. I'll be in the water with my hands on the hull. Wherever I am tomorrow, tonight I'm here."

It wasn't enough. Wren knew it wasn't enough, and it was also, she realized with a strange loosening in her chest, more than Beatrix Ashgrove had given anyone in ten years.

* * *

They worked through the night, and the quarry cove rang with it.

Odile ran the winch and the balance like a conductor, easing the Kestrel over onto her beam against the stone shelf until the wounded seam broke the surface, black water sheeting off the planking, and Wren got her first true look at the damage and understood why Odile had spoken of arithmetic. The sprung planks gaped like a mouth. The frame behind was splintered to white wood. Finch, left-handed and swearing with impressive fluency, held irons; Odile drove the caulks; Wren kept the pitch pot over a driftwood fire and fed it resin and watched the smoke go up into the quarry walls and vanish the way all their smoke had ever vanished.

Beatrix worked in the shallows in her shirtsleeves, in water to her waist, holding planking against the frame while it was fastened, and she did not once look downstream. Wren watched her for that, specifically. She watched her the way she'd once watched assay crucibles — not for what was in them, but for what boiled off.

Near midnight the cold got into all of them, and Odile called a halt, and they sat around the pitch fire on the old loading dock eating the last of the bread soaked in river water, and Finch fell asleep mid-sentence with their head against the tool chest, and Odile draped her coat over them without comment.

It was then, in the fire's small circle, that Beatrix spoke without looking up from the bandage she was rewrapping around her own rope-galled palm.

"When I was twenty-two," she said, "I had a different name, and a boat bigger than the Kestrel, and a crew of eleven, and I was running cargo for men I knew were Company, because the money was good and I told myself I was only ever carrying freight, never questions." The fire cracked. "There was an accident. A barge. Eleven people, and a town downstream that drank what we spilled, and I walked away from the inquest before it started and I have been walking away from things ever since. That's what's under the running, Wren. That's the whole of it. Not mystery. Shame, and habit."

Wren stared at her. Odile had gone very still, but her eyes, when they met Wren's across the fire, said plainly: *she has never told me this either.*

"You didn't have to say that," Wren said.

"No," Beatrix agreed. "But you asked what's underneath, and it seemed like a night for answers." She tied off the bandage and looked up, and the firelight made her face open in a way Wren had never seen — unarmored, unpracticed, almost young. "I still can't promise you tomorrow. But I'll tell you what I felt today, working that hull. It's the first time in ten years I've wanted the boat to survive *for its own sake*. Not as a means of leaving. As a place to be." She shook her head, as if the admission embarrassed her. "That's new. I don't know what to do with it."

"Learn," Wren said. "You're apparently good at hard things."

Beatrix laughed — short, rusty, real — and the sound went up into the quarry walls and, unlike the smoke, seemed to stay.

They worked again until the sky went grey at the edges. By first light the seam was caulked and pitched and the Kestrel sat back on her lines, riding a hand's breadth higher, and Odile pronounced her sound enough for calm water and honest odds on anything else, which was, Wren was learning, the most optimistic sentence an engineer could honestly produce.

It was Finch, waking stiff and sore with their coat smelling of someone else's tobacco, who spotted it — standing on the quarry's rim in the early light, shading their eyes, going suddenly and completely silent in a way that pulled every gaze upward.

Above the cove, on the old haul road that climbed out of the quarry toward the high country, a single rider had stopped. Grey coat. Company cut. He didn't come down. He didn't need to. He simply raised one hand — not a threat, not quite a salute — and turned his horse, and was gone over the ridge toward the headwaters.

Toward the source. Ahead of them.

Odile said what they were all thinking, quietly, in the cold morning air: "They're not behind us anymore. They're *in front*."

Beatrix was already at the mooring line, uncoiling it, and when she spoke her voice had the rapids in it again — but something else too, something Wren had never heard there before, and would not hear her deny again.

"Then we'd better learn to run water no map knows."
