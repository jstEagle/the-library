## 16. Water No Map Knows

The last lock was a ruin before they reached it.

Beatrix saw it from a mile off — the old Company stonework slumped into the river like a drunkard against a wall, its gates rotted to lace, its lockkeeper's cottage swallowed by alder and ivy. No light burned in the tower. No bell rang for passage. Whatever authority had once held this stretch of the Vell had packed up decades ago and gone downstream, back toward the towns and the ledgers and the comfortable lies, and the river had been running wild above it ever since.

She brought the Kestrel alongside the crumbling lower sill and let Odile go up the moss-slick stairs with a lantern, though there was nothing left to inspect. The engineer came back down shaking her head, water dripping from her cuffs.

"Gates are kindling," she said. "Stonework's cracked clean through on the east wall. Nobody's locked this in fifty years."

"Then nobody's charted what's above it either," Beatrix said.

Finch leaned over the rail, squinting at the white thread of water visible through the trees upstream. "So the map just... stops mattering?"

"The map stops *existing*," Beatrix said. "Everything past that ruin is guesswork."

She said it flatly, the way she said most things, but something moved under her ribs when she looked upriver — something she hadn't felt in years and didn't have a name for yet. It wasn't fear. She knew fear the way she knew her own hands. This was older than fear. This was the feeling of standing at the edge of a room no one had entered since the world began.

They passed the ruin at midmorning, threading the gap where the east wall had fallen, and the moment the Kestrel cleared it, the river changed.

It happened all at once, the way weather changes. The broad brown patience of the lower Vell narrowed, quickened, turned from tea-dark to a green so clear Beatrix could count the stones on the bottom ten feet down. The banks drew in close and rose steep, granite shoulders wearing crowns of pine, and the water began to talk — not the low mutter of the freight channels but a bright, insistent speech, rapids talking over riffles talking over deep green pools where the current spun slow as thought.

"Saints," Finch breathed from the bow.

Beatrix throttled down and simply looked. Ten years on this river, and she had never seen it like this. The Company had taken everything tame and made it profitable; everything wild they'd left alone because there was no money in beauty. Above the last lock, the Vell belonged to itself.

"Water's fast but it reads honest," Odile called from the engine hatch, her voice almost lost under the rush. "No hidden holes I can see. What do you think, Captain?"

Beatrix stood at the wheel with her hand resting light on the brass, and she did what she almost never did: she stopped thinking.

On the lower river, piloting was arithmetic. Soundings, charts, dredge reports, the memory of every sandbar some clerk had bothered to record. But here there were no charts, no reports, no clerk's tidy letters. There was only the water saying what it was, if you knew how to listen — and Beatrix had learned to listen before she'd learned anything else, back when she'd been someone else entirely, back on water like this, high cold water in mountains she never named aloud.

She read the river the way you read a face. The smooth green tongue on the left meant depth. The standing wave ahead meant rock beneath, and the way its crest curled told her which side to pass. A line of foam trailing out from the bank marked a sunken snag; a sudden flattening of the surface marked a ledge. None of it was knowledge. All of it was instinct, and instinct lived somewhere below thought, below fear, in the oldest room of her.

"Starboard around the wave," she heard herself say. "Finch, watch the foam line off the left bank — give it thirty feet. Odile, half ahead, no more. Let the river carry us; we're guests here."

The Kestrel answered her like she was part of it. The old boat leaned into the turn, slipped past the standing wave with an arm's length to spare, and came out into a pool so still and so deep that the pines stood doubled in it, upside-down and perfect, and for a long moment Beatrix could not tell which forest was real.

* * *

They ran water all day.

Not the grinding, teeth-bared work of Sorrow Bend — this was different, a conversation rather than a fight. Rapid after rapid, each one unnamed and unrecorded, and Beatrix took them the way she'd once taken them in another life: by feel, by ear, by the lift and drop of the hull telling her things her eyes couldn't see fast enough. She stood at the wheel with her sleeves rolled and the spray coming cold off the bow, and somewhere past noon she realized her jaw had unclenched for the first time in weeks.

Finch perched on the forward rail like a gull, calling out hazards, and the kid had gotten good at it — good enough that Beatrix trusted the young voice more than her own tired eyes. Odile kept the patched engine turning over with a sound like a contented animal, muttering to it, coaxing it, occasionally threatening it. And Wren sat on the cargo hatch with Hollis's map spread across her knees, though there was nothing on it anymore to match the country they ran through.

"It's useless now," Wren said at one stop, holding the wax-stained paper up to the light. "Past the lock he only sketched. Here—" she pointed at a scrawl of lines and a single word, *the river forgets its name*, "—he just wrote that."

"He wasn't wrong," Beatrix said.

"You've run water like this before." Wren said it not quite as a question. "You read it too easy."

Beatrix adjusted the throttle and didn't answer, and Wren, who had learned when to push and when to wait, folded the map away and let it be.

But the question stayed with Beatrix through the afternoon, riding along with her like a passenger. Yes, she'd run water like this before. In another life, under another name, on rivers that fed valleys she'd helped drown — or tried not to help drown, depending on which version of the story she could stand to tell herself that night. That girl had known wild water the way she knew her own heartbeat. Beatrix Ashgrove had spent ten years pretending that girl was dead.

And here, above the last lock, with no charts and no Company and no witnesses but her own strange crew, the dead girl woke up in her hands.

* * *

They camped that night in a bend where the river widened into a lake the color of bottle glass, ringed with peaks whose snowfields caught the last light and held it like coals. Beatrix let the Kestrel drift stern-first to shore, and they tied off to a granite spur and built a small fire on the shingle, small because Corvane was somewhere behind them and possibly ahead of them, and caution had kept them alive this far.

Dinner was beans and river trout Finch had speared with shameless skill. They ate in a circle on the stones, and the talk was easy in a way it had never quite managed before — Odile arguing about valve tolerances, Finch reenacting the afternoon's best rapid with dramatic arm gestures, Wren actually laughing, once, briefly, like a door opening and shutting on a warm room.

Beatrix sat slightly apart, as she always did, and watched them, and felt the strangest ache.

She had spent a decade perfecting solitude. She had made it an art form: the solo runs, the cargo contracts that required no partners, the polite refusals, the bed aboard the Kestrel that needed room for only one. Solitude was armor, and armor was safety, and safety was the whole religion of a woman with a price on her old life. She had believed, truly believed, that being alone on the water was the only honest way to live on it.

But tonight, watching firelight move across three faces that would not be in her story if she'd had her way six weeks ago, she understood that she had been lying to herself with tremendous discipline.

Because the river was more beautiful tonight than it had ever been, and the reason was not the river.

Odile caught her looking. "You're smiling, Captain."

"I am not."

"You are. It's alarming. Should we fetch a doctor?"

"Should we throw you in the lake?" Beatrix said, and Finch hooted, and even Wren smiled into her tin cup, and Beatrix let the smile stay because fighting it suddenly seemed like more work than allowing it.

Later, when the others had rolled into their blankets, she took the first watch alone at the rail, as she always did. The moon came up over the eastern peaks and laid a silver road straight down the middle of the lake, and the river breathed against the hull, slow and enormous, and Beatrix stood in the dark and named the thing she'd been refusing to name all day.

She was happy.

Not safe — she'd given that up the hour she swore the promise to Hollis. Not certain of anything except the next mile of water. But happy, in the bone-deep way she remembered from before, from the years when the river had been wonder instead of escape. And the difference, the whole difference, was not the wild water or the uncharted miles.

It was the snoring. Odile snored like a failing boiler. Finch talked in their sleep about engines. Wren slept sitting up, map case strapped to her body like a child clutching a doll. And every one of those sounds, every inconvenient human noise aboard her boat, was worth more to Beatrix than ten years of clean silence.

For once, she wasn't alone on the water. And the water had never looked like this.

* * *

They broke camp at first light, into fog.

It lay on the lake like spilled milk, thick enough that Beatrix ran by sound alone — the echo of her whistle note off the canyon walls telling her where the banks were, the pitch of the current under the hull telling her where the channel ran. Behind her she heard Finch whispering to Odile, awed: "How does she *do* that?"

"Ten years of running," Odile murmured back, not unkindly. "You get good at knowing where the walls are."

Beatrix pretended not to hear. The fog burned off by midmorning, and what it revealed stopped the words in her throat.

The valley opened. The river braided wide across a gravel plain, splitting and rejoining around islands of pale stone, and beyond it the gorge walls stepped back to reveal the mountain itself — the source-mountain, vast and white-shouldered, filling half the sky. Waterfalls hung off its flanks like thrown ribbons, dozens of them, feeding the Vell from heights that made the eye climb and climb and find no top.

"That's it," Wren said quietly, beside her. She had the map open, though she didn't need it. "That's where he went. That's what he walked into."

Beatrix nodded. Ahead, the braided channels converged again into a single throat between black cliffs, and the water entering that throat moved with a new sound — deeper, heavier, a bass note under the treble. She had run enough wild water to know what that sound meant. Something big was coming. A gorge run, maybe the worst of the trip, and no way around it, because the map's last legible mark — a crude X above a sketch of twin cliffs — waited somewhere beyond.

She put both hands on the wheel and felt the old fear rise, and found, to her surprise, that it rose smaller than it used to. Fear had less room to live in her these days. Wonder had been taking up the space.

"Finch, forward. Watch for rocks under the foam. Odile, full pressure when I call it." She glanced back at Wren. "Strap yourself to something."

Wren did, without argument. Beatrix looked upriver one more time — at the white throat of the gorge, at the mountain behind it, at the road of water leading straight into both — and pushed the throttle forward.

The Kestrel gathered speed, and the river carried them in.
