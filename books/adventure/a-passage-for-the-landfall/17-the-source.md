## 17. The Source

**Beatrix**

The river ended the way all great lies end — quietly, and smaller than promised.

They had left the Kestrel hauled up in a willow shallows two miles below, hull patched with Odile's last sheet of copper and a prayer, and climbed the rest of the way on foot through country no map had ever bothered to insult. The gorge narrowed to a throat. The walls rose grey and dripping, furred with moss that drank the light and gave back nothing, and the water beneath them ran so clear it seemed less like water than like air that had learned to move.

Then the throat opened, and Beatrix stopped walking.

It was a bowl of stone, perhaps two hundred paces across, ringed by cliffs that leaned inward like old men conspiring. And in the center of it, rising out of a floor of pale gravel, the source itself: a spring the size of a barn, boiling up from the earth in slow, silent columns, so blue it hurt to look at directly — a blue that belonged to deep ice and cathedral glass, not to any river she had ever piloted. The water came up and up and did not splash. It simply arrived, the way dawn arrives, and slid away toward the gorge in a ribbon of glass.

No one spoke. Finch's mouth was open. Odile had taken off her hat, which Beatrix had never once seen her do, even in church, even at funerals.

"It's cold," Wren said at last, kneeling at the edge. She had dipped her fingers in. "It's *cold*. Like it never saw the sun."

"It hasn't," Beatrix said. Her voice came out rough. "That water's been in the dark since before there were rivers. It's just now learning daylight."

She had spent ten years on the Vell and its tributaries and had told herself she knew water the way a priest knows scripture. She had known nothing. This was the first page. She stood at the rim of the spring with the spray cold on her face and felt something in her chest unclench that she had not known was clenched — some fist she'd been making since Grayhollow, since before Grayhollow, maybe since the night she'd first run — and for one long moment she simply let the place be what it was, and let herself be small inside it.

Then Wren said, "The adit. It should be on the north wall. He marked it with a *cross-cut symbol* — see, here—" and the moment folded itself away into work, which was, Beatrix supposed, where all such moments went to live.

They found the adit where the map promised: a black rectangle in the cliffside, timbered with rotting beams, half-swallowed by a curtain of ivy gone bronze with autumn. Someone had collapsed the entrance once, deliberately, and someone — Hollis, years ago — had dug it open again and left it looking like nothing. A dead mine among dead mines. The mountains north of the Vell were stitched with them.

"Company drove it," Odile said, running her thumb along a beam and coming away with a crumble of brown rot. "Timber's theirs. Brand's still on it, see — the M in a circle." She spat. "They dug it, stripped it, sealed it, and wrote it off. And your father hid his treasure in their own corpse."

"He'd have liked that," Wren said. There was no bitterness in it this time. Something else. Something that might, in a kinder light, have been pride.

The tunnel ran back forty feet and ended in a fall of rock — and in front of the fall, behind a screen of loose slate that Hollis had stacked and mortared with mud a decade old, was the cache.

It took them an hour to open it carefully, by lantern light, with Odile swearing at the slate and Finch small enough to wriggle into the gap and pass the pieces back. And then the lantern was shining on a tin-lined crate, and Beatrix reached in, and the treasure of the Vell River's source came up into the light in her two hands.

It was heavy. It was dull. It was a jar of water and a jar of gravel.

"Samples," Wren whispered. Sealed jars, dozens of them, packed in wool — water from the source, water from the tributaries, gravel from the streambeds, each one labeled in Hollis's cramped, hard-slanted hand with a date and a place. And beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, ledgers. And beneath the ledgers, a packet of letters bound with wire, and a sheaf of affidavits, signed, thumb-printed, witnessed.

Wren opened the first ledger with hands that were not quite steady, and read by lantern light, and Beatrix watched the color leave her face by degrees.

"Arsenic," Wren said. "Tailings runoff from the Meridian workings above Silverline — he traced it seep by seep, month by month, for six years. Here — the spring at Alder Fork, parts per — he's got it measured against the assay standards, he's got the Company's own standards, he's got *their assayer's mark* on the comparison samples, they must have paid him for half of this without knowing what he was—" She stopped. Swallowed. "The fish kills. The wells going bad at Miller's Bend. The children." Her voice went flat and careful, the way voices go when feeling would otherwise be fatal. "The Company's been poisoning the headwaters for a decade and they knew. They knew, and they bought off the assayers, and the men who wouldn't sell—" She lifted the packet of letters. The wire was rusted through in one place. "These are from miners. Families. Some of these are dated the same month as the cave-in at Silverline. The one where they said the timber failed."

Odile had gone very still. "It didn't fail," she said.

"It didn't fail," Wren agreed. "They blew it. The men who were asking about the water. He's got a letter here from a widow — Mrs. Anselm, her husband was a shift boss — she saw the charge holes. They buried eleven men and called it bad timber, and her letter's been sitting in the dark up here for nine years waiting for somebody to read it."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere above, water dripped from the cliff into the spring, a sound like a slow clock.

Beatrix thought of Hollis in the fog, coughing into his handkerchief, testing her with questions about promises and daughters. *What's it worth, a man's word, Captain? What's it worth when nobody's watching?* He had known exactly what he was handing her. Not gold. A grave, opened. Eleven graves, and a river, and every farm downstream of Silverline.

"Pack it all," she said. "Everything. The jars go in the middle, padded — if one breaks we've lost the proof. Wren, the ledgers and letters stay on your body, not in a bag. *On your body.* Finch, you carry the affidavits. Odile—"

Odile was not listening. She was standing at the tunnel mouth, looking up the gorge, and her face had the particular grey stillness of a woman doing arithmetic she does not like.

"Captain," she said. "Smoke."

**Finch**

Finch smelled it before Odile said the word: woodsmoke and under it something sharper, coal-smoke, and under *that* something Finch had learned to know in the year since leaving home, the way you learn a dog's growl — gun oil.

They climbed the scree on the north side of the bowl, low, fast, Odile first and Beatrix last so no one could fall without her catching them, and came up behind a shelf of granite with a stunted pine leaning over it, and looked down the valley they had climbed that morning.

There were men in it. Eight of them, maybe ten, strung out along the trail with the unhurried spacing of men who are not chasing because they do not need to chase. Grey coats. Company grey. And at the head of the column, walking with a lantern even though the afternoon light was still good, a man Finch recognized from the lock at Grayhollow and had hoped never to see again: square-built, grey at the temples, moving like the ground was furniture he owned.

Corvane.

"They climbed ahead of us," Odile breathed. "While we were fighting the Narrows, while we were patching the hull — they portaged around and came up the east trail. They've been *waiting.*"

"How long have they been here?" Wren whispered.

"Long enough," Beatrix said. Her voice was very quiet and very even, and Finch had learned that this was the most dangerous register the captain owned. "Long enough to make camp. See — there, under the overhang, the fire ring. They've been here since last night at least. They let us come the last two miles because they wanted us to open it."

Finch's stomach went cold. "Open the adit."

"Open the adit." Beatrix did not move. Below, the grey column had stopped. Corvane had raised one hand, and the men had stopped with him, and now they all stood in the trail looking up at the bowl of stone and the spring at the center of it, and Finch thought — absurdly, horribly — that even Company men might feel something, looking at water like that. Then Corvane lowered his hand and the column began to climb, and the feeling passed.

"Options," Odile said.

"One way in, one way out of this bowl, and they're on it." Beatrix's eyes were moving, always moving — the cliffs, the scree, the spring, the adit, the pine above them. "The gorge walls are climbable in two places if we had ropes and a week. We have neither."

"Then we give them the map," Wren said. Her voice shook. "We give them the ledgers and we run, we go down the east trail ahead of—"

"They'd shoot us anyway," Odile said flatly. "You know what's in those ledgers, girl. You've read the widow's letter. Men who blow a tunnel with eleven of their own inside don't leave witnesses on a mountainside."

"Then we fight."

"Four of us. Ten of them. They have rifles and we have a boat-hook and my wrench."

Finch said nothing. Finch was looking at the spring.

It was a strange thing the mind did. Finch's mind had gone somewhere small and safe, back to the forge shed behind the house, back to the smell of coal and the sound of the bellows, back to the last ordinary evening before the fire — and in the forge shed there had been a lesson, the first one, the one that mattered: *water finds its level, and fire finds its food, and your job is to know which one you are.* Finch had been twelve. Finch had thought it was about blacksmithing.

"Captain," Finch said. "The spring."

Beatrix looked. "What about it?"

"It comes up from underneath. Under pressure. It's fed from a long way down — you can see it, the way it boils, there's a head on it. And the bowl floor is gravel." Finch's mouth was dry and the words came anyway, the way they always did when it was too late to be sensible. "Gravel over rock. And there's an adit cut into the north wall — Company timbering — and the mine workings would have run *under* the bowl, that's how they'd have driven them, chasing the vein down-dip. Tunnels under the gravel. Under the spring."

The silence lasted three heartbeats.

"You're saying the whole floor might be honeycombed," Odile said slowly. "Old workings, collapsed in places, open in others — under a gravel floor with a pressurized spring sitting on top of it."

"I'm saying my da worked a mine like it, outside Copperfield. The floor went down one night with four mules on it. The water did the rest."

Beatrix was quiet a long moment. Below, the grey column was halfway up the trail, and Corvane's lantern swung with his stride, patient as a pendulum.

"It's not a plan," Beatrix said at last. "It's a possibility wearing a plan's coat."

"It's what we have."

"It's what we have," Beatrix agreed. And then she did the thing Finch had come, over a thousand river miles, to trust more than rope or boiler or God: she smiled, small and crooked and entirely without comfort, and began to give orders.

**Wren**

Her father had never been here. That was the thought that would not leave her, crouched in the adit's dark with the ledgers strapped against her ribs, listening to the grey men come.

He had dug this cache and filled it and sealed it and climbed away, and he had never once stood where she now stood. She knew it the way you know a stranger's handwriting — the samples were dated, the letters were bundled, everything was finished, tidied, *left*. He had done his work and gone downstream to die, and he had chosen her — not because she was strong, she thought, or clever, but because she was the only person alive who would not sell it, who could not be leveraged, who had nothing left to lose except the anger itself. He had spent her anger like coin. And the terrible thing, the thing she would carry to her own grave, was that it had *worked*.

She heard them before she saw them: boots on scree, the jingle of a canteen strap, a voice — Corvane's, carrying in the bowl's stone acoustics as if the cliffs themselves were Company property, which, she supposed, in every way that mattered to him, they were.

"Garrison! Take the adit. Two men. The rest of you — the spring, the high ground. Nobody in the water."

Wren pressed back into the dark behind the cache crate. The tunnel mouth was a rectangle of afternoon light, and in it, framed like something hung on a wall, appeared a man in grey with a rifle at port arms, then another behind him. The first one stepped inside, and the lantern light reached in and touched the rotting timbers, and the shadows swung.

"Clear," he called back. "Cache crate's been opened. Recent."

"Of course it has," Corvane's voice said, closer now, unhurried. "Captain Ashgrove is many things, but she is not a woman who climbs a mountain for the exercise."

*Ashgrove.* Wren's breath caught. Not the name the crew used — she had heard the dock-masters use it, the lock-clerks, the name Beatrix wore like a borrowed coat. But the way Corvane said it had a weight to it, a *quotation* in it, as though the name were a line from a document he had read. She filed it away, the way her father had taught her, without meaning to, to file things: assay marks, dates, discrepancies. Later. Everything, later.

Boots crunched closer. Wren's hand found the boat-hook Beatrix had pressed into it, and her heart was a fist against the ledger boards strapped to her chest, and she thought of her father's handwriting on the sample labels — *Alder Fork, March; the water is wrong, the water has been wrong for years, God forgive us all* — and she found that she was no longer afraid.

That was new. That was her father's gift, arriving nine years late.

The lantern swung toward the rockfall at the tunnel's end. The soldier raised it to look behind the cache crate.

And from somewhere above — from the cliffs, from the sky, it seemed — Beatrix's voice came down into the adit, clear and carrying and cold as the source water itself:

"Sergeant Corvane. You're standing on eleven graves. I'd step back if I were you."

The lantern stopped swinging.

In the silence that followed, Wren heard it — faint, subterranean, felt in the soles of her feet more than in her ears — a sound like the mountain clearing its throat. Old timber, deep under the gravel floor of the bowl, shifting under ten men and a pressurized spring that had been waiting in the dark since before there were rivers.

Corvane's voice, when it came, had lost none of its patience. That was the frightening thing. It had lost none at all.

"Captain Ashgrove," he said, into the beautiful blue light of the source. "We really must stop meeting like this."
