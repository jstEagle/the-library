## 18. Standoff at the Adit

The source of the Vell did not look like the end of anything. It looked like a beginning — a seam of silver water rising out of black stone, so clear it seemed to have no depth at all, pooling in a basin no wider than the Kestrel's deck before spilling over into the river that would spend two hundred miles becoming wide and brown and patient. Beatrix stood at its edge with her boots in the moss and felt something she had not felt since she was a girl: the strange vertigo of standing where a thing starts.

Behind her, the mountain held its breath. The adit gaped in the hillside above the spring, a mine entrance older than the Company's claims, its timber frame rotted to the color of old tea. Hollis's cache lay inside — they had found it an hour ago, just as the map promised: a strongbox wrapped in oilcloth behind a false wall of loose rock, and inside the strongbox, the truth. Sample vials stoppered with wax, each labeled in Hollis's cramped hand. Ledgers. A bundle of letters tied with wire, dozens of them, from men who had signed their names knowing what signing meant.

Wren had read three of the letters aloud before her voice gave out. Now she sat on a fallen beam with the case open across her knees, touching the papers the way you'd touch a sleeping animal.

Finch crouched near the spring, cupping water in both hands. Odile had climbed back down the trail to check the boat — or to be alone; with Odile it was hard to tell the difference.

Beatrix heard the footsteps before she saw the men. That was the first wrongness: Corvane's people were good enough to move quietly, but the mountain was quieter still, and gravel has a voice if you listen for it. She turned, and there they were — six of them in Company grey, coming up the gorge trail with the unhurried confidence of men who had already won. Corvane walked at their head, his sergeant's coat buttoned to the throat despite the climb, his face carrying the mild expression of a man arriving slightly early to an appointment.

"Captain Ashgrove." He stopped ten paces off. His men spread along the treeline without being told, easy as water finding its level. "You've made us climb."

"You could have telegraphed," Beatrix said. Her heart was a fist knocking on a door, but her voice came out level, and she was grateful to it. Ten years of practice, buying her one more flat sentence.

"I did. The clerk at the toll bridge was thorough." He glanced past her at the adit, at Wren, at the case. "We've been tracking your progress since Grayhollow. I'll confess you exceeded my expectations. The Sorrow Bend run especially. I've seen better boats die in easier water."

"Flattery," Beatrix said. "From a man with six rifles."

"Five rifles and an offer." He took a step closer, and his men did not shift, because they didn't need to. The clearing was small and the trees were close and every line of retreat ran through grey cloth. "May I?" He gestured at a boulder as though he were calling on her in a parlor, and when she said nothing, he sat anyway, resting his forearms on his knees like a tired traveler. Up close, Beatrix could see the climb had cost him — sweat darkening his collar, breath a little short — and somehow that made him worse, not better. A man who would haul himself up a mountain on a dying lung for a box of paper.

"The Company knows who you are," Corvane said quietly. "I want you to understand that before anything else. Not a guess. Not a rumor. We know. We've known since the lock at Marrow's Ferry, when your face went through our clerk's memory like a coin through a slot. There is a file in a drawer in Meridian House with your name on it, and the name underneath it, and the date, and everything that happened on that night ten years ago that you've been rowing away from ever since."

The spring filled the silence. Beatrix kept her eyes on him and let nothing move in her face, but inside, something old and cold turned over, like ice shifting on a river in thaw.

"So here is my offer," Corvane went on. "Give me the case. The ledgers, the samples, the letters. In exchange: the file burns. Tonight, by my own hand, in front of you if you wish. No record, no warrant, no name. You walk out of these mountains as whoever you decided to be. Ashgrove. Or anyone else. A clean slate, Captain — the one thing money can't buy and you can't steal. You go back to your river, and we go back to ours, and neither of us ever speaks of this again."

He let it sit. He was good at letting things sit; she'd learned that about him at the lock, watching him wait out a lie of hers that should have held.

Beatrix looked at the case in Wren's lap. She looked at Finch, who had risen without seeming to decide to, and now stood between the soldiers and the old man's daughter with their burned hands hanging at their sides and their chin up in a way that was going to get them killed someday. She looked at Odile, who had come back up the trail at the sound of voices and now stood at the edge of the clearing with a wrench in one hand and an expression like a closed door.

*Stopping costs more than moving,* she thought. *It always has.* Every mile of the last ten years had been bought with the same currency: never stay, never be known, never give anyone a claim on you. And here was a man offering to refund the whole debt — except the payment was a box full of dead men's names, and the dead had paid more than she ever had.

She thought of Hollis coughing blood into his handkerchief in the fog, asking her oblique questions about promises. She thought of the dredge channel, the two survivors she'd pulled from the water and left on the bank because gratitude was a hook. She thought of Finch's confession in the storm anchorage, and the piece of her own story she'd given back, and how the sky hadn't fallen.

"Here's what I know about clean slates," Beatrix said. "They're only ever offered by people who want to write on them."

Corvane's expression didn't change. "That's not a refusal."

"It's a preface." She stepped forward, putting herself between him and the case, and heard Finch shift behind her. "Ten years ago I did something I'm not proud of, and I ran, and running worked. It worked so well I made a religion of it. Help, then move on. Never moor longer than the cargo takes. Never let anyone learn your name." She paused. "You're offering me that religion back. Anonymity forever. Do you know what I'd have given for that offer a year ago? Everything. I'd have given everything."

"And now?"

"Now I've carried these papers four hundred miles." Her voice was steady. Somewhere under the steadiness her pulse was hammering, but the steadiness held; it was hers; she had built it plank by plank the way she'd built the Kestrel's deck. "Now there are three people behind me who bled for this box, and a dead man who trusted a stranger with the only thing he had left, and forty miners whose signatures are in that case because they believed someone would eventually come for it. You can't buy that back, Corvane. It isn't mine to sell. It never was."

"Captain—"

"My name," Beatrix said, and the word came out of her like a splinter leaving the skin, "is not yours to use as a lever. Neither is the other one. Take your offer back down the mountain. Tell Meridian House the file can burn or it can hang me — either way, the evidence goes to a magistrate."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes — not anger; disappointment, almost, the look of a man watching a sound investment fail. He sighed and stood, brushing moss from his coat, and Beatrix understood, with the terrible clarity of the last second before a wave breaks over a deck, that the offer had never been the plan. It had been the formality. Companies need to be able to say they tried.

"That's unfortunate," he said.

"Corvane—" Wren was on her feet, the case clutched to her chest.

"Take them," Corvane said, without raising his voice.

The world went to pieces in the ordinary way the world does: fast, loud, graceless. The rifles came up and Beatrix was already moving, hauling Wren down behind the fallen beam as the first volley tore through the clearing, splintering timber, shredding moss, ringing off stone. Finch dove for the adit. One of the grey men screamed — Odile's wrench, thrown with an engineer's precision, had found his rifle arm — and then Odile herself was among the trees doing something insane and effective, shouting to draw fire.

"Inside!" Beatrix shoved Wren toward the adit. "Go, go—"

They stumbled through the mine mouth into cold and dark, the gunfire suddenly muffled to a dull hammering, dust sifting down from the rotten timbers. Finch was already there, fumbling with a storm lantern, and its light bloomed and showed the tunnel stretching back into the mountain, and showed, too, the light at the entrance dimming as grey shapes advanced across the clearing.

"They'll come in after us," Wren gasped. "There's no other way out—"

"There's always a way out," Finch said, and Beatrix heard it — the echo of her own rule, help then move on, coming back to her in a young voice, worn like armor. "There's always a way out. We just haven't dug it yet."

Outside, Corvane's voice cut through the gunfire, sharp and commanding, ordering his men forward into the dark after them.

And then, from down the mountainside, rising over everything — a sound Beatrix knew in her bones, a sound she had lived aboard for ten years and would know drowned, deafened, dead: the Kestrel's whistle, screaming long and wild, and beneath it, unmistakable, the groan of her engine being driven somewhere no engine should go.

Odile. Odile had reached the boat.

Beatrix grabbed the lantern and the case and turned to Finch and Wren, and for the first time in ten years she smiled with all of her teeth.

"She's giving us a door," she said. "Run."
