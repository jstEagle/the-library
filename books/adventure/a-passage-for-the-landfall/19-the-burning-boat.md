## 19. The Burning Boat

Odile had spent her whole life listening to engines, and she knew the sound of one about to die the way a mother knows a child crying in the night.

The Kestrel's boiler was singing it now — a thin, rising note beneath the gunfire, beneath Wren's shout, beneath the crack of Corvane's rifles echoing down the gorge like stones dropped into a well. The patch she'd built at Sorrow Bend, the patch she'd rebuilt at the quarry cove, the patch she had sworn on her own ruined name would hold — it was failing. Not yet. Not catastrophically. But failing, the way a bridge fails, the way a promise fails: gradually, and then all at once.

She knelt in the engine room with her ear against the cold iron of the hull and let the river tell her what was coming.

*Hold together, old girl,* she thought. *Just a little longer.*

Then the first bullet found the fuel line, and the world went orange.

* * *

It had started, as most disasters do, with a moment of beauty.

They had come down from the adit in a hard rain, the evidence case wrapped in oilcloth and strapped to Wren's back, Finch limping on a twisted ankle, Beatrix in the lead with her pistol drawn and her face set in that expression Odile had learned to read over a hundred miles of bad water: not fear, exactly. Calculation. The captain had already measured every step of the descent, every bend of the trail, every place a man with a rifle could stand.

The Kestrel waited at the gorge landing where they'd tied her that morning, gray and patient in the rain, her boiler banked and warm. Odile had touched her hull as they came aboard — an old habit, half superstition, half prayer — and felt the boat shudder once under her palm like a living thing glad of their return.

That was when the shooting started.

Not from the river. From above. Corvane's men — the ones who'd climbed the gorge trail on foot, the ones who'd waited at the source like wolves at a den — had not all died in the fight at the adit. Four of them had come back down ahead of the crew, and now they stood on the high shelf of rock above the landing, rifles braced against the rain-slick stone, and they fired down into the boat with the calm efficiency of men who had done this before and would do it again.

"Down!" Beatrix's voice, cracked like a whip. "Everyone down!"

Odile dropped behind the engine housing as the first rounds chewed through the deck above her head. Splinters rained down. Somewhere aft, Finch was screaming something — Wren's name, maybe, or just screaming — and the evidence case hit the deck with a sound like a dropped anvil, and then Wren's voice, ragged: "I'm hit — I'm hit, but I'm up, I'm up—"

The engine. Odile's whole mind narrowed to the engine. The fuel line ran along the starboard side, exposed, stupid, a compromise she'd made at the quarry cove when there hadn't been time to do it right. She could see it from where she crouched, could see the brass fitting she'd sworn she'd replace in Grayhollow, in Callow's Rest, in the next town, in the next town after that — always the next town.

A bullet struck the hull six inches from it. The iron rang like a bell.

"No," she said aloud, to no one. "No, no, no—"

The third bullet found the fitting.

Fuel sprayed in a fine, glittering arc across the hot iron of the firebox housing, and for one suspended moment — one heartbeat, two — nothing happened. The rain drummed. The guns cracked. The river roared below the landing, brown and swollen with the mountain melt, running higher and faster than Odile had ever seen it, so loud it seemed to fill the whole gorge.

Then the spray touched the firebox, and the Kestrel caught fire.

* * *

There is a moment in every engineer's life — Odile had believed this for thirty years, had believed it even in the disgrace years, even in the drinking years — when the machine you have tended turns to you and asks a question. Not in words. Machines don't speak in words. They ask in temperature and pressure, in the pitch of a bearing, in the smell of the air in the firebox. *What will you do with me?* the machine asks. *What am I for?*

The Kestrel was asking it now, fire crawling up the starboard rail, smoke boiling from the deck seams, and Odile Voss — disgraced, dismissed, drummed out of the Meridian Company's own yards for refusing to sign her name to boilers she knew would kill their crews — knelt in the engine room and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like peace, what the answer had to be.

The boat was lost. She'd known it from the first lick of flame. A fire in the fuel line with a full bunkered store, in a gorge, under rifle fire — no patch, no jury rig, no miracle of hers would save her. The question was not whether the Kestrel burned.

The question was what the burning was *for*.

She climbed the ladder into the rain and the smoke and found Beatrix at the wheel, hauling on it uselessly, the boat still tied to the landing, fire spreading aft along the rail in a bright, hissing line.

"She's gone, Captain," Odile said. Her voice came out steady. Thirty years of disasters had taught her voice to do that even when nothing else could. "You know she's gone."

Beatrix looked at her — and Odile saw the moment the captain understood it too, saw it land in her like a stone in deep water. Ten years Beatrix had steered this boat. Ten years of running, of hiding, of the Kestrel as armor and exile and home all at once. And now—

"Not yet," Beatrix said.

"No," Odile agreed. "Not yet."

Above them, on the shelf of rock, a rifleman stood to reload, and Beatrix shot him off his feet with the calm of a woman swatting a fly. Below them, the river tore past the landing, white-capped, furious, carrying whole trees in its grip like a child carrying kindling.

And Odile looked at the fire, and at the trail — the narrow gorge trail that wound down the cliffside from the adit, the only path down, the path Corvane's remaining men were even now descending, three of them, coming for the case on Wren's back — and the answer assembled itself in her mind the way an engine assembles itself in her dreams: part by part, true by true, until the whole machine stood finished and inevitable.

The Kestrel was tied at the base of the trail.

The trail was narrow. The fire was hungry.

"Captain," she said. "Cut her loose."

* * *

Beatrix stared at her. "She'll burn to the waterline and drift."

"She'll burn to the waterline and drift *downriver*," Odile said. "Past the landing. Past the trail. The gorge narrows two hundred yards down — there's the rock shelf, the one we scraped at Sorrow Bend, remember? She'll ground there or she won't, but either way she'll be *between*." She pointed up the trail, where the three riflemen were picking their way down through the smoke, silhouetted against the rain. "Between them and us. A burning boat across the only path down this cliff. Nobody walks past that. Not them. Not anyone."

"And us?"

"We take the case and we climb." She jerked her chin at the goat track on the far side of the landing, the one Wren's maps called the Drover's Way, the one that switchbacked up the gorge wall through scrub and scree. "It's a hard climb and a long one and I'm too old for it. And we do it anyway."

For a moment — one long, rain-drowned moment — Beatrix Ashgrove stood at the wheel of her boat and did not move, and Odile saw ten years of flight pass across her face like weather. The Kestrel had been her hiding place. Her shell. Her name-that-wasn't-a-name made of iron and steam.

Then the captain pulled the knife from her belt, crossed to the mooring line in three strides, and cut it.

"Finch!" she shouted. "Wren! Off the boat — now — take the case and *climb*!"

* * *

They got Wren up the first switchback before the Kestrel cleared the landing.

Odile went last. She told herself it was to make sure the others made the trail, and partly that was true. But partly — and she was honest enough with herself, in her worst moments, to know it — she went last because she could not bear to watch.

From the Drover's Way, fifty feet up the gorge wall, she turned and looked down through the rain and the smoke, and she saw her.

The Kestrel came off the landing slow at first, then all at once, the current taking her stern and swinging her out into the flood like a partner claiming a dance. Fire ran along her rail in a bright unbroken line. Smoke poured from her in a column that the rain bent and broke and could not extinguish. She looked — Odile's throat closed on the thought and she thought it anyway — she looked *alive*. She looked like a creature in her death agony, blazing and magnificent and utterly beyond saving, carried down the gorge by a river that had never once cared what any of them wanted.

And behind her, descending the trail, the three riflemen stopped.

Odile watched it happen. Watched the lead man see the burning boat swinging down toward the narrows, toward the shelf, toward the exact place where the trail met the water. Watched him shout something to the others that the river ate whole. Watched them turn and scramble back up the trail — too late, much too late, the Kestrel moving faster now, fire and flood and gravity all agreed on one thing—

The boat struck the shelf with a sound like the end of the world.

Iron on rock, a concussion that Odile felt through the cliff itself, up through her boots and into her teeth. The hull tore open along the starboard side — the side she'd patched, the side she'd *always* patched, the side that had never once been quite right — and the river poured in, and the fire met the fuel store, and the Kestrel went up in a single blossoming roar that turned the gray rain to gold.

The shockwave hit the cliff face. Scree came down in a rattling slide across the trail below. And when the smoke cleared — when Odile could see again through streaming eyes — there was no trail. There was no landing. There was a burning hulk wedged across the base of the gorge, half-sunk, still burning, a wall of flame and steam between the crew on the cliff and any man who might think to follow.

And there was Sergeant Corvane.

* * *

She never learned, afterward, exactly how he'd come to be there. Whether he'd been descending the trail behind his men, or whether he'd circled ahead along the riverbank, or whether — and this was the version Odile believed, because it fit the man — he had simply refused to believe the boat would be sacrificed, had refused to believe that anyone would give up so much, had stood his ground on the low rocks at the trail's end waiting for the Kestrel to come to him instead of away.

Whatever the reason, she saw him clearly: the grey coat dark with rain, the rifle slung, one hand raised against the heat — standing on the last dry stones at the base of the cliff as the burning boat came down the river toward him like a thrown torch.

"Corvane!" Beatrix shouted it from the switchback above, her voice raw. "*Move!*"

For one strange, suspended instant, the sergeant looked up. Found her on the cliff. Odile was close enough, in that moment, to see his face — and it was not fear on it. It was something worse. It was *incredulity*. The face of a man who had offered her everything, who had offered her old name back, her clean slate, her anonymity, her whole careful hidden life returned to her intact — and who could not comprehend a woman who had said no.

The Kestrel's burning hull struck the rocks below him.

The river, swollen with melt, running harder than it had run in a decade, took him the way it took everything — without malice, without mercy, without ever once pausing to ask. One moment he was there, grey coat and raised hand and incomprehension; the next the flood had him, and the burning boat rolled over his footing, and the river folded him into its brown and boiling heart and carried him away.

Odile watched the water where he'd been. She watched it for a long time.

She had spent her career, her ruin, and her redemption learning what rivers did to things that stood against them. She had signed no papers, she had burned no bridges, she had only ever — she told herself — only ever *refused*. But she knew, watching the flood swallow the place where Corvane had stood, that the river did not distinguish between the men who built the dams and the men who looked away from them, between the ones who signed and the ones who stayed silent. The water took them all the same.

She felt no triumph. She felt the cold.

"Odile." Beatrix's hand on her shoulder, hard, real, alive. "Odile. We have to climb."

"I know," Odile said.

"Odile." And now the captain's voice changed, gentled into something Odile had heard from her only once before, at Sorrow Bend, with the boiler screaming and Finch's hands blistered and the whole world coming apart. "I'm sorry. I know what she was to you."

And Odile Voss, who had rebuilt engines in the dark with bleeding hands, who had drunk herself out of the finest yards on the river, who had come aboard a stranger's boat with nothing but a toolkit and a shame she carried like ballast — Odile looked down at the burning wreck wedged across the gorge, at the boat that had been her redemption, her last chance, the first vessel since the *Meridian's Pride* that she had loved enough to be honest with—

And she found, to her astonishment, that she was not weeping for the boat.

She was weeping because, for the first time in thirty years, she was not the one who had failed it.

* * *

They climbed.

The Drover's Way was a scar of a trail, switchbacking up the gorge wall through wet scrub and loose stone, and they climbed it in the rain with the fire lighting their backs like a second sunset. Finch went first, favoring the bad ankle, jaw set in that stubborn line Odile had learned to read as *I will not be carried*. Wren came next with the case strapped to her back, one hand pressed to the bloody gash along her arm, climbing with her teeth bared. Then Beatrix. Then Odile, oldest and slowest, counting her breaths, counting her steps, counting the things she had lost and the things she had not.

The case was safe. The evidence was safe. Hollis's samples, his ledgers, his dead men's letters — all of it riding up the cliff on a wounded woman's back while the boat that carried them here burned to the waterline below.

*It was never the boat*, Odile told herself, one handhold at a time. *It was never the engine, never the hull, never the iron. It was what she carried. It was who she carried it for.*

Below them, the Kestrel burned on — a broken, blazing hulk wedged in the narrows, steam rising off her in great white ghosts, her name still legible on the stern through the smoke. *Kestrel*. Odile read it once, and did not look again.

Halfway up, Finch stopped on a ledge no wider than a coffin and looked back down, and Odile, hauling herself up beside the kid, saw tears cutting tracks through the soot on their face.

"She held," Finch said. "Right to the end. She held."

"Aye," Odile said. "She did."

It was, she thought, the only eulogy the boat would ever need.

* * *

They reached the top of the gorge at dusk, wet and bleeding and alive, and stood on the high moor with the rain finally thinning around them and the whole darkening world spread out below: the river a ribbon of brown violence, the smoke still rising from the narrows, the mountains to the east going purple and then black.

Wren sat down heavily on a stone and unstrapped the case and simply held it, both arms around it, like a child holding something that might be taken away. Beatrix stood apart, facing the river, and Odile did not need to see her face to know what she was watching. Ten years. A decade of hiding inside iron and steam, and now the iron was at the bottom of the gorge and the hiding was over, and the only thing between Beatrix Ashgrove and the Meridian Company was a four days' walk to the capital and the truth in Wren's arms.

"She's gone," Beatrix said at last. Not to anyone. To the river, maybe. To herself.

"No," Odile said.

Beatrix turned. Rain on her face. Exhaustion and grief and something underneath them both that Odile had watched growing for a thousand miles and could finally, finally name: *purpose*.

"She's not gone, Captain," Odile said. "She's doing exactly what we built her to do. She's carrying something." She nodded at the case in Wren's arms. "She's carrying *that*. Every mile we ever steamed her — the cargo runs, the rescues, the rapids, all of it — it was all just practice. This is the passage. This is the landfall. We just had to burn the boat to see it."

Beatrix looked at her for a long moment. Then she laughed — a short, cracked, astonished sound, half grief and half something else — and wiped her face with her sleeve, and turned east, toward the mountains, toward the capital, toward the magistrate and the testimony and the name she had not spoken aloud in ten years.

"Four days' walk," Beatrix said. "Maybe five, with Wren's arm."

"Three," said Finch, standing up straighter on the bad ankle, "if nobody slows me down."

And high above the burning narrows, on the black shoulder of the mountain, the four of them started down out of the hills — toward the capital, toward the truth, toward whatever waited for them at the end of the river's long story — while behind them, far below, the Kestrel burned like a signal fire, telling the whole dark gorge that they had come this way, and that they had not stopped, and that they were not, any of them, running anymore.
