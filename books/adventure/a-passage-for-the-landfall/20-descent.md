## 20. Descent

The mountain did not want us to leave.

That was the thought Finch kept returning to as they climbed down through the first morning of the descent, if climbing down could be called that — a controlled falling, hand over hand, boot by boot, the evidence case strapped across their back like a second spine. The gorge behind them still smoked where the Kestrel had burned and sunk, and the river that had carried them all this way now thundered somewhere below, invisible, a sound like the mountain grinding its teeth. Above, clouds came down the slopes in slow grey rivers of their own, erasing the trail, erasing the sky, erasing everything except the next ten feet of scree and the four people moving across it.

Finch's hands were wrapped in strips of Wren's petticoat, boiled clean in the last of the Kestrel's fresh water before Odile scuttled her. The burns had gone from white to red to a deep angry pink, and every grip on cold stone sent a bright wire of pain up both arms. Finch gripped anyway. That was the whole trick of it, they were learning. You gripped anyway.

"Footing," Beatrix called from below, her voice pitched low out of habit even though there was no one for miles who wasn't already hunting them. "Stay on the dark rock. The pale stuff shears."

Finch looked down. Beatrix moved over broken ground the way she'd moved over water — without seeming to decide anything, her body simply knowing, weight flowing from one foot to the next like the mountain was just another current she'd been reading all her life. Behind her came Wren, one arm bound against her chest where Corvane's bullet had torn through the meat of her shoulder, her face grey with pain she refused to name. Then Odile, broad and deliberate, carrying everything: the tools, the rope, the tinned food that made up most of what remained between them and hunger. And at the rear, Finch, with the case.

Nobody had discussed who would carry it. After the adit, after the gunfire and the burning boat and the scattering uphill through the pines while Company men shouted coordinates to each other in the smoke, Beatrix had simply looked at Finch and said, *You're lightest and you're fastest,* and Finch had taken the case the way you take a lit fuse — carefully, knowing what it was, knowing it might go off in your hands.

It weighed less than a boiler. It weighed more than a boat.

* * *

They camped that first night in a hollow beneath an overhang, no fire, sharing a tin of beans cold from the can and half a loaf of bread gone hard as slate. Wren couldn't manage the chewing with her bad arm braced, so Odile soaked the bread in bean liquor until it softened, then tore it into pieces small enough to swallow without effort, and fed them to her one at a time without ever making it look like feeding, just passing food along a line, casual as passing a tool. Wren let her. That was new. On the boat Wren had snapped at every offered kindness like it was a hook; up here, hurt and hunted, she'd stopped pretending she didn't need anyone, and Finch found something in that that ached worse than the burns.

"How far to the capital road?" Wren asked.

Beatrix turned her canteen in her hands. In the thin light her face looked carved, all planes and shadow, older than Finch had ever let themselves see. "Four days' walk if we're whole. Six or seven like this."

"And how long before they find the trail?"

"They already have." Beatrix said it flatly, without drama, the way she reported shoals. "There were lanterns on the ridge above us an hour after we left the gorge. They're not hurrying. They don't need to. They know where we're going."

"The magistrate," Finch said. "The journalist. That's the only door."

"That's the only door," Beatrix agreed. "And they mean to be standing in front of it when we knock."

Silence settled, the kind that has weight. Somewhere downslope a bird made a sound like a hinge complaining, and the wind moved through the pines with a long exhalation, and Finch sat with the case between their knees and thought about fire.

Not the fire on the boat. The other one. The one three years and a lifetime ago, in the stables at home, the one everyone had decided was theirs because someone had to decide, because grief wants a shape and blame is the easiest shape there is. Finch had run from that shape so long and so far that running had become the shape instead. And now here they were, walking straight toward the biggest fire in the country, carrying the thing that would light it, because four strangers had taught them that some fires you run toward.

"You should sleep," Beatrix said, and Finch realized she'd been watching them. "I'll take first watch."

"You watched last night too."

"I sleep less than I used to." A ghost of something crossed her face — not quite a smile. "Go on. The case will keep."

Finch lay down on cold stone with the strap still looped around one wrist, because letting go of it felt like a decision they weren't ready to make, and slept the way the hunted sleep: shallow, listening, dreaming in fragments of flame and water.

* * *

The second day the rain came.

It came sideways off the peaks, cold as well-water, turning the scree to grease and the pine duff to black soup. They descended a game trail that kept trying to become a waterfall, and twice Odile went ahead with the rope and anchored it around trunks, and they came down hand over hand through water that fell faster than they did. Wren's arm had begun to throb in a way that showed in her jaw. She climbed with her teeth set and never once asked to stop, and Finch, watching her, understood something about inheritance: Hollis had walked into those mountains alone and sick and stubborn, and his daughter was walking out of them the same way, and neither of them had ever once said *help me*, but both of them had finally learned to take it when it was given.

At midday they sheltered under a shelf of granite and ate nothing, because there was nothing, and Beatrix stood at the lip of the overhang reading the valley below with the same attention she gave a bend in the river.

"There," she said quietly.

Finch followed her gaze. Far down, where the trees broke around a scar of raw earth, a road switchbacked toward a bridge over a tributary — and on the bridge, small with distance but unmistakable, men in grey. Not moving. Waiting. Watching the one crossing that any sane fugitive would use.

"Company?" Odile asked, though she knew.

"Corvane doesn't travel with anyone else's colours anymore." Beatrix's voice did something complicated. "He survived the river. Of course he did. Men like him float."

"He fell into floodwater," Wren said. "I saw him go."

"So did I," Beatrix said. "And I've seen the river give back things it had no business giving back." She turned. "We don't cross there. There's a ford upstream, above the falls — Hollis marked it on his map, back when I thought the map was about gold." Her mouth twisted at the old irony. "It adds a day. Maybe two, in this weather."

"A day we don't have," Odile said.

"A crossing we survive," Beatrix answered. "I've paid for every mile of this river up front. I'm not stopping paying now."

Nobody argued. That was the strange arithmetic of the mountains, Finch thought: every choice cost, and you chose the price you could carry.

They went up instead of down, which felt like blasphemy, climbing away from the capital they were starving toward, and the rain followed them like a debt collector.

* * *

The third night Wren collapsed.

No warning, no complaint — she simply stopped, mid-stride on a stretch of level ground, and folded like a dropped sail. Odile caught her before she hit the rocks. Her shoulder wound had gone hot; the skin around the bandage was flushed and shining, and when Finch touched her forehead their burned fingers registered heat before pain did.

"Fever," Odile said, and her voice, usually so steady, had a crack in it. "The wound's fouled."

"There's a spring Hollis noted," Beatrix said. "Half a mile. Hot water — he wrote it steams even in winter." She was already pulling Wren's good arm across her shoulders. "We make the spring. We boil cloth. We—"

"We what?" Wren said, from somewhere far away inside her fever. Her eyes opened, unfocused, furious even now. "Cut it open? With what? Your paring knife?" She laughed, a dry terrible sound. "My father walked out of these mountains with samples in his boots and died in a stranger's bed, and I'm going to die on a goat path because I wanted to see his handwriting one more time—"

"Stop it." Finch heard their own voice come out harder than they meant. Everyone looked at them. Finch's heart was slamming and their hands screamed inside their wrappings and they kept talking anyway, because someone had to, because this was the thing they knew best in the world, the territory they'd been living in since the stable fire: "You don't get to decide you're dead. I decided that once. Decided I was the boy who burned his brother, decided that was the whole story, start to finish. It nearly finished me. You've got a story with a middle left, Wren. Don't you dare skip to the end."

The rain ticked on the granite. Wren stared at them with fever-bright eyes, and something in her face shifted — not softening exactly, more like ice cracking to show the water underneath.

"Half a mile," she said at last. "Fine. Half a mile."

Odile took her other side. They moved as one creature with six legs and two working arms, and Finch walked ahead with the case and found the spring by smell — sulfur and wet stone — before the map's bearings confirmed it.

They stripped the wound by lantern-less dark, Odile holding Wren down while Beatrix worked with the paring knife boiled in the hot pool and a needle threaded with silk from Wren's own hem. Finch held the light — a stub of candle shielded under their coat — and did not look away, because looking away would have been a lie, and they were done with lies, done with running, done with being the person who couldn't watch. Wren bit down on a rolled sleeve and made no sound at all except at the very end, one syllable — *Da* — that nobody mentioned afterward and everybody remembered forever.

Then Beatrix packed the wound with moss she said Hollis had taught her about, years ago, in another life, and Wren slept, actually slept, and the spring breathed its steam into the freezing air, and Finch sat with their feet in the hot water and their arms in agony and felt, absurdly, close to happy.

*You're lightest and you're fastest.* But that wasn't why Beatrix had given them the case. Finch had figured that out somewhere on the second day. She'd given it to them because carrying it would change what they were. Because she'd seen a kid shaped like blame and handed them a burden shaped like purpose, and trusted them to grow into it.

Endurance, Finch was beginning to understand, was just love that had learned to keep moving.

* * *

On the fifth day they came down out of the trees and saw the world again.

The valley opened below them gold-green in morning light, terraced fields, a river running wide and tame, and far off, hazed with distance, the smoke-smudge of the capital itself — chimneys, spires, the grey crown of the magistrate's court. Real. Reachable. A day's walk, maybe less, along the valley road where there would be people, carts, witnesses — safety in numbers, if the numbers hadn't been bought.

Beatrix stood at the treeline a long time, saying nothing. Finch watched her and saw her doing the thing she did at hard water: reading, weighing, choosing her line. Except this time the line ran through open country, in daylight, past every eye in the valley, and there was no fog to run dark in, no rapids to lose pursuit among. From here on, the only cover was speed and luck and each other.

"They'll be watching the roads," Odile said. "Every approach. If Corvane lives, he's spent five days making sure we arrive escorted."

"Then we don't take the roads." Beatrix's voice was calm, but Finch had learned to hear the current underneath it. "Fields. Ditches. The drainage culverts along the millrace — towns like this never guard their own plumbing." Almost, almost, a smile. "Ten years of running teaches you how towns are built. Turns out it was all practice."

Wren, upright for the first time since the spring, grey-faced and swaying but vertical, snorted softly. "A decade of cowardice repurposed as tradecraft."

"A decade of survival," Beatrix said, and didn't deny the rest, and didn't apologize either, and Finch loved her for both.

They started down. The case rode Finch's back, lighter than it had ever been and heavier than anything, full of dead men's letters and poisoned water and the truth about a company that owned the law it answered to. Below, a church bell rang the hour, ordinary and clear, and Finch thought of the capital waiting with its magistrates and its journalists and its warrants, and of the moment coming — soon now, hours — when Beatrix Ashgrove would stand in front of strangers and say her true name aloud for the first time in ten years.

And then Finch saw them.

Down at the valley's mouth, where the road bent through the last stand of poplars before the fields began: riders. Four of them, dismounted, standing easy beside their horses like men who had been waiting a long time and expected to wait longer. Grey coats. And at the center, leaner than before, river-scoured, one arm stiff at his side, a man who turned his head slowly along the treeline as though he could smell them on the wind.

Sergeant Corvane raised one hand — not a threat, not yet. An acknowledgment. *I see you.*

Beside Finch, Beatrix went very still, the way she went still at the top of the worst water, right before she committed.

"Well," she said quietly. "One last passage, then."
