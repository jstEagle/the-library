## 21. Names Spoken Aloud

The capital smelled of coal smoke and cut stone, and Beatrix hated it on sight.

She had been in cities before — she had been *of* a city once, though that was a life with another name attached to it, a name she had buried so deep that some mornings she had to dig for it like a coin dropped through a floorboard. Cities were where names got spoken. Rivers were where they drowned. For ten years the Vell had been her confessor and her accomplice both, carrying away everything she did not want found, and now here she stood on a granite quay with a leather case full of dead men's words and no river left to hide behind.

The Kestrel was gone. She felt the absence of it the way an amputee feels weather — a dull ache in a limb that wasn't there anymore, burned and scuttled in a gorge two hundred miles upstream to buy four people their lives. Odile had not spoken for a day afterward. Finch had cried without making any sound at all, which was somehow worse than sobbing. And Wren had put her hand flat against the deckhouse as it went down, as if taking a pulse.

Now the four of them came up out of the rail yard into the capital's morning, travel-stained and hollow-eyed, and the case rode against Beatrix's hip like a second heart.

"You're sure about this magistrate," Odile said for the fourth time since Callow's Rest. "Vashti Corren. You're sure she isn't bought."

"I'm sure of what Wren's letters say." Beatrix shifted the case's strap on her shoulder. It had grown heavier with every mile, or perhaps she had grown weaker; she could no longer tell the difference. "A judge who lost her license to practice because she took a mining injury case against Meridian. They ruined her quietly and thought that would be the end of it."

"People who are ruined quietly," Odile said, "usually stay quiet."

"Then we'll be loud."

Finch laughed — a short, startled bark, as if the sound had escaped custody. It was the first laugh any of them had managed since the gorge, and it broke something loose in the gray morning. Even Wren's mouth twitched, though she disguised it as adjusting the collar of her coat, which had belonged to her father and which she had not taken off since the Narrows.

They walked. The capital rose around them in tiers of pale limestone, government buildings shouldering up out of the fog like the ribs of a drowned ship, and everywhere the Meridian Company's mark — the stylized M inside its circle of water — stamped on lamp posts and freight wagons and the brass plates of banks. Beatrix counted them the way she used to count snags in the current. Eleven. Fourteen. Nineteen. The city wore the Company the way the river wore its silt: evenly, everywhere, as if it had always been there and always would be.

*It hasn't,* she told herself. *Nothing has always been anything.*

* * *

Magistrate Vashti Corren received them in a chamber that smelled of ink and cold tea, a narrow room stacked with paper to the ceiling beams, lit by one tall window that turned the dust motes into drifting snow. The magistrate herself was small and iron-haired, with hands stained brown to the second knuckle, and she looked at the four of them the way Beatrix looked at a boat riding too low in the water — assessing what she carried and whether the hull would hold.

"Sit down," Corren said. "All of you. You look like you've been dragged behind a barge."

"Close enough," said Odile.

Beatrix set the case on the desk between them. The sound it made — the soft, final weight of it — seemed louder than it should have been. Corren did not reach for it at once. She looked at Beatrix instead, a long, level look, and Beatrix understood that this woman had spent years learning to read faces the way Beatrix read water, looking for the snag beneath the surface.

"You sent the letters," Corren said. "From Callow's Rest. Signed *W. Hollis.*"

"That was me." Wren stepped forward. Her voice held steady, but her hands, Beatrix noticed, were white-knuckled around the strap of her satchel. "My father gathered what's in there. I only finished reading it."

"Your father is—"

"Dead. He died on the Vell River, on the ninth of last month, of the mine cough he caught in Company shafts at a place called Kelter's Deep. He spent his last weeks buying passage on a stranger's boat because he didn't trust anyone else alive to carry this." Wren unclasped the satchel and began laying things on the magistrate's desk with the deliberate care of someone setting bones. Sample vials wrapped in wool, each labeled in a cramped, slanting hand. Ledgers with columns of dates and tonnages. A bundle of affidavits tied with tarred twine, each page bearing a signature and, beneath several of them, a smudged thumbprint in place of a seal because the men who signed them had never learned to write more than their names. "Water samples from the headwaters above the Kelter works. Arsenic and tailings runoff, assayed twice by my own hand — I run an assay office, or I did. The ledgers show the Company knew the seepage was reaching the wells at Millbrook and Ashford Landing. The affidavits—" Her voice caught, and she steadied it. "The affidavits are from workers they silenced. Some of them are from men who are dead now. My father copied everything twice and hid the originals at the source of the Vell, in an old adit, because he said no one would ever look for truth at the beginning of things."

Corren opened the first vial, sniffed it, and set it down with the reverence of a woman handling relics. Then she read. She read for a long time. The window light moved across the floor, and nobody spoke, and Beatrix became aware of Finch standing close enough that their shoulder nearly touched hers — the kid had done that ever since the storm anchorage, drifted toward her in rooms the way iron filings drift toward a lodestone.

Finally Corren closed the ledger and looked up.

"This is enough to suspend every Meridian operating license on the upper Vell," she said. "It may be enough to bring criminal charges. What it is *not* enough for is a trial — trials need witnesses, and the Company has a way of making witnesses into memories. Which brings me to the question I have to ask, and I'll ask it plainly." Her eyes went around the room, and settled, at last, on Beatrix. "Who are you? Not your boat. Not your cargo. You. Because if this goes forward, the Company will pull every thread attached to it, and I cannot defend a witness whose name I don't know."

* * *

The room went very quiet. Outside, somewhere below the window, a cart rattled over cobbles, and a vendor was crying the price of eels in a voice worn smooth as river glass.

Beatrix had rehearsed this moment for ten years without knowing it. In the worst hours — becalmed in fog, or lying awake listening to rain on the deckhouse — she had imagined being cornered, exposed, dragged into the light by men in Company grey. Sergeant Corvane had come closer than anyone; he had stood on a dock at Grayhollow and spoken her old name like a knife laid flat against her throat, offering it back to her as a bribe. *I know who you used to be, Captain. Everyone can be bought with their own past.* She had refused him and told herself it was courage, and part of it had been, but part of it had been only the oldest instinct of all: *run*.

There was no running from this room. There was only the door in front of her and the door behind her, and she had spent a decade walking through doors that led backward.

Odile shifted beside her. "You don't have to," the engineer murmured. "We can testify. Me, the kid, Wren. You've done enough."

"That's just it," Beatrix said. She heard her own voice as if it belonged to someone standing slightly apart from her. "I haven't. I've been doing the arithmetic wrong for ten years. I kept thinking staying cost too much." She looked at Finch — at the burn-scarred hands, the chin lifted in that stubborn way that reminded her painfully of a girl she had once been, a girl who had also run from a fire she couldn't put out. She looked at Wren, who had inherited a dead man's purpose and carried it like a yoke she refused to call a gift. She looked at Odile, who had scuttled her own redemption with her own hands and stood up straighter for it.

"Ask me again," Beatrix said to the magistrate. "Who am I."

Corren folded her hands. "Who are you?"

"My name is Beatrix Ashgrove." It came out steady, and that surprised her; she had expected it to break. "That's the name I chose, ten years ago, when I bought a broken steamboat with money I'd saved working the lower river under other names. Before that—" and here it did catch, briefly, a snag in the current, and she let it pass through her rather than fighting it, the way you let a flood crest rather than drowning against it — "before that I was Beatrix Marlowe, of Marlowe Shipping, daughter of Aldous Marlowe. My father ran freight contracts for the Meridian Company. When I was nineteen I found his ledgers — the real ones, the ones that showed he was moving Company ore past inspection while Company slag poisoned the towns downstream, and that he knew. I confronted him. Three days later our warehouse burned. Two men died in it. The fire was ruled an accident, and my father's partners made it clear that if I stayed, if I talked, the next fire wouldn't be ruled anything at all. So I ran. I changed my name and I went to the river because rivers forget, and I told myself forgetting was the same thing as being free." She drew a breath. "It isn't. I'm done confusing the two."

Corren had gone very still. "Marlowe," she repeated. "The Marlowe fire. That was in the papers for a week, twenty years ago. They never found the daughter."

"No," Beatrix said. "They didn't. I made sure of it. And then a dying man made me promise to deliver something, and I discovered that a promise is heavier than a name, and it doesn't fit in hiding places." She touched the case on the desk. "Everything in there connects to what my father helped cover up. The poisoning at Kelter's Deep runs through the same veins as the fire in my father's warehouse. Same company. Same methods. Silence, then smoke, then water. If you want witnesses who'll still be breathing when the trial starts, Magistrate, you'll want all four of us — but you'll want me most, because I'm the thread they can't cut without unraveling themselves. I'm the proof that this isn't new. I'm the reason they should be afraid."

The window light had climbed to the top of the paper stacks. Somewhere in the building a bell rang the hour, and its vibration passed up through the floorboards into Beatrix's boots, and she realized her knees were shaking and that it didn't matter, that trembling was just what a body did when it finally set down a weight it had carried too long.

Corren nodded slowly. Then she pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward her, dipped her pen, and wrote at the top of it, in a hand as neat as type: *Testimony of Beatrix Ashgrove, formerly Marlowe.* She dated it. She turned it around and slid it across the desk along with the pen.

"Sign it," she said. "In your own name. Both of them."

* * *

It took three days for the machinery to turn, and Beatrix spent them in a boarding house near the courthouse that smelled of beeswax and boiled mutton, sleeping badly in a bed too soft, waking at dawn out of habit and lying still, listening for a river that wasn't there.

On the second day, Corren suspended the Meridian Company's operating licenses on the upper Vell — all of them, forty-one licenses read aloud in open session, each one falling like a plank knocked out of a bridge. On the third day, warrants went out: for the directors of the Kelter works, for the surveyor who had falsified the water reports, for three foremen whose names appeared in Hollis's affidavits beside the word *silenced*. And for Sergeant Corvane of the Company's private force, wanted for assault, arson, and the deaths of two miners at a place called the Narrows.

Beatrix read the warrant list pinned outside the courthouse, and stopped at Corvane's name, and felt — nothing she had expected. Not triumph. Something quieter. Pity, maybe, for a man who had spent his whole life believing everyone could be bought, and who had never once considered that he might be the thing for sale.

It was the journalist who found them there. A lean woman with ink on her fingers and a notebook already open, who had sat through all three days of session in the back row writing as fast as the magistrates spoke. "Captain Ashgrove," she said, and Beatrix flinched at the sound of it — her name, spoken aloud by a stranger, in daylight, on the steps of a courthouse — and then, deliberately, did not flinch a second time. "The *Clarion* will print your testimony in full tomorrow. Every word. There are towns on the Vell that have waited twenty years for those words. Is there anything you'd like to say to them?"

Beatrix looked past the journalist, down the long stone stairs to where the street opened onto the harbor, and beyond the harbor to the gray glitter of the sea, and beyond the sea to nothing at all — the edge of every map she'd ever known. Finch stood at her elbow, and Odile, and Wren with her father's coat collar turned up against the wind, and the four of them cast one shadow on the courthouse steps, thin and long in the late light.

"Tell them the water runs clean above Kelter's Deep," Beatrix said. "And tell them it's going to run clean below it too. Tell them a dead prospector kept better records than a company worth millions, and tell them his daughter finished the job." She paused. "And tell them the woman who carried it upriver was afraid the whole way. Being brave and being afraid aren't opposites. I spent ten years thinking they were."

The journalist wrote it down. Somewhere behind them, inside the courthouse, a clerk was stamping warrants, each strike of the stamp landing like a paddle stroke, carrying them all — the living and the dead, Hollis and the burned men of Marlowe warehouse and the silenced miners of Kelter's Deep — steadily, irrevocably, downstream.

Wren touched her arm. "There's a boatyard on the east quay," she said. "Odile's been looking at it since yesterday. She says the owner's asking a fair price for a hull with good bones."

"A hull," Beatrix said, "is not a boat."

"No," Wren agreed, and there was something in her face that was almost her father's crooked smile, resurrected. "It needs a name first."

Beatrix looked out at the water, at the wide bright road of it running toward the edge of everything, and for the first time in ten years, the horizon did not look like an escape. It looked like a heading.
