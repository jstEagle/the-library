## 22. A Passage for the Landfall

The river ran clean again.

Beatrix felt it before she saw it — the way the water came off her fingers when she trailed a hand over the rail at dawn, no slick of tailings on the skin, no faint chemical bitterness that had lived in the Vell for as long as anyone could remember. Just cold mountain water and the smell of stone and pine, the smell the river must have carried before the Company ever drove its first adit into the hills.

She stood at the wheel of a boat that was not the Kestrel and watched the light come up over Grayhollow's rooftops, and she let herself feel the thing she had spent ten years refusing to feel: that this was home, or close enough to it that the word no longer burned going down.

* * *

They had built the Landfall in a slip below the old shipyard, where the bones of half-rotted hulls went to be forgotten. Odile had found the frame — a decommissioned packet boat, sound ribs under rotten planking — and had fallen upon her like a woman reunited with a lost sister. For four months Beatrix had come down to the yard each morning to find Odile already there, sleeves rolled, hair tied back with a scrap of tarred canvas, arguing with the shipwrights in three dialects of profanity until they did it her way.

"She'll never win a race," Odile had admitted once, wiping solder smoke from her eyes, "but she'll outlive every one of us. I've built her to be forgiven."

That was the difference, Beatrix thought now, watching the new boat take the current. The Kestrel had been built to run — shallow draft, hard lines, an engine tuned for escape. The Landfall was built to stay. Deeper keel. Wider beam. A cargo deck meant for freight that mattered, and cabins with actual doors, because the people aboard were meant to sleep through the night.

Finch came up from the engine room two at a time, grease to the elbows, grinning like the sun itself had hired them.

"Boiler's singing, Captain. Odile says if I let her run hot past the red line one more time she'll feed me to the paddlewheel, but between us, she was smiling when she said it."

"Between us," Beatrix said, "she always is."

Finch leaned on the rail beside her. Sixteen months since Sorrow Bend, and the burn scars on their hands had faded to pale seams they flexed without thinking, the way other people cracked their knuckles. They'd grown an inch, maybe more. Their voice had settled into something lower. But the grin hadn't changed, and neither had the way they looked at the water — hungry, unafraid, certain the river owed them something good.

"First run," Finch said. "You nervous?"

"No."

"You're lying."

"I'm the captain," Beatrix said. "I'm allowed to lie about being nervous. It's in the articles."

Finch laughed and clattered back down the ladder, and Beatrix turned back to the river, and let the lie sit there between them, warm as a coal.

* * *

Wren came aboard last, as she always did, carrying more than any one person should — a crate of assay equipment, a satchel of documents she still reviewed nightly out of habit, and a paper-wrapped bundle that Beatrix knew, without asking, held Hollis's map. Or what remained of it. The wax seal had been broken at the source; the map itself had been entered into evidence, photographed, copied, argued over by magistrates for the better part of a year. What Wren kept was the original, returned to her by the court with a formality that had made her laugh — a bitter, disbelieving laugh, the first of its kind Beatrix had heard from her.

Now Wren set her burdens down on the cargo deck and stood beside Beatrix at the rail, and for a while neither of them spoke. That was the shape their friendship had taken: long silences that were not empty.

"They're saying the headwater towns are getting compensation," Wren said finally. "Real compensation. There's a commission sitting in Callow's Rest next month." She turned the words over like ore samples, still unable to fully trust their weight. "My father's name is on the founding affidavits. They've carved it into the courthouse stone. Hollis, E. Prospector. First witness."

"He'd have hated that," Beatrix said.

"He'd have pretended to hate it," Wren said, "while memorizing exactly what the stone said." She looked out at the water. "I used to think he left because of me. That I wasn't enough to stay for. It took me thirty years and a dead man's handwriting to understand he left because he couldn't stop, and that those might be the same sickness, but they aren't the same thing."

"And now?"

"Now I'm going upstream," Wren said, "on purpose, with people, and I'm coming back after. That's the whole miracle, Captain. Don't make me say it twice."

* * *

Odile cast off the lines herself, waving off the dockhand who reached for them — some ceremonies a woman keeps for herself. She'd been offered positions at three yards and one university since the trial; the Company's fall had dragged her old disgrace up into the light and turned it over, and everyone could see now that the boiler explosion at Meridian Yard eleven years ago had been a design flaw the Company had known about and buried, and that Odile Vance had spent a decade wearing the blame that belonged to men with better tailoring.

She'd declined all the offers. When Beatrix asked why, Odile had shrugged and said, "I already have a boat," in a tone that ended the conversation.

Now she took the wheel beside Beatrix, checked her heading against the current out of habit even though Beatrix had the con, and said, "Tide's with us. Current's gentle. River's clear of dredge channels for the first time in nine years — I checked the new charts myself."

"I know. I watched you check them."

"Someone has to. You'd navigate by memory and stubbornness."

"That's how I navigated everything for ten years," Beatrix said. "It worked."

"It worked," Odile agreed, "the way a man walking a tightrope works. Doesn't mean the ground underneath wasn't always there, waiting to be stood on."

* * *

They went downstream through the morning, past Grayhollow's rebuilt docks, past the sandbar where it had all begun — the wreck of the rival boat long since salvaged, the channel marked now with proper buoys, painted white and gold by the new River Authority, which employed actual inspectors who could actually be fired. Beatrix looked at the bar as they passed and felt nothing sharp about it. Only the memory of hauling two strangers out of the water and refusing their thanks, and the distance between that woman and this one, measured not in miles but in something harder to chart.

At Sorrow Bend the rapids still roared — the river would never be tame there, and no one wanted it to be — but the patrol cutters were gone, their moorings dismantled, their crews reassigned or scattered. A fishing skiff worked the eddy below the bend, an old man and a boy, and the boy raised an oar to them as the Landfall slid past, and Finch raised both arms back and shouted something joyful and probably unrepeatable.

At the Narrows, where the Kestrel had bled and Odile had rammed the logjam and Wren had caught a bullet-graze shielding a leather map case with her own body, the water ran high and bright through rock walls that remembered nothing. Beatrix slowed there anyway. Not for the danger. For the dead.

They had lost no one that day, by some arithmetic she still didn't trust. Corvane had gone into the flood-swollen river and the river had kept him; his body had washed up forty miles down, and the warrants had gone out for the men above him, and some of those trials were still grinding through the capital like millstones. But the miners whose letters they'd carried out of the adit — the ones the Company had buried along with the evidence — had no one to slow down for them. So Beatrix slowed the boat, and rang the bell once, soft, and the others came to the rail without being asked, because they always knew.

"One pass," Odile murmured. "For the passage."

"For the passage," Beatrix said, and rang it again, and took her through.

* * *

They moored that first night in the flooded quarry cove where the hull had been patched and the crew had nearly torn itself apart. The fire ring was still there, blackened stones arranged in a circle someone had never bothered to scatter. Finch built a fire in it before the tents were even pitched, as though the ring had been waiting all this time for hands it recognized.

They ate river fish and hard bread and Odile's miraculous tinned peaches, saved from some hoard only she understood, and Wren told the story of the magistrate's clerk who had tried to lose her paperwork twice and been found, both times, by Wren standing in his office at dawn with the duplicate copies and a face like a drawn blade. Finch acted out the part of the clerk, cringing and sweating, until Odile laughed so hard she had to put down her cup.

Later, when Finch had gone to their bedroll and Odile sat smoking by the fire with the particular silence she wore when the past was close, Wren moved along the log to sit beside Beatrix.

"You're staying," Wren said. Not a question.

"We're running cargo. Callow's Rest, then the headwater towns, then back. Regular route, if the contracts hold."

"That's not what I asked."

Beatrix fed a stick to the fire. "No," she said. "It isn't."

She had given her true name in open testimony eight months ago — *Beatrix Ashgrove, formerly of the Ashgrove line, formerly of the Company's ledgers, formerly a great many things* — and the sky had not fallen. Her old creditors had surfaced, of course, and her old enemies, and one elderly aunt she'd genuinely believed dead, who had written a letter so scalding about her decade of silence that Beatrix had read it aloud to the crew just to watch them wince. But the name had turned out to be a door, not a trap. People shook her hand now. Some of them thanked her. A journalist had called her *the woman who stopped running*, which was flattering and almost entirely wrong — she had stopped running the day she'd sworn a promise to a dying man, and everything after had merely been the catching-up.

"The thing I never understood," Wren said, watching the fire, "was what it cost you. Staying. My father paid for leaving, and I paid for his leaving, and I thought I understood the whole ledger. But you paid the other side of it. Every port, every friend, every morning — you paid rent on your own life, forever, in advance, and never got to live in it."

"It seemed cheaper at the time."

"And now?"

Beatrix looked across the fire at Odile, who was pretending not to listen and failing beautifully. At the tent where Finch slept with their boots on, because some habits outlive their reasons. At the Landfall riding at her moorings in the cove where she had very nearly lost all of them, holding the water the way a good boat holds it — patiently, forgivingly, built to be forgiven.

"Now the rent's paid," Beatrix said. "Turns out I own the place."

Wren smiled — the real one, the rare one, the one that made her look suddenly and unbearably like her father. "He'd have liked you," she said. "He'd have driven you mad, and then he'd have liked you."

"He did drive me mad," Beatrix said. "And then he handed me a map and a promise and died at dawn, and it was the best trade I ever made, and I will deny having said that if you ever quote me."

"Noted," Wren said, and pulled out a notebook, and wrote it down.

* * *

They launched early on the third day, upstream, toward Callow's Rest and the high country and the source itself, which Beatrix had not seen since the morning the guns went silent and the gorge filled with smoke and the river swallowed the last of the Kestrel's burning deck.

The spring-fed pool was unchanged. Of course it was. It had been pouring out of the mountain since before there were names for anything, and it would pour long after. But the mine adit above it had a new timber lintel, and carved into the lintel, in letters deep enough to outlast weather, were the names of the miners — all of them, every name from Hollis's letters, cut by volunteers from the headwater towns who had climbed up with chisels and grief and taken turns working through a whole summer.

Beatrix stood before it a long time. Behind her, the others waited, because they understood that some landings a captain makes alone.

She touched the lowest name — a boy's name, sixteen years old, the same age Finch had been in the fire — and said, quietly, the words she'd carried up the mountain and never said at the trial, because the trial had been for the law and this was for the dead:

"You're delivered. All of you. Safe passage."

Then she turned and walked back down to the boat, and the crew fell in around her without a word, and they took the Landfall out onto water no map had ever needed to know, because now the whole world knew it, and the knowing had set it free.

* * *

They ran the upper river through the long gold afternoon, and Beatrix stood at the wheel with the sun on her shoulders and her crew scattered along the rails behind her — Odile humming at the engine telegraph, Wren reading survey notes aloud just to hear how they sounded in open air, Finch up in the bow with their arms spread wide into the wind like a figurehead that had climbed down off the prow and decided to live.

Stopping, Beatrix thought, had cost her everything for ten years. Every pause was a debt, every kindness a signature, every name spoken a nail in a coffin she was building for her own freedom. She had taught herself to move the way water moves — without rest, without roots, without looking back.

But water, she understood now, watching the current carry them home, doesn't run *from* anything. It runs *toward*. Downhill, yes, and onward, always onward — but toward the sea, toward the gathering, toward the place where all the separated streams remember they were one water and become it again.

The Landfall swept around the last bend above Callow's Rest, and the town came into view in the evening light — lamplight kindling in windows, smoke rising straight from chimneys, a crowd already gathering at the landing because word traveled faster than boats and everyone in the headwater country knew the name *Landfall* now, and the names of the four who sailed her.

On the dock, front and center, stood a girl of perhaps ten, holding a lantern high, and beside her an old man with a prospector's stoop and a young woman with a surveyor's case, and behind them dozens more, and above them all, fresh-carved into the new courthouse stone, a name that was no longer anyone's shame.

Beatrix Ashgrove brought her boat alongside slow and easy, and threw the line to waiting hands, and stepped ashore without looking back — because there was nothing behind her anymore, and everything ahead, and for the first time in ten years, arriving felt exactly like coming home.

The lantern-light caught the brass nameplate on the bow. Somewhere in the crowd, a fiddle struck up. And the river ran on beneath them, clean and cold and bound for the sea, carrying its long passage of light down through the darkening land — the way it always had, the way it always would, toward the landfall at the end of all running.
