## 1. Nothing to Track Across

There is a particular silence that lives on the salt flats, and Coral Dravenmoor had learned, over nine days of walking into it, that it was not an absence of sound at all. It was a sound. It pressed against the ears like water, like a held breath, like the moment after a rifle shot in a canyon when the echo has gone and the rock is still deciding whether to answer. He had stopped fighting it somewhere around the third day. Now he walked inside it the way a fish swims inside a lake — surrounded, sustained, slowly being made part of it.

The flats stretched white in every direction, cracked into hexagons the size of wagon wheels, each one edged with a rind of glittering mineral crust that caught the late sun and threw it back in knives. No grass. No stone. No shadow but his own, stretched long and thin and blue-black across the blinding ground, a second man walking a half-mile behind him and gaining nothing.

Nothing to track across. That was the first lesson every ranger learned in the Ashvane school, back when Coral had been seventeen and all knees and certainty. Instructor Hale had said it with a piece of chalk in his hand, tapping the great map of the coast: *Sand remembers. Rock remembers. Salt remembers nothing.* On the flats a man could walk for a day and leave less trace than a moth leaves crossing a room. Wind came off the dry lakebed in the afternoons and erased even the memory of footprints, sifting the fine dust into every crack until the whole white world was smooth again, unmarked, innocent.

Which was why Coral was not tracking footprints.

He was tracking a lie.

The compass hung at his chest on a braided leather cord, and it was a beautiful lie, and it was his. Brass case gone soft-edged from forty years of thumbs, the glass scratched in one corner where his mother had once — so the story went — dropped it down a well and fished it out with a hook and a prayer. The rose etched on the face had faded to a ghost of itself. The needle, though. The needle was perfect, or it had been, once. His father used to hold it up on the ridge above their holding at dusk and read it like a psalm: *north by a hair east, boy, always a hair east, because the mountain pulls.* Every compass in the district lied a little, pulled by iron in the hills, but this one lied honestly, the same small lie every day, and a man who knew its lie could trust it better than his own shadow.

Now the needle pointed southeast. Steadily, patiently, unwaveringly southeast — across two hundred miles of country that had no southeast in it worth going to. No town. No water. Nothing but the drowned coast and the sea.

It had pointed that way since the night Vess Marrow took it.

Coral stopped at the edge of a salt hexagon and crouched, more from habit than need, and lifted the compass to eye level. The needle trembled with his pulse and settled. Southeast. Of course. He let it swing, watched it fight its way back, patient as a dog returning to a bone. Nine days ago it had spun like a dying insect, wild, because the thief had been moving fast and doubling back through the river country. Now it held steady, and steady meant the man had stopped running and started traveling, and traveling meant a destination, and a destination meant Coral was gaining.

He had learned to read the needle the way other men read tracks. A wobble to the northeast for half a day, then back — that was a man avoiding the toll road. A long slow arc through the afternoon — that was a man following the curve of the dry river, taking the easy walking. The compass didn't point at Vess Marrow. It pointed at itself. The lodestone in the needle's heart answered the lodestone in the case's base, a whisper between two pieces of the same mountain, and when the two halves were pulled apart the needle strained toward home like a man leaning against a rope. Distance weakened it. Direction never. Wherever the compass was, the needle remembered where it ought to be, and the difference between the two was a line drawn straight through the world to the thief's pocket.

His mother had called the trick the needle's homesickness. She'd thought it was a charming story. She had never needed it to be true.

Coral stood, and the flats took his weight with a faint crunch of crystal, and he walked on.

* * *

He measured the days in small rituals now, because on the flats there was nothing else to measure. Dawn: he drank the half-cup of water that was his ration until Grayharbor, no more, chewing a strip of dried goat to make the thirst feel earned. Midmorning: he checked his boots, because a blister on the flats became a fever, and a fever became a grave with no marker, because salt remembers nothing. Noon: he walked in his own shadow, the only shade in the world. And at dusk, when the white ground turned rose and then violet and then the color of a bruise, he sat with his back against his pack and held the compass and talked to it.

Not to Vess Marrow. To the compass. He was not yet so far gone as to talk to the man who'd robbed him, though some nights it was a near thing.

"Southeast still," he told it. "Good. Stay put."

It was on the sixth dusk that the story came up in him again, the way it did every night, unbidden, wearing a different face each time — sometimes anger, sometimes grief, and worst of all, sometimes that cold arithmetic that felt like clarity but tasted like rot.

His brother Alden had been sick for two years by the end. Lung-rot, the physiker said, from the years Alden spent in the smelter towns downriver before he came home, bringing his cough with him like luggage. The physiker's fee was forty crowns, and forty crowns was a number that did not exist in the Dravenmoor household, and so Coral had done the thing that still sat in his chest like a swallowed stone: he had taken the compass to Pem Varrow's pawnhouse in Lowmark and set it on the counter between them, and Pem had looked at it a long time and said, *This is worth more than forty crowns, ranger, and you know it, and that's why it hurts.*

"Forty," Coral had said. "And I redeem it inside the year."

"Inside the year," Pem agreed, and wrote the ticket in his spider hand, and Coral had carried the money home in a sack against his belly like a stolen child, and for eleven months Alden breathed easier and even laughed sometimes, and Coral let himself believe that debts were just numbers and numbers could be walked off like distances.

Then the year turned, and the money wasn't there, and Alden's lungs were worse, and Coral went back to Pem with thirty crowns and an honest face, and Pem — who was not a cruel man, only a careful one — had already sold. A buyer had come through in the winter, a traveler with soft boots and a soft voice and cash, asking after curiosities, and the compass had gone to him for sixty crowns, and Pem had offered Coral the difference minus his interest, and Coral had taken it because Alden needed medicine that week and pride was a luxury for men with healthy brothers.

Alden died in the spring anyway. That was the joke of it, the black joke Coral carried everywhere and told no one: the compass had been sold for nothing. Forty crowns to buy a year, sixty crowns that arrived too late to matter, and the thing his father had pressed into his hands on his deathbed — *this has kept our family honest for three generations, because a man who carries a true instrument cannot afford to be a false one* — had gone into some stranger's soft-booted pocket and walked out of Coral's life.

Except it hadn't stayed gone. Eight months later, a carter in Lowmark mentioned a man paying for drinks with stories of the coast, a smooth-talking fellow who showed off a brass compass with a faded rose on the face and claimed he'd won it at cards. Coral had gone to the tavern. The man was gone. But he'd left a name, because vain men always leave names: *Vess Marrow.*

And the needle — Coral had borrowed a cheap compass from the carter to test it, held the two side by side, and watched his father's needle strain toward the empty doorway where Vess Marrow had been sitting an hour before — the needle had told him the rest.

* * *

On the eighth day the flats began to end.

It happened so gradually he might have missed it if he hadn't been watching for it. The salt crust thickened, then roughened, then broke into ridges where the wind had piled drift-sand against some long-vanished obstruction. The hexagons widened and flattened. Then came a stretch where the ground was stained faintly brown, and the crystals underfoot gave way to a gritty, salt-hardened clay that held his bootprints for the first time in nine days — held them clearly, sharply, like a confession.

Coral stopped and looked back at his own trail and felt something twist in him that was not quite pleasure. Tracks. Real ones. He crouched and read his own prints the way he'd once read the prints of rustlers and runaways: *a man walking long hours, favoring the left heel, carrying weight. Tired. Determined. Alone.*

Then he looked ahead, and the world changed.

The clay sloped down, gently, so gently he hadn't noticed the descent, toward a horizon that was not the horizon. It was a line of gray-green, and above it hung a haze that was not heat-haze — heat-haze shimmers, this haze *hung*, dense and low, like smoke that had given up on rising. The air came to meet him in slow pulses, and each pulse tasted of rot and kelp and cold stone, and under all of it, so faint he could have imagined it, the sound of water moving where water had no business moving.

The drowned coast. He'd crossed it once, years ago, on a warrant that had nothing to do with any of this, and he remembered thinking it was the saddest country he'd ever seen: a shoreline the sea had abandoned and then, in its moods, reclaimed, harbors silted shut, towns built for a thousand ships housing fifty, and everywhere the salt-eaten ribs of boats that had been beached so long they'd become geography.

The compass at his chest pulled. He felt it before he looked — a faint, insistent tug against the cord, like a hound reaching the end of its lead. Southeast. Straight toward the water.

"Almost home," he said to it, and then hated himself for saying *home*, and then, because there was no one for a hundred miles to hear him be a fool, he let it stand.

* * *

He made camp that night at the last rise above the flats, where a shelf of dark rock broke the clay — the first stone he'd stood on in over a week, and he put his hand flat on it like greeting a horse. From the shelf he could see the coast road, a pale thread running north-south along the base of the cliffs, and where the thread bent toward the sea there was a smudge of roofs and a single tower: Grayharbor, or what was left of it. Lamplights moved in the streets, small and few. A town the size of a held breath.

He built no fire. A fire on the flats was visible for ten miles, and though he'd seen no one since the river country, he'd learned not to mistake empty for safe. Instead he ate cold, wrapped his blanket to his chin, and lay watching the stars wheel over the drowned coast while the needle, propped on his pack beside his head, held its line in the dark like a finger pointing through the night.

Sleep came slow, and when it came, it came with the dream.

He was in the pawnhouse, but the pawnhouse was wrong — the counter stretched away into the dark, and Pem Varrow's face was Alden's face, and Alden was saying, *you should have sold something else, Coral, you should have sold the boat, you should have sold yourself,* and Coral was saying, *it's just a compass, it's brass and glass and a needle,* and Alden was laughing, or coughing, it was impossible to tell which, and saying, *then why are you walking across the end of the world to get it back?*

He woke before dawn with the answer already in his mouth, the one he never said aloud because it was too small and too enormous: *because it's the last thing of ours that ever pointed true.*

* * *

Dawn came up the color of pewter, and with it came the wind, and with the wind came the smell of the sea at full strength — brine and weed and something fouler beneath, the smell of a coast that had been drowning and drying in turns for a hundred years and had stopped caring which. Coral broke camp in twenty minutes, a ranger's economy of motion, and walked the last miles down off the shelf onto the coast road.

The road was hard-packed mud printed with cart ruts and hoof marks and, here and there, boot prints. He walked it with his eyes down out of habit, reading the traffic of the last few days: a heavy cart headed south, two days old. A rider, shod, in a hurry, headed north. And once — his heart went up into his throat and came down slowly — once, a set of boot prints, narrow-heeled, long-strided, the left print landing a half-inch heavier than the right, headed south toward the town.

He knelt and put his hand beside the print and told himself not to be sure. A hundred men had narrow heels. A hundred men favored a leg. But the stride was long, a tall man's stride, a man who walked like he owned the road and expected it to keep up, and Coral had spent nine days imagining that stride, and imagining, it turned out, was its own kind of tracking. He knew this print the way he knew his brother's handwriting.

Vess Marrow had walked into Grayharbor. Recently. Calmly. Without hurrying, which was the part that troubled Coral most, because it meant the man wasn't afraid.

Coral stood in the middle of the road and let the wind push at him and looked at the town. From here he could see the harbor, or the wound where a harbor had been: a brown lagoon behind a broken mole, a forest of masts with no ships attached, and beyond it, past the sandbars, the sea lying flat and gray under the building clouds. The clouds were wrong. He'd been reading weather since he was old enough to be blamed for it, and these clouds were stacked too high and too dark for the season, piled up over the ocean like furniture moved hastily to an attic. The monsoon was weeks out yet. Every almanac said so. But the sky had not read the almanacs, and the air had a weight to it that Coral had felt only twice in his life, both times the day before the world turned upside down.

He touched the compass through his shirt.

The plan was simple, the way plans are simple before they meet the world. Find Vess Marrow. Take back what was taken. He'd rehearsed it on the flats until the rehearsal wore smooth: he'd find the man, and there would be a reckoning, and it would be clean, because Coral was a ranger and rangers did things clean, and then he would walk home with his father's compass in his pocket and set it on the mantel and the debt would be paid and the last four years would close like a ledger.

That was the plan. He held onto it the way a drowning man holds a spar.

Then he started down the road toward Grayharbor, and behind him the salt flats lay white and featureless to the horizon — nine days of nothing, no tracks, no witnesses, no trail that any other soul in the world could follow — and Coral understood that he had just walked off the edge of every map that governed him. No warrant covered this. No school, no instructor, no law of the Ashvane district reached this far. Whatever happened in Grayharbor, he would do it as no ranger at all: as a man with a debt, following a needle that pointed at a thief, into a town that the sea had given up on.

The first fat drops of rain hit the road ahead of him, three weeks early, big as coins, raising dark stars in the dust.

Coral watched them fall and did not turn back.

"Southeast," he told the compass, and walked on into the weather.
