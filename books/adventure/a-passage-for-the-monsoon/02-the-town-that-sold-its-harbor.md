## 2. The Town That Sold Its Harbor

Grayharbor announced itself the way a dying man announces himself: by smell before sight.

Coral caught it a mile out — rotting kelp and tar and old fish left too long in the sun — and then the town rose over the dunes like something the sea had coughed up and couldn't swallow back down. A crescent of gray stone houses huddled behind a breakwater that had been magnificent once, forty years ago, when the Quill fleet still worked these waters. Now the harbor basin sat half-empty at low tide, its mooring posts standing in mud like rotten teeth, and the ships that remained were small ones: fishing skiffs with patched sails, a coastal trader listing at her lines, nothing that needed deep water or wanted it.

He came down off the flats on foot, leading his horse. The animal was called Broomtail for reasons Coral had never asked about; he'd borrowed her from a salt farmer two days east with a promise of return that he was beginning to suspect he'd never keep. She picked her way through the wrack line, snorting at the gulls, while Coral studied the town the way he'd study any terrain before entering it — exits first, then threats, then water.

There were three ways out of Grayharbor by land: the coast road north, which hugged the cliffs and could be watched from a single tower; the salt road east, the way he'd come; and a goat track south through the headlands that looked barely passable. One harbor, half-drowned. A town built like a cup, easy to pour men into and hard to pour them out.

Ranger habits. His father had drilled them into him before Coral had been tall enough to see over a corral fence: *Know the door before you knock.* His father had also taught him to read tracks, to read weather, to read the particular silence a lie makes in a room full of people talking. Coral was going to need all three.

Because Vess Marrow had come through here. Coral was sure of it the way he'd been sure since the salt flats — not from any single sign but from the accumulation of them, the way a dry riverbed accumulates into certainty. Three days ago, at a well station, someone had bought travel rations with southern coin. Yesterday, at a crossroads shrine, someone had left an offering of a silver button cut from a coat cuff — a thief's habit, paying off luck he expected to need. And this morning, ten miles out, Coral had found a campsite under an overturned dory where the fire ash still held the ghost of pipe smoke, sweet and cheap, the same blend he'd smelled on the man who'd slipped out of Dravenmoor's trading post one night in spring with a compass wrapped in oilcloth against his chest.

The needle pointed wrong. That was how Coral thought of it now, though the compass itself was fifty leagues away in a stranger's pocket. For eleven years it had hung at his hip, brass-cased, his grandfather's, and its needle had always swung true to north with a lazy, honest confidence. Since the theft, Coral had felt its absence as a kind of vertigo — every direction equally directionless. He navigated now by dead reckoning and stubbornness, and both said: *Grayharbor.*

* * *

The town's single street ran from the cliff gate down to the customs house, and it was cobbled with stones worn round and slick with weed. Coral walked Broomtail slowly, letting the town look at him, because a town that looks at you is a town you can learn from.

What he learned: Grayharbor was starving politely.

The buildings were sound — good stone, good timber, work done by people who'd expected their grandchildren to live in it — but the paint was peeling and the shutters hung crooked and half the upper windows were dark, their glass gone to cracks or to buyers inland. Men stood in doorways with the particular stillness of the unemployed, hands in pockets, watching strangers with eyes that measured first and welcomed second, if at all. A woman sold smoked fish from a barrel on the corner, and her voice, calling prices, had the flatness of a prayer repeated past all hope of answer.

At the Salt Kettle, the only tavern whose sign still hung straight, Coral paid for a room he didn't need yet and a meal he did, and began doing what rangers do worse than tracking but well enough: talking without seeming to ask.

"Fine deep-water port you've got here," he said to the tavern keeper, a broad woman named Maren with forearms like anchor chain. "Shame to see it idle."

"Shame's got nothing to do with it." She set down his bowl of chowder — thin, mostly potato, honest about it. "Harbor silted after the Quills went under. Dredging costs money. Money went inland."

"The Quill fleet. I heard of them even out west. Big shipping family, wasn't it?"

"Weren't just big." Something moved behind her eyes — grief or anger, hard to tell which, possibly both wearing each other's clothes. "Quills built this town. My grandmother packed cargo for them. Then old Aldous Quill died, and the debts came due all at once, debts nobody knew he'd signed, and inside two years the whole fleet was auctioned off to satisfy creditors nobody could find. Court did it all proper and legal." She spat into the sawdust. "Legal as a knife between the ribs."

"And the magistrate handles your law now?"

"Tolliver." She said the name the way you'd say a diagnosis. "Magistrate Tolliver, yes. You wanting him? Customs house, top of the street. He'll want a fee for the wanting, mind."

"Just passing through. Looking for a traveler — southern man, lean, goes quiet when spoken to, smokes a cheap pipe. Might have come through on the coast road."

Maren's face closed like a shop shutter. "Don't know him."

"You haven't let me finish describing—"

"Don't know him," she repeated, and turned to scrub a pot that was already clean.

Coral ate his chowder and watched the room do the same thing. He tried the question three more times over the next hour — the ferryman nursing a beer at the corner table, the fish-seller's boy delivering a basket, an old man mending net by the fire. Every one of them heard *lean southern man, cheap pipe* and became suddenly fascinated by anything else. The ferryman discovered urgent business with his bootlaces. The boy developed a stammer. The net-mender simply stopped moving altogether, hands frozen in his mesh, until Coral moved on.

It was the most instructive silence Coral had ever walked through. People didn't lie like this about a stranger they feared. They lied like this about a stranger they'd been *paid* to forget — or threatened to forget, which amounted to the same transaction with different currency. Somewhere in this town, Vess Marrow had passed close enough to touch, and someone with authority had made forgetting him a civic duty.

Which meant Vess hadn't merely passed through. He'd been noticed. Noticed and protected, or noticed and hidden — and either way, someone had wanted him moving along quietly toward wherever he was going.

Toward what, at the end of a dying coast, that a thief would want?

* * *

He found the dockhand at dusk, on the mudflat below the ruined quay, prying barnacles off a beached hull to sell as bait.

His name was Pell, or so he said when Coral offered to help turn the hull and earned a grunt of surprise. He was maybe sixty, built like driftwood, with white scar tissue lacing one forearm where a winch cable had taken its tax decades ago. He talked easily about everything — the weather, the price of bait, the scandalous cost of pitch — and Coral matched him topic for topic, patient as tide, until the light went amber and the work was done and Pell sat back on the upturned keel with a flask of something rough and offered Coral a pull.

They drank. The harbor filled behind them, slow and silent, the water climbing the mooring posts inch by inch, reclaiming ground it had lent out at dawn.

"Used to be thirty ships moored along here," Pell said, gesturing with the flask. "When I was a boy you couldn't see the water for masts. Quill colors — blue and white, blue and white, right down the quay." He squinted at the empty mud as if the ships might still be there if he believed hard enough. "Now look at it. Town sold its harbor and doesn't even remember the price."

"Silted up, I heard."

"Silted." Pell laughed, short and bitter. "That's the word they use. Silted. Like it's weather. Like nobody *did* anything." He took another pull. "You're not from the revenue office, are you?"

"Do I look like revenue?"

"You look like a man asking questions about strangers, and around here that's near the same thing." But there was no fear in it — just the flat caution of a man too old and too expendable to bother hiding much. Coral decided to spend some trust.

"I'm looking for a man who came through here recently. Southern. Lean. Smokes cheap pipe. Everyone in town has suddenly forgotten him, which tells me he matters. I'm not revenue, I'm not Tolliver's, and I don't care what he stole or from whom. I care where he went."

Pell studied him for a long moment. Out on the darkening water, a night heron lifted from the mud with a sound like a folded umbrella opening.

"Everyone's forgot him," Pell agreed. "On instruction. Man comes through asking after tide tables and the old charts, asking which inns keep records of guests — that gets reported, see. There's a man at the customs house pays copper for reports on strangers, and Tolliver pays silver for reports on strangers who ask about *specific things*." He tapped his temple. "Charts. Tides. The caves."

Coral kept his breathing level. "Caves."

"Tide caves." Pell pointed south along the coast, where the cliffs marched off into the dusk in long black ranks. "Whole shoreline's honeycombed with them. Old smuggler country, my grandfather called it. Most flood at high tide. One of them — the big one, the one the old families used — the fishermen say the sea pulls back from it further than anywhere else on the coast. Way further than natural. They call it the Monsoon Cave, because the old story says it only opens honest during storm season." He shrugged. "Superstition. But the tides here aren't natural anyway. Haven't been since the Quills fell. Whole coast's gone strange."

"Why would a stranger ask about caves?"

"Why does any man ask about caves?" Pell gave him a look that was almost pitying. "Either he's hiding something in one, or he thinks something's hid in one worth finding. Either way—" he spread his scarred hands, "—he walked the south road three days back, alone, and the last boatman who took coin to row anyone along those cliffs came back and wouldn't talk about what he saw. So."

Three days. Alone. South. Coral felt the trail tighten around his chest like a rope thrown to a drowning man — relief and dread in the same knot. Three days' lead was catchable. But the south road led along cliffs honeycombed with flooded caves, toward a coastline where the tides themselves behaved like liars, and every instinct he owned as a ranger said that country killed careful men and careless ones alike, just careless ones faster.

"One more thing," Pell said, lowering his voice though the mudflat was empty as far as the eye could reach. "You'll see the notices. Read them close. And then decide how much you trust the people who wrote them."

"What notices?"

"Pasted up at the customs house. Been there a month or more." Pell stood, joints cracking like green firewood, and began gathering his bait buckets. "Rewards. For 'unclaimed salvage.' Anything recovered from the caves, the wrecks, the old Quill warehouses — anything of value found along this coast belongs to the magistrate's office until claimed, and claims get processed at Tolliver's discretion, which means never. Bring in salvage, get a reward. Bring in *men* found salvaging without license—" he hoisted the buckets, "—get a cell. Or a rope, if the salvage's valuable enough. They hanged a man for it last spring. Smuggler's daughter watched it happen, they say. Anyway." He didn't finish the sentence. He didn't seem to have an end for it that wasn't a warning.

Coral walked with him as far as the foot of the quay steps. Above them, the customs house windows glowed yellow against the coming dark, and Coral could just make out the pale rectangles of posted notices flanking the door, paper catching the lamplight.

"Thank you," he said. "For the forgetting you didn't do."

Pell paused on the bottom step. In the last light his face was all shadow and scar tissue, and for a moment he looked less like an old dockhand than like the town itself — worn, stubborn, remembering what everyone had been paid to forget.

"My grandfather loaded Quill ships," he said. "I figure somebody ought to remember something true around here." Then he climbed the steps and was gone into the dusk, and Coral stood alone on the mudflat with the tide rising around his boots.

* * *

He read the notices by the customs house lamp, standing in the lee of the doorway while the clerk inside pretended not to watch him through the glass.

There were four of them, printed on good stock — better paper than the town could afford, which told Coral the money came from inland, from whatever office had digested the Quill fleet and was still hungry. Two offered rewards for salvage: *all goods, specie, documents, and articles of value recovered from coastal caves, ruins, and submerged premises within the Grayharbor district shall accrue to the magistrate's office as unclaimed property, finders compensated per schedule.* The schedule was generous. Generous enough to tempt a hungry town into turning informer on itself.

The third notice listed prohibited activities: unauthorized diving, cave entry, wreck survey, chart-making. Penalties escalated from fine to imprisonment to, in cases involving *documents of title or inheritance,* death.

Documents of title or inheritance. Coral read that phrase twice. It was a strange thing to write into a salvage law. Salvage law concerned cargo — chests of coin, bales of silk, cannon off a drowned hull. Nobody wrote *inheritance* into a salvage statute unless somewhere along this coast there was an inheritance loose in the world, and someone intended to make sure it never reached the people it belonged to.

The fourth notice was newer, the ink sharper, the paste still faintly damp at the corners. It offered fifty crowns for information regarding *any person entering or approaching the tidal caverns south of the harbor,* payable at the customs house, no questions asked of the informant.

Fifty crowns. Half a year's wage for a fisherman. Posted specifically against the caves.

Coral stepped back into the street and looked south, where the cliffs ran black against the last band of sunset. Somewhere down that coast, in a cave the sea visited strangely, Vess Marrow was following the same scent Coral was — and had three days' head start, and apparently knew exactly what he was looking for. A compass-thief who asked about tide tables and old charts. A magistrate who paid silver for silence and wrote *inheritance* into his salvage laws. A town that had sold its harbor and been paid in paper.

Every ranger instinct Coral owned said: *this is not your fight. Find the compass, take it back, go home.* The oath was simple. The compass was his family's, pawned in desperation, stolen in transit, and sworn to return. Nothing in that oath mentioned caves, magistrates, dynasties, or drowned coasts.

But the oath also didn't mention the fact that Vess Marrow hadn't stolen the compass to sell it. Any fence in the southern cities would have given him good money for grandfather's compass — it was brass and glass and age, worth perhaps twenty crowns. Instead Vess had carried it north for weeks, spending carefully, asking precise questions, moving like a man with a destination rather than a buyer. Which meant the compass wasn't loot to him. It was a tool. And tools pointed at purposes, and the purpose, whatever it was, lay in the dark below those southern cliffs — in a place where the law had posted a bounty on approach itself.

Coral went back to the Salt Kettle, paid Maren for the room and for a hot breakfast packed in cloth, and spent the evening oiling Broomtail's hooves and checking his gear by candlelight: rope, flint, the long knife, the waxed cloak. Through the shutter he could hear the tide working the breakwater, patient as a debt collector.

In the morning he would ride south along the cliff road, past the notices and the watchers, into country where the sea pulled back from the land like something afraid. He told himself he was still tracking a thief. He told himself the compass was the whole of it.

The candle guttered. Outside, far off toward the headlands, thunder muttered — weeks early, the season turning ahead of its time, the monsoon stirring in the south like a sleeper about to wake.

And beneath the thunder, so faint he might have imagined it, Coral thought he heard the sea drawing breath — pulling back, and back, and back — as if making room for what was coming.
