## 7. First Rain

The first drop hit the back of my neck like a thrown stone.

I was standing at the cave mouth with Sable's spyglass pressed to my eye, watching Tolliver's lanterns crawl along the cliff top like a string of orange beads, and I remember thinking — absurdly — that the sky had no business being that color. Not gray. Gray is honest. This was a bruised green-black, the color of deep water seen from underneath, and it sat on the horizon in a wall so tall it seemed to lean toward us.

The second drop hit the salt flat below and left a crater in the crust the size of a coin.

"Get back from the mouth," Sable said behind me. She didn't shout it. She said it the way you'd say *the floor is about to open*, flat and certain, and she was already moving, hauling her skiff's painter up the sloped stone of the gallery floor, tying it off to an iron ring older than either of us.

I stayed one more second. Long enough to see the wall of rain come off the sea — not approaching, exactly, but *arriving*, the way a door arrives when someone kicks it in. The last thing I saw through the glass before the world went white was a lantern on the cliff top swinging hard, then going dark.

Then the monsoon found us.

* * *

I have ridden out storms in the deep desert, where the rain comes down in ropes and turns wadis into rivers in the space of a prayer. I thought I understood water. I did not understand this.

This was not rain falling. It was the ocean deciding to change address. It came down in sheets so thick they had weight, so continuous there was no gap between one deluge and the next, and the sound — the sound was not weather. It was a crowd. Ten thousand voices hissing at once, filling the cave's throat until I couldn't hear my own heartbeat, couldn't hear Vess shouting something from the upper gallery, couldn't hear anything but the roar.

Lightning turned the cave mouth into a photograph: black rock, white water, the sea beyond already churned to foam. Then dark again. Then light. Each flash showed me the same impossible thing — the tide, which should have been hours from turning, already climbing the rocks below the entrance, climbing fast, fed by surge and storm swell both.

"The water," I shouted, though shouting was useless. "It's coming in early—"

Sable appeared beside me out of the dark, soaked through, her hair plastered flat, and she didn't bother answering. She just took my sleeve and pulled. There was nothing to discuss. We retreated up the gallery as the first tongue of surf licked over the threshold where I'd been standing, spread across the stone, and did not retreat.

That was the detail that lodged in me like a splinter. Waves go in and out. This water came in and *stayed*. Every surge pushed it a handspan further up the gallery floor, and when it withdrew, it withdrew less than it had advanced. The cave was drinking the sea.

We climbed to the upper gallery where Vess Marrow sat with his back against the strongroom door, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man who has been left out of a meeting he considers himself essential to.

"Well," he said, when we'd collapsed onto the dry stone beside him. "This is cozy."

"Shut up," Sable and I said together.

He shut up. For almost a minute.

* * *

We took inventory by candlelight, because inventory is what rangers do when there is nothing else to do, and because counting things makes them feel smaller than they are.

One leaking skiff, twelve feet of patched hull with a cracked thwart and a hull seam weeping a slow bead of seawater even at rest. One sail, rotten at the clews. Two coils of line, one frayed. A bail bucket. Sable's knife, my knife, Vess's knife — three knives between three people who trusted each other exactly none. Half a wheel of hard cheese, a sack of ship's biscuit gone soft, a jug of water Sable had cached against just such an occasion, which told me she'd expected the monsoon eventually, if not this early. A signal lamp with oil for perhaps six hours. And the compass, sitting in my breast pocket, its needle steady and wrong, pointing always toward the drowned channel beneath our feet.

And the ledgers. The whole reason any of us were here — two decades of the Quill fleet's true accounts, wrapped in oilcloth, proving that Magistrate Tolliver's grandfather had stolen a shipping dynasty with forged debts and a bought court. Paper proof. Justice made of ink.

"It'll pass," Vess said. He'd wedged himself into a niche in the rock and was watching the water climb the lower gallery with professional detachment. "Monsoons blow themselves out. Three days, four. We've got water for five if we're careful."

"You've never seen one come ashore here," Sable said. She wasn't looking at him. She was looking down, at the black water rising through her family's tomb, and her voice had a crack in it she was trying to weld shut. "My father said the cave floods top to bottom in a bad year. The whole gallery system connects to the sea through fissures nobody's ever mapped. When the pressure builds—" She stopped. Started again. "There are air pockets. High chambers. You can survive in them. But you can't get *out* of them. Not without a boat. Not through the reef."

"Not without the channel," I said.

Everyone went quiet. The compass needle pointed down through forty feet of flooded rock at the passage only it could find, and none of us said what we were all thinking: that the only way out was also the way only one man among us knew how to navigate, and that man was currently eating our cheese.

"I told you," Vess said, mouth full. "I'm necessary. Try to be gracious about it."

* * *

The water reached the base of the upper gallery sometime in what I judged to be the ninth hour of the storm, though time had gone strange — measured now in surges instead of hours, each one arriving with a boom that came up through the rock into our bones.

By candlelight I checked the skiff, because it had become, without anyone saying so, the most important object in all our lives. I ran my thumb along the seam below the waterline and felt the give in the caulking. Old pitch, dried and crazed. She'd hold in calm water. In surge, in the dark, loaded with three people and a bag of irreplaceable paper?

"She leaks," Sable said, crouching beside me. Not a question.

"She leaks."

"My father's patch kit." Her jaw worked. "Vess."

"He doesn't have it."

"He has it. He went through the skiff the first night while we slept. I found the tin empty this morning." She said it without heat, which was worse than heat. Heat means surprise. This was just arithmetic. "He's keeping his exit options open. He always keeps his exit options open."

I looked up the gallery to where Vess sat silhouetted against the candle, and I thought about the salt flats, and the trail I'd followed for four hundred miles, and the oath I'd sworn over my brother's grave. *Bring it home.* Four hundred miles, and here was the thief, ten feet away, holding the only knowledge that could keep three people alive, and I could no more take the compass from him by force than I could take the tide out of the cave.

"We need that kit," I said.

"I know."

"And he knows we need it."

"I know." She stood, wrung out her sleeves, and walked up the gallery with her spine straight as a mast. I followed, because following her into negotiations had become, somewhere in the last day, a habit I hadn't agreed to.

Vess heard us coming and put his hand casually over the tin at his hip, which was its own confession.

"Patch kit," Sable said.

"Mine now."

"It belongs to my father's boat."

"Your father's boat is at the bottom of your father's cave, girl, along with your father's reputation, and I am the only man in this rock pile who can read a compass needle underwater." He smiled. It was a good smile, built for taverns. "So let's talk terms."

"Terms," I said, "are for after we're alive."

"Terms are *how* we're alive." He patted the tin. "Here's mine. When we run the channel — and we will, because I have no interest in drowning in a Quill family crypt — I keep the kit, I keep the compass, and I row away free. No ranger justice. No smuggler's daughter's justice. Agreed?"

The candle guttered. Below us, the sea boomed against the rock, and the water in the lower gallery rose another inch, patient as a debt collector.

"That's not terms," Sable said quietly. "That's ransom."

"That's *business*," Vess said, "and business is the only honest thing left on this coast."

I looked at him for a long moment. At the man whose bootprints I'd tracked across nothing, whose theft had cost my brother everything, who was now the keystone of every plan we had. And I felt the old thing rise in me — the cold ranger certainty that some men are simply debts to be collected — and I felt something else rising under it, newer and more unwelcome: the understanding that right now, tonight, in this flooding dark, he was the closest thing we had to a way home.

"Sleep," I said. "We'll talk when the water stops rising."

"It isn't going to stop rising," Vess said.

He was right. It didn't.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat at the gallery edge with the candle shielded behind my body and watched the water climb.

It rose in the dark the way dread rises — slowly, then all at once. By the middle watch it had swallowed the strongroom steps. By the next surge it was lapping at the ledgers' hiding place, and Sable was up and moving, hauling the oilcloth bundle higher, cradling it like a child, muttering to it in a language I didn't know. Her father's language, maybe. Or her own.

The cave breathed around us. That was the worst of it — the breathing. Each surge forced air up through fissures in the rock, and the whole gallery exhaled with a long wet groan, and then inhaled, sucking at the candle flames, pulling at loose hair, and in the gaps between breaths I could hear the water working below, grinding stone against stone, patient, enormous, uninterested in our plans or our griefs or our oaths.

Once, in a lightning flash through the distant mouth, I saw the sea itself — a single glimpse, white and vertical, a wave taller than the cliff standing up out of the dark — and then the thunder ate the sight and I sat very still and listened to my heart and decided not to mention it to the others.

Sable came and sat beside me near dawn-that-wasn't-dawn. The candle had burned down to a puck of wax. The water below had stopped twenty feet beneath us, held there by some balance of pressure I didn't understand and didn't trust.

"My father anchored his boat in this gallery," she said. "Every storm season. Forty years. He used to say the cave was the safest harbor on the coast — that the sea couldn't reach what it had already swallowed." She laughed, a small broken sound. "He was hanged for salvage. They said he stole from a wreck. His own family's wrecks, Coral. They hanged him in front of the customs house for taking back what was ours."

I said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't a lie or a platitude, and she hadn't asked for either.

"What did your brother die of?" she asked finally. "You said waiting. Waiting on what?"

"On money." My voice came out rusted. "The compass was the last thing of my father's. I pawned it to pay a surgeon for Tam. The surgeon took the money and the fever took Tam anyway, and the pawnbroker sold the compass before I could scrape the redemption fee together. So I swore—" I stopped. The oath was a private architecture; describing it felt like opening a load-bearing wall. "I swore I'd bring it home. To the house. To the shelf. Put it back where it lived."

"And then what?"

"Then what," I repeated.

"After you put it on the shelf. What's on the other side of the debt?"

I looked at her. In the candle stub light her face was all planes and shadow, and her eyes held the question the way the cave held the sea — completely, without spilling.

"I don't know," I admitted. "I've never seen the other side of it."

She nodded slowly, as if I'd answered correctly anyway. "That's why you track so well," she said. "Nothing behind you pulling. Nothing ahead. Just the trail." She gathered the ledgers into her lap. "I used to be like that. Before the hanging. Before there was something to prove."

Below us, the water shivered. Both of us felt it through the stone — a tremor, then a groan, then a sound like a giant clearing its throat somewhere deep in the rock. The candle flame bent sideways, blown by air pushing *up* out of the fissures, and kept bending, and kept bending, and did not straighten.

"Coral," Sable said.

"I feel it."

The air pressure in the gallery was changing. I'd spent enough years reading wind across open sand to know what it meant when still air started moving with purpose: something big was coming, and it was coming from below. The water in the lower gallery began to spin, slow and deliberate, a whirlpool forming in the dark with a sound like a mill grinding.

From his niche, Vess sat up. "Surge tide," he said, and for the first time since I'd met him, the tavern smile was gone. "Spring tide on top of the storm. It'll fill this place to the roof within the hour."

"To the *roof*?" Sable was on her feet.

"There's an air chamber above the strongroom. Tall enough to stand in, big enough for three, if we're friendly." He was already moving, snatching the cheese, the water jug, the signal lamp. "We swim for it. Now. While there's still air in the galleries between here and there."

"Swim?" My mouth had gone dry. "Through flooded passages? In the dark?"

"In the dark, yes. Through the connecting gallery — it's a straight shot, thirty feet, rises at the far end." He slung the supplies over his shoulder and paused at the gallery edge, looking back at us, and I saw him weigh something — the tin at his hip, the compass in my pocket, the two of us — with the quick merchant's arithmetic he probably applied to everything alive.

"Or," he said, "we stay here, and in an hour we're corpses pressed against the ceiling. Your choice. I'm swimming."

He stepped off the edge into the black water and was gone.

Sable looked at me, the ledgers clutched to her chest, the whirlpool grinding below, the candle dying between us.

"Can you swim?" she asked.

"No," I said. "Can you?"

"Badly." She took a breath that shook only a little. "Together, then."

The candle went out.
