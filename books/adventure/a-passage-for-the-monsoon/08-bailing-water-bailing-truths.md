## 8. Bailing Water, Bailing Truths

The rain found its rhythm somewhere past midnight, and Sable stopped pretending it would stop.

She knelt in the bilge of her father's skiff with a wooden scoop worn smooth by three generations of Quill hands, and she bailed. Scoop, lift, fling. Scoop, lift, fling. The water came back faster than she sent it out, but that had always been the arrangement between the sea and this boat — an argument, not a victory. Her arms burned. She let them burn. Pain was simpler than thinking, and thinking was dangerous tonight.

Behind her, in the cave's black throat, Coral Dravenmoor sat against the wall with his knees drawn up, watching her work. She could feel his eyes the way you feel weather coming. He hadn't slept. Neither had she. The monsoon had come three weeks early and sealed them inside the Monsoon Cave like flies under a turned cup, and the water below was rising with a patience that frightened her more than violence ever had.

"You should rest," he said. His voice carried strangely in the flooded gallery — flattened, close, as if the cave itself were listening.

"I'll rest when the boat floats."

"The boat floats now."

"It floats *low*." She flung another scoopful into the dark. Somewhere it landed on stone with a slap. "There's a difference, ranger. Floating low is a promise the sea makes to you. It keeps promises like Tolliver keeps laws."

He didn't answer right away. When he did, his voice was careful, the way men are careful when they've decided a woman bleeding grief might be handled. "Your father built this boat?"

Sable's scoop stopped mid-lift.

Water dripped from its lip back into the bilge — plink, plink — counting seconds she didn't want counted.

"My father," she said finally, "built nothing. He *kept* things. Kept the ledgers of a fleet he never captained, kept a name that other men spent for him, kept me fed through winters when keeping anything at all was a kind of miracle." She set the scoop down deliberately, because if she kept moving her hands they'd do something her mouth would regret. "And then Magistrate Tolliver kept him too. On the end of a rope, off the customs house beam, where everyone in Grayharbor could watch him swing."

The rain hammered the cave mouth behind them, a white curtain of noise. The lantern between them guttered and steadied.

"They said salvage," Coral said quietly. Not a question. He'd heard it already, then — the word traveled even to rangers in the deep desert, apparently. Or maybe Vess had told him. The thought of Vess Marrow's version of events made her jaw tighten.

"Salvage." She laughed, and hated the sound of it, thin and cracked as old varnish. "That's the charge they use here. You find something in the water — a crate, a spar, a drowned man's boots — and if the magistrate says it wasn't yours to find, it's salvage theft. My father pulled a chest off the wreck of the *Meridian Rose*. A chest, ranger. One chest, washed ashore after a storm, half-buried in sand below the tide line. Any court in any honest port would have called it flotsam and handed him a coin for his trouble."

"And Tolliver called it theft."

"Tolliver called it whatever Tolliver needed it to be." She picked the scoop back up because her hands were shaking and work hid shaking better than stillness did. "The chest held papers. Shipping papers. And my father recognized the seal on them, because Quills had been reading seals for two hundred years. He knew what those papers meant. He knew what they'd prove." Scoop, lift, fling. "So he hid them. And Tolliver's men found him hiding them, and that was the whole trial. Two days. The garrison captain smirked through it. My father stood on the gallows and looked at the crowd — at all those people he'd grown up with, sold fish to, pulled out of riptides — and not one of them met his eyes. Not one."

The scoop came up heavy. She flung harder than she needed to, and spray hit the hull.

"He told me the night before they took him: 'Sable, the paper doesn't rot. Paper waits.' Everyone thought he was talking about the chest. He wasn't. He was talking about what's down in that strongroom." She jerked her chin toward the cave's depths, where the sealed door of the Quill dynasty waited behind forty feet of black water and two hundred years of silence. "Ledgers. Deeds. The original charter with the fleet's true ownership written in ink older than Tolliver's grandfather's grave. Proof that everything the magistrate's family calls theirs was taken — forged debts, false signatures, a whole fleet stolen with a pen instead of a sword. My father died knowing it was down there. That's either a comfort or a cruelty. I haven't decided which."

Coral was quiet for a long time. Long enough that she almost resented it — until she realized he wasn't composing sympathy. He was deciding whether to pay her truth with truth. She'd seen that look before, on gamblers and smugglers and one honest priest who'd lasted four months in Grayharbor before the corruption ate him. It was the look of a man weighing his last coin.

When he spoke, his voice had changed. Lower. Rougher, like a rope dragged over coral.

"I had a brother."

Sable kept bailing, but slower now. Listening with her hands.

"Joren. Younger than me by two years and cleverer than me by ten, though he'd have denied both." Coral's laugh was short and had no floor under it. "Our family keeps a compass. Has for generations — brass and bone, older than any map we own. It's supposed to point true north, but really it points home. Every Dravenmoor carries it eventually. It was mine to inherit, and Joren got sick. Bad sick. The kind of sick that eats a body from the inside and laughs while it does it. There was a physician in Saltmere who claimed he could cut the poison out. For a price that would have bought a small ship."

"The compass."

"The compass." He said it flatly, but Sable heard the ocean underneath — the same undertow she carried. "I pawned it. Signed the paper myself, took the money, rode home through a sandstorm to carry Joren to Saltmere on a cart. Except—" His breath caught, and he mastered it, and Sable felt the mastery cost him. "Except the pawnbroker lied. Sold it the same week to a passing trader, pocketed twice. By the time I traced it, the trail was cold. And the physician wouldn't wait. Said charity was for people with collateral."

Sable set the scoop down again. The bilge water sloshed around her knees, warm as blood.

"How long did he wait?"

"Four months." Coral stared at the lantern flame like it owed him something. "Four months in a cot by our mother's window, asking every week if I'd found it yet. Asking if the money was coming. And every week I'd ride out further, search harder, lie better. *Soon, Joren. Soon.*" His hands curled into fists on his knees. "He died in the spring. Quietly, which was worse somehow. Like he didn't want to inconvenience anyone on his way out. And I swore on his grave that I'd bring the compass home if I had to walk across every mile of this coast barefoot. So when I tracked the thief who has it now — when I followed him across salt flats so empty there was nothing to track across but his sins — everyone told me I was chasing nothing. Maybe I was. But an oath doesn't care about odds. An oath just wants paying."

The rain surged, a fist of wind slamming spray into the cave mouth. The lantern bent sideways and recovered. In the flooded gallery below them, something shifted — the water lapping higher against stone, patient as a creditor.

Sable understood creditors. She'd been raised among them.

"So we're the same, you and I," she said. "Both chasing dead men's debts."

"No." Coral shook his head slowly. "You're chasing justice. I'm chasing property. There's a difference, and yours is worth more."

"Don't flatter me, ranger. Justice is just property that talks." But she smiled despite herself, small and bitter, and she saw him almost smile back — the first movement of his face that wasn't endurance. "Here's what I keep turning over, though. My father hanged for a chest of papers. Your brother died waiting on a piece of brass. And the man holding your brass — Vess Marrow — stole it knowing what it was. Knowing it was somebody's *home*." She spat toward the bilge. "I've hated a lot of men in my life. It's practically a family trade. But I save a special room in my heart for thieves who steal meaning instead of gold."

"Vess said something strange, up in the gallery." Coral's voice went distant, remembering. "Before the storm shut us in. He said the compass isn't just a keepsake. He said it aligns with something — a channel through the reef. That it's a key."

"A key to what?"

"That's what I intend to find out. If he's telling the truth." Coral's eyes moved to the dark water filling the lower gallery, black and glassy and rising. "If he's lying, then we're trapped in a drowning cave with a liar, a leaking skiff, and a magistrate's crew camped above us waiting to blow the roof in."

"Cheerful company you keep."

"I keep excellent company. It keeps finding me."

Despite everything — the ache in her arms, the water at her knees, her father's ghost sitting heavy on her chest like ballast — Sable laughed. Actually laughed, short and startled, and Coral's mouth twitched in response, and for one strange moment the cave felt less like a tomb and more like a shelter. Two strangers bailing the same boat. Two griefs turning together like gears, each tooth catching the other's, making something move that neither grief alone could have moved.

Her father's voice surfaced in her memory, unbidden: *Paper waits, Sable.* And beneath it, unasked, a question she'd never let herself ask: *But what waits for you, girl? What's left when the debt is paid?*

She looked at Coral Dravenmoor — this desert man with salt-crusted boots and a dead brother's weight riding his shoulders, who had crossed an empty country for a piece of brass and found, instead, a stranger bailing water in the dark — and she wondered if he was asking himself the same thing.

The lantern flickered.

Then Sable froze, scoop halfway to the gunwale, her whole body going still as driftwood.

"Coral." Her voice dropped to a whisper. "Look at the lower gallery."

He turned. Below them, where the floodwater had been climbing all night in slow, patient inches, the surface had changed. It was moving — not rising, but *flowing*, sliding toward the cave's inner dark with a soft, sucking hiss, as if something far inside had opened its throat and begun to drink.

And on the black water, faint but unmistakable, bobbed a single lantern flame — drifting out of the depths where no flame should live.

Someone else was in the cave.
