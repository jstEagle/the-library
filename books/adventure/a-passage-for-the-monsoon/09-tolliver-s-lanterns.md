## 9. Tolliver's Lanterns

The rain found its rhythm sometime in the night and never lost it.

Coral woke to it the way a man wakes to a heartbeat — not as sound but as pressure, a drumming on the stone above that came through the rock and into his ribs. The cave breathed around him, exhaling cold air through passages he couldn't see, and somewhere below, water moved where water had no business being. He'd heard it in his sleep: a slow, patient gurgle, like something drinking.

He sat up on the sailcloth Sable had spread for him and listened the way rangers listen — not for what is making noise, but for what has stopped.

The fire was down to coals. Vess Marrow lay curled against the far wall with his back to them, which told Coral nothing except that Vess wanted them to think he slept. Sable was already awake. She stood at the edge of the ledge where their little camp perched above the flooded lower gallery, one hand braced on the wet stone, her head tilted toward the cave mouth.

"You hear it too," she said. Not a question.

"The water."

"Under us." She turned. In the coal-light her face was all angles and shadow, and there was something in her eyes he hadn't seen before — not fear exactly, but the particular stillness of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. "It comes up through the blowholes when the pressure changes. My father called it the cave swallowing. It swallows before every big storm, and then—" she made a small gesture, palm rising, "—it gives back."

"How much does it give back?"

"Depends how angry the sea is."

* * *

They climbed to the mouth of the cave while the light was still deciding whether to exist. It wasn't really climbing — more a scramble along a shelf of slick stone, hands finding holds by memory, because the storm outside had swallowed the dawn whole. The sky over Grayharbor was the color of wet slate, and the rain came down not in drops but in ropes, in sheets, in whole rivers flung sideways off the sea.

But the mouth itself was high enough, and deep enough, that they could stand three abreast in its throat and look out at the world without drowning in it.

That was where Coral saw the lanterns.

At first he took them for stars gone wrong — points of light scattered across the clifftop where no stars should hang so low. Then one of them moved, swinging in a slow arc, and he understood. A camp. Tents or tarps strung against the weather, maybe eight, ten shapes hunched beneath them. And beyond the camp, half-glimpsed through the rain-haze, the dark bulk of something bigger. Wagons. Crates. A tripod of timbers standing over a hole in the earth like a gallows built for the ground itself.

"Count them," Sable said quietly.

"Nine I can be sure of. Maybe twelve." Coral narrowed his eyes, reading the camp the way he'd read tracks across salt flats — weight, spacing, purpose. "Two sentries walking a perimeter. They've got a cookfire going under cover, which means they mean to stay. And those crates—" he pointed to the long boxes stacked near the tripod, "—are the right size and shape for powder."

"Dynamite." Sable said the word flatly, the way you'd name a disease. "Tolliver posted the salvage notice six weeks ago. 'Unclaimed goods within the tide caves of Grayharbor headland to be seized for the public good.' Public good." Her mouth twisted. "He's been waiting for a storm to give him his excuse. Blow the mouth open, haul out whatever the Quills left behind, call it salvage, and no court in the province will ever ask why a magistrate needed dynamite to find paper."

"He doesn't know about the ledgers," Coral said. "Or the vault."

"He knows *something* is worth taking. That's worse." She pulled back from the mouth, out of the gray wash of light, and Coral saw that her knuckles were white where she gripped the stone. "My father used to say Tolliver never gambles. He only collects."

Below them, deep under the floor of the gallery, the water gurgled again — longer this time, hungrier.

* * *

They went back down and told Vess, who received the news sitting cross-legged by the dead fire, turning Coral's compass over in his fingers with the absent familiarity of a man handling something he considered half his own.

"So the magistrate wants to bury the evidence with us inside it," Vess said. "Convenient. Saves him the trouble of hanging anyone." He snapped the compass lid shut. "How long until they come down?"

"They won't climb down in this," Coral said. "The cliff face is a waterfall right now. No rope holds in that; I wouldn't try it, and I've been roped to worse men than Tolliver's hirelings." He crouched, drew lines in the wet grit with one finger: the cliff, the cave mouth, the drowned lower gallery, the reef beyond. "They'll wait for the storm to break. Then they'll set charges at the mouth, bring the whole face down, and walk in over the rubble."

"And how long does a monsoon last?" Vess asked.

Sable answered before Coral could. "Weeks. Normally." She stared at the ceiling, where the rain sounded like a thousand small fists. "This one came early. Nothing about it is normal."

"Then we have days," Vess said. "Maybe less. And between us and the outside world we have a leaking skiff, a flooded passage nobody's ever swum, and a compass that points at ghosts." He smiled his thin, crooked smile. "I've escaped from better situations. But not many."

"There's another problem," Coral said, and hated the weight of it even as he said it. "If they blow the mouth, the cave floods. All of it. Everything below this ledge goes under — including the strongroom, including your ledgers, Sable. Paper doesn't survive that. Whatever proof you have dies with the cave."

The silence that followed had texture. Rain on stone. Water breathing below. Vess's thumb moving slowly along the brass rim of the compass, back and forth, back and forth.

"Then we move them," Sable said finally. "Tonight. Before the water rises any further."

"The strongroom's below the flood line already," Vess said. "You told me yourself the passage in is knee-deep and rising."

"Knee-deep I can manage."

"It won't be knee-deep tonight."

"Then I'll swim if I have to." Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about Sable Quill, Coral was learning — the angrier she got, the quieter she became, until her words came out with edges on them. "Those ledgers are my father's blood written in ink. Tolliver can drown me, but he doesn't get to drown them too."

* * *

They spent the day preparing, because preparation was the only prayer either of them knew how to say.

Coral waterproofed what could be waterproofed — the ledgers wrapped in oilcloth, then sailcloth, then waxed canvas, bound with cord cut from the skiff's spare rigging. Sable knew the cave the way he knew the flats; she led him through side galleries he'd have sworn were solid rock, showing him the old smugglers' caches her father's people had cut into the walls: niches for contraband, iron rings for hoisting, a rusted chain-and-pulley rig that groaned but held when they tested it with a hundredweight of stone.

Vess disappeared for two hours and came back soaked to the collar, reporting on the lower passages in the clipped tone of a man filing a report he hoped no one would act on. "Water's at the third step of the stair. This morning it was the fifth. By dark it'll be the first, and by tomorrow—" he shrugged, wringing out his sleeve, "—there is no stair."

"Then we go now," Coral said.

"Now it's raining like the sky is falling," Vess said. "Now the current through the flooded gallery will pull a strong swimmer under and keep him. Now is when people die, ranger. I've watched it happen. Generally from a safe distance."

"Then stay at a safe distance," Sable said, and started down.

* * *

The strongroom passage was worse than knee-deep by the time they reached it, and it was black.

Not dim — black. The kind of black that pressed on the eyes, that made a man doubt whether they were open. Coral went first with a shuttered lantern hooked to his belt, its beam cutting a narrow cone through water the color of steeped tea. The current tugged at his thighs, steady and insistent, like a child pulling a sleeve. Behind him he could hear Sable breathing — controlled, deliberate breaths, the breathing of someone who had decided fear was a luxury — and behind her, grudgingly, Vess, who had come after all, because of course he had. A thief guards his options.

The strongroom door stood open. It always stood open now; the lock had died a century ago, Sable said, and the dynasty had died with it. Inside, the air was colder, and the water shallower — the room sat higher than the passage, some trick of the old builders, and Coral felt a flicker of respect for the dead Quills who had chosen their ground well.

They worked by lantern light, passing bundles hand to hand up the passage: ledgers, deeds, correspondence tied in ribbon gone brittle as autumn grass. Sable handled each bundle like a sleeping infant. Once she stopped, opened a ledger at random, and held it close to the light, and Coral saw her lips move as she read — her father's name, maybe, in her grandfather's hand, in an entry sixty years old.

"We should go," he said gently.

"I know what we should do." But she closed the book and wrapped it, and her hands shook only a little.

It was on the last trip that the water spoke.

Coral felt it before he heard it — a change in the pull at his legs, the current reversing, strengthening, suddenly purposeful. Then from somewhere deep below came a sound like a great door unbolting, a boom transmitted through the rock rather than the air, and the water in the passage jumped upward, waist-high in a single surge, cold as a grave.

"Move!" he shouted, and the word barely left his mouth before the gallery behind them filled with roaring.

They ran — waded, scrambled, half-swam — bundles held overhead, the lantern dying and relighting as spray hit it. The passage they'd come through was a river now, white-capped in the lantern's failing cone, and Coral got an arm around Sable's waist and drove forward against it, feet finding the old smugglers' footholds by luck and stubbornness. Vess passed them somehow — the man moved through water like an eel through kelp — and had the rope thrown down before Coral reached the ladder, and they came up out of the flood gasping into the upper gallery, four bundles of paper saved, the rest of the world gone to thunder.

For a long moment none of them spoke. The water below rose and rose, filling the lower gallery, erasing the stair, erasing the strongroom door, erasing everything they hadn't carried out.

"That," Vess said at last, shaking water from his hair, "was the cave swallowing."

"No," Sable said. She was staring down into the black rising water, and her voice was very quiet. "That was the cave clearing its throat."

* * *

Night came, though night meant little under the storm. They took turns watching the cave mouth, and it was Coral's watch, deep in the small hours, when the rain eased — not stopped, but eased, the way a fist loosens between blows — and the clouds tore open just enough to show him the cliff top.

The camp had grown.

More tents. More lanterns, doubled now, burning yellow through the wet dark. And new shapes among the old ones: long crates being unloaded from wagons, men working in teams despite the hour, hauling on ropes with the urgent, coordinated rhythm of laborers on a deadline. At the cliff's edge, directly above the cave mouth, torchlight picked out a figure in a coat too fine for the weather — a broad man, hands clasped behind his back, standing motionless at the brink and looking down into the dark as though he could see straight into the cave, straight down the throat of it, straight at them.

Coral knew the posture. He'd seen it on land-grabbers and claim-jumpers, on men who looked at other men's property and saw only the absence of their own name on it.

Magistrate Tolliver had come to watch his dynamite sleep.

Coral counted crates. Fourteen. Fifteen. Enough powder to bring down half the headland. Enough to bury the ledgers, the vault, the channel, the truth — and anyone inside when it went.

He woke the others without ceremony. Sable came to the mouth and stood beside him, and together they watched the lanterns burn, and neither of them said what both of them were thinking, because saying it would make it a plan instead of a dread.

Finally Vess, wrapped in sailcloth at the back of the ledge, broke the silence.

"They'll move the powder down at first calm," he said. "First morning the rain slackens, they'll rope it over the edge and stack it at the mouth. After that, it's a fuse and a match." He turned the compass over once in his hands and looked at Coral across the coal-light. "So the real question, ranger, isn't whether they're coming. It's whether we'll be here when they arrive — or out past the reef, praying that needle of yours knows the way."

Out past the reef. Through the hidden channel. Through black water, in a storm surge, guided by a compass that pointed down.

Coral looked at the lanterns on the cliff, each one a man with a shovel or a fuse, and felt the clock start ticking louder than the rain.

"Then we'd better learn to dive," he said.
