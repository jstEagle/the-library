## 10. The Compass Points Down

Vess Marrow had spent eleven years learning to read rooms, and the flooded lower gallery of the Monsoon Cave was the worst room he'd ever stood in.

The water came to his knees and kept coming — not in waves but in a slow, patient creep, as though the sea had all night and intended to use it. Above them, somewhere up the spiral of galleries, the storm hammered the cliff like a creditor at a door. The lantern Sable had hung on a shelf of rock threw a greasy yellow circle across the black water, and in that circle three faces floated: his own, hollow-eyed; Coral Dravenmoor's, carved out of patience; and Sable Quill's, which watched him the way a cat watches a hole it knows something lives in.

They wanted him to show them.

He'd been putting it off since the rain started. There were things you could take back once they were known, and this was one of them. A trick shared was a trick spent.

"The water's rising," Coral said. Not a threat. Worse — a statement of fact, delivered in that flat ranger's voice that made Vess feel like weather being reported rather than a man being addressed.

"I can see the water's rising."

"Then show us now, while there's still a gallery to stand in."

Vess looked down at the compass in his hand. Eleven days he'd carried it, and in all that time he'd never quite gotten used to its weight. It wasn't heavy. That was the thing people never understood about stolen things — how little they weighed, and how much. His father had taught him lockwork with a pocketwatch balanced on his palm, saying: *a thief who trembles is a thief who hangs.* Vess's hands didn't tremble. They hadn't trembled since he was nine years old and a magistrate's clerk had explained to him, very patiently, what happened to boys who were caught twice.

He crouched at the water's edge and held the compass level.

For a long moment nothing happened. The needle swung lazily through its arc, drunk on the iron in the cave stone, pointing nowhere and everywhere the way it had since he'd lifted it from Dravenmoor's saddlebag outside Saltmere. He felt Sable shift behind him, felt her skepticism like heat off a stove.

Then he turned it flat, parallel to the water, and walked it forward an inch, two inches, three — and the needle shuddered.

It didn't swing. It *shuddered*, the way a horse shudders when it scents a snake, and then it hauled itself around with a will that no needle ought to have, dragging past north, past every honest bearing, until it pointed straight down the flooded gallery into the dark, quivering like a living thing.

"There," Vess said quietly. "There's your channel."

* * *

He'd found the trick by accident, which was the only way Vess ever found anything worth having.

Three nights ago, before the rain, before the others, when the tide had gone out so far it seemed the sea had died of shame — he'd come back into the cave alone with the compass because he couldn't sleep for thinking about Tolliver's reward notices nailed up in Grayharbor. *Unclaimed salvage. Fifty crowns.* Fifty crowns was a new name, a boat, a coast where nobody knew his face. He'd been standing in this same gallery, turning the compass over, wondering if it was even worth the rope it would cost him if Dravenmoor caught up — and the needle had done its shudder and hauled toward the water, and Vess, being Vess, had knelt down and followed it.

The passage mouth was under the ledge on the east side, six feet below the surface at low tide, a black slot in the rock wide enough for a body, maybe barely a skiff. He'd gone in as far as he dared with a lungful of air and a lit candle stub in a glass jar — four lengths, five — and felt the current. Not stillness. *Current.* Cold water moving steadily outward, seaward, the cave breathing out through some hidden throat in the reef. Which meant somewhere ahead, beyond the limit of candlelight and courage, the passage opened. Onto the open sea. Through the reef that sealed this whole drowned coast behind a wall of broken teeth.

Every chart of Grayharbor said there was no way out by water during monsoon season. Every smuggler who'd ever worked this coast had said the same. And every one of them had been wrong, because none of them had a compass that pointed at doors instead of directions.

Vess had sat on the wet rock in the dark and laughed until it hurt, because he finally understood what the old Quill captains had paid a fortune-teller to enchant a compass for. Not navigation. *Escape.* This cave wasn't a treasure vault. It was a lifeboat, built into the bones of the coast, and the key to it fit in a pocket.

And then, this morning, the sky had split open weeks early, and the sea had started climbing, and the door had started closing.

* * *

"It points along the passage," Sable said. She'd crouched beside him without touching the water, arms wrapped around her knees, and her voice had the careful flatness of someone refusing to be impressed. "So does a stick thrown in a current."

"The current runs outward. The needle pointed inward first — toward the mouth — before it settled. You weren't watching." He hadn't expected her to be. He'd expected her to fight him on principle, because that was what Quills did; he'd read enough of the dynasty's history scratched into the strongroom walls to know they'd argued with the sea itself and billed it for damages. "There's a channel through the reef. It's real. I've been inside it."

That got her. Her head came up, and in the lantern light her eyes went from skepticism to hunger so fast it was almost embarrassing to witness. "How far?"

"Far enough that my candle went out and my lungs didn't care for the answer. Forty feet, maybe fifty, of flooded tunnel, then open water on the other side. At low tide." He let that last part sit. Let it do its work.

Coral had moved closer too, and now he catted forward and studied the compass without reaching for it, which Vess noted and appreciated. A man who didn't grab was a man you could deal with. "At low tide," Coral repeated. "What's it now?"

Vess looked at the water lapping at his bootsoles, at the slow climb of it up the gallery wall, marked in old high-water stains like the rings on a tree. "Rising."

"How fast?"

"With the surge behind it? Fast enough."

Sable stood. "Then we go now. Tonight. Before—"

"And dive fifty feet of black water in a storm surge," Vess finished for her, "with a current that'll drag you out through the reef whether your lungs agree or not. Yes. That's the price of the ticket. I did want to mention that part."

The silence that followed had weight. Even the rain seemed to lean in to listen.

He watched them both do the arithmetic. He'd watched a lot of people do arithmetic in bad rooms — debtors, smugglers, once a hangman counting his fee — and he knew all the expressions. Sable's face said she was already halfway through the tunnel, hauling her inheritance out into daylight. Coral's face said nothing at all, which was its own kind of answer: the ranger was measuring the water against his own body and finding the sum ugly.

"Show me the entrance," Coral said at last.

* * *

They went along the gallery with the lantern held low, and Vess led them to the ledge where the rock shelved down into blackness. Up close, the passage mouth was worse than he remembered — a slot of absolute dark, breathing cold, exhaling that faint steady push of outward current that you could feel against your knuckles if you dangled a hand in. The water above it shivered in the lantern light like something alive trying to decide whether to swallow.

"That's it?" Sable's voice came out smaller than she'd meant it to. He heard her hear herself, and hated it for her, because he knew exactly what it was to have your voice betray the size of you.

"That's it."

"It's not wide enough for the skiff."

"No. It's wide enough for a person. Barely." He crouched and dipped the compass again, and the needle hauled toward the slot and quivered there, faithful as a dog. "Fifty feet of that, in the dark, holding your breath, trusting a needle. Then the reef channel, then open sea — in a monsoon."

"You've been in," Coral said. It wasn't a question.

"Four lengths. With a candle." Vess stood and wiped his hands on his coat, though they weren't wet. "I'm telling you the truth because I need you both to understand what you're deciding. I've spent my life selling people comfortable lies, and this isn't one. That tunnel will kill anyone who panics. It will kill anyone who goes in without a line. It might kill us anyway, because the surge is pushing water *in* through the reef, and if the pressure turns that current around mid-passage—" He drew a finger across his throat, gently, almost apologetic.

"So we wait for the tide to turn," Sable said.

"When the tide turns, the gallery floods to the ceiling, and the entrance goes twenty feet deeper, and we're swimming it blind anyway. There's no good version of this. There's only versions where you hold your breath longer."

Coral hadn't spoken. He stood at the edge of the lantern light with the rain-thunder rumbling through the stone overhead, looking at the black slot in the rock the way other men looked at graves. And Vess, who made his living reading faces, realized with a small cold shock that he couldn't read this one at all. The ranger wasn't afraid. Or rather — he was afraid, plainly, visibly, the fear sitting on him like a wet coat — and he was standing there wearing it without any attempt to hide it, and underneath it was something else entirely.

Longing. That was the word. The man looked at that drowning black water the way a starving man looks through a window at bread.

"You want your compass back," Vess said. "That's why you came all this way. So take it." He held it out, flat on his palm, needle swinging toward the dark. "It works. It's yours. Walk into that tunnel tonight and swim out the far side and ride home to whatever brother or mother or ghost you pawned it for, and I'll consider the account between us closed."

He meant it as a trap. He'd admit that later, to himself, in the honest hours. He meant it as a test, because a man who'd dive that tunnel for a compass would dive it for anything, and Vess needed to know what kind of man he was trapped in a cave with. But some part of him — the part that had laughed alone in the dark three nights ago, giddy and half-drowned with the size of what he'd found — some part of him meant it clean, and that part surprised him more than the needle had.

Coral looked at the compass for a long time. Long enough that the lantern guttered and Sable moved to shield it with her body.

"Not yet," the ranger said.

"Why not? It's what you came for."

"Because the tide's still coming in, and because you're the only one of us who knows the way, and because—" Coral stopped. Started again, slower, like a man picking his way across ice. "Because I've spent eight months learning exactly what a thing costs when you take it at the wrong time. Ask me again when the water tells us it's ready."

Vess closed his hand over the compass. Well. He'd offered. Whatever the ranger decided to owe himself was the ranger's business.

* * *

They climbed back up through the galleries in silence, the storm louder now, a vast animal rolling over on top of the world. In the strongroom antechamber Sable built up the fire they'd risked — small, screened, smoke swallowed by some ancient flue the Quill engineers had cut into the rock, because of course the dynasty had thought of everything, right up until the day it all got taken from them — and the three of them sat around it listening to the mountain of water grind against the cliff.

Vess turned the compass over in his fingers and thought about fifty crowns and clean names, and about the look on Coral's face when he'd said *ask me again when the water tells us it's ready*, and about the fact that he had, without planning to, told two strangers the location of the only secret that kept him valuable alive.

Sleep wouldn't come. Sleep never came on the good nights, the nights when the plan was working.

Near midnight — if midnight still existed above the storm — he heard the others' breathing go slow and even, and he rose, and took the lantern, and went back down alone to the flooded gallery.

The water had climbed another foot. The slot in the rock was nearly submerged now, just a darker darkness in the darkness, and the current against his fingers had changed — weaker, confused, the sea arguing with itself about which way to breathe. He held the compass level over the water and watched the needle strain toward the passage, steady, certain, patient as gravity.

*Four days,* he thought. *In four days Tolliver's men stop waiting for the storm and start drilling. In five days there's powder in this rock and none of us are a problem anymore. The water won't wait and neither will they.*

Which meant the tunnel was the only door, and the door was closing, and the key was trembling in his hand.

He crouched there a long time, letting the cold breathe over him, doing sums he didn't like. And that was when he saw it — far out through the cave mouth, beyond the drowned harbor, a light. Not lightning. Too steady. Too low. A lantern moving along the cliff path where no honest man had any reason to walk in this weather, descending, patient, toward the rocks below Tolliver's camp.

Someone was coming to the cave. Tonight. In the teeth of the storm.

Vess snuffed the lantern and stood in the dark with his heart going like the needle, and began, very quietly, to climb.
