## 13. Betrayal at Low Ebb

Vess Marrow had stolen a great many things in his life, and he had a private taxonomy for all of them. There were thefts of hunger — bread, boots, the purse off a drunk who'd never miss it. There were thefts of opportunity — unwatched ledgers, unlocked windows, the five minutes when a harbor watchman went to warm his hands. And there were thefts of necessity, which was what he told himself this one was, though he knew by now that necessity was the word thieves used when they wanted their greed to sound like virtue.

The compass lay on the flat stone where Coral Dravenmoor had left it, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp. The charter copy sat beside it, weighted down with a chunk of coral rock, its ink protected under a sheet of waxed paper Sable Quill had cut from her father's old sail log.

They trusted him. That was the whole of it. They had fed him from their own stores, shared their fire in the flooded gallery's high chamber, let him sleep within arm's reach of both the things that mattered most to them — and he had lain awake listening to Coral breathe slow and even, the breathing of a man who believed the thief beside him had turned honest, and had thought: *you fool, you absolute desert fool.*

He picked up the compass first. It fit his palm the way it always had, since the night he'd lifted it from the ranger's camp outside Saltmere — light, worn smooth at the edges, its brass case dented on one side as if someone had once dropped it and wept over it. He'd sold plenty of compasses. This one he'd kept, because the moment he'd opened it he'd seen the second needle beneath the first, the fine one that swung wrong, that pointed not north but toward the hidden channel through the reef. A key disguised as an instrument. He hadn't known then what it unlocked. He knew now.

And Tolliver would pay for it. Tolliver would pay handsomely, because Tolliver needed it more than anyone — needed the channel sealed or owned, needed the proof of what his grandfather had done to stay buried at the bottom of a drowned cave.

*You're doing it again,* said the voice in his head that sounded uncomfortably like his dead mother. *Naming it necessity so you don't have to name it cowardice.*

Vess shut the voice away and took the charter copy too.

* * *

He should explain something about himself, since no one else would do it fairly. Vess Marrow had been called traitor, liar, rat, snake, and once — by a customs officer in Port Callow with a gift for poetry — "a weasel wearing a man's coat." He didn't dispute any of it. But there was one charge he'd never accepted, and it was the one that stung worst: that he betrayed people.

He didn't betray people. People were difficult. People had faces, and voices, and ways of looking at you that made your hands unsteady. Vess betrayed *situations*. He stole from circumstances, from arrangements, from the general unfairness of the world. If a situation happened to have a person attached to it, well — that was the world's cruelty, not his.

Except that Coral Dravenmoor had shown him the scar on his forearm the second night, turning it to the firelight without self-pity, and said, "My brother sold our father's compass to pay for a surgeon. The surgeon came too late anyway. So the compass bought nothing but time for me to hate myself for not being there." And then Coral had looked at Vess across the flames and said, "I'm not bringing it home for the brass. I'm bringing it home because a promise is the only thing my brother left me that still works."

And Vess had felt something move in his chest, some long-sealed door groaning on rusted hinges, and he had thought — honestly, sincerely, for nearly two full days — *maybe this time.*

Maybe this time he'd help the ranger and the smuggler's daughter, hand over the compass, watch the magistrate fall, and walk out of Grayharbor with nothing but a clear conscience and wet boots. Maybe this time the story ended differently.

Then he'd remembered how every other story had ended, and the memory had the weight of evidence. Twenty-nine years of evidence.

* * *

The skiff was beached in the upper gallery on a slide of old timbers the Quills had built generations ago, back when the cave was a warehouse and the tide was a loading dock. Sable had patched the hull twice already with pitch and sailcloth, and the patch kit — tin box, tarred canvas squares, a pot of resin, the little iron spreader — sat inside the bow beneath the thwart, exactly where she'd said it was, because she'd told him where it was. She'd *told* him. As if trust were a thing you could just hand a man like a cup of water.

He crouched beside the skiff and put his hand on the patch kit and did not pick it up.

Here was the arithmetic, laid out plain, the way he liked all his decisions. Option one: stay loyal. Outcome — Tolliver's men blew the cave or stormed it, or the storm drowned them all, and even if by some miracle they survived, Vess walked away with a clean conscience and empty pockets into a coast crawling with the magistrate's patrols. Conscience, he had learned, was a currency accepted nowhere between here and the capital.

Option two: take the compass and the charter copy to Tolliver tonight, before the sappers came down. The compass proved the channel existed; the charter copy proved the dynasty's claim was real enough to be worth destroying. Together they were worth more than Tolliver's annual salary, and more than that, they were worth his *gratitude* — and a grateful magistrate could give a man a new name, a clean record, passage north on a government ship. Vess Marrow could stop being Vess Marrow. He could be someone with no debts at all.

That was the phrase that did it, every time. *No debts.* He'd spent his whole life owing — owing landlords, owing fences, owing the man in Saltmere whose brother he'd gotten knifed, owing everyone — and the fantasy of a debtless life hit him in the same soft place every time, right behind the sternum.

He picked up the patch kit.

Because here was the part he couldn't say aloud, the part that made him worse than a simple thief: he wasn't taking it out of malice. He was taking it because if he was going to row out through the storm surge to Tolliver's ship, he'd need to patch the leak in whatever boat he found, and Sable's kit was the only one in the cave. And because taking it would slow them down. Because a man running from betrayal needs a head start, and a leaking skiff is as good as a locked door.

See? Necessity. There was the word again, doing its honest little dance.

* * *

He almost left a note.

This was the humiliating truth, discovered only later, in the retelling: Vess Marrow, weasel in a man's coat, sat on the timber slide in the dark with the compass in one pocket and the folded charter against his ribs and the patch kit under his arm, and spent four full minutes composing a note to Coral Dravenmoor in his head.

*I'm sorry. It was never going to end any other way. You're a good man and good men are the easiest marks in the world, because they build their plans on the assumption that everyone else wants the ending they want. Your brother would understand. Maybe that's the cruelest thing I can say, and maybe it's true.*

He got as far as finding a stub of pencil in his coat. He got as far as setting it against the flat of the thwart.

Then he heard Sable's voice from the lower gallery, calling up — "Vess? Brann says the surge's coming in faster than she did last night. We should move the documents up before—" — and the pencil went back in his pocket, and the note died unborn, and Vess Marrow climbed the old rope ladder to the gallery's mouth with everything he intended to sell pressed against his skin like stolen heat.

Behind him, below, the sea breathed in the dark. He could hear it rising. It always sounded patient, the sea. Like it knew everyone's arithmetic eventually came due.

* * *

The exchange point was a shelf of rock halfway up the cliff face on the seaward side, half-drowned at high tide, reachable only by a rope Tolliver's men had fixed three days ago when they first scouted the cave mouth. Vess had found it while pretending to scout escape routes for the others. He'd signaled the cliff-top camp with a shuttered lantern — two flashes, pause, one flash — the old fence's code meaning *seller incoming, goods genuine, name your terms.*

Now he waited on the wet shelf with the storm spitting sideways rain and the sea slamming the rocks below him, and he told himself the shaking in his hands was the cold.

Tolliver came himself. Vess hadn't expected that. The magistrate descended the cliff rope with the gracelessness of a man who paid others to climb for him, and stood on the shelf in a dripping oilskin coat with his lantern raised, and looked at Vess the way a man looks at a tool he's about to use and then throw away.

"You have them," Tolliver said. Not a question.

"I have them." Vess kept his hand away from his pockets. "Compass and the charter copy. The original deeds are still in the vault below — flooded gallery, submerged passage. You'll need divers and the compass to reach them, and you'll need to do it before the spring tide tomorrow night, after which the passage won't surface again for a month."

"Generous of you to volunteer the schedule."

"I'm a generous man."

Tolliver smiled without warmth. Rain ran off the brim of his hat between them like a curtain. "The woman. Quill's daughter. And the ranger. Where are they?"

"Asleep. They'll wake to find me gone and think I ran for the coast road. By the time they work out where I went, you'll have the originals and the powder charges set, and none of it will matter." He heard his own voice, smooth and rehearsed, and hated how easily it came. That was the worst of it — not the stealing, but the fluency. Betrayal fit his mouth like a mother tongue.

"And them?" Tolliver said again, softer. "I asked where they are. I didn't ask what they'll think."

The rain filled the silence.

"They're in the upper gallery," Vess said finally. "Above the flood line. For now."

"For now," Tolliver agreed pleasantly. "Well. The cave has to come down regardless — can't have a charter floating around claiming my family's fleet belongs to a hanged smuggler's line. You understand. Nothing personal."

"Nothing personal," Vess echoed, and discovered that the words tasted different in his mouth than they did in Tolliver's. In Tolliver's mouth they were armor. In his own they were ash.

"The payment—"

"Is aboard my ship," Tolliver said. "You'll come aboard tonight, receive it, and stay aboard until this business concludes. Consider it hospitality."

Consider it custody, Vess thought. He'd known that was the shape of it — no magistrate pays a thief in the open — and he'd decided he could live with it, the way he'd lived with every cage he'd ever climbed into voluntarily because the alternative was poverty. He nodded. "After the charges are set. I want to see the money before your sappers go down."

"Naturally." Tolliver turned to the rope, then paused. "Marrow. The ranger. Dravenmoor. He saved young Brann from the flood, I hear. Pulled him out of the water like a fool."

"He did."

"A fool," Tolliver repeated, musing, and climbed.

Vess stood alone on the shelf in the hammering dark, holding thirty years of his life philosophy in two wet pockets, and found that it weighed nothing at all. That was the trick nobody warned you about. The treasure always weighed less than the leaving.

* * *

He was halfway back along the gallery ledge, lantern hooded, when he saw the light ahead of him.

Not Tolliver's. Smaller, steadier, set low against the stone the way Sable set her lamp when she was reading old paper. His stomach performed a slow, cold revolution.

She was sitting on the timber slide beside the skiff, and the skiff's bow was open, and the patch kit's tin box was gone from beneath the thwart — he'd known it would be, had counted on it, and yet seeing her sitting there with the empty space in front of her made the theft suddenly, stupidly real in a way the taking had never been.

She didn't look up when he stopped. "Where's my patch kit, Vess?"

"Sable—"

"It's a small question. Small questions deserve small answers. Where is it?"

"On me." There was no point lying about the small things. Lies were load-bearing walls; you only spent them on the big ones. "I need it. I'm leaving."

"Leaving." She looked up then, and her eyes in the lamplight were the gray-green of shallow water over rock, and they were not angry yet. Anger would come later; he could see the anger assembling itself somewhere behind her face like weather. Right now there was only a terrible patience. "With what?"

"Sable."

"*With what,* Vess."

He reached into his pocket and set the compass on the timber between them, because some instincts were older than strategy. Then, after a moment, the folded charter copy. He watched her look at them. He watched her understand.

"The compass was never mine to keep," he said. "It was Coral's, and before that it was his brother's coffin money, and I've known since Saltmere that I was always going to sell it to somebody. Better Tolliver than a fence. At least Tolliver will pay what it's worth."

"That's not—" She stopped. Started again, quieter, which was worse. "We showed you the ledger. We showed you the names. My father's name is in that book eleven times, Vess. Eleven shipments he legitimately carried, written down in his own hand, and Tolliver's grandfather crossed them out and wrote *debt* next to each one, and that forgery is why my father died with a rope around his neck on the customs house steps while the whole town watched and said nothing." Her voice didn't rise. It compressed, like water finding a smaller channel. "And you held that page in your hands. You read it. I watched you read it."

"I read it."

"And?"

"And I read it," Vess said, and heard the flimsiness of it even as it left him, and kept talking anyway, because a thief mid-flight cannot afford honesty and he had never learned any other way to run. "Sable, listen to me. Paper doesn't beat magistrates. Charters don't hang judges. You've got a document that says the law is on your side, and the man who owns the law is going to blow the roof in on top of it. I'm not betraying you. I'm just refusing to drown with you. There's a difference."

"Is there?" She rose off the slide, and now the anger arrived, all of it at once, and it was magnificent and awful, and Vess understood in that moment why her father had been a smuggler rather than anything safer — because the Quills had never once in their lives backed down from open water. "Because from where I'm standing it looks like the same old tide. Everyone leaves. Everyone sells. My father used to say the sea keeps no promises and neither does the shore, and I used to argue with him, and now here you are proving him right with a lantern in your hand and my father's justice folded up in your pocket to sell to the man who killed him."

"I never met your father."

"No," she said. "You didn't. That's how you could do it."

The words landed harder than any blow he'd taken in twenty-nine years of hard landings. He actually stepped back. His heel found the edge of the timber slide and the whole structure shifted, and somewhere below them the sea surged against the gallery wall with a sound like a held breath releasing.

"Tell Coral," he said, because he couldn't think of anything else, because the note had died unborn and these were the last words he'd get to choose. "Tell him — tell him the compass points down in the flooded gallery, level, level as a spirit glass, and the channel it finds comes out past the northern reef teeth. Tell him I'm sorry about the patch kit."

"You're sorry about the *patch kit*."

"I'm sorry about everything," Vess snapped, and hated that his voice cracked on it, hated that she'd see it, hated most of all that some traitorous chamber of his chest meant it. "But sorry doesn't float a boat and neither does righteousness, and I have spent my entire life choosing which kind of drowning I can survive. This is mine. Let me have it."

For a long moment she said nothing. The lamp flame bent in the draft from the cave mouth. Then Sable Quill did the one thing he had not prepared for, the one thing no one in his entire thieving career had ever done to him.

She stepped aside. Cleared the path to the ladder. And said, with a coldness that would outlast any fury he'd ever fled from:

"Go, then. Run to your magistrate. Sell your compass. But know this, Vess Marrow — Coral gave you a share. He offered it before you ever asked. A share, and a clean name, freely, to a man who robbed him. And you're trading that for coin from a hanging judge." She picked up the lamp. "So it isn't us you're betraying. It's the one version of yourself anybody ever bothered to offer. Go be him. We'll bury him with the rest."

Vess went. Down the ledge, along the gallery wall, toward the rope and the rain and the waiting lanterns of the cliff-top camp, moving fast, not looking back, telling himself the burn behind his eyes was spray off the surge.

Behind him, faint through the storm, he heard her begin to shout for Coral — and beneath her voice, deeper, the sea coming up the gallery like something that had been promised a debt of its own.

He had the compass. He had the charter. He had the patch kit and a head start and a buyer.

He had everything he'd ever stolen.

It was the first time in his life that having everything had felt exactly like having nothing — and the tide was already rising behind him, and Tolliver's sappers were already lighting their lanterns, and there was no longer any version of this night in which everybody walked away.
