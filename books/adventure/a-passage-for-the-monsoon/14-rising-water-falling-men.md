## 14. Rising Water, Falling Men

The sea came back angry.

Coral felt it before he heard it — a tremor through the cave floor, a pressure in his ears like the whole ocean drawing breath. He had been dozing against the strongroom door with the charter documents wrapped in oilcloth across his knees, and the tremor woke him the way a mother's hand wakes a child: instantly, completely.

"Sable." His voice came out rough. "Sable, wake up."

She was already moving. That was the thing about her he was learning to trust — she never woke slow. She came up off the stone with a knife already in her hand and her eyes already reading the dark, and when she heard the water she didn't waste a question on it.

"Spring tide," she said. "I told Vess. I *told* him the surge would stack on top of it."

"How high?"

"High enough that we stop being people and start being cargo." She was kicking their meager stores toward the skiff as she spoke — the pitch pot, the sailcloth bundle, Brann's borrowed knife. "Get everything above the second ledge. Everything you can carry in one trip."

The main gallery had been a cathedral when they'd first walked it, all soaring arches of wet stone and hanging roots of mineral drip. Now it was becoming a throat. The black water that had filled the lower passage — the drowned gallery where they'd found the vault — was climbing. Coral could hear it lapping at ledges it hadn't touched an hour ago, could see by the dim glow of their single lantern how each wave reached higher than the last, greedy, patient, certain.

He carried what he could and climbed.

* * *

They made camp on the high shelf above the old mooring ledge, wedged between flowstone columns, with the skiff tied below them to an iron ring some Quill sailor had driven into the rock three generations ago. The ring held. The rope groaned but held. Coral lay on his back watching the lantern light shiver on the ceiling and tried not to calculate how much air a cave this size held, and how much of it three people would breathe before the water stopped rising.

Brann sat apart from them, arms around his knees. The rescued sailor had been quiet since dawn — quiet in the way of a man recalculating his whole life. Twice Coral had caught him looking at the flooded lower gallery, at the place where two of Tolliver's men had gone under, and twice he had looked away fast, like a man closing a door on something.

"They were Rennick and Cole," Brann said suddenly, to no one. "The two that went down. Rennick had a wife in Marsh Hollow. Used to complain about her pickling. Cole was nineteen."

Nobody answered him. There wasn't anything to answer with.

It had happened four hours ago, and Coral kept replaying it, because that was what he did — he tracked things, even things he wanted to forget. Tolliver's crew had grown impatient waiting for the storm to break. Six of them had come down the cliff face on ropes in the rain, meaning to enter the cave ahead of schedule, meaning to find whatever the magistrate's gold was paying for before the tide took the option away. They'd come down into a gallery that was already drowning, and the sea, which does not negotiate, had simply closed its hand.

Four had scrambled back up the ropes. Two had not.

Coral had watched it happen from the shelf with Sable's hand hard on his shoulder, holding him back — though he couldn't have said afterward whether she was holding him back or bracing herself. And then he had heard shouting, and seen a man clinging to a submerged stalagmite in the churning dark, and his body had been moving before his mind finished the argument about whether Tolliver's men deserved saving.

His mind still hadn't finished the argument. But his hands had already done the work. He'd gone down the ledge with a coil of mooring line, anchored it to the iron ring, and waded chest-deep into water that hit like fists, and thrown the loop, and hauled. The man — Brann, though Coral hadn't known his name then — had come up coughing half the sea, and Coral had dragged him onto the ledge while the other one, Cole, the young one, screamed somewhere behind them in the roar.

They hadn't found Cole. The water had taken him between one wave and the next, the way the desert takes a footprint.

"You didn't have to do that," Brann said now, still staring at nothing. "You knew whose colors we flew."

"I know what drowning looks like," Coral said. "It looks the same on everybody."

* * *

The rain arrived in earnest near midday — not the coastal drizzle Grayharbor had been suffering for days but the true monsoon, the sky opening like a wound. Even deep in the cave they heard it, a vast white noise pressing down through the rock, and beneath it the deeper sound of the surge: the whole weight of a storm-driven ocean hammering the coast and pouring itself into every crack and throat of the Monsoon Cave.

Sable stood at the shelf's edge for a long time, listening. When she turned back, her face in the lantern light looked older.

"It's going to keep coming until tonight," she said. "Spring tide peaks at moonrise. Storm surge on top of that. This shelf might stay dry. The skiff won't be here in an hour unless we haul it up, and we can't haul it up — no room, no purchase." She wiped rain-spray off her face with the back of her wrist. "So we tie it well and pray to whatever god owns iron rings."

"And if the water takes it?" Brann asked.

"Then we learn to breathe seawater." She said it flatly, without drama, and somehow that was worse than any dramatics would have been.

Vess was gone. That fact sat among them like a fourth person who wouldn't speak. He'd slipped away in the night with the compass and the copy of the charter, down toward the exchange point where Tolliver's silver waited, and none of them had been able to stop him, and Coral had spent the hours since turning it over and over in his mind like a stone with a sharp edge. *You knew what he was,* he told himself. *You tracked him across two hundred miles of salt flat. You knew exactly what he was.*

And yet. And yet there had been that moment in the upper gallery, compass in his scarred hands, showing them the hidden channel through the reef — the moment when the thief had looked less like a thief than like a man who'd finally been handed a reason. Coral had believed in that moment. Believing in it now felt like the real theft.

"He'll sell us to Tolliver," Brann said quietly. "He'll tell them where we are. What we have."

"He'll tell them what gets him paid," Sable said. "That's not the same thing, but it might as well be."

Coral said nothing. He was watching the water.

* * *

It came up faster after noon. Not gradually — in jumps, as if the sea were climbing stairs. A ledge that had been dry at midday was awash within the hour. The sound changed too; the great gallery began to boom and echo like the inside of a drum, waves detonating against walls that had never known surf, and spray drifted up to them on gusts of compressed air, salt-stinging, cold.

They moved everything twice more. Then there was nowhere higher to move it to, and they simply sat with their backs against the flowstone and watched the ocean take possession of the cathedral, arch by arch.

"We should talk about tonight," Sable said eventually. Her voice had to fight the noise. "If the surge gives us a window — if the channel runs deep enough over the reef — we go out on the peak. Not before, not after. Peak."

"In this?" Brann gestured upward, at the whole roaring weight of the storm. "In a skiff?"

"In a skiff with a patched hull, a scrap of sail, and a compass we don't currently own." She smiled without any humor in it. "Yes. In this. Because in this is also the only time Tolliver's boat won't be looking for us, and the only time the reef channel has water enough to float us over the coral teeth. The monsoon giveth."

"The monsoon giveth," Coral agreed. It was the closest thing to a joke either of them had managed in two days, and it landed like a coin dropped down a well.

He was still tasting it when the light changed.

Not the lantern light — the light from the cave mouth, far above and across the flooded gallery, where the storm-grey daylight came slanting down through the entrance arch. Something crossed it. Something that shouldn't have been there.

"Down," Coral breathed, and killed the lantern.

Darkness took them. In it, his eyes did what ranger eyes do — they adjusted, they hunted, they read movement the way other men read print. Across the gallery, at the mouth, ropes were dropping. Three of them, four, whipping down the rock face through the rain, and figures descending them with the clumsy speed of men who are brave because someone else ordered them to be.

"They're coming in," Sable whispered. "Now? In *this*?"

"Tolliver's tired of waiting," Brann said, and his voice had gone strange. "Or he's tired of paying us the courtesy of a head start."

The first man reached the waterline and the water took his boots and he staggered, and Coral saw the flash of a lantern hooded against discovery, saw the glint of a hatchet, saw six shapes picking their way along the last dry margin of stone toward the inner galleries — toward the strongroom, toward the shelf, toward them.

Toward the documents that would hang a magistrate.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and Coral's memory of it would always come back to him in pieces, the way storms come back to coasts.

The sea did not care about Tolliver's schedule. That was the first piece. The second piece was a wave — not a wave really, a surge, a single muscular shove of the whole ocean funneled into the cave mouth — that came through the entrance arch taller than a horse and swept the last dry margin clean. Coral heard screaming. He heard the crack of a body hitting stone. He heard a lantern go spinning out across the black water like a burning star falling sideways, and then the dark swallowed it.

"Rope!" someone was bellowing from the direction of the mouth. "Rope! Rope! *Rope!*"

Two voices. Then one.

Coral was on his feet before he decided anything. Again. His body had apparently settled the moral question permanently, without consulting him. He grabbed the mooring line — the good one, the one that had saved Brann — and Sable was beside him with the second coil before he'd taken three steps, and neither of them said a word about whose men these were, or what these men had come to do to them, or what mercy cost when you paid it to an enemy.

The margin was gone. Where the soldiers had walked, there was now churning waist-deep water and the smooth wet slope of submerged flowstone going down and down. Coral anchored his line to a column, tested it with his full weight, and went in.

The cold hit him like a wall. The current hit him like a second wall behind it. He swam toward the screaming, which was coming from everywhere and nowhere, bouncing off the arches until the whole cave seemed to be crying out. Then a hand broke the surface ten feet ahead of him, clawing, and he threw the loop, and the hand found it, and the weight came on the line like a sinking anchor.

He dug his heels into stone and hauled. Sable was hauling beside him — he hadn't asked her, hadn't looked, but her shoulder was pressed to his and her breath was ragged in his ear and together they walked the man out of the sea hand over hand, foot by foot, up the slick slope, until the water gave him back.

It was the one called Rennick. Coral recognized the face from the cliff-top camp — a heavy, sullen man with a broken nose. The face was different now. It was the face of a man who had been shown the bottom of the world and pulled back from it, and it stared up at Coral with an expression that had no sullenness left in it anywhere.

"Where's Cole?" Rennick rasped. Water poured off him. "Cole was right behind—"

No one answered. Rennick looked at the water, and understood, and turned his face away and was sick, and then just knelt there on the stone shaking while the storm boomed overhead like judgment.

Sable put a blanket around him. Coral watched her do it — watched the daughter of a hanged man wrap a blanket around the shoulders of the magistrate's soldier — and thought: *this is what the law was supposed to look like.* Somewhere it had gotten lost, and here it was anyway, in a drowning cave, made of wool and stubbornness.

"Why," Rennick said finally. His voice was wrecked. "Why'd you pull me? We came to take what's yours. Magistrate's orders. We'd have run you all off this shelf at knifepoint if the sea hadn't—" He stopped. Started again. "Why?"

Coral crouched so they were eye to eye. Up close the man smelled of brine and terror and, underneath it, of something that might have been shame.

"Because your magistrate sent you in here to drown for his profit," Coral said. "And I've buried enough people who died waiting on other men's debts. I'm not burying one who died collecting his."

Rennick stared at him. Behind them, Brann had come down to the waterline, and the two soldiers looked at each other across the space of everything that had happened, and something passed between them that needed no words — the look of men comparing accounts and finding the same sum.

"The magistrate's not waiting for the storm anymore," Rennick said hoarsely. "That's why we came early. He's done being careful. There's a longboat with powder kegs staged at the cliff base under canvas. Tonight, at peak tide — he wants the charges set at high water so the blast carries through the flooded galleries. He wants the cave brought down on whatever's inside." His eyes flicked to Coral, to Sable, to Brann. "All of it. All of you. Then he reports the salvage lost to the storm, collects the insurance writs, and no one ever asks what burned."

The words fell into the booming dark and lay there.

Sable had gone very still. "Tonight," she repeated.

"At peak," Rennick said. "Same window you'd need to run the channel. He knows about the channel — your friend Vess sold him that much already."

Coral felt the name go through the cave like cold iron. Vess. Vess, who had shown them the way out. Vess, who had apparently shown Tolliver the same map, priced by the pound.

Sable turned to him, and in the grey light from the mouth her face was terrible and calm, the calm of a woman who has run out of things that can surprise her. "Peak tide," she said. "One channel. One boat. One hour when everything — the surge, the powder, the patrol ship, all of it — comes due at once."

Outside, the monsoon hammered the coast, and the sea climbed another stair.

Coral looked at the rescued man kneeling in the wreck of his loyalty, and at the water rising to take the rest, and heard himself say: "Then we'd better decide whose side the sea is on."
