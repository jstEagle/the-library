## 15. The Rescued Man's Testimony

Brann Oakes had been a sailor for nineteen years, and in all that time he had learned one rule above all others: the sea does not care who you serve. It drowns magistrates and smugglers with the same indifferent salt. It was Tolliver's men who seemed to have forgotten this, standing on the cliff in their dry coats while Brann hung from a rope over black water, and it was the sea — or rather, the man the sea had nearly taken — who had reached up and pulled him back.

He woke coughing on wet stone, a blanket of sailcloth over him, and the first thing he saw was firelight on a stranger's face. A lean man with desert-browned skin and eyes the color of a storm over flat country, crouched beside him with a tin cup.

"Slow," the man said. "Small sips."

Brann drank. The water tasted of the cave — mineral, cold, faintly of iron. His whole body ached in the way bodies ache when they have been dragged through a surge channel and deposited like driftwood. His left shoulder screamed when he moved it. Rope burn circled his palms in red weals.

"You're the one," he rasped. "In the water. You came back for me."

The man — Dravenmoor, someone had called him, the desert ranger — shrugged as if it were nothing, which was its own kind of answer. Nobody waded into a flooding sea cave for nothing.

"Brann," Brann said, and then, because his mother had raised him honest even if his employer had not, "Brann Oakes, bosun's mate, *Kestrel's Wage*. Tolliver's patrol boat." He said the last part the way a man confesses to a crime.

"I know whose boat you're from. We watched you camp on the cliff for two days."

"Then you know I'm the enemy."

"I know you were hanging off a rope in the dark while the tide came in, and the man holding your rope let it slip." The ranger's voice was flat, but something underneath it was not flat at all. "I want to know if that was an accident."

Brann looked at his hands. The rope burn told the story plainly enough: he had gripped until his palms tore, and the rope had still gone, paid out through Tolliver's foreman's gloved hands with a smoothness that no accident could account for. He remembered the moment of it — the jolt, the drop, the water closing over him like a door.

"It wasn't an accident," he said. "But it wasn't murder either. Not exactly."

The ranger raised an eyebrow.

"Garl Vetch was holding my line. He's Tolliver's cousin and Tolliver's dog, and he's hated me since I told him his knots wouldn't hold a dinghy. When the surge hit, he had a choice — brace, or let go. He let go." Brann's voice cracked, and he hated it. "Maybe he figured the water would do his work for him. Maybe he just didn't care enough to try. Out here, ranger, there's not much difference between those two things."

* * *

They gave him time to warm, to eat — hard biscuit and smoked fish that the woman, Quill, produced from some smuggler's cache in the rock — and then they gathered around the fire to hear him, because they were not fools. They knew why he was here. Three of them in a cave that Tolliver's crew had spent two days mapping from the cliff top, with coils of rope and iron stakes and, Brann had seen with his own eyes, crates of blasting powder stacked under oilcloth behind the foreman's tent.

He told them everything, because gratitude is a debt like any other, and because — he admitted this to himself somewhere in the middle of it, with the firelight on his scarred palms — because he was disgusted down to the marrow of him, and had been for a long time, and had never had anywhere to put it.

"You want to know what the magistrate's salvage writ actually says," Brann said. "I've heard Vetch read it out to the crew twice, smirking the whole time. It says Tolliver is empowered to recover goods of *uncertain ownership* from the Monsoon Cave, and to *render safe* any obstruction to that recovery." He let the words sit. "Render safe. You know what that means when a magistrate says it about a cave?"

The woman — Quill, the smuggler's daughter, with her father's dark eyes and her father's jaw — went very still.

"It means powder," Brann said. "It means they're going to bring the roof down."

* * *

He told it in order, because order was how he kept himself from shaking. Nineteen years at sea taught a man that panic is just a story you tell yourself out of sequence.

The *Kestrel's Wage* had anchored in the lee of the headland the morning the storm's outer edge arrived. Tolliver had come aboard in his good coat, the one with the silver chain of office, and he had stood on the quarterdeck with the rain starting to spit and given them the shape of it in his soft, reasonable voice — that was the thing about Tolliver, Brann thought, he never shouted, he reasoned at you until you felt like the unreasonable one.

"The cave contains the effects of the Quill estate," Tolliver had said. "Effects of uncertain ownership. The crown's interest is in recovering them intact, but the storm complicates matters. If the storm renders them unrecoverable, that is a tragedy, and the crown will record it as such."

He had said *renders them unrecoverable* the way another man might say *if it rains on market day*. And then he had looked at his foreman, Vetch, and said, "You have your instructions," and gone below to drink wine in the dry.

The plan, as Brann had pieced it together from the crew's talk over two days of waiting: Vetch would take six men down the cliff by rope at the next slack water, enter the cave's upper gallery — the one that stayed above the tide even at flood — and set charges at the two choke points where the rock narrowed. The blast would collapse the lower galleries into the sea channel and bury the strongroom under forty feet of rock. No strongroom, no ledgers, no deeds, no charter. Nothing to recover, nothing to dispute.

"And the people inside?" Quill had asked, in a voice like a blade being drawn slowly.

Brann couldn't look at her. He looked at the fire instead.

"Officially, nobody knows anyone's inside," he said. "Unofficially — Vetch told the crew that if anyone asks, three wreckers were trapped in the cave by the storm, and the magistrate's salvage operation was, tragically, unable to reach them in time." He swallowed. "There's a report already written. I saw it. Tolliver keeps his reports written before the events happen — it saves time, he says. It's dated three days from now. *Salvage attempt rendered impossible by collapse. Three bodies of unknown persons presumed lost.*"

The silence in the gallery was the kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the ears like deep water. The rain hammered somewhere far above them, a sound like the whole sky trying to get in. Somewhere in the lower reaches of the cave, water moved and gurgled in the dark, patient as a creditor.

Quill stood up. She didn't say anything for a long moment, and when she did, her voice was quiet in a way that frightened Brann more than shouting would have.

"He hanged my father on a paper charge," she said. "A salvage charge. *Recovering goods of uncertain ownership.* Those were almost the words on the warrant. My father pulled a chest out of a wreck on the open sea — a wreck, in open water, that no crown officer had come within ten miles of — and Tolliver strung him up for it because my father had ledgers too. Ledgers with Tolliver's grandfather's name in them." She turned to Brann. "And now he'll do the same to us, but he won't even bother with a rope and a trial. He'll just write it down first and then make it true."

"That's how he's always done it," Brann said. "The report first. The event after. By the time anything happens, the paper's already old and official and nobody questions it. He's buried half the coast that way. Not with powder — with ink."

* * *

The ranger — Dravenmoor — asked the questions that mattered, in the order that mattered, and Brann found himself answering more fully than he'd meant to, the way a man does when someone finally asks him the right questions and he realizes how long he's been carrying the answers with nowhere to set them down.

How many men did Tolliver have? Fourteen aboard the *Kestrel's Wage*, but half of them were harbor men pressed into service who'd never handled a line in weather, and two of those had already asked to be put ashore. Vetch would take six down the cliff. That left a skeleton crew topside, and a skeleton crew in a storm is a crew that can't chase anything.

When? The next slack water. Brann had heard Vetch argue for tonight — *the tide table says the surge peaks after midnight, we go in at slack, we're out before it turns* — and heard Tolliver refuse, in that soft voice, to risk his cousin in the flooded galleries before the storm's eye passed. But Vetch was impatient. Vetch had been promised a cut, and Vetch did not trust his cousin the magistrate to keep promises longer than convenient. Brann had watched him re-tally the powder crates twice that afternoon, counting fuses, checking the waterproof wrapping, and he had thought: *that man is going to go early. That man is going to go tonight, whatever the magistrate says.*

"You're sure?" Dravenmoor pressed.

"I'm sure Vetch counted his fuses twice," Brann said. "A man counts his fuses twice when he means to use them sooner than he's been told to. And I'm sure of one thing more." He leaned forward, and his shoulder screamed, and he kept leaning anyway. "The charges aren't just for the strongroom. Vetch marked the gallery mouths — the ones above the tide line. The ones where anyone sheltering would shelter. He chalked them this morning when the crew thought he was just surveying." Brann's voice dropped. "He's not burying the salvage and hoping nobody's inside. He's burying whoever's inside and calling it salvage. There's a difference, and the difference is that the first is a crime of convenience and the second is murder with a schedule."

* * *

After that, the fire seemed smaller. The cave seemed smaller. Brann sat with his ruined hands in his lap and watched the three of them — the ranger, the smuggler's daughter, and the thief, Marrow, who had been silent in the shadows by the mooring line the whole time — absorb it in their different ways.

The thief laughed. It was a short, ugly sound with no humor in it. "A report dated three days from now," he said. "You have to admire the craftsmanship. I've forged bills of lading my whole life and I never had the nerve to forge the future."

"It's not clever," Quill said. "It's just what rich men do. They write the ending first and then pay to have the middle match it."

Dravenmoor said nothing for a long time. He sat with his forearms on his knees, looking into the fire, and Brann — who had spent nineteen years reading water and could read a face nearly as well — watched him do arithmetic. Brann could see the columns of it: one leaking skiff, three and now four mouths, a cliff full of enemies, a cave scheduled for demolition, a storm that had sealed the coast and would not unseal it for days. And somewhere in those columns, Brann guessed, a compass, and a debt, and a choice that had nothing to do with any of them and everything to do with all of it.

"You could still go," Brann said quietly. "You and the thief. The lower passage, the flooded gallery — I've heard you talking. The compass finds the channel. You could be out past the reef before midnight and never look back."

"I could," Dravenmoor said. He didn't look up.

"And you're not going to."

The ranger finally raised his head, and his eyes in the firelight were the flattest, calmest things Brann had ever seen on a living man, and underneath the flatness, deep down, something was burning like a lamp in a storm.

"I came here for a compass," he said. "I'm leaving with a debt I didn't expect. It seems the sea's way of doing accounts is different from mine." He turned to Quill. "How much pitch do you have left?"

* * *

They did not sleep, any of them. There was no time in a cave with a schedule for murder, and besides, the rain had changed its voice — Brann heard it even down here, through forty feet of rock, a deeper, steadier drumming, the sound of the storm's heart arriving. The tide would turn with it. The galleries would drink.

Brann did what he could do, which was what he had always done: he worked, and he talked. He talked while Quill and Marrow patched the skiff's seams with sailcloth and pitch, holding the lamp for them, passing the tools, telling them the tide tables he had memorized off the *Kestrel's Wage* chart — the surge would peak near midnight, a spring tide stacked on storm water, higher than anything the cave had swallowed in years. He talked while Dravenmoor studied the compass by lamplight, turning it, tilting it, watching the needle swing toward the drowned passage as though it were pointing at something alive down there in the black.

And once, while the others argued in low voices about fuses and powder stores, Quill sat down beside him on the wet stone and said, "Why did you tell us? You could have kept quiet. Played the prisoner. Waited for Vetch to dig you out."

Brann looked at his palms. The rope burns had begun to scab; they'd hold.

"Because I stood on that quarterdeck," he said, "and I heard a man schedule three deaths in the voice he uses for discussing cargo, and I said nothing. I checked the ropes. I checked the powder crates. I did my job." He flexed his ruined hands. "Then I hung on a rope in the dark and the man beside me let go, and I learned what it's like to be the thing that gets written off in advance. A report dated three days from now." He shook his head slowly. "I've been sailing under Tolliver's colors for two years, and I never once looked at what the colors were made of. Your father —" he stopped. Started again. "Your father was hanged by a man who writes his reports first. I won't be part of the report."

Quill said nothing. But she put her hand, briefly, on his shoulder — the good one — and that was worth more than the blanket and the fish and the fire all together.

* * *

Near midnight, the thief came back from the cave mouth, where he'd gone to listen at the crack of air between the rock and the sea, and his face in the lamplight told them before he opened his mouth.

"Ropes," Vess Marrow said. "I can hear ropes creaking on the cliff. And boots on stone." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man who has been right about the worst thing. "Vetch isn't waiting for slack water. He's coming down now, while the surge is still building — he wants to set his charges and be gone before the peak. Which means," and here his voice dropped to almost nothing, so they had to lean in over the sound of the rain and the sea, "we have maybe two hours before there's powder in this cave. And one skiff. And one channel out through the reef that only a compass can find — and the man who knows how to work the compass is standing on a cliff with a coil of rope, about to come down here and blow us all into the ledger."

Dravenmoor stood. The firelight caught the compass in his hand, its brass casing worn bright at the edges by three generations of Dravenmoor thumbs, and for a moment nobody moved — four people in a drowning cave with a schedule for their deaths, and the tide already turning beneath them.

"Then we'd best get to work," the ranger said. "Brann — you know Vetch's crew. Which of them can be turned?"

Brann Oakes smiled for the first time in two days, and it hurt his face, and he didn't care.

"Half of them," he said. "And I know just where to start."
