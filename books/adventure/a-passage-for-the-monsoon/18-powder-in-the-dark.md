## 18. Powder in the Dark

Vess Marrow had spent his whole life learning how men behaved in the dark, and he had learned this above all: a man carrying powder is a man already half-dead, and half-dead men will believe anything that lets them finish their work and leave.

So when the rope ladder came down through the blowhole at the top of the upper gallery, and the first of Tolliver's sappers dropped into the cave with a shuttered lantern and a coil of fuse, Vess was ready to be exactly what they needed him to be.

He had rehearsed it for two days. He'd rehearsed it while bailing brackish water out of the skiff's seams, while pretending to sleep against the strongroom door, while watching Coral Dravenmoor across the fireless dark with the compass between them like an unhealed wound. He'd rehearsed the voice — a little higher than his own, breathless, frightened, the voice of a man who has been trapped too long and wants only out. He'd rehearsed the lie until it sat in his mouth as comfortably as his own teeth.

The lie was simple. Lies always were. The truth was what took craft.

"Down here!" he called up into the rain-noise, before the second man even touched the stone. "Down here — thank the deeps — I'm one of Tolliver's! Contracted surveyor! I've been mapping the galleries since the tide turned!"

A pause. The lantern swung, throwing a blade of light across the wet rock. Then a voice, rough and bored: "Surveyor? Nobody said nothing about a surveyor."

"Nobody says anything about anything," Vess said, climbing toward the light with his hands visible, palms out, the oldest gesture of harmlessness there is. "That's the magistrate's way, isn't it? He pays me not to be mentioned. Same as he pays you, I'd wager."

The second man dropped down beside the first — big, gray-bearded, with the forearms of a lifetime at the blasting trade. The first was younger, nervous, holding the lantern too high so that its light shook. Vess catalogued them the way he catalogued everything: the old one was Brann's opposite, a man who loved the work itself; the young one was afraid of the water below them, and afraid men were easier to steer than brave ones.

"You're the powder-men," Vess said. "Good. Good. I can show you where the rock's softest. I've been down here three weeks — I know this cave better than the tide does."

The old sapper — Corl, the younger one called him — grunted and unslung his pack. "Magistrate wants the lower gallery brought down tonight. Before the surge peaks. Says whatever's in there comes out as rubble or don't come out at all."

*Whatever's in there.* Vess kept his face arranged into tired relief. In the strongroom behind him, wrapped in oilcloth and strapped beneath the skiff's thwart, lay the original deeds of the Quill fleet and the charter that would hang Tolliver's name from the customs house if it ever saw daylight. Coral and Sable were moving them now — he could hear them, faintly, back in the flooded dark, the soft knock of the skiff's hull against stone, Sable's low voice counting. They thought he didn't know. They thought he was sleeping.

Let them think it. It made everything cleaner.

"This way," Vess said, and led the powder-men deeper into the cave.

* * *

The trick of it — the whole architecture of the thing — rested on a single fact about the Monsoon Cave that Sable Quill had told him on their second night, back when they were still three strangers circling each other like dogs over a bone: the main gallery did not sit under the cliff. It ran *outward*, beneath the harbor shallows, its roof thinning as it went, until above it — forty feet of black storm-chopped water above it — rode His Honor Magistrate Tolliver's patrol boat, the *Swift Retribution*, anchored on the very spot where the chart showed deepest water.

Tolliver had anchored his ship over the cave because deep water meant no diver could reach it. Because deep water meant safety.

Deep water meant he had moored his own gallows directly overhead.

All Vess had to do was walk two fools to the spot beneath their master's keel and let them do the arithmetic of destruction themselves.

"The lower gallery's through here," he said, ducking through the arch where the tide-sculpted stone narrowed. Behind him, boots squelched. The lantern light swam along the walls, waking the wet quartz to a dull glitter. "Mind the third step — it gives."

It gave. The young one yelped. Vess smiled in the dark where no one could see it.

"Three weeks alone down here," Corl said behind him. Not a question. An assessment. Old blasters learned to read rock and men both, and Vess felt the old man reading him. "What'd you eat?"

"Shellfish. Rainwater off the walls." He gestured ahead. "There's a pool in the next chamber — fresh enough. You'll want to fill your flasks before the work starts. Once the charges go, you'll want to be well clear anyway."

"How far's clear?" the young one asked.

"That," Vess said, "depends entirely on where you put the powder."

They came out onto the ledge above the great flooded basin — the drowned gallery, black water stretching away into darkness, the roof descending in wet folds toward its thinnest point far out near the cave mouth. Above, muffled by forty feet of sea, he could almost imagine the *Swift Retribution* riding her anchor chain, lanterns burning at her rail, Tolliver aboard with his brandy and his ledger of stolen things.

Corl stopped at the ledge and held the lantern high. The light reached maybe thirty feet across the water and died.

"Roof's low out there," the old man said.

"Lowest point in the cave," Vess agreed pleasantly. "Softest too. See the staining? Water's been working that seam for a hundred years. One good charge against that roof and the whole gallery floods in minutes — which is what the magistrate wants, isn't it? Seal it. Drown whatever's inside. No salvage, no evidence, no questions."

He watched the lie take root. It was a good lie because it was mostly true — Tolliver *did* want the cave sealed, *did* want everything drowned. The only correction Vess had made to the truth was a matter of a few hundred tons of rock, and whose ship stood above it.

"Sealing it's easy," Corl said slowly. "Charge against the roof out there, the gallery floods, done by morning." He frowned at the black water. "But the magistrate said bring the gallery *down*. Said he wanted it collapsed, not just wet."

"Wet *is* collapsed, eventually," Vess said. "Water does the rest. And besides—" he lowered his voice, leaned close, the confiding gesture of a man sharing professional wisdom— "you've seen the surge coming. When that peak tide hits tonight, the pressure in these lower chambers will be enormous. A charge set against a stressed seam doesn't just crack it. It *runs*. The fracture propagates along the whole fault line. You won't just open the roof — you'll bring down every weak span between here and the sea wall."

The young sapper shifted. "Is that true?"

"It's why they pay me to survey," Vess said, "and pay you to carry."

Corl was quiet a long moment. Somewhere far off, thunder rolled along the coast like furniture being dragged across heaven. The water in the basin shivered.

"Where's your survey marks?" the old man asked finally.

Ah. There it was. Three weeks of solitary confinement bought a man credibility, but old craftsmen wanted to see ink on stone.

"Follow the wall," Vess said, and pointed along the left-hand shore of the basin, out toward the thin roof. "Red pitch. I marked the seam three days ago. You'll see my numbers — depth of overburden, all of it. Forty feet of water above, and the rock no thicker than a man is tall."

He had spent yesterday evening, by candle stub, painting those marks himself. Red pitch, careful numerals, the honest-looking annotations of a fictional survey. He had even gotten the geology right — Sable had taught him which seams were rotten, and the rotten seam did run out toward the thin roof, and the roof *was* thin there, and the water above *was* forty feet deep, and the *Swift Retribution* *was* anchored directly over it.

Every good lie, Vess thought, is just the truth wearing different clothes.

Corl waded to the wall with the lantern held high, and after a moment his rough voice came back satisfied: "Marks are here. Pitch and numeral. Surveyor's hand, right enough." He looked back at Vess, and in the lantern light his eyes were shrewd and tired and not unkind. "You've been paid to drown this place and you're helping us do it. That sit right with you?"

Vess let the pause stretch. Then he said, quietly, "I've been paid by Magistrate Tolliver for three years, old-timer. You learn what sits right and what gets you hanged. Sometimes they're the same thing."

Something moved behind Corl's eyes — recognition, maybe, or its twin. Men who worked powder for corrupt magistrates knew exactly what Vess meant. The old man snorted and turned back to the rock.

"Rig it here, then," he said. "Boy — bring the bundles."

* * *

They worked fast, because the storm was working faster.

Vess carried bundles to the waterline and handed them down while Corl packed them against the stained seam, wedging each clay-wrapped cylinder of blasting powder into the cracks with the tenderness of a midwife. The young one — Tammo, Corl called him — ran the fuse line back along the wall, splicing lengths, checking the burn rate with a practiced thumb. The fuse was slow-match, waterproofed, rated to burn eight minutes. Vess watched every foot of it go down and memorized the route.

Eight minutes. He turned the number over in his mind like a coin he was deciding whether to spend.

Back in the dark behind them, fainter now beneath the drum of rain on the blowhole, he heard the skiff moving. Coral and Sable were loading the last of the documents — he could picture it exactly: Coral's big ranger hands folding oilcloth with a precision that would have surprised anyone who thought rangers were only good at walking, Sable lashing the charter case to the thwart, her jaw set the way it set when she was refusing to cry. They were doing it quietly, without him, because they didn't trust him.

They were right not to trust him. That was the joke of it. He had stolen their compass and their charter copy and their patch kit, and then he had come back, and none of them — least of all Vess himself — fully understood why. Coral had offered him a share and a clean name, and Vess had taken the offer the way a drowning man takes a rope, and now here he was, kneeling in the dark helping two strangers destroy the man who was paying him, and he could not have explained it under torture except to say: *Coral Dravenmoor looked at me like I was worth something, and I found I couldn't stand to prove him wrong.*

"Last bundle," Corl grunted, seating the final charge against the seam. "Fuse to the cap. Stand clear, boy."

Tammo crimped the detonator to the fuse end with cold fingers. The whole assembly lay there against the wet rock: enough powder to crack a cathedral, pressed against the thinnest point of a roof that had forty feet of ocean above it, and forty feet of ocean above *that* held a patrol boat at anchor, and the patrol boat held Magistrate Tolliver, asleep or drinking or counting, directly above the largest hole his own men were about to blow in the world.

"Question," Vess said.

Corl looked up.

"When she goes — the surge is peaking around midnight, yes? Tide tables say the water rises fastest in the hour after. If the roof opens, the sea comes *down* through the breach before it comes *in* through the mouth. Whole basin fills in minutes. Where's your extraction?"

The old sapper wiped his hands on his thighs. "Rope's rigged at the blowhole. We climb before we light. Light it, run the gallery, up the rope, gone before the water's waist-high." He patted the fuse spool. "Eight minutes of match. Plenty."

"Plenty," Vess agreed. "One thing more. The magistrate's orders — he told me himself, three days ago, when he rowed me the supply list — nobody lights anything until the boat signals. He's worried about drift. If the charge goes early, debris fouls his anchor chain." Vess shrugged apologetically. "I'd hate for you to light it and catch his wrath instead of his coin."

Corl's face darkened. "He said signal-first?"

"He said signal-first. Green lantern, twice, from the ship. Then you light and run." Vess spread his hands. "Of course, if you'd rather explain to him why his own orders weren't followed..."

"We'll wait for the signal," Tammo said quickly. Young men feared magistrates more than floods.

"Then we wait," Corl said, and settled onto the ledge with the patience of a man who had waited out worse storms than this. "Might as well sit. Nothing to do now but listen to the rain."

Nothing to do, Vess thought, but everything.

* * *

He left them by degrees, the way smoke leaves a room. First he begged a moment to check his "survey marks" further along the wall. Then he begged a flask-fill at the fresh pool. Then, in the black throat of a side passage where the lantern light couldn't follow, he simply wasn't there anymore, and the two sappers sat alone on their ledge with their powder and their patience, waiting for a green lantern that Vess sincerely hoped would never come — because if Tolliver signaled, it meant Tolliver was awake and watchful, and if Tolliver was watchful, the plan got harder.

He found them at the skiff.

The little boat rode at its improvised mooring in the flooded passage behind the strongroom, riding low, loaded now with oilcloth bundles and the iron-bound charter case and every scrap of paper that mattered. Coral stood in the shallows beside it, soaked to the ribs, one hand on the gunwale. Sable crouched amidships, cinching a lash. Both of them turned at the sound of his footsteps, and both of them went still in that particular way — Sable's hand drifting toward the knife at her belt, Coral's weight settling back onto his heels.

"You're supposed to be asleep," Coral said.

"I'm supposed to be a lot of things." Vess waded closer, hands open again, that same old harmless gesture. "Two powder-men on the ledge above the basin. Charges set against the thin seam. Fuse run and capped. Eight-minute match."

Sable's eyes narrowed. "And you told them to put it there because—"

"Because forty feet straight up, through the roof they're about to open, floats the *Swift Retribution*." He let it land. Watched it land. Coral's face went through disbelief, then arithmetic, then something cold and bright. "Tolliver anchored over the deep water to keep divers away. He moored his own ship on top of the biggest weakness in the cave. His sappers are going to blow a hole in the roof of the sea, and his ship is going to be standing on it when they do."

Silence, except the rain, hammering down through the blowhole like applause from a vast indifferent audience.

"You're telling us," Sable said slowly, "that Tolliver is going to sink his own patrol boat."

"I'm telling you Tolliver is going to sink his own patrol boat, lose his powder crew to the flood or the climbing rope, lose the cave, lose the papers, and have to explain to his garrison why his ship is on the bottom of the harbor with no enemy in sight." Vess allowed himself the smile he'd been saving. "Thieves aren't the only ones who rob themselves, ranger. Give a magistrate enough greed and he'll dig his own cellar under his own feet."

Coral was staring at him. Not with suspicion — with that other thing, that unbearable thing, the look that had ruined Vess's whole career of looking out for number one. "The green lantern," Coral said. "You told them to wait for a signal."

"I told them to wait for a signal that keeps them sitting still while we finish loading and get clear of the blast radius." Vess jerked his chin at the loaded skiff. "Which you're nearly done with. So here's the shape of the night. The surge peaks at midnight. The channel through the reef opens at peak surge — your compass knows the way, ranger, and so do I. We ride the surge out through the reef with the charter dry under the thwart, and somewhere behind us, the magistrate's own powder introduces his ship to the seafloor."

"And the sappers?" Sable asked. "The boy and the old man?"

Vess paused. He hadn't expected that question from her, or rather he had, and had hoped not to. "They've got a rope and eight minutes' head start once the fuse is lit. Whether they use them well is their business. I've stopped being responsible for other men's souls. It's exhausting work."

"No, it isn't," Sable said. "You came back, didn't you?"

He had no answer for that, so he gave her the only currency he had left. "The fuse route runs along the north wall. There's a splice at the second bend — I watched Tammo make it. If someone were to pull that splice apart, the match dies in the damp and the charges never light at all. If someone leaves it, the charges light when the signal comes, and the roof goes, and the ship goes with it." He looked at Coral. "Your oath, ranger. Your call. I've made enough calls for one lifetime."

Coral was quiet for a long time. Out beyond the cave mouth, the storm built itself taller against the coast, and the water in the basin began, very softly, to rise.

"Leave the splice," Coral said at last. "But we light nothing ourselves, and we warn no one, and we owe those two men the same chance anyone gave us — a rope and a head start. If they're smart enough to take it, that's their debt to live under, not ours." He turned to Sable. "Get the papers lashed. Vess — the compass. You know the channel readings as well as I do now. Check them against the surge."

Vess reached into his coat and drew out the Dravenmoor compass — brass-cased, needle trembling, the thing he had stolen and sold and stolen back and could not, in the end, seem to rid himself of. He opened the lid. The needle swung, steadied, and pointed not north but *down and outward*, toward the submerged throat of the reef channel, toward the one road out of a drowning coast.

"Reading's true," he said. "Channel opens at peak surge. Two hours."

Above them, muffled by stone and forty feet of black water, a bell began to ring aboard the *Swift Retribution* — short, sharp strokes, the sound of a ship waking to its own weather. And through it, fainter, from the ledge across the basin, came the young sapper's voice, tight with fear:

"Corl — Corl, the water's coming up faster than the tables said—"

Vess closed the compass lid with a click that sounded, in the dark, exactly like a fuse being lit.
