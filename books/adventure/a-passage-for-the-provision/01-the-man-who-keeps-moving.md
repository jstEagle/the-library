## 1. The Man Who Keeps Moving

The anchor chain came up green.

Not the good kind of green, either — not the bright weed of shallow water that scrubs off with a hose and a curse, but the deep, patient green of things that had decided to live on Dominic Fairweather's salvage and intended to keep living there. He hauled it hand over hand anyway, because the winch on the *Marta B* had died two days ago in a fit of what he could only describe as mechanical spite, and because the client, old Ferro at the harbor office, was watching from the quay with the particular expression of a man who intends to complain about something no matter what you do.

"Chain's fouled," Ferro called down.

"It's fouled," Dominic agreed pleasantly. "That's why it's an anchor."

"I mean it's fouled worse than we discussed."

"We didn't discuss it at all. You pointed at a spot on your chart, said 'something of my grandfather's down there,' and went back to your lunch." The last link broke the surface, trailing ribbons of weed and one extremely indignant crab, which Dominic flicked over the side with a thumb. "Whatever I bring up belongs to whoever's paying me. That's the whole arrangement. It's a beautiful arrangement. It requires no discussion."

Ferro's grandfather's "something" turned out to be a ship's bell, a crate of bottles that had somehow survived forty years underwater without becoming interesting to anyone, and a strongbox that contained, when they finally wrestled it open on the quay, nothing but silt and a single silver spoon. Ferro held the spoon up to the light like a man appraising a crown jewel.

"My grandmother's pattern," he said, voice gone thick.

"There you go, then." Dominic began coiling rope. He was already, in his head, three miles out of the harbor.

"You'll stay for payment tomorrow? There'll be a dinner. My wife makes a fish stew—" Ferro made a gesture suggesting the stew defied description, possibly even physics.

"No dinner."

"A drink, then. The men will want—"

"They don't want anything. They want their shares, which you're paying tonight, and then they want to sleep in their own beds, which is more than I'll be doing." Dominic slung the coil over his shoulder. "It's been a pleasure, Ferro. Your grandfather had excellent taste in bells."

He said it kindly, and meant it, mostly. That was the trick of leaving places: you left them warm. A man who slipped out of town in the night like a thief got remembered as a thief. A man who shook every hand, complimented the stew he wasn't staying for, and left before dawn got remembered as *that Fairweather fellow, decent sort, never could hold still*. It was better branding. It cost nothing but a few compliments, and compliments were the cheapest currency afloat.

His crew — both of them, which was one more than the job strictly needed and one fewer than the boat deserved — were already paid. Tomas had taken his share and his girl and vanished into the upper town with the grin of a man whose evening was fully booked. Pell had taken his share, counted it twice, asked if Dominic was sure about the arithmetic, counted it a third time, and gone to sleep in the fo'c'sle, where he would remain until physically threatened or fed, whichever came first.

Dominic walked the quay alone.

This was the part he liked best, though he'd have denied it under oath. The hour after the work was done and before the leaving began, when the lamps along the harbor threw long yellow smears across black water and the whole port smelled of tar, salt, and someone's late dinner. Other men finished jobs and celebrated. Dominic finished jobs and took inventory — of the boat, of the money, of himself — and the inventory always came out the same way. Boat: sound enough. Money: enough. Self: unexamined, thanks very much, and none of your business.

There was a letter waiting for him at the harbor office.

He knew it before Ferro said anything, because Ferro's face did a thing when he mentioned it — a small, apologetic folding around the eyes, the face of a man handing over bad news he'd promised to deliver and hoped to God wouldn't matter.

"Came through the coastal circuit," Ferro said, producing it from under the ledger with the reluctance of a man pulling a splinter. "Forwarded twice. Took its time finding you."

Everything took its time finding him. That was rather the point of being hard to find.

He knew the envelope before he touched it. Heavy cream stock, the kind of paper that cost more per sheet than Pell made in a day. The Fairweather Foundation's crest embossed in the corner — a trowel crossed with a torch, because his father had never met a symbol he couldn't make pompous. And the handwriting on the front belonged to some foundation secretary, neat and impersonal, addressed to *Mr. D. Fairweather, c/o Harbor Offices (en route)*.

*En route.* As if he were a shipment. As if he were cargo his father's people were trying to locate and take possession of.

"Bad news?" Ferro asked.

"Paperwork," Dominic said, and smiled, and pocketed it unopened.

Here was the thing about letters from the Fairweather Foundation: there were only two kinds. The first kind asked for money, which was funny, since as far as Dominic could tell the foundation existed to spend money the way other institutions spent water. The second kind was worse. The second kind was written in his father's voice — or the ghost of it, filtered through secretaries and stationery and years — and it said things like *I understand you've been working the Serrano coast* and *if you're ever near the university* and once, memorably, *your mother would have wanted*, which was a low blow delivered by proxy, since his mother had been dead eleven years and Marcus Fairweather had never once asked Dominic what she'd actually have wanted while she was alive to say so.

He hadn't opened one in six years. He kept them, though. All of them, in an oilskin packet at the bottom of his sea chest, unread, growing in number the way silt grows on an anchor chain — patiently, weightlessly, until one day the whole thing won't come up.

He told himself he kept them for fire-starting. He had never once burned one.

* * *

The *Marta B* sat low in the water that night, riding heavy with her pay. Dominic sat in the wheelhouse with a chart spread out and a pencil behind his ear, doing the thing he did in every port, which was choosing the next one.

South was easy water and thin pickings; he'd worked the Serrano coast clean. North was the Velloran approach, and nobody ran the Velloran approach this time of year unless they were being paid triple or were actively trying to die, and the monsoon window was closing like a door in a slow-motion film. East there was nothing but islands and rumor. West, home, except home was a word he'd filed under *fictional* somewhere around his twenty-third birthday, between *settling down* and *family business*.

He found himself drawing, without quite deciding to, a line north. Then he caught himself, swore mildly, and erased it.

Pell appeared in the doorway with two mugs of coffee that tasted, as always, like it had been brewed from rope and regret. He was a small, round, methodical man with the emotional range of a tide table, and after nine years together he and Dominic communicated chiefly through grunts and the exchange of objects.

"Letter," Pell said.

"Got it."

"The cream one."

"Noticed."

"You get those a lot." Pell handed over a mug and lowered himself onto the chart bench with the sigh of a man settling in for a long sit, which was how he announced all conversations.

"I get paper a lot. Paper's cheap."

"That paper isn't." Pell blew on his coffee. "Ferro says the foundation's been asking after you in three ports. Not just writing. Asking. Sending men to stand around harbors looking official."

Dominic's pencil stopped moving. "Since when?"

"Since before we got here, I'd guess. Fellow in Caldera described a man with a briefcase asking whether a pilot named Fairweather had come through. Said he looked like he ironed his shirts on his back." Pell shrugged, a compact geological event. "Could be nothing. Could be lawyers. Could be inheritance."

"I'm not inheriting anything. He's not dead."

"He's not young, either." Pell said it gently, which was the most dangerous way to say it. "Man's past sixty, running expeditions into the basin during monsoon season. Even you have to do that arithmetic."

"I don't have to do any arithmetic that isn't mine." Dominic turned back to the chart, which now featured a small angry hole where the pencil had pressed through. "I'm a salvager. I raise bells and spoons. Whatever the great Marcus Fairweather is doing upriver, he's doing it with a hundred thousand crowns of foundation money and thirty people whose job titles contain the word 'associate.' He doesn't need me. He's never needed me. That was the entire lesson of my childhood, delivered daily, tuition-free."

Pell drank his terrible coffee and let the silence do its work, which was Pell's signature move. Silence, deployed by a patient man, was more effective than argument. Dominic had watched him end three bar fights and one marriage proposal with it.

"He taught you the water," Pell said at last.

"The water was already there."

"He taught you to read it, though. Ferro told me. Said you looked at the harbor mouth yesterday and called the sandbar shift before the pilot boat did."

"Ferro talks too much."

"Mm." Pell stood, collected his mug, and paused in the doorway. "For a man who doesn't owe anyone anything, you spend a lot of energy making sure of it."

Then he was gone, down the companionway, leaving Dominic alone with the chart and the letter burning a small rectangular hole through the oilskin in his pocket.

* * *

He didn't sleep, which was normal, and he didn't open the letter, which was also normal, and around three in the morning he did the thing he always did on the last night in a port, which was walk.

The town was asleep in the way port towns sleep — lightly, with one eye open, a dog complaining somewhere, a tavern down the strand still leaking lamplight and laughter onto wet cobbles. Dominic walked the seawall with his hands in his pockets and his collar up against a wind that had teeth in it, the first real breath of the season, and tried to feel free.

Free was the word he used. Free was the word he'd built his whole life on, load-bearing, load-tested. No debts. No anchors. No address that anyone could count on, no name on any lease, no seat saved for him at any table. Other men spent their lives accumulating — houses, wives, obligations, furniture — and Dominic had spent his subtracting, and he'd tell you, if you bought him a drink and asked, that he'd never once regretted it.

He'd tell you that. He'd almost believe it.

But here was the trouble with freedom, which nobody put on the brochures: it was quiet. Terribly, perfectly quiet. When you owed no one, no one waited up. When you stayed nowhere, nowhere missed you. He could vanish from any port on earth and within a month the only evidence he'd ever been there would be a raised bell, a paid crew, and a faint impression in the memory of a harbor master, fading like a footprint in tide-wet sand.

Somewhere south of forty, he'd begun to notice that he could name every sandbar on the Serrano coast and not one person who'd notice if he drowned. He'd noticed it the way you notice a leak below the waterline — a dampness at first, deniable, then a trickle, then the slow cold realization that the sea is coming in and has been for some time.

He stood at the end of the seawall and looked north, where the horizon was a black seam under a bruised sky, and thought about a man with a briefcase asking after him in Caldera. Thought about cream-colored envelopes multiplying in an oilskin packet. Thought, briefly and against his will, about hands — big, ink-stained hands, a memory from before the silence, guiding his small ones over a rock face, saying *read it like a story, Dom, the stone remembers everything, you just have to ask it politely.*

He'd been seven. It was the only lesson of his father's he'd ever kept, and he'd kept it by accident, the way you keep a scar.

"Damn it," Dominic said, to the wind, conversationally.

The wind declined to comment.

* * *

He cast off at half past four, in the dark, in the rain that had just started — fine, needling rain, the vanguard of the season. He woke Pell with a boot to the bunk frame, and Pell rose without complaint, dressed, made rope-coffee, and took the bow line, and between them they took the *Marta B* out of Ferro's harbor on the last of the ebb, past the breakwater lights, past the sandbar Dominic had called before the pilot boat did, out into open gray water where the wind found them properly and the boat leaned into it like a horse scenting home.

Ferro was not on the quay. Nobody was. That was the design. Leave them warm, leave them asleep, leave before dawn, and by the time anyone thought to say goodbye you were a story they'd tell over fish stew — *that Fairweather fellow, decent sort, never could hold still* — and stories don't expect replies.

In the wheelhouse, dry under the glass, Dominic set his course southeast, away from the Velloran approach, away from the monsoon door swinging shut, away from briefcases and cream envelopes and a father who communicated exclusively through stationery. He set the course firmly. Deliberately. With the confidence of a man who had done exactly this in exactly this way perhaps two hundred times before.

Then he took out the letter.

He didn't open it. He told himself he was only checking the forwarding marks — professional habit, wanting to know how his trail read from the outside, how close the hunters were. The postmarks told a little story: sent from the capital six weeks ago, forwarded to Caldera, forwarded again here, each leg slower than the last, the paper traveling patiently through the world's machinery toward a man who kept refusing to arrive.

Six weeks ago. His father's expedition had sailed five weeks ago. He knew that the way he knew tide tables — involuntarily, from the trade papers, from gossip in harbor bars, from Pell. Everyone in the coastal world knew it. *Fairweather's back in the basin,* they said, with the reverence people reserved for weather and royalty. *Big one this time. Foundation's spared nothing.*

Spared nothing. That was the phrase. His father spared nothing — nothing except, Dominic could have added, a single afternoon in thirty years, a single letter answered, a single—

He caught himself doing the arithmetic of grievance, which was the oldest arithmetic he knew, and stopped. Grievance was comfortable. That was the trap of it. You could live your whole life in grievance the way a hermit crab lives in a shell, and it fits, and it protects, and it is not, technically, alive.

The letter sat on the chart table, cream and patient, rain drumming the glass above it.

Pell stuck his head in, dripping. "Course says southeast."

"It does."

"We going southeast?"

"We're going southeast."

Pell looked at the course, then at the letter, then at Dominic, with the expression of a man watching someone steer away from a harbor in perfect weather. "Right," he said, and disappeared.

Dominic steered. The rain thickened. Somewhere behind them, north, the monsoon was gathering over the Velloran basin like a debt coming due, and ahead of them lay three weeks of easy, empty, forgettable water, and the *Marta B* ran steady between the two, and Dominic Fairweather — free man, keeper of no debts, opener of no letters — found that his eyes kept going, again and again, against orders, to the small cream rectangle on the chart table.

At noon, unable to stand it another hour, he picked it up, weighed it in his hand like a stone, and shoved it unopened into the oilskin packet with all the others.

The packet was getting heavy. He noticed that. He noticed it the way you notice a leak.

Three days later, in a port he'd have sworn a week ago he'd never see again, a boy in a foundation cap came down the quay calling his name — and behind the boy, standing at the end of the dock in the rain, holding a leather map case like a weapon, was a woman he hadn't seen in nine years, and the sight of her hit him harder than any telegram ever could.
