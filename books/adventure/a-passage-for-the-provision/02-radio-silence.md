## 2. Radio Silence

The telegram found Dominic Fairweather the way most bad news found him: by outlasting his attempts to outrun it.

He had been in Port Callow's End for eleven days, which was already eight days longer than his personal rule allowed, and he'd spent those eight surplus days telling himself a series of increasingly creative lies. That the *Kestrel's* engine needed a proper overhaul and not the cosmetic one he'd promised the buyer. That the rains would make the coastal road impassable for weeks, so what was the hurry. That the woman who ran the chandlery — Marta, with the laugh like a dropped anchor chain — had said nothing at all when she'd caught him pricing spare propeller shafts, and that her raised eyebrow had meant nothing either.

Marta had known him four years, on and off, in the way of port towns everywhere: long enough to know his face, short enough to never learn where he slept twice running. She was the closest thing to a friend his code permitted, and even she had begun giving him looks.

"You're nesting," she'd told him two days ago, sliding a crate of brass fittings across the counter.

"I'm provisioning."

"You asked me where one buys curtains."

"I asked where one buys *canvas*. For the hatches."

"Canvas with little blue stripes," Marta said. "I've sold sailcloth to pirates, smugglers, and one very confused bishop, and none of them wanted striped canvas, Dominic."

He had left the shop without buying anything, which was how he won arguments, and had spent the evening aboard the *Kestrel* not thinking about striped canvas, or about the fact that he knew which side of the bed Marta slept on because he'd helped her carry the frame up three flights of stairs last spring, or about any of the other small evidences accumulating against him like barnacles on a hull that hadn't been careened in too long.

Then, on the twelfth morning, the telegram boy found him at the fuel dock, wet to the knees from the rain, holding the yellow envelope out at arm's length as though it might bite.

"Fairweather? Dominic Fairweather?"

"No one by that name here," Dominic said, automatically, and then winced, because the boy was reading it off the envelope and Dominic had just signed for a drum of diesel under exactly that name not ten minutes prior.

The boy, to his credit, simply held the envelope higher. "It says urgent."

"They all say urgent." But he took it, because refusing telegrams was a habit that cost more than accepting them, and tore it open with his thumb while the boy stood there dripping and expectant, waiting for a tip that was not coming.

The message was brief. Telegrams always were, but this one had been compressed with the particular ruthlessness of an institution that paid by the word and grieved by the paragraph.

**EXPEDITION VELLORAN MISSED SCHEDULED CHECK-IN 14TH STOP AND 21ST STOP AND 28TH STOP FOUNDATION ADVISES PATIENCE STOP NO ACTION REQUIRED STOP INQUIRE RE BOAT AVAILABILITY SEPT STOP**

Dominic read it three times. The rain came down harder, drumming on the *Kestrel*'s deck, running off the awning in ropes. Down the dock, a gull screamed at another gull about something neither of them would ever back down from.

No action required.

He laughed. It came out wrong — too loud, too sharp — and the telegram boy took a prudent step backward.

*Inquire re boat availability Sept.* They were asking about September. September was six weeks away. September was after the monsoon peaked and crested and began its long sullen retreat, when the rivers would still be running brown and swollen and murderous, when half the channels in the Velloran basin would have moved themselves to new addresses without leaving forwarding information.

September was when you went to recover bodies, if the water gave them back. Sometimes it did. More often it kept them, filed them away in the drowned country under the floodplain, in the dark archive where the river kept everything it took.

Dominic folded the telegram into quarters, then eighths, then a tight angry square that fit inside his fist.

"Boy," he said. "Is there more? A second page?"

"No, sir. That's all of it."

"Of course it is." He dug a coin from his pocket and flipped it over; the boy caught it out of the air with the reflexes of someone whose livelihood depended on such things, and fled.

* * *

The foundation office in Port Callow's End occupied the upper floor of a building that had once been a counting house and now counted nothing but correspondence. Dominic had been there exactly once before, nine years ago, and he remembered the visit the way one remembers a dental extraction: the smell, the light, the sensation of being handled by people who considered themselves reasonable.

He told himself he wasn't going. He told himself this while he walked there through the rain, and he was honest enough — barely — to notice the contradiction, and arrogant enough to proceed anyway.

The clerk who admitted him was young and damp and apologetic. The man waiting in the office beyond was neither. Reginald Ashe had run the Fairweather Foundation's field operations for longer than Dominic had been alive, and he had the look of a man assembled entirely from ledgers: gray, precise, ruled into columns. He sat behind a desk too large for the room and did not rise.

"Dominic." He said the name the way he might read an inventory number. "Thank you for coming promptly."

"I didn't come promptly. I came angrily. It only looks the same in the rain."

"Sit, please."

Dominic stayed standing, which was childish, and he knew it was childish, and he did it anyway, because the alternative was sitting down in his father's foundation and looking like a man with a stake in the outcome. He had no stake. He was a boat pilot. He was here about a charter fee. He needed everyone, including himself, to be extremely clear on that point.

"Three missed check-ins," Dominic said. "Your man Halloway radios every seventh day without fail. I've read the expedition reports. The man radioed on schedule through a fever, through a mutiny scare, through the time the cook fell in the river. Three misses isn't patience, Ashe. It's arithmetic."

"The Velloran basin is notorious for atmospheric interference during the pre-monsoon build," Ashe said. His voice was smooth as silt. "Radio blackouts of two, three weeks are documented. The expedition has supplies for ninety days. Marcus planned for contingencies. He always—"

"He always plans for contingencies, yes." Dominic heard his own voice do something cold. "You've mentioned. In the letters I don't answer."

Ashe's expression didn't change, which was itself a kind of answer. "The foundation's position is that launching a rescue operation into flood-stage waters, with no confirmed distress signal, no last-known-position more precise than eighty river miles, and no pilot willing to take the contract, would constitute an unacceptable expenditure of resources and risk."

"Unacceptable expenditure," Dominic repeated. "There are nineteen people upriver."

"There are nineteen people upriver *presumably*, Mr. Fairweather. We have no confirmation the party is even still at the last anchorage. They may have gone inland. They may be sheltering. They may—" Ashe paused, and chose his next words with the delicacy of a man stepping between floorboards that creak. "They may be perfectly fine, and deeply embarrassed by a rescue party arriving uninvited."

"And they may be dead," Dominic said, "and you'll have spent six weeks finding that out at your leisure."

Silence. Rain on the window. Somewhere below, the harbor bell buoy clanked its idiot rhythm.

"We are not unreasonable," Ashe said finally. "If conditions permit, we will mount a search in September. A thorough one. Properly funded, properly crewed. And in the meantime, we are making discreet inquiries regarding vessel availability, since you are, I'm told, the only pilot who knows the upper Velloran channels well enough to attempt them at all." He laced his fingers. "Which brings me to the delicate matter of why I sent the telegram to *you*, specifically, rather than to the harbormaster's list."

"Because you thought I'd say yes."

"Because your father listed you as next of kin," Ashe said, "and because the foundation is aware of your familiarity with the basin. We hoped you might agree to stand by. On retainer. Nothing arduous. Simply be available, should our assessment change."

"My father listed me as next of kin." Dominic turned the phrase over like a coin he suspected was counterfeit. Nine years of silence, and somewhere in a filing cabinet his name sat in a box marked IN CASE OF EMERGENCY, between the insurance policies and the specimen permits. "How thoughtful of him. Did he fill out the form himself, or did an assistant do it?"

"Mr. Fairweather—"

"What's the retainer?"

Ashe blinked. Whatever he'd prepared for, it wasn't that. "I beg your pardon?"

"The retainer. To stand by. To be available. You want my boat and my knowledge on call for six weeks. Name a figure."

Ashe named a figure. It was insulting. Dominic let the silence stretch until Ashe, visibly recalculating, named a better one. It was still less than the job was worth, but it was enough to cover fuel, caulking, food, and the particular dignity of a man who needed to believe he was doing this for money.

"Half in advance," Dominic said. "Cash. And I'll want copies of every report, every chart annotation, every radio log from the expedition's start. Today."

"That can be arranged." Ashe almost smiled; it looked like a crack in masonry. "Though I should caution you — standing by is all the foundation authorizes. If you take the *Kestrel* upriver before September, you go outside the terms of any agreement, and the foundation will bear no responsibility for—"

"For my funeral expenses. Yes. I read contracts the way you write them, Ashe. Slowly, and looking for the knife." He held out his hand. After a moment, Ashe shook it. His palm was dry and cool, like paper.

"One more thing," Dominic said at the door. "The check-ins. Who received the last one? Before the silence."

Ashe consulted nothing, which meant he knew it by heart. "Halloway reported on the twenty-first. Position logged at the narrows above Sarn's Reach. All hands well. He noted rising water and said they were considering moving camp to higher ground." A pause. "That was the last transmission."

"Considering moving camp," Dominic said. "To higher ground. During the build."

"Yes."

"Then they knew it was coming. Your father — *the expedition* — they weren't caught stupid." He corrected himself fast, but not fast enough, and saw Ashe register the slip with a flicker behind the eyes that was the nearest thing to warmth the man produced. "They had ninety days of supplies and a head start. That's something."

"It is indeed something," Ashe agreed, in the tone of a man agreeing with a child about the existence of Santa Claus, and Dominic left before he could put his fist through the ledger of the man's face.

* * *

Back at the dock, the rain had settled into the steady, businesslike downpour that meant the real monsoon was still out there, gathering itself over the highlands like a debt collector. Dominic sat in the *Kestrel*'s wheelhouse with the door shut and the charts spread across the wheel, and did the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do since the telegram.

The Velloran ran roughly north-northwest out of the basin, and the expedition's last fix put them above Sarn's Reach — call it two hundred and forty miles of river from Port Callow's End, most of it gorge country where the channel braided and shifted with every flood pulse. In fair water, the *Kestrel* could make the run in five days, pushing hard. This was not fair water.

The pre-monsoon build had already raised the river two meters. Every day it climbed further. There was a window — he'd grown up hearing his mother describe it to dinner guests while his father was off being famous somewhere — a window in the first week of the peak flow when the main channel ran deep enough to float a shallow-draft boat over hazards that would ground her in the dry season, but before the floods crested and spread sideways into the drowned forest where boats went to die and stay dead.

Seven days, maybe eight, if the build behaved. After that, the tributaries would come down brown and solid with debris, the gorges would turn into hydraulics, and no amount of local knowledge would buy you passage. The river wouldn't just be dangerous. It would be *closed*.

And in September, when the foundation mounted its thorough, properly funded search, they would be looking for a camp that had been underwater for two months, in channels that no longer existed, following charts drawn for a river that had since redrawn itself.

Waiting didn't mean risk. Waiting meant never.

Dominic sat with that for a long time, listening to the rain.

Here was the thing he kept not saying, even in the privacy of his own skull: he had spent nine years constructing a life with no Marcus-shaped holes in it, and the construction had held beautifully right up until a telegram boy appeared out of the rain holding a yellow envelope. Now he could feel the whole edifice swaying, and underneath the anger — which was load-bearing, the anger, he'd built half the house on it — there was a simpler, stupider feeling that he refused to name because naming it would mean it was true.

His father was missing. Possibly dead. And the foundation's response was to inquire about September.

Somebody had to go now. That was all. It was a job. There was a fee. The retainer covered costs and then some, and if he happened to be the only pilot alive who could take the run this late, well, that was supply and demand, and supply and demand had never made anyone's son.

He got out the harbor directory and started working through the list of pilots licensed for the upper basin. He called on four of them that afternoon, wading through the flooded streets, and got four versions of the same answer delivered with varying degrees of kindness.

Old Ferro, who'd taught Dominic half of what he knew about reading water, actually laughed. "Above Sarn's Reach? In the build?" He wiped his eyes. "Take my boat, boy, it's insured. Take my *life*, it isn't worth much, but it's yours."

By evening Dominic had confirmed what he'd already known: the list of pilots willing to run the upper Velloran this season contained exactly one name, and it was his, and the fact that he was going to do it had been decided somewhere around the moment he'd torn open the envelope, and everything since — the anger at Ashe, the arithmetic, the calls to Ferro — had been ceremony.

He stood on the *Kestrel*'s deck in the dark, rain streaming off the awning, and let himself feel it for exactly as long as it took to light a cigarette, which was his system.

Nineteen people. Nineteen strangers, mostly, though he supposed he knew some of their names from the reports he'd read in the years when he'd still read the reports — Halloway the radio man, the cook who'd fallen in the river, a young assistant whose name escaped him. And one man who was not a stranger at all, whatever Dominic had spent nine years pretending, a man who had taught him to read strata and current and cloud, and who had then walked out of his life with the same unhurried confidence he brought to everything, as if absence were just another expedition and family a base camp you could always return to later, when the season was right.

*Moving to higher ground,* Halloway had radioed. Considering it. Which meant Marcus Fairweather had seen the flood coming, as he saw everything coming except the things that mattered, and had made arrangements, because he always made arrangements, careful meticulous arrangements, for everything except the people waiting at home.

Dominic flicked the cigarette into the water and watched the rain eat the spark.

"Fine," he said aloud, to nobody, to the river, to the general arrangement of the universe. "But I'm billing for every mile."

The universe, having heard this performance before, declined to comment.

He went below, broke out the charts, and began marking the channel changes he'd need to memorize — the new bar below Kettle Bend, the cut through the drowned orchard that hadn't existed three seasons ago, the narrows above Sarn's Reach where the gorge pinched and the water ran green and mean even in the dry months. He worked until midnight, and the work steadied him the way it always did, hands busy, mind occupied, no room left for the unnamed thing.

He was asleep in the wheelhouse chair, charts still pinned under his elbow, when the dream came — the old one, the one he hadn't had in years. In it he was seven years old, standing in a trench in the red earth, and his father's hands closed over his own, guiding the trowel, patient as tide, saying *slowly, slowly, the past doesn't like to be rushed*, and young Dominic had looked up into a face full of light and thought: *this. I will keep this forever.*

He woke before dawn with salt on his face that he decided, firmly, was spray off the harbor.

Outside, the rain had thickened. The real monsoon was close now; you could taste the iron of it on the wind. Two hundred and forty miles upriver, a camp was moving to higher ground or it wasn't, and a window was closing at a rate of inches per hour.

Dominic washed, coiled his lines, checked his fuel, and wrote a note to Marta that said only *Gone upriver. Back in September. Feed the cat.* There was no cat. She'd understand.

Then he cast off the *Kestrel*'s lines, pointed her bow at the mouth of the Velloran, and told himself one final lie, the biggest one, the keystone of the whole arch: that this was business, that the fee was the reason, that a man could spend nine years not thinking about his father and then simply pilot two hundred miles of murder-water toward him and feel nothing at all about it.

The river took the boat and asked no questions. It was the only one who didn't.

At the public docks, where the charter traffic clustered and the touts worked the crowd under leaking umbrellas, a woman stood at the end of the pier studying a set of charts with the particular intensity of someone who had already been told no by everyone else in town — and who, as the *Kestrel* slid past through the rain, looked up sharply, as if she recognized something.

Dominic didn't notice. He was watching the water.
