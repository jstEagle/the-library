## 3. The Stranger at the Dock

The rain in Port Sallow came in two varieties: the kind that soaked you slowly while pretending to be a drizzle, and the kind that arrived like a debt collector. Wren Calloway had been standing in the second variety for the better part of an hour, and she was beginning to take it personally.

"Absolutely not," said the harbormaster, for the third time.

"It's a simple charter," Wren said. "Two weeks upriver. I'll pay in advance."

"And I'll attend your funeral in advance, too, because that's what it'll be." He was a broad man named Tetch with forearms like mooring posts and the cheerful fatalism of someone who had watched the Velloran basin eat better-prepared expeditions than hers. He tapped the barometer on his wall, which had been falling all morning like a stone dropped down a well. "Monsoon's coming early this year. No pilot worth his salt will take you past Sarn's Reach after tomorrow, and no pilot worth less than that is worth the money."

"I don't need a pilot worth anything. I need a boat and someone willing to point it upstream."

"That," said Tetch, "is called suicide, and we have a rate card for it."

Wren pulled her coat tighter and considered her options, of which there were approximately none. She had been in Port Sallow for four days. In those four days she had approached six boat owners, one river captain, a man who claimed to own a barge but who was, on closer inspection, describing a large piece of driftwood, and finally Tetch himself, who at least had the decency to refuse her to her face rather than taking her deposit and disappearing.

Four days of watching the river rise. Four days of doing arithmetic she hated.

Marcus Fairweather's expedition had missed its last check-in nine days ago. The foundation — that great, slow, committee-shaped animal — had voted to wait for the monsoon to pass before mounting any search, which meant waiting until November, which meant waiting until there was nothing left to find but whatever the water decided to leave them. Wren had sat through the meeting via a crackling long-distance line, listening to men use phrases like *due diligence* and *responsible stewardship of resources*, and had understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, that if anyone went up that river soon, it would be her, alone, in something that floated.

She had resigned by telegram. It had taken three words, which she suspected was a personal record for institutional communication.

"You could wait out the season," Tetch offered, not unkindly. "Come back when the water drops. Take a proper crew."

"The water won't drop fast enough."

He looked at her then — really looked, the way harbor people do, reading faces the way they read weather. "Whoever's up there," he said, "you care about them."

Wren opened her mouth to say something deflecting and true-adjacent, and that was when the rain changed its mind and became the first kind again, a fine grey veil over the whole dock, and through the veil she saw him.

She knew him instantly, though she hadn't seen him in eleven years. He was taller than his father, leaner, with the same dark hair going its own way and the same way of standing as though he were already halfway onto whatever was leaving next. He was coiling a rope on the deck of a battered riverboat with the economy of a man who had done ten thousand times, and even from thirty yards Wren could see the family resemblance the way you see a watermark — only when you held the paper up to the light.

Dominic Fairweather.

"Well," she said, mostly to herself.

"What?" said Tetch.

"Nothing." She was already moving. "Thank you for your refusal. It was very thorough."

* * *

Here is what Wren knew about Dominic Fairweather, gathered the way scholars gather everything — secondhand, incomplete, and slightly out of date:

That Marcus's son had walked out of archaeology at nineteen and never looked back. That he worked the rivers now — salvage, cargo, the occasional job whose legality depended on which side of the border you described it from. That he moved from port to port the way some men moved through bad marriages, leaving before the furniture got familiar. And that in nine years he had answered exactly none of his father's letters, which was a fact Wren knew because she had once, in a weaker moment, asked Marcus about it, and had watched a man who could lecture for four hours on Bronze Age trade routes go silent for the length of an entire pot of tea.

She also knew — because she had made it her business to know, in the guilty, compulsive way one rereads old correspondence — that Dominic was the only person on this coast who knew the upper Velloran waterways well enough to run them blind. His father had taught him, back when they were still speaking. The boy had spent summers on those gorges before he'd spent his adulthood avoiding them.

So. There he was, coiling rope, and here she was, walking toward him in the rain with nothing prepared except a speech she'd been drafting since the telegram arrived, and every version of it sounded, in her head, like the opening statement of a trial.

"Dominic Fairweather," she said.

He didn't look up right away. He finished the coil, set it down, hooked it, and then looked up, and she watched recognition move across his face like weather of its own — first blankness, then placement, then something harder settling in behind his eyes.

"No," he said.

"You don't know what I'm going to—"

"You're Wren Calloway. You were my father's research partner until you weren't. And the answer is no, whatever the question is, but especially if the question has anything to do with him." He picked up another length of rope, apparently deciding the conversation was over.

"He's missing."

The rope did not stop moving. But it slowed, just slightly, and Wren, who had spent twenty years reading field notes and excavation reports for the small honest details between the lines, caught it.

"His expedition missed three scheduled check-ins," she said. "Last contact was the twelfth. The foundation voted yesterday to wait until the monsoon passes before organizing a search."

"Then the foundation can keep voting." Coil, hook, done. He reached for the next one. "I'm sure they'll form a subcommittee."

"They'll find a boat skeleton in November. Maybe. If the crocodiles leave anything."

"Then they should've thought of that before—" He stopped. Set the rope down. Looked at her properly for the first time, and up close the resemblance to Marcus was worse, or better; she couldn't decide. Same eyes, storm-grey, the kind that assessed everything and committed to nothing. "Before what? Before he ran off to chase another ruin? Miss Calloway, I haven't spoken to my father in nine years. Whatever he's lost upriver, it isn't mine."

"He's your father."

"He's a man I share a surname with." He said it flatly, without heat, which somehow made it worse. "You want a rescue, call the foundation. Oh — wait. They voted."

Wren took a breath. Rain dripped off the end of her nose. Somewhere down the dock a gull screamed its opinion of everyone involved.

"I'm not calling the foundation," she said. "I resigned from the foundation this morning. By telegram. Three words."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. An ancestor of one. "What were they?"

"'I resign immediately.' Technically that's three words if you count 'immediately,' which I do, because it was doing important work."

Now the smile threatened to actually happen, and he killed it, visibly, like swatting a fly. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I need a boat, and you have one, and you're the only pilot on this coast who knows the upper basin well enough to survive it."

"No."

"You haven't heard the offer."

"There's no offer. There's no number. My father is not a number." He turned back to his ropes with finality. "Find another boat."

"There are no other boats. I've tried. The harbormaster says no one will run the Reach after tomorrow, and the ones who would aren't worth the fare. You know that. You've worked these docks."

"Then it sounds like the river's made its decision, and unlike the foundation, I respect it."

Wren stood in the rain and felt the old frustration rise, the specific frustration of being reasonable at a wall. She had argued with committees, with deans, with Marcus Fairweather himself across a decade of shared fieldwork, and she recognized the type: a person who had decided the conclusion first and built the fortress around it afterward.

"All right," she said. "Not for him, then. For the money."

He glanced up despite himself. "What?"

"Salvage rights. If the expedition's equipment is still intact up there — and some of it will be, Marcus bought good gear, obsessively good gear — there's a recovery fee. Instruments, survey equipment, the photographic plates alone. I'll sign them over to you. That's real money, Fairweather. Enough that you could stop working docks in the rain."

"I like working docks in the rain."

"Nobody likes working docks in the rain."

"People like things," he said, with the serene confidence of a liar, "if they choose them."

It was such an obviously rehearsed philosophy, such a load-bearing sentence in the architecture of his whole life, that Wren nearly laughed. Instead she reached into her satchel — waterproofed, waxed canvas, the one indulgence she'd allowed herself — and drew out a folded packet, oilcloth-wrapped, tied with cord.

"Maps," she said. "The upper Velloran. Survey charts from our first season together, annotated in his hand. Soundings, seasonal flood levels, the safe anchorages, the ones that aren't. There are channels up there that exist for six weeks a year and kill people who meet them the other forty-six. Your father mapped them. I helped. You can pretend you don't care about him, but you can't pretend you don't care about the water, and nobody else alive has charts like these."

He didn't reach for them. But he didn't look away from them either, and Wren, who had learned patience in trenches and library basements alike, simply held the packet out and let the rain fall between them.

"You testified against him," Dominic said quietly.

And there it was. She had known it was coming — had counted on it, in fact, because she'd built her approach around it, the way you build a road around a rockslide. Still, hearing it laid out so plainly, in his voice, with his father's cadence underneath it, landed somewhere below her ribs.

"Yes," she said.

"The provenance papers. The Kestrel Collection. You stood up in front of the review board and told them he forged the documentation."

"I told them what I believed at the time."

"Did you?" His eyes came up, sharp now, all the dockside laziness burned off. "Because that's the part I've never understood. Everyone else hedged. Everyone else said *allegedly*, said *the evidence suggests*. You were the one person who said *he did it*. You were his partner. Eleven years, wasn't it? You knew his work better than anyone alive. And you stood up and ended him."

The rain filled the silence, politely, like a host.

"Yes," Wren said again. "I did."

"Why?"

It was the question she had dreaded for eleven years, and she had answers ready — the true ones, the complicated ones, the ones about pressure and evidence and a young researcher's terror of being wrong — but she heard herself say instead:

"Because I was asked to."

He frowned. "Asked by whom?"

"By people whose asking I shouldn't have permitted." She heard how it sounded — evasive, lawyerly, the sound of a woman building a wall of her own — and watched him hear it too. His expression closed like a door.

"So you were pressured into ruining him," he said, "and now you want me to take you upriver to save him. Forgive me if the math doesn't work."

"I'm not asking for forgiveness."

"Good. Because I'm not offering it."

"Fine."

"Fine."

They stood there, two people glaring at each other in a downpour, and Wren realized distantly that they must look, to anyone watching, like a married couple arguing about directions. A gull landed on the pilings nearby, observed them with what she could only describe as editorial interest, and left.

"Look," she said finally, and made herself lower the maps, made herself put the salesmanship down, because she could feel the moment tipping and she had exactly one thing left that might tip it the other way. "You don't have to trust me. I wouldn't. I've read my own file. But consider the arithmetic, since you clearly like numbers more than people. Every day you stand here, the river rises. Every day, the odds of finding anyone alive drop. You can hate me, hate him, hate the foundation, hate the whole rotten business of digging up the dead — I genuinely don't care, I've been hated by professionals. But if there's even a chance he's alive up there, and you had the boat, and the charts, and the skill, and you stayed at this dock coiling rope—"

"Don't."

"—then you'll live with that the way I've lived with the board testimony. And I can tell you from experience, Fairweather, it doesn't get lighter. It just gets older."

The rope in his hands had gone completely still. He stared at her, and for a moment — just a moment — she saw through the armor to the boy who had once, according to a photograph Marcus kept in his desk drawer and pretended not to, stood on a riverbank at age twelve holding a surveying rod twice his height, grinning like the future belonged to him.

Then the shutter came down, and Dominic Fairweather picked up his rope again.

"No," he said. "Ask me again tomorrow. Maybe I'll feel charitable."

"You're casting off tonight. I saw your fuel order."

"Then ask me again in an hour. Maybe the rain will let up and I'll be in a better mood."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Then she set the oilcloth packet down on the capstan beside him, deliberately, within easy reach, and stepped back.

"The charts stay with you either way," she said. "Consider them a gift from someone who owes your father more than she can repay on land."

"I don't want—"

"I know what you want. You want to be a man with no debts. It's written all over you — you move ports before anyone can owe you a favor, you pay cash, you probably sleep with your boots on in case you need to run." She said it without cruelty, and watched it land anyway, precisely, the way a good probe finds the seam in a wall. "But you can't be debtless and save him. Those are different lives, and you only get to pick once."

She turned and walked away down the dock, boots ringing on wet planks, refusing — on principle, and against every instinct — to look back.

* * *

She gave him forty minutes. She spent them in the dockside cantina, which was generous with its coffee and stingy with everything else, sitting where she could see the boat through fogged glass. She drank coffee that tasted like it had been brewed during a previous administration and rehearsed, for the hundredth time, the thing she would say if he came. And the thing she would say if he didn't.

At minute thirty-one, she saw him come out of the wheelhouse, walk to the capstan, and pick up the packet.

He weighed it in one hand. He looked around the dock — left, right, the habitual scan of a man checking whether anyone was watching him do something he'd regret — and then he unwrapped one corner of the oilcloth, just enough to see the top chart, and Wren watched his thumb move along the edge of the paper, tracing a coastline his father had drawn, and something in his shoulders changed. Not softened. Recalibrated. Like an instrument finding its level.

He rewrapped the packet. He did not bring it back to her, which she noted with professional satisfaction. He did not throw it in the water either, which she noted with considerably more.

Then he disappeared into the wheelhouse, and a light came on, and Wren finished her terrible coffee and allowed herself, for the first time in nine days, to breathe fully.

It wasn't a yes. She knew that. It was a man alone with a map and a decision, and she had been in enough field camps to know that the decision was never made at the table — it was made in the quiet afterward, alone, when the argument stopped echoing and only the facts remained.

She paid for both coffees — his untouched cup included, because she was raised to settle accounts — and stepped back out into the rain.

Behind her, faintly, she heard the wheelhouse door open again, and a voice carry across the wet air, roughened by reluctance and something that might, under laboratory conditions, have been mistaken for curiosity:

"Calloway. What time does the tide turn?"

She smiled at the rain, at the river, at the whole drowned grey world.

"An hour," she called back. "I'll bring the money."

From the wheelhouse came a sound that was half grunt, half surrender, and entirely, unmistakably, a beginning.
