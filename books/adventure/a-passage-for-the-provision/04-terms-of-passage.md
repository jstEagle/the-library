## 4. Terms of Passage

The rain came in off the Velloran like it had a personal grievance, and Dominic Fairweather stood in the doorway of the Pilots' Rest with a mug of tea he hadn't asked for and watched the third pilot of the day tell him no.

"Monsoon's three weeks early," said Bao, a squat man with a face like a knotted rope and hands that had steered everything from grain barges to gunrunners. "You want me to take a boat up the Serrano gorge now? In this?" He gestured at the window, where the river had gone the color of strong coffee and was moving with the kind of purpose that usually ended in funerals. "I have a wife, Dominic. I like my wife. I would like to keep liking her."

"I'm offering triple the going rate."

"You're offering triple the going rate to a dead man's ghost. Take it to the ghost." Bao drained his tea, set the mug down with the gentle finality of a man closing a coffin lid, and left through the back.

Dominic turned to the room. The Pilots' Rest at this hour held four souls: a sleeping deckhand, a dog, a man playing solitaire with such intensity he might have been defusing it, and the barkeep, who was polishing a glass that had been clean since the previous administration.

"Anyone," Dominic said.

The solitaire man didn't look up. The dog opened one eye, assessed the situation, and closed it again. The barkeep said, "Ketchum went up yesterday. Took the Voss contract."

"Voss." Dominic filed that away. Odile Voss and her salvagers had been working the middle river all season, and if Ketchum had signed on with her, then the last independent pilot between here and the gorges was now employed by a woman who paid well and remembered everything. "Anyone who isn't Ketchum?"

"Son," the barkeep said, in the tone of a man delivering a diagnosis, "there isn't anyone who isn't Ketchum. There's you, and there's the dog, and I've seen the dog's paws."

Dominic drank the tea. It was terrible. Everything here was terrible except the river, and the river was trying to kill him, and somehow that was the part that felt like home.

He had spent the last two days doing the arithmetic he'd promised himself he wouldn't do. Three missed check-ins. The foundation's telegram had used the word *prudent* four times, which was how bureaucracies said *we have already begun composing the memorial*. Prudent meant waiting for the monsoon to pass. The monsoon, this year, would not pass for two months, and the expedition had provisions for five weeks, six if they stretched it, and men who stretched provisions in the Velloran basin tended to stretch themselves right into the ground.

So: no prudent option. Only the stupid one, and stupid was a language Dominic spoke fluently.

The problem was that the stupid option required a boat and a pilot, and he was only one of those things. His own boat — the *Magpie*, thirty feet of patched teak and borrowed optimism — sat at the dock with a hull he trusted about as far as he could throw it, which, given the hull, was not far.

He was still doing this arithmetic, badly, when the door opened and let in a slab of rain and a woman.

* * *

He knew her before he saw her face. He knew her by the way she stood in the doorway — not shaking off the water like everyone else did, but letting it run, as if weather were a negotiation she'd already won. Then she pushed back her hood and it was Wren Calloway, nine years older and no softer, and Dominic felt the specific exhaustion of a man who has been outrunning something and just heard footsteps behind him.

"You," he said.

"Me," she agreed.

"I told you no."

"You told me no at the dock. I've had two days to think about it, and I've concluded you told me no for the wrong reasons, which means the no doesn't count." She crossed the room, wrung out her sleeve over the spittoon with the brisk authority of a surgeon, and sat down across from him without being invited. The barkeep, sensing either trouble or a story, began polishing a second glass.

"Those were the right reasons," Dominic said. "They were the only reasons. I don't take passengers."

"Passengers. Is that what you called the man whose boat you salvaged off the Kettle Shoals in March? Because I asked around, Dominic. You took a paying fare upriver in flood season not four months ago."

"That was different. That man was cargo with opinions. You're—" He stopped, recalibrated, and chose the honest word, because lying to Wren Calloway had never once worked and he was too tired to try. "You're someone I know. That's worse."

"Interesting theory. Let's test it." She set a leather folder on the table between them and did not open it. "I've spent two days at the charter office. Do you know what I've learned? Every licensed pilot on this stretch of river is either dead, employed by Odile Voss, or Bao. Bao has a wife he likes. The charter office has a list of boats for sale, and I have looked at every one of them, and I have concluded that I cannot buy a boat, learn to pilot it, and reach the Serrano gorge before your father's expedition runs out of food. Not even if I don't sleep. I did the arithmetic."

"So did I."

"Then you know I'm right."

"What I know," Dominic said, "is that you testified against my father in front of the academic board and ended his career, and now you want to charter a boat to go find him, and I am expected to believe that's a coincidence of scheduling."

Something moved behind her eyes — not flinching, exactly. Recalibrating. He was starting to learn that Wren Calloway's face did everything at one remove, like a telegraph office: the message arrived, was processed, and only then, in carefully measured bursts, was it transmitted.

"Yes," she said. "That is what you're expected to believe."

"And do you believe it?"

"I believe the river doesn't care why we're on it." She opened the folder at last and turned it around. Maps. Good ones — better than good; two of them he recognized as survey sheets that were supposed to live in the foundation's locked cabinet in the capital. She'd stolen them, or been given them, or both. "Current charts, depth notes from the last three seasons, and the coordinates of every anchorage the expedition reported before they went quiet. I am offering you the one thing you can't salvage off a wreck, Dominic, which is knowing where you're going."

He looked at the maps. He wanted, very badly, to look at the maps. He made himself look at her instead.

"Double rate," she said. "Cash, half up front. I ride along, I pay for the fuel, and I stay out of your wheelhouse. I don't touch the helm, I don't question your lines, and I don't talk to you about anything that happened before this week unless you bring it up first."

"That's a long list of things you're promising not to do."

"I'm a thorough person."

"You're a person who testified against my father."

"Yes," she said, and did not look away, and did not explain, and the not-explaining sat between them like a third passenger. "And I'm the only person on this river who knows where he went."

* * *

They negotiated for two hours, which for Dominic was a record in restraint and for Wren was clearly a warm-up.

He held out on the double rate longer than made any sense — he told himself it was principle, though a small honest voice in the back of his skull observed that principle was easier to afford when someone else was paying for it. He held out on the cabin. She would take the forward berth and he would sleep in the wheelhouse; she countered that she'd slept on worse, and he countered that she hadn't slept on his worse, and she'd looked at him with something that was almost a smile and said, "No. Yours is worse. I've read the salvage reports."

They argued about the maps, which he refused to accept as a gift and she refused to sell, so they ended up splitting the difference in a way that satisfied neither: she would navigate from the charts when he asked, and only when he asked, and the charts would live in his cabin, and if she touched them otherwise he'd throw them overboard, and she said, "You'd throw a foundation survey sheet overboard," and he said, "I've thrown better things overboard," and she'd looked at him for a moment too long and said nothing, which was its own kind of answer.

It was while they were haggling over the fuel — she would buy it, but he would buy it, meaning she'd hand him the money and he'd hand it to the chandler, because he would not be on a boat where she'd paid the bills, and she'd agreed to this with the expression of a woman humoring a child who insists on buttoning his own coat — that the rain changed.

It had been falling all day in the ordinary way, the way that soaks you and ruins your mood. Now it fell in the other way. The barkeep stopped polishing. The dog stood up. Somewhere out on the water a mooring line groaned like an old man getting out of a chair, and the light through the window went from grey to the color of a bruise.

"That's not weather," the barkeep said. "That's the front. That's the real one."

Wren was already on her feet. "The *Magpie*," Dominic said, and they were both moving, and neither of them had said yes yet, and somehow that had stopped mattering somewhere in the last hour without either of them signing anything.

* * *

The dock at night, in the first hour of a true monsoon, is a place where you learn what your boat is made of.

The *Magpie* was made, it turned out, of stubbornness and caulk. Dominic went over her bow to stern with a lantern while the rain tried to take the lantern, checking lashings, doubling lines, jamming a wedge under the gangway plank that had developed ambitions. Wren worked without being told — carried his spare lines, held the lantern when his hands were full, and said exactly nothing, which he was beginning to suspect was the most expensive thing she'd offered him all day. Wren Calloway, silent. He'd have paid double for that alone.

"Port line's chafing," she called over the wind.

"I see it."

"You see it, or you want to see it?"

"I see it." He re-led the line. She'd been right. He decided not to tell her she'd been right, and then decided that was childish, and then decided he was allowed to be childish about this one thing, and said nothing.

They got her doubled up and the hatches battened and the tender lashed hard, and by the time it was done Dominic was soaked to the skin and shivering in a way he'd have denied under oath, and Wren stood at the head of the dock with the rain coming off her hood in ropes, looking at the boat.

"She leaks," Dominic said. "Starboard side, aft of the waterline. She's leaked since I owned her. I know every place she leaks and she's never leaked worse than that, and I check her seams every season, so if you're about to tell me the boat is a death trap—"

"I wasn't going to tell you the boat is a death trap." She turned. Rain ran down her face and she didn't bother with it. "I was going to say she's a good boat. I was going to say my father built boats, on the coast, before the war took the yard, and I know a good hull when I see one, and that's a good hull with bad maintenance and a pilot who loves her, which is more than most boats get."

Dominic stared at her. In nine years of the story he told himself about Wren Calloway — the testimony, the board, the wreckage of his father's name — there had been no room for a boatyard father, or for the way she looked at the *Magpie*, which was the way you looked at a thing you'd once had and lost.

"You never mentioned that," he said. "At the hearing. Anything about — you. Where you came from."

"The hearing," Wren said, "was not about me."

"No," Dominic said. "It never is, is it? Until it is."

The rain hammered the dock. Down the river, a light moved — a boat running the dark water with no running lamps, which meant either smugglers or fools, and on this river the categories overlapped. It passed. The light went out toward the middle channel and was gone, and Dominic filed it away in the drawer where he kept things he'd worry about tomorrow.

"Terms," Wren said. She put out her hand. Rain ran off both their sleeves and dripped between them, and her grip, when he took it, was dry inside the wet, and strong. "Double rate. Half now. I stay out of the wheelhouse. I don't touch the helm. I don't talk about the past unless you do."

"And if I ask you something about the past?"

She held his hand a moment longer than the bargain required. "Then I'll answer," she said. "That was always the deal, Dominic. You just never asked."

He let go. He turned to the boat, because looking at the boat was easier than looking at her, and because the boat needed another check of the aft line, and because he was a man who kept moving, and moving just then meant three steps to a mooring cleat.

"Cast off at first light," he said. "If the river lets us."

"The river," Wren said, "doesn't care why we're on it."

"That's the second time you've said that."

"It's the second time it's been true."

* * *

He did not sleep in the wheelhouse so much as hold a vigil in it. The front worked itself into a fury around two in the morning and the *Magpie* rode it the way she rode everything — complaining, rolling, and refusing, with the deep mulish integrity of a good hull, to do anything as undignified as sink.

Dominic sat with the charts open under the lamp, her charts, the stolen ones, and traced the route upriver with one finger. The gorges narrowed. The anchorages marched up the page like a trail of small, increasingly desperate footprints — and then stopped, at a bend in the Serrano where the survey sheet had a coffee ring and, in the margin, in handwriting he did not recognize, a single notation:

*W.C. — you were right. I should have told you years ago.*

He sat very still. The rain came down. Somewhere aft, in the forward berth, Wren Calloway was either asleep or pretending to be, and the coffee ring sat on the map like a small brown moon, and Dominic Fairweather, who had spent nine years perfecting the art of not caring, discovered that the art had failed him completely, and that he was going up that gorge in the morning no matter what the river thought about it.

The notation was dated eleven days ago.

His father had been alive eleven days ago — and he had been writing to her.
