## 5. Upstream

The gorges began an hour after dawn, and by the second hour Dominic had stopped talking.

Not that he'd said much to begin with. He'd given Wren Calloway exactly three sentences since they cast off from Sarn's Reach: *Life jacket's under the bench. Don't touch the wheel. Head's a bucket and a hole in the deck — your choice.* She had accepted all three with the tight nod of a woman signing a contract she intended to litigate later, and had installed herself on the cargo crate amidships with her maps and her silence, and that was that.

Which suited him fine. He'd piloted this stretch of the Velloran half a dozen times in better seasons, and it demanded attention the way a jealous lover demands it — constantly, and with consequences. The monsoon had come early and greedy, and the river that was normally a brown ribbon between limestone walls was now a churning brown ocean squeezed into a throat. The walls rose sheer on either side, two hundred feet of wet grey stone furred with ferns, and the water between them moved like something with opinions.

He worked the wheel in small corrections, reading the surface the way his father had taught him to read strata — *the river tells you what it's done, boy; you just have to look* — and hated himself a little for the thought. Nine years without the man and he still heard the voice every time the current shifted.

Behind him, paper rustled. Wren was unfolding one of her maps again, the third time in twenty minutes, and he could hear her frowning at it.

"They're out of date," he said, not turning around.

"I know they're out of date."

"Then why keep looking?"

"Because knowing *how* out of date is its own information." A pause. "The channel markers at Kiln Bend were on the east side when this was drawn."

"They're on the bottom now. The flood took the whole bend last week." He glanced back despite himself. She sat with her knees drawn up, grey-eyed and rain-damp, her hair escaping its braid in wiry commas, and there was ink on her fingers — actual ink, from an actual pen, which told him she was one of those people who still wrote things down because typing felt like lying. "You've been up here before," he said. It wasn't a question.

"Twice. With your father."

"When?"

"The first time you were eleven."

The wheel went light under his hands for half a second before he corrected. "I would have known about that."

"Would you?" she asked, mild as milk, and went back to her map.

Dominic faced forward and let the gorge have his attention, because the alternative was letting Wren Calloway have it, and he'd sworn off both.

But the question sat in him like a swallowed hook. Eleven. He was trying to build the picture — eleven years old, where was he? Boarding school in Corvale, the year of the broken wrist, the year Marcus had sent a telegram instead of coming home for the surgery. And all that time the man had been upriver, showing the river to someone else's eyes. Showing it to *her.*

"How many times total?" he asked, because he was apparently a glutton.

"Four expeditions over six years."

"And then you stopped."

Paper rustled. He could feel her deciding how much truth the moment could hold. "And then we stopped," she said finally.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I'm giving today. Ask me tomorrow."

"Why not today?"

"Because today I'm seasick, Mr. Fairweather, and because you're fishing, and I don't bite on the first day."

"We're on a river."

"Then riversick. The principle holds."

Despite himself — genuinely against his own vote — Dominic felt the corner of his mouth move. He killed it fast. But she'd seen it in the set of his shoulders, probably; she struck him as a woman who read backs the way he read water.

They ran the next mile in silence, if you didn't count the river, which roared continuously enough to make silence a relative term. The gorge narrowed. The walls leaned in until their crowns nearly met overhead, shutting out most of the sky, and the light went green and submarine, and the current picked up speed like it had somewhere important to be and resented the boat for slowing it down.

"Your father used to stand right where you're standing," Wren said suddenly, apropos of nothing, or possibly of everything. "Same stance. Left hand low on the wheel. He said the left hand feels the river and the right hand just takes orders."

"My father," Dominic said evenly, "has never taken an order in his life."

"He took mine once."

That got him to turn around fully. "What order?"

She met his look without flinching, which he grudgingly noted. Most people, caught in a slip like that, looked away. "To turn back," she said. "At the Gorge of Bells, four years ago. Water higher than this. I told him we should go back and wait it out." Her jaw tightened around whatever came next. "He almost did."

"But he didn't."

"No. He didn't." She folded the map along its tired creases. "We lost two weeks' supplies and very nearly the survey launch. He laughed about it afterward. That laugh of his."

Dominic knew the laugh. It lived in his memory attached to a dozen small disasters — the collapsed tent, the burned dinner, the time the family dog had eaten the notes for an entire chapter of Marcus's book and Marcus had laughed until he cried while nine-year-old Dominic cried for entirely different reasons. *It's only paper, Dom. Paper comes back.* Except paper hadn't come back. Paper had kept leaving, year after year, telegram after telegram, until the paper in the house was mostly the kind other people signed.

"He shouldn't have been laughing," Dominic said. "He should have been listening."

"Yes," Wren said quietly. "He should have been doing a great many things." And there was something under the words, some sediment of old anger that matched his own so exactly it was almost worse than disagreement, and Dominic decided — firmly, deliberately — that he did not want to know what she meant by it.

The river decided otherwise. The river, in fact, interrupted.

* * *

Wren saw it first, though she wouldn't claim credit for it later, because what she actually saw was birds — a scatter of white egrets lifting off the water ahead in a hurry, the way birds do when the ground beneath them is about to stop being ground.

"Dominic."

"I see them."

"Is that normal?"

"Nothing about today is normal." He was already standing taller at the wheel, weight forward, reading the water ahead with an intensity she recognized from watching his father do the same thing, and the recognition landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. Same stance. Same squint. Twenty years younger and thirty pounds lighter and hating every resemblance, but there it was, written in the way he tilted his head.

Then she heard it too: a sound underneath the river's roar, a deep grinding groan, wood against stone, the sound of something enormous being chewed.

Around the next bend, wedged across the entire gorge, was the logjam.

It looked, Wren thought wildly, like the river had gotten into an argument with a forest and both parties were still present. Whole trees — trunks forty feet long, root balls like exploded artillery — were packed together in a tangle that spanned bank to bank, jammed against a granite spur that jutted from the eastern wall. Water piled against the upstream face in a brown ridge, curling and sucking, and pieces of debris circled in the eddies like vultures waiting for the wreck to finish happening.

"That's new," Dominic said, in the tone of a man describing a minor inconvenience, which Wren was beginning to understand was his native language for catastrophe.

"Can we go around?"

"There's no around. That's the point of a gorge." He cut the engine to idle and let the current carry them, studying the jam. "There." He pointed. "See the gap? Along the far wall, where the spur throws an eddy. The water backs up behind the granite and goes slack. There's maybe eight feet of clear channel between the rock and the logs."

"Eight feet." She looked at the boat. The boat was a converted salvage tug named — with a lack of ambition she'd already commented on — *Boat*. "This vessel is eleven feet wide."

"Nine and a half at the waterline."

"Oh, well then. Practically a needle."

"You want to thread it or swim it?" He was already moving, kicking the engine up, swinging wide toward the western wall to line up his approach. "Listen carefully, because I'm only going to say this once. The eddy will try to spin us. When I say *pole*, you take the boathook off the starboard rail and push off any log that gets friendly. Push, don't hook — a hooked log will take the boat apart. And when I say *down*, you get flat on that crate, because some of those trunks are leaning on each other and one good bump brings the whole house down."

"And what will you be doing?"

"Driving."

"That's it? Driving?"

"Miss Calloway." He gave her a look over his shoulder, and for the first time since the dock — since the scandal, since the testimony, since everything — there was something in his face besides frost. It might have been excitement. It was certainly alive. "Some men play cards. Some men collect stamps. I thread logjams. Try to contain your envy."

The current took them.

Later, Wren would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and find they refused to be reconstructed — they came back only as fragments, sensory shrapnel. The roar doubling as they rounded the spur. The wall of packed timber rising beside her, wet and black and dripping, close enough to touch, hung with torn vines. Dominic's voice, calm as a man reading a grocery list: *Pole.* The boathook heavy in her hands, her arms shocking her with their own strength as she shoved a spinning trunk away from the hull. *Down.* Going flat against the crate as a root ball the size of a carriage scraped overhead with a sound like a giant clearing his throat. Spray. Green light. The smell of crushed wood and river mud. And through all of it, the wheel turning in small, certain increments, the engine breathing in short bursts, the whole boat moving through that impossible slot with the grace of something threaded rather than forced —

— and then they were through, out the other side, into open brown water and sudden ordinary daylight, and the gorge walls fell back, and the terrible chewing sound faded behind them like a door closing on a bad room.

Wren realized she was still holding the boathook in a death grip. She made herself let go, finger by finger.

"Well," she said, when her voice agreed to work. "That was."

"Yes," Dominic agreed.

"I've run rapids on three continents and I don't think I breathed during any part of that."

"Breathing's optional. Steering isn't." But he was grinning — actually grinning, brief and unguarded, and it changed his whole face, knocked ten years off and thirty years of armor off with it, and Wren caught herself thinking that Marcus must have grinned exactly like that at exactly such moments, and she looked away quickly, out at the water, because that thought was none of her business anymore.

Neither of them spoke for a full minute. The boat settled into its long rhythm. Somewhere upstream the sun found a seam in the clouds and laid a strip of gold across the water, and the river, having tried to kill them, went back to pretending it hadn't.

"You pushed instead of hooked," Dominic said at last. "First log. I didn't tell you why. You just did it."

"You mentioned it."

"People mention things and then do the other thing. Under pressure, especially." He was looking at her sidelong, reassessing, and she could practically hear the abacus clicking behind his eyes. "Where'd you learn river work?"

"Your father's expeditions included boats. Boats include emergencies. I was the person who read the manuals." She paused. "All of them. Twice."

"Huh." He turned back to the wheel. "Useful."

From Dominic Fairweather, Wren suspected, that was roughly equivalent to a sonnet. She allowed herself, cautiously, one degree of warmth, the way you'd test ice with one foot.

* * *

They made camp that night on a gravel shelf below a waterfall that came down the gorge wall in a silver rope, and the rain held off long enough for Dominic to get a fire going under an overhang, which he privately considered the day's second-best piece of navigation.

Wren produced food from her pack with the efficiency of a quartermaster: hard bread, dried fish, a tin of something labeled in three languages and trusted in none. They ate sitting on opposite sides of the fire, and the fire did what fires do, which was make silence comfortable before either party had authorized it.

"Question," Dominic said eventually.

"You said questions were my fare, not yours."

"I'm renegotiating. You've been upriver with him four times. I've been upriver with him zero times. That makes you the expert on a subject I've spent nine years avoiding, and I find I hate that more than I expected." He turned a stick in the fire. "So. One question. You can pick whether to answer it honestly."

"That's generous."

"I'm a generous man. Ask anyone who owes me money."

Wren watched him across the flames. He had his father's profile and none of his father's ease, and she thought: *he's been carrying this question for years and doesn't even know which one it is yet.* She knew, because she'd carried hers the same way, in the same pocket, worn smooth from handling.

"Ask," she said.

"What was he like up here?" Dominic gestured at the dark gorge, the unseen miles above them. "Not the expedition reports. Not the lectures. Not the man in the newspaper photographs pointing at things. Up here. On a boat. In the rain. What was he like when nobody important was watching?"

It was, she thought, the wrong question shaped exactly like the right one. She considered several answers and discarded them — the funny ones, the cruel ones, the safe ones.

"He hummed," she said finally. "When he was working something out. Badly. Some tune he never finished, like the song itself was still an excavation." She poked the fire. "He talked to the porters in their own languages — all of them, badly, cheerfully. He gave away his gloves to anyone who looked cold and then complained about his hands for a week. And when we found something — really found something, a carving, a stratum, anything old and true — he'd go quiet. Completely quiet. Like a man in a church who'd stopped believing in churches and found one anyway."

Dominic had gone very still. The stick stopped turning.

"He never hummed at home," he said, after a while. His voice had a crack in it that he pretended was the fire popping. "I mean — I don't know. I was a kid. Maybe he did and I forgot."

"Maybe," Wren said gently, and let it lie.

The fire burned down. Above the overhang, the waterfall kept up its silver rope, and the gorge walls held the last grey light like cupped hands. Wren rolled into her bedroll and stared up at stone older than any argument either of them had ever had, and she was nearly asleep when she heard him say it, quiet, to the fire rather than to her:

"She said *we*, earlier. At the logjam. 'We lost two weeks' supplies.' You said *we* about the Gorge of Bells too." A pause, the length of a held breath. "Four expeditions, and then you testified against him, and then nothing. What happened up here, Wren? What really ended it?"

She lay still in the dark, heart going oddly, the old document-shaped secret sitting in its pocket against her ribs.

"Tomorrow," she said.

"You said tomorrow yesterday."

"I know." She closed her eyes. "I keep hoping you'll forget."

Above the gorge, the wind changed, and came down the river cold and smelling of iron — the smell, though neither of them said it, of much heavier rain still to come.
