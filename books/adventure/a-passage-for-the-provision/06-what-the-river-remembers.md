## 6. What the River Remembers

The trading post at Kel Anoor announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke, drying fish, and the particular sweetness of overripe mangoes that no one had the heart to throw away. Wren smelled it a full ten minutes before the buildings appeared — a huddle of stilt houses leaning against each other like tired travelers, perched on a shelf of rock above the waterline with the confidence of something that had survived forty monsoons and intended to survive forty more.

"Fuel and food," Dominic said, cutting the engine so they drifted in on momentum alone. He'd been doing that all morning, she'd noticed. Conserving. A man who treated every drop of petrol like it was blood he might need later.

"And information," Wren said.

He gave her a sideways look. "We're not stopping to gossip."

"We're stopping because your father's expedition came through here or they didn't, and if they did, someone sold them rice and someone else remembered their faces." She was already gathering her satchel, checking that her notebook was dry. "Gossip is data, Fairweather. It's just data that hasn't learned to behave yet."

Something flickered across his face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, quickly escorted off the premises. "Fine. But we're gone within the hour."

*Within the hour,* Wren thought, *he says, as though rivers run on schedules.* But she kept it to herself. They had developed a working silence over the past two days, the kind built from mutual suspicion and shared seasickness, and she'd learned not to poke at it unnecessarily. Poking was for later. Poking was, in fact, the entire reason she'd insisted on this stop.

The post's main building was a long room open on three sides, its fourth wall given over entirely to shelving: bolts of cloth, tins of kerosene, coils of rope, batteries of uncertain vintage, and a glass case containing items Wren suspected had been waiting decades for a buyer — a brass compass with a cracked face, a taxidermied fish of frankly implausible proportions, and a single elegant porcelain teacup that looked as lost among its neighbors as a soprano in a marching band.

Behind a counter stacked with ledgers sat a woman of about seventy, silver-haired, sharp-eyed, shelling beans into a bowl with the rhythmic efficiency of a machine designed for exactly this purpose.

"You're going upriver," the woman said, before either of them had spoken.

Wren blinked. "Is it the boat? People always guess from the boat."

"It's the boat." The woman didn't look up from her beans. "Nobody takes a boat up the Velloran in this season unless they're fools or looking for somebody. You've got the look of both."

"Dominic Fairweather," Dominic said, extending a hand. "And this is—"

"Paying customers," Wren finished smoothly. "We need fuel, rice, and whatever fruit isn't past arguing its case."

The woman — her name, according to a hand-painted sign, was Merta — took Dominic's hand, shook it once, and returned to her beans. "Fuel I have. Rice I have. Fruit you're too late for, unless you want the mangoes that have gone philosophical." She nodded toward a basket whose contents had achieved a state Wren could only describe as contemplative. "You'll want to talk to Sef, too. He ran supplies up to a foreign party three weeks back. Big tents, machines, a lot of talking in low voices."

Wren felt it go through her like a current. "Three weeks ago. Where?"

Merta's eyes lifted at last, and they were the eyes of a woman who had spent a lifetime deciding what information cost. "Sef drinks at Dano's in the evenings. Dano's is the house with the blue door that used to be green." She went back to her beans. "Fuel first. Talk after. That's the order of things here."

---

They loaded fuel drums and sacks of rice while the afternoon light turned the river to hammered tin. Dominic worked in silence, efficient and closed, and Wren watched him out of the corner of her eye and rehearsed what she was going to say.

She had been rehearsing it, if she were honest, since the dock. Since before the dock. Since the telegram had reached her in Meridian and she'd read the words *expedition silent* and felt something crack along a seam she'd thought was healed scar tissue and was apparently still just wound.

Here was the problem, as she saw it: somewhere upriver was a man she had helped destroy, and she was traveling toward him in the company of the son who hated her for it, carrying a confession she'd never managed to make to anyone, and the river between here and there was rising by the hour. If she waited for the right moment, the right moment would drown.

If she waited for courage, same result.

"So," she said, when the last drum was lashed down. "Dano's doesn't open till evening. We have time."

Dominic tied off a knot without looking at her. "Time is fuel, Calloway. We just spent some."

"We have time," she repeated, "and there's something you should hear from me before you hear it from Sef, or from anyone else upriver who remembers. Because people upriver remember everything, and they will remember me."

That got his attention. He straightened, wiped his hands on his trousers, and leaned against the rail with his arms crossed — the posture of a man preparing to enjoy something unpleasant. "Go on, then."

*Not here,* some cowardly part of her whispered. *Wait for a private moment.* But the boat was six meters of leaking hull and there were no private moments on it, and besides, she was tired. Tired of carrying it. Tired of the way it clanked around inside her at two in the morning like cargo that hadn't been lashed properly.

"In 4119," she said, "your father and I published a provenance analysis of the Ashkarrel bronzes. You may have heard of them. The foundation's crown jewel for a decade. Museum acquisitions, lecture circuits, Marcus on the cover of two magazines I still can't look at."

"I know the bronzes." His voice had gone flat. "Everyone knows the bronzes."

"The papers were forged." She said it plainly, because she had learned that plainness was the only mercy left in the telling. "At least four of the eleven. The inscriptions were copied from fragments in a private collection — Renner's collection, though nobody knew that then — and aged chemically. The work was good. It fooled peer review, it fooled the museums, it nearly fooled me."

"But not quite."

"Not quite." She looked out at the water rather than at him. "I found an anomaly in the patina stratification. Something that shouldn't have been possible. I brought it to Marcus, and he—" She stopped. Started again. "He told me I was tired. He said anomalies always resolved themselves under better light. And I wanted to believe him, because I loved the work, and because I loved—" She caught herself. Redirected. "Because the work mattered. And then the accusation came anyway, from outside, from a journal that had received an anonymous dossier, and when the inquiry convened, I was called to testify."

Dominic hadn't moved. "And you testified that the papers were genuine."

"No." The word came out smaller than she intended. She made herself say the rest at full size. "I testified that I had raised concerns and been dismissed. Which was true. And then they asked whether I believed the documents authentic, and I said—" Her throat worked. "I said yes. Under oath. With the foundation's counsel sitting three feet away, having spent the previous evening explaining to me, very gently, what happened to researchers who destabilized institutional holdings. What happened to funding. To careers. To the careers of everyone associated."

The river slapped against the hull. Somewhere ashore, a dog was conducting a one-dog argument with a chicken.

"They ruined him anyway," Dominic said quietly.

"They ruined us both. That was the design. A scandal needs two bodies to look like a war instead of an execution." She finally made herself meet his eyes. "I lied, Dominic. I lied under pressure, and my lie was the centerpiece of the case against him, and I have spent nine years knowing it. That's what I'm telling you. Not because it excuses anything. Because you deserve to hear it from me before the river gives you a worse version."

For a long moment he said nothing. Then he laughed — short, disbelieving, with no humor in it at all.

"That's it? That's your confession?"

Wren's stomach dropped. "What do you mean, *that's it*?"

"I mean I've heard this story before." He pushed off the rail and paced two steps, turned, paced back — a caged thing in a space too small for pacing. "From the foundation lawyers, when they tried to buy my silence after he disappeared from public life. From journalists. Everyone has a version where poor Wren Calloway was pressured, poor Wren Calloway had no choice. And every version has one thing in common." He stopped, facing her. "It keeps the person telling it clean."

"And what would you have done?" The question came out sharper than she meant. "Twenty-eight years old, no independent standing, an institution holding every credential I had — what would *you* have done?"

"I don't know." He said it fast, which meant he'd thought about it. "But I know what I wouldn't have done. I wouldn't have spent nine years letting him carry it alone."

"He never once contacted me!" It burst out of her louder than she'd intended; the dog ashore paused its argument to listen. "Nine years, Dominic. Do you know what I would have given for one letter? One line saying *I know what they did to you too*? He cut every thread. Him. Not me. So don't stand there and tell me about carrying things alone, because the man you're trying to rescue made an art form of it!"

The words hung in the wet air between them. Wren became aware, distantly, that Merta had come out onto the post's porch and was watching them shell beans with the serene attention of a woman who had witnessed a great many arguments on this dock and expected to witness a great many more.

Dominic's jaw worked. When he spoke, his voice was low and controlled, which was somehow worse than shouting.

"Here's what I believe, Calloway. I believe you found your anomaly. I believe you panicked. And I believe that afterward, when it had cost you everything anyway, you needed the story to be about coercion, because the alternative — that you were a coward who saved herself a little misery at the price of a man's life's work — was unbearable. So you made it bearable." He picked up a fuel drum as though the conversation were concluded. "People do it all the time. The river's full of drowned men who were excellent swimmers right up until they weren't."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed, hauling the drum toward the stern. "It isn't. Fairness is upriver with everything else we're short of."

* * *

Dano's — blue door, formerly green, the paint job visible from fifty meters like a boast — opened at dusk, and it opened the way Wren imagined a lung opening: slowly, reluctantly, exhaling lamplight and the smell of fried plantains into the blue evening. Inside, perhaps a dozen people sat at tables that had achieved a state of perfect unevenness, and behind the bar a large man with forearms like mooring posts poured arrack with the gravity of a priest administering sacrament.

Sef was easy to identify. He was the only patron drinking alone and radiating the specific loneliness of a man recently returned from upriver, where the company is sparse and the stories accumulate with nowhere to go. Wren bought him a second cup before introducing herself, which was, in her experience, the correct order of operations in every river town from here to the sea.

"Three weeks ago," she said, sliding the cup across, "you ran supplies to a foreign party."

Sef drank, considered, and decided to be forthcoming, possibly because the arrack was good and possibly because loneliness is a currency that spends itself. "Big outfit. Tents, crates, instruments. The old one — gray beard, quiet — he paid in advance and tipped like a man settling accounts. The others argued a lot. There was another one, slick coat, city boots, talked about *timing* and *windows* like the river was a train schedule." Sef's mouth twisted. "I don't care for men who talk about the river like it owes them punctuality."

Wren's pen was moving. "Where did they go from here?"

"Up the Narrows, past the bell gorge." He said it casually, and Wren felt the words land in her chest like dropped stones. *The bell gorge.* The landmark from the rubbing. She kept her face still and her pen moving. "Then up the old terraces, toward the high country. Nobody goes there. Water comes down off it like a temper." He shrugged. "The old one gave me a message, though. Said if anyone came asking — anyone at all — I should tell them the party split. Some went inland with the slick-coat man. Some went home. And the old one went where the bells are loudest."

Dominic, beside her, had gone very still.

"That's a strange message," Wren said carefully.

"It's a strange river." Sef drained his cup and stood, weaving slightly, a man returning to the bosom of his bed. At the door he paused. "The old one also said — he said to tell any pilot that the Narrows flood three days after the rain starts in the high country, and the rain started yesterday." He tapped his ear. "You can hear it coming. Like the whole sky clearing its throat."

He left. The door swung shut behind him, and the lamplight steadied, and Wren became aware that Dominic was staring at his hands on the table as though they belonged to someone else.

"Three days," he said.

"Maybe less. Sef's estimate could be off by—"

"Three days, Wren." He looked up, and in the lamplight his face had shed something — not the anger, not yet, but a layer of it, the way floodwater sheds mud as it spreads. "My father sent a message through a supply runner addressed to *anyone who comes asking*. He knew someone would come. He knew it would be—" He stopped. Swallowed. "He knew."

Wren said nothing. There was nothing useful to say, and she had learned — late, expensively, nine years too late — that sometimes the kindest thing you could offer a man was the absence of your voice.

Outside, far upriver, past the dark shoulders of the gorges, the sky grumbled once, low and long, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Dominic stood, threw coins on the table, and headed for the door. Halfway there he stopped, without turning around.

"For what it's worth," he said, to the doorframe, to the night, maybe to himself, "I don't believe you lied because you're a coward. I think you lied because you were scared, and scared and cowardly aren't the same thing, and I know the difference because I've been running for ten years and calling it freedom." A muscle moved in his jaw. "It doesn't mean I forgive you. It means I'm too tired to keep being angry at the wrong person."

Then he pushed through the blue door into the dark, leaving Wren alone with her notebook full of facts, her chest full of nine years, and the sound — faint, distant, unmistakable — of the first rain beginning to fall on the roof of the world upstream.
