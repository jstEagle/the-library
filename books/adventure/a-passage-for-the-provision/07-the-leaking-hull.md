## 7. The Leaking Hull

The river told you things before it showed them to you.

Dominic had learned that from a man named Ferro when he was nineteen and too green to know he was being taught anything at all. *Listen with your feet,* Ferro used to say, barefoot on the deck plates of a rust-bucket freighter not so different from the one Dominic now steered through the Velloran's lower reaches. *The hull talks. Most people just don't speak stupid.*

For two days the boat had been speaking stupid.

It started as a whisper — a faint change in the engine note, a half-beat of lag when he came off plane in the slack water below the second gorge. Then the bilge pump began cycling more often than it should, its little motor clicking on for ten seconds, twenty, then off again, like a nervous man clearing his throat. Dominic had said nothing. He'd spent the first evening lying flat on the deck sole with his ear pressed to the planking while Wren sat in the bow pretending to read a survey report she'd clearly read four times already, both of them maintaining the fiction that they were not sharing a boat so much as occupying adjacent territories.

By the third morning, the whisper became a conversation.

"Your boat is drinking," Wren announced, appearing at the wheelhouse door with her hair tied back and an expression that suggested she'd been awake long enough to form opinions.

"She's sipping."

"The pump's run six times since dawn. I counted." She came up beside him and looked out at the water sliding past, brown-green and muscular with the early monsoon swelling. "I also noticed the list. We're leaning left like we've had a few."

Dominic checked the compass card, which did indeed sit a degree or two off true, and felt something cold settle behind his sternum. He'd been telling himself it was fuel weight, uneven loading, the extra drums he'd lashed portside. But a pilot who lied to himself about his own boat was a pilot who deserved what he got.

"Take the wheel," he said.

"I don't know how to—"

"Keep the nose pointed upriver and don't touch anything. Even you can manage that."

"Charming," she said, but she took the spokes, and he went aft, popped the inspection hatch, and dropped into the bilge with a flashlight clamped in his teeth.

The water came up over his boots almost immediately. Not catastrophic — not yet — but three inches where there should have been a damp film, and rising in that slow, patient way water has, as though it had all the time in the world and intended to use it. He waded forward along the keel line, running his palm over the planking seam by seam, feeling for the softness, the give, the telltale weep of a joint letting go.

He found it under the forward storage bay, tucked where no casual glance would ever fall: a seam below the waterline, maybe eight inches of it, opened like a patient wound. Not sprung — *worked*. The caulking hadn't failed from age or strain. It had been picked at. Pried. Someone had gone along that seam with a blade or a marlinspike and worried the oakum loose, a quarter inch at a time, so the damage would show itself hours downstream, far from any dock, in water where nobody could help anybody.

Dominic crouched in the dark with river water soaking through his trousers and let the flashlight beam hold steady on that small, deliberate ruin.

Somebody wanted this boat to die quietly.

* * *

He surfaced into grey daylight and rain-smell and found Wren holding the boat in the current with white-knuckled competence and visible terror, which between them amounted to roughly the same thing.

"How bad?" she asked.

"We're putting in at the next shingle bar. Now."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got until I see how much caulking we've got left." He relieved her at the wheel and brought the bow around toward a gravel spit that jutted from the eastern bank a half-mile ahead, a pale tongue of stone where the gorge widened enough to forgive a mistake. "You wanted to stay out of the wheelhouse. Consider this your reward."

"You're bleeding," she observed, nodding at his hand on the spoke. He looked down. Somewhere in the bilge he'd opened a knuckle against a frame without noticing, and the blood had mixed with bilgewater into a thin pink smear across his palm. He wiped it on his shirt.

"It's not mine," he said. "It's the boat's. We're very close, she and I."

Wren made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth, and went forward to ready a line.

* * *

The shingle bar accepted them grudgingly, gravel grinding under the hull as Dominic ran the bow up just far enough to hold, then killed the engine. Silence rushed in — the particular silence of the upper basin, all wind and water and the distant complaint of birds, the kind of quiet that made a man want to fill it with talk just to prove he still existed.

They beached, anchored stern lines to a boulder the size of a dining table, and got to work.

The spare caulking lived in a canvas roll under the aft bench: two part-used coils of oakum, a brick of pitch compound gone hard at the edges, a mallet, a reaming iron, and a handful of cedar wedges that had come with the boat and had never once been needed until now. Dominic laid it all out on a spread tarp and looked at it the way a gambler looks at his last chips.

"That's it?" Wren asked.

"That's it."

"And the leak is...?"

"Eight inches of seam, forward, below the waterline. We'll need to careen her to work it properly."

"Careen."

"Tilt her over on her side so the seam comes out of the water." He glanced at her. "You do know boats, right? You chartered one at the dock like you knew boats."

"I know *of* boats. Boats are a subject I'm familiar with in the abstract, the way one is familiar with, say, astronomy. I can identify the important parts. I simply haven't operated them personally."

"Wonderful. You're the astronomer, I'm the telescope. Grab that line and pull when I say."

Getting a thirty-foot riverboat onto her beam ends on a gravel bar with two people, one of whom was an astronomer, consumed the better part of two hours and most of Dominic's vocabulary. They winched her over with the stern anchor line and a spar levered against a boulder, the hull groaning the whole way down like an old man lowering himself into a bath, until the damaged seam stood clear of the water, glistening, exposed, accusing.

Up close, there was no ambiguity about it. The oakum hadn't rotted. It had been *extracted* — worked loose in neat increments, the caulking iron marks fresh and clean-edged, done by someone who knew exactly what they were doing and had done it recently. Within days. Possibly within hours of their departure.

Wren crouched beside him at the waterline, rain beginning to speckle the gravel around them, and studied the seam in silence for a long moment.

"Somebody did this," she said finally.

"Somebody did this."

"At the dock."

"At the dock."

She sat back on her heels. Rain gathered in the creases of her jacket. "Who?"

Dominic turned the question over, examining it from angles, the way he examined everything — cargo manifests, charter contracts, the fine print on salvage claims. The dock at Puerto Sombra held perhaps forty souls who'd watched him load: the harbormaster, honest as a fencepost; the fuel clerk; two families of net-menders who liked him well enough; a pair of brothers who ran a rival charter and would happily see him sink but lacked the craft to do it this neatly. And then the others. The men who'd asked no questions and offered no help and watched his loading with the patient attention of men counting someone else's money.

And the telegram. Three missed check-ins, the foundation wringing its hands, and him — the only pilot fool enough to go — announcing his departure date to anyone standing within earshot because he'd needed a witness to make the decision real.

"Someone didn't want a rescue reaching that camp," he said. "Or didn't want *me* reaching it."

"Renner?" Wren said the name carefully, like testing ice.

"I don't know any Renner." He caught the flicker in her face — there and gone, a shutter closing — and filed it away with the other small evasions she'd been stockpiling since the dock. "Do you?"

"No," she said, too quickly, and then, with the air of a woman changing lanes: "Whoever it was, they were good. That's not vandalism. That's craftsmanship."

"It is." He ran his thumb along the opened seam. "Which narrows the field. There are maybe three people on that waterfront who could pick a seam this clean, and I've drunk with two of them."

"And the third?"

"The third never drinks with anyone." He reached for the reaming iron. "Hand me the oakum. We'll argue about assassins after we stop drowning."

* * *

The work ate the day whole.

Caulking a seam is not complicated, but it is merciless — every inch must be driven tight, the fibers packed and rolled and set, the compound laid over smooth, no gaps, no pride, no shortcuts, because the river will find a shortcut before you finish congratulating yourself on it. Dominic drove the oakum while Wren, promoted from astronomer to apprentice over her mild objections, learned to roll fibers into tight twists and hold them steady while he seated them. Her hands were quick and precise once she stopped thinking about them. Of course they were. Everything about Wren Calloway was quick and precise except her answers.

"You're staring at my hands," she said at one point.

"I'm staring at your technique. There's a difference, legally."

"Mm." She rolled another twist, held it out. "My father was a watchmaker. Before the war. I spent summers picking lint out of movements smaller than your thumbnail. This is practically carpentry."

"A watchmaker's daughter who testifies in academic scandals and reads survey reports for fun. You're a whole anthology, Calloway."

Something crossed her face — not quite pain, not quite amusement. "An unfinished one," she said, and handed him the twist, and said nothing else for an hour.

By late afternoon the seam was closed, the compound curing under a tarp rigged against the drizzle, and the two of them sat on the upturned hull eating tinned beans and hard bread while the light went copper and the gorge shadows climbed the far wall. The boat lay on her side between them like a beached whale with opinions, and Dominic found, to his mild irritation, that he was tired in the comfortable way — the way a body gets after honest work with a clear problem at the center of it. It had been a long time since his problems were clear. Usually they were shaped like letters from foundations.

"Most of our caulking's gone," he said, licking a spoon. "What's left wouldn't seal a rowboat. If she opens another seam, we beach and walk."

"Walk where?"

"Inland, eventually. Toward wherever my father isn't."

Wren looked at him over the bean tin. "You could turn back. Report the sabotage. Let the foundation mount a proper expedition with proper boats."

"With what schedule? The monsoon peaks in nine days. After that, these gorges run forty feet over normal and nothing floats but debris and regret. A 'proper expedition' would take three weeks to organize and arrive in time to hold a memorial." He scraped the tin. "No. Whoever loosened that seam understood the arithmetic. Kill the boat, kill the window. They weren't trying to slow us down. They were trying to make us miss it entirely."

"Which means they think there's something at that camp worth missing."

"Which means," Dominic said, "that whoever sent those men knows more about my father's expedition than the foundation does. Than *I* do." He heard his own voice tighten and made himself loosen it. "Everybody on this river seems to have read the last chapter except the family."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Rain ticked on the tarp. Downstream, somewhere out of sight, the river kept up its low continuous argument with the stones.

"Dominic," she said, and it was the first time she'd used his name without an agenda attached, and it landed strangely, like a key fitting a lock he'd forgotten he owned. "When we find them — if we find them — what are you going to say to him?"

He considered lying. It would have been easy; he was good at it; he'd been practicing for nine years. Instead he looked out at the water going dark and said, "I'll bill him for the caulking."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got." He stood, brushed gravel from his trousers, and offered her a hand up, which she took after a pause that managed to contain both suspicion and grace. "Come on. Tide's coming up the gorge — such as it is — and we need to float her before dark. Tomorrow we make Sarn's Reach by midday, and I want to buy every coil of oakum that town owns."

"Sarn's Reach," Wren repeated. Something in her voice had shifted — a wariness sharpening into something closer to dread. "You've been there?"

"Once. Why?"

She looked downriver, into the gathering dark, and for a long moment said nothing at all. When she finally spoke, the rain had thickened and her voice came low beneath it.

"Because the last time I saw it, it belonged to people who make dockside saboteurs look like choirboys. And if they remember me half as well as I remember them—" She stopped. Started again. "We should be off this bar before full dark. Trust me on that."

Dominic looked at her — this woman who paid double, answered sideways, and flinched at the names of towns — and felt the day's exhaustion rearrange itself into something less comfortable.

"What exactly," he said, "did you walk away from at Sarn's Reach?"

Wren picked up the mallet and held it out to him, handle first, like a truce or a warning.

"Ask me tomorrow," she said. "If we're still floating."
