## 8. Salvagers at Sarn's Reach

Dominic saw them first — three longboats tucked into the shadow of the cliffs at Sarn's Reach, hulls painted the gray-green of river water so they'd disappear against any bank. He'd grown up around men who hid boats like that, and he'd spent ten years being one of them.

"Company," he said.

Wren looked up from the chart she'd been annotating in pencil. "Friendly company?"

"Nobody hides friendly."

The Reach itself was a mile of slack water where the gorge briefly relented — a gravel shelf, a few leaning palms, and the kind of stillness that made Dominic's skin itch. Places like this existed so that people could do things to each other without witnesses. He cut the engine and let them drift, buying time, watching the longboats for movement.

They got movement. A woman rose from the bow of the nearest boat as if she'd been waiting all morning for the pleasure, which she probably had. She was tall, broad-shouldered, with iron-gray hair cropped close to her skull and a machete hanging at her hip with the casual authority of a wristwatch. She raised one hand — not a wave, exactly. More like a summons.

"That's Odile Voss," Wren said quietly.

"You know her?"

"By reputation. She runs salvage on this stretch of the Velloran. Half pirate, half debt collector." Wren paused. "Mostly debt collector."

"Wonderful. Which are we?"

Wren didn't answer, which he took to mean: *we're about to find out.*

* * *

Wren had heard stories about Odile Voss for years — back when she and Marcus had been mapping tributaries together, other pilots would mention her name the way sailors mention weather. *Voss took a barge off the Serpent's Teeth last spring. Voss recovered a drowned church bell and sold it back to the parish.* The stories never agreed on whether she was a menace or a public service. Wren suspected the answer depended entirely on whether you owed her money.

Now the longboats closed around them with practiced ease, and Voss stepped aboard without asking permission, her boots finding the deck of the *Merry Adeline* — Marcus's old survey boat, which was what it was, though Dominic insisted on calling it a charter — like she owned the planks already.

"Fairweather," she said. Not a question. She looked him over once, head to boots, the way an assessor looks at cargo. "You have his jaw. And his habit of arriving late."

"I don't know you," Dominic said.

"No," Voss agreed. "You don't. That's rather the whole of my complaint." She turned to Wren, and something flickered across her face — recognition, then a kind of grim satisfaction. "And Dr. Calloway. Well. The expedition collects its ghosts."

"We're not with the expedition," Wren said carefully.

"No. You're chasing it. Same thing, from where I stand." Voss settled herself on the gunwale as though invited, which she was not, and gestured at her crew — six of them now lining the rail, armed with machetes, two shotguns, and one man holding a fishing spear with genuine affection. "Here is how today goes. You'll hear me out, because I know where your father anchored last, and I'm the only soul on this river who does. In exchange, we'll discuss what he owes me. Nobody bleeds unless somebody's stupid. I've met both of you, so I'm hopeful."

"She's charming," Dominic muttered.

"I've been told," said Voss.

* * *

They put ashore on the gravel shelf because Voss preferred negotiating on solid ground — "boats tip, people lie, gravel keeps everyone honest" — and because, Dominic suspected, she liked choosing the terrain. Her crew built a small fire and started cooking rice with the unbothered efficiency of people who did this every week. One of them offered Wren a tin cup of sweet tea. She took it. It seemed unwise not to.

"So," Dominic said. "Talk."

Voss crouched by the fire, machete across her knees, and told it plainly, without theater, which somehow made it worse.

"Six weeks ago your father came downriver to Sarn's Reach with a survey crew, twenty-two souls, and a contract. Hired my boats to run supply and haul equipment above the gorge — water too shallow and fast for his own vessel past the Bells. Good pay. Half up front." She turned a stick in the coals. "Marcus Fairweather's name opens doors on this river. His foundation's money has always been good. So we worked three weeks. Ran fuel, ran food, ran crates he wouldn't let us look inside — fine, not our business. Then the monsoon forecast turned ugly, and instead of pulling out, he pushed further up. And one morning his assistant comes down with a message: contract's ended, thanks very much, payment will follow through the foundation office."

"Let me guess," Dominic said.

"It hasn't followed." Voss's voice stayed level, but the firelight caught something hard underneath it. "Three weeks of labor, two boats, wear and tear, and a man lost overboard at the second rapids who left a widow in Kessarin. The foundation office says no contract exists in their records. Signed paper or not, Fairweather's word used to be worth more than paper. Now his son sits in front of me, and I find I'm interested in family rates."

"He's not my partner," Dominic said. "I haven't spoken to the man in nine years."

"Then you can appreciate," Voss said, "how little that moves me."

* * *

Wren watched Dominic absorb it — the set of his shoulders, the way his thumb found the scar on his knuckle and worried it. He was doing arithmetic; she could see it. Not just about money. About what it meant that Marcus had stiffed a salvage crew, cut loose twenty-two people ahead of a monsoon, gone dark upriver with crates nobody was allowed to open.

It fit the man Wren had testified against. It also, uncomfortably, didn't. The Marcus she'd known was arrogant, secretive, and careless with people's feelings — but he'd paid his crews. He'd carried a porter's pack when the man twisted an ankle and never mentioned it afterward. Debts were the one religion he'd kept.

"The widow in Kessarin," Wren said. "What's owed her?"

Voss glanced at her, reassessing. "Four hundred, plus passage home. The rest of the claim — wages, boats, damages — runs to about nine thousand."

"And if the foundation won't pay?"

"Then I collect from whoever profits from whatever's up there." Voss smiled without warmth. "Which brings us to the interesting part. Because here's what I know that you don't, children. Your expedition didn't anchor at the mouth of the Karst channel like every sensible crew would. They went up the old flood channel — the one that drowns twice a day and eats propellers. Last anchorage is a gravel bar under the overhang at Coiled Root, thirty-one miles up. There's a deadfall marker, two trunks crossed. My pilot watched them set it."

"Why would they hide their own anchorage?" Dominic asked.

"That," said Voss, "is the question I've been chewing for three weeks. Crews hide anchorages when they expect visitors. Or when they've found something worth hiding." She poked the fire. "Either way, it cost me a boat to learn it. One of mine ran the channel behind them against my orders. She's still there, wedged on the bar, minus her fuel drum, which they helped themselves to. Add it to the bill."

Dominic laughed — short, disbelieving. "So he stole your boat's fuel too."

"He did. And here's the part that should interest you most, Fairweather." Voss leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for the first time her voice dropped its ledger-keeper's flatness. "When my pilot limped back down, he said the camp was quiet. Too quiet. No generator, no radio traffic, and half the tents struck. Like they were packing in a hurry — or like half of them had already gone somewhere else." She let that sit. "Your father's missing check-ins started eleven days ago. Whatever he's into, it's deeper than a survey. And whatever it is, I intend to be paid for my part in it."

* * *

Dominic walked to the waterline while the terms got argued, because Wren had volunteered — volunteered eagerly, with a glint he distrusted — to handle negotiation, and because he needed a minute with the river.

Nine thousand. The number wasn't the problem. The problem was the shape of the story. A father who skipped check-ins, hid his anchorage, poached fuel, and abandoned a salvage crew unpaid — that was a man coming apart, or a man running from something. Neither version left much room for the third possibility Dominic kept refusing to say aloud even in his own head: that Marcus might simply be dead, and this would all resolve into paperwork and a grave he'd be expected to stand beside.

He crouched and put his hand in the current. Cold, fast, indifferent. Somewhere upstream, thirty-one miles and a drowning channel away, his father had crossed two tree trunks and disappeared.

"You're brooding," Wren said, appearing beside him with the tea she'd apparently saved for him. Sweet, over-steeped, terrible. He drank it anyway.

"I'm calculating."

"You're brooding with numbers. I've seen you do it." She nodded back toward the fire, where Voss sat listening to Wren's own crew-less silence with the patience of a cat at a mousehole. "She's not unreasonable. She wants the widow paid first — she led with that, before her own wages. That tells you something about her."

"It tells me she knows which debts play well in front of witnesses."

"It tells me she's a person, Dominic, not a toll gate." Wren wrapped both hands around her cup. "And she knows the channel. We need her."

"We need *information*. There's a difference."

"Is there? When's the last time you navigated Coiled Root at flood stage?" When he didn't answer: "Right. Me neither. Nobody has, except her pilot, and he's standing over there eating rice."

Dominic looked at the fire, at Voss, at the six armed salvagers who had appeared out of cliff-shadow like a bad omen with good manners. Then at Wren, who was watching him with that expression she'd perfected — the one that said she'd already decided and was merely permitting him the fiction of choice.

"What did you offer her?" he asked.

"A share. Contingent on recovery, capped at her documented losses, with the widow's portion guaranteed off the top regardless of outcome." Wren sipped her tea. "Also I promised her first refusal on salvage rights to any vessel we recover, and I got her to knock four hundred off for the fuel drum, since technically her pilot violated her own orders going up the channel, making the loss partly self-inflicted."

There was a pause.

"Remind me never to buy a boat from you."

"You couldn't afford me." She handed him the last of the tea. "Terms are drafted. She wants your signature, since you're piloting. And Dominic — she asked one condition beyond the money."

"Which is?"

"If we find Marcus alive, she gets five minutes with him. Face to face. Before anyone else." Wren's voice was careful. "She says a man who leaves a widow ought to explain himself to somebody with a machete."

Despite everything, despite the leak in the hull and the monsoon stacking up over the mountains like a debt of its own, Dominic felt his mouth twitch. "That might be the most reasonable thing anyone's said about my father in nine years."

* * *

They signed on the flat of Voss's ammunition crate, using a pencil stub her clerk produced from somewhere improbable. Voss read the terms twice, initialed each page, and shook Dominic's hand with a grip like a mooring cleat.

"Pleasure doing business," she said, and meant approximately forty percent of it.

Her crew broke camp in minutes, longboats sliding back into the cliff-shadow, and then it was just the two of them again on the gravel with the *Merry Adeline* tugging at her line and thirty-one miles of bad water between them and a deadfall marker made of crossed trees.

Wren stood at the bow with the chart, marking the channel entrance. Dominic stood at the wheel. Above the gorge, the light had gone the color of wet slate, and the air had that thick, held-breath quality it got before the sky made good on its threats.

"One thing I keep turning over," Wren said, not looking up from her pencil work. "Twenty-two people, half the tents struck, packing in a hurry. Where would the other half go? There's nothing up there but the channel and the karst country past it."

"Maybe they evacuated downriver. Maybe we passed them."

"In the last three days? On this stretch? We've seen two fishing skiffs and a dead dog floating with tremendous dignity." She finally looked at him. "They went up, Dominic. Whatever split that camp, half of it went *deeper*."

Dominic fed fuel to the engine and pointed the bow at the narrowing gorge, where the water had already begun to quicken, and thought about crossed trees, and stolen fuel, and a widow four hundred dollars poorer, and a father who had always been best at leaving.

"Coiled Root," he said. "Hold on to something."

The river, as if it had been waiting for the invitation, took them.
