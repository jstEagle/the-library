## 9. The Last Anchorage

Odile Voss gave them the directions the way a gambler lays down a card she's already counted twice — face up, but not quite all at once.

"Past the second confluence," she said, standing on her deck with her arms crossed and the river sliding brown and heavy behind her. "You'll know it because the east bank falls away into clay and the water goes the color of old tea. Keep north after that. There's an oxbow where the current doubles back on itself — lazy piece of water, good for anchoring. Your father's people moored there three weeks ago. I'd stake my wages on it." A pause, dry as gunpowder. "Which is to say I have nothing left to lose."

"You could have come with us," Wren said.

Voss looked at her the way one looks at a suggestion to swim the channel in flood season. "I don't chase ghosts, professor. I chase debts. If your archaeologist surfaces, I'll find him. Until then—" she flicked two fingers off her brow in something between a salute and a dismissal, "—good luck. You'll need more than you're carrying."

Dominic had said almost nothing through the whole exchange. He was watching the way she never quite looked at him directly, the way people avoid looking at a man when they've decided he resembles his father. It happened more than he liked. In ports, in markets, once from a customs officer who squinted at his papers and then at his face and said, *Fairweather? Any relation?* as if the name were a disease you caught by blood.

They cast off before noon. The monsoon had gone sullen rather than violent — a low gray ceiling pressing down on the gorge walls, rain that fell in long patient sheets instead of hammering blows, the kind of weather that made you feel the sky had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

The *Kestrel* — Wren had asked the boat's name on the first day out of habit, and Dominic had told her, and she'd written it in the margin of her chart like it mattered — pushed upstream against a current that fought like a living thing. Dominic kept her nose into the worst of it, throttling up through the chutes and easing back in the slack water behind every point bar, reading the river the way other men read faces.

Wren sat in the bow with the charts spread across her knees under an oilskin, annotating. She'd been quiet since Sarn's Reach, working through something. He let her work. He'd learned long ago that silence shared with someone who wasn't demanding anything from it was rarer than clean drinking water out here, and worth savoring.

It lasted most of the afternoon.

"You didn't ask her about the pay," Wren said finally, without looking up.

"What?"

"Voss. She claimed Marcus hired her crew and cut them loose without paying. You didn't ask whether it was true. Most men would've wanted to know if their father was a deadbeat."

Dominic fed a little more throttle into a bend where the current piled up against the outside bank. "Most men would've wanted a lot of things."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I give free of charge."

He felt her look at the back of his head. He kept his eyes on the water. The truth was that he hadn't asked because he already believed it — believed it in the specific, tired way you believe a thing about your father, which is to say instantly and without surprise and with a small hot coal of shame lodged somewhere under the ribs for believing it so easily. Marcus Fairweather had a genius for beginnings. It was the middles and ends he'd always left for other people to sort out.

The second confluence announced itself exactly as promised: the east bank slumped into raw red clay, and the water darkened to the color of steeped tea, tannins bleeding out of drowned forest. Dominic took the northern channel. The gorge walls began to lean in closer here, older rock, black stone streaked white where centuries of runoff had painted their own slow maps.

"There," Wren said, pointing.

The oxbow opened on their port side like a cupped hand — a wide, still lagoon cut off from the main current by a crescent of gravel and fallen timber. The water in it lay flat as poured glass, holding the gray sky perfectly, so that for a moment Dominic thought they were about to drive the boat straight into the clouds.

And there, at the head of the lagoon, on a shelf of ground above the high-water mark: the camp.

Or what was left of it.

* * *

He brought the *Kestrel* in slow, backing down on the gravel shelf with the engine ticking over, and killed the motor while they were still ten feet out. The silence that followed was enormous. Rain ticked on the canopy. Somewhere upstream, water fell over stone in a long unbroken note.

The tents were gone. That was the first thing. Not torn down, not scattered — *gone*, poles and canvas both, leaving behind a constellation of peg holes in the mud and rectangles of paler grass where floors had been. The fire pit was cold and neatly filled. And along the shelf, stacked with a care that made the hair rise on Dominic's arms, stood the equipment: crates lashed and labeled, coils of rope hung off a frame of cut saplings to keep them off the wet ground, survey instruments nested in their cases, even the expedition's spare fuel drums, bunged tight and arranged in a row like soldiers at attention.

Nobody abandons a camp like this. Nobody leaves in panic and labels the crates.

"They evacuated," Wren said quietly. She'd come to stand beside him at the rail without him hearing her move. "In order. On purpose."

"Evacuated from what?"

She didn't answer, because neither of them knew, and the not-knowing sat down between them like a third passenger.

They went ashore. The mud took their boots to the ankle, and the air smelled of wet stone and crushed vegetation and, faintly, of woodsmoke gone cold days ago. Dominic walked the camp the way he'd once walked salvage sites — methodical, hands in pockets, cataloguing. His father had taught him that too, though neither of them would have claimed it now: *read the ground, boy, the ground never lies, only people lie about the ground.*

The ground said: twenty-odd people, maybe more. Said: they left carrying what mattered and leaving what could be spared. Said: no blood, no scuffle, no burned documents. Said: they had time.

Said, in a set of boot prints half-heeled with new rain, that some of them had gone inland, up the slope, toward the black wall of the gorge forest.

Wren found the page.

He heard her make a small sound — not a word, just a note, the sound of someone finding a thing they'd been afraid to hope for — and turned to see her crouched at the base of the largest crate stack, where a flat stone the size of a dinner plate sat squarely on top of a folded sheet of paper weighted against the wind.

She didn't touch it. She looked up at him instead, and her face did something complicated. "Dominic."

He crossed the mud in four strides. She stepped aside. He knelt, slid the stone off with both hands — it was heavier than it looked, chosen deliberately, chosen to last — and lifted the page.

The paper was good stock, foundation letterhead, the kind Marcus had used for thirty years. The handwriting was his father's. Dominic knew it the way you know a voice through a wall: the sharp decisive capitals, the small impatient crossbars, the letters that leaned forward like a man walking fast. He had spent nine years not looking at that handwriting, and here it was anyway, arriving through his fingertips before his eyes had finished the first word.

Two lines. Only two.

*Moving inland. D. should understand why.*

No signature. No date. No map reference, no list, no instruction. Just that. Weighted under a stone in an empty camp at the edge of the flooded world, addressed to a son who hadn't answered a letter in nine years.

*D.*

Not *Dominic*. Not *my son*. *D.* — the way Marcus had called across dig sites and dinner tables and train platforms, shorthand for a person he'd assumed would always be within shouting distance. As if nine years were a long weekend. As if the boy who'd walked out of the house in Portsmouth saying *find someone else to dig up your dead* might simply be standing by, waiting, ready to be consulted.

Dominic became aware that he was gripping the page hard enough to crease it, and made himself loosen his fingers.

"He wrote to you," Wren said. It wasn't a question. She'd read it over his shoulder; there'd been no way not to.

"He wrote *at* me," Dominic said. "There's a difference."

"Dominic—"

"Why?" It came out harder than he meant it to, and he heard it land on her and didn't take it back. "That's what I want to know. Why *should* I understand? Understand what? He doesn't say where. He doesn't say how many. He doesn't say if anyone's hurt, or what chased them inland, or why he couldn't spare five more words for—" He stopped. The rain filled the pause, patient, indifferent.

"For his son," Wren finished softly.

"For anyone," Dominic said. "For anyone at all."

But that was a lie, and they both knew it, and the knowing sat in the space between them, humming. Because the message wasn't for anyone. It was for him. Of all the people who might pass through this camp — porters, salvagers, Renner's men, the river itself — Marcus had weighted a stone over a page and written *D. should understand why*, and that single letter changed the whole shape of the thing. It meant his father had thought about him here, in this place, in whatever hour had preceded the moving-inland. Had thought about him and written to him and trusted him — *trusted* him — to follow.

Hope arrived so suddenly it hurt, sharp under the breastbone, unwelcome as a fishhook. Because hope meant expecting. Expecting meant caring. Caring meant that everything his father failed to do for twenty years could be done to him all over again in the space of one bad monsoon.

"We should search the rest of it," he said, standing, folding the page into his inside pocket where it lay against his chest like a coal. "Before the light goes."

* * *

They searched until the gray afternoon thickened toward dusk, and the searching turned up less than either of them wanted and more than either of them expected.

Wren worked the equipment stacks with a professional's hands, checking inventory against the manifest she'd carried in her head for years. "Survey transit — left behind, too heavy. Water testing kit — gone. Rock saw — gone. Blasting permits—" she paused over an empty document wallet, frowning, "—taken. Who takes blasting permits and leaves the explosives?" She held up a case of detonators, untouched, dry, sealed.

"People planning to come back for them," Dominic said. "Or people who already have what they need."

He found the cook's station: a spirit stove, a rack of empty tins stacked for packing, a knife driven point-down into the cutting block — left behind, which no cook alive does lightly. He found the latrine trench properly filled. He found, half-buried at the edge of the shelf, a child's marble, glass, blue, which made no sense at all until he remembered that some of the local porters brought their families' tokens on long contracts, and put it in his pocket without deciding to.

And near the inland trailhead, where the boot prints converged and climbed, he found the thing that ended the argument about whether they were going to sleep aboard the *Kestrel* or up on the slope.

A tin lantern, modern, expedition-issue, hanging from a cut branch at eye height. Inside it, a stub of candle, burned down. Beside it, nailed to the branch with a piton, a strip of cloth torn from a shirt — white, once — marked with a single charred symbol Dominic didn't recognize, though something about its geometry nagged at him, a spiral broken by a straight line, like a path interrupted.

"A marker," Wren said behind him. "Trail sign. They're telling anyone who follows which way they went."

"Or telling Renner's people," Dominic said. "If Renner's people are the ones who followed."

"You think this is bait?"

"I think," Dominic said slowly, "that my father has spent forty years thinking three moves ahead of everyone on any river he's ever touched, and I think a man like that doesn't leave a trail unless he wants it followed. The question is by whom." He looked up the slope, where the forest stood black and dripping and utterly indifferent, swallowing the trail thirty feet in. "And I think the answer is supposed to be me."

Wren was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had lost its academic polish and gone plain. "Then we go up."

"At dusk. In rising water. With half our supplies and a boat we can't defend."

"Yes."

He looked at her. Rain ran off the brim of her hood and she didn't wipe it away, just stood there with her chin up and her jaw set, a woman who had testified against his father and lied about it and crossed half a flooded country to fix it, and who was asking him — not telling him, asking — to walk into the dark after a man who had broken both their lives in different currencies.

"All right," Dominic said.

They went back down to the boat for the night — prudence, he insisted, and Wren didn't argue, because even the impatient know you don't climb into unknown country without light or dry socks. They ate tinned food heated on the spirit stove they'd liberated from the camp, sitting in the wheelhouse with the rain drumming the roof, and neither of them talked much, and the not-talking was different from the earlier kind. Fuller. Like a room with furniture in it now.

The journal page lay on the chart table between them. Twice, Dominic reached for it. Twice, he made himself stop.

The third time, Wren slid it across to him without a word, and he read it again under the lamp, and the letters leaned forward like a man walking fast, and *D. should understand why* sat in his chest like a question he'd been carrying since he was nineteen years old and had only just now been handed the bill for.

Why. Why inland. Why silent. Why the neat stacks and the weighted stone and the trail markers and the trust. Why, after nine years of nothing, *D.*

He looked up and found Wren watching him across the little lamplit table, her face tired and open in a way it hadn't been since the docks, and she said:

"Why you?"

The rain came down on the roof of the wheelhouse, steady as breathing, and the black forest waited up the slope, and somewhere inland — hours, maybe a day, maybe less — a man who signed his letters with a single letter was walking fast, and had left the answer weighted under a stone for whoever loved him enough to pick it up.

Dominic folded the page closed.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Ask me tomorrow."

But they both knew he didn't have an answer. Not yet. And that the river, which never hurried and never stopped, was going to make him find one.
