## 10. Why Me

The rain stopped an hour after dark, which was the first kind thing the day had done for them.

Wren Calloway sat on an overturned supply crate with her boots off and her socks steaming gently over a fire no larger than a dinner plate, because Dominic Fairweather had opinions about fires — that they should be small, smokeless, and built in a hollow lined with wet stone so the heat reflected instead of escaped. He'd built it without being asked, without comment, the way he did everything: quickly, competently, and as though anyone who noticed might charge him rent for the noticing.

She had known his father for eleven years, and Marcus Fairweather had never once built a small fire in his life. Marcus built fires like monuments. Marcus built fires you could navigate by.

The son, apparently, built fires like apologies.

Dominic was at the boat's edge, twenty feet down the bank, rinsing silt out of the fuel filter by lantern light. The *Kestrel* — she'd finally learned the boat's name, pried it out of him on the second day like a splinter — lay moored against the bank with her nose into the current, riding low and grumbling. Tomorrow they would leave her with a watchman and go inland on foot, following a trail of boot prints and cut vines and one cryptic sentence weighted under a stone.

*Moving inland. D. should understand why.*

Wren turned the page over in her mind for perhaps the hundredth time since the abandoned camp. She had read it four times before Dominic took it from her hands — not snatched, exactly, but taken with a care that told her everything about how much it cost him. She'd watched him read it. Watched his jaw do something complicated. Watched him fold the page once, precisely, and put it in the inside pocket of his jacket, over the left breast, where a man keeps things he intends to think about later whether he wants to or not.

*D.*

Not "whoever finds this." Not "to the party following." A letter. An initial. An address.

She had been turning that over for two days now, and tonight, with the rain gone and the fire ticking softly and nothing between them but the river's mutter and the enormous indifferent dark of the Velloran basin, she decided she was done turning it over alone.

"Dominic."

He didn't look up from the filter. "Filter's nearly clear. We'll have decent pressure tomorrow if I—"

"Why you?"

The rinsing stopped.

She watched his shoulders take the question the way a man takes a blow he saw coming from across the room. He set the filter down on a flat stone with exaggerated care, the way people do when they're buying themselves three seconds, and stood, and wiped his hands on his trousers, and did not turn around.

"Because I'm the pilot," he said. "He needed someone who knew these waterways. It's practical."

"It's addressed to you by initial. On a page he weighted under a rock in an abandoned camp." She kept her voice level, almost idle. "Marcus Fairweather wrote forty-page monographs on ceramic typologies. When he wanted to be practical, he footnoted. That page wasn't practical. That page was personal, and he knew exactly who would be reading it."

"You don't know that."

"I knew him for eleven years."

"And I've known him for thirty-one," Dominic said, "and I'm telling you it's practical," and then he heard himself, heard the absurdity of it, and finally turned around. In the lantern light his face did something she hadn't seen it do yet — dropped its working expression entirely, like a mask set down on a table.

Wren put another stick on the fire. Small gesture. Invitation without demand. She'd learned that trick from watching Marcus fail at it for a decade; he'd never once managed to ask anyone anything without making it sound like a summons.

Dominic came up the bank slowly. He didn't sit on the crate beside her. He sat on the ground opposite, forearms on his knees, close enough to the fire to be warm and far enough to be leaving.

"He didn't write to me," Wren said. "Did you know that? Nine years. Not a letter, not a note, not so much as a postcard of some ruin with 'thought of you' scrawled on the back. And then a month ago—" She stopped. That was a card she wasn't ready to play. "Nothing. Silence. And then this expedition goes quiet, and the first thing he leaves behind is a message with your initial on it."

"Maybe it's not a message. Maybe it's just—"

"*D. should understand why.*" She quoted it back at him gently. "Understand *why*, Dominic. Why what? Why he moved inland? Why the camp was stripped? Or why he spent nine years not writing to whoever D. is?" She tilted her head. "You've been carrying that page around like it's a live coal. You know what it means. You just don't want to say it out loud, because saying it out loud makes it a thing we both have to deal with instead of a thing only you have to deal with."

"That," Dominic said, "is a very Marcus observation."

It landed harder than she'd meant it to, and she watched it land. His mouth went flat. For a moment she thought he'd get up and go back to the filter, back to the river, back to any task that couldn't talk back.

Instead he laughed. One short, unamused breath of a laugh, but a laugh.

"You want to know why me?" he said. "Fine. Here's why me. Because I haven't spoken to the man in nine years. Not a word. Not since the night I told him I wouldn't join the great family enterprise of digging up dead civilizations and putting them in glass cases for people who collect them." He fed a twig to the fire with more force than the twig required. "Nine years, Wren. He missed my mother's funeral — well. Everyone missed that, she made sure of it. But he missed everything else. My graduation. My first command. The year the *Kestrel* nearly sank under me in the Sarn delta and I spent two days clinging to the mast thinking, *if I live through this, I'll call him.* I lived. I didn't call."

"Why not?"

"Because calling means—" He stopped. Started again. "Because he taught me that people leave. It's the first thing he ever taught me, before he taught me to read strata. He'd pack a kit, kiss my mother on the cheek, ruffle my hair, and be gone four months, six months, once eleven. And every time he came back he'd sit me down with artifacts and tell me about the wonderful things in the ground, as if the ground were where all the wonderful things lived, and I was supposed to be grateful for the souvenirs." He looked up. The firelight caught the anger in his face, but underneath the anger, plain as a coin at the bottom of clear water, was something else. "So when I left, I made sure I did it properly. Thoroughly. I'm told I have a gift for it."

The river muttered. Somewhere up the gorge, water fell a long way onto stone.

"You're angry," Wren said.

"Brilliant deduction. Eleven years as a research partner and it shows."

"You're angry," she repeated, "the way a man is angry at a door that won't open. All that force, and none of it does anything, and you keep pushing anyway because the alternative is admitting the door isn't locked — it's just closed, and it's been closed the whole time, and you could have knocked."

Dominic stared at her.

"I testified against your father," Wren said quietly. "I'm aware I don't have standing to say any of this. But I watched him for eleven years, and I'll tell you what I saw, since you asked why you. Every dig, every site, every paper — whenever there was a decision worth making, he'd pause. Half a second. Like he was checking whether there was someone he wished were there to make it with him. I used to think it was my mother he was missing. Then I met her at a symposium in Corvane and realized she'd been dead eight years. It took me longer than it should have to work out it was a boy he used to teach to read dirt."

"Don't," Dominic said.

"—and every time he paused, he'd decide alone, and the decision would be slightly wrong, and he'd know it was slightly wrong, and he'd never say why—"

"I said *don't*."

His voice cracked on the second word. Not loudly. Just a hairline fracture, the way good timber splits — clean, sudden, along a grain that had been there all along.

Wren shut her mouth. She pulled her socks off the warming stones and put them on, giving him something to look away from while he reassembled himself, which was the other trick she'd learned from watching Marcus fail at kindness: sometimes the best thing you can offer a man is the pretense that he isn't crying.

He wasn't crying. But it was close enough to the border that customs officials would have asked questions.

"The night I left," Dominic said at last, to the fire, "he followed me out to the truck. Stood in the rain — of course it was raining, it's always raining in family catastrophes — and he said, 'You'll come back to it. The past doesn't let go of people like us.' Like it was a promise. Like it was *comforting*." He shook his head. "And I drove away thinking, I will prove you wrong. I will find something in the present worth staying for, and I will stay, and I will never once look back at the ground."

"How's that going?"

"Ask the boat," he said bitterly. "Named after a bird that migrates. I bought her used from a man in Sarn who asked me one question — 'you staying long?' — and when I said no, he knocked a third off the price. That's the whole of my life's philosophy, Wren. Never stay long enough to owe anyone anything. Never own anything you can't carry. Never love anything that can't be sold by morning." He gestured at the dark basin around them, the drowned gorges, the trail of boot prints leading inland after a father he claimed to hate. "And here I am. Chasing the one man alive I swore I'd never chase, up a river nobody sane sails in monsoon season, because of a scrap of paper with my initial on it."

"Yes," Wren said. "I had noticed."

"So when you ask *why me* — that's why me. Because I'm the idiot. Because he knew I'd come. He knew it the way he knows stratigraphy. Put enough layers down, press hard enough, long enough, and eventually the pattern tells you what's underneath." He looked at her, and the anger was still there, but it had gone transparent, the way anger does when grief finally gets its boots on. "He left that page because he knew his son couldn't leave a mystery alone. Even a mystery shaped like him. *Especially* one shaped like him. It's not a message, Wren. It's bait. And I took it, and I hate him for knowing I would."

The fire ticked. Downstream, an animal Wren couldn't name made a sound like a hinge.

"Do you want to know the worst part?" Dominic said.

"I suspect you're going to tell me regardless."

"The worst part is that I read that page and my first feeling — before worry, before dread, before any of the respectable feelings — was *he thought of me*. First time in nine years, and he thought of me, and I was—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "I was glad. Standing in that ruined camp with everything stripped and the flood coming, I was *glad*, like a dog hearing its name after years of strangers. And then I remembered I'm furious with him, and I've been furious with him so long I don't actually remember what I'm like without it, and now I'm standing in the middle of nowhere wondering who I even am if the anger turns out to be—" He lowered his hand. "If it turns out I was wrong about him."

Wren let the silence sit for a while. It deserved the dignity.

"For what it's worth," she said finally, "I don't think you were wrong. I think you were half right, which is worse. He did choose the ground over the living. Repeatedly. Catastrophically. I have the career wreckage to prove it." She held her palms to the small fire. "But I also think men like Marcus don't know how to knock. They only know how to build doors and wait on the far side, hoping someone walks through, and then pretend they weren't waiting. That page under the stone? That's Marcus knocking. Badly. From the wrong side. With a rock."

A muscle moved in Dominic's jaw. It might have been the beginning of a smile, ambushed halfway.

"You make him sound pathetic."

"He was many things. Pathetic was never one of them. Stubborn, brilliant, cowardly in exactly one direction, generous in every other — yes. Pathetic, no." She reached for the kettle and poured hot water over the last of their tea leaves, filling two chipped tin cups. She handed him one. He took it. Progress, she noted, could be measured in tin cups accepted. "He waited nine years to reach out, and when he finally did, he did it the only way he trusted — buried, coded, addressed to the one person whose curiosity outweighed his resentment. That's not pathetic. That's a man who knows his son better than his son thinks he does."

"Stop analyzing me through him. It's unsettling."

"Noted. I'll analyze you directly henceforth. You hold your cup like it might invoice you."

That got the smile the rest of the way out — brief, crooked, gone again, but real. He drank his tea. The fire settled. Above the gorge walls, through a tear in the cloud, a few stars appeared, indifferent and clean.

"Dominic," Wren said, after a time. "Tomorrow we walk inland after him. Before we do — I need to ask you something, and I need the true answer, not the pilot answer."

He eyed her over the rim of the cup. "That sounds ominous."

"If we find him. If he's alive, and we find him, and he starts to explain — the silence, the years, all of it — what do you want to happen?"

Dominic was quiet for a long moment. The river ran. The stars wheeled their slow invisible degrees.

"I want him to be sorry," he said at last. Simply. Almost quietly. "That's the shameful truth of it. I've spent nine years telling myself I want nothing from him, and it's a lie, and the lie has a name, and the name is *sorry*. One word. I want to hear it so badly I sailed a leaking boat into a monsoon to chase a man I supposedly despise." He turned the cup in his hands. "And I'm terrified that if he says it, I'll forgive him, because I'll realize the anger was the last piece of him I was still holding on to, and letting go of it means admitting I wanted the rest of him all along."

Wren nodded slowly. She thought of a letter she carried in her own kit, a month old, unread for the first week, read a hundred times since. *I am asking forgiveness, Wren, not because I deserve it, but because I have finally learned that pride is just grief wearing a good coat.*

"There's something I should probably tell you," she began—

—and stopped, because Dominic had gone rigid, his head cocked toward the inland trail, his cup frozen halfway to his mouth.

"What?" she whispered.

"Light," he breathed. "On the ridge. Moving. Two — no, three torches, spaced like a patrol." He set the cup down without a sound and was already reaching for the field glasses. "Nobody friendly travels at night in flood season, Wren. Nobody friendly travels at all."

Through the glasses, against the last starlight, three points of flame bobbed along the ridgeline above the trail they were meant to take at dawn — moving fast, moving together, moving the same direction as the boot prints.

Toward Marcus.

* * *

"We break camp now," Dominic said, already kicking soil over the fire. "They'll reach the ford by midmorning. If we move in the dark, we reach it first."

"In the dark? Through flooded karst country? You'll break an ankle, and then I'll have to carry you, and I've seen you eat — you're heavier than you look."

"Wren." He slung his pack, and in the dying firelight his face had shed every trace of the man who'd confessed to wanting one word of apology. This was the pilot now, the salvager, the son with a live coal in his pocket. "Someone's racing us to my father. Whatever else is true — whatever I feel about him — I am not letting strangers get to him first."

Wren grabbed her own pack, shoved her damp socks in the top, and followed him into the dark.

The anger, she noted, walked faster than grief ever could.
