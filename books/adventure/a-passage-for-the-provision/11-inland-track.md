## 11. Inland Track

The watchman's name was Teodor, and he had three teeth, a shotgun older than both of them put together, and the unshakable calm of a man who had been paid in advance.

"You'll be back in four days," he said. It was not a question. He was leaning against the mooring post of *Magpie's Rest* — the name painted on her stern by some previous owner with more optimism than Dominic had ever managed — and he was looking at the boat the way other men looked at their daughters' suitors: with polite, total suspicion.

"Three, if the trail's good," Dominic said.

"Four." Teodor spat thoughtfully into the brown water. "Floodwater rises. You come back, you swim to me." He patted the gun. "I wait."

Dominic decided that was as close to a guarantee as the Velloran basin offered, and paid him half again anyway. Money spent on peace of mind was the only money he'd never regretted, and besides, if someone came to strip the boat while they were gone, he wanted Teodor motivated rather than merely present.

Wren came down the bank with her pack already cinched, which told Dominic she'd been packed since before dawn. She'd slept aboard — or lain awake aboard, which amounted to the same posture — and there was a new quality to her this morning that he couldn't quite name until they'd been walking an hour. Then it came to him: she was lighter. Not physically. Something had been set down at the abandoned camp, some weight she'd been carrying since Sarn's Reach, and whatever it was, the journal page under its stone had taken part of it.

*D. should understand why.*

He still didn't understand why. But he understood, now, that his father had expected him. That was either the most comforting thing Marcus Fairweather had ever done or the most infuriating, and Dominic hadn't finished deciding which.

The trail began where the camp ended, a thread of trodden mud heading east into country that stopped pretending to be navigable by anything with a keel. The river behind them widened into a drowned forest — trees standing to their chests in water, their reflections so perfect that for the first ten minutes Dominic kept glancing down and feeling like he was walking along the edge of a second world, upside down and patient.

"Teodor's going to sell the engine," Wren said.

"He's going to guard the engine."

"He's going to guard the engine until someone offers him enough, and then he's going to help them carry it away, and he's going to feel bad about it for a whole afternoon." She stepped over a root without looking at it. "I've dealt with his type before. Advance payment buys presence, not loyalty."

"Then it's a good thing I only need presence."

"And if someone comes with more money than we've got?"

"Then I'll want to meet them," Dominic said, "because anyone spending that kind of cash on a leaking riverboat has worse problems than we do."

That earned him something from Wren — not quite a laugh, but the ghost of one, a small exhalation through the nose that he was beginning to collect like coins. Nine days ago she'd been a stranger at a dock offering him money he didn't want. Now she knew how he took his coffee — badly, black, from water boiled twice — and he knew that she talked in her sleep, once, briefly, saying *the dates don't lie, Marcus* in a voice that had made him sit up in the dark and stare at her for a long time before deciding not to ask.

There were things they didn't ask each other yet. The trail ahead of them was full of them.

* * *

By midmorning the floodwater found their boots.

It came gradually, the way bad news does — first a seep over the toe caps, then a cold grip at the ankles, then long stretches where the path existed only as a suggestion beneath six inches of moving water, and you walked by faith and by the occasional exposed root. The basin was filling. Somewhere upcountry the monsoon had begun its real work, and all this water was the vanguard, sent ahead to soften the ground and test the resolve of anyone foolish enough to be walking into it.

"Foolish" being the operative word, Dominic thought, and kept walking.

They found the first boot print forty minutes past the drowned fence line. It was pressed deep into the clay of a stream crossing, heel dug in, toe splayed — the print of a man carrying weight and hurrying. Beside it, smaller prints, lighter, quicker. And beyond those, a whole constellation of them, a dozen-odd people funneling through the same gap in the same fence, all headed the same direction, all in a hurry.

"They're a day ahead," Wren said, crouching. She touched the edge of the deepest print. "Maybe less. The edges haven't slumped much. Clay dries slow in this humidity, but floodwater softens them faster." She looked up. "Whoever's driving them is driving them hard."

"Or they're running from something."

"Same thing, sometimes."

Dominic crouched beside her and studied the pattern. His father had taught him tracking the way other fathers taught catch — patiently, on weekends, with commentary. *Read the story, not the print.* A boy of nine, kneeling in Wyoming dirt while a famous man pointed at a line of deer tracks and asked what happened here. *One deer stopped. Looked back. Then ran.* Why did it look back? *Because it heard something it wasn't sure about.* And what do we do when we're not sure? *We look back, Dom. Only fools and dead men don't look back.*

He'd forgotten he remembered any of that. It came back now with the smell of the clay, whole and unwanted, like a song from childhood.

"One group split here," he heard himself say. "See — most went east through the gap. But these three—" he touched the smaller prints, "—cut north along the stream. Lighter loads. Someone sent scouts, or someone bolted."

Wren looked at him differently then. Not with surprise exactly. With recalculation, the way she'd looked at him when he threaded the logjam upstream, reading the current instead of fighting it.

"You've done this before."

"I grew up around archaeologists. They track everything. Artifacts, grants, each other." He stood, knees wet, and deliberately brushed off the memory along with the mud. "Come on. If they're a day ahead and moving hard, we're losing light we can't afford."

* * *

The instrument case appeared just after noon, lying in the shallows of a flooded swale like something washed ashore.

It was brass-cornered leather, expedition-stamped, and when Dominic fished it out the latch fell open and water poured out of it along with a sextant that would never read another star. The glass was crazed. The arc was bent. Whatever had happened to it, it had happened fast and without ceremony — dropped, not lost. Nobody loses a sextant. A sextant is the last thing you lose.

Wren turned it over in her hands with an expression Dominic was learning to read as grief held at arm's length.

"This is Halvorsen's," she said quietly. "Erik Halvorsen. He ran navigation on the last three expeditions. He used to say he'd be buried with this thing." She tilted it, watching muddy water run out of the housing. "Marcus made him leave it behind."

"How do you know?"

"Because Marcus doesn't carry dead weight, and a man who won't abandon his instruments won't abandon them halfway." She closed the case gently, the way you'd close a casket. "He ordered it dropped. That means he ordered speed. That means—"

"That means whatever's inland matters more than the instruments." Dominic took the case and stowed it in his pack, because leaving it felt wrong and because Erik Halvorsen, whoever he was, deserved better than the flood. "And that means he knew we might follow. He's leaving sign whether he means to or not."

"Do you think he means to?" Wren asked. "The page said your initial. *D. should understand why.* Maybe the whole trail is a message. Boot prints, dropped gear — breadcrumbs."

"Breadcrumbs are for people who want to be found." Dominic shouldered his pack and started walking. "My father wants to be understood. There's a difference, and it's about forty years wide."

* * *

The vines were the strangest part.

In the late afternoon they entered a stretch where the trail climbed a low ridge above the floodplain, and the ridge was clothed in secondary growth — tangles of liana and wild yam vine thick as a man's wrist, the kind of growth that swallowed paths whole within a season. Someone had cut through it. Recently. The cuts were pale and clean-edged, sap still glossy at the seams, and they weren't hacked — they were trimmed. Neat diagonal strokes, waist height, in a corridor just wide enough for laden men walking single file.

"Machete work," Wren said, running her thumb along a cut. "But look at the spacing. One cutter, consistent angle, consistent height. This isn't twenty guys flailing. This is one person doing the work while the rest conserve their strength."

"A disciplined party."

"A well-led party." She glanced sideways at him. "Sound like anyone?"

Dominic didn't answer. He was looking at the cuts and thinking about a man who had once reorganized a dig site in the Atacama because the wheelbarrow routes were inefficient, and who had made the graduate students paint lines on the ground, and who had been right, and who had been insufferable about being right, and who had missed every one of Dominic's birthdays between the ages of eight and seventeen because the strata wouldn't excavate themselves.

Someone was moving fast and light through the Velloran basin, trimming vines like a gardener, and every detail of it was a signature.

"He's alive," Wren said. It was almost a whisper. She'd been holding that sentence, Dominic realized, since the abandoned camp — carrying it the way you carry a full cup across a crowded room. "Dominic. Whoever's cutting these, the crew discipline, the pace — he's alive, and he's moving, and he's *organized*. After the flooding, after whatever scattered them—"

"Don't."

She stopped. So did he, though he hadn't meant to. The rain chose that moment to arrive, a fine gray sift through the canopy, beading on everything.

"I'm not doing hope," Dominic said, to the vines rather than to her. "Hope is expensive and I'm not buying. We walk the trail, we find the camp or we find bones, and either way I'll deal with it then. In the meantime it's just terrain and weather and a boat tied up with a man named Teodor who may or may not still own our engine."

"That's not dealing with it. That's just refusing to look at it."

"Yes," Dominic agreed. "That's the technique. It's taken years of practice. My father taught me that too, actually — though probably not on purpose."

Something flickered across Wren's face — the old wound, the scandal, the testimony, all of it — and then she let it go, which was a skill of hers he was starting to envy. She pulled her hood up.

"For what it's worth," she said, starting forward again, "refusing to hope is also hope. Just hope wearing armor."

"And what's yours wearing?"

"Mine's wearing a very good argument," she said, "which I'm saving for later, when you're too tired to fight it."

Despite himself — despite everything — Dominic laughed. It came out rusty, underused, startling a bird out of the understory, and the bird's alarm call rattled away through the dripping green like a struck bell. He noticed Wren smiling at the sound of it, quickly, the way you smile at something you're not supposed to have seen.

They walked on.

* * *

Dusk came early under the storm clouds, the way it always did in the basin — not a sunset so much as a dimming, the world turned down like a lamp on a rheostat. They made camp on the ridge top, the one dry-ish ground for miles, and ate cold rice and smoked fish while the rain worked itself up from a sift to a drumming.

Dominic took first watch, because he always did, because sleep was a country he visited reluctantly and rarely stayed in long. He sat under a lean-to of palm fronds with the rifle across his knees and watched the darkness eat the floodplain below, and listened to the rain, and let himself think the thoughts he'd been outrunning all day.

A day ahead. Twelve, maybe fourteen people. Moving organized, moving fast, cutting clean trails, dropping sextants. Headed inland toward — what? The ruins his father had hunted for twenty years, the ones the foundation's maps showed as blank space and rumor. And somewhere behind all of it, the question the journal page had planted like a splinter:

*D. should understand why.*

Why the expedition went dark. Why the camp was stripped neat as a parade ground. Why the message was addressed to a son who hadn't spoken to his father in nine years — who had, the last time they spoke, said things that could not be unsaid, in a kitchen that smelled of coffee and old paper, while Marcus stood by the window with his back turned like a man listening for a train.

*You dig up the dead because the living bore you,* Dominic had said. Seventeen words. He'd counted them often enough since.

Behind him, Wren shifted in her bedroll. "Anything?"

"Rain."

"Anything else?"

He considered. "Boot prints, a broken sextant, and a gardener with a machete," he said. "And a day's gap that's getting wider."

"Then tomorrow we walk faster." Her voice was muffled, half-asleep already, but there was iron under it. "And Dominic — the gap's not getting wider. Flood slows everyone. It'll slow them more than us. They're carrying equipment and frightened people. We're carrying spite and a grudge." A pause. "Spite weighs nothing. Best cargo there is."

He almost smiled. Almost.

An hour into his watch, the rain eased, and in the easing he heard it — faint, from the east, carried on the wind off the floodplain. Not an animal. Not water. A voice, human, distant, calling out once and falling silent. Then, after a long minute, another voice answering it, farther off, higher up.

Two voices. Calling to each other in the dark. Checking on each other.

The party ahead was close. Closer than a day. Closer than they should be, unless they'd stopped — unless something had stopped them.

Dominic sat very still in the dripping dark, rifle across his knees, and watched the eastern ridgeline where the voices had gone, and felt the pursuit turn in his chest from a walk into a pull.

Tomorrow they'd find out what had slowed twelve frightened people to a standstill in the middle of the rising flood.

He had a guess. He didn't like it. Somewhere ahead, between here and the high ground, the trail crossed the river gorge — and there was only one way across a gorge in monsoon season, and it wasn't a way at all. It was a rope bridge, old, and the river under it was no longer a river but a brown, muscular animal with the whole sky in its mouth.

He woke Wren at first gray light.

"Up," he said. "We're making better time than I thought. Which means the bridge is closer than I thought. Which means today stops being a walk."

Wren sat up, hair plastered flat, eyes going instantly sharp. "How close?"

"Close enough that I'd like to get there before the water gets ideas."

She was on her feet before he finished packing. Behind them, far downstream, Teodor sat his watch beside a boat he may or may not still own. Ahead, the trail ran down off the ridge and into the roar of the gorge, and the voices in the dark had gone quiet, and the rain began again as if it had only paused to listen.
