## 12. The Bridge Out

The bridge had been old when the river was young, and the river had finally won.

They came around a bend in the trail at mid-morning and stopped as one, the way people stop when they've walked into a room where something terrible has just happened. Where the map — Wren's map, Marcus's notation marching across it in that precise, patient hand — showed a rope bridge spanning the flood channel, there was now only a pair of severed anchor posts jutting from opposite banks like broken teeth, and between them, nothing but brown water moving with the calm confidence of something that could kill you without breaking stride.

"Well," said Dominic.

"That's not a well," said Wren. "That's a catastrophe."

"It's a well of information. It tells us the water's up another meter since yesterday. It tells us whoever we're following crossed before dawn, when this was still passable." He crouched at the near anchor post, running his thumb along the frayed rope ends. "And it tells us they cut it behind them."

Wren looked at him. "Cut it?"

"Or it snapped. But look at the fibers. Clean shear on one side, tearing on the other. A blade did half this work." He stood, wiping his hands on his trousers. "Your friend Renner doesn't want anyone following his trail."

"He's not my friend."

"Your patron's man, then. Your foundation's man. Whoever he is to you."

She didn't answer that, which was itself an answer, and Dominic let it go because he was busy doing arithmetic he didn't like. The channel was maybe thirty meters across here, funneling between rock walls into a churning throat downstream. The far bank rose steep and green, and somewhere beyond it, a day's hard walk inland, his father was either alive or not, and every hour they spent standing on this bank admiring the problem was an hour the answer got further away.

"We go around," Wren said. She'd already unrolled the map against her pack, pinning the corners with stones. "There's a ford marked here — two days north, if the elevation lines mean anything—"

"Two days north through flooded lowland with no high ground if the water keeps rising. And two days behind whoever cut this bridge." He looked at the channel again. The middle current ran fast, white-capped, ugly. But along the near wall, where the rock funneled the flow, there was an eddy line — a seam of slower water, maybe two meters wide, hugging the cliff face all the way across. If it held. If it went all the way. If the water under it wasn't full of strainers and submerged trees waiting to wrap around a leg and keep a person for its own.

"You're thinking about swimming it," Wren said. Not a question. She'd been reading him for three days now, and she'd gotten annoyingly good at it.

"I'm thinking about the eddy line."

"Of course you are." She pressed her fingers to her eyes. "Of course you're thinking about the eddy line. Do you know what happens to a body in floodwater? It doesn't swim, Dominic. It gets *processed*. The river takes it apart and files the pieces wherever it likes."

"Then we stay in the eddy."

"The eddy is a rumor. The eddy is a polite fiction rivers tell to lure in overconfident boat pilots."

He almost smiled. Almost. "You have a better idea?"

"I have several worse ideas and one good one, which is that we are not fish, and I would like to register my objection formally, for the record, so that when we die your ghost knows whose fault it was."

"Noted for the record." He was already shrugging off his pack, lashing it tight, checking the waterproof roll. "We cross light. One pack at a time, floated on the rolled tarp. Rope tied between us — twenty meters, no more, so if you go I can pull you back."

"If *I* go," she said. "You assume I'm the one who goes."

"You're the one who argued against crossing. Arguers always go first. It's the law."

"That is not the law."

"It's the law of me." He tested the rope, coiled it across his chest. "Look — the eddy holds along the wall for most of the span. We swim the first ten meters flat out, hit the seam, and ride it. Don't fight the current. The current wins every argument it's ever been in. You let it carry you and you steer, gently, like you're asking it a favor."

"And if it says no?"

"Then you scream, and I come get you, and we both feel very foolish about it afterward."

* * *

The water took his breath before he'd swum five strokes.

Not cold exactly — monsoon water never was — but *violent*, a whole landscape's worth of rain trying to be in this one place at once, shoving him sideways, down, up, indifferent to his plans. He surfaced gasping, found the wall, found the seam of slower water hugging the rock, and clawed into it. Behind him, faintly, he heard Wren hit the channel with a sound like a dropped melon and a word he hadn't known she knew.

For a while it worked. That was the trap of rivers; they let you believe.

He rode the eddy along the cliff, one arm hooked over the current's shoulder, kicking toward the far bank in slow increments. Twenty meters. Twenty-five. He could see the landing rocks ahead, slick and dark, and he thought — foolishly, he would think later, unforgivably — *this is going to work.*

Then the seam failed.

It happened without drama: the slow water simply ended, swallowed by a boil where a submerged shelf kicked the main current back up, and the river reached out and took him like a hand closing. He went under, spun, came up spinning, and in the half-second he had to orient himself he saw two things: the landing rocks, impossibly far now, and Wren — who had entered the water behind him and been carried wide of the seam entirely — being taken down the channel toward the throat.

"WREN!"

She heard him. He saw her hear him — saw her turn her head against the current, saw her try to swim for the wall and get nowhere, the water laughing at the effort. The throat was fifty meters downstream, maybe less, where the channel narrowed and the river dropped through a chaos of boulders. People did not come back from the throat. The river filed their pieces wherever it liked.

Dominic did the math in half a heartbeat and hated every number. He was upstream of her. The current was faster than any swimmer. Which meant there was exactly one play, and it was the kind of play that only worked if you committed so completely that fear didn't have time to file an objection.

He dove.

Not across the current — *with* it, angling downstream in a flat racing dive, letting the river do the work, gaining on her not by swimming harder than the water but by presenting less of himself to it. He caught her at the edge of the boil, his hand closing on the strap of her pack, and the impact nearly tore his arm from its socket.

"PACK OFF!" he roared. "DROP THE PACK!"

Her hands were fumbling at the buckle, shaking too hard, and the throat was coming up fast — he could hear it now, a low grinding roar like millstones. He hauled himself along the strap to her, got an arm across her chest from behind, and shouted directly into her ear: "Breathe in. Hold it. On three we go under the shelf — there's a pocket — *trust me*—"

"There's a WHAT—"

He took her under.

The world became brown thunder. They were rolled, tumbled, slammed — and then, exactly where he'd gambled it would be, the hydraulic off the shelf curled back on itself and opened a pocket of relative calm behind the falling water, and they were inside it, pressed against wet stone, the river roaring past them like a freight train they'd somehow fallen between the cars of.

Wren was coughing, retching, gripping his jacket with both hands hard enough to tear seams. Her eyes were enormous. For a long moment neither of them said anything, because saying anything would have required breath, and breath required believing the air was real.

"Okay," Dominic managed finally. "Okay. There's a ledge. Two meters to your left. Can you move?"

She moved. They both moved, hand over hand along the rock, and dragged themselves onto a shelf of stone barely wider than a door, where they lay side by side, streaming, while the channel went on roaring past close enough to touch.

Above them, the bank loomed — four meters of wet vertical rock, but with roots, with handholds, with *possibility*. Below them, the throat ground its millstones. They were, Dominic reflected, in the river's vestibule rather than its stomach, which was as much as anyone could ask for on a Tuesday.

Wren started laughing.

It was a small, unhinged sound at first, then a bigger one, and then she was laughing helplessly, flat on her back on the stone, and after a moment Dominic joined her, because the alternative was shaking, and laughter was at least louder.

"You," she said, when she could speak, "are a *maniac*."

"Registered objection. Filed."

"You dove into a flood channel. Toward a *throat*. On a gamble about a *pocket*."

"The pocket was there."

"You didn't know the pocket was there!"

"I hoped the pocket was there." He sat up, wringing out his sleeve. "Hoping is a legitimate navigational technique. Ask any pilot."

She propped herself on her elbows and looked at him, and something in her expression shifted — the wariness that had lived in her face since the dock softening into something else, something more tired and more honest. Water dripped off her braid. Her hands were still trembling, and she looked at them as if they belonged to someone else.

"You went in after me," she said. "You didn't hesitate. You didn't calculate whether I was worth the risk. You just—" She stopped. Started again. "Why?"

"You'd have done the same."

"I don't know that I would have." She said it quietly, without self-pity, just laying the fact on the stone between them. "I've spent nine years telling myself I wouldn't have. That I'm not the sort of person who—"

She stopped again. The roar of the channel filled the pause.

Dominic waited. He was learning that about her — that Wren Calloway kept things locked in rooms, and that rattling the doors only made her lock them tighter, but that sometimes, if you simply stood in the hallway long enough, she opened one herself.

She sat up. Pulled the sodden canvas folder from inside her jacket — the one thing she'd refused to let out of her sight since Sarn's Reach — and held it against her chest, though it was already soaked through and past protecting.

"The real reason I came," she said. "Since we're apparently being honest on ledges today."

Dominic said nothing.

"A month ago, I got a letter. Private. No foundation letterhead, no lawyer's phrasing, none of the language he used to hide behind." Her thumb moved over the wet canvas. "It was from Marcus."

The name landed between them like a third person sitting down.

"He wrote—" Her voice caught, and she steadied it with visible effort, the way she did everything, as if emotion were a current to be negotiated rather than felt. "He wrote that he'd been wrong. Not about the facts — he was never wrong about the facts, that was the whole infuriating problem with him — but about what it cost. He wrote that he'd spent nine years knowing I lied on that stand to protect him, and nine years letting me carry it alone, because contacting me would have meant admitting what I'd sacrificed, and Marcus Fairweather did not admit things. He *expedited* past them." She laughed, short and bitter. "His word. Expedited. As if our careers were luggage."

Dominic stared at the water. His chest felt strange — too large and too small at once.

"He asked me to forgive him," Wren said. "In plain words. He said he was going somewhere the foundation couldn't follow, and he wanted to go there having said it. And I—" She shook her head. "I read that letter forty times. I told myself it was a trick, or sentimentality, or that he wanted something. Because if it was genuine, then I'd spent nine years hating a man who was spending those same years being sorry, and I couldn't afford that. So I came out here to prove the letter was a lie. That's the truth. I chartered a boat and chased a dying expedition halfway up a flooded basin to catch Marcus Fairweather in the act of being insincere."

She turned and looked at him directly, and her eyes were red-rimmed and fierce.

"And instead I find his son, who hates him more cleanly than I ever managed, risking his neck in floodwater for a stranger because that's apparently just what Fairweathers *do* when no one's watching."

The channel roared. Somewhere below, the throat went on grinding.

"My father," Dominic said at last, and his voice came out rougher than he intended, "has never once written me a letter."

Wren flinched. "Dominic—"

"Nine years. Not a telegram. Not a note forwarded through the foundation. Nothing." He picked up a loose pebble and turned it in his fingers, watching the water instead of her. "I told myself I didn't care. I built a whole life on not caring. Never staying anywhere long enough to owe anyone anything — because owing is how you get left, you understand? If nobody expects you, nobody's disappointed when you go." The pebble turned. Turned again. "And the whole time, he was writing letters. Just not to me."

"Dominic, listen to me." Wren's voice was gentle now, and gentleness from her was somehow worse than anger. "The journal page said *D. should understand why.* Why would a man who cut his son off for nine years leave a message addressed to him? Why would he write *me* asking forgiveness and leave *you* a riddle?" She leaned forward. "Because he couldn't ask you. Forgiveness from you isn't a letter he could post. It's something he has to say to your face, and Marcus Fairweather has been walking toward that conversation for nine years, and he's running out of river."

Dominic closed his hand around the pebble until it bit.

"Get up," he said quietly. "The ledge climbs to the bank. We've lost an hour, and he's a day ahead, and I want—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started over, softer, the armor showing its first hairline crack. "I want to know why."

They climbed.

* * *

The far bank delivered them onto trail by late afternoon, soaked, bruised, and lighter by one pack — hers, gone to the throat, along with most of her notes and spare clothes. She bore the loss the way she bore everything, jaw set, inventorying aloud what remained, and Dominic split his own kit without comment, and she accepted it without thanks, and between them passed the particular silence of two people who had seen each other underwater and could no longer pretend to be dry.

Toward dusk, they found the second sign.

It lay in the mud of the trail where the ground rose above the floodline: boot prints, many, moving fast — and beside them, half-buried, a brass instrument case, dented, empty, its latch sprung. Dominic turned it over in his hands. Stamped inside the lid, worn but legible:

*M. FAIRWEATHER — VELLORAN SURVEY, 3rd EXPEDITION.*

"He left it," Wren said. "Marcus wouldn't leave an instrument case. He catalogued his pencil shavings."

"No." Dominic looked down the trail, into the deepening green, where the prints ran straight and urgent toward the karst country rising black against the last light. "He left it *for* someone. Everything on this trail is a message. The page under the stone. The cut bridge behind Renner. And now this." He closed the empty case and slung it on his pack. "He's leaving breadcrumbs. He's marking the route for whoever's clever enough to follow."

"Or for one specific follower," Wren said quietly.

The wind came down off the heights then, carrying the first fat drops of the evening rain — and beneath the rain, faint and impossible, threaded a sound neither of them could name at first. A ringing. Thin, silver, many-voiced, rising and falling with the gusts, like a hundred small bells hung somewhere in the dark gorge ahead.

Wren went still. "Dominic. The rubbing. The notation on the margin — I thought it was decorative—"

"It wasn't decorative." He was already walking, faster now, the instrument case knocking against his hip, the bells ringing louder with every step. "It was a landmark. He drew us a map made of sound."

Behind them, far down the flooded channel, something else answered the storm — a radio crackle, thin and human, carrying on the wind from the direction of the bridge they'd crossed. Voices. More than two.

Someone else had found a way over.
