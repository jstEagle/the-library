## 13. Voss Follows

Wren Calloway had spent enough years in the field to know the difference between the jungle being quiet and the jungle being *watchful*. The first was ordinary — birds holding their tongues through the heat of noon, insects pausing between verses. The second had a texture to it, a held breath, and she'd been feeling it for the better part of an hour before she let herself admit it.

She stopped on the trail under the pretense of adjusting her pack strap and looked back the way they'd come.

The track wound down through the trees toward the flooded lowlands, a brown ribbon of churned mud and cut vines. Nothing moved in it. That was the problem. For two days they'd been following signs of Marcus's party — boot prints, a discarded instrument case, vines hacked with a machete in a hurry — and for two days those signs had been fresh enough that anything behind them should have been audible.

"Dom," she said quietly.

He was ten paces ahead, reading the trail the way other men read newspapers, and he didn't turn around. "Vines are fresher again. They're making better time than us."

"That's not what I —"

"I know." He turned then, and she saw that he'd already stopped pretending to study the ground. His eyes were fixed on the ridgeline behind her, where the canopy broke against a shoulder of grey limestone. "Been watching that saddle for twenty minutes. Something's using it."

"Voss."

"Voss." He said the name without heat, which somehow made it worse. "Better armed, better supplied, and motivated by money instead of whatever it is that's motivating you and me."

"Guilt," Wren offered.

"Speak for yourself. I'm motivated by a boat fee." He crouched, touched one of the cut vines, rubbed the sap between his fingers. "They're maybe two hours back. Less if they've got a guide who knows this country, and Voss doesn't hire anyone who doesn't."

Wren did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding all morning. Two of them, half their supplies gone to the flooded crossing, one pistol between them that Dominic kept like a family heirloom and cleaned like one too — reverently, and never at the right moments. Behind them: six or seven salvagers who'd already stripped one boat and weren't shy about it.

"So," she said. "We confront them or we evade them."

Dominic looked up at her with an expression she was beginning to catalog — the particular flat patience he reserved for her suggestions, as though each one were a specimen he had to examine before deciding whether it was dangerous. "Confront seven armed salvagers. With what? Harsh language?"

"I have a very extensive vocabulary."

"I don't doubt it. I've heard you use it on outboard motors." He rose, shouldering his pack, and his gaze went past her again — not to the ridge this time but up-slope, to where the limestone shoulders of the hills folded together into shadow. "Evade, then. But not downriver. They'll expect downriver. Everyone runs downhill toward water."

"Where do people run when they're not running downhill?"

He smiled, and it wasn't entirely a nice smile. It was the smile of a man about to do something he'd already decided to do regardless of anyone's opinion, and Wren recognized it because she'd seen it on his father's face, once, a lifetime ago, right before Marcus Fairweather had done something academically unforgivable.

"Up," Dominic said. "Into the caves."

* * *

The karst country announced itself gradually — first as stones in the soil like knuckles pushing through skin, then as sinkholes breathing cool air, then as whole cliffs riddled with openings, black mouths in the grey rock, some no bigger than a fist, others wide enough to swallow a house. The monsoon had filled the lower galleries; water poured from half the cave mouths in bright ropes, and the air coming out of the others smelled of wet stone and something older, mineral and cold.

Dominic picked their entrance with the offhand confidence of a man choosing a doorway in his own home. "This one," he said, pointing at a slot in the cliff face half-hidden by ferns. "Air's moving through it. Means it connects somewhere. And the floor slopes down, not up — floodwater's already drained past this level."

"You've done this before," Wren said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I grew up doing this before." He ducked into the shadow, and his voice came back altered, hollowed. "My father thought caves were better than school. Said a cave teaches you everything geology can teach you, plus humility."

"What a charming pedagogy."

"He also lost me in one for four hours when I was nine. So, mixed reviews."

Inside, the darkness closed around them like a hand. Wren got her lamp going — an old carbide rig she'd carried through three continents and two disgraces — and its white cone revealed a passage wide enough for two, walled in flowstone the color of old ivory. Water dripped somewhere ahead with the regularity of a metronome. *Drip. Drip. Drip.* The jungle noise fell away behind them until there was nothing but stone and the sound of their own breathing.

"This is either very clever or very stupid," she said.

"The best plans are usually both." Dominic walked with his lamp low, watching the floor. "Voss will find our trail ends at the cliff. She'll guess we went underground, but guessing and knowing are different animals, and knowing costs time she may not want to spend. Every hour she spends poking into holes is an hour we're ahead."

"And if she does follow?"

"Then we hear her coming long before she sees us. Sound carries in here like a cathedral." He glanced back at her, and in the lamplight his face had softened into something almost boyish. "Besides. There's something I want to show you. If my memory's any good, it's about a kilometer in."

"You've been in *this* cave?"

"I've been in caves like it. All through this basin." He hesitated, and Wren watched him decide to say the rest. "My father mapped half of these when I was a kid. He used to say the limestone was the index card of the whole region — everything the river ever did, everything the people here ever knew, it's written in the rock if you bother to read it."

Wren felt something shift in her chest, an old ache finding new purchase. She'd spent nine years telling herself she understood Marcus Fairweather completely — ambitious, careless with truth, careless with people. But a man who took his son caving and called limestone an index card was also a man capable of the things the villagers had described. Mending roofs. Paying debts. Laughing with children.

She said none of this. Some observations were better left to do their work in silence.

* * *

An hour in, the passage opened without warning into a chamber so large their lamps couldn't find its far wall. The ceiling vanished upward into blackness hung with stalactites like the pipes of some drowned organ. Their footfalls, which had echoed crisply in the corridor, now went out into the dark and didn't come back.

"Hold still," Dominic murmured, and swept his light slowly across the near wall.

Wren's breath caught.

Carvings covered the flowstone from the waterline to well above their heads — not the scattered graffiti of later visitors but deliberate, composed work, whole registers of figures cut into the stone and worn soft by centuries of mineral bloom. Processions of people. Boats with high curved prows. Rows of stacked vessels, granaries by their look, drawn in cross-section like diagrams. And above it all, repeated again and again, a single symbol: a spiral, coiling inward, that might have been water and might have been time and might have been both.

"Oh," Wren said, which was inadequate, and she knew it, and she said it anyway.

"Yeah," Dominic said softly. "That was more or less my reaction at nine years old, too."

She moved closer, lamp raised, and the scholar in her — the part of her that scandal and disgrace had never managed to kill, only drive underground like these galleries themselves — woke up hungry. The style was unlike anything published from the Velloran basin. Older, she thought. Or simply hidden. The boats were wrong for the river cultures in the literature; the granaries were wrong for a society this far into flood country, unless —

Unless they weren't wrong at all. Unless they were the point.

"Dom. Look at the sequence." Her fingers hovered over the stone without touching it. "Here — procession, harvest, storage. Then the spiral. Then —" she traced the register below, where the carved figures climbed staircases cut into terraced hillsides, carrying jars, driving stakes, building walls that curved rather than straightened. "They're not fleeing the water. They're *receiving* it. This isn't a flood myth. It's a flood manual."

Behind her, Dominic had gone very quiet.

When she turned, he was standing before a different section of wall, his lamp held close, and his expression was one she'd never seen on him — stripped of irony, of restlessness, of all the careful distance he wore like armor. He looked young. He looked, she thought, like a boy whose father had just shown him something marvelous.

"There's writing here," he said. "Under the spiral. Same notation system as the rubbing."

The rubbing. Wren crossed to him, and there it was, incised small and precise beside the great coiling symbol: a column of characters in Marcus Fairweather's own hand, cut into the living rock with a chisel tip — his notation, the idiosyncratic shorthand he'd invented decades ago and taught to exactly two people. One of them was standing beside her, pretending not to notice that she'd noticed.

"He was here," she said. "Recently. Those cuts haven't weathered at all."

"He was here, and he wanted whoever came next to be able to read the wall." Dominic's voice was careful, controlled, the control costing him visibly. "Which means he knew someone would come next. Which means —"

"Which means he left you a trail, Dominic. Not a message. A *trail*. Starting from the journal page and running through every cave he ever loved." She hadn't meant to say it so plainly. But watching him stand in his father's handwriting, in a chamber his father had clearly chosen for him, she found she couldn't wrap it in tact. "He's been leading you since Sarn's Reach."

"Don't." The word came out flat and fast. He stepped back from the wall, and the lamplight caught the muscle working in his jaw. "Don't make it sentimental. He needed a reader. I'm the only one alive who knows his notation besides you, and you —" He stopped. Recalibrated. "It's logistics."

"It's logistics," Wren agreed, in the tone she used with students who were lying to themselves about their thesis topics, and she saw him register the tone and resent it and, underneath the resentment, hear it.

For a while neither of them spoke. The drip of water kept its patient time. Somewhere deep in the chamber's darkness, air moved through unseen passages with a sound almost like distant surf.

Then Dominic raised his lamp again, deliberately, and pointed along the far register. "Look at the last panel. Before the modern cuts."

She brought her light up beside his. The final carved scene differed from the others: the terraces were complete, the granaries full, and at the center stood a single figure larger than the rest, arms raised, facing a doorway cut into a hillside. Around the doorway, the spiral again — but here the coil ended not inward but outward, opening like a hand releasing something.

"A vault," Wren breathed. "A sealed archive. And the figure — Dom, the figure isn't guarding it. Look at the posture. Arms open. It's *handing it forward*. Passing it on."

"To whoever comes after," Dominic said quietly.

The words hung in the cold air between them, and Wren watched him understand them — really understand them, past the armor, down where he kept the nine-year-old boy who'd waited at windows for a father who kept moving. She watched him understand, and then she watched him refuse to, turning away, clicking his lamp higher, becoming practical again by force of will.

"We should keep moving," he said. "There's a second entrance on the north side, if the map in my head hasn't rotted. We come out above the gorge, put three hours on Voss, and —"

The sound stopped him mid-sentence.

It came from the passage behind them — faint, rhythmic, unmistakable. Not water. Not wind. Footsteps. Many of them, echoing up the corridor they'd walked, accompanied by the thin metallic music of equipment knocking against equipment.

Wren killed her lamp. In the sudden blackness she heard Dominic do the same, and then his hand found her sleeve and drew her sideways, away from the open chamber floor, toward a fold in the rock that might have been cover and might have been nothing at all.

"They followed the air current," he whispered against her ear, barely breath at all. "Clever woman."

Down the long throat of the tunnel, still distant but closing, the footsteps came on — and beneath them now, threading through the echo, Wren caught another sound that turned her blood colder than the cave air.

A voice. Low, amused, giving orders in the unhurried tone of someone who had all the time in the world and knew exactly where everyone in it was standing.

Odile Voss had found their trail after all.
