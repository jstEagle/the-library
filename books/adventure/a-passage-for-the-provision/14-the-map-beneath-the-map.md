## 14. The Map Beneath the Map

The karst caves swallowed the light behind them in stages, like a miser counting back coins.

Dominic went first with the lantern held high, because that was the arrangement — he led, she followed, nobody talked about anything that mattered. It had been a workable system for three days now, and he intended to defend it the way he defended his boat: stubbornly, and without admitting how much of it was habit.

"Mind the left wall," he said.

"I see the left wall."

"You walked into the right one."

"The right wall," Wren said, "ambushed me."

He let that stand. Behind them, somewhere back along the decoy route they'd cut through the flooded lowlands, Voss's crew would be finding an empty trail and a very convincing set of false campfires Dominic had built out of wet driftwood and spite. The caves had been Wren's idea in the end — or rather, she'd suggested them in the particular offhand way she had, the way that made it sound like she didn't care whether anyone listened, which meant she cared enormously. He'd pretended to think it over for a full minute just to keep the ledger between them balanced.

The passage opened without warning into a chamber the size of a chapel, and the lantern flame leaned as if bowing.

"Oh," said Wren.

It was the first time he'd heard her say anything so small.

The walls rose in smooth grey curves, and across them — everywhere, floor to ceiling and beyond the reach of their light — ran carvings. Not the scattered glyphs they'd glimpsed at the entrance, the ones that might have been territory markers or prayers. These were deliberate. Composed. Rows of figures moving through water: boats with high prows, processions of people carrying bundles overhead, terraces stacked like wedding cakes with channels cut between them. Rivers ran through the stone in long spiraling lines, and at the top of the far wall, above everything, a great circle divided by wavy bands — water above, water below, and something held safe in the middle.

"They built *up*," Wren said quietly. She'd stopped walking. "Everyone else in this basin built levees and prayed. These people built for the flood. Look — the terraces are stepped so the water fills the lower levels first. They gave the river rooms of its own."

"You can read all that from here?"

"I can read the shape of it. The rest will take years." She turned a slow circle, lantern outstretched, and for a moment she looked younger — or maybe just less armored. "Dominic, this isn't a shrine. This is a *manual*. This is engineering carved in stone."

He should have been looking at the walls. Instead he was looking at her face, lit gold and flickering, wearing an expression he hadn't seen on her before — something unguarded and hungry, the look of a woman who had spent years being told her life's work was worthless and had just found proof otherwise written forty feet tall.

Then she saw it too, and the expression changed.

"Dominic." Her voice had gone careful again, the professional voice, the one she used when she was about to hand him something heavy and wanted him to take it gently. "Come look at this."

She'd moved to a flat section of wall near the chamber's eastern curve, where the stone had been polished — actually polished, rubbed smooth by hands or tools until it shone faintly even under lantern light. And fixed to it, wedged into a natural seam at shoulder height, was a sheet of paper.

Not old paper. Not ancient vellum, not some miracle of preservation. A modern field notebook page, water-spotted at the corners, weighted in place by a flat stone the size of a saucer.

Wren lifted the stone away with two fingers, the way you'd handle something that might still be alive.

"It's a rubbing," she said. "Charcoal on survey paper."

Dominic came closer. The rubbing showed a panel of the carvings — the spiral rivers and the stepped terraces, transferred faithfully in black and grey — but what stopped his breath wasn't the image. It was what was written *on* the image. Small annotations crowded the margins in a cramped, slanting hand, numbers and symbols threaded between the copied glyphs like stitches through fabric. Little triangles with dots inside. Circles bisected by horizontal lines. A shorthand of arrows and superscripts that meant nothing to him at all.

Except it didn't mean nothing. It meant something he knew the way you know a smell from childhood — instantly, bodily, before thought could get involved.

"That's his notation," Wren said.

"I know."

"You *recognize* it?"

"I grew up watching him write it." Dominic heard his own voice come out flat and strange. "He used to do crosswords in it. Actual newspaper crosswords, filled in with those triangles and circles, so nobody else could read his answers. My sister thought it was a code. I thought—" He stopped. "I thought it was a way of keeping us out."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Then: "It's not a code. It's a compression system. He developed it himself — I watched him build it, year by year, back when we were still..." She didn't finish that sentence either. They were both getting good at unfinished sentences; it was practically their native language now. "Every symbol is a whole phrase. The triangle-with-dot means 'compare against foundation records.' The bisected circle means 'unverified, pending survey.' He designed it so a page could hold ten times the information and only be readable by someone who'd learned it from him directly."

"Which is why none of it ever appeared in his published notes."

"Which is why none of it ever appeared anywhere." She angled the rubbing toward the lantern. "Look at the density. This margin alone would be six pages of standard field notes. He wasn't hiding it from the world, Dominic. He was hiding it from the *foundation*."

Dominic took the paper from her. His hands were steady, which surprised him, because nothing else felt steady.

The annotations tracked the carvings methodically — glyph by glyph, terrace by terrace — and threaded among them were dates. Recent dates. Dates from weeks ago, from the window when the expedition's radio check-ins had already gone silent. One annotation sat boxed twice over, the way Marcus boxed things when he wanted to shout on paper:

*Site predates all basin chronologies. Foundation maps wrong by 400 yrs. Do NOT transmit coordinates.*

Below that, smaller, almost crammed into the corner as if written last and quickly:

*If found: proceed inland by water-line. Trust the bells.*

"Bells?" Wren said.

"I don't know." But something ticked over in the back of his mind, some half-heard thing, and filed itself away without showing him its face yet.

He read the rubbing again. Then a third time, slower, letting each symbol land where it lived. And the picture assembled itself in his head with the grim click of a lock turning — the same feeling he got when a boat's engine finally caught after an hour of cranking, that moment where all the parts you'd been worrying separately suddenly revealed themselves as one machine.

Three missed check-ins. The foundation's polite panic, their committee-formed search plan, their insistence on waiting for the monsoon to pass because *the risk calculus doesn't support immediate action*. The expedition's base camp abandoned but tidy, equipment stacked, tents struck rather than fled. The journal page under the stone: *Moving inland. D. should understand why.*

And now this. A site the foundation had never heard of. Coordinates explicitly marked *do not transmit*. Notation designed to be unreadable to everyone except the people Marcus chose to teach.

"He wasn't lost," Dominic said. "He wasn't silent because the radio broke, or the river took them, or any of the hundred things the foundation has been moaning about in triplicate." He looked up at Wren. "He went dark on purpose. He cut the check-ins himself. He found something he decided the foundation couldn't be trusted with, and he took his whole expedition and *disappeared into it*."

Wren had gone very still. In the lantern light her face was hard to read, but her hands weren't — her hands were doing what her hands always did when she was thinking hard, worrying the strap of her satchel, thumb working the worn leather like a rosary.

"That tracks," she said at last. "That tracks with the man I knew. Before." She said *before* the way other people said a dead relative's name. "Marcus Fairweather, circa fifteen years ago, would sooner have sawn off his own leg than falsify a record. Provenance, documentation, chain of custody — he lectured graduate students until they wept. The idea that he forged papers was always—" She exhaled. "It never fit. That's all I'll say tonight. But this—" she touched the edge of the rubbing, not quite touching the symbols, "—this fits. This is a man protecting something. And there's only one kind of thing men like Marcus protect badly enough to ruin themselves over."

"What kind is that?"

"The kind they love more than their reputations."

Dominic looked at the wall. At the procession of carved figures carrying their bundles above the floodwater, generation after generation climbing the terraces with whatever mattered most held high overhead.

*Moving inland. D. should understand why.*

His father had addressed a message to him. Not to the foundation, which employed him. Not to his funders, who financed him. To the son who hadn't spoken to him in nine years, who had walked out of a dinner in Corvane and taken a deckhand job on a barge the same week specifically so there would be no address to forward letters to. Who had, in fact, spent a decade constructing a life whose entire architecture was *unreachable* — and had somehow failed, because here he stood in a flooded cave holding his father's handwriting.

*D. should understand why.*

"Well, I don't," he said aloud. It came out angrier than he'd planned, and he let it stay angry, because the anger was easier than the other thing underneath it. "I don't understand why. Why me? Why leave a note for the one person who told him exactly where he could put his expeditions? If he wanted a confidant he had a whole foundation of people who answer his telegrams."

"Yes," Wren said. "Why you." There was no mockery in it. She folded her arms and studied him with that clinical attention she usually reserved for eroded stonework. "You've been asking that since the base camp. You ask it like it's an accusation."

"Because it is one."

"Mm." She turned back to the wall, lantern raised, and he understood he'd been dismissed — politely, thoroughly, in the manner of someone who has decided a question answers itself if you're willing to sit with it. He hated that about her. He was starting, in a way he refused to examine, to rely on it.

They worked the chamber for another hour. Wren photographed what she could with the battered camera she'd hauled halfway across the basin ("for the archive," she'd said, and for once Dominic hadn't asked which archive), sketching panels in her own notebook while Dominic checked the chamber's other exits. There were two: the passage they'd come in by, low and half-drowned, and a second opening on the north side, higher and dry, breathing a faint cool draft that smelled of open air and rain coming.

It was while he was standing at that northern mouth, listening to the draft, that the ticking thing in the back of his mind finally showed its face.

*Bells.* Trust the bells. He'd heard about bells. Recently. On a boat, in the rain, from a woman who collected debts—

"Sarn's Reach," he said.

Wren looked up from her sketching.

"Voss. When we were negotiating, she was showing off, listing everything she knew about the upper basin to prove her price was fair. She mentioned a gorge the boatmen call the Gorge of Bells — hanging stones that chime when the wind comes through. She said no one runs it at flood stage, and she said it in the tone of a woman daring me to try." He turned back into the chamber. "Marcus wrote *trust the bells*. He left a landmark. He left a *route*."

Wren set down her pencil. For a moment neither of them spoke, and the cave breathed around them, that slow cool exhale from the north.

"He's leaving a trail," she said slowly. "For whoever was clever enough to find the rubbing. Or—" and here her voice did something complicated, "—for you. Specifically. Because he knew you'd be the one person alive who could read his shorthand *and* run a river at flood stage. He built a message only his son could follow."

"Don't say it like that."

"Like what?"

"Like it's a gift." Dominic shoved the rubbing carefully into the waterproof sleeve of his jacket, next to the journal page, next to every other piece of his father he'd been accumulating on this river like a man collecting evidence in a trial he didn't know he was prosecuting. "It's instructions. It's him managing me from a hundred miles away, same as he managed everything else. Same as he managed Mother, same as he managed the foundation, same as he managed—" He stopped, jaw tight.

"Same as he managed me," Wren finished quietly.

He glanced at her. She was looking at the carved procession again, at the figures with their burdens held high, and her thumb had gone back to worrying the satchel strap.

"He asked my forgiveness once," she said. "In a letter. I never answered it. I've been telling myself the whole ride upriver that I came for the truth, for my name, for the scandal — and that's true, all of it's true. But I also came because a man I helped destroy wrote to me like I was still someone worth writing to, and I needed to know whether that letter was kindness or strategy." She looked at him then, direct as a bearing line. "Now I'm standing in a cave looking at proof that the man planned everything — the silence, the route, the message shaped exactly for the one person who'd come. So I'll ask you honestly, Dominic, because you're the only other person who knew him: when Marcus Fairweather loves something, does he manage it? Or does the managing just *look* like love from the outside?"

The question hung there. Dominic found he couldn't answer it, and that she'd known he couldn't, and that she'd asked anyway — not to wound him, but because she was the sort of person who believed unanswered questions rotted, and better to open the cask in fresh air.

"We should move," he said. "Light's going, and that draft smells like weather."

Outside the northern mouth, the sky confirmed it. The clouds that had been stacking on the horizon for two days had finished stacking; they now stood over the basin like a verdict, blue-black and total, and the air had gone thick and warm and electric, the particular pressure that every river pilot learns to feel in his teeth before his eyes confirm it. Far off, thunder rolled — not the sharp crack of a passing storm but the long continuous grumble of something settling in for days.

"That's not a squall," Wren said.

"No. That's the monsoon arriving properly. We've had the previews." Dominic scanned the terrain ahead — a steep forested slope dropping toward what his memory of Voss's maps insisted was a river valley, the water-line route, the way inland. "We need to be under stone before dark, real stone, not an overhang. And after that—" he patted the pocket where the rubbing rode against his ribs, "—we go find some bells."

They descended together, quick and quiet, two small figures on a green slope under a bruising sky. Behind them the cave mouth exhaled its cool breath one last time, and above the drowned terraces of a civilization that had learned to live with the flood, the first fat drops of the true monsoon began to fall — slow, spaced, deliberate, like footsteps coming down a stair.

By the time they reached the treeline, the footsteps were running.
