## 15. Monsoon Breaks

The sky had been threatening all morning — a bruised, low-hanging ceiling of cloud that pressed down on the gorge like a lid on a pot — but Dominic had told himself it would hold. He had told himself that about the weather, about the trail, and about the increasingly alarming sound the boat's engine had made before they'd moored her, and he was two for three on being wrong.

The first drops came at midday. Not rain so much as an announcement: fat, deliberate drops the size of grapes striking the canopy leaves like someone testing a drumhead. Then the drumhead broke.

"Is it always like this?" Wren shouted over the roar, hunched under a broad-leafed plantain frond that was doing approximately nothing.

"No," Dominic shouted back. "Sometimes it's worse."

They were on the inland track, following the signs of Marcus's party — boot prints in the mud, the discarded instrument case, vines cut with a machete in clean strokes — when the world simply turned to water. The trail became a stream, the stream became a river, and the river became something with opinions. The floodwater they'd been wading through rose from knee-deep to thigh-deep in the space of twenty minutes, brown and fast and full of debris.

"We need high ground," Dominic said. "Now."

"Define high ground."

"Anything above this." He gestured at the churning brown expanse that had recently been a path.

They scrambled up a slope slick as soap, Wren grabbing roots and Dominic grabbing Wren twice when her feet went out from under her, until they found it: a dark mouth in the limestone, half-hidden behind a curtain of hanging ferns. A cave. Not deep — twenty feet back to a dry shelf of stone — but dry, which at that moment made it feel like a palace.

They collapsed onto the shelf just as the monsoon arrived in earnest. It wasn't rain anymore; it was the sky emptying itself all at once, a white roaring curtain that erased the gorge, the trees, the world. The temperature dropped ten degrees. Thunder rolled through the karst like furniture being moved upstairs by giants.

"Well," said Wren, wringing out her shirt. "At least we won't die thirsty."

Dominic laughed — actually laughed, a short surprised bark of a thing — and then looked at her as if she'd done it to him on purpose.

* * *

Wren counted their assets while the storm screamed outside. One waterproof satchel containing maps, the journal page, and a tin of matches that had better be drier than she was. One machete. Half a block of dried fish. Two people who disliked each other, though she was beginning to suspect the dislike was mostly habit, like a coat you kept wearing because it was already broken in.

"I'm going to check the back," Dominic said, and disappeared into the dark with the matches.

She heard him stop. Heard the sharp intake of breath.

"Wren."

She followed the light. Ten feet past the dry shelf, the cave narrowed into a passage — and against the far wall, wrapped in blankets that had once been expedition canvas, lay two men. Porters. She recognized the Velloran trade cloth, the expedition's red-and-grey weave. They were soaked through, shivering, and one of them had a gash along his forearm bound with strips of the same canvas.

They were also awake, and watching them with the wary expressions of men who had been found by strangers in a cave during a monsoon and were running the odds on whether that was good news or very bad news.

"They say," Dominic said slowly, translating, "that they are Tam and Beso. They were with my father's expedition."

The older one — Tam, grey at the temples, hands like knotted rope — said something long and urgent. Dominic listened, his face doing something complicated.

"What?" Wren asked.

"He says—" Dominic stopped. Started again. "He says he's sorry. He thought we might be Renner's men."

"Who's Renner?"

Tam spoke again, and this time both porters talked at once, overlapping, gesturing, the words tumbling out in the particular cadence of men who have been holding a story underwater for days and have finally let go of it.

* * *

It took an hour, the matches, and most of their dried fish to get the whole account. The storm raged on outside, sealing them in, and perhaps that helped — there is something about a cave in a deluge that loosens tongues.

The expedition had split, Tam explained through Dominic's translation and Wren's patchy grasp of the trade tongue. Three weeks ago. Before the last check-in, before the silence.

"There was a man," Dominic said, his voice flat in a way Wren had learned to recognize as the sound of him keeping himself level. "A patron's agent. He'd been with them since the lower river. Polite. Well-supplied. He carried letters of credit from a foundation office — real ones, Tam says, they checked."

"Aldous Renner?" Wren asked.

"That's what I asked." Dominic turned back to the porters. Tam answered, shaking his head.

"He doesn't know the name. But the description fits what Voss said. And the manner." He rubbed his jaw. "This man — the patron's man — he wasn't there for the archaeology. Tam's clear on that. He was there for the site. For control of it. When your father found the outer ruins, this man sent word downriver. More men came. Boats. Radios. And your father—"

Tam spoke again, quieter this time. Beso, the younger one, stared into the fireless dark.

"He refused," Dominic said. His voice had gone strange. "My father refused him."

"He split the party," Wren said, working it forward. "That's why the camp was stripped so neatly. That's why the journal page said 'moving inland.' He wasn't leading an expedition anymore. He was running one."

"Some followed the patron's man inland," Dominic translated as Tam continued. "Men who owed him money, or who believed him when he said your father had lost his mandate. Others fled downriver. These two got caught in the flood channels and ended up here." He paused. "Beso says the patron's man has radios. Supply boats. He says he has enough men to work the site for a season."

"And your father?"

Tam answered at length, and Dominic listened without interrupting, which Wren had noticed was rare for him. When Tam finished, Dominic sat back against the stone and was quiet for a long moment.

"He took four people and went deeper," Dominic finally said. "Into the gorge country. Toward the inner site. To keep it away from them." A muscle worked in his jaw. "He's leading them on a chase. At sixty-one years old. In monsoon season."

"Or he knows something they don't," Wren said. "About the site. About the water." She thought of the iconography she'd glimpsed in the carvings, the terraces built to be drowned. "Marcus never did anything without an exit."

"You haven't spoken to him in nine years. How would you know?"

The words came out harder than Dominic probably intended — Wren could see him hear it land, see him decide not to take it back anyway. She let it sit. There was no point fighting about it in a cave, in a flood, with two strangers listening.

Instead she said, "Tell me about the patron. What does Tam know?"

What Tam knew, it emerged, was mostly rumor braided with certainty. The patron had funded expeditions in the basin before — always quietly, always through agents, always arriving after the discovery like a gull after a fishing boat. Local crews called him *the Collector*. Nobody had seen his face. Everybody had seen what happened to sites he took an interest in: fenced, stripped, documented in private ledgers, and gone. Artifacts that surfaced years later in auction catalogs half a world away, provenance papers immaculate.

Provenance papers.

Wren felt the old cold move through her chest. Immaculate papers. She knew exactly how immaculate papers got made, because nine years ago she had stood in a hearing room and sworn that Marcus Fairweather had made some of them.

"Say that again," she said, when Dominic relayed the part about the documents. "Slowly."

He did. And then he watched her face do whatever it did, because he frowned. "What?"

"Nothing. Keep going."

"It's not nothing. You've gone the color of the river."

"The river's brown, Dominic. Everyone's the color of the river." But she pulled her knees up and hugged them, and after a moment she said, "Nine years ago, the papers in the scandal were too good. That's what I told the inquiry — that no field man produces documentation that clean. What I didn't say, what I couldn't prove, was where they came from. I assumed Marcus had bought them somewhere. From someone." She stared at the rain-wall at the mouth of the cave. "I'm starting to think I had the direction of purchase backwards."

Dominic was quiet. Then: "You're saying someone gave him forged papers. To hang him with."

"I'm saying someone gave him forged papers to *keep* him. A man who owns your reputation owns you. You don't refuse the Collector if he can end you with a memo." Her voice cracked slightly on the last of it, and she hated that. "And Marcus refused him anyway. Out here. With everything to lose. Again."

Outside, the thunder rolled its furniture across the sky. Beso fed a few damp twigs to a small fire he'd coaxed alive in a hollow of stone, and the light jumped across all their faces.

* * *

Dominic couldn't sleep, which was normal, but tonight the reasons had multiplied.

He sat near the cave mouth where the spray drifted in cool and silver, watching lightning stitch the gorge together and pull it apart. Behind him, Wren slept sitting up, chin on her chest, in the way of people who had learned not to expect comfort. The porters slept like the dead, or like men who had finally stopped running.

*A patron.* He turned the word over. It sounded benign. Patrons endowed chairs and bought seasons at the opera. This one collected civilizations.

And his father — his impossible, absent, infuriating father — had looked this thing in the eye and chosen to run *deeper into the gorge*, taking four people with him, rather than hand over whatever was in there. Nine years of silence, and the first real thing Dominic learned about the man was this: that when it mattered, Marcus Fairweather had apparently developed a spine made of the same stubborn limestone they were sheltering inside.

It would have been easier, Dominic thought, if he hadn't. Easier to keep hating a coward.

A pebble clattered. Wren had come awake without seeming to move, the way she did, and settled beside him at the mouth, close enough to share warmth, far enough to pretend otherwise.

"Can't sleep either?" she asked.

"I'm resting my eyes."

"Liar."

"Occupational habit." He nodded out at the storm. "The water will drop in six hours, maybe eight. The trail ahead is going to be a swamp. Behind us, Voss may or may not still be tracking us. Ahead of us, a private army is chasing my father through flood country." He shook his head. "Two weeks ago my biggest problem was a client in Porto Verde who paid in counterfeit coin."

"And now?"

"Now I'm going to find him." He said it simply, and was surprised how little argument his own voice raised. Somewhere between the logjam and the leaking hull and the journal page weighted under a stone, the *why* had stopped mattering. Or rather, the lie he'd been telling — the boat fee, the professional obligation — had quietly drowned, and he hadn't bothered to fish it out.

Wren studied him in the flicker of lightning. "For what it's worth," she said, "I think he left that page for you on purpose. 'D. should understand why.' He knew you'd come. Maybe that's the only vote of confidence he had left."

"He should have voted nine years ago."

"Yes," she said. No deflection, no softening. "He should have. And so should I, about other things. We're a pair of cowards, your father and I. Though I notice cowardice doesn't survive a monsoon. There's no room left for it when you're bailing."

Dominic almost smiled. Almost. "Get some sleep, Calloway. Tomorrow we go inland."

"Tomorrow we go inland," she agreed, and then, as she turned to go back: "Fairweather? The patron's man. If he has radios and boats and a season's supplies — and we have a leaky boat, half a fish, and two exhausted porters—"

"I know."

"—then we're not the rescue party anymore, are we? We're the interruption."

Dominic looked out at the black roaring gorge, at the country where his father was running out of ground to give up, and found that being an interruption suited him fine.

"No," he said. "We're the tide."

Behind them, unnoticed in the dark, old Tam opened his eyes. He had understood more of their language than he let on — a useful habit in a man who had survived thirty years of expeditions — and there was something he had not yet told them. Something about the inner gorge, and what the old people said waited there when the great waters came.

But that could wait for morning. Even the truth needed dry ground to stand on.
