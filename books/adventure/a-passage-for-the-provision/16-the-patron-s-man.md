## 16. The Patron's Man

The cave the porters led them to was less a shelter than a wound in the hillside, a low mouth of stone breathing cold air and the smell of wet fern. Wren ducked in behind Dominic, who had to fold himself nearly in half to fit, and decided — not for the first time that week — that archaeology was a profession invented by people who hated their spines.

The porters were already inside, wrapped in the tarps they'd carried since the anchorage, shivering in that particular way of men who had been half-drowned twice in one day and were trying to be polite about it. Their names were Tam and Osei. Tam was older, gray at the temples, with the patient hands of someone who had spent thirty years lashing loads. Osei was young enough that his fear sat on him openly, like a wet coat he couldn't take off.

Wren crouched by the small fire they'd coaxed from damp kindling and did what she did best, which was not run toward danger but sit near it and wait for it to talk.

"You said," she began, gently, "that the party split. Some went inland with the patron's man."

Tam looked at Osei. Osei looked at the fire. Dominic, wedged against the cave wall like a folded deck chair, went very still in the way he did when he was listening hard and wanted no one to know it.

"The patron's man," Wren repeated. "Tell me about him."

"He was not a bad man," Osei said quickly, which was the sort of thing people said about men who were exactly bad enough. "Polite. He learned our words. He carried his own pack, even when he didn't have to."

"His name?"

Tam stirred the fire with a stick, buying himself a moment. "Renner," he said. "Aldous Renner."

The name went through Wren like a fishhook finding old scar tissue.

She kept her face still. She had gotten very good at that, years ago, sitting in a hearing room while men in gray suits explained what her testimony would cost her and what her silence would cost her, and how the two figures had been calculated to make silence seem like the generous option. You learned things in rooms like that. You learned how to hold a face like a full cup of water across a slick floor.

"Renner," she said, as though tasting it for the first time. "I don't know the name."

It was not entirely a lie. She knew the name the way you know a word in a language you don't speak — she'd seen it once, years ago, on a letterhead she was never supposed to see, in a file she was never supposed to open, and she had filed it away in the part of herself where she kept everything she was too afraid or too proud to say out loud.

Dominic was watching her. She felt it the way you feel weather change.

"Go on," she said to Tam. "What was his business with the expedition?"

Tam glanced at the cave mouth, at the rain coming down outside in gray ropes, and seemed to decide that a cave in the middle of nowhere was as safe a confessional as he was likely to find.

"He came to us at the second camp," Tam said. "Before the gorges. Not with the crew — he arrived separately, by his own boat, with two men who did not talk and did not smile. He had papers. Letters of credit. He said he represented—" Tam searched for the phrase, found it, and delivered it with a faint, bitter precision, "—*an interested private party*."

"An interested private party," Dominic repeated from the wall. His voice was flat in the way Wren had learned meant the opposite of flat.

"Your father's expedition was supposed to be funded by the foundation," Wren said. "Officially."

"Officially," Tam agreed. "But the foundation money was slow. It was always slow — you know how it is, the grants, the committees. And then Mr. Renner appeared, and suddenly there was fuel, and food, and the good radios, and no one asked too loudly where it came from. Your father—" Tam hesitated.

"Say it," Dominic said. "Whatever it is, say it. I've spent nine years imagining worse."

Tam gave him a long look, and whatever he saw in Dominic's face seemed to satisfy him. "Your father did not like him," Tam said. "That much was true. They argued — quietly, always quietly, your father was never loud — but I heard the shape of it. Mr. Renner wanted the site found *quickly*. Your father wanted it found *right*. And Mr. Renner had brought something your father could not refuse."

"Which was?"

"Enough supplies to get everyone home alive," Osei said quietly. "The monsoon was coming. Mr. Renner said, in effect: take my money, or turn back now and lose everything. And your father looked at us — at all of us, at the young ones, at the boats — and he took the money." Osei's hands tightened around his knees. "He hated it. You could see him hating it. But he took it."

Wren fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. Her mind was moving fast now, back down old corridors, opening doors she had nailed shut.

"Tell me about the leverage," she said.

Tam and Osei exchanged another look — the look of men deciding how much truth the fire could hold.

"Some of the crew owed him," Tam said at last. "Not money. Worse than money. Debts of the kind a man cannot pay in a village. Kofi had signed a paper in Sarn's Reach once — a paper he did not read carefully, because the man who handed it to him was friendly and the rum was free. It was a debt contract. Mr. Renner bought it. He bought two others like it. And he said to those men, very kindly, very reasonably: you may leave the expedition whenever you like. But the paper follows you. And I follow papers."

"He didn't threaten anyone," Osei added. "That's what I want you to understand. He never once raised his voice. He just — arranged things. He arranged the world so that doing what he wanted felt like the only sensible thing to do. It was like being steered. You never felt a hand on you. You just kept ending up where he wanted."

Wren closed her eyes briefly.

She knew that hand. She had never felt it, exactly — she had felt its absence, which was worse, the way you feel a missing stair. She had spent nine years believing that the scandal that ended her career was a storm, a random catastrophe of ambition and bad luck: Marcus cutting corners on provenance, the foundation needing a scapegoat, her own testimony — true testimony, she had always believed, *honest* testimony — landing where it landed.

But storms have weather behind them.

* * *

Dominic had gone quiet in the way that made Wren nervous. He was staring at the fire with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

"Renner," he said. "The patron's man. And the expedition goes silent. And the camp is stripped, and the crew scattered, and my father leaves a note saying *D. should understand why*." He looked up. "You knew that name before he said it."

It wasn't a question. Wren had known it wasn't going to be, from the moment the name left Tam's mouth. She had felt the hook go in and had known the line would pull taut eventually, because Dominic Fairweather missed nothing that mattered and pretended to miss everything that didn't, and she had never figured out which category she fell into.

"I saw it once," she said. "Years ago. After the hearing."

The fire popped. Outside, the rain thickened, drumming on the stone like impatient fingers.

"There was a file," she went on, before he could decide whether to be angry. "I wasn't supposed to have it. One of the foundation's junior clerks — a girl I'd mentored, back when I was still someone people mentored — she was horrified by the whole thing, and she copied a few pages for me. Letters about the provenance papers. Internal correspondence. I read them a hundred times looking for the thing that would clear Marcus, and I never found it, and eventually I stopped reading them because reading them was like pressing on a bruise to check if it still hurt." She smiled, without humor. "It always still hurt."

"But the name."

"The letters were unsigned. But one of them had a routing mark — a collector's agency. And the routing mark had a name attached, in the corner, the way these things do when the sender is important enough to assume you'll know it." She said it plainly, because there was no other way left to say it. "Aldous Renner."

Dominic sat forward. "And you're telling me this *now*?"

"I'm telling you this now because nine years ago I told myself a story about that scandal, and the story was that it was weather. Bad weather. Marcus did something wrong, or was made to look like he did, and I told the truth as I saw it, and we all drowned." She held her hands out to the fire. "But weather doesn't route letters. Weather doesn't buy debt contracts. Weather doesn't *arrange the world*." She looked at him. "Osei's word. Arranged. Dominic — I think I've been standing in the middle of a arranged thing for nine years and calling it a storm."

"Say the rest," Dominic said quietly. "You've got the rest. I can see you carrying it like a heavy bag."

Wren laughed — a short, startled sound, because he was right, and because being seen that accurately by a man who claimed to want nothing from anyone was still, after everything, disorienting.

"The rest," she said. "The provenance papers. Everyone assumed Marcus forged them, or I assumed it, or the foundation assumed it — the story was that he wanted the site badly enough to invent a clean chain of ownership. But Marcus was many things, and careless with people, and absent, and vain about his fieldwork, but he was *not* careless with paper. I knew that better than anyone. We argued about citations, Dominic. He footnoted his *field notes*. A man like that doesn't forge provenance. A man like that alphabetizes his forgeries if he makes them, and he wouldn't make them."

"So if he didn't—"

"Then someone forged them *for* him. Well enough to convince a tribunal, badly enough to unravel under pressure. Someone who wanted Marcus discredited — or, better, wanted Marcus *indebted*. Because that's the trick of it, isn't it?" She was talking faster now, the old corridors opening all at once, doors she'd nailed shut coming off their hinges in her memory. "You don't blackmail a man outright. You ruin him first, quietly, and then — years later — you appear with money and supplies and a reasonable voice, and you *rescue* him. And a ruined man who's been rescued doesn't ask where the rope came from. He just holds on."

The cave was silent except for the rain.

"Renner framed my father," Dominic said. He said it the way you test ice — weight on it slowly, ready to pull back.

"Renner framed your father," Wren agreed, "and I handed him the verdict. I stood up in that hearing room and I testified to exactly what the arrangement required me to testify, because the arrangement was built so that the truth — *my* truth, the truth as I could see it from where I stood — would do the work. I wasn't lying, Dominic. That's the part that's been eating me for nine years. I didn't lie. I was *placed*. I was given a view of the fire that made the smoke look like proof, and I described the smoke honestly, and honest description was the weapon." Her voice cracked, just slightly, and she hated it. "They didn't need me to lie. They needed me to be *credible*. And I was. God help me, I was very credible."

Dominic was quiet for a long moment. Then, in a voice that had lost all its armor and didn't seem to know it:

"He let me believe it. All those years — I let myself believe it, because it was easier to hate him for being a fraud than to miss him for being—" He stopped. Started again. "And the whole time, someone was *keeping* him. Like a specimen. Like something in a jar."

"Kept, and used, and moved," Tam said softly, from across the fire. "Yes. That is what it was like, on the river. Like a man in a jar who had finally found a crack in the glass." He looked up, and his eyes were wet in the firelight, though his voice stayed level. "At the last anchorage, the night before the split, I heard your father tell Mr. Renner no. For the first time. Loudly — well, loudly for him. He said: *the site is not for sale, and neither is the way to it.* And Mr. Renner smiled, and said something I did not understand then." Tam paused. "He said: *Doctor Fairweather, everything is for sale. Some things are simply priced in other currencies.*"

"What currencies?" Osei asked the fire, though the question was really to no one, to the dark.

"The crew," Wren said. "The debts. The monsoon. Us." She looked at Dominic, and the hook in her old scar pulled taut, and for once she didn't mind the pain, because pain with a *shape* to it — pain with a name and an address — was almost a relief. "Nine years ago, Aldous Renner spent my credibility like currency. Now he's spending your father. And if we don't get ahead of him, Dominic, the next thing he spends is the site — and everyone still standing on it."

Dominic stood — or tried to; the cave ceiling had opinions about that, and he ended up half-crouched, which would have been funny under any other circumstances, a tall man folded into an attitude of permanent apology. He didn't look funny. He looked like a drawn bow.

"Then we move at first light," he said. "Faster than Renner. Ahead of Renner. Whatever it costs."

"That's what worries me," Wren said. "Cost is his currency. He *invented* the exchange rate."

Dominic almost smiled. It was a terrible, crooked almost-smile, the first one she'd seen from him that had his father somewhere in it, and it frightened her more than the rain did.

"Then we'll pay in something he can't price," he said.

Outside, far off down the flooded valley, a sound rolled through the gorge — not thunder, or not only thunder. A deeper note under it, rhythmic, almost deliberate, like something striking stone in the wind. Tam's head came up. Osei went pale.

"The bells," Tam whispered. "That is the gorge of bells. That means we are close to the old terraces — closer than we thought." He swallowed. "And Mr. Renner's boats were faster than ours. If we can hear the bells from here..."

"Then so can he," Dominic said.

Wren looked at the fire, at the two exhausted porters, at the rain-walled dark beyond the cave mouth, and felt the nine-year-old wound in her chest reorganize itself — not healing, not yet, but finding, at last, a direction to bleed in.

Toward Renner. And toward whatever he was about to do at the end of the gorge of bells.

"First light," she agreed. "And Dominic — we don't stop for anything. Not weather, not water, not *anything*."

He nodded once, and outside, the bells rang again, patient as a clock, counting down something none of them could see yet.
