## 17. Ambush at the Ford

The river they needed to cross was called the Sessimi by the porters and "that bloody thing" by Wren, who had been studying it from the bank for a full ten minutes with the expression of a woman reading a letter she already knew was bad news.

"It's not a river," she said finally. "It's a rumor of a river. It's what happens when a mountain has an argument with the sky."

"Can we cross it?" Dominic asked.

"We can cross it," said Tamu, the elder of their two remaining porters, in the tone of a man who had crossed many rivers and buried several friends.

Dominic looked at the water. It ran wide here, maybe forty meters, brown and muscular, braiding itself around gravel bars that surfaced like the backs of sleeping animals. The flood channel in the middle moved fast enough to make his eyes hurt trying to follow it. On the far side, the trail climbed away through bamboo toward the terraced foothills where the boot prints — always fresh, always a day ahead — disappeared into country no map had ever bothered to insult.

A day ahead. They had been a day behind Marcus's party for three days now, and the gap refused to close, which meant whoever was moving so fast and light either knew the ground intimately or was being driven by something worse than urgency.

"Ford's shallowest at the gravel bar," Tamu said, pointing. "Waist-deep to the bar, then the deep channel, then waist again. We rope together. Bags high."

"And if someone goes under?" Wren asked.

Tamu considered this with genuine philosophical interest. "Then we pull them out."

* * *

They crossed in the late morning, under a sky the color of wet slate. Dominic went first with the rope, testing each step, and found the porters' assessment accurate: waist-deep, then a heart-stopping moment where the bottom dropped away and the current took him like a hand on his collar before his boots found the bar again, then waist-deep once more. Wren came second, bag held over her head, swearing with a fluency that would have impressed sailors. The porters came after, steady as furniture.

On the far bank, wringing out his shirt, Dominic allowed himself one small, stupid feeling of accomplishment. They had crossed the bloody thing. The trail continued. Somewhere up there, his father was moving inland with a message that said *D. should understand why*, and Dominic still didn't understand why, but he was closer than he'd been nine years ago, which was a distance measured in silence rather than kilometers.

It was Wren who noticed.

She had turned back toward the crossing — Dominic assumed to check the rope — and gone very still in the way she did when her academic mind snagged on something. She had a whole vocabulary of stillness, he was learning. There was *I've-found-a-typo-in-my-own-translation* stillness, and *this-carving-is-older-than-it-has-any-right-to-be* stillness, and now this one, which was new, and which he did not like at all.

"Dominic." Her voice was quiet. "Look at the near bank."

He looked. The gravel shore where they'd started. The bamboo. The—

"The birds," she said.

There were no birds. All morning the riverbank had been loud with them — small brown flitting things, a heron, something raucous and yellow that Tamu had called a thief-bird with evident affection. Now the near bank was silent, and the silence had a shape. It sat in the treeline about two hundred meters downstream of the ford, and it was watching them.

"Tamu," Dominic said, without raising his voice. "How many men does it take to empty a riverbank of birds?"

Tamu didn't turn around. That was the thing about the good ones, Dominic thought; they never turned around. "More than four," Tamu said. "Fewer than twenty."

"Options?"

"Run uphill. Or swim back. Or stand here and be polite."

"I've tried being polite," Wren said. "It doesn't scale."

Then the treeline gave up pretending.

* * *

They came out of the bamboo in a loose crescent, eight of them, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who had done this often enough that it no longer qualified as exciting. Two carried rifles slung across their backs — not brandished, just present, the way weather is present. The others carried machetes and the flat expressions of professionals. Their clothes were wrong for soldiers and right for salvagers: mismatched, practical, recently rained on.

"Downstream side stays open," one of them called out, in accented trade-Velloran. He was younger than the rest, with a red bandana and the air of a man delivering lines he'd rehearsed. "You'll want to stay on the upstream side. Nobody gets hurt on the upstream side."

"That's reassuring," Wren murmured. "I love a man who specifies."

"Give them the page," Tamu said quietly, at Dominic's shoulder. "Paper is not worth blood."

But it wasn't paper they wanted first. The leader — a broad man with a shaved head and a scar that forked at his jaw like a river delta — walked forward until he stood ten paces off, and looked at each of them in turn with the mild thoroughness of a customs officer.

"You're the son," he said to Dominic. Not a question.

Dominic felt that land somewhere behind his ribs. "And you're the welcoming committee."

"I'm Kesin. I work for Mr. Renner." The man said the name the way people say the name of a church they don't attend but respect. "Mr. Renner asks me to tell you: you are welcome to follow. He says the young Fairweather pilots boats well and reads water better than most, and it would be a shame for talent like that to drown in country it doesn't know."

"He's been asking about me."

"Mr. Renner asks about everyone. It's how he stays alive." Kesin smiled without warmth. "He also asks me to take the page from the journal. The one under the stone at the camp. You have it in your breast pocket. Left side."

Wren made a small sound beside him — half outrage, half professional admiration. "We haven't stopped since dawn," she said. "How—"

"There was a man on the ridge above your camp last night," Kesin said, as if explaining arithmetic to a child. "He watched you read it by firelight. He watched you fold it. He watched which pocket." He shrugged. "Mr. Renner believes in preparation."

Dominic's hand had drifted, without his permission, to his left breast pocket, where the folded page sat against his heartbeat like a coal. *Moving inland. D. should understand why.* Nine words in his father's cramped hand, and apparently the most valuable piece of paper on the river.

"And if I say no?"

Kesin's smile widened by a millimeter. It was somehow worse. "Then my colleagues become less polite, and I become sad, because I liked the pilot. But the page comes with us either way. Mr. Renner is not a cruel man, but he is a complete one."

Here is what Dominic learned about himself in the next four seconds: he was going to fight anyway. Not because it was smart — it was categorically, almost impressively stupid, eight against five with rifles on the other side — but because some part of him that had spent nine years handing things over, walking away, leaving towns before dawn so he'd owe nobody nothing, looked at this shaved-headed functionary and simply refused. His father had written those nine words for him. Whatever else Marcus Fairweather had taken from the world — his mother's patience, his sister's childhood, his own son's faith — he had left this page under a stone like a hand reaching back through the dark, and Dominic would not hand it to a man who said *complete* when he meant *ruthless*.

"No," Dominic said.

Kesin sighed, genuinely regretful. "Upstream side," he reminded his men, almost kindly. "Nobody gets hurt on the—"

Wren threw the gravel.

It was, Dominic would think later, the single most effective act of academic aggression he had ever witnessed. A full double handful of coarse river shingle, delivered with a scholar's precision into the eyes of the nearest rifleman, followed immediately by her shrieking *"RUN!"* in a register that scattered what few birds remained for half a kilometer.

The world became noise and motion. Dominic grabbed Tamu's arm and drove uphill through the bamboo, which fought back with whips and hooks. Behind him: a rifle crack, shockingly loud, and the whip-snap of a round passing overhead — a warning shot, or a miss, indistinguishable in the moment and identical in effect. Someone shouted in Velloran. Wren was running beside him now, bag abandoned, hair full of leaves, wearing the expression of a woman who intended to survive out of spite.

"The page!" she gasped. "Tell me you kept the page!"

"Left breast pocket!"

"If they shoot you, aim for the pocket!"

They broke out onto a game trail and ran along it, downhill toward the river again — Tamu shouting directions in a language Dominic didn't speak but understood perfectly, because every language has a word for *not that way*. The second porter, a wiry teenager named Besh who had barely spoken in three days, was suddenly everywhere at once, cutting branches aside, hauling Wren over a root she'd have broken an ankle on.

They burst from the bamboo onto the gravel bar — the same bar they'd crossed an hour ago, now between them and the far bank like a promise. The deep channel churned beyond it, brown and furious.

"They won't follow into the water," Tamu panted. "Renner's men fear the river. It's why they hire boatmen instead of becoming them."

Small comfort. Because between them and the far bank, in the shallows at the edge of the deep channel, Besh had stumbled.

* * *

Later, Dominic would try to reconstruct exactly how it happened, and fail. There was a root, or a hole in the gravel, or simply the river deciding. Besh went down hard, the rope tangling, and the current took him sideways with the casual strength of something that had been practicing for ten thousand years. The boy surfaced once, twice, arms windmilling, and the third time he surfaced he was further out, in the deep channel, where the water moved like freight.

"BESH!" Tamu was already in to his knees, rope paying out, but the angle was wrong, the current was dragging the boy downstream toward a strainer of fallen trees, and Tamu knew it, and his face was doing something Dominic never wanted to see again.

Dominic went in.

The cold hit him like a slammed door. He swam the way his father had taught him in another life — *angle upstream, let the river help you, never fight it straight on* — and caught Besh's wrist on the second stroke, and then the river had them both, and the world was brown roar and the taste of silt. The boy was strong and terrified and fighting him, which was the real danger; drowning people climb whatever floats. Dominic got an arm around his chest from behind, locked it, and put his mouth against the boy's ear.

"Stop. Stop fighting. I have you."

Whether Besh understood the words or just the tone, he went limp enough to steer. Dominic angled for the gravel bar, feet finding bottom, and then hands were on them both — Tamu, Wren, all of them dragging the boy onto the stones, where Besh lay coughing up half the Sessimi while Tamu held him and made low sounds that were either prayer or scolding, possibly both.

Which is when Dominic realized two things, in order:

First, that during all of it — the swimming, the choking, the dying a little — his left hand had stayed clamped flat over his breast pocket, and the page was still there, pulp-damp but intact, pressed against skin that had forgotten everything else.

Second, that the ambushers had used the diversion to take something after all.

Because when he got his breath back and looked up, Kesin stood on the near bank with the journal page — *the other one*, the spare copy Wren had transcribed at the camp, weighted under a stone of its own — held delicately between two fingers like a specimen. And beside him, at the treeline, stood a man Dominic had never seen before and recognized instantly anyway.

* * *

He was smaller than Dominic had imagined, which was the first betrayal. In the stories the porters told, in the shape of the fear that traveled ahead of him down the river, Aldous Renner should have been large. Instead he was a neat, silvering man in a linen shirt that had no business being clean this far upriver, standing with his hands clasped behind his back at the water's edge as though waiting for a ferry. He wore spectacles. He had the tan of a man who holidays deliberately.

He did not shout across the water. He didn't need to; the river had gone quiet around him, or perhaps Dominic had.

"Mr. Fairweather." Renner's voice carried easily, pleasant, pitched like a dinner-party anecdote. "I confess I hoped we'd meet under better circumstances. Though I suppose these are the only circumstances on offer, this season."

Dominic stood on the gravel bar, soaked to the bone, bleeding from a bamboo scratch he hadn't noticed acquiring, and said nothing, because everything available to say seemed designed to prove something to the wrong audience.

"You have your father's stubbornness," Renner went on, conversationally. "Marcus, too, preferred to learn things the expensive way. I want you to know I bear you no ill will. Truly. What I'm doing here is — well, it's scholarship, of a kind. Expensive scholarship. And you're a working man; you understand cost." He tilted his head, and behind the spectacles his eyes were patient and absolutely certain, the eyes of a man who had already read to the end of the book. "Your father made a bargain with me. A generous one. When he honors it, everyone goes home rich, or at least dry. You could make it easier on him. Sons do that, sometimes. Make things easier."

Beside Dominic, Wren had gone rigid. He could feel her fury coming off her like heat off a stove — and underneath it, he realized, something older and more complicated, because she knew this voice too, had heard it years ago in rooms where careers ended.

"You have the copy," Dominic called back. "Keep it. It says the same nine words."

"Oh, I know what it says." Renner smiled, and it was a good smile, warm even, which was the worst thing about it. "What it says isn't why I want it. Paper carries hands, Mr. Fairweather. Pressure. Habits. Your father's hand tells me things his words won't." He turned the page over delicately, examining its blank back. "For instance, it tells me he wrote it quickly. Worried. Which means he's worried now, which means the flooding is ahead of schedule, which means we're all on the same clock after all." He tucked the page into his breast pocket — the left one, Dominic noted, and hated him a little for the elegance of it. "Do give my regards to the boy. And do go home, if you're wise. The river's rising faster than any of us planned."

He turned and walked back into the treeline, unhurried, certain, a man leaving a shop he intended to return to. Kesin lingered a moment longer, and — absurdly — touched two fingers to his brow in something like a salute aimed at Dominic, before following.

Then the near bank was empty, and the birds began to come back, one thieving yellow voice at a time, as if nothing had happened at all.

* * *

They didn't recross. There was no point; the trail continued on this side, climbing, and the light was going, and Besh needed dry clothes and a fire. They made camp in the lee of a rock overhang, and Tamu fed the boy sweet tea from the thermos with the tenderness of a man feeding a rescued bird, and Besh, wrapped in every blanket they owned, insisted repeatedly that he was fine and was ignored by everyone.

Wren sat down next to Dominic at the fire. For a while neither of them spoke. The river talked instead, low and continuous, the way it had talked all day, all week, all monsoon.

"He took the transcription," she said at last. "Not the original. He knows we have the original, and he took the copy anyway, because—" She stopped, and laughed, short and bitter. "Because he wanted us to know he could. It's a message. Everything that man does is a message."

"He knew my name before we ever met him." Dominic stared into the fire. "He knew about the page. He knew which pocket. He's known about us since the dock, Wren. Maybe since the telegram."

"Yes."

"He said my father made a bargain with him." The words tasted like river water. "Traded himself, or traded the site. Either way, my father is walking ahead of us toward that man on purpose, and I just watched the man who owns him tip his hat and walk into the trees."

Wren was quiet a moment. Then she reached over and, with great deliberation, took the original page out of his breast pocket herself, unfolded it, and read the nine words aloud into the firelight, as if recharging them.

"*Moving inland. D. should understand why.*"

"He still hasn't told me why," Dominic said.

"No," Wren agreed. "But Renner just told us something better. He said the flooding is ahead of schedule." She looked up at him, and the firelight caught the exhaustion and the iron underneath it. "Your father isn't running from us, Dominic. He's racing the water. And if we're a day behind him now, we'd best stop being a day behind him tomorrow."

Above them, far up the black valley, thunder rolled — or something that wasn't quite thunder — and the river, patient as a creditor, began, very slowly, to rise.
