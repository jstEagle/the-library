## 18. Blood in the Water

The porter's name was Tam, though in the first hour after the ford nobody used it. He was simply *the wounded one*, the *him* in every sentence — *Can you carry him? Is he breathing? Keep him upright* — as if naming him might make the wound more real than it already was.

It was a machete cut across the back of the shoulder, deep and ugly, delivered by one of Renner's men who had wanted the journal page more than he'd wanted to be careful. Tam had taken it protecting the satchel, and the satchel was empty now, its contents crossing the river in Renner's possession, and that arithmetic — a man's blood for a page of paper — sat in Dominic's chest like a swallowed stone.

"He's not going to bleed out," Wren said, for the third time. She was walking beside the litter they'd lashed together from two paddles and a rain tarp, her hand resting on Tam's uninjured shoulder in a way that managed to be both comforting and clinical. "But he's not going to walk the inland track either, and he can't lie in wet ground overnight. He needs a proper healer, thread, and something to fight the fever."

"And you know where to find all three of those things in the middle of the Velloran basin," Dominic said. He was carrying the front of the litter. His arms had stopped complaining an hour ago, which he suspected was worse than complaining.

"I know where to find a healer who owes me a debt," Wren said. "The thread and the fever are her business."

"Owes you a debt." He glanced back at her over the litter pole. "You've been in this basin before."

"I've been *everywhere* before, Dominic. That's what a career in ruins gets you. A map of people who are annoyed with you and a smaller map of people who aren't."

"And she's on the smaller map."

"She's on a map of her own, honestly. Suri doesn't keep maps. Suri keeps *accounts*." Wren's mouth did something complicated. "I paid for her daughter's passage downriver during the fever year. She's been trying to repay me ever since, and I've been refusing, because I didn't do it to be repaid, and because watching her try is one of the few entertainments I have left."

Ahead, the two remaining porters — Kesin and the boy, Anlu — moved through the undergrowth with the easy, economical stride of people for whom this country was a language rather than a landscape. Kesin had not spoken since the ford. He walked with his machete drawn, and every hundred meters he stopped and looked back the way they'd come, and every time he looked back, Dominic found himself looking too.

The jungle had gone quiet after the ambush. Not silent — the Velloran was never silent; something was always dripping, droning, or dying — but quiet in the way a room goes quiet after an argument. Renner's men had melted back across the water with their prize, unhurried, and that unhurriedness was the worst part. They hadn't chased. They hadn't needed to. They had what they'd come for, and they knew exactly where Dominic and Wren were going, because there was only one place to go: inland, toward the vault, toward Marcus.

"Kesin," Dominic said quietly, when the older porter dropped back alongside. "How far to this village?"

Kesin considered him. He had a broad, seamed face that gave away nothing cheaply. "Two hours, if the flood hasn't taken the lower path. Suri's village moves, you understand. Not the people — the *village*. When the river rises, they rise."

"The whole village moves?"

"The houses. They're built to float. You'll see." Something in Kesin's expression loosened, just slightly, for the first time since the ford. "It is a good thing to see, when you have spent a day thinking about water."

Dominic had spent the entire day thinking about water, most of it in the form of the ford, the brown flood channel that had swallowed the bridge and nearly swallowed Wren, and the moment her hand had slipped from his and the river had taken her under. He didn't let himself think about that part directly. He approached it sideways, the way you'd approach a dog you suspected of biting. What he thought about instead was the weight of her in his arms when he'd pulled her out — lighter than he'd expected, coughing, furious, alive — and the way she'd said *thank you* not with words but with a single sharp nod, as if gratitude were a debt she'd entered into a ledger she kept somewhere behind her eyes.

"Fairweather." Her voice now, from behind. "You're drifting left. The path drifts left, not you."

He corrected without answering, and heard her small huff of almost-amusement, and decided to count it as a victory.

* * *

The village announced itself by smell first — woodsmoke and river fish and something green and medicinal — and then by the extraordinary sight of it: a dozen houses resting on the flooded plain like sleeping birds, each one mounted on great bundles of bamboo and sealed barrels, tethered to one another and to the drowned trees by ropes as thick as a man's wrist. The whole settlement breathed with the water. It rose and fell, rose and fell, and the people moving between the houses crossed on gangplanks that flexed like the backs of animals.

"They built *for* the flood," Wren said softly. She had gone still in the way she went still at ruins — and then she caught herself, because this wasn't a ruin, it was a living thing, and living things deserved a different kind of attention.

A woman met them at the first gangplank. She was sixty if she was a day, small and upright, with silver-streaked hair bound at the nape and the forearms of someone who had spent a lifetime doing precisely what she pleased. She took one look at Tam, one look at the wound, and one look at Wren, in that order.

"You," she said.

"Hello, Suri," Wren said. "I've brought you—"

"You've brought me a machete wound and four strangers and a debt I have been trying to settle for *six years*, and now you stand on my plank and say *hello, Suri* as though we are neighbors discussing fish." She was already waving the litter forward, barking instructions in the local tongue that Kesin and Anlu translated with their bodies, lifting, turning, carrying. "Get him inside. Boil water. Anlu — the red tin, second shelf, and do not drop it this time, the scar tissue is *still* angry with you."

The boy went pale and obeyed at a run.

Dominic set down his end of the litter and stood there, abruptly useless, watching Wren get absorbed into the machinery of the household as though she'd never left it. Suri directed her with the same brisk contempt she directed everyone, and Wren took it with the same faint, private smile, and Dominic realized he was seeing something he hadn't known existed: Wren Calloway, *known*. Wren with a history in a place, with people who had opinions about her, with a red tin on a second shelf.

It made him feel like a man reading someone else's letters.

"You," Suri said, materializing in front of him. She looked him up and down with the unhurried thoroughness of a customs officer. "You are the pilot."

"I'm— yes."

"You look like your hands shake."

"They don't."

"Good. Then you can hold a needle steady, because my eyes are fifty years old and Tam is going to need a second set of stitches before morning, and I do not trust the girl with a wound this deep. She is a scholar. Scholars sew like they are mending a book." She thrust a stool, a needle, and a spool of fine gut into his hands. "Wash. Then come."

* * *

He held the needle. It was, he would think later, the strangest hour of a strange week: crouched in a floating house by lamplight, closing the shoulder of a man he'd known for two days, while Suri directed him in a mixture of trade tongue and gesture, and Wren hovered at the edge of the lamplight being pointedly *not* a scholar.

"You're doing it wrong," she said, at one point.

"You said you weren't watching."

"I'm not watching. I'm standing here, not watching, and you're doing it wrong. Smaller bites. You're not lacing a boot."

"She's right," Suri said. "Smaller bites."

Dominic took smaller bites. Tam, stretched on his stomach with his face turned away, made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a groan, and murmured something in his own language that made both Suri and Kesin snort.

"What did he say?" Dominic asked.

"He said," Kesin translated, "that the foreigner sews like his father. Who also could not sit still and also took too much thread."

The needle stopped.

The lamplight hissed. Somewhere outside, water slapped against the hull of the house, patient as a clock.

"His father," Dominic said carefully, "has never sewn anything in his life."

Kesin looked at him for a long moment, and there was something in the look that Dominic couldn't read — something that wasn't quite pity and wasn't quite apology. "You would be surprised," Kesin said, "what a man learns, when he decides to learn it."

* * *

Later, when Tam was sleeping under a mosquito net with his shoulder dressed and a fever draft working through him, Suri fed them all — river fish in a broth that tasted of lemongrass and forgiveness — and the story came out the way stories come out in small houses at night: sideways, in pieces, prompted by nothing.

It was Anlu who started it, the boy, because boys always start it. He was mending a fishing net by the lamp, badly, and he said, "Is it true you came upriver because of the quiet foreigner?"

"The what?" Dominic said.

The boy looked at Kesin. Kesin looked at Suri. Suri, unbothered, refilled Dominic's bowl.

"The foreigner who came through in the dry season," Kesin said at last. "Before the rains. He came from downriver, alone except for two guides, and he stayed four days."

"Foreigners come through," Dominic said. "Surveyors, collectors—"

"Not like this one." Kesin turned his bowl slowly in his hands. "This one came with money and spent none of it on himself. He spent it on *us*. Old Somchai's roof had a hole the size of a canoe — the foreigner mended it, badly, and paid Somchai's nephew to mend it properly. The school boat had a cracked hull. He paid for the sealing and then helped carry the wood, and dropped a plank on his own foot, and laughed about it." Kesin's mouth moved toward a smile. "He asked questions. Not the questions collectors ask — *how much, how old, who owns it*. He asked what the village needed. He wrote the answers in a book."

Dominic's bowl had become very interesting. "What did he look like?"

"Tall. Grey in the beard now. Sunburnt. Hands like—" Kesin made a shape in the air, the shape of a man who had spent fifty years handling stone and rope and other people's artifacts. "Hands that had worked. He wore a hat that had been repaired so many times it was more repair than hat."

Wren had gone very still beside him. Dominic was aware of her stillness the way you're aware of weather.

"What was his name?" Dominic asked. "Did he give a name?"

"He gave the name Marcus," Kesin said. "But we would have called him the quiet foreigner regardless. He was quiet the way deep water is quiet." He paused. "He asked after the gorge, at the end. The gorge with the bells. He asked whether the flood ever closed it. And when we told him it does, in the worst years, he wrote something in his book and looked — I don't have the word. *Relieved*. He looked relieved, that the river could close the door behind him."

"Why would that relieve him?" Dominic asked, and his voice came out wrong, rough at the edges, and he heard it and couldn't fix it.

Kesin considered. "Because a door that can close," he said, "can also be kept."

* * *

Dominic didn't sleep. He sat on the gangplank outside Suri's house with his boots off and his feet in the warm black water — a thing he would never have done a week ago, before a river had tried to drown Wren Calloway and he'd learned, in one sick lurching moment, exactly how much the water could take — and he turned the evening over like a stone with something underneath it.

A roof, mended. A school boat, sealed. A plank dropped on a foot, and laughter. A book of *needs*, not of *values*. A man who looked *relieved* that the river could close a door behind him.

That was not his father. That was a stranger wearing his father's repaired hat.

His father was a man who had once, memorably, been presented with a village facing relocation — a real village, with real people, on the route of a survey — and had spent the evening drafting a letter about how the data from the site would be *irreplaceable*. His father had missed birthdays, funerals, a graduation, an appendicitis, and nine consecutive years of everything. His father was a headline and an absence and a name on a foundation letterhead that Dominic had spent a decade not opening.

And yet.

*He sews like his father,* Kesin had said. A throwaway line. Except that Kesin had met Marcus, and Dominic had not, not for nine years, and so Kesin knew a version of the man that Dominic had no access to — a version who sat still, and took too much thread, and apparently mended roofs in river villages for no reason anyone could invoice.

The water lapped against his ankles. Somewhere across the flooded plain, a night bird made a sound like a question.

"You're doing the thing," Wren said, from behind him.

He hadn't heard her come out. "What thing?"

"The thing where you decide what a person is and then defend the decision against all evidence." She lowered herself onto the plank beside him, a careful arm's length away. "I watched you do it to me for two hundred kilometers. You're very good at it. It's almost a skill."

"Kesin described a man who mends roofs."

"He did."

"My father doesn't mend roofs. My father *excavates* roofs. There's a difference."

"Is there?" Wren pulled her knees up. The lamplight from inside caught the edge of her face. "Because I've been thinking about it, and I'm not sure the difference is what you think it is. I knew him for eleven years, Dominic. I watched him argue with museum boards for three years over a single repatriation — lost, by the way, lost badly — and I watched him give his lecture fee to a dig crew's families when the season collapsed. Quietly. I only found out because one of the wives told me, years later, and made me promise not to tell him I knew." She was quiet a moment. "He was never the man the articles said he was. And he was never the man you think he was either. He was something in the middle that nobody got a clean look at, including, I suspect, him."

"You testified against him."

"Yes." No flinch. She'd stopped flinching at that somewhere on the river, around the time she'd told him about the pressure from the foundation, around the time he'd refused to believe her. He was aware, distantly, that the refusal was getting harder to maintain, the way a wall gets harder to maintain when the ground under it keeps moving. "I did. And you don't believe why. And I'm not going to re-litigate it on a gangplank at midnight. I'm telling you something else." She turned to look at him. "The man in that village — the quiet foreigner — I believe Kesin. Every word. Because I've met that man too. I just met him years ago, and I lost him to the foundation and to Renner and to whatever else was squeezing him, and I have spent six years telling myself the man I knew was the real one and the man in the papers was the mask." Her voice did something small and unsteady. "And now you're telling me there was a third one. A roof-mender. And I don't know what to do with that, except that it means the squeezing was worse than I thought, and he was still — *still* — finding ways to be decent in the gaps."

Dominic said nothing. The water held his ankles like a warm hand.

"Your father sent me a letter," Wren said. "A month before he went dark. Asking forgiveness. I told you that on the far side of the flood channel, when you were wringing the river out of me, and you didn't answer, and I didn't expect you to." She stood, brushing off her trousers. "But here's what I keep coming back to. A man who asks forgiveness is a man who has been doing an accounting. And a man who mends roofs in secret is a man who's been making payments against a debt nobody let him name." She paused at the door. "Get some sleep, Dominic. Suri wants us on the water at first light, and she says the gorge road floods by mid-morning, and she says—" the ghost of a smile, "—she says the quiet foreigner also never slept the night before a hard passage. She recognized you before you said your name. She said you sit the same."

The door closed behind her.

Dominic sat a while longer, feet in the river, in a village that floated, in a country his father had moved through paying debts and mending roofs, and he tried to hold the two men in his head at once — the absent one and the quiet one — and found they wouldn't fit in separate rooms. They kept leaking into each other. The hat was the same hat. The hands were the same hands.

*He asked after the gorge,* Kesin had said. *The gorge with the bells.*

Dominic looked upriver, into the dark where the water came from, where a door could close and be kept.

In the morning, Suri pressed a small wrapped bundle into Wren's hands — medicine, thread, and, over Wren's objections, a debt formally declared *paid*, which started an argument that lasted the length of the gangplank and was, Dominic suspected, the closest thing either woman had to a love language. Then the porters shouldered the litter, Tam riding pale but feverish and grumbling, and they took to the flooded track with the water already rising around their ankles.

Half an hour in, Kesin stopped at a fork in the drowned path, where a marker post stood with fresh notches cut into it — three notches, arrow-deep, in a hand Dominic had last seen on excavation reports twenty years old.

"He left signs," Kesin said, "for whoever followed. He said his son would read them."

Dominic stared at the notches — at the *provision*, the message, the father he was only now beginning to meet — and felt the last of the wall under his feet shift.

"Which way?" he asked.

Kesin smiled for the second time in two days. "The way he knew you'd read."
