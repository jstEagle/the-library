## 19. A Different Man

The village was called Sen-Maru, though the headman pronounced it three different ways in the first ten minutes, and Dominic got the sense that even the villagers had stopped correcting him.

It sat on a bluff above the river, a scatter of houses on stilts with tin roofs gone soft and orange with rust, connected by walkways that creaked like old men getting out of chairs. The flood had spared it — mostly. The lower terraces of the rice fields were underwater, and two boats were tied to the railings of houses that had, in a wetter year, been a full street back from the waterline.

They had carried the injured porter, Tam, up from the bank on a stretcher of lashed poles and a tarp, and the healer — a small, fierce woman named Amaya who owed Wren a debt from some year neither of them would explain to Dominic — had taken one look at the gash in his leg and begun issuing orders in a voice that carried across three villages, possibly four.

Which left Dominic with nothing to do. Which was, he was learning, the most dangerous condition a man could be in.

"You're staring at the rice," Wren said. She'd come up beside him on the walkway, a mug of something hot and brown in each hand. She passed him one. It tasted like tea that had been through a war, but it was hot, and he drank it.

"I'm staring at the water where the rice should be."

"Same thing this time of year." She leaned on the railing. Below them, children were fishing off a half-drowned fence post with lines made of thread and bent pins, with the supreme confidence of children who had never once considered that the fish might not cooperate. "Amaya says Tam will keep the leg. She says it in a way that suggests she's personally offended by the alternative."

"Good."

"You should sleep."

"I slept."

"You closed your eyes for forty minutes on the walk up and flinched twice."

"That *was* sleeping," Dominic said. "The flinching is a bonus feature."

Wren didn't laugh, exactly, but the corner of her mouth did something she would have denied under oath. She was quieter since the ford — since he'd hauled her out of the flood channel by the strap of her pack and she'd spent the rest of the day pretending her hands hadn't shaken. She'd told him about Marcus's letter. About the forgiveness asked for a month before the expedition went silent. He still didn't know what to do with that. It sat in his chest like a swallowed stone, and every time he thought he'd gotten used to it, he moved wrong and felt it again.

Before he could find something deflecting to say, the headman appeared.

His name was Osei, and he was a broad, silver-templed man who moved with the unhurried authority of someone who had never once been late for anything, on the theory that everything would simply wait for him. He wore a shirt that had once been white and had since achieved a kind of philosophical gray, and he had been watching Dominic since they arrived with an expression Dominic couldn't read and didn't like.

"Fairweather," Osei said. Not a question. He said it the way you'd say *storm front*.

"That's me."

"You are the son."

Dominic set down the mug. "I'm a boat pilot."

"Mm," said Osei, in the tone of a man who had just been told the rain was technically water. "Come. You will see something."

"I really should—"

"Come," Osei said again, already walking, with the serene certainty of a man for whom *no* was a word that happened to other people.

Dominic looked at Wren. Wren looked at the river. *Traitor*, he thought, and followed.

* * *

The headman's house was the largest in the village, raised high on ironwood posts, with a wide covered gallery that looked out over the drowned fields. Osei led him up the notched-log stairs, past a dog who regarded Dominic with the weary tolerance of a customs officer, and into a room that smelled of smoke and dried fish and decades of the same cooking fire.

There were photographs on the walls.

That was the first strange thing. In a village this remote, photographs were rare — expensive, difficult, the kind of thing that happened to other people. But here they were, a whole row of them, framed in salvaged wood and hanging slightly crooked: village weddings, a new boat being launched, children holding certificates, a man in a suit shaking hands with someone off-frame.

And there, in the middle of them, unmistakable, was Marcus Fairweather.

Dominic stopped walking.

His father was thinner in the picture than Dominic remembered — the famous Marcus of the magazine covers had been broad and sun-cured, all jaw and certainty, standing with one boot on a crate of artifacts like the world was a podium. This man was leaner, grayer at the temples, wearing a work shirt with the sleeves rolled and mud to the knee. He wasn't posing. He was laughing — head tipped back, eyes shut, one hand on the shoulder of a boy of maybe seven who was holding up a fish with both hands like an offering.

The fish was enormous. The boy was triumphant. And Marcus Fairweather — Marcus Fairweather, who had once missed Dominic's graduation because a trench in Velloran was *at a delicate stage* — was laughing like the fish was the best thing that had ever happened to him.

"When did he—" Dominic's voice came out wrong. He cleared his throat. "When was this taken?"

"Two seasons past." Osei had come up beside him, and now he stood with his hands clasped behind his back, watching Dominic watch the photograph. "The rainy season. The river took the roof off Maren's house — Maren is the boy's mother. Widow. Two children." He said it without drama, the way you'd read a supply list. "Your father was passing through with his expedition. He stayed four days."

"Four days." Dominic's father had once summarized an entire Christmas in a postcard. *Digging is going well. Weather cold. Love, Dad.* Dominic had kept the postcard for years before he understood that it wasn't love, it was a receipt.

"He rebuilt the roof," Osei said. "He mended the walkways. He argued with Amaya about medicine — she won, but it was close. And when he left, he left this." He lifted the frame off its nail and held it out.

Dominic took it the way you'd take something that might be rigged.

Up close, the photograph was worse. Better. Worse. Marcus's shirt was buttoned wrong — one hole off, the collar crooked, the kind of mistake a man only makes when he's been interrupted mid-dressing by something more important than his collar. There was mud on his hands. There was a scratch healing on his forearm. And the boy — Maren's son, some small solemn-faced child in the other pictures — was leaning into Marcus's side with the total, unguarded trust of a child who has decided a thing and cannot be argued out of it.

Dominic had spent nine years building a version of his father out of evidence. Absent. Ambitious. A man who loved the past because the past never asked him to stay for dinner. He'd built it carefully, plank by plank, the way you'd build a case, because a case was something you could win. And now here was this — this laughing stranger in a crooked shirt, holding a fish, mending roofs, *paying debts* — and the case fell apart in his hands like wet paper.

"This isn't him," he said.

Osei raised an eyebrow, which on a face like his was a full paragraph.

"The man I know—" Dominic stopped. Started again. "My father didn't do this. He didn't fix things for people. He *excavated* things. He arrived with a camera crew and left with the interesting parts." He heard his own voice rising and made himself stop. Made himself breathe. "He missed my mother's last year. He missed—" He handed the frame back, too fast. "This is some other man."

"Perhaps," Osei said, taking the photograph and hanging it back on its nail, straightening it with two fingers. "Or perhaps you knew a different man than the one he was trying to become."

"That's very generous."

"I am a headman. Generosity is my profession." But his eyes weren't smiling. "He asked us for nothing, your father. That is rare in a man of his kind. The ones with cameras and crews, they take. He asked what the village needed and then he did it, badly at first—" Osei's mouth twitched. "His first roof leaked. Amaya has not let him forget it."

Dominic stared at the photograph again. *His first roof leaked.* As if there had been a second. As if his father had come back, or written, or — no. Two seasons past. That was before the expedition went silent. Before the journal page under the stone. Before *D. should understand why.*

He hadn't understood why. He was standing in a house full of evidence and he still didn't understand why.

"Did he say anything?" Dominic asked. "When he left. Anything about—about his family. His children."

Osei was quiet for a moment. Outside, the rain had started again, soft on the tin roof, a sound like slow applause.

"He said," Osei said at last, "that he had two children who would be grown by the time he was worth knowing. And that he hoped he was wrong about the arithmetic." He turned. "I did not understand him. Perhaps you do."

Dominic's throat was too tight to answer.

* * *

He found the gallery railing again because his legs wanted somewhere to go and the railing was the nearest thing that would hold still. The rain had thickened. Below, the children had abandoned their fishing and were running the walkways with the shrieking joy of the newly released, and the river under the bluff was the color of strong tea and moving like it had somewhere to be.

Wren was already there. Of course she was. She'd followed him up from the healer's house at a distance — he'd seen her in the corner of his eye and been too proud to acknowledge it, which was its own small humiliation — and now she stood a few feet down the rail, hands wrapped around a fresh mug, saying nothing.

She was very good at saying nothing. It was, he was beginning to suspect, the most dangerous thing about her.

"So," she said, after a while.

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were winding up."

"I was *breathing*," Wren said. "There's a difference. One of them is involuntary."

Dominic gripped the rail. The wood was rain-slick and warm from his hands. "He fixed a roof, Wren. He mended walkways. He lost an argument about medicine." His voice did something humiliating on the last part and he pushed through it. "There's a photograph of him laughing. Laughing. I went through nine years of his letters — his *postcards* — and I couldn't tell you what his laugh sounded like. I genuinely don't know. And this kid—" He gestured at the house behind them, at the crooked frame visible through the doorway. "This kid gets four days and a fish and the laugh."

Wren said nothing.

"You're not going to say *I told you so*?"

"Would it help?"

"No."

"Then I'm not going to." She sipped her tea. Rain dripped off the gallery roof between them, a thin silver line. "For the record, I don't know that man either. The one in the picture. The Marcus I worked with gave a keynote address the same week his daughter had surgery and answered questions about ceramic dating while she was still under anesthesia." She paused. "I'm not saying that to defend you. I'm saying it because I don't understand it either, and I've had longer to sit with not understanding than you have."

Dominic looked at her. Rain had gotten into her hair and she hadn't bothered with it. There was a tiredness around her eyes that had nothing to do with sleep.

"The letter," he said. "The one he sent you. Asking forgiveness."

"Yes."

"When did he change?" It came out rougher than he meant. "Because men don't just — men don't *become* that. The roof-fixing, the fish, the—" He made a vague gesture that was meant to encompass the entire impossible photograph. "Something happened to him. Something made him decide the expedition ledger mattered less than a widow's roof, and I want to know what it was, because the man I got instead of that was a postcard."

"Maybe nothing happened," Wren said quietly. "Maybe he just finally did the arithmetic."

"What arithmetic?"

"The kind where you count what you've spent." She turned back to the river. "He told me once — years ago, before everything — that he was saving up. That when he'd found something big enough, something that mattered, he'd have earned the right to go home. He thought discovery was a currency you could exchange for being a person." Her mouth twisted. "I thought it was rubbish at the time. I still think it's rubbish. But it wasn't a lie. It was a man waiting for permission he'd have been better off just taking."

Dominic said nothing. The rain filled the silence for him.

Because that was the thing he couldn't say out loud, the thing sitting under his ribs like floodwater: if Marcus had been waiting to earn his way home, then the failure wasn't absence. The failure was the *waiting*. Nine years. His mother's last year. His sister's whole childhood, spent in the margins of a man's ambition. All of it not because Marcus didn't care, but because he cared *wrong* — because he'd built a tollbooth on his own road home and then stood behind it, waiting for a discovery big enough to pay the fare.

And the cruelest part — the part that made Dominic's hands curl on the rail — was that it had almost worked. Somewhere in the last two years, in some village he'd never heard of, his father had apparently paid the toll. Had learned to fix roofs and lose arguments and laugh at fish. Had become, two seasons too late and a river too far, the man Dominic had spent his whole childhood waiting for.

*He had two children who would be grown by the time he was worth knowing. And he hoped he was wrong about the arithmetic.*

"He wasn't wrong," Dominic said. His voice was flat and steady and cost him everything. "The arithmetic. I'm grown. I've been grown a long time."

"Then maybe," Wren said, "the point was never whether he was worth knowing in time. Maybe the point is whether you're willing to know him now." She held up a hand before he could answer. "I'm not asking you to forgive him. I'm the last person who'd ask that. I'm asking you to keep the photograph. Both photographs. The one on the wall and the one in your head. A man can be both. God knows I've been worse than both."

Dominic looked at the river. The rain was coming down harder now, drumming the tin, and somewhere down the bank their boat sat moored with a paid watchman aboard, and somewhere upriver his father was — alive, he had to be alive, the smoke and the tin cup and the trail said *alive* — moving toward something with a collector's agent at his back.

"I keep thinking," Dominic said, "that if I'd answered any one of those letters—"

"Then you'd have gotten the postcard man," Wren said. "Not this one. This one cost him something. You can hear it in the picture." She nodded back toward the doorway, toward the crooked frame and the laughing stranger in the wrong-buttoned shirt. "That laugh isn't a man who's having a nice day. That's a man who's surprised by his own life."

* * *

They stayed two days. Amaya decreed it — Tam's fever needed breaking, and Amaya's decrees had the force of weather — and Dominic spent them the way he spent all forced stillness: badly. He split firewood that didn't need splitting. He re-caulked a section of the walkway that three village children then immediately tested to destruction. He asked Osei forty questions about the river ahead and got thirty-nine answers and one long look that said *you are your father's son and you both think work is a place to hide.*

Wren, meanwhile, sat with Amaya for hours over the healer's bundles and tinctures, talking in a low rapid professional shorthand, and came away on the second evening with a pouch of poultices, a vial of something that smelled like it could strip paint, and an expression Dominic had learned to be nervous about.

"You're planning something," he said.

"I'm always planning something. That's why I'm alive."

"Something I'm not going to like."

Wren weighed the vial in her hand. "Amaya says there's a faster route upriver from here. Old logging cut, on the far bank. Saves a day and a half around the bend, if the water cooperates."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we get to find out what kind of pilot you are." She smiled, thin and sharp. "Also — Osei says a boat came through yesterday evening. Heading upriver. Motor wrapped in rags to muffle it. Armed men. He didn't like the look of them."

The warmth went out of the evening all at once. "Renner."

"Or Voss. Or nobody." Wren tucked the vial away. "But Osei says they were asking at the landing about a pilot's boat moored below the bluff. Asking about *us*."

Dominic looked down the bank, through the rain, to where their boat sat dark against the water — and felt, with the cold certainty of a man who has spent his life reading rivers, that something was already wrong.

"We leave at first light," he said. "Before then, I'm checking on my boat."

Wren was already reaching for her pack. "I'll get the watchman."

But when they came down to the landing in the gray wet dark, the watchman's fire was cold, the mooring line hung slack and cut clean —

— and their boat was gone.
