## 20. The Watchman's Tale

The river had opinions about our return trip, and all of them were rude.

We went back downstream in the grey hour before dawn, when the water ran low and glassy and the mist sat on the surface like something waiting to be introduced. Dominic piloted the borrowed dugout with the economy of a man who had never once wasted a motion on beauty, and I sat in the bow watching the banks slide past and trying not to think about how many days we'd lost to floodwater and ambush and the particular slowness of moving injured men through jungle.

Two days. That was what the detour to the healer's village had cost us — two days of poultices and bitter tea and old women clucking over Teodor's gashed leg like he was a wayward grandson. Necessary days. I kept telling myself that. Necessary, and yet every hour spent ashore was an hour Renner's column moved closer to whatever Marcus had found, dragging my former partner along as a reluctant key.

"You're doing the thing," Dominic said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare at the middle distance and your jaw does arithmetic." He nodded at my face without looking at it. "You've been doing it since the ford."

"My jaw is not doing arithmetic."

"It's carrying the one right now."

I let that go, mostly because I couldn't think of a rebuttal that didn't prove his point. The truth was I'd been turning over the same three facts since dawn: Renner had Marcus. Voss had vanished upriver two nights ago with her crew and her guns. And somewhere ahead of both of them, a site existed that Marcus Fairweather had kept off every official record for reasons that were becoming less mysterious and more heartbreaking by the day.

A man who hid a discovery from his own foundation did not do it for glory. A man who left a journal page weighted under a stone with *D. should understand why* scrawled across it — that man was writing letters he didn't expect to live to post.

"Boat's around the next bend," Dominic said. "Past the snag with the herons."

I remembered the snag. I remembered the herons too, white as laundry against the dark trees, offended by our existence. What I did not remember — because it hadn't been there when we'd moored — was the smell.

Diesel. Faint, but unmistakable, threading through the green smell of the river like a lie through a testimony.

Dominic smelled it the same moment I did. His hands went still on the tiller for exactly one heartbeat, then resumed their work, smoother than before, which was how I was learning to read him: the calmer he looked, the worse the news.

"That's our fuel," he said quietly. "Or it was."

* * *

The watchman was gone.

Not dead — there was no sign of violence, no blood, nothing dragged into the brush. Just gone, along with his cooking pot, his blanket roll, and, judging by the flattened grass, most of his dignity. He'd been a lean, taciturn man named Eku who had accepted our money with the expression of someone being paid to watch a coffin, and apparently he had interpreted the job description loosely enough to include abandoning it.

"He ran," I said, standing where Eku's little fire circle still held its ashes. "Or he was paid to run. Or—"

"Or he never intended to stay." Dominic crouched by the cold fire, touching the ash. "Still damp underneath. He left this morning. Maybe last night." He rose and turned toward the boat, and I watched his face do something complicated.

The *Persephone* — Marcus's old river boat, the one we'd chartered out of Sarn's Reach with money I'd rather not account for — sat lower in the water than she should have. Not sinking. Just deflated, the way a dog looks when you take away its ball. Her deck hatches stood open. Her rail showed fresh rope burns where something heavy had been winched off her.

Dominic went aboard first. I followed, and the inventory assembled itself in my head with terrible efficiency: the spare fuel drums, gone. The reserve cans lashed under the gunwale, gone. The tool chest — gone. Even the case of trade goods we'd been hoarding like dragons, machetes and salt and bolt cloth, gone. Someone had stripped her with the thoroughness of locusts and the courtesy of professionals. They hadn't even bothered to make it look like accident.

"They took everything that burns or cuts," I said.

"And left everything that floats." Dominic's voice was flat, which I knew by now meant furious. "Voss."

"You can't know that."

"I can." He pointed at the wheelhouse door, and only then did I see it: a square of oilcloth, folded once, weighted on the deck with a river stone. The kind of stone you'd find anywhere. The kind of stone Marcus had used to weight his journal page.

I felt the echo of that down my spine. Apparently so did Dominic, because his jaw did its arithmetic again before he reached for the note.

He read it once silently. Then he handed it to me without a word, which I understood to be either a courtesy or a test.

The handwriting was quick, slanted, confident — a hand used to writing orders in bad light.

*Fairweather. Took the fuel, the tools, and the trade goods. Kept your hull intact, which cost me, so don't waste the favor. Your father hired my crew six weeks ago and cut us loose at Sarn's Reach without a coin. I've been following his trail since, and now I'm following yours, and I notice we keep ending up in the same places. So here are my terms: I want a share of whatever Marcus Fairweather found. One part in five, negotiated in person, before anyone goes any further inland. In exchange you get your fuel back and thirty hours of my crew's muscle, which you will need, because the man you're chasing has forty and a radio. Meet me at the mouth of the Kessin channel, two days hence, dawn. Come armed if it makes you feel better. It won't matter.*

She had signed it simply: *O.V.*

Below the signature, in smaller script, almost an afterthought:

*I know about Renner. I've known about Renner longer than you have. Ask your partner why a collector's agent was at the university the year the provenance papers burned.*

I read that last line twice. Then a third time, while the river slapped against the hull and Dominic watched me with the patience of a man who already knew what it said.

"She knows," I said finally. "About the scandal. About Renner being there."

"Does she." It wasn't a question. It also wasn't an accusation, which surprised me. I'd braced for it — for the look people gave me when the old story surfaced, the polite recalibration, the *ah, so you're the one who testified.* Nine years of that look teaches you to hear it coming.

But Dominic just leaned against the wheelhouse frame and rubbed the bridge of his nose. "Of course she does. She worked for him. She probably watched the whole thing from the outside, which means she saw more than either of us did from inside it." He paused. "Did Renner really come to the university that year?"

The question landed softly, but it landed. "You remember that."

"I remember my father coming home with his career in a sack and refusing to say one word in his own defense. I remember everyone saying your testimony finished him." He said it without heat, which was somehow worse than heat. "So. Was he there?"

"Yes." I made myself hold his eyes. "Renner came twice that autumn. Officially he was consulting the department on acquisition ethics — the irony could have powered a small city. Unofficially he met with the dean, and the dean met with the provost, and three weeks later the committee reopened the provenance question that had been closed for a year." I heard my own voice go dry and careful, the voice I used for lectures. "I told the committee what I'd seen. Which was true. The papers Marcus brought back from Karveth were inconsistent. What I didn't tell them — what I couldn't prove then and only pieced together later — was that the inconsistencies had help. That someone had gotten into Marcus's field files between the dig and the submission."

"And you think that someone was Renner."

"I think Renner needed Marcus compliant. A disgraced man doesn't get funding. A disgraced man takes jobs he'd otherwise refuse." I stopped, because the next part was the part I'd carried alone for nine years, and saying it aloud to Marcus Fairweather's son felt like setting down something heavy in a canoe — you had to be very sure of the balance. "And I think the pressure came to me through channels I didn't recognize until years later. My department head suddenly had a fellowship to dangle. Suddenly my own fieldwork needed 'clarification.' I testified honestly, Dominic. But I testified in a room that had been built for me before I ever walked into it."

The river filled the silence. Somewhere upstream a bird made a sound like a rusty hinge complaining.

"Why didn't you say any of this nine years ago?" Dominic asked. Not cruelly. Genuinely, the way he asked most things — as though the answer might actually change something.

"Because I had no proof, and because the one man who could corroborate half of it wasn't speaking to me." I gestured vaguely upriver, toward the gorge, toward everything. "Because he let me believe he believed I'd betrayed him, and I was too proud to chase after him and beg. Because pride is cheaper than groveling, right up until it isn't." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Your father and I are the same animal, Dominic. We'd both rather lose everything than ask."

"No," Dominic said slowly. "He'd rather lose everything than *owe*. There's a difference. I'd know. I invented it."

Despite everything, I smiled. "Runs in the family, then."

"Unfortunately." He pushed off the wheelhouse and walked the deck, checking lines, checking the bilge, running his thumb along the rail where the rope burns were freshest. I watched him work and recognized the ritual for what it was: a man buying time to think by pretending to inspect things. His father's habit, though neither of them would thank me for the observation.

"One in five," he said at last.

"That's what she wrote."

"That's steep."

"It's also fuel, thirty crew, and forty of Renner's men answered by thirty of hers." I ticked it off on my fingers. "Plus she's the only person on this river who seems to know where Renner is going before he gets there. You don't track a column like that through monsoon country by luck. She's had eyes on him since Sarn's Reach."

"Which means she let us walk into the ford ambush knowing it was coming." His voice hardened. "Her note says she's been following *us*, Wren. She watched Renner hit us and did nothing."

"Or she arrived too late, same as she arrived too late to stop the stripping of this boat, and she's managing her resources like a professional instead of a martyr." I held up the note. "Read it again. *Kept your hull intact, which cost me.* That's not a raider talking. Raiders don't apologize to hulls. That's a contractor talking — a woman who wants a working relationship and is opening negotiations from strength because it's the only position she's ever been allowed to hold."

Dominic looked at me for a long moment. "You've negotiated with people like her before."

"I *was* people like her, for about four years after the scandal. Broke, competent, and furious." I tucked the note into my jacket. "Trust me. I know the species."

* * *

We argued about it through the morning, which is to say we talked about it the way two people talk when they've already decided and are only negotiating the terms of admitting it.

Dominic's objections were sound: Voss couldn't be trusted; a fifth of the find was extortion; bringing thirty armed salvagers within a mile of an archaeological site was how sites got gutted. My answers were equally sound: we had no fuel, no tools, no muscle, no radio, and a two-day head start that was shrinking by the hour; a fifth of a properly documented site was worth infinitely more than all of a looted one; and if we didn't reach Voss first, Renner would — and Renner settled labor disputes the way he settled academic ones, by burning the building down with everyone inside.

By noon we'd run out of sound objections and started making unsound ones, which is how I knew we were done.

"We'll need to leave Teodor and the others somewhere safe," Dominic said, staring at the chart he'd spread across the wheelhouse desk. "Can't drag wounded men into a parley."

"The healer's village will take them. Mariam owes me more than she admits."

"Mariam. The one who threatened to break my fingers for touching her mortar."

"She threatens everyone. It's a greeting."

He almost smiled. It was strange, watching that happen — watching the permanent wariness in his face loosen by degrees, like ice going out of a river. Two weeks ago he'd have sooner swum the flood channel than accept help from anyone, including me. Now he was planning logistics around a village healer's grudges and considering terms with a woman who'd robbed him at anchor. The river was remaking him, and I don't think he'd noticed yet.

"And if Voss's parley is a trap?" he asked.

"Then we're armed, we're shallow-draft, and we'll have seen her crew's positions before we commit. Also—" I tapped the note, "—she told us where and when. Traps don't come with invitations. Ambushers prefer you uninformed."

"Renner sent invitations once. To my father. Funded his whole expedition."

"And look how that's going for Renner." I shrugged. "Voss isn't Renner. Voss wants wages. Renner wants ownership. There's a whole moral universe between those two appetites."

Dominic was quiet a moment. "My father would have said something pompous here. About how greed is predictable and therefore navigable."

"Did he actually say things like that?"

"Constantly. Usually while losing his keys." He rolled up the chart. "Fine. Kessin channel, dawn, two days. We go see the woman who robbed us and offer her a partnership." He said it the way a man says *we go see the dentist.*

"There's the Fairweather spirit."

"Don't." But the corner of his mouth betrayed him.

* * *

We made the healer's village by late afternoon, delivered Teodor and the two other walking-wounded into Mariam's formidable care, and stood on her dock while she extracted promises from me with the efficiency of a customs officer. By the time we pushed off again the light had gone long and gold, and the river had softened into something almost kind.

It didn't last. Rivers never do.

We anchored that night in the lee of a bluff, and I took first watch because Dominic hadn't slept properly since the ford and because I wanted the quiet. The mist came up the way it always did, and the frogs started their evening parliament, and I sat on the deck with the note in my lap reading O.V.'s last line again.

*Ask your partner why a collector's agent was at the university the year the provenance papers burned.*

She'd written *your partner.* Not *Calloway.* Not *the woman who testified.* Partner. As if she'd already seen the shape of us — the pilot and the archivist, the son and the witness — and filed us under something sturdier than circumstance.

Behind me, the wheelhouse hatch scraped. Dominic came out with two tin cups of the terrible coffee we had left, and sat down beside me without asking, which was new, and handed me one, which was newer.

"Couldn't sleep either?" I asked.

"I slept. I dreamed about the boat leaking. Woke up checking seams that aren't there anymore." He drank. Made a face. "This coffee is a war crime."

"It's the last of the beans. Drink it with respect."

He drank it with respect, or at least with silence. For a while we just watched the water move, black and patient, carrying everything downstream except us.

"Dominic," I said, because the watch was long and the night was honest. "When we find him. Your father. What are you going to say?"

I felt him consider not answering. It was practically audible.

"I don't know," he said finally. "Nine years of things to say and none of them fit in my mouth anymore. Mostly I think I want to know why. Why the site, why the secrecy, why leave a page under a rock addressed to a son who hasn't spoken to him since—" He stopped. Started again. "Since I told him I'd rather haul salvage than dig up dead civilizations. He didn't argue. That's the part I've never forgiven, maybe. He just... agreed. Let me walk. Like I was one more expedition he'd decided not to fund."

"Maybe he thought arguing was what you wanted him to do, so he refused to give you the scene."

"That's either very wise or very convenient."

"With Marcus Fairweather it's usually both."

That startled a laugh out of him — short, rusty, real. And then, because the night was honest and getting more so: "Wren. If Voss knows about Renner and the university, other people do too. When this ends, when the documents come out — your name is going to be in the middle of it again. Testifying, un-testifying, whatever we call it. Are you ready for that?"

I looked at the river instead of at him. "I spent nine years being ready for it. Ready is easy. It's hoping that's hard." I turned the cup in my hands. "But yes. If clearing his name costs mine a second time, I'll pay it. I owe the truth that much. I owed it nine years ago and I paid in the wrong currency."

"You didn't pay wrong," Dominic said. "You paid early. Everyone else is just catching up."

I didn't have an answer for that, so I drank my war-crime coffee and let the frogs talk.

Downstream, somewhere past the dark bend of the world, a woman with our fuel was counting her rifles and her grievances, and a man with our future was marching on a vault with my oldest friend as his prisoner-key. Two days to the Kessin channel. One chance to turn a thief into an ally before the water — and Renner — closed over everything.

"Dawn watch is yours," I said, standing.

"Wren." I paused at the hatch. Dominic was looking at the note in my jacket pocket as if he could see through the fabric. "The part in five. If it comes to it — if we have to choose between paying her and protecting the site —"

"Then we renegotiate," I said. "That's what partners are for."

I felt him weigh the word behind me. *Partners.* He didn't object to it. From Dominic Fairweather, that was practically a signature on a contract.

I went below, and slept, and dreamed of bells.
