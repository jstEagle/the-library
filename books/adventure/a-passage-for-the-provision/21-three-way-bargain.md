## 21. Three-Way Bargain

The driftwood fire threw more smoke than light, but after three days of rain that felt like a luxury. Dominic fed it another branch and tried not to think about how visible they were — a beacon on the riverbank for anyone with eyes and a grudge.

Odile Voss had a grudge. She also had forty gallons of diesel, which was a better argument than most.

Her crew had stripped his boat to the fuel lines and left the watchman trussed like festival goose, but she'd left the note too, and the note had brought them here: a bend in the river two miles downstream, where her own vessel sat at anchor behind a screen of deadfall, and Odile Voss sat across the fire from them with a pistol laid openly on her knee like a piece of fruit she'd grown bored of holding.

"So," she said. "The son comes to negotiate."

"The son came to get his boat back," Dominic said. "The negotiation is what happens if he doesn't."

Wren made a small sound beside him that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. He'd learned over the past week to read those sounds. This one meant *you're doing it again* — the thing where he opened negotiations by insulting the person who held all the cards. It had worked exactly once, with a customs official in Puerto Verde, and Wren maintained that even then it had been luck rather than talent.

Voss smiled. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a woman doing arithmetic she already knew the answer to. "Your boat is worth maybe eight hundred dollars in a fair market. Your fuel is gone. Your watchman —" she nodded toward the man, who sat apart from the fire rubbing his wrists and radiating the specific dignity of someone who intended to describe this evening to his cousins for years — "was paid for four days and gave me one. I have forty gallons of diesel, eleven people, two rifles I will admit to, and I know these waters better than you ever will, pilot. Tell me again about the negotiation."

"I'll tell you about Marcus Fairweather instead," Dominic said.

Something moved behind Voss's eyes. Not softening. Recalculation.

"He hired you weeks ago. Cut you loose without pay when the money got strange. And now you're sitting on this river with no wages and a monsoon coming down the valley like a debt collector." He let that sit. "You've been tracking Renner's supply barges since Sarn's Reach. Don't bother looking surprised; your wake is easy to read. You're not hunting us. You're hunting *him*, because he's the one holding whatever Fairweather promised to pay you out of."

The fire popped. Downriver, something large shifted in the current — a floating log, or the river changing its mind about where it wanted to be.

"And if you're right?" Voss said.

"Then we want the same thing," said Wren.

*

Wren Calloway had spent the walk down from the mooring composing arguments, which was her version of prayer. Now she set aside the ones that depended on charm — Voss would smell charm the way a cat smelled fear — and went straight for the ledger.

"You're owed wages. Say six hundred dollars, plus hazard pay because Fairweather cut you loose mid-season, plus the cost of the fuel you'd be spending. Call it nine hundred, call it a thousand. Renner will never pay it. Men like Renner don't pay debts; they *are* debts, and everyone around them spends their lives servicing the interest. The only person on this river who can settle your account is sitting next to me, and his credit is good, because unlike his father he has never once promised anyone anything he didn't deliver."

"That's true," Dominic said. "It's the only thing I've ever been praised for."

"It's not nothing," Wren said, and kept going before Voss could redirect. "Here's the shape of it. You supply fuel and muscle. We supply the boat, the pilot — and there is no better pilot on this water, ask anyone at the docks, ask the men who wouldn't take this run — and the route. Whatever salvage comes out of the site, you take an honest share, negotiated now, in front of witnesses, written down. Not a promise. A paper."

Voss turned her pistol slowly on her knee, barrel tracing a lazy arc that included all three of them and none of them. "And why does a woman who testified against Marcus Fairweather want to help him now?"

The question landed the way she'd meant it to — square in the middle of the fire, where everyone had to look at it.

"Because I owe him," Wren said. "Not money. Something worse." She held Voss's gaze. "You want to know if I'm trustworthy. I'm not. Neither are you. That's why we write everything down."

For a long moment the only sound was the river and the rain starting up again somewhere up the gorge, a sound like applause from an audience very far away.

Then Odile Voss laughed — a short, genuine bark, quickly suppressed, as though laughter were another expense she hadn't budgeted for. "Calloway. I know that name. You were famous once, weren't you? Before."

"Infamous, technically. It pays worse."

"And you'd sit at my fire and bargain like a fishwife after everything?"

"After everything," Wren agreed, "I find I have very little left to lose and excellent reasons to want other people to lose less. It makes one generous."

*

Dominic watched the two women circle each other and understood, with the particular clarity that comes from exhaustion and bad coffee, that he was the least dangerous person at this fire.

He'd known Voss's type his whole working life. Salvagers ran on grievance the way engines ran on diesel — it wasn't fuel you could buy anywhere else, and it burned dirty but it burned long. Her crew had stripped his boat not out of malice but bookkeeping: assets against receivables. What she couldn't forgive was being made a fool of, and Marcus Fairweather had made her one, and so she'd come upriver after the thing that had humiliated her, the way a dog returns to a fight it lost.

He could respect that. He'd been running the same engine for ten years, just pointed in the opposite direction.

"Terms," he said, and pulled the notebook from his jacket — the same oil-stained notebook he kept his tide tables in, because he trusted nothing else to survive this river. "One. Fuel returned tonight, full tanks, plus twenty gallons reserve. Two. Your crew rides as hired hands, five dollars a day each, same as I'd pay any deckhand, food included, mutiny excluded."

"Mutiny's extra," Voss said.

"Mutiny's a breach of contract and I leave you wherever we happen to be."

"Which is why the contract matters." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for the first time looked at him without the performance — just a tired woman with a business problem. "Three. My share of salvage. Calloway says honest. Honest is a word men use right before the arithmetic starts. Name a number."

Dominic glanced at Wren. She gave him a look that said *you name it, this is the part you're for.*

"Ten percent," he said. "Of documented value. Which means everything gets catalogued — Wren catalogues it, she's the best archivist I've ever seen waste her talents, and I include my father in that. Nothing gets sold off a boat deck in the dark. If it's gold, you get ten percent of the gold. If it's papers, you get ten percent of whatever papers fetch, and if they fetch nothing because they turn out to be priceless and go to a museum—" he shrugged— "then you get ten percent of the finder's fee, and my word that the fee gets negotiated hard."

"And if your father found nothing?" Voss said. "If the great Marcus Fairweather dragged eleven people up a river to die for empty rooms?"

"Then you get your thousand dollars and my apology, and you can tell everyone you met the son and he was even cheaper than the father."

That got the laugh again, longer this time, and some of her crew along the fireline shifted and grinned, and Dominic felt the room tilt — not toward trust, never that, but toward the weaker, more useful thing: shared interest.

"Twelve percent," Voss said. "And my crew eats at your table, not separate."

"Eleven, and your cook takes a shift at the pump. We've got a seam leak that needs watching."

"Done." She spat in her palm and held it out, then caught herself, glancing at Wren. "Sorry. Habit."

"Don't apologize," Wren said. "Spit makes it binding. Ink just makes it deniable." She spat in her own palm and shook, and then Dominic did, and her grip was dry and strong and brief as a bird's landing.

*

They wrote it out afterward by lantern light, Wren drafting in her precise hand while Voss dictated amendments and her second — a huge, silent Tongan named Faleolo who communicated mostly through eyebrow — witnessed. Dominic kept the fire fed and kept his hand near his knife, not because he expected trouble but because Voss's crew kept drifting past to look at him, the way sailors look at a new engine they haven't decided whether to admire or sabotage.

One of them, a young woman with a shaved head and a burn scar down one arm, stopped beside him longer than the others.

"You piloted the Gorge of Bells at flood stage?" she asked. "Last season. The mail run."

"Who told you that?"

"Everybody." She looked at him with frank appraisal. "They said the river took the mail boat sideways and you backed her out of the eddy backwards, in the dark."

"They exaggerated. It was dusk."

She grinned and moved on, and Dominic realized he'd just been vetted, and passed, and that somewhere upstream a gorge full of hanging stones was waiting to test whether the reputation would hold.

Wren finished the document, read it aloud twice — Voss corrected one clause about desertion penalties, Wren countered, they settled — and three copies were signed. Voss kept one. Wren kept one. The third went into the tin box bolted under the wheelhouse seat, next to the flare gun and the photograph of a family that no longer assembled.

"There," Wren said, capping the pen. "Now we're all equally capable of betraying each other, and equally expensive to betray. That's the most stable arrangement there is."

"You're a romantic," Voss said.

"I'm a realist with a romantic's vocabulary. It confuses people, which is useful."

*

They loaded the fuel by lantern and moonless dark, a chain of bodies passing jerry cans from Voss's hold to Dominic's deck, and the boat settled lower in the water and rode truer, and Dominic felt something in his chest unknot that he refused to name as hope. Hope was for people with margins. This was just ballast.

Faleolo took the first pump watch without being asked, settling beside the leaking seam with the patience of a man who had decided, provisionally, that these strangers were worth keeping dry. The watchman — paid, apologized to, and plied with Voss's own rum as damages — curled up in the bow and was asleep before the anchor came up.

Voss stood at the rail beside Dominic as he brought the engine up. Behind them her own vessel swung at anchor, half her crew staying aboard under Faleolo's cousin, the rest embarking as the newest and least welcome members of the expedition.

"You understand," she said quietly, under the engine noise, "that if this goes wrong, I will take everything you have and leave you walking."

"I'd expect nothing less."

"And if it goes right?"

Dominic watched the black water slide past, and thought about a tin cup, and a journal page weighted under a stone, and a message he still didn't understand: *the provision was never the gold.*

"Then you get paid," he said, "and I get answers. Everybody walks away rich in their own currency."

Voss studied him for a moment, then nodded once, sharply, the way she might acknowledge a wave correctly read. "Your father," she said, turning to go below, "never talked like that. Everything was glory with him. You talk like a man who wants to *finish* something."

She disappeared down the hatch. Wren appeared in the space she'd vacated, thermos in hand, and poured two cups of coffee that tasted like boiled rope and cost more per ounce than the diesel.

"She likes you," Wren said.

"She threatened me."

"That's how she likes people. Did you notice she didn't post a guard on us tonight?"

Dominic had noticed. He'd noticed and told himself it was a trap. "Maybe she figures we can't run. No fuel to spare."

"Maybe." Wren sipped her coffee and looked upriver, where the gorge walls had begun to close in around them, black against marginally blacker sky, and where — faint, intermittent, almost lost under the engine and the rain — came a sound neither of them could place at first. A ringing. Thin and silver, like a hundred small bells hung in the dark and stirred by wind.

Wren lowered her cup. "Dominic," she said. "Tell me that's the river."

He throttled down. They both listened.

It wasn't the river.

Ahead, out of the throat of the gorge, the sound swelled and faded and swelled again — the landmark from his father's rubbing, the one every mapmaker had missed and every local feared, and they were going to reach it at flood stage, in the dark, with a boatload of armed strangers and a leaking hull.

"Bells," Dominic said, and pushed the throttle forward.
