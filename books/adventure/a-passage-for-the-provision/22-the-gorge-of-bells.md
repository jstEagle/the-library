## 22. The Gorge of Bells

Dominic heard them before he saw them.

He was at the wheel, third watch of a gray morning that couldn't decide whether to rain or merely threaten it, when the sound came threading through the engine's growl — high, thin, almost musical. A chime. Then another, off-key from the first, and then a whole scattered chorus of them, rising and falling with the wind.

"Tell me you hear that," he said.

Wren looked up from the charts she'd been annotating in the bow, pencil tucked behind her ear like she'd been born holding it. "Hear what?"

"That." He tilted his head toward the river ahead, where the walls of the gorge were closing in like a book being shut. "Listen."

She listened. The wind shifted, and the sound came again — dozens of small bright voices, ringing and fading, ringing and fading, as if the gorge itself were tuning an instrument nobody had asked for.

"Oh," Wren said softly. "Oh, that's it. That's the landmark."

From the stern, where Odile Voss had spent the morning cleaning a rifle with the kind of patience most people reserved for prayer, came a dry voice: "The landmark sings. Wonderful. I've been kidnapped by a fairy tale."

Nobody had kidnapped anybody, but Dominic had learned over the past two days that arguing with Voss was like arguing with the current — technically possible, never productive. He throttled down instead and let the boat drift while he studied the water ahead.

The Gorge of Bells — though of course Marcus hadn't called it that; on the rubbing Wren carried, folded soft as cloth from years of handling, it bore a string of notation that Wren translated as something like *the place where the stone remembers the wind* — opened ahead of them like a throat. The river narrowed from forty meters to perhaps fifteen, then less, squeezed between walls of dark limestone that rose sheer on either side, streaked white with old flood lines. And hanging from those walls, suspended on natural stone threads as delicate as fishing line, were hundreds of formations — slender stalactites, some longer than a man, some no thicker than a finger — and every time the wind funneled up the gorge, they swung and touched and rang.

Small ones rang clear as struck glass. The big ones made a sound lower, deeper, almost like a bell heard underwater. Together, in the shifting gusts, they made music that had no pattern and yet never quite resolved into noise.

"It's beautiful," Wren said, and there was something in her voice Dominic hadn't heard before — not the careful academic tone she used for iconography, not the guarded flatness she used for anything touching his father. Just wonder, plain and unguarded.

"It's a death trap," Voss said, coming forward to stand at the rail. She nodded at the water. "Look at your flood stage, pilot."

He was already looking. The monsoon had been feeding the river for days, and here in the narrows it showed its teeth. The water ran fast and brown and muscular, folding into standing waves where the current hit the walls and rebounded, and along both banks the normal channels — the slack water a sensible pilot would use — were drowned entirely. There was no eddy to catch, no backwater to rest in. Once they committed to the gorge, they were committed all the way through.

"How long?" Voss asked.

"At this flow?" Dominic did the arithmetic without wanting to. "Maybe four minutes. Maybe five if we live long enough to be slow about it."

"And the stones?"

"Some of them hang low enough to matter." He pointed. Halfway down the visible stretch, a formation the thickness of a mast hung nearly to the waterline, swaying gently, ringing its low note whenever the wind gusted. At flood stage, the clearance under it would be — he squinted — perhaps a meter. Perhaps less. Perhaps nothing at all, if the water rose another hand's width between now and when they got there.

Voss looked at the stone, then at him, then at the stone again. "You're going to take a wooden boat through that."

"I'm going to try."

"With my fuel in her tanks."

"With your fuel in her tanks," he agreed. "Which is why I'm going to try *carefully*."

Wren had come aft to join them, chart in hand. "The rubbing shows the passage continues past the gorge. Marcus marked a landing on the far side — a shelf above the flood line. If the bells are the landmark, then the site is close. Today-close."

"Then today we go through," Voss said simply, and went back to her rifle, which was apparently her way of expressing confidence.

Dominic turned the wheel and brought the boat's nose around to face the narrows.

* * *

There is a particular silence that falls over a boat in the last hundred meters before something dangerous. The engine keeps talking but nobody else does. Hands find things to hold. Even the river seems to lean in.

Dominic had run bad water before — more times than he could count, in boats better than this one and boats considerably worse — but he'd never run anything like this, because nobody had. The gorge wasn't a channel so much as a suggestion, and the suggestion was: *good luck.*

He talked himself through it aloud, quietly, the way his father had taught him to read strata — one layer at a time, don't look at the whole cliff, just the next band of stone. *Entry current sets left. Compensate before the throat, not in it. Watch the rebound off the right wall — it'll want to shove us into the left, and the left has rocks under that brown water, I can hear them working.*

The first wave hit them thirty seconds in.

It came off the right wall in a green shoulder of water taller than the gunwale, and it took the boat and lifted her and threw her sideways, and for one lurching moment Dominic was steering a thing that was no longer entirely a boat — half vessel, half projectile — and then she came down, slammed, shipped a ton of cold brown water over the bow, and answered her helm.

"Still with us?" he shouted.

"Define 'us,'" Wren shouted back, soaked, clinging to the chart table with both hands, her precious notes pressed under her elbow like a mother shielding a child.

"The boat!"

"Barely!"

"Good enough!"

The gorge closed around them. The walls came so near he could have reached out and touched the limestone, could see the fossil shells embedded in it like buttons on a coat. Above, the hanging stones swung in the wind of their own passage — the boat's speed drove air ahead of her, and the chimes rang faster now, wilder, a carillon gone mad, and somewhere underneath the fear Dominic thought: *he heard this. He stood where I'm standing and heard this and wanted to show me.*

He put the thought away. Later. Everything later.

The big stone appeared around the bend exactly where he'd feared it would be — dead center of the channel, hanging low, its tip trailing in the flood itself, dragging a wake of ripples. Under it, the water boiled smooth and fast and shallow-looking, which meant deep, because brown water lies.

Clearance: he gave it a meter. Maybe a meter and a bit. The boat drew just under a meter with the tanks full.

That math didn't work. He knew it didn't work. So he stopped doing that math and started doing different math.

"Everyone low!" he roared. "Center of the boat! Now!"

They dropped — Wren, Voss, the two porters who'd joined them after the bargain at the fire, all of them flattening against the deck plates amidships. Dominic stayed at the wheel because someone had to, and because the wheel was where he'd decided he would die if it came to that, and because — this was the true thing, the thing he'd admit to no one — because he wanted to see it. Whatever happened, he wanted to be looking.

The trick, if there was one, was weight and angle. A boat heels; a heeled boat draws less on one side than the other. If he could get her leaning hard to starboard as they passed under the stone — throw everything, including himself, against the starboard rail at the exact right moment — the port side might rise just enough. Just barely enough. It would be like threading a needle with a moving needle.

Or it would capsize them in the worst water in the basin. Fifty-fifty. Sixty-forty, if he was generous.

"Voss!" he bellowed. "When I say *lean*, everyone throws themselves starboard! All at once! Don't think, just fall!"

"I don't *fall* on command," Voss said, but she was already bracing.

The stone grew. The wind gusted and it rang its great low bell directly overhead, so close now that he could see the wet sheen of it, the mineral drip building at its tip like slow amber. The channel bent slightly left beyond it, which meant the current would set them right, which meant — fine, fine, it could work, it could actually —

"Now! LEAN!"

He threw the wheel hard left and threw himself across the wheelhouse against the starboard frame, and the boat heeled, groaning, the port side lifting, lifting, the brown water sliding beneath them with a sound like a held breath —

The stone's tip kissed the water six inches off their port rail.

Six inches. He watched it pass. He heard it ring once, softly, almost politely, a bell tolling for a ship that hadn't sunk.

Then the current caught the heeled hull and did exactly what currents do to heeled hulls, which is punish them, and the boat snapped upright and slewed hard right toward the wall, and the next ten seconds were the loudest of Dominic's life — engine screaming in reverse, wheel hard over, the wall rushing close enough to count fossils — and then, somehow, impossibly, they were through the bend and the channel opened and the gorge let them go the way a fist lets go of a bird.

Behind them, the bells rang on, indifferent, beautiful.

* * *

They floated in the widening water below the gorge for a full minute before anyone moved.

Wren surfaced from behind the chart table first, hair plastered to her forehead, glasses fogged, clutching her notes aloft like a survivor raising a flag. "Is everyone —"

"Alive," Voss said. She sat on the deck where the *lean* command had deposited her, rifle beside her, and she was looking at Dominic with an expression he'd never seen on her face before. It took him a moment to identify it, because it was so out of place among her usual inventory of contempt, calculation, and armed patience.

It was respect.

"Six inches," she said. "You passed that stone with six inches."

"Five and a half, probably. I rounded up to be modest."

"You heeled a loaded boat in flood-stage current on purpose."

"It seemed better than the alternative."

"The alternative was turning back."

"The alternative," Dominic said, easing the throttle forward, "was explaining to your unpaid crew why we turned back at the singing door when the treasure was on the other side of it. I've met you, Odile. You'd have shot me."

A sound came out of Voss that on any other human being Dominic would have called a laugh. On her it was more of a controlled detonation, brief and surprising, gone before it could be held against her.

"Pilot," she said, standing, brushing off her trousers with dignity, "when we first met, I sized you up as a boy playing with his father's river."

"And now?"

"Now I size you up as a man who passes under bells with five inches and doesn't spill his coffee." She nodded at the thermos wedged in the wheelhouse bracket, miraculously upright. "I revise my prices accordingly. My crew works cheaper for competent captains. It's a flaw in me."

"Take your time fixing it."

Wren had come up to the wheelhouse, drying her glasses on a rag that had recently been dry itself. She was quiet a moment, watching the gorge walls recede behind them, the chimes fading by degrees until the river's ordinary roar covered them.

"He knew you could do that," she said.

Dominic kept his eyes on the channel. "He's never seen me pilot anything."

"He didn't need to. That's what I keep trying to tell you, Dominic, and you keep not hearing it. He taught you to read water when you were nine years old. I was there. You've forgotten it because you wanted to forget him, but the reading stayed. All of it stayed." She tapped the chart, the rubbing, the whole buried architecture of the expedition. "Why do you think the message said *D. should understand why*? Not the foundation. Not me. *D.* Because you're the only one who ever learned to see what he sees."

The engine hummed. The river ran wide and gray-green ahead, and somewhere beyond the next bend, according to a rubbing carried out of a drowned civilization, his father had found a place worth dying for.

"I don't want to talk about it," Dominic said, but it came out without any edge, and Wren seemed to hear the difference, because she just nodded and went forward to her charts, letting him have the wheelhouse and the silence.

* * *

They found the landing shelf an hour later, exactly where the rubbing promised — a shelf of pale stone above the flood line, sheltered by an overhang, with room enough for the boat to tie off against iron mooring rings so old they'd been worn to a shine by hands centuries dead.

Whoever built those rings had known about floods. Had planned for them. Had, Dominic realized as he made fast and stepped onto stone that hadn't felt a rope in a thousand years, engineered this entire approach — the gorge, the bells, the deep protected channel — as a road designed to survive the very water that drowned everything else.

His father hadn't found ruins. His father had found a harbor.

Voss climbed up beside him and stood scanning the terraces above, where the jungle pressed close and the air smelled of wet stone and green rot and, faintly, unmistakably, woodsmoke.

She smelled it too. Her head came around slowly.

"Pilot," she said, all business again, hand drifting toward the rifle. "Tell me your father's expedition cooked their meals."

"They had a cook. Why?"

"Because somebody," Voss said, nodding at the thin gray thread of smoke rising from the terrace above, "is cooking lunch."

Above them, on the drowned terraces of a city that had outwaited its own flood, someone had made a fire within the hour — and neither the smoke nor the fire nor whoever tended it had any idea they were coming.
