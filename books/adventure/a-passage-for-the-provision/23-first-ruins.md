## 23. First Ruins

Wren had spent thirty years learning to read stone the way other people read letters from home — hungrily, closely, with her thumb tracing the lines as if touch might tell her what the eye missed. So when the terraces rose out of the brown water ahead of them, drowned to their shoulders in monsoon flood, she forgot entirely that she was standing on a leaking boat piloted by a man who barely tolerated her, and pressed both hands to the rail like a girl at a train window.

"Oh," she said. It was not a scholarly observation.

Dominic glanced at her sidelong. "That's it? 'Oh'? You dragged me up half of Velloran for an 'oh'?"

"Give me a moment," Wren said. "I'm having a religious experience and you're interrupting it."

The ruins came at them slowly, the way mountains do — first a suggestion beneath the surface, then a shape, then an undeniable presence. Terraces stepped down into the flooded valley like the ribs of some enormous sleeping animal, each level crowned with broken walls and the stumps of pillars, all of it furred green with a decade's patient moss. The floodwater lapped at the second terrace from the top. Somewhere below all that brown water, Wren knew, were more levels, more chambers, perhaps whole streets — a city holding its breath underwater.

And above the highest terrace, catching the grey light, stood a causeway.

It ran out from the central mound toward the gorge wall, a spine of fitted stone maybe four meters wide, and here was the thing that made Wren's hands go cold on the rail: the causeway was *dry*. The water around it sat lower than its paving by a good half-meter, so that it surfaced like a whale's back — no, not like a whale's back, like a road. Because that was exactly what it was. A road built high enough to ride above the flood.

"They knew," she whispered.

"Knew what?" Dominic cut the engine to idle, and the sudden quiet let the river speak — that deep, muscular mutter of moving water, everywhere at once.

"The floods." She was already digging in her satchel for the field notebook, the pencil stub, anything. "Look at it, Dominic. Look at the *engineering*. That causeway isn't decorative. It's not processional, it's not ceremonial — well, it may be those things too, but primarily it is a road that stays above the waterline during monsoon. They built for the flood. Not against it. *For* it."

Dominic looked at the causeway, then at the drowned terraces, then back at her with the expression of a man who has just realized his passenger knows something useful after all. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning this civilization didn't survive despite the water." Wren heard her own voice go bright and fast, the way it did when the past reached up and grabbed her by the collar. "They survived *because* of how they met it. Do you understand how rare that is? Every other culture in this basin built levees and prayed. These people said: the water is coming, it always comes, so let us build a city that expects it. Let us make the flood a season instead of a catastrophe."

She was aware she was gesturing with the pencil stub. She did not stop.

* * *

Getting ashore was its own small adventure, which was to say Dominic insisted on doing everything himself while Wren stood in the shallows holding coiled rope and offering advice he ignored with impressive consistency.

"Left," she called. "Your left. There's a submerged step."

"I see the step."

"You're about to stand on the step."

He stood on the step, staggered, swore with real creativity, and recovered. Wren bit down on a smile. It would not do to enjoy herself. Enjoying herself implied she'd made peace with any of this — the boat, the man, the nine years of silence between her and Marcus Fairweather that had nothing to do with rivers and everything to do with a courtroom and a lie she'd told under pressure. She was here for her own reasons. Complicated ones. Reasons she had not yet found the courage to say aloud, though they sat in her breast pocket in the form of a month-old letter, soft at the folds from handling.

The causeway took their weight without complaint. Whoever had laid those stones had dressed them to interlock, and the joints were so tight that after ten centuries of floods Wren couldn't have slid a knife-blade between them. She knelt and put her palm flat on the paving. Cool. Solid. Utterly indifferent to her wonder, which she found oddly comforting. Good stone didn't care who admired it. It simply held.

"Marcus's rubbing showed a causeway," Dominic said behind her. He'd gone quiet since the shore, watchful in the way he got whenever his father entered the conversation, even obliquely. "But the drawing didn't give scale. I thought it was a stair."

"It's better than a stair." Wren stood, knees cracking — she was fifty-one and the river had opinions about that — and followed the causeway's line with her eyes as it climbed toward the central mound. "This is the argument the whole site makes, if we let it speak. Come on. The iconography will be on the upper terraces, above the flood line. That's where they'd put the important statements."

"How do you know?"

"Because every civilization does the same thing, Dominic. They write their proudest sentences where everyone can read them, and they keep their secrets downstairs." She started up the causeway. "Governments haven't changed in three thousand years. Only the stationery."

Behind her, she heard him exhale through his nose — the Fairweather laugh, abbreviated. She'd known his father long enough to recognize the family resemblance in it, and she suspected Dominic knew it too, and hated it, and that was why he rationed it out like medicine.

* * *

The upper terraces gave them their first full look at the carvings, and Wren stopped talking altogether, which anyone who had ever worked with her would have recognized as the truest sign of awe.

Friezes ran along the terrace walls in bands, worn but legible, the figures softened by centuries of rain into something like memory. Processions of people carrying vessels. Rows of tall jars. A great serpent rendered in profile — no, not a serpent. She moved closer, heart hammering, and saw that it was a river, drawn as a living thing, with eyes and a long body coiling through the composition, and the people were not fighting it. They were *feeding* it. Setting offerings into its open mouth. Building their houses along its spine.

"Dominic," she said. "Come look at this. Please."

He came. He crouched where she pointed, and for a long moment neither of them spoke.

"That's a flood," he said finally. His finger hovered over a band of carving near the bottom: wavy lines rising up the sides of little carved houses, water climbing to the doorways, and the people — the people weren't fleeing. They'd climbed to their roofs, and they were sitting there calmly, some of them playing what looked like instruments, while the water rose around them and then, panel by panel, receded.

"And there's no panic in it," Wren said softly. "Look at their faces. Or — the suggestion of faces. Nobody's running. Nobody's drowning. The water comes, they go up, the water goes down, they come back down and replant." She traced the sequence with one finger, panel to panel. "It's not a myth of punishment. It's a calendar. This is a flood *almanac*, carved in public, at eye height, where children could read it. They taught their kids the water the way we teach them the alphabet."

Dominic was quiet for a while. When he spoke, his voice had lost its edge. "My father's notes called it a temple complex. Storage cult, ritual granaries, the usual framing. He never mentioned—" He stopped. Started again. "He never said they'd *won*."

"No," Wren agreed. "He wouldn't have seen it yet. He was looking for the vault, the treasure, the thing underneath. Everyone always looks underneath." She sat back on her heels, careless of the wet stone. "But the answer isn't underneath, Dominic. It's right here at eye level, carved where any farmer could read it. They survived because they planned for the water together. Granaries up high. Causeways above flood stage. Seed stores sealed against the wet. This whole city is a *handbook*, and nobody in the last century of scholarship noticed because we were all too busy hunting for gold in the basement."

Something shifted in Dominic's face — a flicker, quickly suppressed, of something that might have been grief or might have been its close cousin, recognition. Wren watched him wrestle with it and generously pretended not to see. She was getting better at that. Nine years ago she'd have said something cutting and clever. Now she just looked out at the drowned terraces and let the man have his private weather.

"He knew," Dominic said at last. Quietly. "Didn't he. Dad knew what this place was. That's why he went dark. Why he kept it off the foundation's records." He turned to look at her, and his eyes were very much his father's, and very much not. "If this gets out wrong — if Renner or the foundation or anyone treats it as a treasure site—"

"Then they'll strip the vault and leave the handbook to rot," Wren finished. "Yes. I know."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon while the light held, Wren sketching and transcribing, Dominic hauling her pack, standing guard, and asking questions that were increasingly hard to deflect. The rain held off, which felt like a personal favor from a sky that owed them nothing.

At the third terrace they found the inscription band — a continuous text running along the wall base, raised characters worn shallow but still traceable. Wren copied what she could and read aloud in fragments, translating as she went, her voice dropping into the cadence she used only for old things.

"'When the great water rises, the houses remember their duty.'" She moved along the wall. "'The seed sleeps dry. The child sleeps high. The grandmother tells the story twice.'" She paused. "I love that. *Twice.* Even three thousand years ago, grandmothers understood repetition is pedagogy."

"Is that a joke?"

"It's a footnote. Keep up."

Further along, the text changed register — became formal, declarative, almost legal. Wren slowed over it, pencil moving, and then stopped entirely.

"What?" Dominic said.

She stared at the characters. Read them again. Read them a third time because the first two readings seemed impossible, and then a fourth because the third one had made her chest hurt.

"'What we saved, we saved for you,'" she read slowly. "'Not for kings. Not for the mountain. For whoever comes after, walking out of their own water.'" Her voice caught on the last line and she had to clear her throat. "'Take it. Add to it. Pass it on.'"

Silence, except the river, muttering below.

"They meant it for strangers," Dominic said. "For *us*. Whoever found this place."

"For whoever needed it." Wren closed her notebook with hands that weren't quite steady. "Do you understand what your father found, Dominic? Not a tomb. Not a treasury. An inheritance, deliberately left, addressed to the future. And he—" She stopped herself. Careful. Careful. But the words had momentum now, and some tired, honest part of her let them run. "And he tried to protect it. From Renner. From the foundation. From everyone who'd turn it into cargo. Whatever else Marcus Fairweather has been in your life, he understood what this place was asking of him."

Dominic didn't answer. He stood looking at the inscription, jaw working, and Wren thought he was going to say something — something that mattered, something that might crack open the nine years between him and his father — and she braced herself to receive it.

Instead he went very still, head tilting, the way he did when the boat's engine changed pitch.

"Smoke," he said.

Wren smelled it a heartbeat later — faint, woodsmoke and something green, threading through the river smell. Coming from beyond the central mound, from the shadowed side where the terraces stepped down toward the gorge.

"Renner's men don't cook over wood fires," she said. "They've got fuel stoves. I saw the crates."

"I know." Dominic was already moving, low and quick along the terrace wall, and there was something in his stride she hadn't seen before — not the restless drift of a man perpetually leaving, but the focused hunger of a man who might be about to find what he'd told himself he wasn't looking for.

Wren shouldered the notebook and followed, her heart doing something complicated between dread and hope.

Beyond the mound, sheltered in the elbow of a collapsed wall, stood a lean-to of cut saplings. Beside it, a fire ring. And resting on a flat stone, placed deliberately — left out, the way you leave a cup for someone you expect —

— a tin cup, dented on one side, glinting dully in the last of the light.

Wren saw Dominic stop dead three paces from it. Saw him stare. And she remembered, with a lurch, a dig site twenty years ago, a younger Marcus Fairweather drinking tea from a battered tin cup he refused to replace, dent and all, because — he'd said, laughing — *a cup remembers every campfire you've shared with it.*

"Dominic," she began gently.

"Someone's alive," he said. His voice was rough. "Someone's alive, and they were here recently, and they left this where it could be found." He looked at her, and for once there was no armor in his face at all. "Wren. Who leaves a cup out like that?"

She looked at the lean-to, at the fresh ashes, at the trail of flattened ferns leading away inland — and felt hope arrive so hard it hurt, exactly the way she'd once been warned it would.

"Someone who wants to be followed," she said.
