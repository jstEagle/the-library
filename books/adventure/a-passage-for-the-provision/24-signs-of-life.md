## 24. Signs of Life

The smoke found them before they found it.

Dominic had been reading the river's moods for half his life — the green bruise of deep water, the pale seam where a sandbar shifted overnight, the particular silence birds kept when something with teeth was nearby. But smoke was different. Smoke was a language with only one word, and the word was *people*.

"There." Wren pointed upstream and to the left, where the flooded terraces gave way to a shelf of higher ground. A thread of gray rose from behind a stand of bamboo, thin and deliberate, the kind of fire someone fed carefully rather than carelessly. "That's not a lightning strike."

"No," Dominic said. His mouth had gone dry in a way that had nothing to do with the heat. "Lightning doesn't burn wet wood. Someone's keeping that alive."

They had left Voss and two of her crew with the boat at the second anchorage — an arrangement nobody trusted and everybody needed — and pushed inland through the drowned terraces for most of the morning. The water here came to their thighs in places, warm as bathwater and twice as opaque, hiding steps and holes and whatever else ten centuries of silt preferred to keep private. Wren had gone quiet an hour ago, which Dominic had learned was either a very good sign or a very bad one.

Now she was quiet again, but it was a different quality of quiet. He could feel her watching him watch the smoke.

"Could be Renner's people," she said carefully.

"Could be."

"Could be a trap."

"Could be."

"You're not listening to a word I'm saying."

"I'm listening fine," Dominic said, already wading toward the bank. "I'm just choosing to ignore you, which is different, and frankly more efficient."

Behind him he heard Wren exhale through her nose — her version, he was beginning to understand, of laughter. Nine days on the water together and he'd catalogued maybe six of them. He was starting to keep count without meaning to.

The bank rose in a series of old agricultural steps, each one built by hands that had understood something the modern world had forgotten: that water wasn't an enemy to be walled out but a guest to be seated properly. Marcus would have loved this. Marcus had probably written four papers about it that nobody read. The thought arrived uninvited, carrying its usual cargo of resentment, and Dominic set it down on the trail like a pack he wasn't ready to open yet.

The bamboo thinned. The smell of woodsmoke thickened, and underneath it — faint, almost imagined — something else. Tin. Char. Old coffee burned down to tar.

Then he saw the lean-to.

It was nothing. That was the first thing he noticed — how little it was. A frame of cut saplings lashed with vine, roofed with palm fronds gone brown at the edges, barely big enough for a man to lie down in. It sat against the shoulder of the terrace wall like something a child might build and then abandon. But every joint was tight. Every lash was doubled. Whoever built it had built it to last exactly as long as it needed to and not one day more, because one day more meant complacency, and complacency got people killed in country like this.

And beside it, ringed with stones, a fire. Still breathing. Coals banked with the particular neatness of someone who expected to come back.

"Wren," Dominic said, and his voice came out strange, flattened, like sound underwater.

"I see it."

"Nobody's here."

"I can see that too."

He should have waited for her. Some part of him — the part that had survived a decade of salvage work by never walking into anything blind — knew he should wait, circle the site, check the ground. Instead his feet carried him forward, and the careful professional who kept other people's boats and other people's secrets watched from somewhere far away as Dominic Fairweather knelt at a stranger's fire pit and touched the coals.

Warm. Not hot. An hour old, maybe less.

"They were here within the hour," he said. "Maybe two."

Wren crouched opposite him, and for once she didn't argue, didn't hedge, didn't offer three alternative interpretations ranked by probability. She just looked at the fire, then at him, and something in her face made him brace himself.

"Dominic—"

"Don't say it yet."

"I haven't said anything."

"You were going to say we don't know whose fire this is."

"I was going to say," she said slowly, "that whoever it is, they're moving fast and light, they're heading deeper into the terraces, and they left their fire burning because they'll be back tonight. Which means—" She stopped herself. He watched her decide whether to finish the sentence, and then finish it anyway, because Wren Calloway had never once in her life let a true thing go unsaid when it mattered. "Which means whoever built this expects to survive the night. And there aren't many people left up here expecting that."

The rain started while they searched the lean-to — a soft monsoon drizzle, more mist than water, beading on the palm-frond roof and dripping a slow metronome onto the packed earth floor. The shelter held a rolled sleeping mat, still dry under oilcloth. A coil of fishing line. A tin plate scrubbed clean. And hanging from a peg driven into the terrace wall, wrapped in a scrap of tarpaulin, a small cache: matches, salt, a stub of pencil, and a folded page torn from a survey notebook covered in handwriting so familiar it felt like a hand closing around Dominic's throat.

He didn't read it. Not at first. He stood there with the page in his fingers and let the drizzle hit the back of his neck and told himself, in the flat salvage-pilot voice he used for bad weather and worse clients, that lots of people wrote in small cramped capitals slanting slightly left. Lots of people underlined their measurements twice. Lots of people drew little arrows in margins pointing at things only they understood.

Lots of people did not own a tin cup with a dent in it shaped like a crescent moon.

It was sitting on the flat stone that served as a table, upside down to drain, and Dominic saw it before he understood what he was seeing — just a cup, battered tin, blackened at the base from a decade of campfires. Then he turned it over and the firelight — no, the gray daylight, there was no firelight, the fire was dead coals — caught the rim, and there it was. The dent. Low on the body, left side, crescent-shaped, the size of a thumbnail.

He was eight years old. He was sitting cross-legged on the deck of a research barge on some river whose name changed depending on which government currently claimed it, and his father was telling him about sediment layers, using the dented tin cup as a prop, tapping the dent and saying, *This happened before your time, D., everything worth knowing happened before your time, your job is just to listen to what the damage remembers.* And Dominic, eight years old and furious about being handed a cup instead of attention, had secretly loved every second of it, and had spent thirty years pretending otherwise.

"Dominic." Wren's voice, very quiet. "What is it?"

He couldn't answer. His throat had closed around something enormous and he could only stand there holding the cup like it might dissolve, like hope was a bird that would startle if he moved too fast, and God, it hurt. That was the thing nobody warned you about hope — everyone talked about it like a gift, a light, a harbor lamp, and nobody mentioned that after nine years of practicing not wanting anything, wanting this much felt like a wound. Hope arrived so hard it hurt, and it hurt because it meant there was still a boy in him who had waited at windows for a father who chose rivers over birthdays, and that boy had never actually left, he'd just been very, very quiet.

"It's his," Dominic managed. "The cup. It's my father's."

Wren crossed the lean-to in two steps. She didn't take the cup from him — he noticed that, noticed how deliberately she kept her hands at her sides — she just looked at it, and then at him, and her eyes went bright in a way she'd have denied under oath.

"The dent," she said. "Marcus dropped it off the *Corvina* in '09. Off the Amazon leg. He fished it out with a gaff and refused to replace it for eleven years. Used to say a field kit isn't broken in until it's survived a mistake." Her voice caught on something. "He told that story at every dig. Every single one. We used to time how long into an expedition it took him to—"

She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Looked away at the bamboo, at the rain, at anything but him.

"He's alive," Dominic said. Saying it aloud made it real, made it fragile, made it a thing the world could now take away. "Wren. He's *alive*. This fire is warm. This mat is dry. He was standing right here drinking coffee out of this stupid cup within the last two hours, and he walked north, and he's coming back tonight, and—"

"And Renner knows roughly where he is too," Wren said, gentle and merciless, because she was the only person alive who could be both at once. "Dominic. If we can find this camp, so can they. The smoke—"

"—is a beacon. Yeah." He set the cup down with exaggerated care, the way you handle evidence, the way you handle the last intact piece of a wrecked ship. "I know."

They worked fast after that, the way fear makes people efficient. They couldn't stay — following the trail now meant possibly walking past Marcus in the rain-blind terrain, or worse, leading Renner's men straight to him by their own backtrail. But they couldn't leave the camp undefended either, because if Marcus came back tonight and found strangers—

"We leave a sign," Dominic said suddenly. "Something he'd recognize. Something Renner's men wouldn't understand even if they found it."

Wren looked at him. "Like what?"

Dominic stared at the tin cup, and thirty years fell away all at once, and he heard his father's voice as clearly as if the man were standing in the rain beside him: *your job is just to listen to what the damage remembers.*

"The cup," he said. "We don't touch the cup. But we move it. Left side of the stone instead of right. Upside down to right side up." He was already rearranging it, heart hammering, feeling absurd and desperate and certain all at once. "When I was a kid, he had a signal system for me. Stupid little things. If the cup was upright, all clear. On its side, stay put. He taught it to me on the *Corvina* when I was nine, and I pretended to forget it, and I didn't forget it, I never forgot any of it—"

"Dominic." Wren's hand landed on his shoulder, steadying him the way you steady someone on a pitching deck. "Breathe."

"He'll see it. If he comes back tonight, he'll see the cup turned over and he'll know someone was here who knows the code. Nobody else would think to do it. Renner's men would steal the cup, not talk to it."

For a long moment Wren said nothing. Rain ticked on the palm roof. Somewhere in the terraces below, water moved through channels cut by dead hands, patient as ever.

"When he sees it," she said finally, "he'll know it's you."

"That's the idea."

"He won't know it's me."

Dominic glanced at her. There was something raw under the archivist composure, something she was holding very still, like a woman standing at the edge of a bridge she wasn't sure would hold her weight.

"Leave something too," he said. "If you want."

She hesitated. Then she took the pencil stub from the cache, and on the back of a scrap of oilcloth she wrote three words in her small precise hand, folded it once, and weighted it under the tin cup where only someone lifting it would find it. She didn't show him what it said. He didn't ask. Some letters were addressed to one reader only, and he was learning, slowly, painfully, that his family's whole history was a stack of such letters, most of them never delivered.

They slipped back down through the terraces as the drizzle thickened toward real rain, taking a different line than they'd come, doubling back along a stone causeway that surfaced from the floodwater like the spine of something enormous sleeping under the silt. Dominic kept looking north, into the gray curtain where the higher ruins vanished into cloud, and every few minutes he caught himself doing it and made himself stop, and then did it again anyway.

Somewhere up there, a man with a dented tin cup was moving fast and light through the rain, and he was alive, and he was close enough that the smoke of his fire had reached them across the water.

* * *

They made the boat by late afternoon. Voss looked up from cleaning a rifle with the expression of a woman who had counted the minutes of their absence and charged them for each one.

"Well?" she said. "You look like dogs that smelled dinner."

"Smoke," Dominic said. "Fresh camp. One person."

Voss's hands paused on the rifle. "One person. Up there." She jerked her chin toward the drowned terraces, and for the first time since Sarn's Reach, Dominic saw something in her face that wasn't calculation. Something older and wearier. "In this season, alone? Either that's the toughest son of a gun on this river, or—"

"It's him," Wren said simply. "It's Marcus."

Voss studied her for a moment, then went back to the rifle, working the bolt with a small metallic click that sounded, in the gathering dusk, almost like punctuation. "Then I suggest," she said, "we figure out where he's going before Renner figures out where he's been. Because that man's radios have been quiet since yesterday, and quiet radios mean he's stopped searching and started hunting."

Dominic stood at the bow as the light failed, watching the northern terraces dissolve into rain and dark. Somewhere up there a fire was being rebuilt, a cup was being lifted, and a folded note was waiting under it with three words that weren't for him.

Tomorrow they'd follow the trail inland, toward whatever his father had found and whatever Renner wanted from it. Tonight there was only the rain, the river, and the impossible, unbearable, wonderful arithmetic of it:

His father was two hours ahead of him. After nine years, two hours.

He could close that distance. He intended to.

He was still standing there when Wren came to stand beside him, and neither of them spoke, because some silences are full enough on their own — until, low on the dark water downstream, a lantern flickered where no lantern should be, and Voss said quietly, "Company."
