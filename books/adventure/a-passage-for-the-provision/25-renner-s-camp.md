## 25. Renner's Camp

The smoke was a thin gray thread against the green, maybe half a mile off through the drowned terraces, and it did something to all three of them that none of them wanted to name out loud.

"Campfire," Voss said, flat and professional, the way she said everything. "Cooking fire. Somebody's making breakfast."

"Somebody," Dominic repeated.

"Could be your father."

"Could be Renner."

Voss shrugged, which in her language was an entire paragraph of agreement. She'd been with them four days now — long enough that the arrangement had stopped feeling like a knife balanced on the table and started feeling like a knife someone had thoughtfully set down but not put away. She moved through the flooded jungle like she owned the deed to it, machete in one hand, rifle slung across her back, and she had a habit of looking at Dominic when he wasn't watching, the way a buyer looks at a boat before naming a price.

Wren crouched at the edge of the water, studying the thread of smoke the way she studied everything — as if it might be lying to her. "Smoke means people. People mean information." She pushed wet hair out of her face. "It also means we're not the only ones who can read a trail."

"We've known that since the ford," Dominic said.

"Yes, but I enjoy saying things we already know. It's how I cope."

They went forward single file along a ridge of old stonework that surfaced from the floodwater like the spine of some enormous sleeping animal. The morning was thick and warm, birds going about their business overhead with a total indifference to human affairs that Dominic found almost offensive. Somewhere ahead, the smoke rose and bent and rose again.

He was thinking about the tin cup. He hadn't told Wren or Voss about the cup — the dented rim, the particular way the handle had been mended with wire, exactly the way his father used to mend everything, badly and permanently. A man who was dead didn't make tea. A man who was running didn't stop to leave cups behind. The arithmetic of it kept turning over in him like river stones, wearing down his edges.

Voss stopped so suddenly that Wren walked into her back.

"What—" Wren began.

Voss's hand came up. Below them, maybe two hundred yards, tucked into a fold of the terrace where the stone curved around a dry shelf, was a camp. Not a campfire. A *camp*.

Dominic counted without meaning to — ten years of salvage work had made counting automatic. Three large tents, olive drab, military surplus. Two radio masts, guyed wire glinting. Wooden crates stacked under tarps, more crates than any survey team needed. A generator humming its small arrogant hum. And men — six he could see, seven probably — moving with the unhurried competence of people who were paid well and knew it.

"That's not a rescue party," Wren breathed.

"No," Voss said. "That's a supply line. Radios, resupply, rotation of personnel. That's how you run an operation into country like this for weeks." Her eyes narrowed. "That's how *he* runs one."

"You know this setup," Dominic said.

"I know whose money buys olive drab like that." Voss spat, delicately, over the side of the ridge. "Renner doesn't hire soldiers. Soldiers want glory. He hires quiet men who like being comfortable. Look at them. Coffee. Real coffee, I'd bet."

And then they saw the cage.

It wasn't called a cage. It was, Dominic supposed, technically a stockade — a square of cargo netting stretched between posts, weighted at the corners, with two men sitting inside it in the listless way of people who had stopped expecting anything good to happen. One of them wore the remains of an expedition shirt, sun-faded, the foundation's little stitched compass logo still visible on the pocket. The other was younger, barefoot, cradling one wrist.

"Those are Marcus's people," Wren whispered. "That's — I think that's Okafor. The geologist. And the young one, the one who carried the core samples—"

"Tomas," Dominic said. He'd seen photographs. His father sent them, once a year, every year, in envelopes Dominic never opened until the guilt got loud enough. Tomas Ferreira, twenty-three years old, grinning next to a drilling rig in a picture taken two seasons ago.

"They're being held 'for their protection,'" Voss murmured, and there was enough venom in it to drop a water buffalo. "That's the phrase. I've heard it before. It means: we are protecting you from leaving."

* * *

They withdrew to a hollow behind the ridge, where a fig tree had grown straight out of a crack in the ancient masonry and given them cover and, incidentally, breakfast. Voss chewed a fig and drew in the mud with a stick.

"Six, seven men. Two on watch at any time, one at the radios, rest sleeping or working. Generator noise covers movement. Good for us." She stabbed the stick at a point in her mud map. "Bad for us: if the radio operator sends a scheduled check-in and we've disturbed anything, the main force knows within the hour. And the main force has your father."

"So we don't disturb anything," Wren said.

"We're going to disturb everything. We're just going to do it *well*."

Dominic looked at her. "I'm listening."

"Voss creates a diversion; you two free the captives" — she waggled the stick — "is what I was going to propose, yes, thank you for reading my mind, it's very tidy in there. Here's how." She drew a line along the water below the camp. "Their latrine trench drains toward the channel. Every camp like this has one man assigned to haul water and one man who wanders off to smoke where he shouldn't. The generator is here, close to the tents, because Renner's people are lazy about generators because nobody has ever shot at them." She smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "Somebody should shoot at them. Metaphorically. Today."

"And your diversion?" Wren asked.

"I'm going to be a damsel," Voss said, with the enthusiasm of a woman ordering dessert. "A distressed salvager, alone, boat broken, very lost, possibly concussed. They'll want to help me. Helping me will require at least three of them to come down to the water, away from their rifles, away from the radios, and away from the cage." She paused. "Also I am going to steal whatever is in those crates while they're helping me, because I have standards."

"You're enjoying this," Dominic said.

"I haven't been paid in months, my boat is at the bottom of a gorge, and I'm allied with two academics." She stood, brushing mud from her knees. "This is the happiest day of my year."

* * *

It went, by the standards of every plan Dominic had ever been part of, suspiciously well for almost eleven minutes.

Voss went down to the water first, alone, having spent five minutes beforehand artfully ruining herself — mud on one temple, shirt torn at the shoulder, hair loose. She paddled a scrap of dugout noisily into view of the camp and then, with impeccable timing, capsized it, surfacing with a cry that would have gotten her work in any theater on the coast.

The camp reacted exactly as predicted. Shouts. Two men jogging down to the bank, then a third waving arms. Someone called for a rope. The radio operator stuck his head out of the tent, decided drowning women were not a radio matter, and went back to his frequencies.

"Go," Wren whispered.

They came along the base of the ridge in a low crouch, water to their thighs, moving through the drowned terrace the way Dominic had moved through a hundred flooded wrecks — slow, silent, reading the geometry of cover. Wren kept pace beside him, and he noticed, with a small private surprise, that he'd stopped worrying about whether she could keep up. When had that happened?

The cage sat at the edge of the dry shelf, guarded now by exactly one man, who was watching the rescue drama at the water with his rifle slung and a look of profound boredom. Dominic picked up a length of broken branch, weighed it, and regretted, briefly, every life choice that had led to hitting people with sticks instead of, say, owning a shop somewhere.

He didn't need the stick. As they closed, the guard turned, saw two soaked figures rising out of the floodwater, opened his mouth — and Wren threw the fig she'd been saving directly into his face. It was, Dominic would think later, the single most effective act of violence he'd ever witnessed. The man staggered back, blinking fruit pulp, and Dominic's shoulder took him into the masonry, and that was that. Wren bound his wrists with the guard's own belt, gagged him with his own hat, and patted his cheek almost apologetically.

"For their protection," she muttered.

Inside the netting, the older man had come up onto his knees. Sunken, bearded, eyes fierce above the exhaustion. He stared at Dominic and his mouth worked.

"You're — you're the spitting image of him, you know that? At thirty. Exactly him at thirty."

"I get that a lot lately," Dominic said, cutting the netting with his knife. "Okafor?"

"Dr. Okafor, yes." The man gripped his hand hard enough to hurt. "You came. God help us, you actually came. Your father said you would. He said — he said, 'my son will come, he always knows the water.'" Okafor laughed, a cracked sound. "We told him you wouldn't. No offense."

"None taken. I'm still deciding myself."

The younger one, Tomas, couldn't stand — his wrist was swollen bad, badly splinted with tent pole and tape. Wren got under his arm. "Can you walk?"

"I can hop," Tomas said, white-faced and grinning. "I've been practicing hopping. There's not much else to practice in a cage."

At the waterline, the diversion was fraying — Voss had, apparently, decided three rescuers weren't enough and was now loudly refusing to come ashore anywhere except near the crates, and one of Renner's men was beginning to develop suspicions, because he kept looking from the drowning woman to the crates to the drowning woman with the expression of a man doing unwelcome arithmetic.

"Time to go," Dominic said. "Now. All of you. Downstream, along the ridge base, Wren leads, Okafor follows, Tomas between you. I'm rear guard."

They went. And it was in the last fifty yards, chest-deep now, supporting Tomas's weight while the boy swore cheerfully through his teeth, that Okafor, gasping, gave him the thing that changed the shape of everything.

"Your father," Okafor said. "When Renner's men took us at the vault road — there were fourteen of us. Fourteen. Renner wanted everyone marched inland, hostages, leverage. And Marcus — Dr. Fairweather — he walked out in front of them, hands up, and he said, 'You don't need fourteen. You need me and one witness that the site is real. Let the rest go downriver before the flood crest, and I'll open whatever you want opened.'"

Dominic stopped moving. Water pulled at him. Somewhere behind them, faintly, Voss's voice was singing a fake drowning aria at full volume.

"He traded himself," Okafor said. "For us. He stood there in the rain and negotiated our lives like it was a supply contract, and Renner — the bastard *respected* it, you could see it, he likes people who bargain clean. Twelve of us walked free. Marcus stayed." Okafor's grip tightened on Dominic's arm. "He made us swear to tell you. He said you'd understand why. He said—" the doctor's voice caught, "—he said, 'Tell Dominic it's the provision. Tell him the provision was never the gold.'"

The words landed in Dominic's chest like ballast dropped from height. *D. should understand why.* The journal page under the stone. Nine years of silence, and at the end of it, his father had spent himself like coin to buy strangers a way home — and had left the explanation addressed to the one person who'd sworn never to ask for one.

"He also said," Tomas added weakly, from between them, "that if his son turned out to be half the pilot he was, we'd all live to be insufferable old men. So no pressure."

Behind them, the diversion collapsed into shouting — Voss had evidently concluded her performance and was now sprinting along the bank with a crate under each arm, pursued by three damp and furious men, laughing like a hyena on holiday.

Dominic turned downstream and drove them all forward through the water, toward the treeline, toward the boat, toward the gorge where his father was spending himself hour by hour in a vault built by people who had understood, better than anyone, what it cost to survive a flood.

There was a message to decode. There was a father to reach.

And the monsoon, judging by the bruised color of the western sky, was done waiting for any of them.
