## 26. The Trade

The freed crew came down the ridge in a straggle, like beads off a snapped string.

Wren counted them twice from the treeline — seven, then eight, then a ninth limping figure who turned out to be two men supporting each other — and felt something in her chest unknot by a single turn. Not all of it. There was a great deal of knot left. But Dominic beside her let out a breath he'd probably been holding since the lean-to with the tin cup, and that was worth something.

"Eighteen," she said quietly. "Eighteen left of thirty-one."

"Twenty," said the first man down the slope, a wiry fellow with a salt-cracked voice and a surveyor's theodolite slung across his back like a rifle. "Counting the two of you. And the salvage woman, if she counts, which I suspect she'd insist on."

"She'd insist on back pay first," Wren said. "I'm Wren Calloway. This is Dominic Fairweather."

The wiry man stopped. Looked at Dominic. Looked at him the way people look at a photograph they've been told is out of date.

"You're the son," he said. "The one he talks about."

"He talks about me?" Dominic's voice was carefully flat, the way a floor is carefully flat before you discover the trapdoor under the rug.

"Talks, argues with, loses arguments to, apologizes to." The man stuck out a hand. "Emory Vance. Assistant surveyor, which is a grand title for a man who spends most of his time telling Dr. Fairweather that the ridge he wants to climb is made of mud."

"Is it?"

"It's made of mud, sir, and he climbs it anyway. It's how we all ended up in this mess."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a collapsed terrace wall, out of the driving rain, and the story came out the way stories do in such circumstances: in pieces, from different mouths, overlapping, corrected, occasionally interrupted by someone eating. Wren let it assemble itself. She had learned long ago — in lecture halls, in archives, in the wreckage of one career — that the truth was less like a document and more like a mosaic, and your job was to sit still and let the tiles land.

The shape of it was this:

Three weeks ago, Aldous Renner's men had come up the vault road in force. Not the polite two-man advance party the expedition had been promised. Forty men, radios, crates of tinned food, and a river barge that had no business being that far upstream in monsoon season — which meant Renner had known the water year better than anyone gave him credit for, which meant he'd planned this from the beginning.

Marcus had met them at the outer ruins. Alone, mostly. Ilsa — the young assistant, sharp-eyed, still catching her breath from the descent — told this part, and told it with her jaw set.

"He walked out with his hands empty. No guards, no weapon, just that ridiculous hat. And he said, 'You've made an expensive journey, Mr. Renner. Let me make it worth your while without making it a tragedy.'"

"And Renner listened?" Wren asked.

"Renner listened," Ilsa said, "because Dr. Fairweather had already done something clever. He'd moved the expedition's radio and most of the food stores somewhere only he knew. And he'd told us — all of us, before Renner ever arrived — that if anything happened to him, the site's location died with him. He'd memorized the route. No map. He burned the map in front of everyone, actually. Made rather a ceremony of it." She paused. "It was very theatrical. He's a theatrical man."

"He's my father," Dominic said, to no one in particular, in the tone of a man confirming a diagnosis.

"The bargain," Emory picked up, "was this. Dr. Fairweather would guide Renner to the site — to the inner vault, eventually, because the outer chambers Renner's men could find on their own — and in exchange, the expedition's people would be released. All of us. Free to go downriver before the flood peak. Renner's own engineers had told him what the water was going to do to these terraces. He didn't want thirty-one witnesses drowning on his worksite."

"He wanted them off the books," Wren said.

"He wanted them *alive*," Ilsa said sharply, and then, more quietly: "Both things can be true. But I watched the man's face when he made the offer. Whatever else Renner is, he wanted the drowning even less than he wanted the witnesses. He's not a monster. He's something more annoying than that. He's a man who has read enough history to know how these stories end for the villains, and he's decided to be careful about it."

"And Marcus agreed," Dominic said. It wasn't a question.

"Marcus agreed," Emory said. "He shook on it. He made Renner shake on it in front of forty men, which I suspect was the point — a bargain witnessed by that many hired guns is a bargain that's hard to quietly unmake. And then we were marched down to the barge, and half of us were put aboard and sent downriver."

"Half," Wren said.

"Half," Emory confirmed. "Renner kept the porters with families in the basin — men who wouldn't dare run. And he kept two of us as, and I quote, 'guests.' Myself and Ilsa. He said Dr. Fairweather worked better with familiar faces nearby." Emory's mouth twisted. "It was meant as a kindness, I think, in whatever currency kindness is denominated in when you're a hostage."

* * *

The rain eased toward dusk, and Voss's crew — who had caught up an hour behind, drawn by the same smoke and the same logic — circulated among the freed expedition members with a familiarity that told Wren these people had worked together before, before the cutting loose and the unpaid wages. There were greetings. There were also some long looks and one muttered word that made a Voss man laugh and an expedition porter go stiff, and Wren filed that away. Old grievances didn't dissolve just because a new villain arrived. They just agreed to a ceasefire.

She found Dominic sitting apart, on a block of dressed stone that had once been part of a stair, staring up at the drowned terraces where the last light was turning the floodwater to hammered copper.

She sat down next to him. She didn't say anything for a while. This was, she had found, one of the few reliable services she offered.

"Nine years," Dominic said finally. "Nine years of silence, and the man spends his silence learning how to negotiate with mercenaries to save his crew."

"He was always good at that part," Wren said. "The part where he spends himself. It was the part where he *kept* anything — kept people, kept promises, kept in touch — that he was hopeless at."

"You hated him for a long time."

"I hated him for exactly the right amount of time," Wren said. "Then I hated myself for a while, which was less efficient. And then I hated Renner, whom I hadn't even met, which was very efficient, because he deserved it and it required no self-examination whatsoever." She pulled her knees up. "Dominic. Listen to the shape of this."

"The shape of what?"

"The bargain. Really listen to it. Marcus trades the site for his people's lives. Fine. Noble, even — and I say that as a woman who once watched the man spend three days arguing about the correct way to label a pottery shard." A ghost of a smile. "But here's the thing. Renner *agreed*. Why?"

"Because he needs Marcus alive. You said it yourself — the vault, the seal, whatever's inside. Marcus is the key."

"Marcus is the key to *opening* it. But think about what that means. Renner has forty men. He has radios, boats, engineers, a barge, and a hostage with a memorized route. He does not need Marcus's *cooperation*. He needs Marcus's *knowledge*. And knowledge —" Wren tapped her own temple — "is the one thing you cannot take from a man by force. You can only get it by trade, or by time. Torture is slow and unreliable and produces garbage. Renner knows this. So he made a deal that keeps Marcus alive and cooperative and *grateful* — grateful, Dominic, because Renner let his people go. Marcus is down there right now feeling *indebted* to the man who took him hostage. That's not a kindness. That's a leash made out of a man's own decency."

Dominic was quiet for a long moment. The bells of the gorge — the hanging stones far upstream — rang faintly through the rain, a sound like a distant chandelier in the wind.

"So we break the leash."

"We break the leash. But here's the part I keep turning over." Wren lowered her voice, though the nearest listener was twenty feet away and arguing about rice. "Marcus made this bargain three weeks ago. He knew the flood was coming — Renner's engineers told him, and Marcus knows water better than any of them. He knew the lower chambers would drown. He knew the archive — *if* the archive is what the inscriptions say it is — was built to survive it, because the people who built it survived their own flood. He designed his sacrifice around the water."

"You think the bargain is a stall."

"I think Marcus Fairweather has spent four months down here doing something he never once did in thirty years of expeditions." She looked at him. "He's been *waiting*. Buying time. Keeping Renner's hands full and his people safe and the water rising, and every day the water rises, Renner's options get worse. Marcus isn't a hostage down there, Dominic. He's a man playing for the flood. The only question is whether he's got a move left when the water peaks — or whether he's just planning to drown with the vault and call it even."

* * *

They gathered the council after dark, around a fire shielded by a slab of fallen masonry, and Wren watched the shape of the thing assemble the way the story had assembled itself earlier — tile by tile, until a picture emerged that none of them had planned and all of them could see.

Voss came armed with a stick and a flat voice. "Renner's main camp is at the vault road's lower switchback. Forty men, but he's split them — twelve at the camp, the rest strung along the road and the terraces, guarding his crates and his radios. His supply barge is moored at the last anchorage, the one where your father left you his little note." She glanced at Dominic. "The barge is the throat. Food, fuel, ammunition, the radios that keep him talking to his patron. Cut the throat and forty men in monsoon country start counting days."

"Or we go straight at the vault," said one of Voss's crew, a big man named Ferro with a face like a closed door. "While the water's got him busy."

"And trade bullets in a flooded chamber with the man holding your father," Voss said to Dominic, sweetly. "By all means."

"Nobody's trading bullets anywhere near my father," Dominic said, and Wren heard it — the *my* — land in his own ears a half-second after it left his mouth. He covered it by leaning forward. "Voss is right about the barge. And there's a third thing nobody's said yet."

"Say it," Wren said.

"Renner needs Marcus to open the inner vault. Which means the vault isn't open yet. Which means everything Marcus wants to protect is still *behind a door he controls*. He's had three weeks alone with that door." Dominic looked around the fire. "My father does not sit still for three weeks. He's not just waiting down there. He's *preparing*. And when Renner finally forces the seal — and he will, the water's going to make him desperate — I want to be standing next to my father when it opens. Not storming the road. *There.*"

"So we don't cut the throat," Ferro said.

"We cut the throat," Wren said, "quietly. A small crew — Voss's fastest people — takes or disables the barge. No firefight. Fuel, radios, and Renner's ability to call for more men: gone. He'll have to move on the vault before his position rots, which is exactly what we want, because it means *he* comes to *us*, on ground where the water is on our side. Meanwhile the rest of us go up the old processional way — the route on Marcus's rubbing. It comes out above the vault chamber. Emory, you've surveyed those terraces. Is it passable?"

Emory was already scratching a map in the dirt with a stick, and Wren felt the old familiar pull of it — the pleasure of a plan, of a puzzle with moving parts, of being the person in the room whose answer was wanted. She had missed this. She had hated that she missed it, and missed it anyway.

"Passable at this water level. Suicidal in three days, when the peak hits. Which means we go tomorrow. Which means," Emory added, with the air of a man delivering the fine print, "sleeping tonight, which I recommend, and eating, which I insist on, because the last man who tried to climb the processional way hungry is currently a story we tell to frighten new porters."

"Then it's settled in outline," Wren said. "Voss picks her barge crew. Dominic and I take the processional way with Emory and Ilsa — Ilsa because she knows the vault chamber's anterooms, and Emory because he knows where the mud is, and the two of us because —" she glanced at Dominic — "because Marcus will open that door for exactly two people without being asked. His son. And the woman he wrote to."

The fire crackled. Somewhere up the gorge, the bells rang, and the water kept rising in the dark, patient as a promise.

Voss stood, brushed off her trousers, and paused at the edge of the firelight. "One amendment," she said. "When this is done, my wages come out of *Renner's* crates. Not the archive. I've been paid in stolen goods once already this year. I find I've developed a taste for honest loot."

"Renner's crates," Wren agreed. "Every last tin."

Voss nodded once, satisfied, and went to wake her crew.

Wren stayed by the fire a moment longer. Dominic was looking up at the terraces again, at the black shape of the vault road switchback somewhere beyond them, where a man with a ridiculous hat was spending himself one more time.

"Tomorrow," she said.

"Tomorrow," he said. And then, quietly, almost to himself: "Hold on, old man. We're coming to ruin your plan."

Which, Wren thought, gathering her bedroll, was probably the most filial thing he'd ever said — and, knowing Marcus Fairweather, very possibly the first time in nine years that father and son would agree on something.
