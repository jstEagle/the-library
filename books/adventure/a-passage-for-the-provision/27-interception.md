## 27. Interception

The river forked at a place the porters called Tchava's Elbow, where the current split around a gravel bar shaped like a sleeping animal and neither branch seemed entirely committed to going anywhere in particular. It was, Dominic decided, the perfect place to catch people who didn't want to be caught — or rather, people who were moving too fast and too frightened to be careful.

"They'll take the western channel," Wren said, crouched beside him on the bank with the map spread between them and three river stones pinning down its corners against the wind. "The eastern one narrows past the karst. If they've got wounded, they won't risk it."

"If they've got Renner's men behind them," Dominic said, "they might not have a choice."

Wren glanced up at him. Rain dripped off the brim of her hat onto the map, blurring the pencil line of the channel she'd just drawn. "You're cheerful this morning."

"I'm always cheerful."

"You're always *armed*. There's a difference."

He almost smiled. Almost. The last two days had done something strange to the space between them — not softened it exactly, but worn it smooth in places, the way water wears stone without ever meaning to. She'd nearly drowned in his arms two nights ago and then told him his father had written to ask her forgiveness, and somehow both of those facts now lived in the same small boat of silence between them, and neither of them had bailed it out yet.

Voss came down the bank with two of her crew, moving with the unhurried confidence of a woman who had never once doubted that the world would eventually pay her what it owed. She squatted at the edge of the map, studied it, and tapped the western channel with one scarred finger.

"She's right. And if your father sent them downriver ahead of the flood, they'll be moving slow. Porters don't run downhill with loads." She looked up. "Question is whether you want to wave hello or stay hidden until they pass."

"We want to warn them," Wren said. "Renner's men took our porter and the journal page at the ford. They know there's someone else on the river. If an evacuation party comes through here blind—"

"Then we'd better hope they see us before they shoot us," Voss said cheerfully, and went back up the bank to arrange her people along the ridge like chess pieces.

Dominic watched her go. "I liked her better when she was robbing us."

"No, you didn't."

"No," he admitted. "I didn't."

* * *

They waited four hours in the rain, which in the Velloran basin was less weather than atmosphere — a condition of existence, like gravity. Dominic crouched under a leaning fig tree with his field glasses and watched the western channel slide by, brown and swollen and carrying whole trees like matchsticks. Somewhere upstream, his father was being marched toward a vault by a man who collected civilizations the way other men collected stamps. Somewhere downstream, presumably, was safety. The evacuation party would be somewhere on that brown ribbon between the two.

At the fifth hour, Wren touched his arm.

"Smoke," she said. "There. Above the treeline, on the far bend."

It was thin and gray and deliberate — not camp smoke but signal smoke, one long plume, cut short, then another. Someone who knew river protocol. Someone trained.

"Foundation hand signals," Dominic murmured. "My father drilled every expedition in them. One long, one short means *friendly party requesting contact*." He lowered the glasses slowly. "He really did send them away."

"He traded himself for their lives," Wren said quietly. "Of course he sent them away. That was the whole point."

Dominic didn't answer. There was a stone in his chest that had been growing since the ford, since the ambush, since the moment he'd seen Renner standing calm and dry-shod on the far bank like a man inspecting property. Every new fact about his father seemed to add weight to it. He had spent nine years carrying a very specific man in his head — brilliant, absent, hungry for glory, willing to let children grow up on postcards — and that man kept refusing to hold still. First the village with the mended roofs. Then the photograph of Marcus laughing with a child. Now this: a father who had sold himself into captivity so that other people's children could go home.

"Ilsa," Wren said suddenly. "If any of the core team made it out, Ilsa would be with them. She wouldn't leave him otherwise — he'd have had to order her twice."

"Who is she?"

"His assistant. Twenty-four, maybe twenty-five. Came to him straight out of university. Fiercely loyal." Wren paused, and something complicated moved across her face. "She used to ask me about the scandal, in the early days. Before it happened. She wanted to understand why everyone said such terrible things about him. I told her the truth as I understood it then." Her jaw tightened. "I owe her a correction."

* * *

They came down to the water's edge with hands visible and Voss's people conspicuously not aiming anything, which by Voss's standards was practically a parade. The signal smoke had moved closer. Through the rain-haze on the far bend, Dominic could make out shapes now — a battered pirogue, then two more, riding low, paddled by exhausted figures who stopped dead when they saw the armed company waiting on the gravel bar.

For a long moment nobody moved. The river talked to itself. Rain needled the surface.

Then a voice carried across the water, young and cracked and furious with hope: "*Is that Fairweather? Is that Dominic Fairweather?*"

"It's us," Dominic called back, and heard how strange his own name sounded in his mouth — his father's name, really, borrowed. "We're looking for the expedition. We're looking for Marcus Fairweather."

The nearest pirogue turned toward them so fast it nearly shipped water.

They came ashore in ones and twos — six of them, seven, eight — gaunt and mud-caked and staring at the rescue party as if rescue were a language they'd half forgotten. Two were foundation staff Dominic vaguely recognized from photographs in the files Wren had shown him. Four were local porters, and when these saw the two porters who had survived the ambush and traveled with Dominic, the gravel bar erupted into embraces and rapid-fire dialect and one old man weeping openly without any apparent embarrassment about it whatsoever.

And at the center of it, steadying a limping older porter with one shoulder, stood a young woman with rain-flattened dark hair, a canvas satchel strapped across her chest like armor, and eyes that went around the group counting heads with mechanical precision even while her face tried to fall apart.

"Ilsa," Wren said.

Ilsa Venn looked up. For a heartbeat she simply stared at Wren Calloway, and Dominic watched something pass between the two women — history, apology, recognition — compressed into a single glance across a flooded gravel bar.

"You're real," Ilsa said. "He said you'd come. He said—" Her voice snagged. She cleared her throat, hauled herself back together with visible effort, and tried again. "Dr. Fairweather said if anyone came after us, it would be you two. He was very smug about it. He said, 'My son knows the water better than any pilot the foundation could hire, and Dr. Calloway has never once left a problem unsolved, mostly because she can't stand them.'"

"That sounds like him," Wren said, and her voice wasn't entirely steady either.

"He also said," Ilsa continued, and now she was digging in the satchel, "that you'd probably arrive angry, Mr. Fairweather, sir, and that we shouldn't take it personally. He said anger was just—" she pulled out an oilcloth packet, handled it like something breakable, "—he said anger was just love with nowhere to put itself. His words. He made me memorize it. He made me memorize all of it, three times, in case the rain got the paper."

Dominic stood very still.

Around him the reunion churned on — porters clasping arms, Voss's people already sharing water and questions, the limping porter being eased down onto a dry crate — but it all seemed to happen at a distance, behind glass. Ilsa held out the oilcloth packet with both hands, the way you'd offer something to a stranger you hoped would become a friend.

"He dictated this six weeks ago," she said. "Before the second anchorage. He said, 'If my son finds you, give him this. If my son doesn't find you, eat it, because no one else gets to read it.'" A wet, shaky laugh escaped her. "I was going to eat it. I want that on record. I'd worked up to it."

Dominic took the packet. His hands, which had spent ten days doing competent things — splicing lines, caulking seams, pulling a drowning woman out of a flood channel — felt suddenly clumsy, oversized, untrustworthy.

He unwrapped it.

Inside was a single page, torn from a survey notebook, covered in his father's handwriting. He knew that handwriting the way he knew his own pulse: the impatient slant of it, the way Marcus crossed his t's like a man fencing. He hadn't seen it in nine years. He had thought — he had told himself for nine years — that he didn't miss it.

*Dominic.*

*If you're reading this, then you ignored every sensible instinct and came upriver in monsoon season, which means you are either very brave or very much my son, and I have never believed those were different things.*

*I haven't earned the right to explain myself in a letter. I know that. Nine years of silence doesn't get fixed with paper. But I need you to carry one thing out of this place, whatever happens to me, and it's this:*

*Tell my son the provision was never the gold.*

*He'll know what it means. Or he will, once he sees it. I built my life on the wrong reading of that word, and I built yours on my wrong reading, and I am sorry for it in ways I don't have the scholarship to express. The site will explain the rest. The site is honest. I only ever wanted to leave you something honest.*

*Your father,*
*M.F.*
*(who keeps moving, but apparently not forever)*

Dominic read it twice. Then a third time, standing in the rain on a gravel bar in the middle of the Velloran basin, while a young woman watched him with anxious eyes and Wren Calloway very carefully watched the river instead of watching him.

"*The provision was never the gold,*" he said aloud. It came out rougher than he intended. "What does that mean? Is it a code? Is it—"

"The inscription," Wren said softly. She had come to stand beside him, close enough that he could feel the warmth of her shoulder through the wet. "In the gorge carvings. The provision chamber — the archive. Remember the glyph sequence I couldn't resolve? The word we translated as 'provision' had a secondary root. I flagged it and got distracted by the flood engineering." She was talking fast now, the way she talked when her mind was outrunning her mouth. "Marcus would have known the secondary root. He invented half that notation system. Dominic — I think your father is telling you the site isn't what Renner thinks it is. I think whatever Renner is dragging him toward—"

"—isn't worth what Renner thinks it's worth," Dominic finished.

The stone in his chest shifted. Not gone. Moved. Because here was the thing he kept refusing to say out loud: his father had written to him. Weeks ago. Had sat in some leaking tent with the monsoon coming down and thought about his son — thought about him carefully enough to compose a message, dictate it three times, entrust it to a girl with a satchel, and build the whole shape of his final gamble around the possibility that Dominic would come. Not hoped. *Built around.* You didn't build around a maybe. You built around a thing you knew the way you knew the current under your keel.

"He knew I'd come," Dominic said.

"He knew," Ilsa agreed gently.

"I haven't spoken to him in nine years."

"Yes," Ilsa said. "He mentioned that too." She hesitated, then, with the particular courage of the very young facing the very old grief of strangers, added: "He mentioned it every day, actually. Usually at breakfast. Usually while complaining that you'd inherited his stubbornness and none of his sense."

Despite everything — despite the ambush and the stolen journal page and the rising water and the man named Renner waiting somewhere ahead with his father in tow — Dominic laughed. It startled him, the sound of it, rough and unexpected, like a bird flushing from reeds.

"Sounds about right," he managed.

* * *

They got the survivors fed and sorted within the hour. Voss proved alarmingly efficient at triage, distributing rice and dried fish from her own stores with the air of a woman making investments. The two foundation staff — a hydrologist named Perrin and a cook named Beto who had, by all accounts, kept the entire expedition alive on nothing but cassava and defiance — gave their accounts in fragments, overlapping, finishing each other's sentences.

Renner's men had come to the base camp eleven days ago. Polite. Unhurried. They'd set up radios and crates and started calling it a "protection detail," and everyone had understood exactly what kind of protection it was. Marcus had negotiated — that was the word everyone used, *negotiated*, with a kind of awe — and the terms were simple: the crew could evacuate downriver before the worst flooding, and Marcus would guide Renner to the inner vault personally.

"He wasn't frightened," Perrin said, staring into the fire they'd dared to build under a tarp. "That's the thing I keep trying to tell people. He sat across from Renner like they were colleagues. Like he'd already won something and was just waiting for the rest of us to see it."

"He asked about you," Ilsa said to Dominic, later, as the light began to fail and the porters rigged shelters. "Every day. Whether the letter had reached the coast yet. Whether the foundation had forwarded it. When we lost the radio, he—" she stopped, restarted. "When we lost the radio, he wrote the message again. In case. He said important things should survive in duplicate. He said that's the whole point of archives."

Dominic looked at her. Young, exhausted, loyal past the point of reason, guarding a satchel of papers like a dragon guards gold. His father had chosen her well.

"What else did he say?" Dominic asked. "About me. About — anything."

Ilsa considered the question with the seriousness it deserved. "He said you used to read strata like other kids read comics. He said he taught you on your mother's kitchen table with sugar and salt and flour, and you got it in one afternoon, and he'd never been prouder of anything, including the site." She smiled a little. "He said it wrong, though. He said 'the site' and then he corrected himself. He said, 'No. Including everything.'"

The rain thickened. Downriver, somewhere in the dark, Renner was moving toward the vault with Marcus Fairweather as his reluctant compass. Upstream, the flood was rising according to a schedule set by engineers dead three thousand years. And in Dominic's breast pocket, wrapped in oilcloth, a single page weighed almost nothing and almost everything at once.

*The provision was never the gold.*

He didn't know what it meant yet. But he knew — with the certainty of a man who had spent his whole life reading water — that the answer was waiting for him upstream, in a chamber his father had found and refused to sell, guarded by rising water and a collector's greed and whatever his father had understood in the end that Dominic was only beginning to.

Wren settled beside him at the fire, close enough to share warmth, far enough to pretend she wasn't.

"You okay?" she asked.

"No," Dominic said honestly. "But I'm going to be. Eventually. After."

"After what?"

He looked upriver, into the dark where the bells of the gorge would be ringing somewhere beyond the rain.

"After I go get my father."
