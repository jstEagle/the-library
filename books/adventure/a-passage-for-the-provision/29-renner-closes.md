## 29. Renner Closes

The rain had settled into a steady gray drumming by the time they regrouped at the edge of the drowned terraces, and Wren found herself thinking — absurdly, traitorously — that it was almost cozy. Two of Marcus's porters, Tam and Sefu, were boiling something over a small fire under an overhang that smelled of wet stone and wetter wool. Voss's crew had spread out along the ridge like crows who'd decided to be useful for once. And Dominic stood at the lip of the overlook with binoculars pressed to his eyes, watching the far side of the valley where Renner's main force was making its move.

"Talk to me," she said, coming up beside him.

He handed her the binoculars without looking away from the valley himself, as if he could see through solid rain through sheer stubbornness. "Forty, maybe fifty men. Mules. A radio mast they've just raised on the spur. They're moving up the old processional way toward the inner vault."

Wren put the glasses up. Through the gray curtain she picked out the column: lanterns bobbing, canvas-covered loads, the glint of rifles slung across backs. And near the front, walking with his hands unbound but his shoulders carrying the particular weight of a man being escorted rather than accompanied, a tall figure in a ruined expedition coat.

Marcus.

Her chest did something complicated. She passed the binoculars back before Dominic could see her face do whatever it was doing.

"He's walking," Dominic said quietly. "Not stumbling. Not being dragged."

"He's negotiating every step," Wren said. "That's your father. He never once in twenty years let anyone else set the pace of a march."

Dominic made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been its close cousin. "You'd know."

"I would." She didn't flinch from it. They were past flinching, mostly.

Below them, Voss finished giving orders to two of her people and came up the slope with the rolling, unhurried gait of a woman who had never once hurried anywhere and yet always arrived first. Rain beaded on her oilskin and ran off her like she'd personally negotiated unfavorable terms with it.

"Well?" she said. "You've seen them. Now let's talk about how we die."

* * *

They gathered under the overhang around the fire, which Tam kept fed with driftwood that spat and hissed. The porters' stew turned out to be river fish and some tuber Wren couldn't name, and it was, against all odds, good. She'd learned long ago that expeditions lived or died on small comforts, and that Marcus Fairweather had always hired cooks who could make miracles out of nothing. Some habits of his, apparently, had survived even the years he'd spent being someone else's ghost.

Voss laid out her plan with a stick in the mud.

"They're fifty strong, we're eleven, and half of us are archaeologists," she said. "So we don't fight them. We starve them." She drew a line up the valley. "Their supply line comes down the gorge trail — mule train, twice daily if they're smart, once if they're Renner. We hit the mule train. Take their food, their ammunition, their radios. Fifty men in monsoon country without resupply stop being an army inside four days. They become fifty hungry men arguing with each other in the rain."

Sefu nodded slowly. "It is how you hunt a big animal. You do not attack the animal. You cut it off from water."

"Exactly so." Voss tapped the mud map. "We take the supply line, we hold the lower gorge, and when Renner comes down to find out why his dinner stopped arriving, we negotiate from strength. Or," she added, with a shrug that admitted nothing and conceded less, "we simply keep everything and go home rich. I remain flexible."

"That's not a rescue," Dominic said. "That's a siege. Sieges take weeks. My father is up there *now*, with a man who needs him alive only until the vault opens. The moment that seal is breached, Marcus stops being an asset and starts being a witness. You want to wait out a siege? Fine. Bring flowers."

"And what do you propose?" Voss asked, mild as milk. "Eleven against fifty? I have seen that arithmetic before. It ends with the eleven becoming a story other people tell."

"We don't need to beat fifty," Dominic said. "We need to beat *time*. There's another way up." He pulled out the rubbing Wren had identified weeks ago — Marcus's own notation, copied from the cave carvings — and smoothed it flat on a crate. His finger traced a route that avoided the processional way entirely, climbing instead through the flooded terraces on the vault's blind side. "The builders didn't approach their own archive from the front. Why would they? The front door is for pilgrims and thieves. This—" his finger followed a contour line, "—is the maintenance way. Drainage galleries, service stairs. My father will know it exists. It's why he's leading Renner up the processional way at a crawl. He's buying time because he knows there's another door, and he's praying somebody remembers it."

The fire popped. Rain drummed. Wren looked at the route and felt the truth of it settle into her bones the way a correct translation settles — no argument, just recognition.

"He taught you strata," she said softly. "He taught you how sites think."

"He taught me how *he* thinks," Dominic said. "Which took me nine years to appreciate, and cost me a boat fee I'm still going to bill him for."

Voss studied the young man for a long moment, and Wren watched her do it — watched the salvager weigh speed against safety, greed against grievance, and come out somewhere unexpected.

"A frontal approach with eleven people," Voss said at last, "through flooded galleries, against a force that outguns us five to one. And your contribution is that you can pilot a boat and read your father's mind."

"My contribution," Dominic said, "is that I'm the only one here who grew up reading this man's maps for fun while other children were playing football. Also I make excellent coffee, which morale-wise should not be underestimated."

"He does," Wren confirmed. "It's insufferable."

"It's a French press method," Dominic said with dignity. "It requires patience."

"You," said Voss, "are going to get us all killed, and I am going to allow it, because my wages are up there behind those fifty guns and slow sieges bore me." She snapped her fingers at her crew. "But we do it my way as well. Split the difference. Half my people hit the mule train tonight — that costs me nothing but sleep and buys us chaos. When Renner's radios start screaming about bandits in the rear, he'll peel men off to protect his supplies. Every man he sends down is a man not standing between us and the vault."

"That's actually clever," Dominic said.

"I contain multitudes," said Voss. "Mostly debts."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon dividing roles, and Wren discovered that planning a battle was not so different from planning an excavation: everything came down to who could be trusted with which delicate task, and everyone lied slightly about their qualifications.

Tam and Sefu would guide Voss's strike team down to the gorge trail — they knew the mule path from the expedition's early days, before the split. Voss herself would lead them; she refused, with genuine offense, to send anyone into danger she wouldn't personally attend, framing it not as honor but as quality control. Four of her crew would stay below with the boat and the radio, running interference and ready to extract anyone who came back down the river in a hurry.

That left the ascent party: Dominic, Wren, and Odile's second, a laconic ex-soldier named Brannigan who communicated primarily in nods and, once per day, a full sentence.

"The terraces are flooded to the third level," Tam warned them, drawing in the mud again. "In the old days the drainage galleries carried the water away. If the galleries still work, the stairs above the third terrace will be dry. If they don't work—"

"Then we swim," Dominic said.

"Then you drown," Tam corrected gently, "and I will say kind things about you at the proper ceremonies."

"Your father raised cheerful employees," Wren observed.

"He raised *loyal* ones. Cheerfulness is extra. He paid for it out of his own pocket, I think."

Wren laughed despite herself, and caught Dominic almost smiling too — the two of them orbiting the same absent man, warming their hands at the same strange fire. Grief, she thought, was mostly a family resemblance you couldn't see in mirrors.

By dusk the plan had hardened into something like shape. At first light, Voss's team would slip downriver to the mule trail. An hour later, the ascent party would climb. Renner's force, they estimated, would reach the vault entrance by midday at their current pace — Marcus's pace, Wren reminded herself, *his* pace, every step a negotiation — which gave them a window. Narrow, rain-slicked, and closing.

"Questions?" Voss asked the assembled group.

"Yes," said Brannigan, spending his sentence. "What happens if the galleries are flooded?"

"Then we improvise," said Dominic.

Brannigan nodded, apparently satisfied, which told Wren a great deal about the sort of life he'd led.

* * *

She found Dominic later at the overlook, checking the climbing rope with more attention than it strictly required. Night had come down thick and soft, and below them the enemy camp glowed with cook fires — dozens of them, scattered up the processional way like a fallen constellation.

"One day left, maybe," he said, without turning. "Maybe less. Depends how slow my father decides to walk tomorrow."

"He'll walk very slow," Wren said. "And then, at the last possible moment, he'll do something reckless and brilliant, and it will infuriate you, and it will work."

"You say that like it's a documented pattern."

"It is a documented pattern. I have twenty years of field notes." She sat down beside him on the wet stone, close enough that their shoulders touched, which neither of them acknowledged. "Can I tell you something, since we may die tomorrow?"

"Always a fan of deathbed candor."

"When your father sent me that letter asking forgiveness, I threw it in a drawer for three weeks because I couldn't stand the idea that he'd gotten noble in his old age without my permission." She watched the distant fires. "I built my whole life on the story that Marcus Fairweather was a fraud who ruined me. It was a comfortable story. It explained everything and required nothing of me. And then he goes and spends his last expedition paying villagers' debts and trading himself for his crew, and now the story doesn't fit anymore, and I'm standing here in the rain with no story at all."

Dominic was quiet for a while. Below, a dog barked in the enemy camp, absurd and domestic.

"I used to think," he said finally, "that if I ever saw him again, I'd have a speech prepared. Nine years of material. Very cutting. Well-structured." He shook his head. "Now I've got one look at him across a valley and all the speeches fell out of my head. All that's left is — I want him to walk slow. That's it. That's the whole speech. Walk slow, Dad. Give me time to catch up."

Wren reached over and squeezed his arm once, hard, the way you'd test a rope before trusting it with your weight.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we go get him."

* * *

First light came gray and reluctant, and with it the sound of Voss's team slipping away downriver, oars muffled with rags. By the time the sun made its first failed attempt at the clouds, the ascent party stood at the base of the third terrace, where brown floodwater lapped at ancient stone steps that hadn't seen dry air in five hundred years.

Dominic tested the first submerged stair with a boot, then looked back at Wren and Brannigan, rain streaming off his jaw, and grinned — a real grin, sudden and bright as a match strike.

"After you," he said, gesturing grandly at the drowning architecture. "Mind the fifth step. It's been waiting five centuries to trip somebody, and I refuse to let it be me."

Wren rolled her eyes, hoisted her pack, and stepped into the water.

Behind them, far up the valley, faint through the rain, came the first crackle of Renner's radios waking to bad news — and the bells of the gorge began, very softly, to ring.
