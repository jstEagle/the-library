## 30. The Vault Road

They climbed in the dark because Dominic insisted on it, and for once nobody argued.

"The flood's our friend tonight," he'd said, spreading the rubbing across the driftwood table while the last light died behind the gorge walls. "Renner's men will wait for dawn. Men like that always do. They like to see what they're shooting at." He tapped the carved lines Wren had traced weeks ago from the cave wall. "We don't have that luxury. We go up while the water's doing half the work of hiding us."

Voss had looked at him for a long moment with those flat river-gray eyes, then shrugged and started checking her rifle. "Your funeral," she said, which from Odile Voss was practically a blessing.

Now it was full dark, and the terraces rose above them like the steps of a drowned giant.

The site — Marcus's site, the one he'd kept off every record, the one he'd traded himself to protect — spread up the valley wall in a series of stepped platforms, each one built by hands three thousand years dead. In the dry season they would have been fields, maybe, or gardens, or something stranger; Wren had theories involving ceremonial flooding that she delivered in excited bursts whenever they stopped to breathe. But now, in the teeth of the monsoon, the terraces were a staircase of shallow lakes, water spilling over each lip in thin silver sheets, falling terrace to terrace down the dark hillside with a sound like endless quiet applause.

They went up through the water itself, ankle-deep, then knee-deep, then thigh-deep where the channels deepened between the old planting beds. The flood was their road and their cover both: no sentry could hear six people wading over the sound of a hundred small waterfalls, and no lantern could be seen through the mist that rose off the warm rain-warmed pools.

Dominic led. Behind him came Voss and two of her steadiest crew, then Ilsa — who had refused, flatly and furiously, to stay behind with the other survivors, and whom nobody had been foolish enough to argue with twice — and then Wren, and finally the porter Tammas, who knew these terraces from the expedition's first ascent and moved through the flooded channels like a heron through reeds.

The rain came and went in slow pulses, sometimes a whisper, sometimes a drumroll on the water. Above them, somewhere past the topmost terrace, the vault entrance waited in the cliff face — and somewhere inside it, Renner's lamps were burning, and his men were loading crates, and his father was being marched through chambers he had spent years trying to reach, at gunpoint, as a tool.

*Hold on,* Dominic thought, not for the first time that night, and not quite sure who he was saying it to. *I'm coming. Just — hold on.*

* * *

Halfway up, they stopped in the lee of a terrace wall where an ancient spillway channeled the overflow, and the noise of the water made a pocket of privacy even in the open air.

"Twenty minutes," Voss said, checking a waterproof watch. "Then we move whether we've caught our breath or not."

Nobody caught their breath. They stood in the dark, passing a canteen, listening to the terraces sing around them.

It was Wren who broke the silence, quietly, the way she did when she wasn't sure she wanted an answer. "You're doing the thing again."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you go somewhere else." He could just make out her face in the spillway's faint gleam — the set of her jaw, the way she watched him without pretending not to. "You've done it since the anchorage. Every time we get close to him, you climb inside your own head and lock the door."

"I'm planning the approach," Dominic said.

"You're planning the approach out loud. This is something else."

He should have lied. He was good at lying; he'd had ten years of practice telling dockmasters and customs men and himself that he was fine, that moving on was freedom, that the letter from the foundation meant nothing. But there was something about Wren Calloway at midnight, soaked to the ribs, holding a canteen out to him like an offering, that made lying feel like a waste of breath they couldn't spare.

"When I was nine," he said, "my father taught me to read strata."

Wren went very still.

"We were in some quarry — I don't even remember where. One of a hundred holes in the ground. And he put my hand on the rock face, his hand over mine, and he said—" Dominic stopped. The words had been locked away so long that bringing them out felt like prying open a crate that might be empty. "He said, *every layer is a year, and every year leaves something behind if you know how to look.* And then he traced the bands with my fingers, one by one, oldest to youngest, and told me the rock was a book and most people never learned to read."

The water applauded. Somewhere below, thunder muttered its disagreement.

"And?" Wren said softly.

"And I hated it. I hated standing in a hole in the ground while other boys were playing football. I hated that he could look at dirt and see everything and look at me and see — nothing. An assistant. A pair of small hands to hold the notebook." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "I spent thirty years thinking that day was the beginning of losing him. The quarry. His hand over mine. That was the last time I remember feeling like he was actually *there*, actually looking at me instead of through me. Everything after was expeditions and absences and headlines."

"And now?"

"Now I'm climbing a drowned staircase in the middle of a monsoon," Dominic said, "to rescue a man I haven't spoken to in nine years, using skills he taught me in a quarry before I was ten. Reading the water. Reading the stone. Knowing where the old channels run because I can see how the silt lies against the terrace walls." He looked down at his own hands, ghost-pale in the dark. "Every decision I've made since the anchorage — every shortcut, every safe crossing, every time I've known the ground under us before we stepped on it — it's all him. It's all that day. I've been carrying the inheritance around for thirty years and calling it a wound."

Wren didn't say anything for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was rougher than usual. "My mother taught me to read," she said. "Properly read — manuscripts, marginalia, the things people wrote in the spaces. She died when I was eleven, and I spent twenty years being angry that she left me nothing but books." She handed him the canteen. "Turns out she left me everything but money. It took me about as long to notice as it took you."

Dominic took the canteen. Their fingers touched around the metal, briefly, and neither of them hurried away from it.

"Voss is going to tell us to shut up in about four seconds," he said.

"She absolutely is," Wren agreed.

"Three seconds," Voss said from the darkness. "Move."

* * *

The upper terraces were older, wilder, half-swallowed by the jungle that had crept back over them in the centuries since the builders walked away. Trees grew straight out of the retaining walls, their roots gripping the ancient masonry like fingers, and the floodwater ran deeper here, chest-high in the channels, cold where the mountain seepage fed in beneath the warm rain.

Tammas led now, reading the submerged architecture by touch and memory. Twice he stopped them with a raised fist and rerouted them around channels that looked identical to every other channel but weren't — old cisterns, he signed, deep enough to drown a man, their mouths hidden under the black water. Both times, Dominic knelt and read the silt himself, confirming the porter's judgment: the sediment told the truth even in the dark, piling soft and thick over still water, scoured clean where current ran.

His father's voice lived in that knowledge now, and for the first time in his life, Dominic let it live there without flinching.

*Every layer is a year.*

The third time they stopped, it wasn't Tammas who signaled. It was Ilsa, ahead and to the left, dropping flat into the water with a splash she smothered against her own sleeve.

Lights. Below them, two terraces down, lanterns bobbed along a lower channel — Renner's men, sweeping the terraces with the methodical patience of people who expected to find nothing. Dominic pressed himself against a root-mass and counted four lights, maybe five, moving away to the east, toward the far side of the complex.

"They're checking the lower approaches," Wren breathed, sliding in beside him. "Renner's hedging. He knows someone might follow."

"He knows *we'll* follow," Dominic said. "He just doesn't know how fast."

Above them, the cliff face finally closed over the last of the terraces, rising sheer and black into the rain. And there — exactly where Marcus's rubbing had promised, exactly where the carved procession of figures climbed toward a doorway in the stone — the vault entrance opened in the mountainside. A rectangular mouth, tall as a cathedral door, framed in dressed stone that the jungle had failed to claim. From somewhere deep inside came a yellow flicker of lamplight, and the distant, echoing sounds of industry: men working, crates dragging, orders snapped in a language of impatience.

And one sound underneath the others that stopped Dominic's heart and restarted it sideways.

A cough. Dry, tired, unmistakably human. Unmistakably, somehow, after nine years and a thousand miles and every bitter mile between them, familiar.

His father was alive. Twenty meters of flooded terrace and one guarded doorway away, Marcus Fairweather coughed in the dark like any mortal man, and Dominic's whole body turned toward the sound like a compass needle finding north at last.

"Steady," Voss murmured, and her hand on his shoulder was the only reason he didn't move.

They gathered in the shadow of the final retaining wall, water streaming off them, and Dominic laid out the approach in whispers while the lamplight flickered in the doorway above. Two guards visible outside — one at the entrance, one pacing the ledge. More inside, judging by the voices. The flooded channel ran right to the base of the stairs; the last thirty feet would be exposed.

"I take the ledge man," Voss said. "Quietly, if the river's kind. Loudly, if it isn't."

"And the door man?"

"That's yours," she said, and there was something almost gentle in it. "Been waiting the whole climb to see it, I expect."

Dominic looked up at the doorway. At the light spilling out of it. At the shape of everything he'd been running from for a decade, waiting for him at the top of a staircase built by people who had understood, three thousand years ago, that the water always rises — and that what matters is what you carry up ahead of it.

"Yeah," he said. "That part's mine."

He put his father's lessons under his feet like a foundation, and started up the last stretch of the vault road — and from inside the chamber, clear through the rain and the roar of the terraces, came the sound of Aldous Renner's voice, smooth and unhurried, saying:

"Well, Professor. Your son always was predictable. Shall we greet him together?"
