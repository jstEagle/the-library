## 31. Father and Son

The last stretch of the vault road was not a road at all but a staircase drowned to its third step, and above it a corridor of black basalt that swallowed their torchlight and gave back nothing.

Dominic went first. He had been going first for eleven days now, and his body had stopped arguing about it somewhere around the second night, the way a mule stops arguing once it understands the load is permanent. Behind him came Wren, breathing in the controlled rhythm she used when she didn't want anyone to know how tired she was — he'd learned to hear the difference between her tired and her frightened, and tonight she was both. Behind her, Voss's two steadiest hands, Marta and the boy they called Finch, carrying rope and crowbars and the particular silence of people who expected trouble and had made peace with it.

Voss herself had taken the supply line with the rest of her crew, upriver, where Renner's fuel barges sat fat and unguarded. That had been the bargain: split the enemy's attention, hit him from two directions at once. *You take the vault,* Voss had said, adjusting the strap of her rifle with the air of a woman discussing a dinner reservation. *I'll take his boats. Try not to die before I get there. Dead men are terrible witnesses.*

The corridor climbed. Water ran down its center in a thin, musical thread, feeding the dark, and the walls on either side were carved — Dominic could feel them more than see them, long processions of figures cut into the stone, each one holding something out: a bowl, a seed, a child, a flame. Giving things away. Eleven days ago he would have walked past them without a thought. Now he found himself slowing, letting his torch linger, and Wren caught him doing it and said nothing, which was its own kind of commentary.

"He'd have loved this," she said quietly, after a while. "Your father. A corridor full of gifts."

"He'd have catalogued them," Dominic said. "Numbered them. Argued with another academic about the dating until somebody cried."

"That too." But she was smiling in the torchlight, and so, against his will, was he.

They heard the voices before they saw the light.

* * *

It came filtering down the corridor like smoke — lantern-glow, yellow and wrong after so many hours of torch-orange, and beneath it the sound of men working. Not digging. Something more careful than digging. The scrape of metal on stone, patient as a surgeon.

Dominic killed his torch and pressed himself flat against the wall, and the others did the same, and for a moment the only sounds were the water-thread singing in the channel and his own heart conducting an unscheduled percussion solo.

He edged forward. The corridor opened, without warning, into a chamber so large his torch — when he dared relight it, hooded, low — couldn't find the ceiling. The floor spread out before them in stepped terraces, dry here in the upper gallery, and across the middle of it stood a wall.

Not a wall built. A wall *sealed* — a single slab of fitted stone, forty feet high if it was an inch, covered edge to edge in relief carving, and set into its center a mechanism of interlocking discs that turned like the inside of a lock no locksmith on earth had ever dreamed of. Lanterns on stands ringed it. Men moved among them with pry bars and hand drills, and none of them, Dominic noted, looked happy about any of it.

And standing before the seal, sleeves rolled, gray hair tied back with what appeared to be a piece of twine, was Marcus Fairweather.

Nine years. Nine years since a kitchen table and a slammed door and a sentence Dominic had rehearsed ten thousand times since — *you chose the dirt over us, every time, you always will* — and nine years of imagining this moment in every possible weather, and in none of those imaginings had his father looked like *this*: thinner, sunburnt, one wrist wrapped in dirty bandages, standing in front of the greatest archaeological discovery of the century with the expression of a man being asked to do something he had sworn he wouldn't.

Renner stood beside him.

Dominic had seen him once across a river, at the ford, urbane and unhurried, and the memory had prepared him poorly. Up close — well, as close as thirty yards of torchlit terrace allowed — Aldous Renner was still urbane. Still unhurried. He wore a field jacket that had never seen honest mud and he held a pistol the way other men held a fountain pen, casually, as though it were an accessory to his real instrument, which was his voice.

"I admire your stubbornness, Professor," Renner was saying, and his voice carried in the great chamber the way voices carry in churches — effortlessly, and with a faint suggestion that everyone else was lucky to be present. "Truly. Most men would have broken weeks ago. But you must understand my position. My employers have invested a very great deal in this venture. And you are the only key we have."

"Then your employers," Marcus said, "should have invested in a locksmith."

There was scattered laughter from the workmen — quickly smothered, but real — and Dominic felt something twist in his chest that he refused to examine. It was the voice. That was the unbearable thing. Nine years and the man sounded exactly the same: dry, unhurried, faintly amused by the universe, the voice that had read him bedtime stories about tomb robbers and taught him to tell schist from shale, the voice from the kitchen table, from the slammed door, from every silent Christmas since.

"Professor." Renner's amusement cooled by exactly one degree. "My patience is a renewable resource, but it is not infinite. Open the seal."

"And I've told you," Marcus said, "what happens when I do."

"You've told me a story. About flood chambers and defensive engineering. Very dramatic. Also unverifiable, which is convenient for a man who doesn't wish to cooperate." Renner examined his fingernails. "Here is what I believe. I believe you're stalling. I believe the monsoon is your ally and you are waiting for it to arrive and save you the trouble. And I believe that if I put a drill through the mechanism myself, I will get the same result you would, minus the theatrics."

"The mechanism isn't a lock, it's a *sequence*. Drill it and you'll open perhaps a tenth of the seal. Perhaps. Or you'll bring half this mountain down on your inventory." Marcus's voice stayed level, but his hand — his right hand, the bandaged one — had closed slowly into a fist at his side. "You'll get your treasure, Mr. Renner. In pieces. Underwater."

"Then let us hope," Renner said pleasantly, "that your memory improves."

Dominic became aware that Wren had drawn up beside him in the shadow of the doorway, close enough that he could feel the tension coming off her like heat off a stove. Her eyes weren't on Renner. They were on the workmen — counting, he realized. Counting guns.

"Eight," she breathed. "Two by the lanterns, three on the terrace steps, one at each entrance, one walking. Renner carries his own."

"Voss?"

"Upriver. Twenty minutes, if the boats burn fast. Thirty if they don't." Her jaw tightened. "Marcus can't hold out thirty minutes."

Dominic watched his father turn back to the seal, place both hands — bandaged and unbanded — against the carved discs, and stand there. Not turning them. Just standing, touching it, the way a man touches a door he has decided not to walk through yet. Buying seconds. Spending them one at a time, and everyone in the room knew it, and Renner was letting him because Renner believed, with the serene confidence of the well-funded, that time belonged to him.

"Professor," Renner said again, softly now, and raised the pistol, and pointed it — not at Marcus, but at the young woman standing at the edge of the workmen, thin and brown-haired and holding a satchel of tools like a shield. One of the crew from the camp. One of the ones who hadn't escaped. "Perhaps a change of perspective would help. Shall we begin with someone whose memory is less burdened?"

The chamber went very quiet.

"All right," Marcus said.

The word dropped into the silence like a stone into a well. Dominic heard it land. He felt Wren go rigid beside him.

"Marcus—" the girl started.

"It's all right, Ilsa." His father's voice was steady. Steadier than it had any right to be. "Mr. Renner wants the seal opened. I'm going to open it. And then, Mr. Renner, you and I are going to have a conversation about what your employers actually purchased, because I suspect they were sold something rather different from what they think they bought."

"Marcus, don't—" Wren whispered, and it wasn't strategy in her voice, it was something older and rawer, and Dominic understood suddenly that she had spent twenty years carrying this man in some compartment of herself she'd nailed shut, and the nail was coming loose.

He put his hand on her arm. She looked at him.

"We can't take eight guns in the open," he murmured. "But we don't have to. Look at the entrances."

She looked. Two doorways flanking the lower terrace, besides the one they crouched in — and between them, running along the base of the far wall, the carved procession continued, figures holding out their bowls and seeds and flames, and below the procession, at ankle height, a channel. The same water-channel that had run down their corridor. All three corridors fed into this room. All three channels met here.

And the channels, Dominic noticed, were flowing faster than they had been an hour ago.

Later — much later, in the bad nights that followed — he would try to remember deciding. He never could. There was no decision, just a gap between one heartbeat and the next, and then he was moving, low along the wall, Wren peeling off toward the opposite entrance with Finch, Marta holding the center with the crowbar and a face like Sunday. The workmen were watching the seal. Everyone watches the seal; it's the biggest thing in the room. Nobody watched the shadows at the edges except the one man who did, and Finch solved him gently with a crowbar handle behind the ear, catching him before he finished falling, which Dominic would think kindly of for years.

What happened next happened in pieces.

A lantern went over — Marta, precise as a bill collector — and the near end of the chamber fell into rolling shadow, and shouting bloomed everywhere at once. Voss's diversion, wherever it was upriver, chose that exact moment to announce itself, because a distant boom rolled down the gorge and every head in the chamber turned toward the sound, and in that borrowed second Dominic crossed the open terrace at a dead sprint and put himself between Aldous Renner and Marcus Fairweather.

Renner turned. To his credit — and Dominic would grant him very little credit, ever, but this much — the man did not panic. He simply brought the pistol around, unhurried even now, even with a stranger materializing out of the dark six feet away, and said, with genuine curiosity:

"And who are you?"

"His son," Dominic said.

He hit Renner's wrist with the crowbar Marta had pressed into his hands at the doorway — he honestly didn't remember taking it — and the pistol spun away into the dark, and then everything was motion and noise, workmen surging and falling back, Wren's voice cutting through it all with the crisp authority of a woman who had spent decades making men in committee rooms feel stupid (*Down! Down, you idiot, DOWN—*), and Marcus Fairweather stood rooted before the seal, staring at the intruder who had just disarmed his captor, with an expression Dominic had never seen on that face in his entire life.

It was the expression of a man watching something impossible happen.

"Dominic," his father said.

Just that. Just the name. But his voice broke on it — broke clean through the middle, the composure cracking like river ice in spring, and the sound of it went through Dominic worse than any blow. Nine years of rehearsed speeches, of imagined confrontations, of the perfect devastating thing he would say if he ever saw the man again — *you chose the dirt, you always will* — and every word of it evaporated, because his father had said his name like a drowning man says *shore*, and there was nothing left in Dominic's chest but the awful, ungovernable fact that he had crossed a flooded mountain range to hear exactly that.

"Dad," he said. His voice came out wrecked. He hated it. He kept talking anyway. "You look terrible."

Marcus laughed — a cracked, wet, astonished laugh — and took one step forward with his arms half-lifting, an embrace begun and abandoned and begun again, nine years of hesitation compressed into a single lurching gesture—

And the seal spoke.

* * *

It was not a loud sound. That was the horror of it. A deep, mineral click, somewhere inside the forty-foot slab, like the first bone of something enormous shifting in its sleep — and then a second click, and a third, moving downward, and the water in the channels at their feet stuttered, reversed, began to *drain*.

"No," Marcus breathed. All the joy went out of his face at once. "No, no — the sequence — who touched the—"

One of Renner's men, it emerged later, had gotten a drill partway into the mechanism during the chaos, exactly as Marcus had warned, opening a fraction of the seal — a fraction that was, by design or by catastrophe, enough. The builders of this place had engineered for water instead of against it, Wren had said, wonder in her voice, weeks ago, on a causeway surfacing at low tide. They had built their archive to drown its thieves.

The upper galleries were already flooding. Somewhere above them, meltwater and monsoon were finding the channels, and the channels were finding this room, and the beautiful dry terraces underfoot would not be dry for long.

"Everyone out of the lower gallery!" Marcus's voice transformed — the broken thing vanishing, the expedition leader snapping into place, and even Renner's men obeyed him, because some voices are simply built to be obeyed in emergencies. "Now! Take nothing! MOVE!"

They moved. Workmen and captors alike scrambled for the high terraces, Wren hauling the girl Ilsa by the arm, Finch dragging the man he'd clubbed — *evidence*, he said, with the first words Dominic had ever heard him speak — and Dominic grabbed his father's sleeve and pulled, and for one suspended moment father and son stood together in the torchlight with the water rising around their boots and nine years of silence still unspoken between them, raw, unfinished, a door opened but not yet walked through.

"Dominic," Marcus said again, urgently, gripping his son's wrist with both hands. "Listen to me. When the water comes — there's an order to it. The builders left a passage. I can read it. But I need—"

Above them, with a groan like a waking giant, the first true flood burst through the upper channel, and the lights went out.

In the dark, in the roar, his father's grip on his wrist was the only fixed point in the world — and Dominic, who had spent a decade keeping one hand on the tiller and the other free, held on.
