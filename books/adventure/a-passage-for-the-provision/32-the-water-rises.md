## 32. The Water Rises

The sound the vault made when the seal gave way was not the sound Dominic had expected.

He'd braced for a boom, a crack of stone, something cinematic. Instead it was a sigh — a long, low exhalation from deep inside the mountain, like an old man settling into a chair he'd been standing beside for three thousand years. Then, beneath the sigh, a second sound: water.

Not trickling. Not seeping. *Arriving.*

"Everyone off the lower gallery," Marcus said, and his voice carried the particular calm of a man who had rehearsed this moment in his head for months and was only now discovering that rehearsal and reality disagreed about the volume. "Now, please. The builders designed this. The breach opens the flood channels. It's a defense —"

"It's a *drain*," Renner said.

Dominic had been watching Aldous Renner since they'd entered the chamber, and he'd catalogued the man the way you catalogue weather: urbane, unhurried, the sort of man whose linen shirt stayed pressed even in a torchlit cave. What Dominic saw now, in the first gray seconds after the breach, was the weather turning.

Renner did not retreat. He turned to his men — six of them, armed, wet to the knees already — and said, with the mildness of a man requesting another coffee, "Crates. Start with the index chest. The long gallery will hold for an hour at least."

"An hour," Wren repeated. She'd come up beside Dominic in the dark, torch held high, and her voice had that edge she got when she was doing arithmetic she didn't like. "You're guessing."

"I'm *estimating*," Renner said, "and you're welcome to leave. In fact, I insist."

The water was coming through the breached seal in a way that made Dominic's pilot's stomach clench — not a torrent, not yet, but a *committed* flow, the kind of flow that didn't negotiate. It came from channels in the walls, four of them, spaced around the chamber like the spokes of a wheel, and it fell in silver ropes into the lower gallery where the archive's true depths lay drowned in shadow. The floor there was already shin-deep and rising.

The builders had designed this. Of course they had. A civilization that built *for* water, Wren had said, a civilization that survived its own great flood — they would not leave their archive guarded by locks and soldiers. They'd guard it with the thing they understood best. Breach the seal, and the mountain itself would swallow the treasure, holding it under water until the water chose to give it back.

To whoever came after. Properly. Patiently.

Renner, Dominic thought, had read the same inscriptions and arrived at the opposite conclusion: that anything drownable could be drained, and anything drainable could be carried out in crates.

"Marcus." Renner's voice was pleasant again, which was somehow worse. "The inner chamber. You know the secondary access. The water won't reach the upper archive for some time — the engineering is elegant, I'll grant them that — but I'd like the index catalogued before it does."

Marcus stood very still. Dominic, watching his father in torchlight for the first time in nine years, saw something he'd never seen in any photograph: fear. Not of the water. Of the choice.

"You said my people would be released," Marcus said.

"They were. Days ago. I keep my arrangements." Renner smiled. "This is a different arrangement. This one is between me and the water."

* * *

They retreated up the causeway steps two at a time, torches hissing where spray caught them. Dominic counted heads out of habit — Wren, the two porters who'd stayed with them, Voss's lieutenant Sef with his crowbar and his permanent expression of mild grievance — and found everyone accounted for, which was, by the standards of the last three weeks, a miracle he didn't intend to question.

The upper gallery opened onto a wide terrace of fitted stone, half open to the gorge sky, and here the monsoon found them properly. Rain came in sideways, cold and enormous, and beyond the terrace lip the gorge roared white and brown, swollen past anything the river had shown them on the way up.

"He's going to drown himself," Sef said. He said it the way you'd note that it was Tuesday.

"He's going to drown the *archive*," Wren said. "Himself is optional."

Dominic leaned against a pillar and did his own arithmetic, the kind he was good at. The lower gallery had been shin-deep when they left. The flow from four channels, into a gallery maybe sixty paces long — call it a hand's depth every few minutes, accelerating as the channels cleared. The stairs they'd come up rose perhaps four meters. The upper terrace was higher still, but the upper terrace wasn't where the crates were.

"An hour," he said aloud. "He wasn't guessing. He's been down there before. He knows the rate."

Wren looked at him. Rain ran off her chin. "You sound like you admire him."

"I sound like I've watched a lot of men misjudge water," Dominic said. "He doesn't. That's what makes him dangerous. Stupid men panic when the river turns on them. He'll be calm right up until it closes over his head, and he'll spend every minute until then *loading crates*."

"So what do we do?" Sef asked. "We're six. They're six, they have the rifles, and they have the stairs."

Dominic looked down the dark throat of the passage they'd climbed. Somewhere below, water was rising over three thousand years of careful work, and a man in a pressed shirt was directing its removal like a foreman at a dock.

"We do what the builders did," he said. "We build for water."

* * *

The plan, such as it was, took shape the way their plans always had — quickly, badly, and with Wren improving it.

The terrace had a second exit. Marcus had described it weeks ago in his dictated message, and Ilsa's relay had confirmed it: a service stair cut for the archive's keepers, descending to a landing *above* the flood line, where — and here Wren had gone very quiet when she'd worked it out — the builders had installed their own loading dock. Because the archive was meant to be *inherited*. Provisioned. You didn't build a pantry you couldn't open.

"Their dock, their stairs," Wren said, crouched over the rubbing she'd carried since the karst caves, rain spattering the wax paper. "The water defends the lower gallery, but the upper chambers were designed for access. Renner can't reach them without Marcus, and Marcus —"

"Marcus is down there being reasonable at a man with a rifle," Dominic said.

"Which is why," Wren continued, with the patience of a woman talking a mule across a bridge, "we don't go down the main stairs like polite guests. We take the service stair, we come out on their loading dock, and we become the water's problem instead of theirs."

"Six against six," Sef said.

"Six against six, but they're carrying crates," Dominic said. "You ever try to fight a man who's holding something he can't put down?"

Sef considered this. "I have, actually. My brother-in-law. At a wedding."

"And?"

"I let him keep the crate," Sef said. "It was full of pickled fish. Some victories aren't worth the smell."

Despite everything — the water, the rifles, the nine years and three weeks and the general state of Dominic's life — he laughed. It came out of him sideways, surprised, and Wren looked at him with an expression he couldn't read in the rain, and he decided not to try.

* * *

They moved along the terrace in the rain, single file, and Dominic found himself walking beside his father.

It was the first time. There hadn't been a moment for it — there had been a reunion across a chamber of torchlight and rising water, three seconds of Marcus saying his name like a man testing whether ice would hold, and then Renner's men, and then retreat. Now there was rain and stone and forty paces of terrace, and Marcus Fairweather walked beside his son in silence for most of it, and then said, "You pilot well. Voss told me. She doesn't compliment people, so I assumed she was dying."

"She was being generous. I nearly killed us in the gorge."

"Your grandfather nearly killed us in a gorge once," Marcus said. "Different gorge. Same technique of nearly."

Dominic stared at the path ahead. "You remember that."

"I remember everything about those years," Marcus said. "That's rather the problem." He said it lightly, and then didn't say anything else, and Dominic understood — with a clarity that hurt more than anger would have — that his father was saving the rest. Saving it the way you save the last of the water on a long portage, not because you're selfish but because you know exactly how far you still have to go.

"Not now," Dominic said.

"No," Marcus agreed. "Not now." And then, because he was apparently constitutionally incapable of leaving a silence alone, he added: "The service stair is behind the third pillar. The one with the water glyph. Your mother found it in the survey photos in '89 and wouldn't stop talking about it for a week."

Dominic stopped walking.

"You kept her survey photos."

"I keep everything," Marcus said. "That's rather the other problem."

* * *

The third pillar's water glyph was worn nearly smooth, but the seam behind it was real — a joint in the masonry so fine that rain itself seemed to hide it. Sef and the porters worked the crowbar into it while Dominic kept watch back down the terrace, and below them, through the open throat of the main stair, he could hear it: the water, still climbing, and beneath the water, faint and obscene in its calm, Renner's voice. Giving orders. Naming crates.

*"...the sealed jars first, they're the most fragile — no, the *index* chest, I said the index —"*

"He sounds like a man moving house," Wren murmured at Dominic's shoulder.

"He sounds like a man who thinks the house will wait for him."

The seam gave with a crack, and stone swung inward on hinges that three millennia had not defeated — oiled, somehow, or greased by the mountain's own seep, and the builders' confidence in their own engineering made Dominic's throat tighten in a way he decided not to examine. Beyond the seam: stairs, descending. Dry stairs. And at the bottom, a landing, and beyond the landing, through a doorway carved with the same provision glyphs Wren had spent a week translating —

Light. Torchlight, and voices, and the scrape of a crate across wet stone.

The loading dock. And Renner's men on it.

* * *

They gathered at the top of the service stair, and Dominic laid it out in a whisper while the rain hammered the terrace behind them.

"Four men on the dock, two with Renner below. The dock men are busy. Busy men are slow. We take the dock, we hold the doorway, and we let the water do the arguing about the lower gallery."

"And if Renner uses Marcus as a shield?" Wren asked. It wasn't a challenge. It was the question she had to ask, and they both knew it.

Dominic looked at her. Rain, torchlight, three weeks of shared boats and shared bread and one near-drowning she'd never thanked him for and never needed to.

"Then I remind him," Dominic said, "that I'm the one who knows the waterways. All of them. Including the ones this mountain is currently making."

Wren held his gaze for a moment, then nodded once, and something passed between them that was not quite a plan and not quite a promise but would hold weight, which was all either of them had ever asked of anything.

Sef hefted the crowbar. One of the porters — Tam, the quiet one, who had not spoken a full sentence since the ford — produced from somewhere in his soaked jacket a machete and a look that suggested the machete had opinions.

"Wait," Marcus said.

They all turned. His father stood at the seam, torch high, and the light caught his face — older than the photographs, thinner, with a new scar at the hairline that Dominic's chest ached at without permission.

"The lower gallery," Marcus said. "When the water takes it — and it will, within the hour — the sealed jars will hold. They were built to hold, that's the entire genius of this place, they were *designed* to be flooded and reopened. But the index chest is cedar and cord. It will not hold. And Renner knows it, which is why it's the first thing he'll move." His voice was steady. His hands were not. "Whatever happens on that dock — the index cannot leave this mountain. Everything else can be argued about. Recovered. Rebuilt. The index is the map to all of it. It's —"

"The provision," Wren said quietly.

"The provision," Marcus agreed.

Dominic looked at his father — this man who had left, who had stayed away, who had missed everything and then, at the end, had chosen a flooded archive over a collector's money and a son's name over a foundation's — and found that the anger was still there, exactly where he'd kept it for nine years, but it had changed shape somehow. It fit differently now. Like a tool you'd carried so long you'd forgotten it was a tool and not a part of your hand.

"Then we go get it," Dominic said.

* * *

The service stair was narrow, dry, and steep, and it smelled of cold stone and old resin, and Dominic went down it first because he was the best climber and because if there was a rifle at the bottom he'd rather it found him than Wren, a calculation he did not examine closely.

The landing opened onto the loading dock through a doorway that the builders had carved with figures: people passing vessels from hand to hand, generation to generation, a chain of arms stretching into stone forever. *The provision was never the gold.* Ilsa's relayed message. He'd carried it for days without understanding, and now, passing under the carved arms, he felt the meaning settle on him like rain — patient, total, unavoidable.

The dock was a wide shelf of stone jutting over the drowned lower gallery, and it was chaos of the organized kind: four of Renner's men, torches in brackets, a rope rig over the water, crates on sledge runners. The water below had climbed past the waist-high marks on the gallery pillars. Two men were hauling on the rope, and a crate was coming up out of the black water, streaming, and the men were laughing about something, and the sound of it — laughter, here, in the belly of the mountain, over the drowning of a three-thousand-year-old gift — made Dominic's jaw set so hard his teeth ached.

And there, at the edge of the dock, directing the rig with a lantern in one hand and his shirt somehow still pressed, stood Aldous Renner.

He saw them the moment they came through the doorway. Dominic would give him that — the man's eyes went to the door before anyone else's did, some animal ledger of entrances and exits always running behind the urbane face. And then Renner did the last thing Dominic expected.

He smiled.

"Ah," Renner said, over the rush of the water. "The pilot. And the archivist. And —" his lantern swung to Marcus — "our guide, returned to us. How efficient. I was going to send for you."

"You're out of time," Dominic said. "The gallery's at your men's waists. You've maybe twenty minutes before this dock goes under, and the stairs behind you go before that."

"Twenty minutes is ample." Renner said it with the serenity of a man reading a menu. "The index chest is nearly up. Two more crates and we're done, and then, Mr. Fairweather, you and I are going to have a conversation about the service stair, because I assume that's how you got in, and I assume it's how we're all getting out." He raised the lantern slightly. "You've been very useful. I do hate to waste useful people."

Below them, the water surged — a long, deep swell, the mountain clearing its throat — and the crate on the rope swung and knocked against the dock edge, and somewhere inside it something ceramic chimed, faint and mournful, like a small bell.

And from far above, down the main stair, faint through the rain and the roar, came a sound that turned Renner's smile into something else entirely:

Voss's voice, carrying the way it carried across a whole river, announcing to Renner's men on the upper stairs that she had arrived, that she was armed, and — Dominic could have kissed her — that she was *in a mood about wages.*

Renner's lantern dipped. His men looked at each other. And the water rose another inch.

"Twenty minutes," Dominic said. "Nineteen, now."
