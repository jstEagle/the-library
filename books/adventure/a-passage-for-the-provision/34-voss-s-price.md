## 34. Voss's Price

Wren had spent thirty years in the company of academics and had learned to read a room the way other people read weather. The vault chamber was a storm front.

Water to her ankles and rising — not fast yet, but with the patient confidence of something that had all the time in the world and intended to use it. Torches guttering in the damp air. Renner's men hauling crates up the flooded gallery stairs, their movements getting quicker, less careful, the way men moved when they'd stopped believing their own orders. And at the center of it, Aldous Renner himself, standing on the second terrace step with his sleeves rolled and his voice still infuriatingly level, directing the looting of a three-thousand-year-old archive like a man supervising the packing of a country house.

"You're loading those wrong," Wren said.

Renner turned. He actually smiled at her, which was the thing about him she'd never been able to forgive — not the forgery, not the ruin of two careers, but the smile. The absolute ease of it.

"Dr. Calloway. I wondered when you'd surface. You always did have a talent for arriving late to conversations that had already been decided."

"I'm punctual," Wren said. "I just don't attend yours."

Behind him, Marcus stood at the breached seal with his hands half-raised, an old man playing hostage in the house of his own discovery. His eyes found Wren's across the torchlight, and what passed between them wasn't apology — there wasn't time for apology, and they both knew it would take longer than this night allowed anyway. It was simpler than that. It was *I'm glad you came*, which was more than Wren had expected from anyone, ever.

The water climbed past her bootlaces.

* * *

She had planned the moment on the climb up from the ford, in the wet dark, with Dominic's breathing steady beside her and Voss's crew fanning out along the terraces above. She had rehearsed it the way she'd once rehearsed lectures: twice through, then trust the material.

So now she took the oilcloth packet from inside her jacket — dry, because she had wrapped it in three layers against a monsoon, because some things you protect even when you've forgotten why — and held it up where the torchlight could find it.

"Before your men carry another crate," she said, "you'll want to hear this."

Renner's smile thinned by a degree. "If this is a negotiation—"

"It's testimony." She let the word sit. "Original documents. Correspondence between you and the Velloran Foundation's trustees, dated eleven years ago. Instructions regarding the provenance papers for the Ashgrove collection. In your hand, Aldous. I kept the originals."

The chamber went quiet except for the water, which did not care about drama and kept rising.

"You're bluffing," Renner said. But he said it the way men say things when they are doing arithmetic behind their eyes, and Wren watched the sum come out badly for him.

"I sat in that hearing room and lied," she said, "because your people told me my career would survive it and Marcus's wouldn't, and I was twenty-nine years old and frightened, and I have hated myself for it every day since. That's mine to carry; I'm not asking sympathy for it. But you—" she stepped forward, water slopping over the terrace edge, "—you built a whole architecture out of one frightened woman's lie. You kept Marcus compliant for a decade with the threat of it. And here's the part your men should hear, since they're the ones standing in floodwater committing crimes for you: the documents name them too. Every man who signed a receipt. Every crate logged. It's all in the packet, and copies exist in three places I won't describe."

One of the crate-haulers — a big Salenzan with a rope burn scarred across his forearm — set down his load. Just set it down, carefully, like a man putting down something hot.

"Pick that up," Renner said.

The Salenzan looked at the water, which was now at mid-shin, then at Wren's packet, then at his own hands, as if checking whether they still belonged to him.

"Son," said Marcus quietly, from the seal, "I'd listen to her. She was always the best of us at being right at the worst possible moment."

It was, Wren thought later, the most Marcus thing anyone had ever said, and it nearly undid her composure right there in the flood.

* * *

That was when Odile Voss came down the stairs.

She came unhurried, boots in the water, her crew filtering in behind her along both galleries until Renner's six remaining men realized — in that slow, cold way realizations arrive — that they were surrounded by people who were better armed, better fed, and considerably angrier.

"Aldous," Voss said pleasantly.

"Madam Voss." Renner recovered beautifully, Wren granted him that. "I assume we can discuss—"

"Wages." Voss stopped three paces from him. She was not a tall woman, but she occupied space the way a mooring post occupies space — immovably, and with the implication of everything tied to it. "Six weeks ago you contracted my crew for salvage work in the basin. Fourteen hundred in advance, balance on delivery. Then you cut us loose above Sarn's Reach with nothing, and left us to eat our own boats. My cook has been eating river eel for a month. Do you know what my cook says about river eel?"

"I can't imagine—"

"*Nothing good,*" Voss said. "So here is the arithmetic, since Dr. Calloway has established that we're all doing sums tonight. You owe me four thousand, two hundred. The water is going to take this chamber in—" she glanced at the stairwell, where the flood had begun to pour through in earnest, brown and muscular, "—call it twenty minutes. Your boats are below. My boats are below. Everything you have loaded is going into *my* hold as payment, and you are going to sign a statement saying so, and then you are going to walk out of this gorge ahead of us so that if anything goes wrong, it goes wrong for you first."

"That's robbery," Renner said.

"Yes," Voss agreed. "You taught the whole basin how it's done. Consider this apprenticeship completed."

And Wren — who had braced herself for gunfire, for knives, for the ugly mathematics of men cornered — found herself instead watching a salvage captain conduct a hostile audit with the serene efficiency of a customs officer, and had to bite down on something dangerously close to laughter.

Renner's men made it easy. Two of them waded over to Voss's side before she finished speaking. The Salenzan helped carry crates. The last two looked at Renner, looked at the water — which had reached their knees and was shoving crates off the lower shelves like a bored giant — and made the only sensible decision available to anyone that night.

Which left Renner alone on his terrace step, and the water at Wren's thighs, and the torches dying one by one with little hissing sounds, like the chamber itself closing its accounts.

* * *

"There's a boat passage," Marcus said suddenly.

Everyone turned. He was crouched at the seal, one hand flat against the ancient stone, reading it the way he'd once taught a small boy to read strata — though Wren only knew that story secondhand, and filed it away, because now was not the hour for it.

"The builders engineered this," Marcus went on, fast. "The flood defense isn't destruction, it's *routing*. There's a channel — the provision channel — it carries the overflow down to the lower causeway. If we move now, along the north gallery, we can stay ahead of it. All of us. Even the crates, if we're quick and honest about what's worth saving."

"You mean leave the index," Renner said.

Wren saw it happen. She saw it the way you see a card sharp's tell only afterward, in memory, frame by frame: Renner's glance flicking left, to the bronze case strapped under his arm — the archive index, the master catalog of everything the vault contained, the single object in the chamber worth more than all the crates combined. His weight shifting to his back foot. His eyes finding, over Marcus's shoulder, the black mouth of the flooded stairwell where the water ran fastest — and beyond it, faintly, the gray suggestion of open air.

He was a strong swimmer. Of course he was. Men like Renner rowed at university and talked about it for the rest of their lives.

"Aldous," Wren said, in the voice she'd used on first-year students caught plagiarizing, the voice that gave them one last chance to be a person instead of a problem. "Don't."

He smiled at her. That was the last thing he ever gave her free of charge, that smile, and she would spend years deciding what it had meant.

Then he shoved Marcus into the water and ran.

* * *

Later, Wren would try to reconstruct the next ninety seconds and never quite succeed. Memory broke it into pieces, the way floods break bridges:

Marcus going down with a shout, coming up sputtering, alive — Voss's nearest crewman hauling him by the collar onto the terrace steps, cursing fluently in three languages.

Renner at the stairwell mouth, tearing off his coat, the bronze case clutched to his chest like a life vest, which was exactly backwards, which was the whole man in one gesture.

Voss roaring "*Let him drown, he owes me money*" — and meaning it, probably, seventy percent of it —

—and Marcus, dripping, coughing, pushing past all of them toward the water.

"No," Wren said. It came out very small. The chamber was loud with flood.

"He can't swim that channel with a case strapped to him," Marcus said, already thigh-deep, turning back to look at her, and his face in the last torchlight was ten years younger and nine years sorrier than any face she remembered. "Nobody knows the routing but me. I drew it. I *read* it. If he drowns in there with the index, the archive dies with him — and worse, Wren, worse than that—"

He didn't finish it. He didn't have to. She understood: worse than the archive drowning was Marcus standing on dry stone and letting a man drown while he calculated value. That was the old Marcus, the one the scandal had built, the one Renner had designed. And whatever these months in the basin had made of him instead, he was not going to unmake it tonight, not for Aldous Renner, not for anything.

"Marcus!" she shouted after him. "Dominic will kill you!"

"Then he'll have to get in line behind the foundation, the academic council, and your good self," Marcus called back, and dove.

* * *

The water took him the way the river takes anything — completely, without malice, without pause.

Wren stood on the terrace with floodwater surging around her waist and the torches dead and Voss shouting orders about ropes and the north gallery, and she thought, with terrible clarity: *he does this. This is what he does. He walks into rivers for people who haven't earned it.*

She had testified against him for it, once, in her way — had called him reckless, self-mythologizing, a man who mistook sacrifice for strategy. She had believed every word. Standing in the rising dark, she understood that she had been describing a wound and calling it a character.

"Rope!" Voss bellowed, and her crew threw a coil down the stairwell after the two men, and the current ate forty feet of it before the slack went taut, and somewhere in the black channel below, two sets of hands were fighting each other and the water at the same time.

"Hold it!" Voss screamed. "Hold it and *walk it back*, walk, you dogs, *walk!*"

They walked. The rope groaned. The water rose past Wren's ribs, and someone — the Salenzan, she thought — got an arm around her and hauled her toward the north gallery, and she went, because staying would have been drama and she had never in her life confused drama with usefulness.

At the gallery mouth she turned, one last time, toward the stairwell.

The rope was moving. Hand over hand, foot by foot, something was coming up out of the channel — heavy, dragging, fighting the suction of the outflow. A head broke the surface of the flooding gallery: gray-haired, gasping, one arm locked around a second figure who was coughing and clawing and very much not drowned.

No bronze case.

"Marcus!" Wren waded to him, grabbed him, felt how cold he was, how light, how *old*, and held on anyway. "The case—"

"Gone," he rasped. "Took it off him in the channel. Current wanted it more." He coughed, laughed, coughed again. "First honest appraisal Renner's ever received."

"And Renner?"

Marcus looked back at the black water. So did Wren. The stairwell mouth churned, empty, indifferent. Somewhere downstream, in the dark, a man who had spent his life arranging for other people to bear risk was learning what it weighed, alone, with nothing to hold.

"He's alive," Marcus said. "He surfaced past the third bend — I heard him swearing at the river. The river doesn't negotiate, Wren. It'll keep him till morning and give him back humbler."

"Nothing humbles that man."

"The Velloran basin," said Marcus, teeth chattering, "hasn't met him yet."

* * *

They got everyone up to the upper terraces as the lower chambers finished filling — the great doors of the archive swallowing the flood exactly as their builders intended, sealing the deep stores under cold dark water, safe as a bank vault, safe as a grave, safe as a promise. The provision channel thundered somewhere beneath them, doing its ancient work. Above, through a crack in the gorge wall, the sky was going the color of wet slate, and the rain had eased to a whisper, and the wind in the gorge above began, very faintly, to ring the hanging stones.

Voss appeared at Wren's elbow, wringing out her hat with an expression of profound grievance.

"My crates," she said, "are on high ground. My crew is accounted for. My debtor is currently marinating somewhere downstream." She paused. "Your friend Marcus owes me a rescue fee. Standard rates. I'll invoice him."

"He'll pay it," Wren said. "He's terrible with money and wonderful with debts."

"Mm." Voss settled the ruined hat back on her head. "That's the same thing, usually. It wasn't, tonight." She looked at Wren then, directly, and for the first time since Sarn's Reach there was nothing transactional in it. "You kept those papers eleven years. Through disgrace and poverty and whatever it is you two have been doing to each other's hearts since the dock at Corvane. Why?"

Wren watched the water rise over the last visible shelf of the lower gallery, closing over three thousand years of somebody's careful hope.

"Because I knew I'd need them," she said. "I just didn't know the need would look like this."

Downstream, faint through the gorge, came a sound that stopped her heart and started it again in the same breath: a voice, hoarse and furious and unmistakably alive, calling a name up through the rain-washed dark.

Calling *Dominic*.

Marcus gripped her arm. "That's Ilsa," he said. "That's Ilsa's voice — she was with the evacuation party, she was supposed to be miles downriver by now—"

The bells rang overhead. The water roared below. And between them, upstream, something was very wrong.
