## 35. The Handhold

The water took his father the way the river had taken everything else in that gorge — without malice, without ceremony, simply because it could.

One moment Renner was there at the edge of the flooded gallery, clutching the archive index to his chest like a man hugging a lifeboat, and the next the current had him, and then — God help them all — Marcus went in after him.

"Marcus!" Wren's voice cracked across the chamber, but the water was louder.

Dominic didn't think. That was the thing he would remember afterward, the thing he would turn over in his hands for months like a river stone: there was no decision in it at all. Ten years of running, ten years of keeping himself light and mobile and unattached so he could always be somewhere else by morning, and when it finally mattered, his body made the choice before his pride could file an objection.

He dove.

* * *

The lower gallery was a drowned cathedral. Torchlight from above came down in broken columns, and beneath them the water moved with a purpose that felt almost personal — channeled through the ancient sluice-ways the builders had cut a thousand years ago, funneled exactly where they wanted intruders to go. The archive's architects had designed this defense the way other civilizations designed walls. Come for our inheritance, the water said, and we will show you what inheritance costs.

The cold hit Dominic like a slap from a dead god. The monsoon melt was in it, mountain-cold, and it closed over his head and shoved him sideways into a pillar before he'd finished his first stroke. He caught the stone, pushed off, got his bearings by the pale smear of torchlight on the surface far above.

And there — twenty feet downstream, half-submerged, arms windmilling — his father.

Marcus Fairweather had crossed deserts, mapped rivers no surveyor had named, outlasted fevers and mutinies and one memorable sandstorm in the Karash that had made the papers. He was sixty-three years old and he swam like a man fighting the ocean itself, which is to say badly, heroically, and with total disregard for technique. He had Renner's collar in one fist. The collector's agent was thrashing, dragging them both under, coming up coughing, going down again.

"Dominic—" His father saw him. Even now, even drowning, the name came out like an apology.

"Don't talk," Dominic shouted, and swallowed half the gallery doing it.

He struck out. The current fought him every foot of the way, and somewhere in the white noise of it he heard Voss bellowing orders above — her crew rigging a line off the broken balustrade, rope hissing out into the dark — and Wren's voice cutting through it all, steady as a metronome: "Line's anchored! Dominic, the line! Get to the line!"

But the line was upstream and his father was downstream, and there had never really been a question about which direction Dominic Fairweather traveled.

* * *

He caught Marcus's wrist at the mouth of the sluice-channel, where the water compressed itself between two great carved guardians — long-faced stone figures, their features worn to suggestions, their hands raised in gestures that might have been blessing or warning. The channel beyond was black and roaring. It wanted them. It had been waiting a thousand years to want something.

Renner was gone.

One second the man was there, fingers scrabbling at Marcus's sleeve, and the next the current peeled him away like a label off wet glass. Dominic had one image of him — eyes wide, mouth open, the archive index still clutched against his chest, that precious sheaf of vellum and notation blooming outward in the water like a dark flower — and then the sluice took him, and the roar swallowed everything, and Aldous Renner, who had spent years arranging other people's lives like furniture in rooms he never had to live in, went wherever the water decided.

Marcus lunged after him.

Dominic grabbed him around the chest. "No!"

"He has the—"

"It doesn't matter!" The words tore out of him rawer than he meant. "It doesn't matter, do you hear me? Let it go!"

His father struggled against him — not to escape, Dominic realized, but toward, always toward, the same man who had walked away from every dinner table and birthday and hospital waiting room because some horizon was pulling at him. Even now. Even here. Duty like a compass needle, pointing away from everyone who loved him.

"You let me go," Marcus gasped, and there was something terrible in his face, something that wasn't about the index at all. "You have to let me go, I can't—"

"You can." Dominic hauled him back from the channel's lip, arm locked under his father's ribs, boots finding purchase on a submerged step. "You can, and you're going to, because I am not letting go. That's the whole point. That's the only point."

The water slammed them both against the guardian statue. Stone ground against his shoulder blade; he tasted blood and didn't care. Above them the torchlight swung — Voss's people leaning over the balustrade, the rope snaking down, Wren's silhouette against the fire-glow, shouting something the water shredded before it reached him.

"I've got you," he said into his father's ear. "I've got you, old man. Stop swimming."

And Marcus Fairweather — who had never once, in Dominic's entire memory, stopped moving, stopped reaching, stopped choosing the far shore over the near one — stopped.

He went limp against his son's chest. Not surrender. Something harder than surrender. Trust, maybe, or its first draft.

* * *

Getting up was worse than getting in.

The rope was there, slick and thrashing, and Voss's crew hauled while Dominic climbed one-handed with his father's weight dragging at him like an anchor with a heartbeat. Twice the current tried to peel Marcus loose. Twice Dominic got a fist deeper into his jacket and held. His arms were shaking, his lungs full of iron, and somewhere below his exhaustion a voice that sounded suspiciously like Wren said, *you know, most sons just write letters*, and he laughed — actually laughed, a wild bark of a sound that the gorge ate whole.

Then hands were on him from above — Voss herself, iron-fingered, hauling him over the balustrade as if he were a crate of dubious provenance — and he was down on wet stone with his father beside him, both of them streaming, both of them breathing, both of them alive.

For a moment nobody spoke. The gallery roared. Water climbed the steps behind them, patient as arithmetic.

Voss looked at the two of them — the famous archaeologist flat on his back, and the pilot who'd once refused to shake her hand now coughing up half a civilization's flood defenses — and something in her granite face shifted.

"Well," she said. "That's the first honest thing anyone's done on this river all season."

* * *

Marcus sat up slowly, the way old men sit up when their bodies have started sending itemized invoices. His gray hair was plastered to his skull. His hands, those hands that had taught a boy to read strata in the red dirt behind their house, were trembling — and not, Dominic suspected, entirely from the cold.

"The index," Marcus said hoarsely. "The master record. Every chamber, every catalog entry—"

"Gone." Wren dropped to her knees beside them, wringing out her sleeves, her face pale but her eyes ferociously bright. "Along with Renner. And before you say one word about going back in — look at the water."

They looked. The lower gallery was a lake now, rising in visible increments, swallowing the steps two at a time. The builders' design unfolding exactly as intended: the treasure defended not by locks but by arithmetic, each intruder's greed weighed against the flood schedule, and greed always arriving too late.

"It's not gone," Dominic said.

Everyone turned to him. His teeth were chattering so hard the sentence came out in pieces, but he made himself finish it. "Wren copied the notation system off the rubbing in the caves. You told me yourself — the site records everything twice. Surface archive and deep archive. If your index followed the same principle—"

Marcus stared at him. And then, slowly, unbelievably, the famous explorer began to laugh — a cracked, wheezing, waterlogged laugh that bent him double and ended in coughing.

"He reads strata," Marcus managed, looking at Wren. "Did you know that? I taught him myself. He used to complain the whole time."

"You taught me to complain," Dominic said. "The strata were incidental."

* * *

They got him up the stairs by fits and starts, Marcus protesting that he was fine, that they should secure the upper chambers, that someone needed to check on Ilsa and the porters — and Wren overruling him with the flat authority of a woman who had waited nine years to be right about something. In the antechamber, where the last dry torches burned, they wrapped him in a salvager's oilcloth and put hot tea in his hands, and Voss posted two of her people at the stair with instructions that amounted to *if he moves, sit on him.*

Dominic stood apart, dripping, watching the water rise through the doorway below.

He kept seeing it. Not Renner going under — he found, examining himself honestly, that he did not much care about Renner going under, though he hoped the man had washed up somewhere embarrassing rather than nowhere at all. What he kept seeing was his father turning back. The lunge. The reflex of a lifetime, reaching for the thing that mattered less than the person holding him back.

Nine years, Dominic had built a father out of grievances. A man who chose ruins over recitals, headlines over homework, a stranger's mummy over his own son's fever. The building had been good work. Solid. Load-bearing. He had lived in it comfortably.

And now here was the actual man, shivering under an oilcloth, complaining about the tea, and the whole structure was coming down faster than the flood could fill the galleries.

"You could have died," Dominic said. It came out angrier than he intended. Most true things did.

Marcus looked up. Torchlight moved on his ruined, familiar face. "So could you."

"That's different."

"Is it?" His father set down the cup. "Because from where I was — from where I have been, these past weeks, these past years, if I'm honest — it looked remarkably similar. Two fools in a flood channel, neither one willing to let go of something."

"The index wasn't worth—"

"No," Marcus agreed quietly. "It wasn't. But that's not what I was holding onto, son. And I don't think it was what you were holding onto either."

The water rose. Somewhere below, the deep archive was closing its stone throat over everything Renner had come to steal, keeping it safe the way it had been kept safe for a thousand years — for whoever came after, for whoever inherited it, for whoever was willing to arrive on the flood's own terms instead of fighting them.

Dominic sat down next to his father. Their shoulders touched. Neither of them moved away.

"I've got you," he said again, quieter this time. Testing how it sounded outside the roar of the water.

Marcus's hand found his — cold, trembling, unmistakably real — and held on.

Above them, faint through the gorge's throat, carried on the wind that was already beginning to soften as the storm spent itself, the hanging stones of the gorge began to ring.

* * *

They surfaced into a world scrubbed clean.

Dawn was working its way down the gorge walls, gilding the terraces, finding the tops of the drowned causeway. The rain had thinned to a drifting silver haze. On the landing shelf, survivors were counting themselves aloud — Ilsa's voice cracking on the number, the porters answering, Voss's crew adding themselves with the grudging precision of people unused to being counted among anyone's survivors.

Wren found Dominic at the edge of the shelf, both of them watching the water where it churned over the sealed galleries.

"He'll want to dive for it," she said. "The index. As soon as he can stand."

"He'll want to. He won't." Dominic rolled his shoulder, which had begun sending complaints of its own. "There's a copy. There's always a copy. He taught me that too — the useful lesson, anyway."

Wren glanced at him sidelong. "You went in after him."

"You went in after me."

"Somebody had to hold the rope." She said it lightly, but her hand found his arm and stayed there, brief and warm. "For what it's worth — the way he looked at you, when you pulled him out. I spent fifteen years working with that man and I never saw him look at anything like that."

Dominic watched the flood settle by degrees, the gorge accepting its new waterline, the bells overhead ringing soft and irregular in the clearing wind.

"Yeah," he said. "I saw it."

Down the shelf, his father was arguing with Voss about salvage rights, gesturing at the water with the undimmed enthusiasm of a man who had very nearly drowned an hour ago and considered it a reasonable price of admission. Voss was not smiling. Then, briefly, terribly, she was.

Dominic realized he was smiling too, and that for the first time in ten years, he had absolutely nowhere else he wanted to be.

Then Ilsa came running along the shelf, waving something above her head — a waterproof case, river-stained, stamped with the foundation's seal — and shouting words that turned the morning upside down:

"There's more of them! Downriver — a second party — they're signaling from the causeway!"
