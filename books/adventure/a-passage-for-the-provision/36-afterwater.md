## 36. Afterwater

The dawn came up gray and then gold, the way it does in the Velloran basin when the monsoon spends itself overnight and leaves the sky scrubbed clean as a recovered plate.

Dominic sat on the highest terrace of the outer ruins with a tin cup of tea that tasted mostly of smoke and watched the water fall. Not rain — the river itself, dropping. You could measure it against the drowned causeway: an hour ago only the crown of the stones had shown, slick and dark; now a full arm's length of masonry stood above the current, steaming faintly in the early light, like something surfacing from a long sleep.

Below him, on the terrace steps, people were counting themselves.

He'd heard them doing it all night, actually — not aloud so much as by habit, the way sailors do. Wren had counted twice before she finally slept, once by names and once by breathing. Voss's crew had counted their own three times, and then, grudgingly, everyone else's. The porters had counted in their own tongue, soft and rhythmic, a sound like prayer or bookkeeping, which in Dominic's experience amounted to the same thing.

Now the count came up the steps to him in person: Ilsa, Marcus's young assistant, mud to her knees, hair drying into a shape that defied both gravity and diplomacy.

"Forty-one," she said. "Forty-one alive. Two lost in the flood year, before we ever reached the site — that's separate. And Renner's men—" She paused, choosing. "Renner's men are accounted for too. Eleven walked out. Four didn't."

"His problem," said Odile Voss, arriving behind her with the particular satisfaction of a woman whose invoice was about to get very interesting. She lowered herself onto the wet stone beside Dominic without asking, which he was learning was simply how she sat anywhere. "Though his creditors will make it everyone's problem eventually. Men like that don't drown clean."

"He didn't drown at all," Dominic said. "The river just took what he came for."

"The index." Voss turned her cup slowly, watching the light move through it. "Thirty years of cataloguing every stolen thing in this basin, gone into the gorge. You know what that is?"

"A mercy?" Dominic offered.

"It's *poetry*," said Voss, and drank her tea.

Ilsa lingered, shifting her weight. "Dr. Ashgrove is asking for you," she said. "He won't say so directly. He's asked me three times whether you're sleeping, which is how he asks."

"How is he?"

"Old," Ilsa said, and then, catching herself, "tired. Tired and old are different things. He's tired."

She went back down the steps. Voss looked sideways at Dominic over the rim of her cup.

"You want me to clear off," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I want you to finish your tea at your own pace."

Voss smiled — briefly, crookedly, the smile of a woman who had spent three chapters' worth of trouble deciding whether she liked him and had apparently, somewhere around the Gorge of Bells, come down on the yes side. She drained the cup, stood, cracked her neck like a rifle bolt, and went down the steps calling for someone named Piet to account for a missing crate of rope, in tones that suggested Piet's morning was about to become educational.

Dominic stayed where he was a while longer.

* * *

The upper chambers had survived.

Everyone kept saying it to each other through the morning, the way people repeat good news to make sure it stays true. The flood defense — the builders' great deliberate machine of channels and counterweights and sealed galleries — had done exactly what its architects intended four thousand years ago. It had swallowed the lower vaults whole, taken Renner's ambitions and the archive index down into the dark, and left everything above the third terrace dry, intact, and waiting.

Wren spent the morning walking those chambers with her notebook open and her hands behind her back, touching nothing, cataloguing everything. Dominic found her in the long gallery they were already calling the granary — rows of sealed clay vessels taller than a man, each stamped with the same sign: a hand open, palm up, holding a sprout.

"They're seed stores," she said, without turning around. She always knew his footsteps now; he'd stopped finding that unsettling and started finding it — he didn't have a word for what he'd started finding it. "Grain, legumes, tree seeds, packed in ash and resin. Still sealed. Some of these may still germinate." Her voice did something it rarely did — wavered. "Four thousand years, Dominic. They built a library of *next springs*. For whoever came after. For us, if you want to be sentimental about it."

"I'm told sentiment runs in the family."

"So I've heard." She turned. There was clay dust on her cheekbone and she looked, he thought, happier than he'd ever seen her, in the specific way of a scholar standing inside the answer to a question she'd been asking since she was nineteen. "Your father understood it months ago. Before anyone. That's why the message — *the provision was never the gold.* He wasn't being cryptic. He was being precise. He just couldn't explain it in one line to a man who wouldn't read his letters."

"He could have tried two lines."

"He could have," Wren agreed. "Men could do a lot of things." She said it gently, and let it go, which was also becoming a habit between them — setting things down near each other instead of forcing them together, letting time do the joining.

They walked the gallery together. At the far end, a wall of inscriptions ran floor to ceiling, and beneath it, on a folding table salvaged from Renner's own camp — Voss had insisted on the irony — lay the beginnings of a proper record. Cameras. Drawing boards. Numbered tags. Wren's system, argued into existence over Voss's objections at roughly two in the morning, with Marcus arbitrating from a camp stool like a very tired judge.

"Documented properly this time," Wren said, looking at it. "No rubbings smuggled out. No private maps. Every find logged, photographed, published. It stays here, and the world comes to it."

"That'll go over well with certain people."

"Certain people can learn to swim."

* * *

Marcus was sitting alone at the edge of the high court, on an overturned crate, eating ration biscuits with the concentration of a man performing surgery on them.

Up close, the expedition had cost him visibly. His hands trembled slightly — cold, exhaustion, nine days on short rations in a flooded vault, take your pick. His hair, always iron-gray in the photographs Dominic had grown up resenting, had gone fully white around the temples sometime during the weeks Dominic hadn't seen. He looked smaller than the legend. He looked, in fact, about the size of an ordinary father, which was possibly the most disorienting thing Dominic had witnessed all week, and he had witnessed a man lose thirty years of stolen history to a river.

"Dominic." Marcus set down the biscuit as though it might be needed as evidence later. "You slept?"

"Some."

"Good. Good." A pause. The river filled it, running gold below the terrace. "I've been composing something," Marcus said. "In my head. All night. An apology. It's quite thorough — I've covered the expeditions you missed, the birthdays, the letter I never answered, the whole— there's a section on your mother that I think is necessary, though I warn you it isn't easy listening—"

"Papa."

The word came out of Dominic before he'd decided anything about it. Nine years since he'd used it. It landed on the stone between them like a dropped coin, ringing.

Marcus stopped. His jaw worked once.

"Don't," Dominic said. "Not the speech. Not now. Maybe not ever in one piece — maybe in installments, over years, with tea." He sat down on the crate beside his father, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, and found that he wanted them to, which was new information. "We're forty-one people on a mountain that's still flooding, with one boat, no fuel contract worth the paper it isn't written on, and a four-thousand-year-old seed library to document before the next rains. Whatever you have to say to me — say it slowly. Say it staying."

Marcus looked at him. And Dominic watched something leave his father's face — not the guilt, that would take longer, that would take years and probably should — but the *bracing*, the flinch he'd been carrying since the vault chamber, the expectation of the blow. It went out of him like tension out of a cable, and what was left underneath was just a tired old man on a crate in the sun, blinking.

"Staying," Marcus repeated, testing the word the way you test ice.

"If we both choose it," Dominic said. "That was going to be my condition. I had a whole list. Wren helped."

"She would. She always drafted my better arguments too." Marcus picked up his biscuit again, considered it, and then laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a door opening in a house long shut. "You know, when you were six, you told me you'd grow up to be a river pilot specifically so you'd never have to dig anything. You were very firm about it. You'd worked out that pilots got to leave."

"I remember."

"And here you are," Marcus said, gesturing with the biscuit at the drowned city, the terraces, the rising light, the whole improbable inheritance of it, "having piloted up a river to dig me out."

"Technically I piloted up a river because the foundation owed me a boat fee."

"Naturally."

"The fee has gotten considerably more complicated."

"They usually do." Marcus was quiet a moment. Then, carefully, the way a man reaches across a gap he's spent nine years widening: "There will be time, then? For the installments?"

Dominic looked out at the water, which was falling, steadily, measurably, exposing another course of ancient stone with every hour — the past coming up to meet them, patient as anything.

"Yeah," he said. "There'll be time."

* * *

By midmorning the camp had organized itself into the kind of controlled chaos that Wren privately considered the highest form of human achievement.

Porters ferried equipment up the stairways. Ilsa ran the tagging station with the serene tyranny of a much older woman. Voss's crew, under strict treaty, carried crates and touched nothing unnumbered, and Voss herself stood at the edge of the flooded causeway negotiating — loudly, joyfully, with a notebook — the salvage terms for Renner's abandoned forward camp downstream, which she regarded as rightfully hers by maritime law, frontier custom, and personal grievance, in ascending order of importance.

It was Wren who noticed the boat first.

Their boat — leaking, patched, half-mourned — sat at its mooring below the terrace where they'd left it, riding noticeably higher in the falling water. And standing in it, holding the painter, wearing the expression of a man who has been paid in advance and intends to earn it, was the watchman.

"You're supposed to be at the inland mooring," Dominic called down.

"I was," the watchman called back, entirely unbothered. "Then the fuel thieves came back. Then the water came up. Then the water went down. I made decisions."

Wren leaned toward Dominic. "I like him."

"You like anyone who inconveniences other people on schedule."

"I contain multitudes."

They went down together. The watchman handed over the painter and delivered his report with the economy of a man billing by the word: Voss's crew had returned the fuel — most of it — two nights ago, along with a note. He produced it from inside his shirt, damp but legible.

Wren read it and went still.

"What is it?" Dominic said.

She handed it to him. Voss's handwriting, blunt as a chisel:

*Terms amended. Found something in Renner's camp you'll want. Paper trail. Names, dates, payments — including some old ones. Ask your archivist what she's been sitting on. We talk before Sarn's Reach. — O.V.*

Dominic read it twice. Then he looked up at Wren, who was studying the far bank of the river with tremendous scholarly interest.

"'What she's been sitting on,'" he read aloud.

"Mm."

"Wren."

"There are documents," she said, to the far bank. "From the scandal. Originals. I've kept them for eleven years."

"Kept them *where*?"

"In a waterproof case, in a bank, under a name that isn't mine, because I testified against your father under pressure from the foundation and then spent a decade making sure that if anyone ever came to collect on it, I'd have proof of who actually forged what." She turned and faced him at last, chin up, eyes steady, a woman stepping off a ledge she'd been standing on for years. "I was going to tell you. On the boat, I mean. Several times. I got as far as *there's something you should know* at least four times and then a logjam happened, or a leak happened, or your father happened."

The river ran gold between them. Somewhere above, the bells of the gorge caught the wind and rang, faint and clear, like the place clearing its throat.

"Sarn's Reach is two days downriver," Dominic said finally.

"Two days," Wren agreed.

"Long enough to tell me the rest?"

Her mouth twitched — relief and terror sharing the same expression, which he was learning was simply how courage looked on her. "Long enough," she said, "if you buy the tea at the trading post. I draw the line at confessing dehydrated."

Dominic coiled the painter over his shoulder and looked back once, up the terraces, at the drowned city giving itself up stone by stone to the morning — at his father small and white-haired on a crate in the sun, already leaning over Wren's drawing boards, pointing, explaining, *staying.*

Two days downriver. One conversation eleven years overdue.

He pushed off into the gold water, and for the first time in his life, Dominic Fairweather was not leaving anything behind.
