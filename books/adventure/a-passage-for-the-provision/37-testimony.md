## 37. Testimony

The river gave them back to the world in stages.

First the gorge walls fell away, and the sky opened like a hand unclenching. Then the water widened and gentled, brown going gold in the morning light, carrying them past villages where children ran the banks shouting at the boat as if it were a festival float. And finally, on the fourth day downriver, the town of Merrow's Landing appeared out of the haze — a proper town, with a telegraph office, a customs house, a constabulary with an actual roof, and a bakery whose smell reached the dock before the dock did.

Wren stood at the rail and breathed it in — woodsmoke and wet stone and frying bread — and felt something in her chest unknot by degrees. Nine days since the vault. Nine days since the water took Renner's index and very nearly took Marcus Fairweather along with it. Nine days of porridge cooked over driftwood fires, of Ilsa singing porter songs off-key, of Dominic sleeping in shifts with one hand always near the wheel even when there was no wheel to be near.

She was tired down to her bones. She was also, she realized with some surprise, happy. Or at least standing in the general neighborhood of happiness, waving politely.

"You're smiling," Dominic said beside her. "It's unsettling."

"I'm told it happens."

"To other people, mostly." He nodded toward the customs house. "That's your stop, isn't it? The documents?"

Wren touched the oilskin packet inside her coat — the same packet she had carried for eleven years, through three countries and two careers and one long, slow-burning shame. The originals. The provenance papers Marcus had supposedly forged, the correspondence proving who had actually commissioned the forgeries, the ledger entries in Aldous Renner's own tidy hand. She had kept them the way people keep a loaded thing they're afraid to set down: carefully, and with both hands.

"They're my stop," she agreed.

* * *

Constable-Inspector Dree Halloway was a broad, unhurried woman with ink-stained fingers and the expression of someone who had heard every kind of story the river could deliver and believed perhaps half of them. She listened to Wren's account without interrupting — a full hour, while rain ticked against the office window and Marcus waited outside on a bench like a schoolboy summoned over a matter of stolen apples.

Then she spread the documents across her desk and read.

Wren watched her read. Watched the inspector's eyebrows perform a slow, complicated dance. Watched her stop at the third page, look up, and say, "This is Aldous Renner. Of the Renner collection. The one with the wing of the civic museum named after him."

"The same."

"He drowned."

"Renner drowned," Wren said. "His operation didn't. There are buyers, brokers, two shipping agents, and at least one foundation trustee in these pages. Renner was the hands. Somebody else is still the head."

Inspector Halloway looked at the pages a long moment. Then she did something Wren hadn't expected: she pulled a fresh sheet toward herself and began writing, quickly, the ink-stained fingers suddenly not unhurried at all.

"Eleven years," Halloway said as she wrote. "You sat on this for eleven years."

"Yes."

"Why?"

It was the question Wren had asked herself in the dark of a hundred camps, and she gave the inspector the only honest answer she had. "Because using them meant admitting what they cost. If I'd turned them in, I'd have had to say aloud that I lied under oath, that I helped destroy a man's career, that I let myself be frightened into it. Keeping them felt like cowardice with a purpose attached." She paused. "I've recently learned there's a difference between keeping a thing safe and hiding behind it. It took a flood and a rope bridge to teach me, which I don't recommend as pedagogy."

Halloway snorted — the first human sound she'd made in an hour. "And now?"

"Now a man nearly died in rising water because the lie I helped tell put him in a collector's pocket. Now his son pulled him out of it. Now—" Wren stopped, surprised by the thickness in her own voice. She cleared her throat. "Now it costs more to keep them than to give them away. That's all. That's the whole arithmetic."

Halloway finished writing, blotted the page, and slid it across. "Read that. Sign it if it's true."

It was true. All of it, including the parts that made Wren wince — her own false testimony quoted back to her in flat official language, her recantation set beside it like a wound next to its stitching. She signed. Her hand shook only a little.

Outside, Marcus was feeding crumbs to a duck with the air of a man who had done nothing more remarkable that year than wait for his research partner outside a constable's office. When she came out, he stood, and for a moment neither of them spoke.

"Well?" he said.

"Your name will be cleared. Formally. There'll be a review, statements, the usual machinery grinding slowly forward, but—" She handed him the inspector's receipt. "It's done, Marcus. Eleven years late, but done."

Marcus looked at the paper. His eyes went bright, and he blinked hard and turned slightly toward the river, and Wren had the grace to study the duck until he'd recovered.

"You kept them," he said finally. "All this time. You could have burned them and been free of the whole business."

"Burning evidence seemed beneath my standards."

"It's what I'd have done."

"That," Wren said, "has always been the problem with you, Marcus Fairweather. Your standards are load-bearing and your self-regard is structural. Between the two of us, the building stands." She offered her arm, formal as a treaty. "Come on. I'm told the bakery on Harbor Street makes a custard bun that justifies the entire monsoon season."

* * *

The machinery of justice, Wren learned over the following week, ground slowly but with surprising appetite once fed. Halloway's superiors in the provincial capital sent investigators; the investigators sent telegrams; the telegrams came back with names attached. A foundation trustee resigned by return post. A shipping agent was detained at a port two hundred miles south trying to board a freighter with a trunk full of artifacts that had no business existing. The civic museum quietly repainted the lettering on Renner's wing — Wren heard they were considering renaming it for the flood archive, which she intended to suggest, loudly, at every opportunity.

She wrote statements. She answered questions. She sat in warm rooms across from officials who kept expecting her to be either defiant or broken and seemed genuinely puzzled to find her instead drinking their tea and correcting their dates.

"You don't seem afraid," one young investigator said, on the last day.

"I was afraid for eleven years," Wren said. "I'm told it's like a tide. Eventually it goes out and you find out what it left on the beach." She tapped the folder between them. "Mostly it left paperwork."

* * *

Odile Voss arrived in Merrow's Landing on the ninth day, aboard a patched and pugnacious river boat crewed by salvagers who looked, collectively, like they'd been through a washing machine set to "boulders." She came up the dock road to the inn where the expedition survivors had gathered, found them on the veranda — Marcus dozing in a chair, Ilsa mending a net, Dominic cleaning a compass that did not need cleaning because he could not sit still — and planted herself in front of them with her arms crossed.

"Wages," she said.

"Good afternoon to you too," said Ilsa.

"Afternoon. Wages." Voss held up fingers. "Three weeks' contract, cut short by Fairweather senior going dark. Hazard pay for the gorge — and I mean the gorge with the bells, which I have decided never to discuss again. Compensation for fuel stolen from your own boat, which I'll note was a misunderstanding of strategy rather than theft, and which we have since returned most of." She paused. "Plus salvage rights."

"Salvage rights," Dominic repeated, not looking up from the compass.

"Your father's site sits in flood country. Things wash out of it every season — potsherds, tool heads, whatever the terraces cough up downstream. My crew knows those waters better than any survey team. We want rights to surface recovery, honestly declared, fairly split."

"And why," Wren said mildly, "would anyone negotiate that with you? Last month you stripped our fuel and left a ransom note."

Voss grinned. It transformed her face from weathered to wolfish. "Because last month I was owed money and angry about it. This month I'm owed money and being *offered* it, which is a different animal entirely." She dropped onto the veranda rail, uninvited, comfortable. "Also because your boy here took the Gorge of Bells at flood stage without losing so much as a plank, and my crew talks about it the way fishermen talk about the big one that got away. I'd rather have him friendly than otherwise."

Dominic set down the compass. "Fair split means documented. Everything logged, everything reported to the archive authority, nothing sold dark."

"Obviously. I'm a professional."

"You sank a rival's barge in '09."

"That was also professional," Voss said, "and he had it coming, and it's not relevant."

Wren watched Dominic open his mouth and got there first. "Terms," she said, pulling out a chair and a notebook, because some negotiations you simply steered into like weather. "Surface recovery only, below the high-water mark. Forty percent to Voss's crew, sixty to the archive fund. Joint inventories monthly. Either party can audit the other with forty-eight hours' notice. And a clause—" she flipped to a fresh page, savoring it— "stipulating that any party caught dealing dark forfeits all rights and pays the other party's legal costs, plus a penalty equal to triple the value of the goods involved."

Voss read the clause upside down. Her grin widened. "Triple. You vindictive little archivist."

"I've been called worse by better-dressed people."

They negotiated for two hours. Marcus woke midway through, listened with his chin on his fist, and contributed exactly one sentence — "the seed-vault chambers are structurally fragile, keep your divers out of them" — which Voss wrote down without argument, because everyone at that table understood by now that when Marcus Fairweather spoke about the site, you wrote it down.

By dusk the agreement was signed in triplicate, witnessed by Ilsa and a bemused innkeeper, and Voss was shaking hands all around with the brisk sincerity of a woman who considered contracts a form of affection.

"Fairweather," she said last, to Dominic, gripping his hand. "You pilot like a man arguing with the river and winning. Come work for me someday."

"No."

"Offer stands." She swung up onto the rail, then paused, and did something Wren had never seen her do: she hesitated. "The old man," Voss said, jerking her chin at Marcus. "He paid my crew half-wages out of his own kit before he went inland. Didn't have to. Told me he'd settle the rest or die owing, and I believed him, which should tell you how far gone I was." She shrugged. "Consider the salute part of the settlement."

She snapped two fingers to her brow — a salvager's salute, quick and irreverent — spun on her heel, and strode down the dock road toward her boat, whistling something that was probably a work song and sounded more like a threat.

Dominic watched her go. "I think I liked her better when she was robbing us."

"You didn't like her then either."

"No," Dominic admitted. "But I respected the robbery."

* * *

That evening the whole strange fleet of them crowded the inn's common room — porters and freed crew, Ilsa and the watchman who had finally resurfaced with a sheepish story involving dice and a promise of employment, Marcus and Dominic orbiting each other at a distance that was, Wren noted, measurably smaller than it had been a week ago. The innkeeper brought out the good bottles. Someone produced a fiddle. The evening went loud.

Wren found herself at the quiet end of the table with a cup of wine she wasn't drinking, watching it all, doing what she did: keeping the record. Eleven years ago she had written things down that ruined a man. Tonight she was writing things down that repaired him — field notes, testimonies, the inventory of a flood archive that would outlast everyone in this room. Same skill. Different use. It occurred to her that this was, possibly, the entire point of a life: not to be blameless, but to be useful in the repair.

Marcus settled into the chair beside her. He moved carefully these days — cracked ribs, the doctor said, and a heart that had been squeezed by a river and come back complaining.

"You're writing," he observed.

"Always."

"About the evening?"

"About the site. About the terms. About the fact that Odile Voss hugged Ilsa goodbye and threatened to drown anyone who mentioned it." Wren capped her pen. "History is mostly bookkeeping, Marcus. The glory is just what the bookkeeping looks like from a distance."

He laughed — a real laugh, rusty at the hinges. Then he was quiet awhile, turning his cup, and she let him be quiet, because she had learned that about him too: the silences before his important sentences were long, and worth waiting out.

"Wren," he said at last. "There's something I want to ask you. Both of you. But I want to ask it properly, and tonight the room's too full, and—" he glanced across the table, where Dominic was letting Ilsa teach him a porter card game and losing with visible dignity— "and I'm not sure I'm brave enough yet. Give me a day."

"You have until the morning boat," Wren said. "After that I start charging consultation rates."

"Extortionate, I hope."

"Ruinous."

He smiled and raised his cup to her — to the testimony, though he didn't say it, and didn't need to. Across the room, the fiddle struck up something fast, and Ilsa crowed in triumph over a won hand, and Dominic Fairweather, who had spent ten years leaving before dawn so he'd never owe anyone anything, stayed at the table past midnight and lost three more games on purpose.

Wren wrote none of that down. Some records you keep in the body, where they hold their shape better.

The morning boat would come with the light. Whatever Marcus wanted to ask, he would ask it on the water, where all the important questions in this family had always been asked — and Wren, packing her documents one final time, found she was looking forward to the answer more than she had looked forward to anything in eleven years.
