## 39. Anchored

The boat, it turned out, was not dying. It was only tired.

Dominic discovered this on the third morning after they'd limped her downriver to the trading post at Kessel's Landing, when he knelt in the bilge with a lamp and a chisel and found that the sabotage at the dock had been crude — a single loosened strake, a seam opened like a bad argument, everything else sound beneath. The planking was good teak. The frames were honest oak. Somebody had built her to last, and she had lasted, and the only real sin she'd committed was being owned by a man who had never once stayed in one place long enough to thank her.

"You could sell her," Wren said from the deck above, her voice dropping through the open hatch along with a rain of sawdust. "There's a buyer at the Landing. Voss mentioned it. Some rubber trader who'd pay cash and never ask what the stains are."

"She'd sink inside a season under a rubber trader."

"So would half the boats on this river. That's never stopped anyone."

Dominic worked the chisel into the rotten caulking and said nothing, which was its own kind of answer. Wren, to her credit, let it stand. She had gotten good at that over the past months — at reading his silences the way she read inscriptions, patiently, letting the meaning surface on its own schedule.

He told himself the repairs were practical. The foundation would want the site documented before the next flood season, and the gorge was only reachable by water, and pilots were scarce, and — the list went on, a whole ledger of sensible reasons. But on the fourth day, when he caught himself sanding a strake that didn't need sanding, he had to admit the truth was simpler and stranger: for the first time in his adult life, he was fixing something instead of abandoning it.

He'd sold three boats in his career. He'd walked away from a share of a salvage company in Port Merrow because the owner had started calling him *partner* in a tone that sounded like a hook. He'd left a woman in Sarn's Reach with nothing worse than a note and a fair split of the takings, and he'd told himself for years that this was a code — the moving man's creed, nothing owed, nothing held, nothing that could be taken from you. It had taken a flooded gorge, a father's drowning gamble, and a tin cup with a dented rim to show him that the code had never been freedom. It had just been a smaller kind of cage, one he'd built himself and called a door.

The boat stayed.

He named the work, because work unnamed sprawls. New caulking along the port seam. A fresh plank where the salvagers' crowbar had bitten. The engine came apart on a tarp in the sun and went back together with two new bearings and an apology. He re-caulked the deck seams, oiled the wood until it drank, and repainted the name — she'd never had one he knew of, so he gave her one, lettering it himself in white on the stern with a steady hand and a certain disbelief: *Provision*.

Wren saw it and laughed for a full minute.

"That's sentimental," she said. "That's the most sentimental thing I've ever seen a salvage man do, and I once watched a man bury his dredge with full honors."

"It's a working name."

"It's a love letter to your father."

"It's a reminder that the provision was never the gold," Dominic said, and she stopped laughing, and looked at the name a while, and then went below without saying anything else, which from Wren was practically a blessing.

* * *

They argued about the preservation plan the way some couples argue about furniture — often, at length, and with a growing undercurrent of enjoyment neither would admit to.

The problem was this: the archive chambers survived. The flood defenses that had nearly killed them all had done their work and settled, and the upper galleries stood dry behind their ancient seals, holding ten centuries of seed stores, water charts, and the carved record of a people who had looked at the rising river and built for it instead of against it. Marcus wanted everything opened, catalogued, published. Wren wanted most of it left sealed.

"You're describing a tomb with better lighting," she said, pacing the deck with the site drawings rolled under her arm like a weapon. "Every chamber you unseal changes the humidity. Every visitor changes the air. We watched Renner's men nearly destroy in a week what took a civilization a thousand years to build. And your solution is a gift shop?"

"My father's solution is a gift shop. Mine is a field station, a rotation schedule, and controlled access through the causeway at low water only."

"Which floods."

"Which floods *predictably*." Dominic tapped the drawings. "That's the whole point of the place, Wren. They didn't fight the water. They scheduled around it. We can do the same. Open in the dry months, seal in the wet, let the site breathe the way it was designed to."

Wren stopped pacing. He watched her want to disagree, watched her run the numbers against her own convictions, watched her lose — gracefully, which was the only way she ever lost anything.

"That's," she said, and paused, and he braced himself, "*annoyingly* reasonable. Your father proposed something similar in 'nineteen, for the Karst survey. The foundation laughed him out of the room. Too slow, too expensive, not enough artifacts for the donors."

"And now I'm proposing it under my name, and the foundation can laugh at me instead."

"You'd let them?"

"I'd *make* them." He said it before he thought it through, and then, having said it, found he meant it. "It's my plan. It goes out under my name. Fairweather. The whole embarrassing inheritance of it."

Wren sat down on the rail beside him, the drawings across her knees, and was quiet for a moment in the way she was quiet when something mattered.

"You know he'd sign it as co-author if you let him."

"I know."

"And you're not going to let him."

Dominic looked out at the river. Evening was coming down gold on the water, and somewhere upriver the gorge bells were ringing their faint, wind-driven chime, a sound he had learned to hear as the site's own voice.

"He can be consultant," Dominic said. "He knows when to be quiet. He's been practicing."

Wren snorted. "He has not. He talked for two hours yesterday about hydraulic lime."

"He talked for two hours about hydraulic lime because you asked him a question about hydraulic lime."

"I asked a *short* question."

"You always do. It's your best trick." He took the drawings from her, rolled them, and tapped them against his palm. "We'll split the authorship. His name second. He'll pretend that's what he wanted all along."

"He'll cry at the letter."

"He will not cry at the letter."

"He absolutely will. I've known him thirty years, Dominic. The man cried at a stratigraphy diagram in 'eleven. It was a *beautiful* diagram, but still."

Dominic laughed — really laughed, the kind that surprised him on its way out — and Wren looked pleased with herself in the way of a person who has just won an argument she officially lost.

* * *

The letter to the foundation took him four days, which was three days longer than any letter had ever taken him, and he wrote it seven times.

The first six drafts were various flavors of wrong. Too curt — a single line, *Site located. Details to follow. D. Fairweather*, which was the old Dominic, the moving man, giving nothing and asking nothing. Too angry — two pages of everything the foundation had cost his father, the forged provenance, the silence, the way they'd wanted to *wait* while three check-ins went dark. Too eager — the groveling draft, which he burned in the cook stove with a certain satisfaction.

The seventh draft he wrote on the deck at dawn, with the river running quiet and Wren still asleep below, and it came out the way true things come out: plain.

*To the Trustees of the Fairweather Foundation:*

*This is Dominic Fairweather. You will have records of me, or you will have none; either way, I am my father's son, and I am writing under my own name because I intend to keep signing things with it.*

*The Velloran site is found. It is intact, it is extraordinary, and it will be documented properly or not at all — properly meaning slowly, meaning on the site's terms, meaning by people who remember that the river was here first. The preservation plan is enclosed. It carries my name as field director and Dr. Calloway's as archivist. My father's name appears second, as consultant, which is where he has earned the right to stand: near the work, with his hands off the seals.*

*You wanted to wait for his check-ins. I want you to understand that waiting is a choice, and choices have costs, and I intend to be the one making them from here forward.*

*Reply by post to Kessel's Landing. I'm moored there. I'm not hard to find anymore.*

*— D. Fairweather*

He read it twice, decided it was as good as it was going to get, and folded it into an envelope with the preservation plan — forty pages, three of them in Wren's handwriting, all of them argued over — and walked it up to the Landing post office himself, because some letters you do not trust to anyone else's hands.

The postmaster, a leathery woman named Ilsa-who-was-not-that-Ilsa (the coincidence had caused confusion for a full day before the younger Ilsa went downriver with the evacuation party), weighed the envelope, licked a stamp, and looked at him over her spectacles.

"Fairweather," she said. "Any relation to the one who came through here paying debts and mending roofs?"

"That would be my father."

"Mended my sister's roof. Never said who he was. Just said he was *staying put for a while*." She slid the letter into the canvas sack. "First time I've seen you in this office, Fairweather, and I've seen you pass through maybe a dozen times. Always heading somewhere."

"I'm moored," Dominic said. "Third berth. You can send things."

She looked at him the way river people look at a change in the current — with interest, and a little suspicion, and the beginning of a smile. "Well," she said. "I'll be damned."

* * *

He walked back down to the boat in the late light, and found Wren on the deck with her boots off and her feet on the rail, going through her preserved documents one last time — the originals of the forged provenance, the ones she'd kept for fifteen years as armor she'd never worn until the night she'd held them up to Renner's men in a flooding gallery.

"They're going to the authorities downriver next week," she said, not looking up. "After that, I don't know what I do with fifteen years of holding on."

"You could start a new collection."

"With what? I've given everything else to the site."

"You could keep the site," Dominic said, and sat down across from her, and the words came easier than he expected, the way they'd been coming easier all month, as if the current between them had finally found a direction they could both row with. "Archivist. It's in the letter. It's in your handwriting, even. Three pages of it."

"I saw." She finally looked up, and there was something in her face he'd learned to read as carefully as she read stone — a wariness that wasn't distrust anymore, just the habit of it, wearing thin. "You realize we'll be arguing about humidity control for the rest of our lives."

"I'm counting on it. You argue well."

"I argue *correctly*. You just refuse to lose."

"Someone has to pilot the boat."

"Someone has to be right on the boat."

They sat with that, and with the river, and with the evening coming down around the moored *Provision* like a hand settling on a shoulder. Down the dock, a fisherman was singing something tuneless and content. The gorge bells rang, far off, faint, steady.

Dominic realized, with the mild shock of a man noticing the tide has turned while he wasn't watching, that he was not making plans to leave.

He had no next job lined up. No port in mind, no departure set for dawn, no letter to avoid. The foundation's reply would come to Kessel's Landing, to him, by name. The site waited upriver with its bells and its dry chambers and its patient, water-wise dead. His father was two days away, learning — slowly, with Wren's coaching — the discipline of silence. And Dominic Fairweather, who had spent ten years proving that nothing could hold him, sat on the deck of the one thing he'd ever chosen to keep, and found that stillness did not feel like a cage.

It felt like an anchor that held because he'd set it himself.

"Wren," he said.

"Hm."

"When the reply comes from the foundation — and there will be a reply, there's always a reply — I'm going to want to read it out loud. On the deck. Probably at an unreasonable hour."

She smiled without opening her eyes. "I'll bring the coffee."

"And when the dry season starts—"

"We'll be ready."

"And when my father—"

"Will be fine. Go see him tomorrow. He's been re-caulking his own tent flaps for three days waiting for you to visit. He thinks you don't know he's bad at waiting."

"I *know* he's bad at waiting. I've had thirty years of field data."

"Then go put the poor man out of his misery." She opened one eye. "Unless, of course, you're planning to keep moving."

Dominic looked at the mooring line — new rope, his own knots, set fast to a piling that wasn't going anywhere — and found the old answer had gone out of him like water out of a drained channel.

"No," he said, and it was the easiest word he'd said in ten years. "I'm anchored."

And far upriver, so faint it might have been wind, the bells rang — as if the gorge itself had heard, and approved, and had already begun counting the days until the dry season brought them all home.

The morning post, when it came two days later, carried a foundation envelope with a trustee's seal and a single line of handwriting on the back that made Dominic laugh before he'd even opened it — because it was his father's hand, and it read: *About time.*
