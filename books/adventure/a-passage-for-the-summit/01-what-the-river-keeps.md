## 1. What the River Keeps

The river took Marrow Bend in the spring of the year Silas stopped counting, and it never gave the town back. It just kept it.

He worked the drowned streets at first light, when the water lay flat as poured tin and you could see the ghosts of things below — a fence line, a chimney cap, the arch of the old mill bridge with its stones furred green. His punt slid over the roof of the cooper's shop. He'd been eleven when the levee failed. He remembered standing on the high ground with his mother's hand crushing his shoulder, watching the Ansel fold the town under itself like a hand closing over a coin. The river didn't destroy Marrow Bend. It collected it.

Twenty years later, he collected it too. That was the joke nobody said out loud.

Silas Hartwell, dredger. Salvager of other people's drowned lives. He ran his drag-net along the silted lane where Harker Street used to bend past the churchyard, and the net came up heavy with the usual liturgy: a chair leg, a kettle gone to lace, a doll's head with its paint bleached to a blind white stare. He shook them out onto the boards and let the doll's head roll to the gunwale and stop there, facing him.

"Not today," he told it, and pitched it back.

The salvage law was simple and everyone broke it. Anything pulled from the floodlands belonged to the Crown surveyor's office unless reported within three days, and anything reported was taxed until it wasn't worth reporting. So nobody reported. The whole economy of the Bend — what was left of it, strung along the levee road in tar-paper shacks and houseboats — ran on what the river kept quiet about. Silas was good at being quiet. He'd had practice. Twenty years of being the son of Elias Hartwell, the man who lied to the Royal Geographic Society, the man who took an expedition upriver to find the source of the Ansel and came back with nothing but a story no one believed and a name that opened no doors except the ones you wanted shut.

*The source of the River Ansel.* People still said it sometimes in the taverns, the way you'd say a curse word in another language — testing the shape of it without meaning anything. There was no source. That was the finding. The Ansel rose from nowhere, from mist, from a country that did not appear on any map worth trusting, and Elias Hartwell had claimed otherwise, had claimed he'd *seen* something, and when he couldn't produce it — couldn't produce even a photograph that survived the journey — the Society had stripped his fellowship and the newspapers had stripped everything else. *The Cartographer of Nothing*, one headline called him. Silas had been eleven. His father had been gone again within the year, back upriver on his own coin this time, with four men and a promise to return by the thaw.

The thaw had come. And come. And come.

Silas hauled the net again and tried not to think in that direction. Thinking in that direction was like poking a bruise to check if it still hurt. It always still hurt.

"Oi. Hartwell."

He knew the voice before he turned. Dree Marsh, who bought for the factor's warehouse down at the landing, a wide woman in a canvas apron with a ledger chained to her belt like a weapon. She poled her skiff alongside his punt and looked at his catch with the enthusiasm of a customs officer.

"Kettle," she said. "Chair leg. You're feeding me garbage this morning, Silas."

"You're welcome to dive for better."

"I might." She squinted downstream, toward the cut where the old grain warehouse stood flooded to its second floor. "Surveyor's men were through last night. Asking after lead."

Silas kept his face doing nothing. "Lead?"

"Lead-lined anything. Chests, coffers, casks. Somebody's been pulling lead out of the floodlands and not declaring it, and the office wants to know who." She looked at him a beat too long. "You know how they get. First they ask nice. Then they ask with a fine attached. Then they ask with a boat and two men who don't smile."

"I don't have lead," Silas said. "I barely have a kettle."

Dree snorted and pushed off. But the words stayed with him the way cold water stays in your boots, because there *was* lead in the floodlands, and Silas knew exactly where, because he'd found it nine days ago and hadn't told a living soul.

* * *

It had snagged his net off the sunken chapel road, out where the water went twelve feet deep over what used to be the tollhouse. He'd felt the weight before he saw it — a dead pull, a stubbornness, like the river had grown a fist. He'd rowed over the spot and looked down into the green murk and made out a shape too regular to be wreck. Corners. A lid. Iron bands gone to orange bloom.

It had taken him three days and a borrowed block-and-tackle to bring it up, working alone at dusk so no one would see, and when it finally broke the surface, streaming, dripping, sitting on his punt boards like a thing that had been waiting, he understood why the river had held it so long.

A chest. Lead-sheathed under rotted oak planking — he could see the dull grey metal where the wood had sloughed away — and stamped on the lid, deep enough to survive twenty years underwater, a seal he had never seen and could not place: a mountain above a circle of water, ringed in lettering worn nearly smooth. Not a merchant's mark. Not a guild stamp. Not the Crown. He'd dredged a thousand objects out of that river and he knew every stamp this valley had ever pressed into wood or metal, and this one belonged to none of them.

And underneath the seal, scratched shallow, almost hidden — someone had added letters by hand, after the fact, with a knife or a nail:

*A.P.*

His father's initials. Elias Paul Hartwell.

Silas had sat in his punt in the dark with his heart going like a fish in a net, and he had not opened it. He'd rowed it home instead, wrapped in his tarp, and sunk it in the flooded cellar of his own shack where the water was black and cold and nobody dredged, weighted under a slab of levee stone. Nine days ago. Every night since, he'd lain awake listening to the river move against the pilings and told himself he'd open it tomorrow, and every tomorrow he found a reason — the light was wrong, the neighbors were awake, the surveyor's men were out — until the reasons wore thin as the excuses they were.

Today, floating over the ghost of Harker Street with a net full of garbage and Dree Marsh's warning still ringing, Silas ran out of tomorrows.

* * *

His shack sat at the end of the levee road where the road gave up and became water, built on pilings from a bridge that no longer existed, which struck Silas as the most honest architecture in the valley. Inside: a stove, a cot, a table scarred with the rings of a hundred mugs, and a floor hatch he'd disguised under a fish-drying rack. He waited until full dark. He ate nothing. He listened to the shacks around him settle into their noises — Osk Venni coughing next door, the Kessler kids fighting over something, the general rustle of a community of people all pretending not to watch each other.

Then he hauled up the chest.

Water sheeted off it onto his floorboards. In the lamplight the seal looked worse than he remembered — or better, depending on how you counted, because now he could see the lettering around the ring had been deliberately defaced, chiseled flat by some careful hand. Someone had wanted this crest unreadable. Someone else had wanted it findable. Both someones had left their work half-done, and the chest sat between intentions like a argument frozen mid-word.

The lock was iron and ancient, but the hasp had been forced once already — long ago, by the look of the bright scratches under the corrosion. Silas worked his pry-bar into the gap, breathed, and leaned.

The lid came up with a groan and a breath of air that smelled of lead and river-rot and, underneath both, faintly, impossibly, of lamp oil and paper.

Inside, packed in wool gone to grey felt: a lacquered case, cracked; a bundle of oilcloth tied with wire; and a book.

Not just a book. A ledger — expedition-grade, the kind the Society issued, bound in oiled canvas with brass corners, and Silas knew that binding the way a priest knows his own scripture. He'd grown up with these stacked on every surface of a house that no longer existed. His hands were shaking when he lifted it out.

The cover had been torn. Not damaged — *torn*, deliberately, vertically, the front board split clean down the middle and half of it missing entirely, along with whatever pages had been sewn into that half. What remained was a fragment. Half a ledger. Half a record.

He untied the oilcloth bundle with fingers that wouldn't behave. Inside: a sextant missing its mirror, a tin of matches fused solid, and a folding knife with a broken tip that Silas recognized so hard his vision blurred, because he'd watched his father whittle with that knife at a hundred campfires, because it had a notch in the handle shaped like a crescent moon that Elias Hartwell had cut himself the day Silas was born.

The lacquered case held nothing but a paper tag in his father's handwriting, water-blurred but legible: *For the child, if it comes to that.*

Silas set it down carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

Then he opened the ledger to the first surviving page and read his father's hand marching across it, cramped and certain, the hand of a man writing fast in bad conditions:

*Day 41. Above the gorge the Ansel does not widen. This is the thing I cannot make anyone believe. Every river widens as it runs, and this one narrows, as if it is being gathered, as if somewhere ahead it is being held. Marta says we are fools to go on. She may be right. The compass—*

The page ended mid-sentence. The next leaf had been torn away with the missing half of the binding.

Silas turned pages with the care of a man handling ash. Survey notes. Soundings. Sketches of a gorge he didn't recognize, drawn with the loving precision his father had been famous for — the precision that had made the disgrace worse, because a liar with a beautiful hand is more damning than a sloppy one. On one page, a drawing of an instrument: a compass unlike any Silas had seen, its needle housing built into a socketed disc, annotated in the margin: *the key turns only at the door. E.H.*

And then, near the end of the surviving fragment, a page that stopped Silas's breath.

A sketch of the compass itself, full-size, exact — and beneath it, in fresher ink, a single line:

*If you are reading this, you are holding my half. Find the other half or find me. Either will do. — E.H.*

Silas sat back. The lamp hissed. Outside, the river moved against the pilings with its endless patient sound, the sound of a thing that keeps what it's given.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing he'd been circling for nine days without letting himself land on it.

Silas owned a compass. One compass. His father's — left behind on the mantel of a house that washed away, fished out of the mud afterward by an eleven-year-old boy who'd waded into the ruins against his mother's screaming because it was the only thing of his father's worth drowning for. A brass compass with a needle housing built into a socketed disc. He'd carried it twenty years. He'd assumed it was a surveyor's tool, a curiosity, a keepsake. He'd never once wondered why it was built so strangely, why the disc had a square socket at its center like it was meant to seat over something, why his father had never explained it.

He got up on legs that felt borrowed, pried up the third floorboard by the stove, and took out the oilskin wrap.

The compass sat in his palm, warm from the hiding place, cold from the years. He set it down on the sketch in the ledger.

Line for line. Rivet for rivet. Identical.

The needle swung once, twice, and settled — pointing, as it always pointed, north-northwest, upriver, toward the gorge, toward the mist, toward the country that wasn't on any map. It had always pointed that way. Silas had always assumed the needle was simply wrong, magnetized crooked, a flaw in the instrument. Standing in his shack at midnight with his father's torn ledger open on the table, Silas Hartwell considered, for the first time in twenty years, the possibility that the needle had never been wrong at all — that it had been pointing at something the whole time, patiently, the way the river holds what it's given, waiting for someone to follow.

A knock hit his door. Three raps, unhurried, official.

Silas closed the ledger with one hand and put the lamp out with the other, and in the dark, with his heart slamming, he heard the voice through the planks — a stranger's voice, smooth as wet stone:

"Mister Hartwell? We'd like a word about river iron."
