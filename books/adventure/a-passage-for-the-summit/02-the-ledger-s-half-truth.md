## 2. The Ledger's Half-Truth

The chest sat in the bottom of Silas's skiff all night, under a tarp, under a coil of rope, under the flat gray stare of a sky that couldn't decide whether to rain.

He didn't sleep. He lay in his room above the chandlery with the lamp unlit and listened to the river working at the pilings, and every hour or so he got up and looked out the window at the boat tied below, as if the thing inside might climb out on its own. It wouldn't. That was the problem with it. It just sat there being patient, the way heavy things are patient.

By first light he'd made his decision twice and unmade it three times. Report it. Salvage law was simple enough: anything of value pulled from the flood came to the magistrate's office, got appraised, got claimed or auctioned, and the finder took a finder's cut — ten percent, minus fees, minus the creditors who had standing claims against Hartwell property, which was to say against Silas, which was to say all of it. Ten percent of nothing was nothing. But reporting it was clean, and clean was how he'd survived twenty years in Marrow Bend without anyone spitting when he passed.

He reported nothing.

He told himself it was because the seal bothered him. A crest stamped into lead — not engraved, *stamped*, pressed deep while the metal was soft — showing a tower rising out of water, crowned with a star. He'd dredged half the drowned outskirts for six years and pulled up chamber pots, stove doors, wedding rings, one perfectly good church bell. He knew what salvage looked like. This didn't look like salvage. Salvage drifted down from the flooded farms and the old mill district. This had come up from the channel itself, from the deep cut where the Ansel ran cold and fast even in flood season, thirty feet down where nothing settled except what the river had carried a very long way.

Somebody had put that chest in the water on purpose. And somebody had weighted it to stay there.

He ate nothing. He rowed out past the breakwater while the mist still stood on the water in columns, found his spot above the deep channel, and opened it there, alone, with the town still asleep behind him.

The lead lid fought him. The hinges were crusted with river glass — that smooth mineral skin that forms on submerged iron given enough years — and when he finally broke them loose and lifted the cover, the smell that came out was dry and mineral and old, the smell of a room that had been closed since before the flood. Whatever was inside had been sealed away from the water the whole time. Somebody had built this box to keep things safe *from* rivers.

Inside, on a bed of tarred canvas: paper.

A book. Half a book, actually — the ledger was torn vertically down the spine, raggedly, as if someone had ripped it apart in haste or fury, and what lay in the chest was the left half, pages sheared so that lines of writing ended mid-word at every margin. The binding was oiled leather gone stiff as board, and stamped into the front cover, worn nearly smooth by handling, was an etching.

Silas stopped breathing.

A compass rose. Not the decorative kind you saw on charts and tavern signs — this was specific, drawn with a draftsman's precision, a sixteen-point star with a needle rendered as a slender diamond, and around its rim a ring of small characters in no alphabet he knew, looping and angular at once, like handwriting pretending to be ornament.

He knew it because he'd been staring at it his whole life.

His hand went to his shirt before he decided to move it, and drew out the keepsake on its cord: a brass compass, small as a pocket watch, tarnished green at the seams, that had been in his jacket or under his pillow or knotted around his neck for twenty years — since the night neighbors brought boxes to the house and his mother burned papers in the yard and told him, the only time she ever said it plainly, *take this and don't let anyone see it.* The same compass rose on its case back. The same sixteen points. The same ring of impossible letters. He'd assumed it was decoration, some maker's flourish. It wasn't a flourish. It was a signature. The compass and the ledger were made by the same hand, or for the same purpose, and both of those hands had belonged, once, to Elias Hartwell.

His father.

Silas sat in the rocking skiff with the mist burning off around him and read.

The handwriting hit him first, harder than the content. He'd seen it exactly once — a single letter his mother had let him hold for a minute when he was nine, before she fed it to the fire with everything else. A slanted, impatient hand, letters leaning forward like a man walking fast. The ledger leaned the same way. Twenty years dead and the man was still walking fast, still pulling Silas after him.

The pages were expedition notes. Soundings. Supply tallies. Names — most of them torn away with the missing half, but fragments surviving: *—rran* (his mind snagged on that, filed it, moved on), *—ckworth, the surveyor*, *Voss—* (a common enough name; he'd think about that later, much later). And running through everything, in the margins, in between entries, always circling back:

*Day 41. The Ansel does not behave. Sounding at the narrows gives 90 ft; the water is moving uphill if my instruments are to be believed, which they are not.*

*Day 58. The boy is wrong about the current. Or right. Need to stop thinking of it as a current.*

*Day 63. Found the marker stone. The script matches the compass. We are being watched by people who do not wish to be found. Good.*

That last word stopped him cold. *Good.* Underlined twice.

And then, near the end of the surviving half, the ink changing — darker, pressed harder, written fast:

*They will call it a source because they need it to be one thing on one map. It is not one thing. The river is born the way a family is born — underground, out of sight, out of many rooms. If you are reading this and the name Hartwell means disgrace to you, then you have the wrong half. Find the other half. The source of the River Ansel is not where the maps say. It is where the maps were made to NOT say.*

Below that, a final line, smaller, almost hidden in the gutter of the tear:

*—and if the compass points wrong, you are holding it wrong.*

Silas read that line four times. Then he took the brass compass off his neck, held it flat in his palm, and watched the needle swing lazily, settle, tremble, and point — steadily, insistently, unmistakably — upstream.

Not north. Upstream. Into the gorge country, toward the headwaters, toward the blank space on every chart he'd ever salvaged where cartographers wrote *unreliable surveys* in apologetic italics.

He'd owned the thing twenty years and never once noticed, because he'd never once held it still long enough, facing any direction long enough, to catch it lying. Or not lying. He thought of his father's line again — *if the compass points wrong, you are holding it wrong* — and turned it over. On the case back, the compass rose. He tilted it toward the light and saw, for the first time, faint scratches around the rim of the etching, so fine they might have been wear. They weren't wear. They were numbers, or coordinates, or a sentence in that looping alphabet, worn almost to invisibility by a thumb rubbing over them — his father's thumb, maybe, or his mother's, or twenty years of his own.

The river slapped the hull. Somewhere upstream a heron screamed. Silas put the compass back around his neck, tucked the ledger inside his coat, and sat very still for a long time, doing arithmetic he didn't want to do.

His father hadn't drowned drunk off a ferry, the way Marrow Bend told it. Hadn't died fleeing debts, the way the creditors told it. He'd gone upriver with an expedition, found something worth tearing a ledger in half over, and come back — or meant to come back — with proof. And then the house had burned with papers, and the man had stayed gone, and the town had needed a story, and *disgraced* was the story that fit the shape left behind. Silas had spent his whole life living inside that shape. Fitting himself into it. Keeping his head down, keeping his nets down, keeping his name quiet.

And now the river had handed him the other end of it.

* * *

He should have gone straight to Odile Ferran. That was the sensible move, and he knew it even then, and he didn't take it, because sensible required admitting out loud to another human being what he'd found, and he wasn't ready. Instead he did what he always did: he worked. He ran his salvage route through the drowned orchards, hauled up a wagon axle and a crate of preserve jars, sold the jars, kept his eyes down, and told himself the ledger was safe under his floorboards and nobody knew and the world would go on being small and survivable.

It lasted two days.

On the second evening he was drinking one beer slowly at the Drowned Ox, making it last, minding his own business with the professional diligence of twenty years' practice, when the stranger came in.

He noticed the boots first. Everyone in Marrow Bend wore river boots — cracked, patched, smelling of silt. These were city boots, oiled leather, clean, wrong for the mud on the floorboards, and their owner walked across that mud like it owed him an apology. He was broad, gray at the temples, with the easy posture of a man who'd never had to hurry anywhere, and he bought a round for the room without being asked, which in Marrow Bend was either charity or advertising, and nobody here believed in charity.

"Friends," the stranger said, in a voice pitched to carry, "I'll keep it short. I'm a buyer. Antiquities, curiosities, river finds." He let that sit. "Particularly interested in river iron. Old pieces. Sealed containers, marked metal, anything with unusual stamping. I pay well, I ask few questions, and I'm staying at the Anchor House through the week."

Silas kept his face on his beer. Around the room, heads nodded the way heads nod at free drink. Someone asked what he'd pay for a cannonball. The stranger laughed the right amount.

But Silas was watching his eyes, and the stranger's eyes weren't laughing. They were working the room, booth by booth, face by face, the way a fisherman works a line of traps — and when they passed over Silas, they stopped. Just for a beat. Just long enough.

Because Silas had done the one thing he'd trained himself never to do. When the man said *marked metal,* Silas had flinched. Not visibly. Not enough for the room. Enough for a buyer who reads rooms.

The stranger drifted over eventually, casual as weather, and set himself against the rail beside Silas without looking at him. "You're the Hartwell boy."

"I'm a salvager."

"Hartwell." He said the name like he was tasting it. "Elias Hartwell's son. Your father did survey work upriver, didn't he? Before the trouble." A pause, delicately measured. "I collect the history of expeditions. Failed ones especially. They leave such interesting litter." He turned, finally, and smiled, and it was a good smile, warm as a lamp. "If your father's things ever surface — and things do surface, in a flood country, things always surface — I'd make it worth your while. Substantially." He set a card on the rail between them. Heavy stock. Embossed. A crest of some syndicate office Silas didn't recognize, though the weight of the paper said money the way the stranger's boots said money. "Bram Voss," the man said, though Silas hadn't asked. "For the syndicate. Remember the name. Men who remember names tend to profit."

He pushed off the rail and left. The door swung. The room went back to its noise.

Silas sat there with his heart going like a bilge pump and understood, with total clarity, two things.

First: Voss wasn't fishing. Buyers cast wide nets, but buyers didn't say *sealed containers, marked metal* unless they already knew what shape the thing was. Someone knew a chest existed. Someone might even know it had surfaced — the river gave up secrets to more than one net, and Marrow Bend had more salvagers than honesty.

Second: the name on the card had been in the ledger. *Voss—*. Torn mid-word, but the shape of it, the way his father's pen had dug in — that hadn't been a supply entry. You didn't press that hard over cargo weights.

He walked home the long way, along the floodwall, checking the street behind him twice and hating himself for it. At the chandlery he went up the outside stairs instead of through the shop, locked his door, pried up the floorboard, and took out the ledger and read it again end to end, and it was worse the second time, because now he read it as a man being hunted rather than a son being haunted. *We are being watched by people who do not wish to be found. Good.* His father had known. Had welcomed it, even. Had torn the ledger in half and split the truth so no one person could carry the whole map — and had sent one half downriver in a lead coffin, trusting the river to keep it better than any bank.

Trusting it to find whoever the compass was made for.

Silas sat on the floor with his back against the bed and the ledger open on his knees and felt something shift in his chest that he didn't have a name for yet. It wasn't fear, though fear was in it. It was the feeling of a shape he'd lived inside for twenty years cracking along a seam. He could burn the ledger. That was the safe move. Burn it, melt the compass down for brass, keep being invisible, keep being nobody's son, die in Marrow Bend of nothing in particular.

Or.

*Find the other half.*

His father's handwriting, leaning forward like a man walking fast. Twenty years dead and still pulling him upstream.

He was still sitting there when the knock came.

Three slow knocks, deliberate, unhurried. Silas went cold from the scalp down. Through the window he could see the street, and on the street, across the way, under the flicker of the corner lamp, stood a man in clean city boots, arms folded, watching the chandlery with the patience of heavy things.

The knock came again. Three slow knocks.

Silas looked at the ledger. Looked at the compass in his palm, its needle trembling, pointing — steady, insistent, unmistakable — upstream.

He blew out the lamp, and began packing.
