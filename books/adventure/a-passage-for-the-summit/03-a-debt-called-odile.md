## 3. A Debt Called Odile

Odile Ferran knew a man was trouble the moment he stepped onto her deck, because trouble always arrived the same way: too polite, too quiet, standing just past the gangplank like it was waiting to be invited.

This one was young. River-lean, with the forearms of a dredger and the eyes of somebody who'd spent twenty years watching doors. Mud to the knees. A canvas bag slung across his chest the way men carry things they'd rather die than put down.

"You're Ferran," he said.

"Depends who's asking."

"Silas Hartwell."

The name landed on her deck like a crate of wet iron. She kept her hands busy with the mooring line, because her hands always wanted to do something stupid when her chest did something worse. "Hartwell," she repeated. "That a common name on this river?"

"You knew my father."

"I knew a lot of fathers." She tied off the line. "Most of them owed me money."

"Elias Hartwell. Twenty years ago. The Marrow Bend survey."

She looked at him then, properly, and hated what she found — the jaw was wrong, the coloring was wrong, but the way he stood with his weight slightly back, like he expected the world to swing at him, that was Elias down to the bone. Twenty years of river water hadn't been enough to drown that man out of the world. Apparently he'd left a copy behind.

"I don't know what you heard," she said, "but whatever my father told you—"

"Nobody told me anything. My father's dead. Or gone. Same thing." The boy's voice stayed flat, but she heard the seam under it. "I need passage upstream. Past the drowned steeple, past the gorge. As far as the boat can go."

Odile laughed, and it came out uglier than she intended. "Past the gorge. On what, exactly? Good looks?"

"Whatever you'll take. I have some salvage money. Not much."

"Not much doesn't go upriver, Hartwell. Not much doesn't even go to the reservoir. The gorge takes fuel, it takes permits, it takes bribes, and past the toll bridge it takes blood." She wiped her hands on her trousers and finally gave him the full weight of her attention. "Why upstream? Nobody goes upstream. Upstream is where money goes to die."

He hesitated. It was a small hesitation, a hairline crack, but she'd spent thirty years reading men on water and hairline cracks were how hulls sank.

"That's a long silence for a simple question," she said.

"It's not a simple question."

"No. It isn't. Which is why I'm going to say no and you're going to get off my boat."

He didn't move. Of course he didn't. "They say you owed him."

"I don't owe dead men."

"Then I'll pay you. Whatever the going rate is for a fool and his bag."

"The going rate is more than you have and more than you're worth, no offense."

"None taken." He shifted the bag on his chest, and for half a second his hand covered it protectively, and Odile's whole career — the smuggling, the running, the fifteen years of reading cargo manifests and the men who lied about them — narrowed to that half-second. People guarded two kinds of things that way. Things worth killing for, and things that could get you killed. On this river, lately, the two categories had started to overlap.

"Open it," she said.

"No."

"Then no passage."

"It's not mine to show."

"Whose is it?"

His jaw worked. "My father's."

Something cold walked up Odile's spine and sat down at the base of her skull. She kept her face bored. "Elias Hartwell's been dead or disappeared for twenty years. Whatever he left you, it's either worthless or it's a noose. Either way, I don't want it on my deck."

"Then I'll find another boat."

"You won't. I'm the last one running this stretch who isn't syndicate, and the syndicate boats won't take you past the first checkpoint without papers you don't have."

"Then you're my only option. That's not a threat. It's just arithmetic."

Odile looked at him for a long moment. The river slapped against the hull. Somewhere down the dock a gull screamed over fish guts, and the morning fog was burning off the water in long gray rags, and this boy — this ghost's ghost — stood on her gangplank with his dead man's bag and his dead man's stance, and she thought: *Elias, you absolute bastard, even twenty years gone you're still handing me bills.*

"Come back at dusk," she said. "If I say yes, you'll know by dark."

* * *

She said yes at dusk, and she hated herself for it, and she knew exactly why, and that was the worst part.

It wasn't the debt. Or it wasn't only the debt. Debts were currency, and Odile had spent twenty years learning that the only debts worth honoring were the ones you could still feel. She could feel this one. It lived in her left shoulder, an old ache that flared in cold weather and in memory: the night the syndicate's patrol boat had run her down in the narrows, hull cracked, taking water, seventeen years old and certain she was going to die in the dark with her cargo of stolen medicine going soft in the flood. And a survey boat that had no business being out there, no business at all, had put its searchlight on her and a rope in the water, and a man with ink-stained fingers and a voice like a patient schoolteacher had hauled her aboard and said, "Breathe first. Explain after."

She'd never explained. She'd never repaid. She'd told herself for two decades that a life saved was a debt discharged the moment it was lived, and she'd almost believed it, right up until a young man with Elias's shoulders stood on her gangplank and said *they say you owed him.*

But the debt alone wouldn't have been enough. Odile was many kinds of rotten, but she wasn't sentimental enough to run a suicide run for a feeling in her shoulder. What tipped her was the thing she hadn't told the boy.

Three nights ago, a man in a good coat had come down to the Marrow Bend docks asking questions. Not syndicate-uniform questions — no badges, no manifest checks. Worse. Civilian questions, soft-spoken, from a man with a ledger-keeper's hands and a collector's eyes, asking about river iron, old seals, anything stamped or engraved from before the Resettlement. Offering to buy. Offering, quietly, to pay for information too.

And this morning, Odile's fuel supplier — a woman who'd sold to her without questions for eleven years — had taken her aside and said, voice low, "There's a price posted, Ferran. Not official. Word-of-mouth. Anyone running Vessian artifacts upriver, there's a finder's fee for reporting them, and a bigger one for what they're carrying. And a bounty on the runners themselves, if they don't stop."

Vessian. The word from a country that wasn't on any map, from a nation that had been erased so thoroughly that half the people who used the word thought it was a curse. Odile had grown up on stories of Vessia the way river kids grew up on stories of the thing under the dock — half warning, half wish. And now somebody with soft hands was paying real money for its ghosts.

Which meant the bag on Silas Hartwell's chest wasn't salvage. It was a lit fuse, and the boy was carrying it straight up the river toward the powder store.

She should have said no. She'd said no to better men for worse reasons.

But here was the arithmetic the boy didn't know he'd offered her: if the syndicate was paying bounties on Vessian cargo, then they'd pay bounties on the *runners*, and a runner who was already marked had nothing left to lose — which made him, in Odile's professional experience, the most valuable kind of crew. A man with nothing to lose runs a river like he's already dead. And if the syndicate was hunting Vessia's ghosts this hard, this far downstream, then whatever Elias Hartwell had found at the source of the Ansel twenty years ago was worth more than Odile's boat, her debts, and her life stacked together.

She could turn the boy in. The finder's fee would clear her fuel debt, recaulk the hull, buy a season of honest — well, honest-adjacent — work.

She thought about that for exactly as long as it took to pour a drink.

Then she thought about a rope in the water and ink-stained fingers and *breathe first, explain after*, and she poured the drink back in the bottle.

"Damn you, Elias," she said to the empty cabin. "Damn you twice."

* * *

She found him where she'd told him to wait, on the sea wall by the coal yard, sitting with his bag and watching the water like it owed him an answer.

"Terms," she said, without preamble. "You get a bunk, a place at the oars when I say, and silence. You don't touch my charts, you don't touch my wheel, and you don't lie to me about what's in the bag. I won't ask what it is. I'll ask if it's dangerous, and you'll answer true, and if you lie I'll put you off at the first lock, in the rain, with your bag and your secrets and my sincere good wishes. Understood?"

"Understood."

"It's dangerous."

He didn't hesitate this time. "Yes."

"Good. That's the first honest thing you've said." She sat down on the wall beside him, an arm's length away, close enough to talk, far enough to move. "One more term. You tell me why. Not what — I don't care what, yet. Why. Why is a dredger with no money trying to reach the source of the Ansel, which no boat has reached in twenty years, carrying dangerous cargo that isn't his?"

The fog was coming back in off the water, and in it his face went gray and young. "My father disappeared up there," he said. "Twenty years ago. Everyone says he ran. That he stole from the expedition, that he took the findings and vanished. My mother died believing it. I grew up believing it." His hand found the bag strap. "Three days ago I pulled a chest out of the floodwater with his handwriting inside it. And it doesn't read like a thief's ledger. It reads like a man who was trying to make sure something survived him."

"And you need to know which one he was."

"I need to know where he went. The rest is — " He stopped. Started again. "The rest I'll figure out when I get there."

Odile watched the water. A barge went by downstream, running lights doubled in the black river, heading toward the ordinary world with ordinary cargo, and she felt the old pull of it, the downstream pull, the pull toward easy.

"Here's what you don't know, Hartwell," she said. "There's a price on that bag. Not on you — on what you're carrying. The syndicate's posted word-of-mouth bounties all along the lower stretch. Every dock from here to the reservoir, somebody's looking for Vessian iron, and they're paying in amounts that would make a saint inform." She turned to look at him. "I could clear my debts twice over by selling you tonight. I want you to understand that I know that, and you know that, and we're both going to be thinking about it for the next three hundred miles."

He met her eyes. Elias's eyes weren't, actually — his were darker, harder, a river with more silt in it — but the steadiness was the same. "Why don't you, then?"

"Because twenty years ago your father pulled a drowning girl out of the narrows when every other boat on the water had already decided she was cargo lost. And because I've spent twenty years telling myself that settled the account." She stood, and the old ache flared in her shoulder like it knew it was being talked about. "It didn't. Debts to dead men don't settle. They just wait."

"So that's a yes."

"That's a yes, and don't make it pretty. We cast off at first light. You'll work for your bunk — I don't carry passengers, I carry crew. And Hartwell." She waited until he looked up. "If the syndicate catches us, you give them the bag. You hear me? You give them the bag and you tell them you stole it from a dead man and you never heard my name. The bag is the bounty. The bag is the whole bounty. Nobody dies for a chest."

He stood too, and for a moment she thought he'd argue — he had the look of a man assembling an argument, jaw tight, breath drawn — but then something in him let go, some knot she recognized from her own mirror, and he nodded once.

"First light," he said.

"First light." She turned to go, then stopped, because there was one more thing, and it had been sitting in her chest since the gangplank like water in the bilge. "The night he pulled me out," she said, not turning around, "he asked me one question. Just one. He said — *what's your name, and who's waiting for you?* And I said nobody. And he said, *then from tonight, the river is.*" The fog swallowed her voice a little, and she let it. "I thought it was a line. Something river men say to girls they fish out. Took me fifteen years to figure out he meant it as a job description."

She heard him shift behind her. "What job?"

"Looking after people who've got nobody," Odile said. "It's a bad trade. Terrible margins." She started down the dock. "Be on time."

* * *

She didn't sleep. She never slept the night before a run, but this night the insomnia had a different flavor — less fear, more arithmetic. She sat in her cabin with her charts spread out and her fuel ledger open and she did the numbers three times, and all three times they came back the same: the run was impossible. Fuel for the gorge was short. The toll bridge had doubled its fees since spring. The reservoir checkpoints were crawling with new men she didn't recognize, men with soft hands and good coats.

And all three times, under the numbers, the same other sum kept surfacing, the one she couldn't chart: a rope in black water. A voice saying *breathe first.* A boy on a sea wall with a dead man's bag and nobody waiting for him anywhere in the world.

"Damn it," she said again, to no one, and it was becoming a prayer.

Near dawn she heard the gangplank creak, early, the way a good crew shows up, and she came out on deck with her charts rolled under her arm and her heart doing something she refused to name.

The boy was there. And behind him, at the foot of the gangplank, half in shadow, was a second figure — small, quick, a kid really, fifteen maybe, with a picklock's fingers and a runaway's eyes, holding a bindle like the world might take it.

"Captain Ferran," Silas Hartwell said, and there was something in his voice she hadn't heard before — not quite apology, not quite hope. "This is Tuck. He's coming with us."

Odile looked at the kid. The kid looked at her, chin up, the way strays do when they've decided not to flinch.

She looked up the river, where the fog was lifting off the water and the first gray light was finding the drowned steeple's tip, miles off, a finger of stone pointing at nothing.

"Nobody," she said, mostly to herself, "said anything about two."

Then she threw them the mooring line, because the tide was turning, and Odile Ferran had never once in her life let a tide wait.
