## 4. The Collector's Men

Silas woke to the sound of Odile's boot heel cracking against the hull.

"Up. We've got company coming up the towpath, and I'd rather not be aboard when it arrives."

He rolled out of his bunk in the *Maggot's Blessing*—Odile's boat, a flat-bottomed barge that smelled of diesel and dried fish—and squinted through the porthole at the gray morning. Marrow Bend sat three miles downstream, its drowned church spire just visible above the waterline where the reservoir had swallowed the old quarter. They'd anchored in the reeds overnight, close enough to town for supplies, far enough to be nobody's business.

"Company," Silas said. "What kind?"

"The kind that wears good boots in a place where good boots get stolen." Odile was already at the galley stove, cracking eggs into a pan with the efficiency of a woman who'd learned to eat fast and leave faster. "Man came through the fish market at dawn asking questions. Paid in new notes. Nobody pays in new notes here, Silas. New notes are for people who want to be remembered."

Silas's stomach tightened. He thought of the chest stashed under his bunk, wrapped in oilcloth, its lead lining cold as a corpse even through the wrapping. He thought of the ledger inside, his father's handwriting marching across the torn pages like ants that refused to die.

*The source of the River Ansel.*

"Describe him."

"Big. Gray coat. Hands like a man who's never done honest work but has watched a lot of it done." She flipped the eggs. "He had a list. I heard him reading names off it at the fish stalls. Every salvager who works the Bend."

Silas dressed quickly. "My name on it?"

"Wouldn't know. I left before he got to the F's." She glanced at him sideways. "But I saw him show a drawing around. Charcoal on paper. A chest."

The eggs hissed. Neither of them looked at the bunk.

"We should run," Silas said.

"We should eat first. You can't outrun a syndicate on an empty stomach." Odile slid the eggs onto two tin plates and shoved one across the table at him. "Besides, running tells them what they want to know. Right now we're just two more river rats. The moment we bolt, we're the two river rats with something worth taking."

Silas ate mechanically, tasting nothing. Twenty years he'd spent perfecting the art of being unremarkable—the dredger with the dead father and the dead name, the man creditors ignored because there was nothing to take. He'd built his invisibility plank by plank, and in one night a chest of lead and secrets had torn it all open.

"I need to go into town anyway," he said.

Odile's eyebrows went up. "That's the stupidest thing you've said since I met you, and you told me my boat was ugly."

"I need rope. Real rope, not the rot you've got coiled aft. And we need fuel if we're going upstream." He set down his fork. "And I want to see this man's face. If he's got a drawing of my chest, I want to know how good a drawing it is."

Odile studied him for a long moment. Outside, a heron lifted off the reeds with a lazy beat of wings, and somewhere downstream a barge horn moaned across the water.

"You're your father's son," she said finally. "That's not a compliment. It's a diagnosis." She stood and jerked her chin toward the bow. "There's a boy on the dock at Miller's Landing. Sixteen, maybe. Calls himself Tuck. He's been watching our boat since yesterday—badly, I might add, like a cat watching a bird through glass. He's got lockpicks in his coat and debt-collector scars on his knuckles. I say we talk to him."

"Why?"

"Because a boy who watches boats that closely is a boy looking for a way off the river. And because a boy with lockpicks might be useful, if the chest ever needs opening again." She smiled without warmth. "And because I'd rather have him where I can see him than behind us."

* * *

Bram Voss did not like rivers. Rivers were honest in the way that made them useless—everything on the surface, nothing hidden, all that motion going nowhere. He stood on the fish market's rotted boards in his gray coat and let the smell of carp and diesel settle into the wool, and he reminded himself, as he had reminded himself for eleven years in the syndicate's service, that unpleasant places held valuable things.

The salvager in front of him was called Dree Halloway. She was sixty if she was a day, with forearms like mooring cable and a face that had stopped negotiating with the world sometime in the last century. Voss had bought her breakfast. It sat untouched between them, going cold.

"The chest," he said, for the fourth time. "Lead-lined. This seal." He laid the charcoal drawing on the table between them: a circle bisected by a river, the crest of a nation that did not exist. "You pulled salvage from the Bend for thirty years, Mistress Halloway. You know everything the river gives up."

"I know the river gives up what it gives up," Dree said. "Bones. Bottles. A piano once, God knows how. Last month it gave up a wedding dress and I sold it to a bride who thought tragedy was romantic."

"The chest."

"Never seen it."

Voss smiled. He had practiced the smile in mirrors until it reached his eyes, because eyes were where people looked for lies, and the best lie was the one the liar believed. "Mistress Halloway. I am not the police. I am not the tax man. I don't care about duties or salvage rights or any of the small laws that govern this charming mudbank. The syndicate I represent pays for information the way rivers pay for rain—continuously, and without asking where it came from." He slid a fold of new notes across the table. "A name is worth this. A chest is worth ten times this."

Dree looked at the money the way a mule looks at a whip.

"There's a Hartwell boy," she said.

Voss kept his smile in place and let the silence do the work. He had learned long ago that people filled silences with the things they'd sworn not to say.

"Silas Hartwell," Dree went on, because silences were for amateurs. "Dredges the east channels. His father was the archaeologist—the one who disgraced himself, twenty years back, went upriver chasing the Ansel and never came down. Boy's been pulling salvage ever since. Quiet. Keeps to himself." She shrugged. "If anyone found something in the floodwater and didn't report it, it'd be him. He doesn't report anything. Doesn't trust anyone. Can't say I blame him, the way this town treated the family."

Voss turned the name over. *Hartwell.* It was on his list—had been on the list since the syndicate's archivists flagged every descendant, debtor, and drinking companion of Elias Hartwell within two hundred miles of the Ansel. The father had vanished with half a ledger and a nation's secrets. Sons inherited debts. Sons inherited grudges. Sons, most reliably of all, inherited obsessions.

"Where does he berth?"

"Wherever he isn't." Dree finally ate a piece of the cold breakfast, chewing with the deliberation of a woman who had decided the meal was hers by right. "He's got a boat now, I heard. Been seen with Odile Ferran."

The name landed like a hook in water. Voss knew it from the syndicate's files: *Ferran, Odile. Smuggler. Formerly of the Ansel trade. See appendix: Hartwell expedition, personnel.* She had been there. Twenty years ago, at the edge of the story, and now here she was at the edge of it again, moored in the reeds with a Hartwell aboard.

"You've been very helpful," Voss said, and added a second fold of notes to the first.

Dree took the money without apology. "One thing," she said, as he rose. "The boy's not what you'd call a fighter. But his father wasn't either, and look how long it took the river to finish him."

"I'll keep that in mind," Voss said, and meant it. The ones who weren't fighters were the ones who ran, and running left trails, and trails were the only honest thing about rivers.

* * *

Miller's Landing was a shelf of rotted planks jutting into the current, home to a fuel pump that worked on alternating days and a boy who was doing a very poor job of not watching them.

Tuck was small for sixteen, all elbows and wariness, with a cap pulled low and hands that never fully left his pockets. He sat on an upturned crate mending a net he clearly didn't own, and his eyes tracked Silas and Odile the whole way down the dock with the desperate attention of someone memorizing an escape route.

"You've been watching my boat," Odile said, without preamble.

"I've been watching the river."

"The river's that way." She pointed at two hundred miles of water. "You've been watching the *Maggot's Blessing* for a day and a half. You watched her when she tied up. You watched her when she took on diesel. Yesterday you watched me load crates for an hour, and I know because I could see you in the mirror of my compass case, and you flinched every time I turned around." She crouched to his eye level. "So either you're the worst spy on the Ansel, or you want something. Which is it?"

The boy's jaw worked. Up close, Silas could see the details Odile had catalogued: the calluses on his fingers in the wrong places for net-mending, right places for picks; the way he sat with his weight forward, ready to bolt; the bruise fading yellow along his jaw that had a fist's geometry.

"Word at the Bend," Tuck said finally, "is you're going upstream."

"Word at the Bend," Odile said, "is a currency printed by idiots. Who's spending it?"

"The toll men. They said Ferran was provisioning for the gorges." He swallowed. "I want passage."

"Passage costs."

"I'll work. I can cook, I can mend, I can—" He hesitated, then made the decision Silas could see arriving like weather. "I can open things."

"Can you," Odile said.

"Locks. Boxes. Anything with a mechanism." He pulled a hand from his pocket and let a set of picks fan across his knuckles, a quick silver ripple, then vanished them again. "I'm the best on this stretch of river. Maybe the best on the whole Ansel. Nobody knows because nobody's caught me."

"Nobody's caught you," Silas said, "because you're sixteen and you steal from people too embarrassed to admit it."

Tuck's eyes came to him for the first time—sharp, dark, measuring. "You're Hartwell. The dredger." Something moved behind his eyes, some quick recalculation. "They say you found something in the flood."

"They say a lot at the Bend."

"They say a chest." The boy leaned forward. "They say a man in a gray coat is asking about it. Paid Dree Halloway enough to fix her roof. He's got drawings." Tuck's voice dropped. "He came to the toll house last night asking after boats that provisioned for the gorges. He's got a list of names, and I heard mine read off it."

Silas went still. "Yours? Why would the syndicate have your name?"

"Because I picked a lock I shouldn't have, back in spring. A syndicate warehouse, down in Corvess. I didn't take anything worth taking—I didn't know what it was, just papers, and I got spooked and ran. But they logged the break. And then the debt men started following me too, and I couldn't tell anymore which ones were theirs and which ones were mine, and—" He stopped, jaw tight, pride and fear grinding against each other. "Point is, I'm on a list. You're on a list. Seems to me the smart move is for everyone on the list to be in the same boat."

Silas looked at Odile. She was watching the boy with an expression he was learning to read: not warmth, exactly, but the particular attention of a woman assessing a tool that might also be a wound waiting to happen.

"He's lying about something," Silas said quietly. It wasn't an accusation, just an observation. The boy's story was too smooth in places, too rough in others, like a stone that had been worked.

"Everyone's lying about something," Odile said. "The question is whether what they're lying about sinks the boat." She straightened up and addressed Tuck directly. "Rules. One: I'm captain. Not first among equals, not a democracy with hats—captain. Two: you steal from anyone on this boat, I put you off at the next lock, in the water, at night. Three—" she paused, and something flickered across her face, some old ledger of her own— "you don't ask me about the Ansel's source, and I won't ask you about Corvess. Deal?"

Tuck looked from her to Silas and back. "That's it? No questions about my people, my—"

"Everyone on this river is running from something," Silas said. "It's the only cargo we all carry."

The boy stared at him a moment longer, and then something in his shoulders let go—a half inch of tension, no more, but Silas had spent enough years around wary things to know it for what it was. Not trust. The decision to find out whether trust was possible.

"Deal," Tuck said.

* * *

Voss found the toll keeper at dusk, counting the day's coins by lamplight with the furtive speed of a man who skimmed.

The toll house sat where the river narrowed above Marrow Bend, a stone arch and an iron chain and a hut, and Voss had walked the towpath three miles to reach it because he did not trust his hired boatmen not to talk. He had learned, in eleven years of collecting, that the best intelligence came from small men in small rooms who believed no one would ever check their sums.

"Toll keeper," he said, laying the charcoal drawing of the chest on the coin table. "This boat came through yesterday."

The keeper glanced at it, then at Voss's coat, then at the notes Voss set beside the drawing. Greed and fear fought briefly in his face, and greed won, as it usually did in small rooms.

"Ferran's barge. Aye. Paid double, no cargo manifest, heading up toward the gorges." The keeper wet his lips. "There was a young one with them. Boy. And the Hartwell lad."

"You're certain it was Hartwell?"

"Known him since he was in swaddling. Looks just like his father, God rot the man." The keeper hesitated, weighing something. "There's a rumor going downriver. That the syndicate's paying for Vessian relics. That there's a price on them." His eyes flicked to the crest in the drawing. "Is it true? About Vessia? That it's real?"

Voss considered the question. It was, in his experience, the most dangerous kind: the kind people asked when they wanted the answer to be no.

"Vessia," he said, "is a story told by people who found something in a river and want it to be worth more than it is. My employers pay for stories. That's all."

The keeper laughed, relieved, and Voss smiled his practiced smile and left him the notes, and walked back along the darkening towpath with the boy's name added to his list and the route fixed in his mind: *upstream, toward the gorges, toward the Gorge of Names, where twenty years ago Elias Hartwell had stood on this same water and lied to everyone about where he was going.*

Voss had read the surviving file. He knew what the syndicate believed: that Hartwell had found the source, that he had died of it or hidden it, and that the ledger's missing half was the only map in the world to a nation's corpse. The syndicate wanted the corpse. Corpses could be catalogued, claimed, sold.

Voss wanted something else, though he'd never have said it aloud in any room the syndicate owned. He wanted the thing that had made a respectable archaeologist burn his reputation and walk into the mountains and never come back. Eleven years he'd worked for collectors, prying secrets out of the world's cracks, and every so often he found one that made the finder choose exile over wealth, and those were the only secrets he'd ever envied.

He reached the hired boat at full dark. His three boatmen sat around a lantern, dice clicking, and they straightened when they saw him, because they'd learned what his smiles meant.

"We leave at first light," Voss said. "Upstream. We're behind a barge, a dredger, and a boy, and we're going to stop being behind them." He looked up the river, where the gorges waited, black on blacker. "And if anyone offers them help along the way—fuel, food, a night's mooring—I want their names too. Everyone who touches this trail comes onto the list."

One of the boatmen, a heavy man with a broken nose, shifted. "And what happens to the people on the list?"

Voss picked up the lantern and let the light move across his face, so they could see the smile arrive properly this time, all the way to the eyes.

"Nothing," he said. "As long as they stay off the river ahead of us."

The dice went quiet. Above the gorge, the first stars were coming out over water that had already begun, in its patient way, to forget them.

* * *

They cast off before dawn, the *Maggot's Blessing* sliding out of the reeds with her lamp unlit, running dark on the last of the night. Silas took the tiller while Odile dozed in the bow and Tuck sat amidships, turning his picks over in his fingers like a man saying a rosary he didn't believe in.

Behind them, three miles down, a lantern moved along the towpath. Silas watched it for a long time, a small yellow point crawling against the dark, keeping pace with the current.

"Tuck," he said quietly. "How good are you at knowing when you're being followed?"

The boy looked back at the lantern, then up at Silas, and his face in the gray pre-dawn light was suddenly very young and very old at once.

"Good enough," he said, "to know we already are."
