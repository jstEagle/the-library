## 5. Drowned Steeple

The reservoir announced itself a full day before they reached it.

Silas smelled it first — that flat, mineral stillness that comes off deep water held behind stone — and then the trees changed, the riverbank pines giving way to drowned trunks standing in gray water like the masts of a fleet that had gone down at anchor. The Ansel slowed here, spread wide and sullen, its current all but gone. Odile brought the *Magpie* in close to the western shore, where an old towpath ran along the flooded margin, cracked concrete vanishing into the shallows in long broken teeth.

"Kettlewick," she said, nodding at a rusted sign half-swallowed by moss: KETTLEWICK LANDING — POP. 412. "They moved everyone out when they built the dam. Thirty years back, maybe more."

"And left everything else," Tuck said. He was standing on the bow rail, one hand on the stay, squinting north.

Silas followed his gaze and felt something turn over quietly in his chest.

Out past the drowned trees, maybe two miles across the flat gray water, a church steeple broke the surface. Just the spire and the top of the belfry, black with wet, a rook's nest of sticks jammed into the louvers. The rest of the village lay beneath them — streets, houses, wells, gardens, forty years of ordinary lives — pressed under water so still it looked like glass laid over a photograph.

"They didn't even take the church down," he said.

"Cheaper not to." Odile cut the engine to a low idle. "Governments don't pay for sentiment. They drown it instead."

The words sat wrong in Silas's ears, though he couldn't have said why. He thought of his father's study, the boxes hauled out to the curb after the disgrace, the neighbors watching from their porches as the university carted away twenty years of work and left the man himself standing in the driveway with nothing but a coat over his arm. Institutions didn't drown villages. They just made sure no one remembered where the steeple stood.

"We'll camp ashore tonight," Odile said. "Fuel's low, and I want daylight for crossing open water. Reservoirs eat small boats in wind." She glanced at Silas. "And you wanted to look around. I can see it in your face. You've got your father's look — the one that means you've already decided something and haven't told anyone yet."

"I don't have a look."

"You absolutely do. It's worse than Tuck's."

"My look is excellent," Tuck said.

They made camp in the lee of the old boathouse, which stood on high ground above the waterline, its doors hanging open on empty racks. Sefa built the fire small and screened, out of habit Silas was beginning to understand — a woman whose people had survived by being untraceable did not make fires that could be seen from ridgelines. She brewed something bitter and green from her satchel and handed cups around without asking if anyone wanted any.

Silas drank his and walked down to the water alone.

The reservoir at dusk was the quietest place he had ever stood. No current, no birdsong, no wind — just the light dying slowly on the far hills and the steeple going black against it, and beneath his feet, somewhere down in the cold, the shape of a whole town. He crouched at the margin and let his fingers trail the water. It was colder than the river had been. River water moved; it forgot things. This water kept everything.

He was still crouched there when he saw the light.

Not on the water — under it. A pale gleam, low down near what might once have been the village square, too steady to be a fish, too warm to be anything the sun had left behind. It pulsed once, faintly, like something breathing behind glass.

Silas told himself it was phosphorescence. He told himself it was a reflection off his own compass, which had swung loose from his pocket on its cord and hung over the water, needle trembling.

But the needle wasn't trembling randomly. It was pointing — steadily, insistently — down. Into the drowned town. Toward the light.

He'd owned the compass since he was nine years old, the night his father pressed it into his hands and said, *Keep this where you can reach it, Silas. Not where it's safe. Where you can reach it.* Twenty years of carrying it, and in all that time it had behaved like any other compass: north, always north, dull and faithful. Now it pointed at the bottom of a reservoir, and the brass housing was warm against his palm, warmer than the evening justified.

"Silas." Odile's voice, behind him. "You planning to sleep out here, or—"

She stopped. He felt her stop, the way you feel a change in pressure before weather. He turned. She was looking not at him but past him, at the water, at the light.

"That's new," she said quietly.

"You see it too."

"I see it." She came down beside him, boots grinding on the old concrete, and crouched. For a long moment neither of them spoke. The light pulsed again, deeper now, dimming as the sky went fully dark, until it was only a ghost of itself — and then it went out altogether, and the compass needle swung lazily back toward north as if embarrassed to have been caught doing anything else.

"Frescoes," Odile said.

"What?"

"The church. Kettlewick's church had frescoes — my mother ferried pilgrims up this stretch when she was young, before the dam. She used to talk about it. Paintings inside the nave, older than the country that drowned the town. She said the priest wouldn't let outsiders photograph them." Odile's jaw worked. "I never thought much about it. Smugglers don't think much about churches."

"Why are you thinking about them now?"

"Because whatever's glowing down there is sitting right about where a church floor would be." She stood, brushed off her knees. "Get some sleep. We cross at first light. And Silas—" she paused at the top of the bank, firelight catching the scar along her cheekbone, "—if that compass does anything else strange, you tell me. Not tomorrow. Then."

* * *

First light on the reservoir was a slow bruise of pink and gold, and the water lay so smooth that the drowned trees seemed to grow both ways at once, trunks descending into their own reflections without end. Odile took the *Magpie* out slow, engine at a whisper, threading the snags. Tuck stood at the bow with the boat hook, pushing off deadfall. Sefa sat amidships with her eyes closed, but Silas noticed her lips moving — counting, or praying, or reciting names. He was learning not to ask. She answered questions the way the river answered stones.

The steeple grew as they approached, and with it the scale of what lay below. They passed directly over what must have been the main street: rooflines visible in the clear morning water, a chimney, the corner of a garden wall with a rosebush gone wild and white-blooming on the surface, feeding on nothing but memory. A child's bicycle stood against a fence thirty feet down, upright, patient, its handlebar tassels drifting like weed.

Nobody spoke. Even Tuck, who narrated everything, said nothing.

They rounded the last stand of drowned oaks and the church opened up beneath them.

It was bigger than Silas expected — a proper stone church, its roof collapsed inward but its walls standing, the tower leaning slightly, the whole structure settled into the silt like a ship run aground. Through water gone transparent in the early sun they could see down into the nave: fallen beams furred with algae, pews in rows, and on the walls—

Silas's breath went out of him.

Color. After decades underwater, color — blues and reds and golds, muted but unmistakable, covering every wall of the nave from floor to where the vaulting had been. Figures. Processions. A river rendered in lapis blue running the length of the east wall, and along it, hundreds of small painted people carrying things — lanterns, sheaves, children, books — toward a single point where the painter had set a great golden circle, ringed in white.

Odile killed the engine. They drifted.

At the center of the golden circle, painted large enough to be seen from the back pews, was a seal. A stylized river coiling through a mountain gate, enclosed in a ring of script Silas couldn't read — angular, flowing, nothing like any alphabet he knew.

Except he did know it. He'd known it for six days. It was stamped into the lead lining of the chest sitting under the tarp in the stern of this very boat.

"It's the same," Tuck breathed. He'd come aft without anyone telling him to. "That's the mark on the chest. That's Vessia's mark."

"How do you know that name?" Sefa's voice was soft and dangerous, the first full sentence she'd offered in hours.

Tuck flinched. "I — the ledger. Silas's ledger. There were pages with that word written in the margins. Vessia. I assumed it was a person."

Sefa looked at Silas. Her eyes were dark and level and asked a question he couldn't answer yet. He gave her a small shake of the head — *not here, not now* — and she let it go, but he saw her move slightly, unconsciously, so that her body sat between the frescoes and the tarp-covered chest. Guarding it. Or guarding the world from it.

The compass hung at his throat, and it was pulling again. Down and east, down and east, insistent as a hooked fish.

"There's someone on the tower," Tuck said suddenly.

Silas looked up. At first he saw nothing — just the black louvers of the belfry, the rooks stirring — and then movement: a figure standing on the narrow ledge beside the bell chamber, sixty feet above the waterline, where no person had any business standing. Small. Wrapped in gray. Watching them.

As they drifted closer, the figure raised one arm — not waving. Pointing. East, along the line of the drowned valley, toward the dam and the country beyond it.

Then it stepped backward off the ledge and vanished into the belfry, impossibly, as if the tower had swallowed it.

"Tell me someone else saw that," Tuck said.

"Everyone saw that," Odile said grimly. "Which means we're leaving. Now."

"No," Silas heard himself say.

Every head turned. He hadn't meant to speak; the word had come up from somewhere below thought, from the same place the compass pulled from. He stood at the gunwale with the water sliding past, the frescoes glowing faintly through ten fathoms of cold, and he found that his hands were steady.

"A ferrywoman," he said. "There's a ferry landing on the north shore — I saw the pilings when we came in. Someone lives out here. Someone who stands on a drowned church and points directions. If there's anything left to know about this valley, she knows it, and Voss isn't here yet, and I am not sailing past the only living soul who might recognize what we're carrying."

"You don't know she's friendly."

"I know she didn't signal to anyone but us."

Odile held his gaze for three long seconds. Then she swore softly in river cant and swung the tiller. "North shore. Ten minutes. If she turns out to be syndicate, Tuck, you're negotiating."

"Why me?"

"Because you're cheap to replace."

* * *

The landing was rotted but sound, and the path beyond it climbed through alder scrub to a low stone cottage with smoke rising from its chimney — real smoke, woodsmoke, the first sign of ordinary human life they'd seen in two days. Nets hung on frames outside. A rowboat, ancient and tarred, sat overturned by the door. And in the doorway, wrapped in the same gray wool they'd seen on the tower, stood a woman old enough to be Silas's grandmother, small and straight-backed, with eyes the pale gray of the reservoir itself.

She looked at each of them in turn, unhurried, the way you'd read a ledger. When her gaze reached Silas, it stopped.

"You're the dredger's boy," she said. Her voice was gravel over water. "From Marrow Bend. Hartwell."

Silas's skin prickled. "I don't think we've met."

"We haven't. Your father stopped here, twenty years back, coming upstream. Asked me the same question you're about to ask me." She stepped aside, opening the doorway onto warmth and lamplight and the smell of dried fish and herbs. "Come in before the wind changes. And bring the compass out from under your shirt, boy. You've been clutching it since you landed like it might bolt."

He hadn't realized he was touching it. He followed the others inside, and as he crossed the threshold the needle strained against its housing, swinging hard east, quivering like a live thing.

The cottage interior was one room, dense with a life pared to essentials: a stove, a bed, nets, jars, a wall of photographs gone sepia with age. The old woman put a kettle on without asking names. Only when they were seated did she lower herself into the chair opposite Silas and fold her hands.

"Maren Ostrova," she said. "Ferrywoman. Forty years on this water, and eleven more before that, before they drowned it." She nodded toward the window, toward the steeple. "I married in that church. My mother is buried under the water you crossed this morning. The government paid us each a sum and a lie — that the flooding would be quick and painless and forgotten." Her mouth twisted. "Water doesn't forget. Water keeps. That's the first thing a ferrywoman learns."

"The frescoes," Silas said. "In the nave. The seal on the east wall. What is it?"

Maren Ostrova was quiet for a long moment. The kettle began to sing, and she ignored it.

"When I was a girl, the priest told us those paintings were older than the kingdom that built our roads. He said they were put there by people who came upriver before there were maps, and that the golden circle was a door, and the writing around it was a promise." She leaned forward. "He said the promise was this: *the river remembers what it was told to forget.* That when the world tried to erase those people — and the world did try, boy, there was a nation up this valley once, and every map in every capital agreed it had never existed — the river itself would keep their name. In its currents. In its flood-marks. In places like this church, sunk where only the patient and the honest would ever look."

Sefa had gone very still. Her hands were flat on her knees, pressing down, as if holding herself to the earth.

"And my father?" Silas asked. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Twenty years ago. Did he ask about the seal?"

"He asked about the source." Maren's pale eyes didn't waver. "He showed me a ledger — half of one, torn clean down the spine — and a compass that behaved exactly the way yours is behaving right now, straining east like a dog on a leash. And I told him what I'm going to tell you." She rose, joints cracking, and took a lamp down from a shelf. Beneath it, hidden in the wood, was a shallow depression in the grain — a socket, round, worn smooth, sized precisely to hold a compass housing.

"Your father set his compass in this hole and it spun until it pointed up the valley, and then it stopped spinning and simply *sang* — hummed against the wood like a struck bell. He wrote for an hour by my fire. Then he thanked me, paid me in silver, and went up into the gorge with two men I never saw again." She set the lamp down and looked at Silas with something that was almost pity and almost warning. "Three weeks later, men in fine coats came downstream asking the same questions. They weren't honest. I lied to them, and they burned my boat for it, and I built another, because that's what ferrywomen do."

Outside, the wind was rising off the reservoir, dragging little white horses across the water toward the drowned steeple. Somewhere beneath the surface, Silas imagined, the frescoes glowed on in the dark, keeping their promise.

"What happened to the men in fine coats?" he asked.

"One drowned in the gorge. One went home rich." Maren Ostrova sat back down and fixed him with her gray eyes. "The rich one had a name the river people still use to frighten children. Voss."

The kettle screamed. Nobody moved to lift it.

In Silas's pocket, warm as a heartbeat, the compass pulled east — toward the gorge, toward the source, toward whatever his father had found and the man named Voss had spent twenty years hunting — and for the first time since Marrow Bend, Silas understood that he was not following a treasure at all.

He was following a debt. And the river, patient as the drowned, intended to collect.
