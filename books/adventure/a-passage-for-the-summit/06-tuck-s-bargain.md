## 6. Tuck's Bargain

Tuck had learned to sleep in thirds. A third of the night for real sleep, a third for listening, and a third for thinking — because in Marrow Bend, sleeping whole got your boots stolen, and worse. Old habits didn't drown just because you were a hundred miles upstream on a smuggler's boat with two strangers who hadn't decided yet whether you were cargo or crew.

He lay in the bow of the *Marrow Maid* under an oilcloth tarp, listening to the river slap the hull and Odile Ferran snore like a woman settling scores in her dreams. Silas Hartwell was on watch, sitting at the stern with his back against the tiller post, turning that brass compass over in his hands the way other men turned worry beads.

The ledger was in the deck box beneath Tuck's knees.

He'd known it since the second night. He'd watched Silas open the box at dusk, check the oilskin wrapping, close it again — always with the same little pause, like a man touching a bruise to see if it still hurt. Nobody guarded something that carefully unless it was worth more than the boat.

And Tuck needed it to be worth more than the boat.

He sat up slowly, keeping his breathing even, the way he'd been taught by a lockpick named Corvo who'd died owing money to the same people Tuck owed money to now. The debt collectors in Marrow Bend had a name for boys like him: *renewable assets*. You couldn't squeeze blood from a stone, but you could keep a boy working until he was a man, and then squeeze him some more.

Nobody was coming for him out here. That was the problem. Out here, nobody knew he existed at all.

* * *

He'd made the photographs three nights ago, back at the last fuel stop, and he still wasn't sure if he was proud of himself or sick about it.

It had been simple, which was the insulting part. While Silas haggled with the fuel seller and Odile argued with a dockhand about mooring fees, Tuck had slipped below, taken the ledger from the box — he could open any lock on the river with two picks and a hairpin, and this one hadn't even been locked, just wrapped, like trust was the only seal it needed — and laid the pages flat on the bunk.

Then he'd used the camera.

It had cost him eleven days of scavenging to buy that camera, back when he was twelve, off a dead photographer's estate sale. A Mirelle field camera, folding bellows, glass lens, the kind journalists carried into war zones. He'd told himself it was for taking pictures of things worth remembering. He'd never actually developed a single plate. It was easier to believe you were an artist if you never looked at what you'd made.

Eleven exposures. The torn edge of the first page, where the paper had been ripped ragged as a wound. His father's handwriting — no, not his father, Silas's father, but the handwriting hit Tuck somewhere anyway, all those careful annotations in brown ink, measurements of current and depth, place names that weren't on any map Tuck had ever stolen. And on the cover page, drawn in a fine engraved line: the compass. The same compass Silas touched every night like a rosary.

Tuck had rewrapped the ledger exactly as he'd found it, down to the fold of the oilskin, and gone back topside with his heart going like a paddle wheel.

He told himself it was insurance. That was the word he kept using, insurance, because it sounded responsible, almost grown-up. If Odile decided halfway up the Ansel that a debt-collector's kid was more trouble than ballast, if she put him off at some drowned village with nothing but his picks and his nerve — well. Then he'd have something to trade. A syndicate man named Voss was paying for Vessian artifacts, everyone on the river knew it, and half a ledger pointing at the source of the River Ansel wasn't an artifact, it was the artifact. Eleven photographs. Enough to buy a new name in a city where nobody collected debts.

Enough to matter to somebody, even if it was the wrong somebody.

That was the part he didn't say out loud, even inside his own head, very often. Not just the money. The mattering. He was seventeen years old and he had been sold twice, abandoned once, and forgotten so many times he'd stopped counting, and there was a hole in him shaped exactly like being wanted, and he'd learned that the only way anyone ever filled it was if you brought something to the table. Pictures. Locks opened. Value.

You couldn't just be a person. Being a person was free, and free things got left behind.

* * *

"You breathe too loud when you're scheming."

Tuck froze. Odile was standing at the cabin door, and she did not look like she'd been asleep. She was holding two tin cups of chicory coffee, steam curling off them into the cold morning air, and her face had the particular blankness Tuck had learned to read as danger. On Odile Ferran, blankness was what anger looked like when it was saving itself up.

"I don't scheme," Tuck said. "I plan. Scheming is for people with better clothes."

"Come eat."

She handed him a cup and didn't watch him drink it, which was somehow worse than watching. She went forward instead and started checking the tie-downs on the fuel drums, yanking each strap hard enough to make the boat shudder, saying nothing, letting the silence do the talking. Tuck sat on the gunwale and drank coffee that tasted like burnt acorns and waited for the axe to fall.

She knew. He was almost certain she knew. She'd come out of the cabin at the fuel stop twenty minutes before they cast off, and she'd looked at him — just looked, that flat gray look — and said, "You've got ink on your fingers," and he'd said, "Ledger," and she'd said, "Mm," and that had been the whole conversation, and he'd spent four days since then waiting for the other shoe, and the shoe had not dropped, and the not-dropping was its own kind of torture.

"Silas," Odile called without turning around. "How deep is the channel past the steeple?"

"Twelve feet if the reservoir gates are closed, six if they're running water," Silas said from the stern. He hadn't slept either; Tuck could tell by the grit in his voice. "Why?"

"Because I'm thinking about what we throw overboard if we draw too much."

She let that sit. Tuck felt it land in his chest like a hook finding flesh.

* * *

They ran the drowned country all that day. The reservoir had swallowed three villages whole, and their rooftops broke the surface like the backs of sleeping animals, and once, passing over what must have been a street, Tuck saw a church spire slide by beneath the hull, its bell still hanging, green with weed, tilted as if ringing for a congregation of fish.

Silas stood at the bow the whole time, reading the water. Tuck studied him sidelong. This was the thing about Silas Hartwell that Tuck couldn't fit into any of his categories: he wasn't soft, exactly, but he wasn't hard the way the collectors were hard, hardness as a business model. He was hard the way a rope is hard — everything pulled tight around something that had happened to him, and if you cut him open you'd find the shape of his father coiled at the center of every strand.

Tuck knew the type. He was going to be that type himself in ten years if he didn't get out.

At dusk they tied up in the lee of a flooded orchard, dead trees standing in black water, and Odile cooked rice with dried fish and too much salt, and they ate in the particular silence of three people who are each hiding something. Tuck wondered what the other two were hiding. Everybody was hiding something; that was the first law of the river.

Afterward Silas took the ledger out to check it — Tuck watched him do it, watched the ritual of unwrapping, counting pages, rewrapping — and Tuck's stomach turned over. Eleven plates in his pack, wrapped in wool socks. If Silas counted wrong someday, or noticed the oilskin folded a hair differently, or if the camera fell out of Tuck's bag during a capsize and cracked open on the rocks—

"Your turn on watch," Silas said to him. "Midnight to four."

"Aye."

"And Tuck." Silas paused at the cabin door, the lantern light catching the hollows of his face. "Whatever you're carrying that's heavier than your pack — the boat rides better without it."

Then he was gone, and Tuck sat alone in the dark with the dead orchard standing around him like a congregation of the drowned, and hated him a little, and hated himself more.

* * *

It was on the fourth night after the fuel stop that Odile finally came for him.

Not with violence. With cards.

She dealt him in at the little table in the cabin, three hands of a game called Beggar-My-Neighbor that required no skill and no conversation, and she played it in silence while the lamp swung overhead and the river muttered against the hull, and Tuck understood, with the animal clarity of a boy who had survived adults his whole life by reading rooms, that this was the room. This was it.

On the third hand, without looking up from her cards, Odile said:

"When I was fourteen, I sold my sister's location to a man who beat her."

Tuck's hand stopped moving.

"She'd run from our uncle's house. I was hungry, and the man paid in bread, and I told myself it was bread or starvation, which is true, and also a lie, because I would have done it for less." She played a card. "She found out. Of course she found out. They always find out. And she didn't scream at me, Tuck. That was the worst of it. She just stopped writing me letters. Twenty years, and I would give this boat, both engines, and my good knee for one more letter, and I know — I *know* — that the silence was fair payment."

The lamp swung. Somewhere above, Silas moved on watch, boot heels on deck.

"So here is what happens now," Odile said, and finally she looked up, and her eyes were the gray of deep water over deep rock. "I know about the camera. I know about the eleven plates. I have known since the fuel stop, because I searched your bag while you were buying that terrible sausage you ate, and I want you to understand that I am not telling you this because I'm careless. I'm telling you because Silas is going to get us all killed at that source, and when he does, I intend to be the one holding the map, and I need to know — right now, tonight, with no audience and no way for either of us to lie well — whether you are mine or you are theirs."

The river slapped the hull. Tuck's mouth was dry as paper.

"If I'm theirs," he managed, "what happens?"

"Nothing dramatic. We reach the next landing, and you get off, and I wish you luck, and I mean it, because whatever else you are, you're the best lockpick I've ever run with, and it will cost me to lose you." She set down her cards. "But you'll be off my boat, and I'll sleep better, and you can go sell your pictures to a man who executes guides who won't talk. Ask around about Bram Voss at the floating market. Ask what he did to the ferryman at Cold Larder. Then decide whose you are."

She gathered the cards, squared them, and stood.

"You don't have to answer tonight," she said at the door. "But the boat moves at dawn, and it only carries people who are on it all the way. There's no such thing as half aboard, Tuck. Believe me. I tried it for twenty years, and the river charges interest."

Then she was gone, and Tuck sat alone in the lamplight with eleven photographs hidden in wool socks and a question sitting on his chest like ballast.

He thought about the letter his mother had sent him at the workhouse, the only one, four lines long, saying *be useful and someone will keep you*. He'd built his whole life on those four lines and he was starting to suspect, here in the swinging lamplight, a hundred miles from anywhere, that she'd been wrong — that usefulness was just another word for rent, and rent never ended, and the people who kept you for your usefulness would drop you the day the market changed.

He thought about Silas checking the oilskin wrap every dusk, the same fold every time, like a prayer.

He thought about asking around about Bram Voss, and knew already what he'd hear, and knew already that hearing it wouldn't change the arithmetic, because fear was a currency too and he'd been paid in it his whole life.

Tuck blew out the lamp and lay down in the dark and did not sleep in thirds or halves or at all.

At dawn the boat moved, and Tuck was on it, and his pack was heavier than it had ever been, and ahead the river bent north toward a drowned steeple rising out of the mist like a finger pointed at heaven — or at whatever was buried underneath it — and Odile took the wheel without looking at him, and Silas looked at him once, briefly, with those tired rope-tight eyes, and said nothing at all.
