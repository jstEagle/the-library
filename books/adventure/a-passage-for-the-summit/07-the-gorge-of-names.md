## 7. The Gorge of Names

Odile Ferran had run the Ansel for eleven years, and she had never once taken the Gorge of Names at anything less than full daylight.

She stood at the wheel of the *Magpie* as the canyon walls closed in, watching the light go thin and gray between them, and she thought about turning back. She didn't say it. Saying it would give Tuck an opening, and Tuck was already leaning over the rail with that look he got — the one that said he was calculating exits, counting who'd notice if he slipped away in the night. She'd seen a hundred boys wear that face. She'd worn it herself, once, on a different river, under a different name.

"Slow her down," Silas said from the bow.

"We're not slowing down." Odile kept her voice flat. "Current through here runs nine knots in the narrows. You want to fight it or ride it?"

"I want to *look* at it." He turned, and she saw he was holding his father's compass open in his palm, the needle swinging lazy circles like something drunk. "The walls are doing something to it."

The walls were doing something to everything. They rose sheer on either side, two hundred feet of black stone streaked with mineral bleed, and carved into them — Odile had heard the stories but hearing wasn't seeing — were names. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Rows upon rows of letters cut deep into the rock, some no bigger than her thumbnail, some the size of doors, climbing up past where the light reached and down into the water itself, drowned now, their lower halves furred with moss. Names in scripts she half-recognized and scripts she didn't recognize at all. Names chiseled over other names, older ones effaced by newer hands, newer ones gouged out by someone with a hammer and a grudge.

"It's a ledger," Sefa said quietly. She'd come up from below deck without Odile hearing her, which was its own small alarm — a woman that size shouldn't move that quiet. "Every name Vessia lost. Every person they erased from the rolls when they burned the country off the maps. Families came here for generations and cut their dead into the stone so the river would carry the memory downstream." Her hand rose toward the wall, hovered, fell. "And then the erasers found it too."

Tuck ran his fingers along a row of defaced carvings, the chisel-marks still sharp after who knew how many decades. "They scratched 'em out."

"They couldn't scratch out all of us," Sefa said. "So they scratched out the proof we'd been here."

Silas hadn't moved. He was staring at the compass, then at the wall, then at the compass again, and Odile watched his jaw work the way it did when he was chewing on something he didn't want to swallow. Twenty years since his father walked up this river and vanished. Twenty years, and the man's handwriting was sitting in a chest in Odile's hold, pointing them all toward whatever waited upstream.

"Keep moving," she said. "We gawk on the water, not stopped in it. Stopped boats are coffins."

She eased the throttle forward and the *Magpie* slid deeper into the gorge.

* * *

The trouble started an hour in, at a bend where the river doubled back on itself and the current piled against the outside wall like a fist against a door.

Odile felt it before she saw it — a wrongness in the water's texture, a smoothness where there should have been chop. She cut the engine to idle and listened. Above the river-sound: oars. Muffled, disciplined, more than one boat.

"Down," she breathed. "All of you. Now."

They dropped behind the gunwale. Odile killed the engine and let the *Magpie* drift, hand on the wheel, reading the water. Around the bend came three longboats — shallow-draft, low-slung, painted the mud-gray of the lower Ansel. Eight men apiece, rowing in that easy professional rhythm she knew from smuggler crews and worse. At the prow of the lead boat stood a man with field glasses raised, sweeping the water ahead of them.

Not looking back. Not yet.

"They're ahead of us," Silas whispered. "How are they ahead of us?"

"Because Voss doesn't follow rivers," Odile murmured. "He buys them. Those'll be local boatmen on retainer — probably been posted here since before we left Marrow Bend, waiting for anyone coming upstream who looks like salvage hunters." She risked a glance. The lead scout was lowering the glasses, saying something to the stroke oar. "They've spotted our wake residue. Oil sheen. Give them ten minutes."

"Then we have ten minutes," Silas said.

"We have maybe six, and one way through." She pointed with her chin at the bend ahead, where the current slammed the outer wall and rebounded into a standing wave train that even she respected. "That's the Squeeze. Ride the eddy line along the inner wall, punch through the seam where the return current meets the main flow, and we're through before they can round the corner. Miss the seam and the river puts us on the rocks, or worse, spins us back into their guns."

"Guns?" Tuck's voice cracked on it.

"Boatmen working syndicate money carry guns," Odile said. "Everybody quiet. Sefa, get below with the chest. Tuck, bail cover on the fuel drums — if a spark finds those, none of this matters."

She waited until the last head disappeared, then took a breath the length of a prayer and opened the throttle.

The *Magpie* leapt. Odile brought her hard against the inner wall, so close the hull kissed stone and the carved names streamed past close enough to read — *MAREN VESS, KEEPER OF THE THIRD DOOR; THE CHILDREN OF HALLOR; WE WERE HERE, WE WERE HERE, WE WERE HERE* — and then the eddy line took them and the world went sideways.

This was the part people never understood about rivers, she thought, wrestling the wheel through the boil. A river wasn't water. It was architecture. Invisible walls and floors and staircases, all of it moving, all of it lying. You didn't fight it. You knocked on the right door at the right moment and asked politely to be let through.

The seam showed itself as a ribbon of glass-smooth water between two chaos zones, no wider than the boat. She aimed for it.

"Hold on," she said, to nobody, because there was nobody left above deck to hear her, and drove the *Magpie* into the gap.

The river hit them like a mob. The bow lifted, slammed down, lifted again; spray came over the rails in sheets; somewhere below deck something heavy broke loose and crashed. Odile rode it, hands white, eyes on the far eddy, counting seconds — three, four, five — and then the chaos let go all at once and they shot out the far side into calm water, listing hard to port, engine screaming a note it had never screamed before.

Behind them, around the bend, came shouting. And then, distinct and unhurried: three shots, pocking the water well wide.

"Go," Silas said, appearing beside her, soaked, alive. "Go, go—"

"I'm going, I'm going." But the engine note was wrong, wronger than wrong, and thirty seconds later it caught, coughed, and died.

The silence afterward was enormous. The *Magpie* drifted in the gorge's shadow, among the names of the dead, while oar-strokes echoed closer around the bend.

* * *

They drifted maybe half a mile before the pursuit sounds faded — the boatmen, Odile guessed, unwilling to run the Squeeze blind any faster than caution allowed. It bought them time, not safety. She got the *Magpie* into a side channel behind a fallen slab of cliff, a slot of green water barely wider than the boat, and shut everything down.

Below deck told the story fast. The crash they'd heard had been the spare fuel rack tearing free of its brackets. Two drums had split. The third had ruptured its seal and slowly, quietly, bled itself out across the bilge while they ran. What remained of their stores fit in a single dented can — maybe four gallons, enough for a day of careful running on a river that had weeks left in it.

Nobody spoke for a long moment. Then Tuck said, "So we row."

"So we row," Odile agreed. "Or we walk. Or we starve. Options abound."

"The floating market," Sefa said. "Two days downstream of here — but we're not going downstream." She said it carefully, watching Odile. "There's another market. Smaller. Upstream, past the toll bridge. My people trade there sometimes."

"Your people," Silas said.

"People who remember what the walls remember." Sefa met his eyes. "You wanted the source of the Ansel. Everything upstream of here belongs to that memory. Including the markets. Including, eventually, the answer to what your compass is pointing at."

Odile watched Silas take that in. She'd spent eleven years reading passengers and crew, and she could tell you within a day which of them would hold and which would fold. Silas Hartwell folded slow, like wet rope taking a knot — but he held. She'd known it since Marrow Bend, since the night he'd shown up at her mooring smelling of river mud and desperation, holding a piece of lead-lined history and asking for passage with nothing to offer but a name she owed a debt against.

"How much fuel do the traders sell?" she asked Sefa.

"Enough. If you can pay."

"And if we can't pay?"

Sefa smiled for the first time since Odile had known her, and it wasn't entirely comforting. "Then you barter. Everyone upriver is poor in coin and rich in other things. What do you carry that you love?"

The question sat in the cabin like a third passenger. Odile saw Silas's hand move, without him seeming to notice, to the inside pocket where the ledger pages lived. Saw Tuck's eyes track the movement with a hunger he was getting worse at hiding. Filed both away.

"We'll figure it out," Silas said.

* * *

They took turns at the oars through the afternoon, the four of them rowing the *Magpie* up a river that fought every stroke, past walls that climbed and climbed, the names growing denser as they went. Toward dusk Odile called a halt at a gravel shelf beneath an overhang and they made cold camp — no fire, not with hired guns somewhere behind them.

It was Tuck who finally broke the silence, sitting with his knees drawn up, chin on them, staring at the wall across the channel where the last light caught the carvings.

"My mum's name isn't on anything," he said.

Nobody answered. He wasn't talking to anybody, exactly. Odile kept her back turned, coiling a line, listening the way she listened to everything.

"When they erased people here, somebody wrote 'em down first. Somebody decided they were worth carving." His voice was carefully casual, the way voices go when they're carrying more than they can lift. "Nobody ever decided that about us. We're just — gone. No wall. No ledger. You lot are all hunting some grand lost nation, and I'm just trying to be owed something by somebody before I die."

"You're owed," Silas said.

"Yeah?" Tuck laughed, short and ugly. "By who? Odile picked me up because I can pick locks and I don't eat much. That's not being owed. That's being useful."

Odile set down the coil. She crossed the gravel, sat down next to the boy with the deliberate heaviness of a woman twice his width, and said nothing for a while. Below them the river moved in the dark, carrying the memory of ten thousand names toward the sea.

"Eleven years ago," she said, "a man pulled me out of the Ansel with a boat hook. I'd capsized in the White Water with a hold full of contraband and no business being alive. He didn't know me. Didn't owe me. He just — did it. Cost him his cargo, nearly cost him his life." She watched the dark water. "His name was Hartwell. He had a son he talked about like the son was a religion he'd lost faith in. Said the boy was the only honest thing he ever made, and the one thing he'd made dishonestly, by staying away."

Beside her, Silas had gone very still.

"I never repaid that man. There was no repaying. So when his boy showed up at my mooring asking for passage, I said yes, and I told myself it was business." She shrugged. "It wasn't business. It was a debt, and debts are just love with paperwork. You're on this boat, Tuck, because I decided you're worth pulling out of the water. That decision's already made. You don't have to be useful for it to stand."

Tuck stared at her. In the dimness his face did something complicated and young. "That's the most words you've ever said to me."

"Don't get used to it."

But she saw it land — saw the boy's shoulders come down half an inch from wherever they'd been living for sixteen years — and she thought: *there*. That's the whole trick of it. Nobody survives alone on a river. You build a crew or the river builds your grave.

Across the fire-ring-that-wasn't, Silas was looking at her with an expression she couldn't parse and didn't try to. He turned the compass over in his hands instead, and frowned.

"Odile," he said. "Come look at this."

She went. The compass lay open in his palm, and the needle — which had swung drunk and erratic all day in the gorge — was steady now. Steady, and pointing not north, but straight up the channel. Straight upstream, into the dark, toward whatever waited beyond the toll bridge.

"It's been correcting itself since the ambush," Silas said quietly. "Like the farther in we go, the surer it gets." He looked up at her, and the last light was gone from the sky, and his face was mostly shadow. "My father drew these currents. I keep feeling that. Like the river's not fighting us. Like it's *checking* us."

Above them, faint on the wind from miles back down the gorge, came a sound that didn't belong to any river: engines. More than one. Running upstream through the night, against all sense and all custom, closing the distance they'd bought with their fuel.

Odile stood, and started waking the boat.
