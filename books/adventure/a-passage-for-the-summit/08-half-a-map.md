## 8. Half a Map

The floating market of Corren's Reach announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke, frying fish, tar, and under it all the green rot of a hundred hulls lashed together and slowly becoming an island. We came around the last bend of the Ansel with our patched fuel line hissing like a kettle and found the whole settlement spread across the water — barges welded to punts welded to rafts, walkways sagging between them, lanterns strung on wire from mast to mast so that from a distance it looked like a constellation that had given up on the sky and settled for the river.

Odile brought us in slow. "Rules," she said, not looking at me. "We buy what we need. We sell nothing. Nobody mentions Vessia, nobody mentions ledgers, nobody mentions why we're going upstream. As far as anyone asks, we're fuel runners."

"And if someone doesn't ask?" Tuck said.

"Then they already know, and it won't matter what we say." She killed the engine and let the current carry us the last fifty yards into the crowd of hulls.

I'd been to markets on the Ansel before, but never one like this. Marrow Bend's market was mud and shouting. This was something older and quieter — bargaining done in murmurs, goods passed hand to hand across gunwales, children running along planks with the surefootedness of cats who'd been born on water. Nobody stared at us, which was its own kind of unnerving. In Marrow Bend I was invisible because I was nothing. Here we were invisible because everyone had something to hide.

The damage was worse than Odile had hoped. The gorge ambush hadn't just cost us our fuel drums; it had cracked the intake manifold and chewed the propeller shaft's bearing half to gravel. The mechanic we found — a broad woman named Hesk who worked out of a barge stacked with engine parts like a junkyard that had learned to float — took one look and laughed.

"Three days," she said.

"We have two," Odile said.

"Then you have a problem. Three days, or you find another mechanic who'll do it faster and worse." Hesk spat over the side. "There's a chandler two decks down sells decent tea while you wait. Don't touch my tools."

So we waited. That's how I met Sefa.

* * *

She had a stall no bigger than a coffin, hung with drying herbs in bundles so dense the air around it tasted bitter and sweet at once. I noticed her the way you notice anything in a place like that — sideways, without meaning to. She was grinding something with a mortar, and her hands were wrong for the work: too fine-boned, too deliberate, like a surgeon doing a laborer's job. When she looked up at me, I saw she'd already been looking.

"You're limping," she said.

"I'm not."

"You favor your left knee. You've been favoring it since you came off that boat." She set down the mortar. "Sit. I have a liniment that will fix what pride won't admit to."

I should have walked on. Instead I sat on the crate she kicked toward me, because my knee had been screaming since the gorge and because there was something in her voice that reminded me — absurdly — of my mother, before she stopped speaking to me altogether. Sefa knelt, pushed up my trouser leg without asking permission, and began working a green-smelling paste into the joint with her thumbs. It burned, then went cold, then went quiet.

"What is that?"

"Wormwood, camphor, river clay, and three things I won't tell you." She tied it off with a strip of linen. "Keep it dry tonight. Who hurt you?"

"Rapids. And before that, men with guns."

"Mm." She didn't press. She began rebundling her herbs, and I should have left then too, but I'd caught sight of something hanging at the back of her stall, half-hidden behind a curtain of dried yarrow.

A charm. Small, wooden, carved with a spiral that broke into seven lines and rejoined itself.

I knew the shape. I'd spent two nights with the ledger open on my knees, tracing that same spiral where it was stamped into every margin, the same seal that was branded into the lead lining of the chest. My father's compass bore it etched into the case, worn nearly smooth.

I must have gone still, because when I glanced back at her face, Sefa was watching me with an expression I couldn't read — not alarm, exactly. More like a woman listening for a sound she'd been told she'd never hear again.

"That's Vessian work," I said. Stupid. Odile's rules, broken inside an hour.

Sefa didn't answer right away. She reached up, took the charm down, and turned it over in her fingers. Around us the market went on — haggling, laughing, a child shrieking with delight as a fish escaped her — and none of it touched the small silence between us.

"It's a protection knot," she said finally. "For travelers. Very common." Her eyes stayed on mine. "Very common among people who know what it protects against."

"I don't know what it protects against."

"No," she said softly. "But you know what it is. You said the name like a man reading a word he's only ever seen written down." She set the charm on the counter between us. "Where did you learn it?"

I thought about lying. I was getting better at lying — the last week had been a crash course. But her hands were still dusted with the paste that had quieted my knee, and I was so tired of carrying the chest's secret alone, and there was a hunger in her face that matched something in mine.

"My father," I said. "He left me things. Papers. A compass with that mark on it. He was an archaeologist. He disappeared twenty years ago looking for—" I lowered my voice until it was barely breath. "—for the source of the Ansel."

Something moved through her then, quick as a fish under the surface. She glanced past me, scanning the walkway, the neighboring stalls, the faces drifting by. Then she pulled the curtain of yarrow half-closed and leaned in.

"Not here. Tonight, after the lamps are doused, the tea barge at the north end. Come alone." She pressed the charm into my palm and closed my fingers over it. "And bring the compass. If you're lying to me, I'll know within a minute. If you're telling the truth—" For the first time, her composure cracked, just at the edges. "If you're telling the truth, then twenty years of waiting has an end to it."

* * *

Odile didn't want me to go. We stood in the narrow cabin of our rented berth while Tuck pretended to sleep in the corner, and she laid it out flat.

"It's a hook," she said. "Somebody clocked the chest, or the ledger, or the way you look at your own compass, and now they're reeling. A stranger invites you to a private meeting at night on a lawless raft-city? Silas. Even you can't be this hungry."

"She knew the seal."

"People know lots of seals. Syndicate people especially."

"She wasn't syndicate. Syndicate looks at you like merchandise. She looked at me like—" I searched for it. "Like I was a letter she'd been waiting for."

Odile rubbed her eyes. "That's exactly what a good hook feels like."

"So come with me. Stand across the walkway. If it goes wrong, you'll see it before I do."

She was quiet a long moment. Outside, the lanterns of the market swayed, throwing moving shadows across the porthole. "Tuck stays here," she said at last. "And you wear this." She handed me a knife, short and ugly and sharp. "Not to fight with. To make them think twice about grabbing you. Grabbing is how these things go wrong."

I took the knife. It felt heavier than it was.

* * *

The tea barge at the north end was shuttered and dark, but a single lamp burned in its stern, and Sefa sat beneath it with two cups already poured. She'd changed into plainer clothes, and she'd brought an old man with her — white-bearded, weathered, with the same fine-boned hands. He watched me come aboard with eyes that missed nothing, including, I suspected, the knife.

"Sit," Sefa said. "This is my uncle, Davor. He keeps the old words. Show him."

I put the compass on the table between the cups.

Davor didn't reach for it immediately. He bent close instead, studying it without touching, and his breathing changed — a small catch, quickly mastered. Then he turned it over with one finger, gentle as a man handling an egg, and found the socket on the underside: the recessed ring of interlocking notches that I'd never been able to explain, that no compass needed, that I'd assumed was some maker's quirk.

His hand shook.

"Twenty years," he said, in a language I didn't understand, and then again in ours. "Twenty years we kept the other half, and told ourselves the day would never come, because if it never came we could stop being afraid of it." He looked up at me. "Your father made this. Do you know that? Not bought it. Made it. There were two, once. Twins."

"Made what?" I said. "What does it open?"

"The council hall," Sefa said quietly. "In Vessia."

The word landed in my chest like a dropped anchor. Not the word from the ledger margins, not the seal on the chest — the actual word, spoken aloud, by living people, on a tea barge on the Ansel. I realized my mouth was open and closed it.

"Vessia fell," I said. "It's not on any map. It hasn't been on any map for—"

"For a hundred years, no. Because maps are how they find you." Davor turned the compass in the lamplight. "Our grandparents walked out of the burning archives with nothing but what they could memorize. Names. Songs. The shape of the valley. They scattered along the river and became ferrymen and herb-sellers and dredgers, and they raised their children to be forgettable. And they kept one thing safe." He tapped the compass. "Half a ledger. Your father carried the other half. Twenty years ago he came to us with it, and he said: you've hidden well enough to survive, but not well enough to live. Let me finish what your grandparents started. Let me build the concealment properly — not hiding from the world, but invisible to it."

"He reached you," I said. My voice came out strange. "He reached the source."

"He lived among us for four years," Sefa said. She was watching me the way you watch someone receive news they don't yet know the weight of. "He married. He had a child."

The river seemed very loud suddenly, slapping against the hull, filling the silence I couldn't fill myself.

"A son," Davor said. "Named Silas."

I stood up. The stool went over backward. Somewhere behind me Odile shifted on the dark walkway — I heard the creak of her weight changing — but I barely registered it. "No," I said. "No, he drowned. Upstream. Everyone said—"

"Everyone said what we paid them to say," Davor said. "When the syndicate sent its last expedition, twenty years ago, your father went out to turn them back. He did. But he didn't come home from it. The river took him at the floodgates, and we buried him above the waterline, in Vessian ground, with Vessian rites." His voice roughened. "We sent word downstream. To Marrow Bend. To a woman named Hartwell."

My mother had received a letter once. I remembered it — I'd been nine, and she'd burned it unopened on the stove, and told me my father's ghosts could stay upstream where they belonged. I had grown up believing the letter was a creditor's notice or a stranger's cruelty. I had built my whole life on the ash of it.

"He had a son," I said again, uselessly.

"And that son is sitting in front of me holding the key to everything his father died protecting." Davor set the compass down and slid it back across the table. "The syndicate thinks the source is treasure. Gold, relics, a nation's grave to pick clean. What it actually is, is eight hundred people. Children. Elders. A city that has spent a century learning to be invisible. And now the syndicate is hunting the river again — we've had word. Men asking about river iron. Men buying charts. If they find the valley with half a map, they will find it eventually with the whole one, and then everything my grandmother memorized in a burning archive dies with the last of us."

"So take me there," I said. "You have the other half. I have this. Take me—"

"No," Sefa said.

The word was flat and final, and it stopped me cold.

"Not like this. Not because you're a key that walks." She leaned forward. "You need to understand what you're asking. Every generation, someone comes to us with a piece of Vessia and a story about destiny, and every generation we weigh them, and most of them we turn away, and a few of them we bury in the shallows. My uncle is old and sentimental and he sees your father's hands when he looks at yours. I see something else. I see a man who pulled a sealed chest out of the river and hid it instead of reporting it — yes, we know about the chest, the river tells us more than you'd believe — and I don't yet know if he hid it because it was sacred or because it was valuable." Her eyes held mine, steady and unkind in their kindness. "Which is it, Silas Hartwell?"

I thought of the ledger on my knees at night. Of the compass, and the ache it carried. Of creditors, and the syndicate's price on Vessian artifacts, and Odile's rules, and Tuck's shifty eyes. Of twenty years of being nobody's son.

"I don't know yet," I said. It was the truest thing I'd said in weeks. "Ask me again at the source."

Sefa held my gaze a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled — a small, tired, real smile. "That," she said, "is the first honest answer anyone's given us in twenty years." She stood and began bundling herbs into a travel satchel with quick, practiced motions. "I'm coming with you. Not because I trust you. Because if the syndicate takes that compass off your body somewhere between here and the ridge, my people are finished, and I'd rather die watching it than hear about it secondhand."

"Sefa," Davor started.

"Hush, Uncle. You're old and sentimental." She cinched the satchel closed and looked at me. "One condition. When we reach the valley, the council decides — not you, not me, not your smuggler. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"Then wake your crew. We leave before the market stirs. And Silas—" She paused at the gangplank, lamplight catching the fine bones of her face. "Your father used to say the river gives you exactly what you can carry and nothing more. I used to think it was a proverb." Her voice dropped. "Now I think it was a warning."

Behind us, out on the dark walkway, a plank creaked under a weight that wasn't Odile's — lighter, quicker, retreating fast toward the market's heart. Tuck's berth, when we got back to it, was empty, his blanket still warm, and folded neatly on top of it, a page torn from a cheap notebook with a single line in a boy's careful hand:

*Gone to make sure we all get paid. Don't follow me.*
