## 9. Voss Closes In

The photograph arrived in a tin of river caviar, which Bram Voss considered a small joke by whoever had packed it, and he did not appreciate small jokes.

He sat in the back room of the Gilded Eel, a floating gambling house moored three days downstream of Marrow Bend, and turned the tin over in his hands before opening it. The caviar inside had gone warm and oily. Beneath it, wrapped in waxed cloth, lay six photographs — small, silvered, the kind a pawnshop camera produced for identification papers — each showing a page of a ledger held flat against what looked like a crate lid by two dirty thumbs.

Voss put on his reading spectacles. He did this slowly, the way he did everything, because he had learned at fifty-one that haste was a currency the poor spent and the rich hoarded.

The handwriting on the pages was cramped, slanting, the hand of a man writing against time in bad light. *Elias Hartwell, Expedition Ledger, Third Entry.* Voss knew the name the way a debt collector knows a name — as a sum outstanding. Twenty years ago Elias Hartwell had walked into the upper Ansel country with a syndicate charter in his pocket and walked out of history instead. The syndicate had written him off as dead, written off the expedition as a failure, and quietly written off the considerable money they had advanced him. Voss had been a junior man then, the one who'd carried the termination papers to Hartwell's wife. He remembered her hands. He remembered the boy in the doorway, maybe six or seven, watching him with the flat stare children give to weather.

Now the boy was a man, and the man was pulling Hartwell's ledger out of a flood, and the syndicate's interest in Elias Hartwell had been resurrected by a single word that had come down the chain three months ago: *Vessia.*

Voss turned the first photograph toward the lamp.

The page described a confluence — the Ansel meeting an unnamed tributary that Hartwell had labeled, in a different ink, *the Vein*. Beyond it, a gorge. Beyond the gorge, a notation that had been half torn away with the rest of the ledger, but the surviving fragment read: *the river does not begin where the maps say it begins. It begins where it was told to stop.*

Voss read that line four times.

He had been in the memory trade for thirty years. He preferred the term himself — *the memory trade* — though the men who employed him preferred *provenance services* and the people he took things from preferred other words entirely. He had recovered looted manuscripts in Kessarin, authenticated a forged crown for a client who wanted it real more than he wanted it true, and once spent eleven months tracking a single Vessian coin across four borders because a collector in the capital had offered a sum that would have bought the village the coin was dug out of, entire, with its church and its graveyard thrown in.

That was the thing about Vessia that the pamphlets never explained. It was not that the country had been erased. Countries were erased all the time; Voss had watched three of them go in his lifetime, and the maps always adjusted, and the rivers kept running. What made Vessia valuable — what made it worth a syndicate, worth armed men, worth Voss himself leaving his rooms in the capital with their good light and their quiet — was that Vessia had been erased *badly*. Sloppily. Someone, twenty years ago or two hundred, had decided that the erasure should be complete, and had failed so thoroughly that the failure itself had become the treasure. Every map that omitted it was a map with a hole. And collectors, Voss knew, did not pay for objects. They paid for holes.

He laid the six photographs out on the table in sequence and looked at the route they described.

Confluence. Gorge. Then a page that was mostly water-stained but showed a sketch of a bridge — a fortified crossing, drawn with the loving precision of a man who had been made to wait at it. Then a page of botanical notes in a second hand, a woman's or a careful man's, listing herbs with Vessian names beside them. And the last photograph, the one Tuck had clearly saved for last, the one the boy must have understood was worth the most: a page where Hartwell's handwriting changed. It got larger. Looser. Almost joyful.

*Day 41. Sefa's people — I will not write the name, there are ears in every reed — have agreed. The compass works. God forgive me, the compass works.*

Voss took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve and did not put them back on.

He knew, of course, that the photographs had come from inside the crew. The tin had been left with a fish-seller who owed the Gilded Eel money, with instructions that reached him through two intermediaries, and the instructions had included a demand: *pardon, passage, and no names.* The handwriting on the note was young and uneven, pressed too hard, the writing of someone who had learned to write in fear. The photographs themselves were amateur — thumbprints in the corner, bad light, one page shot at an angle — but they were complete. Six pages. The boy had been thorough.

A thorough boy. A frightened boy. Voss had been both, once, and he recognized the combination the way a locksmith recognizes a lock he'd built himself.

* * *

He found the boy three days later, because of course he did. That was the part of the work people never understood — that finding people was not a matter of skill. It was a matter of patience and of understanding that frightened people always, always moved toward the thing they were frightened of.

Tuck was in the floating market at Halfway Reach, and he was not hiding. He was working a crowd of pilgrims with a shell game so old it should have been in a museum, and he was good at it — Voss watched him work three marks in twenty minutes, light-fingered, quick-smiled, reading the crowd the way a helmsman reads water — and he was good at it *here*, in the open, in the exact town where a syndicate agent would come looking. Because being visible was a kind of armor. Because a boy who had sold photographs of a dead man's ledger needed to believe he was still in control of something, and the only thing he could control was the shell game.

Voss waited until the crowd thinned, then sat down across the upturned crate the boy was using as a table and placed a single silver coin on it.

"Three shells," Voss said. "One coin. Show me."

Tuck's eyes came up, and Voss watched the recognition happen — not of his face, which meant nothing to the boy, but of his clothes, his hands, the quality of his silence. Boys like this one could read a man's purse from across a room. Voss watched him calculate, watched him decide that refusing a customer was worse than serving one, watched him pick up the shells.

The boy was good. He was genuinely good. The coin vanished and reappeared and vanished, and Voss lost track of it twice, and on the third reveal the coin was under the left shell and Tuck was smiling with his whole face, the smile of a boy who had just beaten a rich man in front of witnesses.

"Keep it," Voss said, and stood, and said, quietly, only for the boy: "Walk with me or I'll have the harbor watch search your bedding. I understand you've been carrying photographs."

The smile died the way a lamp dies — all at once, no flicker.

They walked. The market at Halfway Reach was built on eleven barges lashed together and had been there, according to the sign, for ninety years, which meant it had outlasted three governments and would outlast a fourth. Voss bought two cups of tea from a woman frying dough and handed one to the boy, who did not take it, then did.

"Six photographs," Voss said. "Good ones. You held back the last page until the end, which shows sense — a seller who gives everything upfront has nothing left to bargain with. I paid your price through the fish-seller. I'm not here about the money."

"You're here to kill me."

"I'm here because you're the only person on that crew who understands what he's part of." Voss sipped his tea. It was terrible. He drank it anyway. "Hartwell is a sentimentalist. The woman Ferran is a mercenary with a debt she's dressing up as loyalty. The Vessian girl — yes, I know about the herbalist — believes she's saving a nation. None of them know what they're carrying. You're the only one who looked at those pages and thought: *this has a price.* That's not treachery, boy. That's clarity."

Tuck said nothing, but he drank the tea, and his hands had stopped shaking, which Voss noted with professional satisfaction.

"I want to offer you employment," Voss said.

"I'm not going to photograph them again."

"You won't need to. I know the route. Confluence, gorge, toll bridge — the bridge is the interesting one, because the toll-keeper there is in my pocket, and when your crew trades something precious to cross, I will know exactly what they had and exactly what they kept. I know about the compass, boy. I've known about the compass since before you were born." He let that sit. "What I need from you is simpler. When they reach the source — and they will reach it, because Hartwell's blood is a key and keys turn — I need a man inside the walls. A small man. A man nobody counts. A man who can open a gate."

"And if I say no?"

"Then nothing happens to you. I want to be very clear about that." Voss stopped walking and turned to face the boy fully, and he let his face do the thing it did when he chose to be honest, which was rare enough that people found it frightening. "I don't threaten children. I've found it produces poor work. Here is what happens instead: nothing. I leave you on this market, and Hartwell's crew goes on, and one night you'll notice the woman Ferran looking at you a certain way, because she's a smuggler and smugglers can smell a seller, and you'll never know if she knows. And you'll spend every night after that waiting. That's what happens if you say no. You'll be safe and you'll be alone, and you'll be sixteen forever, because boys who burn their boats at sixteen never learn to build new ones."

The boy's face did something complicated. Voss watched it and thought: *there it is.* Not greed. Not fear, or not only fear. The thing underneath, the engine. The boy had sold the photographs not for money — the price he'd asked was modest, almost apologetic — but because he believed, with the whole flat certainty of the abandoned, that he would be left. That everyone leaves, or everyone is left, and better to be the one holding the photographs when it happens.

Voss knew that belief intimately. He had built a career on the roof of it.

"What's the pardon?" Tuck said. His voice had gone careful, a bargainer's voice, and Voss almost admired him. "You said pardon. Pardon from what?"

"From the syndicate. You have debts — the same creditors as Hartwell, I believe, which is not a coincidence, because Hartwell has been buying other people's debts for years, quietly, the way he does everything quietly. Those debts are held by syndicate paper. I can burn them. All of them. A clean name, at your age, is worth more than the coin under any shell." Voss resumed walking, and the boy fell into step beside him without deciding to. "And passage. Anywhere. With money enough to arrive as someone new."

"And in exchange I open a gate."

"In exchange you open a gate, and no one is hurt who doesn't make it necessary. I'll be honest with you, because you'll hear lies enough on that crew: there will be armed men, and the Vessians will not welcome us, and some of it will be ugly. That's the trade. That's every trade." Voss finished the tea and set the cup on a bollard. "You don't have to answer now. You'll answer at the source, when the moment comes, and I'll know your answer by whether the gate opens. That's the whole contract. No signatures. Signatures are for people who intend to keep records, and I don't intend for any of this to have happened."

They had come back around to the shell game. The crate was still there. The coin was still on it, and a pair of dockhands were eyeing it, and Tuck moved toward it automatically, and then stopped.

"His ledger," the boy said, not turning around. "The father. What happened to him?"

Voss considered lying, and decided against it, for the reason he had decided against threats: lies produced poor work.

"Twenty years ago he went up the Ansel with our charter and stopped sending reports. We sent men. The men came back with nothing. The syndicate declared him dead and his expedition void and took the loss." He paused. "What the photographs suggest, boy, is that he found what he was looking for and decided the world didn't deserve the address. Which makes him either a saint or a thief, depending on who you ask. The syndicate asks: thief."

"And what do you ask?"

Voss smiled, and it was not the smile he used at negotiations. "I ask what a man saw, up a dead river, that made him give up his own child to keep it. Because that's the real treasure, boy. Not the city. Not the artifacts. The *reason*. And I have wanted to know it for twenty years, which is longer than you've been alive, and I will know it, and then I will decide what it was."

He left the boy there, and the boy picked up the shells, and by the time Voss reached the end of the market the shell game was running again and the dockhands were losing money and laughing, and Tuck's face was quick and bright and completely unreadable, which was, Voss thought, the boy's real gift, and the reason he would open the gate.

* * *

That night, in his cabin on the *Charitable Interest* — a salvage tug, armed, flying the flag of a dredging company that existed on paper — Voss wrote two letters.

The first was to the syndicate, and it was brief. *Ledger authenticated. Route confirmed. Asset inside the crew. Expect the source within the month. Recommend the second team stand ready at the toll bridge.* He sealed it and gave it to his fastest runner with instructions to travel by road, not river, and to burn the reply unread if it contained the word *abandon*, because he had no intention of reading it.

The second letter took him longer.

It was to no one. It was the letter he wrote at the start of every job, the one that never got sent, the one that lived in the false bottom of his writing case with thirty others like it, going back twenty years. In it he set down, in plain language, what he wanted and why, because Voss had learned that a man who could not say why in private would invent reasons in public, and invented reasons got people killed.

*What I want,* he wrote, *is the thing Hartwell found. Not the city. Cities are stone. I want the moment a man looks at the world he was given and decides it can be refused. I have never refused anything. I have collected, catalogued, sold, and preserved, and every door I ever opened was opened for someone else's collection. Hartwell closed a door on the entire world and kept the key in his son's pocket. I want to stand in that doorway. I want to know what a man breathes on the other side that we don't have here.*

He looked at what he'd written and, after a moment, added:

*I am fifty-one years old. I tell myself this is the last acquisition. I have told myself that eleven times. The boy on the crew is sixteen and already knows how to sell the people feeding him, and I hired him, and I cannot decide whether that makes me a monster or the only honest man he's ever met. Both, probably. Both is usually the answer.*

*If the Vessians are real — and they are real, the photographs say so, the compass says so, the hole in every map for two hundred years says so — then the syndicate will strip them the way we stripped the Kessarin libraries, and I will be the man who held the lantern. I have held the lantern before. I have never once looked into the room.*

He sealed the letter and put it in the false bottom with the others, and he did not reread the older ones, because rereading them would mean noticing that they all said the same thing, and that the same thing had never once been satisfied.

Above him, on deck, the night watch called the hour, and the river went on moving in the dark — north, toward the gorge, toward the bridge he owned, toward the source that two hundred years of cartographers had agreed did not exist.

Voss climbed up and stood at the rail. Somewhere upriver, in the black, a boy was deciding, a smuggler was watching, a Vessian girl was leading them through country that erased people, and a dead man's son was carrying a compass that pointed at the one thing in the world Bram Voss had never been able to buy.

"Full steam at dawn," he told the mate. "And wake me if anything floats down."

Nothing floated down. But in the morning, at the toll bridge forty miles north, the toll-keeper's first message of the day arrived by pigeon, and it read, in full:

*They're coming. Four of them. The boy is asking the ferry crews about the gorge. And the woman Ferran bought lamp oil — enough for a long way.*
