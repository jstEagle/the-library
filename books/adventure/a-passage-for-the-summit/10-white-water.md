## 10. White Water

Odile Ferran had run the Ansel for eleven years, and in that time she had learned one rule above all others: the river did not negotiate.

She stood at the tiller of the *Marrow Maid* and watched the sky go the color of wet slate, and she thought about how many times she'd broken her own rules for money, for pride, for people who didn't deserve it. The barometer — a chipped brass thing bolted to the wheel housing, older than Tuck — had been falling since noon. By three it had fallen off the bottom of its scale entirely, which was the instrument's way of saying *you are an idiot for still being on the water.*

"We should tie up," Silas said beside her. He'd been reading the current the way he always did, lips slightly parted, like a man listening to a conversation just below hearing. "There's a cutbank two miles down. High ground behind it."

"There's no two miles," Odile said. "Listen."

He listened. So did Tuck, who came up from the hold with his hair full of sawdust and his face already answering the question. Beneath the wind, beneath the engine's laboring knock, there was a sound like a freight train running under the earth. A long, continuous roar, coming from ahead, from around the bend where the valley pinched shut between black cliffs.

"That's not the gorge," Tuck said.

"That's the gorge full," Odile said. "Storm water. The whole upper watershed is dumping into us at once." She looked at the sky again, doing the arithmetic she hated. Behind them: Voss, or men like him, close enough now that they'd found their abandoned campfire at the toll bridge still warm. Ahead: rapids that in normal water were rated as a hard day's work and in flood water were a coffin with scenery. Above them: rain coming down so hard it bounced.

"Options," Silas said.

"Run it, or climb out and walk, and lose the boat and everything in it, and let whoever's behind us find us on foot in open country with no supplies." She spat over the side, a habit she'd never managed to kill. "So we run it."

Tuck made a small sound. Not quite a word. Odile had heard that sound before, from hired hands and desperate passengers, and she knew what it meant: *I am sixteen years old and I would like to register a complaint.*

"You can swim?" she asked him.

"Badly."

"Then hold onto something that floats and don't let go until your feet touch gravel. That's the whole religion." She turned to Silas. "You take the bow. I need weight forward and I need eyes on the channel. When I call the line, you tell me if I'm wrong. You know this water better than you think you do."

"I grew up on the lower river," he said. "This is different."

"It's all the same river, Hartwell. It's just louder up here." She put her hand on the throttle. "Everything you love is about to try to kill you. Try to be polite about it."

She opened the engine up and drove the *Marrow Maid* into the mouth of the storm.

* * *

The first mile was survivable, which is how rivers lie to you.

They ran standing waves the size of barns, the bow climbing and slamming down in explosions of spray that hit like thrown gravel. Tuck lashed himself to the cargo net amidships and screamed what might have been prayers or profanity; from the sound of it, he hadn't decided. Silas worked the bow pole, calling hazards back to her in a voice gone flat and fast — *rock right, hole left, pull right, PULL RIGHT* — and she pulled, and the old girl answered, wallowing and groaning but answering, and Odile allowed herself the dangerous luxury of thinking they might actually do this.

The river heard her thinking it.

Around the fifth bend the canyon narrowed to less than a boat-length and a half, and the water, having nowhere else to go, went up. She saw it before she could name it — a line across the entire channel, smooth and green and utterly wrong, like a door lying flat on the surface of the world. A pour-over. A ledge drowned under six feet of flood, and beyond it nothing but churning white where the river dropped into chaos.

"No line through that," Silas shouted. "There's no line!"

"There's always a line!" she screamed back, and even as she said it she knew it was a lie, the kind of lie you tell a river because the truth is too heavy to carry at speed.

The *Marrow Maid* hit the ledge beam-on.

It wasn't a collision so much as an erasure. One moment there was a boat, and Odile at the wheel, and twenty years of her life lashed to the deck; the next moment the world was white noise and cold that went through her ribs like nails, and she was underwater, tumbling, with no idea which way the surface was.

She'd been drowning twice before. Once at nine, in a flooded quarry, saved by a cousin who never let her forget it. Once at thirty-one, in the Ansel itself, in water much like this — but that memory lived in a sealed room in her head, behind a door she had painted over so many times it no longer looked like a door. Both times, someone had pulled her out. She remembered thinking, both times, with a clarity that felt obscene: *so this is what it costs other people, loving me.*

Now the river rolled her like laundry. Her left boot snagged something — a plank, a rope, the wreck of her own boat — and held, and the current pulled the rest of her against the anchor of her own ankle, and she felt her leg bend in a direction legs don't go, and the pain arrived a half-second later, enormous and almost grateful, because pain meant she was still alive to feel it.

*Don't breathe,* she told herself. The oldest rule. *Don't breathe.*

Her lungs disagreed. Her lungs staged a mutiny. And just as the black started closing in from the edges of everything —

— hands. Hands on her collar, hauling, brutal, certain. The world tore open into gray light and driving rain and air, and she coughed half the river out of herself and hung from the grip of someone kneeling on a gravel bar, dragging her by the shoulders like landed fish.

Silas Hartwell's face swam into focus above her, streaming wet, bloodied along the jaw, wearing an expression she had never seen on him before. Not fear. Something past fear. Fury, maybe, at the river, at her, at the sheer unfairness of nearly losing something he hadn't yet admitted he wanted.

"Don't you dare," he said.

"Don't I dare what?" she croaked.

"Dare anything. Just — don't." He got his arms under her and carried her above the waterline, which should have humiliated her and instead felt like being set down inside a warm coat. Behind him, Tuck came stumbling along the bank, soaked, bleeding from a dozen small cuts, clutching the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like an infant. The boy had saved the ledger. Of course he had. That boy would save the thing that was going to get them all killed, because saving things was the only way he knew to be worth keeping.

Odile lay on the gravel and laughed until it turned into coughing, and let the rain hit her face, and took inventory. Leg: broken, probably, below the knee, screaming now that she'd given it permission. Boat: gone. She could see pieces of it strung downriver like a child's abandoned toys — a hatch cover spinning in an eddy, the brass barometer wedged in stones, pointing uselessly at nothing. Cargo: whatever Tuck held and whatever the river chose to return. Crew: three, alive.

Twenty years of work, gone in four seconds of white water.

And underneath the grief, quiet as silt settling, another feeling she refused to look at directly: relief. Terrible, shameful relief. Because the boat had been the last thing tying her to the woman she'd been before the Ansel, before the debt, before the man named Hartwell who had walked into her life and paid for her sins without ever once asking her to explain them.

"Your leg," Silas said. He'd already stripped off his jacket and was wrapping the break with splints improvised from a cargo slat and the laces of his own boots, working fast, working gently, not looking at her face while he did it because he understood, somehow, that being seen in this particular position was worse than the pain.

"Do that tighter," she said. "I've had worse."

"Have you?"

She looked at him. Rain ran down his face and he didn't blink it away. There was a question in him that wasn't about the leg at all, and she realized, lying there with her bones rearranged and her livelihood floating downstream, that the sealed room in her head had flooded along with everything else, and the door she'd painted over was standing open, and there was nothing left in her strong enough to shut it.

"Tuck," she called. "Get a fire going. Anything dry. Go."

The boy went, gratefully, and then it was just the two of them and the roar of the river and the dark coming down early through the rain.

* * *

They got a fire lit under an overhang — Tuck's one undisputed genius, coaxing flame out of soaked wood through sheer stubbornness — and Odile sat propped against the stone with her leg splinted straight and a blanket around her shoulders, watching Silas turn his father's compass over in his fingers. The needle was behaving strangely. It kept swinging, hesitating, swinging back, like a dog scenting something on the wind. He didn't seem to notice. Or he noticed and didn't say.

"You almost died today," he said finally. "Second time on this river, you said. Back at the gorge you said 'the river's taken me twice and I'm still here.' You never told me the first time."

"Didn't I?"

"No."

She fed a stick to the fire. Across the flames, Tuck had fallen asleep mid-sentence, curled against the pack, one hand still hooked through the strap. Trusting them, in his way. God help him.

"The first time," Odile said, "I was thirty-one. Running guns, if you want the clean version. Contraband, mostly — medicine, sometimes, when the mood took me, but mostly things that went bang. I had a partner named Corvo who was very good at smiling at customs men and very bad at reading water. We were running the Ansel at night, low water, loaded too heavy, because Corvo had promised the buyer delivery by the new moon and I was young enough to think promises mattered more than hydrology."

She paused. The fire popped. Somewhere upstream, the flood was still talking to itself.

"We wrapped on a wing dam near the old ferry crossing. Boat broke up just like today. Corvo drowned — I watched him go under with a crate of rifles strapped to his back, because he wouldn't let go of the merchandise. I went under too. Snagged, tangled, the whole business. And a man came out of the dark after me. Waded into floodwater at night with no rope, no light, nothing but his own stupid arms, and pulled me out the way you pulled me out today. Same grip on the collar. Same fury."

Silas had gone very still. "That was him."

"That was Elias Hartwell." His name in her mouth tasted like river water — like drowning and breathing at once. "The disgraced archaeologist. The thief, according to every journal in the country. The man whose picture was on the wall of every customs office between Marrow Bend and the sea. And he fished me out of the water, dragged me ashore, built me a fire, splinted my wrist — I'd broken it on the rocks — and sat with me until morning. And do you know what he said? All night, the whole night, he asked me questions. About the river. About the gorges upstream. About whether the old ferry paths still existed, whether anyone still remembered the names of the drowned villages. He wasn't interested in my crimes. He wasn't interested in turning me in. He was mapping, Odile, in his head, the whole time — and I was so grateful and so stupid that I answered every question he asked."

The compass needle swung in Silas's hand. He looked down at it, then back at her.

"He used what I told him," she said. "A year later, when the expedition went upriver and vanished, I understood. Every path I'd described, every crossing, every safe harbor — that was his route. I gave a dead man his map and I've spent twenty years telling myself it doesn't matter, because he was already dead to everyone anyway, because what difference does it make who shows a ghost the road." Her voice cracked, and she hated it, and kept going. "But here's the part I never told anyone. Here's the part that's yours, if you want it."

"Tell me."

"After he pulled me out — before dawn, before he left — I swore I'd repay him. I'm a smuggler, Hartwell, not a savage. Debts are the only scripture I keep. And I had chances. Years of chances. I found his son's name in a debtor's roll in Marrow Bend and I did nothing. I heard rumors he'd survived, that he'd gone to ground somewhere up the Ansel, and I did nothing, because going to look meant admitting the route existed, meant drawing attention to the one place in the world he might actually be safe. I told myself my silence was protection." She laughed, short and ugly. "It was cowardice wearing protection's coat. I owed that man my life and I paid him back with twenty years of staying comfortable."

Silas was quiet for a long time. The fire burned down. The rain eased from a hammering to a steady whisper, and through the overhang's mouth they could see the river running high and fast and indifferent, carrying the last sticks of the *Marrow Maid* toward the sea.

"You think he needed repaying," Silas said at last.

"What?"

"My father." Silas turned the compass over. "Everyone who talks about him talks about what he took. The artifacts, the funding, the reputation. Nobody ever says what he gave. But you just did — you told me he waded into floodwater for a stranger with no rope. That's not a man collecting debts. That's a man paying them. Whatever he was running from, whatever he'd done — he was spending the rest of his life trying to hand something back." He looked up, and in the firelight his eyes were wet, though whether from smoke or grief she couldn't say and didn't ask. "Maybe the route wasn't the payment. Maybe you were. Maybe he asked you all those questions because he liked you. Because you were the first person in years who talked to him like he was a man instead of a headline."

Odile stared at him. Something in her chest that had been clenched for two decades loosened, painfully, like a fist uncurling around broken glass.

"That's a generous read," she managed.

"I'm a generous man. Ask anyone." A ghost of a smile. "Ask the creditors."

She laughed — really laughed, wet and cracked — and reached over and gripped his shoulder, hard, the way you grip a rail in rough water. "All right, Hartwell. Here's the new arrangement. You pulled me out of the river today. That makes us even, you and me. Your father's account is closed. Mine starts fresh." She nodded at the compass in his hand, at the needle still trembling toward the dark upstream. "Which means tomorrow, when we start walking, we walk as crew. No debts. No passengers. Whatever's up that river — your father, my ghosts, Vessia, all of it — we see it through together, and afterward nobody owes anybody anything."

"And Tuck?"

"Tuck's crew too. Even if he's carrying secrets in that pack besides the ledger." She said it lightly, watching the boy sleep, filing it away where she filed everything. "We'll deal with that when it surfaces."

Silas followed her glance, frowned faintly, and let it go. Then the needle moved.

Not swung — *moved*, deliberate, dragging against the compass card like a finger writing, and settled pointing dead upstream into the black throat of the gorge. Silas held it flat on his palm and stared at it, and Odile watched the color leave his face.

"It's never done that," he said quietly. "In twenty years, it's never done that."

Upstream, through the thinning rain, the river roared on toward some hidden origin — and the needle pointed at it, steady now, unwavering, like a thing that had finally been given permission to speak.
