## 11. The Toll Bridge

They smelled the crossing before they saw it — woodsmoke and tar and something sweeter underneath, like rotting fruit left in the sun. Odile called it the Toll's perfume. "Money," she said, spitting over the gunwale. "It always smells a little like something dying."

The bridge spanned the Ansel where the river narrowed to half its width, pinned between two cliffs the color of old iron. It was not a bridge in any honest sense of the word — more a fortress that happened to have planks laid across it, a sprawl of palisades and watchtowers and tin-roofed shacks climbing both banks, connected by a single span wide enough for one cart and no arguments. Chains hung from the towers at either end, thick as a man's thigh, ready to be raised out of the water and stop anything that tried to run the gap.

Above the tollhouse, on a pole like a flag, hung a windsock made of faded red cloth. Under it, smaller banners: the marks of every crew that had paid here and been allowed to remember it.

"Kestrel's Crossing," Tuck said, reading the sign nailed crookedly above the water gate. He'd been quiet since the gorge, quieter even than usual, and Silas had noticed him counting things — fuel cans, food tins, the coins in Odile's lockbox — with the grim arithmetic of someone who already knew the sum came up short.

"You've been here before," Silas said.

"Everyone's been here before." Tuck didn't look at him. "You pay or you turn around. There's no third door."

There was a third door, actually. Silas could see it from the boat as they drifted toward the chains — a low iron grate set into the cliff below the eastern tower, half-drowned, through which the river slid into some kind of culvert. A smuggler's entrance, probably. Probably watched. Probably a story people told each other right up until the crossbow bolts arrived.

Odile brought them alongside the western landing with the easy contempt of a woman who had been extorted by professionals her whole life. She tied off, straightened her coat, and said, "Rules. I talk. You don't. If anyone asks what we're carrying, we're carrying salt and regret. Tuck, hands visible. Silas—"

"I know. Invisible."

"Worse than invisible. Boring. Be boring."

The tollmaster received them in a room built entirely of things other people had surrendered. That was Silas's first thought and he couldn't shake it: the walls paneled in carved headboards, the shelves lined with wedding silver and sextants and a child's rocking horse, painted blue, its rocker worn smooth by small feet that were somewhere else now. A taxidermied heron stood in the corner with glass eyes that followed you whether you believed they did or not.

The tollmaster himself was a narrow man in a green coat, clean-shaven, with the patient face of someone who had heard every plea ever constructed and found them all structurally unsound. His name, stenciled on the desk, was M. Corvane. He did not offer a first name. He did not offer tea.

"Odile Ferran." He said it the way a doctor reads a chart. "Last through four years ago. You owed me nothing then. That's rare enough to be memorable."

"I like to leave debts behind me," Odile said. "Not ahead."

"And this time?"

"This time we're paying."

Corvane laced his fingers. Behind him, through a window slit, Silas could see the far tower and the men on it, and beyond them the river going on north into country none of the maps bothered to draw properly. Twenty years ago his father had come through a place like this. Maybe this exact place. Had he been boring? Had he kept his hands visible?

"Passage for three, upstream, return passage guaranteed or not," Corvane said. "Standard rate is forty crowns a head, plus cargo assessment. Let's see the cargo."

"We're carrying salt."

"Then you won't mind if my assessor confirms that."

The assessor was a boy of maybe sixteen with a ledger of his own and the flat, incurious manner of a customs official everywhere in the world. He went through the *Vespera* hold by lamplight while they all stood in the cold and pretended not to care. Salt, yes — Odile had laid real sacks of it over everything else, and under the salt, wrapped in oilcloth and hidden in the bilge where the boy's lamp barely reached: the spare tools, the medicine kit, Sefa's bundled herbs, and the chest.

The chest rode with them still. Silas had argued for leaving it downstream, cached somewhere only he could find again, but Sefa had looked at him with an expression he was beginning to dread — the expression of a woman deciding how much truth a stranger could carry without spilling — and said, "If the syndicate finds it buried in mud outside Marrow Bend, your town pays for it. If it stays with us, only we do." So it stayed. It lay under the salt now like a sleeping animal, and Silas found himself holding his breath until the assessor's boots moved past it.

The boy finished, wrote something, and told Corvane: "Salt. Some personal effects. Nothing assessed."

Corvane raised an eyebrow at Odile.

"I told you," she said.

"You told me salt. The personal effects are new information." He opened a drawer — Silas braced for a weapon and it was only paper, a tariff schedule, printed, official-looking, the kind of document that existed to make extortion feel like law. "Personal effects are assessable. Especially—" and here Corvane's eyes did something Silas didn't like, a small bright flicker, a fish turning in shallow water, "—especially when crews come through asking after river iron."

The word landed in the room and sat there.

Silas kept his face doing nothing at all. Beside him, Tuck went very carefully still, the stillness of a boy who had learned that motion attracts eyes. Odile laughed, one short bark, unimpressed. "River iron. That's the second time this month somebody's said that to me. First time was a man with soft hands and a syndicate pin, and I told him the same thing I'll tell you: I haul salt and salvage. If there's treasure on this river, it's upstream of my ambitions."

"Mm." Corvane turned a page he clearly wasn't reading. "A man came through yesterday. Soft hands. Paid double to have word sent ahead of him. Described a woman matching you, a boy, and a tall salvager with river shoulders." He looked up. "That's you, isn't it. River shoulders."

"It's a common build on a river."

"It is." Corvane closed the tariff schedule. "Here's what happens now. You can pay the standard rate, forty crowns a head, and go on your way, and my men will remember nothing about you because my men forget everything when they're paid to. Or you can argue with me about the personal effects, and I'll start remembering things, and the next soft-handed man who comes through will hear a very complete description. Including the boat. Including the herb smell. Including—" his gaze touched Silas, light as a fly landing, "—whatever's under the salt."

Nobody breathed.

"The rate," Odile said finally, "is robbery."

"The rate is the rate. Robbery is what happens to people who can't pay it." Corvane spread his hands, a reasonable man in a room full of dead people's possessions. "Forty crowns a head. One hundred twenty. Plus the assessment fee for the personal effects, call it thirty more. One hundred fifty crowns, and the road is yours."

They had, Silas knew without checking, sixty-one crowns, most of them Tuck's, which was its own small wound.

* * *

They counted it out on the landing planks in the gray afternoon light, and it came to sixty-one, and the wind off the river smelled like money dying.

"There's the culvert," Tuck said, for the fourth time. "Under the east tower. I could pick the chain housing. I've done worse."

"You've never done worse," Odile said. "You've done different and gotten lucky. And if they catch you, they don't put boys in their debt cages, they put them in the river." She rubbed her eyes. She'd been rubbing them a lot since the gorge, since the fuel went into the current along with nearly everything else. "We're short eighty-nine crowns. We can't fight, we can't sneak, so we sell."

"Sell what?" Tuck asked. "We don't have anything worth eighty-nine crowns. That's the whole problem with us."

And that was true, mostly, except that Silas had been thinking about it since Corvane's window-slit eyes had flickered, and the answer had been sitting against his ribs the entire conversation, in oilcloth, in the bottom of his pack, where it had ridden for eleven days and twenty years.

His father's journal pages.

He hadn't shown them to anyone. Not Odile, not Tuck, not even Sefa, though she had watched him too closely once when he'd checked on them during a rain and he'd known she'd guessed. They were the heart of the torn ledger — the pages Aldous Hartwell had sewn into the lining of his coat before whatever had happened to him happened, recovered from the chest along with the compass, written in a hand that started confident and grew hurried and then, near the end, grew strange. Currents measured in numbers. Sketches of the gorge. A passage about a valley that read less like geography and more like a prayer. And marginalia in a second hand, older, that Silas had never been able to read fully — Vessian script, Sefa had said, breathing like the pages were hot.

To a collector, those pages were worth more than the boat. More than the boat and the salt and all of them together. To Bram Voss, they were worth anything.

"They're worth eighty-nine crowns to exactly one kind of buyer," Silas said.

Odile went still. "No."

"They're worth it to Corvane, who sells to that kind of buyer. Or they're worth it to the soft-handed man, who's a day behind us and will pay triple to not have to chase us." He heard his own voice and hated how level it was, how much it sounded like a man pricing cargo. "It's paper, Odile."

"It's not paper and you know it's not paper."

"It's the only thing on this boat that is worth eighty-nine crowns, and we need eighty-nine crowns, and the river doesn't care what it costs us because the river has never once cared." He was surprised to find his hands were steady. Everything else felt like it was coming apart quietly, like ice on a warm day, from the inside. "I'll keep the compass. They're just pages. The words are in my head. Most of them."

*Most of them.* Even as he said it he knew it was a lie, the comfortable kind, the kind you tell standing at the edge of a thing. The marginalia he couldn't read. The sketches he'd memorized but couldn't reproduce — the way his father drew currents was a language of its own, arrows within arrows, a grammar of water that lived in the strokes themselves. Sell the pages and he sold the handwriting, and the handwriting was the closest thing to his father's voice that existed anywhere on earth.

"That's the debt talking," Odile said quietly. "Your father bought my life with his. You don't owe this river his ghost too."

"He's not a ghost. He's a choice I keep making every morning not to think about." Silas shouldered his pack. "Watch the boat."

* * *

Corvane's office had not changed, except that the heron seemed closer.

"Eighty-nine crowns' worth of personal effects," Silas said, and unwrapped the oilcloth on the desk between them.

He watched Corvane's face do what faces do when they encounter real value: nothing, immediately, completely. But the tollmaster's hands — the hands gave him away. They hovered over the pages without touching, the way people hover over fire.

"Expedition papers," Corvane said. "Upstream survey work. Where did you get these?"

"My father was a surveyor."

"Aldous Hartwell was many things." Corvane said the name gently, almost kindly, and that kindness was the worst thing that had happened to Silas all day. "The syndicate would pay you five hundred crowns for these, you understand. Possibly more. There are men south of here who frame things like this on their walls."

"Then why aren't you offering me five hundred?"

"Because the soft-handed man offered me a finder's fee for information, not inventory, and I intend to stay a tollmaster rather than become a target." Corvane finally touched the top page, one fingertip, reverent and quick. "Eighty-nine crowns. And I'll tell you something for free, since you're being honest with me, which is rarer than the papers. The man behind you isn't alone anymore. He picked up boatmen at the gorge mouth — six of them, armed, and a fast hull. He's maybe eighteen hours back. If you're through my bridge tonight, you might stay ahead of him. If you're arguing with me tomorrow, you won't."

Silas nodded. His throat had closed around something and he worked it loose. "The compass stays with me."

"I don't want the compass." Corvane was already sliding the pages into a leather folder, already done with him, already a man handling merchandise. "Compasses are common. Fathers are rare." He glanced up, and for one moment the merchant fell away and something almost human looked out. "My own died owing money in a town like this one. I built this place so I'd never be the son at the counter again. You're sure about this."

"No," Silas said honestly.

Corvane almost smiled. He counted out eighty-nine crowns in mixed coin, pushed it across the desk, and kept the folder under his palm like a man keeping a hand on a dog that might bolt.

At the door, Silas stopped. He didn't mean to. His body simply refused to leave without saying something, though he had nothing to say, no words that would make any difference to a tin-roofed fortress full of other people's surrendered lives.

"The rocking horse," he heard himself ask. "In the corner. Blue. Who did it belong to?"

Corvane was silent a moment. "A family that couldn't pay," he said. "Everything in this room belonged to someone who couldn't pay. That's what a toll is. Now get off my bridge before I charge you for the question."

* * *

They raised no chain for them. The coins changed hands, the gate opened, and the *Vespera* slid through the narrows into the long northern reach of the Ansel as the light went copper and then gray.

Tuck counted the remaining coins twice and announced, unnecessarily, that they were rich now, in the voice of a boy trying to make a joke land in a room where nobody was laughing. Odile took the tiller and said nothing for a long time, which from Odile was a eulogy.

Sefa sat down beside Silas in the bow, where he was watching the cliffs slide past, and didn't speak either. That was the thing about Sefa — she understood that silence could be a gift or a weapon, and she always chose gift.

"You kept the compass," she said at last.

"It was the only part that was mine."

"It was never only yours. That's what I keep trying to tell you, and you keep not hearing it." She pulled her shawl tighter. "The needle behaves strangely near certain stone. My grandmother told stories of a compass that pointed home instead of north. When we make camp, I want to see it near the walls again. Near the places your father's pages described — the ones still in your head."

"In my head," Silas said. "That's all that's left of them."

"Words survive transfer better than you think." She looked at him sideways. "You recited the current-grammar to yourself last night in your sleep. Arrows within arrows. Odile thought you were praying."

Ahead, the river bent west into country that rose and darkened, cliffs giving way to forested slopes, and the first real cold of the season came down off the high ground and touched the water like a warning. Somewhere behind them, eighteen hours back, six armed men in a fast hull were buying the same current their coins had bought.

Silas put his hand in his pocket and closed it around the compass, its brass worn warm by twenty years of his grip, and felt the needle trembling inside like something alive, like something waiting, like something that had finally begun to lean — very slightly, very steadily — away from north.
