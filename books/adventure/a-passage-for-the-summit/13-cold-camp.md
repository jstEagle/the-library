## 13. Cold Camp

The snow started an hour after they lost the treeline, and by dark it had eaten the world.

Tuck crouched under the tarp Odile had rigged between two boulders, watching flakes come down fat and silent as ash. The fire was a fist-sized thing in a ring of stones, fed on the last of the scrub pine they'd carried up from the gorge, and it gave off more smoke than heat. His fingers wouldn't close all the way anymore. He kept flexing them anyway, because stopping felt like admitting something.

Behind him, Sefa was boiling snow in the dented pot, one handful at a time, because that was all the fire could manage. Each melt took ten minutes and produced half a cup of gray water that tasted like tin. They'd finished the last of the hard bread at noon. The last of the dried fish yesterday. Tuck's stomach had moved past growling into a kind of low permanent complaint, like a dog left out in weather.

"Rationing's done," Odile said. She stood at the edge of the tarp with her back to them, watching the slope where the snow came down out of nothing. "There's nothing left to ration."

Nobody answered. There wasn't anything to say to that.

Tuck looked at Silas. Silas sat against the boulder with his father's compass open in his palm, turning it over and over, the needle swinging slow and drunk and settling — always — toward the same point northeast, up through the whiteout, toward whatever was waiting for them at the end of this. He'd been doing that for hours. It was the kind of thing a man did when he didn't want to think about what his hands were telling him.

*He doesn't know,* Tuck thought. *Odile knows. Silas doesn't.*

That was the arithmetic he'd been carrying since the floating market, since he'd knelt in the shadow of the fuel crates with Sefa's borrowed camera and photographed every page of the ledger half while the others argued about propellant prices. Eleven exposures. He'd hidden the film spool inside the heel of his boot, wrapped in oilcloth, and told himself a hundred stories about why.

*Insurance.* That was the word he used. If they abandoned him — if Odile decided the kid who ate too much and talked too much and couldn't row worth a damn was dead weight on the white water — then Voss would pay for what Tuck knew. A pardon. Passage home. Maybe coin. Voss had said so himself, back in Marrow Bend, in that soft reasonable voice: *A boy your age shouldn't have to carry other people's debts.*

Except Voss had also, according to the ferrywoman at Drowned Steeple, put a price on anyone carrying Vessian artifacts, and prices like that got paid in blood, and Tuck had been carrying the ledger's face in his boot for six days while four people trusted him around a fire.

He looked at the fire. It looked back, small and dying.

"I'm going to say something," Tuck heard himself say.

His voice came out wrong — too loud in the hush of falling snow. Sefa looked up from her pot. Odile turned. Silas closed the compass.

"The pass closes tomorrow," Odile said. "Whatever it is, say it fast."

* * *

It didn't come out the way he'd rehearsed it. None of the versions he'd built in his head — the calm one, the strategic one, the one where he framed it as intelligence rather than betrayal. What came out was:

"I took pictures of the ledger."

The words fell into the snow between them and just lay there.

Sefa's hand went still on the pot handle. Steam curled up past her face. She didn't look surprised, exactly. She looked like someone confirming a diagnosis she'd already made.

"When," Odile said. Not a question. A demand with no question mark anywhere in it.

"At the market. While you were arguing with the fuel broker." Tuck's mouth kept going without permission from the rest of him. "All of it. Every page. I sold the film to Voss's man three days ago, at the toll bridge, before we crossed. I got — " He stopped. There was no version of the next part that helped. "I got passage home. That was the deal."

Silas hadn't moved. That was the worst part. Tuck had prepared for shouting, for Odile's knife, for Sefa's cold contempt. He had not prepared for Silas Hartwell sitting perfectly still with the compass closed in his lap, looking at him the way you'd look at water rising in a hull — not angry yet, just measuring how much time there was.

"You sold my father's handwriting," Silas said quietly, "to the man who's been following us since Marrow Bend."

"He was going to get it anyway. He had people everywhere, he — "

"That's not why you did it."

"No," Tuck admitted. His voice cracked on it. "I did it because I thought you'd leave me. Sooner or later. Everybody leaves, or everybody gets left, and I wanted — I wanted one card. One thing that meant I'd eat next winter." He was crying now, which he hated, which made everything worse, tears freezing at the corner of his jaw. "I'm thirteen years old and I've been running debt collectors since I was eleven and I never once slept a whole night without figuring out the exits, and you people were — you were going to be another set of people who used me up and — "

"Stop," Odile said.

Tuck stopped.

She crossed the camp in four steps. She wasn't big — she was wiry, rope-muscled, half a head shorter than Silas — but she moved like weather, and Tuck pressed back against the boulder until the stone bit through his coat.

"Three days," she said. "Voss has had our route for three days. The gorge ambush — "

"Wasn't from me. The photographs were just pages, I swear, no route, no landmarks. But the toll bridge — he'll know we crossed north. He'll know the valley's ahead." Tuck swallowed. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'll take it back, I'll steal the film, I'll — "

"You can't take back a man reading a map," Odile said. "You understand that? Everything downstream of this is already moving. Men are already walking. And every one of them is walking because of you."

Sefa rose. She didn't say anything; she just picked up her pot and her satchel and moved to the far side of the fire, putting the flames between herself and Tuck, and somehow that small quiet relocation was worse than any blow.

Silas finally stood. He did it slowly, joints cracking in the cold, and he came and stood beside Odile, and for a long moment the two of them just looked down at him, and Tuck thought: *this is it, this is the exit, this is the night I sleep alone in the snow or walk back down the mountain to Voss with nothing but my boots.*

"Cast him out," Sefa said from across the fire. Her voice was level. "We're starving. We're being hunted. He sold us once and he'll sell us again the moment he's frightened, and he will always be frightened. This is not cruelty, Odile. It's survival."

"It's a vote," Odile said. "Not yours alone."

"It should be mine. My people are the ones his photographs endanger."

"And mine are the ones standing in this snow." Odile rubbed her face with both hands, and Tuck saw — really saw, for the first time — how old she looked. How worn. Like a riverbank after flood season. "Tuck. Give me one reason. One true one. Not a bargain. Not a card. A reason."

And Tuck opened his mouth and found that he didn't have one. Every reason he owned was transactional. Every reason was a trade. He'd been trading since he was small enough to fit through windows — trades were the only language anyone had ever taught him, because nobody had ever stayed long enough to teach him another one.

"I don't have one," he said. "That's the truth. I don't have a single reason you should keep me."

The snow hissed on the coals. Somewhere upslope, wind moved through the rocks with a sound like a long exhale.

Then Silas spoke.

"Everyone here was bought or broken once."

They all turned to him.

"Odile ran contraband for the Marrow Bend syndicate for six years because they held her brother's papers." He said it flatly, without accusation, the way you'd read coordinates off a chart. "She bought her way out with runs she still won't talk about. Sefa's family survived an erasure by making themselves untraceable — by teaching their children that trust is a luxury that gets nations killed. And me." He laughed, short and airless. "I spent twenty years pretending my father was a stranger because believing he was a fraud hurt less than believing he'd chosen the river over me. We are not a crew of good people, Sefa. We're a crew of people who got bent early and learned to live crooked. The only question is whether we stay crooked together or go back to being crooked alone."

"That's not an answer," Sefa said. "That's a speech."

"It's both." Silas crouched down in front of Tuck, level with him, and Tuck flinched — but Silas didn't touch him. He just held out his hand, palm up, and in it lay the compass, its brass case catching the firelight, the needle trembling toward the northeast. "My father carried this into a country that didn't want to be found, and either it killed him or he died keeping its secret, and I won't know which until we get there. You want to know why I'm not casting you out? Because I know what it is to carry something in your boot that you're ashamed of. Mine's been shame my whole life. Yours is a film spool. The weight's the same."

Tuck stared at him. "I could do it again. Being scared doesn't stop."

"No," Silas agreed. "It doesn't. But here's what changes." He closed his fist around the compass. "Tomorrow morning, you tell us everything Voss's man said to you. Every word, every detail, what he paid, what he promised, where he said he'd be. Because if we're walking into what you sold, I want to walk in knowing the shape of it. That's your reason, Tuck. Not forgiveness. Usefulness. You're going to be the person in this crew who knows the most about the man hunting us. That's worth more than a pardon."

Odile exhaled through her nose. It might have been a laugh. It might have been the opposite.

"Sefa?" she said.

Sefa was quiet a long time. The pot steamed. Snow accumulated on her shoulders and she didn't brush it off.

"My grandmother had a saying," she said at last. "*The river does not ask the rain which clouds it fell from.*" She looked at Tuck, and her eyes were not warm, but they were not shut. "You photograph anything of mine again, boy, and the river will be the least of your problems."

"Yes ma'am," Tuck whispered.

"Then it's settled." Odile turned back to the fire and threw on the last split of pine, and the flames jumped, throwing all their shadows huge against the snowfall. "We vote at dawn whether to go on. Nobody decides anything starving and frozen. Eat what's left of the broth and sleep."

"There's barely any broth," Sefa said.

"Then barely eat it."

* * *

Tuck didn't sleep. He sat at the edge of the tarp with his knees pulled up, watching the fire die by degrees, and somewhere past midnight Silas came and sat down beside him without a word, close enough that some of the older man's warmth reached him through the frozen air.

For a while neither of them spoke. Then Tuck said, "Why'd you really do it? Back me. You could've let them cut me loose and saved yourself the worry."

Silas was quiet long enough that Tuck thought he wouldn't answer.

"Because when I pulled that chest out of the river," he said finally, "the first thing I thought was *how much is it worth*, and the second thing I thought was *who do I have to become to keep it*. And I've been fighting the second thought ever since." He turned the compass over in his fingers. "You're just further along the same road, kid. Somebody's got to stand at the fork and point."

"What if I point wrong again?"

"Then we point you back. That's what family does." He said it simply, like it cost him nothing, and Tuck understood suddenly that it had cost him everything — that Silas Hartwell had grown up with no one to point him back, and had decided, somewhere in the drowned villages and the gorges behind them, that no one else would have to.

Tuck's throat closed. He looked out at the snow instead, and that was when he saw it.

Downslope, maybe two hundred yards, where the whiteout thinned for just a moment between gusts — a light. Small, orange, steady. A campfire that was not theirs.

And below it, faint but unmistakable on the wind, the sound of men's voices, and the ring of iron on stone.

"Silas," Tuck breathed. "Silas. Look downslope."

Silas followed his gaze. The needle in his open compass swung, steadied, pointed northeast — straight through the strangers' fire, straight up the mountain, toward the valley that did not want to be found.

"How many fires do you count?" Silas asked softly.

Tuck counted. His blood went cold in a way the snow hadn't managed all night.

"Four," he said. "Maybe five."

"They're not following us," Silas said. "They're ahead of us. They're between us and the pass."
