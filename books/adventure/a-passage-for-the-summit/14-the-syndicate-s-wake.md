## 14. The Syndicate's Wake

Odile smelled the camp before she saw it. Woodsmoke, yes — but under it something sweeter and wrong, like meat left too long in the sun, and beneath that the chemical bite of a stove doused with fuel rather than banked for the night.

"Kill the engine," she said.

Tuck shipped the oars without argument. The river carried them the last hundred meters, quiet as a held breath, past a bend where the pines crowded down to the waterline, and then the camp opened up on the eastern bank like a wound.

Three tents. A canvas mess shelter. Two aluminum boats hauled up on the gravel, one of them the syndicate's long grey runabout with its outboard still canted in the water position — abandoned in a hurry, or abandoned in confidence that nothing upstream could touch them. A stack of supply crates, some opened, some not. And a fire pit with the remains of a large blaze, ash still warm when Odile crouched over it and held her palm above the coals.

Hours old. Not days.

"They knew we were behind them," Silas said quietly.

"Or they didn't care." Odile stood, scanning the tree line. "There's a difference. One means they're running. The other means they think there's nothing back there worth running from."

Sefa had gone very still near the boats. She was looking at the ground by the mess shelter, at a pattern of scuffed gravel and drag marks that Odile's eyes had catalogued but hadn't yet understood.

"Odile." Sefa's voice was flat, careful, the voice she used when she was translating herself out of her own language to keep it steady. "Come here."

Odile came.

The drag marks led off through the trees, maybe forty meters, to a shallow depression where the hillside drained toward the river. The ground there was soft, worked-over, recently turned — but whoever had dug had done it fast and badly, and the spring melt had already begun to undo their work. One boot heel had broken through the crust of soil at the edge. Beyond it, half exposed, was a hand.

Odile had seen dead men before. She'd pulled them out of the Ansel after the spring floods, she'd left two of her own crew buried in river mud along the border gorges, she'd watched a man she'd hired in Kessel Port take a knife in the lung over a disputed cargo share. She thought she was past the point where a body could rearrange anything inside her.

She was wrong about this one. Not because of the body itself — because of what was around it.

The man had been made to kneel. You could read it in the earth: two knee prints pressed deep into the soft ground, close together, the way a man kneels when he knows what's coming and has decided not to beg. His hands had been bound in front of him — the cord was still on the wrists, frayed where he'd twisted against it. He was young. Local, by the make of his coat and the stitched felt boots, mountain work, the kind Sefa's people wore. There was no wound on his front.

They'd shot him in the back of the head and put him in a hole barely deep enough to be called one.

"He's Vessian," Sefa said. Her hand had found the boy's coat collar and turned it, gently, the way you'd turn a sleeping child. On the inside seam, worked in thread the same color as the wool so only someone looking would ever see it, was a single stitched character. Odile couldn't read it, but she'd seen the script now often enough to know it when it was in front of her. "A guide. Someone from the high villages. They paid him to walk them up the valley approaches."

"And when the walking ran out," Silas said, "they asked him for more."

Tuck had come up behind them and stopped dead. Odile heard his breath catch — a small sound, quickly smothered, the sound of a kid who'd learned early that showing fear cost you. But he didn't look away. She gave him credit for that. She'd known grown men, hard men, who'd have found an urgent reason to be back at the boats.

"He wouldn't talk," Tuck said. It wasn't a question.

"No," Odile said. "He wouldn't."

* * *

They searched the camp while the light lasted. It told its story plainly enough; Bram Voss had never been a man who believed in covering tracks, because tracks were how you taught people to be afraid of you.

The mess shelter held a folding table with maps weighted down with tin cups. Odile recognized the survey style immediately — syndicate issue, the kind commissioned by collectors' houses that never put their own names on anything. The route was marked in red pencil: Marrow Bend, the drowned steeple, the Gorge of Names, the floating market. Every stop they'd made. Every stop Tuck's photographs had shown him.

But the map went further than they had. Past the market, past the hidden paths Sefa had led them through — marked, though imperfectly, with corrections in a different hand, the guide's hand, she realized. The boy had been drawing them a way in. And past even that, a circled X in the upper valley with a notation in Voss's tight block capitals: SOURCE APPROX. AWAITING LOCAL CONFIRMATION.

Awaiting local confirmation. That was what the kneeling boy had been for. That was what he'd refused to give.

In one of the tents they found the rest of it. A crate of syndicate rations. A lockbox, forced open, empty of whatever it had held. And pinned to the tent pole, deliberate as a notice nailed to a church door, a sheet of paper.

Voss's handwriting again. Odile read it twice, then read it aloud, because some things needed air:

*"To any party following: the source of the River Ansel is the property of the Meridian Collectors' Consortium, acquired by right of discovery and contract. Interference will be treated as theft of commercial assets. Persons assisting local elements are accessories. This region contains nothing of value to anyone else. Turn back."*

"Nothing of value," Silas said. His voice had a strange, scraped quality. "He shot a boy and wrote *nothing of value.*"

"It's not for us," Odile said. "It's for everyone else. It's a message to any village downstream that might think about helping us. *This is what neutrality buys you.*"

Sefa had wrapped the guide's body in his own coat and sat beside it on her heels, saying words under her breath in a language Odile didn't know. When she finished, she looked up, and Odile saw something in her face she hadn't seen before — not grief exactly, though grief was in it, but a kind of terrible confirmation. Like a woman who'd spent her whole life being told the family stories were exaggerated, and had just been handed the proof.

"You understand now," Sefa said. "What my grandmother meant. What all of it meant. They didn't erase us from the maps because we were worthless. They erased us because we were *in the way.* And every generation since, they come back to check whether the erasure took." She touched the boy's folded hands. "This one's name was Edeven. I don't know his village. But my people will know him. The kept names will hold him now."

Tuck stood apart from all of it, arms crossed tight, working his jaw. Finally he said, "I sold him our route."

Nobody answered. Nobody had to. The boy's eyes went around the circle — Odile, Silas, Sefa — looking for the punishment, and finding something worse: three faces that simply agreed with him.

"I didn't know—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought it was paper. Maps, routes, distances. I thought nobody got hurt by paper."

"Paper is how it starts," Sefa said, not unkindly. "A ledger. A survey. A list of names to cross out. My country died on paper before it died anywhere else."

Tuck looked at the grave. Then he walked to the syndicate's mess table, picked up the red-pencil map, and tore it in half. Then in half again. He did it methodically, almost ritually, until the pieces were too small to mean anything, and dropped them in the fire pit among the cooling ashes.

"That doesn't unmake your photographs," Odile said quietly. "He has copies. He's hours ahead of us with copies."

"I know." Tuck's chin was up, defiant, daring her to say more. "I'm just — I need to have done *something.*"

Odile nodded once. She understood that better than the kid knew. Half her life was built out of things she'd done too late, for exactly that reason.

* * *

They burned what they couldn't carry and buried Edeven properly, which took most of the remaining daylight, because the ground was frozen below the first foot and Silas refused — absolutely, immovably refused — to leave him in the syndicate's shallow scrape. He dug with the fury of a man paying a debt he hadn't incurred, sleeves rolled, breath steaming, until the grave was deep and neat and worthy of the name carved nowhere but in their memories.

Sefa spoke the burial words. Odile stood at the head with her cap off, feeling the cold get into her scalp, and let herself think the thing she'd been refusing to think since the smell of the camp reached the boat.

For twenty years she'd run cargo on the Ansel under a simple code. Don't ask what's in the boxes. Don't take sides in other people's wars. Get paid, get home, keep your crew alive. It wasn't cowardice — she'd told herself it wasn't cowardice — it was professionalism. The river was full of causes, and causes sank boats. Neutrality was a hull shape. It shed trouble the way a good bow sheds water.

And here was the proof of what neutrality bought. A boy in the ground with a bullet through the back of his skull, executed by men whose paperwork Odile had ferried more than once in her smuggling years without ever opening the crates. Men she'd taken coin from. Maybe not Voss himself — but the same house, the same money, the same polite machinery that reduced a living valley to *commercial assets* and a murdered guide to *local elements.*

She had been part of the current that carried these men upstream. She'd just never followed her own cargo far enough to see where it landed.

"Odile." Silas stood beside her at the graveside, dirt to the elbows, watching her the way he watched water — reading the undertow. "You've gone somewhere."

"Yeah." She put her cap back on. "I've gone to the place where I admit I've been running freight for the people who dig holes like this."

"You couldn't have known."

"That's the trick of it, isn't it." She looked upstream, where the valley narrowed into shadow and the river ran dark and quick. "Not knowing is the whole business model. They build it so decent people can't know. And then a boy ends up kneeling in the dirt, and suddenly everybody downstream gets to say they couldn't have known either."

Silas said nothing for a moment. Then: "So what do we do?"

Odile laughed — short, humorless, surprised out of her. "You're asking me? I'm the one who spent twenty years not doing."

"You're the one with the boat."

That was true. That had always been true. Whatever else she was — smuggler, debtor, coward by her own accounting — she was the one with the boat, and the boat was the difference between Voss reaching the source uncontested and Voss reaching it with the Hartwell boy and a Vessian herbalist and a teenage thief and one tired woman with a grudge against her own reflection coming up his wake.

"Here's what we do," she said, and heard her own voice settle into the register she used in bad water, the one that brooked no discussion. "We stop thinking of this as salvage. We stop thinking of it as a debt, even. Debts end. This doesn't end. As long as that man is ahead of us with guns and maps, there'll be another Edeven in another shallow hole, and the one after him, until there's nothing left up that valley but a syndicate warehouse full of stolen gods." She turned to face the others. "We go faster. We go harder. We travel light and we travel armed and we do not stop to be careful, because careful is what killed that boy. And when we catch up to Bram Voss—"

She let it hang. Let them each finish the sentence in their own heads.

Tuck finished his out loud, and his voice cracked in the middle of it, and he didn't care: "We stop him."

"We stop him," Odile agreed. "One way or another. Because there's no neutral water left on this river. He made sure of that."

* * *

They launched at dusk, running lights dark, engines low. The syndicate's abandoned camp fell away behind them, and then the second bend took it entirely, and there was only the river and the closing walls of the valley and the cold coming down off the peaks like a tide.

Odile took the tiller herself for the night stretch. She wanted it. She wanted her hands on something that answered her.

An hour on, Sefa moved forward from the bow and crouched beside her, pointing up-slope into the dark. "There," she said. "Do you see it?"

At first Odile saw nothing but black timber and starless ridge. Then, high on the western wall, a light flickered — once, twice, three times. A pause. Twice more.

"Not a village," Sefa said softly. "No village signals from the heights anymore. That's a watcher. Someone posted on the ridgeline, counting boats that pass." Her voice tightened. "And those aren't my people's signals. Mine would have been answered by now."

Odile cut the engine to a whisper and stared up at the dark ridge, at the place where the light had been, willing her eyes to pull more out of the night. Somewhere up there, between them and the source, someone was tracking their progress — and deciding whether to report it.

"Hold course," she said finally. "And wake me if that light moves."

It moved.
