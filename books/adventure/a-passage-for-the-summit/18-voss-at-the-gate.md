## 18. Voss at the Gate

Tuck had never been good at waiting. Waiting was what happened to you when nobody needed you, and for most of his fifteen years, nobody had. So when the sentinels locked him in a guesthouse with a bolted door and a bowl of barley soup going cold on the sill, he did what he always did with locked doors.

He picked it.

Not to run. He told himself that twice, testing the truth of it the way he tested a lock — by feel, by give, by listening for the catch. He wasn't running. He was going to find Silas, or Odile, or Sefa, anyone who'd look him in the eye and tell him whether he was a prisoner here or a guest, because the difference mattered more than he could say out loud.

The corridor beyond was empty. Lamplight came through windows of thick, green-tinted glass, and outside those windows was a city that shouldn't exist — rooftops stacked down the valley walls like coins poured into a cup, bridges strung between them, water running everywhere through carved channels, singing. Vessia. The word from the ledger. The word from every erased map his father's generation had ever burned.

Tuck pressed his forehead to the glass and felt like a thief standing inside a house too fine to steal from.

That was when he heard the boots.

* * *

They came up the western trail an hour before dawn, thirty men in river-gray oilskins, moving quiet as silt. Tuck saw them first because he wasn't where he was supposed to be — he'd slipped out along the aqueduct walk, following the sound of voices, and found two sentinels arguing at a watchpost above the western ridge.

One of them was named Davel. Tuck knew him because Davel had shown him how the signal mirrors worked three days ago, laughing at Tuck's city accent, letting him flash a message across the valley just to see it answered. Davel had a gap in his teeth and a way of talking about his daughter that made her sound like weather.

The other sentinel was a stranger, lean and hard-eyed, and he was holding a lantern shuttered to a knife-slit of light, and he was pointing west.

"You'll open the lower post," the stranger said. "Nothing else. When they come through, you walk away."

"And if I don't?"

"Then your daughter learns what happens to families of traitors. The syndicate keeps records, Davel. We keep them very well."

Tuck's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

He knew that voice. Not the stranger's — the cadence behind it. Patient, reasonable, the voice of a man who never raised it because he never had to. Bram Voss had talked to Tuck exactly twice, both times in a room that smelled of lamp oil, both times offering him the same bargain: *you're disposable to them, boy; be useful to me instead.*

Tuck had sold them photographs. Ledger pages, route notes, the shape of their crew. He'd done it scared, alone in a floating market stall, telling himself it was insurance, telling himself Odile would cast him off eventually so he might as well eat first. And then Silas had stood in the snow at Cold Camp and said *everyone here was bought or broken once,* and hadn't cast him off, and Tuck had carried the guilt since like a stone sewn into his coat.

Now the stone had teeth.

Voss was coming through the lower post tonight. Tonight. And the only people who knew were a traitor sentinel, a syndicate agent — and a fifteen-year-old lockpick crouched under a watchpost railing with his heart hammering loud enough to give away a regiment.

He should have gone to the sentinels. He started to. Then he thought: *who believes me?* The boy who already sold them once. The boy under guard. They'd lock him somewhere with no windows and by the time anyone believed him, Voss would be drinking tea in the council hall.

No. If this was going to be believed, it had to be stopped, not reported.

Tuck backed away along the aqueduct, mind working faster than it ever had, and one fact kept surfacing, cold and clear:

He'd seen the photographs he sold. Every one. And in the background of the last one — taken at the toll bridge while everyone argued about passage fees — there had been crates. Syndicate crates, stenciled with the collector's mark, being hauled upriver behind Voss's boats.

Tuck couldn't read much, but he could read cargo. He'd grown up reading cargo on the Marrow Bend docks, judging weight by the way porters' knees bent.

Those crates were explosives.

* * *

He found Odile first, because Odile was always awake before the boats were.

She was on the repair dock below the eastern terraces, re-caulking the shattered hull of their old boat with resin the Vessians had given her, working by lamplight, sleeves rolled, hands black to the wrist. She didn't look up when he skidded onto the planks.

"You're out of your room," she said.

"Davel's selling the lower watchpost. To Voss. Tonight." The words came out in a single breath, all of them, no order. "There's a man with him, syndicate, and Voss has blasting powder, I saw the crates at the toll bridge, I know what stencils look like, Odile, I *know*—"

She looked up then. Her face did something complicated — fear and fury and something older than either — and then it went still, the way water goes still over deep current.

"How do you know it's tonight?"

"The stranger said the lower post. Sentinels change shift at the dark hour. That's when."

Odile set down her caulking iron very carefully, the way she did when throwing it would feel better. "You're certain these are the same photographs you sold him."

Tuck's throat closed. He nodded anyway. "I'm not asking you to believe I'm sorry. I'm asking you to believe my eyes."

For three seconds Odile Ferran said nothing at all, and Tuck had time to remember every way a person can be thrown off a boat.

Then she grabbed the lamp. "Get Silas. Get Sefa. Wake the council — Corvess, the hall, tell them Davel by name. And Tuck—" She caught his shoulder, grip hard enough to bruise. "If this is some play to buy your pardon back from Voss, I will put you in the river myself."

"It isn't," Tuck said. "It's the opposite."

* * *

It was almost enough, and almost is a word that kills.

By the time the council roused, by the time sentinels ran the ridge paths with shutters drawn off their lanterns, the lower post was already dark. Davel was gone — fled, or taken, no one knew which — and the western trail lay open beneath a sky just beginning to gray.

Corvess stood at the head of the council hall, ancient and upright, listening to reports come in like rain. Silas arrived still buckling his coat, compass in hand, its needle swinging in slow, agitated circles.

"They'll come down the goat path to the aqueduct gate," Corvess said. "It's the only approach wide enough for thirty men and heavy cargo. We can seal it — the old mechanism still turns — but if they carry blasting powder, sealing it only tells them where to drill."

"So we don't seal it," Silas said. "We let them in partway and hold the inner gate. Buy time."

"Time for what?" Sefa asked quietly. "The floodgate decision hasn't been made. The council is split. Some would rather negotiate."

"With Voss?" Odile's laugh had no humor in it anywhere. "I've met men who negotiate. Voss collects."

Silas turned to Tuck. "The crates. How many could you judge?"

"Six, maybe eight. Heavy ones. Enough to bring down the aqueduct gate and half the cliff with it, if they place them right."

Silas looked at the compass in his palm, then at the green glass windows, at the city below with its lamps beginning to gutter out one by one as the valley slept. Tuck watched him decide something, watched it happen in his face the way weather happens.

"Then we don't let them place them," Silas said. "Tuck. You know syndicate ordnance?"

"I know crates. I know fuses. I grew up around dredging charges — my uncle used them on the Bend before the creditors took the boat." Tuck heard his own voice go flat and steady, which surprised him. "I can't fight thirty men. But I can make their powder worthless."

Odile stared at him. "You're proposing to walk into their column."

"I'm proposing to walk into their *crates.* Nobody watches the cargo. Nobody's ever watched the cargo. I've been cargo my whole life — I know how it gets treated." He swallowed. "And if they catch me, they catch a boy who wandered off. Not a saboteur. Not yet."

"That's a lie and a bad one," Odile said.

"Yeah," Tuck said. "But it's mine."

* * *

They went down through the terraces in the last dark, Silas and Tuck, past sleeping houses where the doorposts were carved with names in Vessian script — thousands of names, generations of them, the canyon wall of the Gorge of Names continued here in wood and stone. Tuck kept touching them as he passed, lightly, like a man checking that a rail holds.

At the aqueduct gate, Silas stopped. "There's a maintenance crawl under the gate road. My father built it — the council showed me yesterday. It runs the length of the approach, with grates opening under the roadway." He pressed a small pry-bar into Tuck's hands and a stub of dark candle. "You get under the road. You get to the crates before they're placed. What do you need?"

"Wet," Tuck said. "Blasting powder hates wet. If I can open the seams and pack the charges with soaked clay from the channel beds, they'll fizzle or they'll blow early and sideways — either way, not where Voss wants. But I need the column to stop over the grates long enough. Can you give me noise? Something that makes them bunch up and wait?"

Silas almost smiled. "I grew up on the Marrow Bend. I can make a river argue with itself."

They shook hands. It was the first time Silas Hartwell had ever shaken Tuck's hand like he was someone whose hand was worth shaking, and Tuck held onto that feeling all the way down the crawl, into the cold and the dripping dark, alone with the water talking overhead.

* * *

Dawn came gray through the grate slits. Tuck lay on his back in the crawl space, clay paste ready in a waterskin, pry-bar in his teeth, listening to boots.

They came in loose order at first, then bunched — because upstream, right on schedule, the river made an argument with itself. A great cracking boom of water against stone, a false collapse of the weir upstream that Silas had rigged with levers and patience, and the whole column froze, thirty men staring at white water, officers shouting, porters setting down their loads.

Six crates. Right over the grates. Close enough that Tuck could smell the tarred canvas.

He worked fast and quiet, the way he'd learned picking locks nobody knew he was picking. First seam: pried, packed with wet clay, sealed again so the damage hid. Second crate: same. His hands were shaking now, properly shaking, but his fingers stayed clever, the way they always did when the rest of him was terrified. Third crate. Fourth.

On the fifth, the grate above him shifted.

A porter had stepped back from the crowd to relace his boot, and he was standing directly over the open seam, and through the slit Tuck saw the man's eyes drop — saw them widen — saw the man draw breath to shout—

Tuck did the only thing left. He drove the pry-bar up through the grate slit, hard, into the man's ankle. The shout became a yelp, the yelp became a fall, and the column turned toward the commotion, and in that tangle of confusion Tuck packed the fifth crate blind, by feel, clay squelching under his fingers, and slid back into the dark as boots thundered overhead.

The sixth crate he reached as the column started moving again. Less time. Sloppier work. Clay in one charge, not both. Half-ruined, maybe. Maybe not ruined at all.

Then a hand closed on his collar through the grate, and Tuck learned what cold iron smells like up close.

* * *

They dragged him out into the gray morning light and set him on his knees on the gate road, and the column formed a ring around him, and then the ring opened, and Bram Voss walked through it.

Up close, Voss was smaller than Tuck remembered and somehow worse for it — a neat, dry man with ink-stained fingers and eyes like ledger entries, everything recorded, nothing forgiven.

"Tucker Wren," Voss said, consulting a small book he actually carried. "Fifteen years of age. Formerly of the Marrow Bend. Sold me six pages of a Vessian expedition ledger for food and passage." He closed the book. "And now found sabotaging syndicate property. That's a new entry."

"I dropped my pry-bar," Tuck said. "I was hiding. I got lost."

Voss crouched to his level, and his voice went gentle, which was the most frightening thing yet. "Boy. I've read your photographs. I know your handwriting better than your mother does, if she's alive. Here is what happens now." He straightened. "We place the charges at the inner gate within the hour. You will stand beside us while they're placed, and afterward you'll come with us, and when this valley is a hole in the world, you'll have a purse and a boat ticket and no debts anywhere. Or—" he gestured mildly at the drop beyond the aqueduct rail, where the river ran white over the drowned steps of the old approach— "the river takes another orphan. No one will ever file a report."

Tuck looked at the crates being hoisted and carried onward, up the road toward the inner gate, six tarred shapes swaying on poles.

Five of them dead weight. One of them maybe alive.

He thought about Silas shaking his hand. About Odile saying *I will put you in the river myself* and meaning, underneath it, *don't make me.* About a city full of carved names that had never once been written down anywhere else in the world.

"Fine," Tuck said, and hung his head, and let them believe it.

Because the inner gate was narrow, and the column would bunch again to pass through it, and the sixth crate would be carried in the middle of the bunch, and a half-packed charge blown early and sideways in a narrow gate might do many things.

It might even do the right one.

As they marched him up the road, Tuck counted his breathing the way Odile had taught him on the white water — in for four, hold, out for four — and behind them, far downstream, he heard the river begin to rise, a low grinding note rolling up the valley like something enormous waking at last.

The inner gate stood ahead, old iron over old stone, and Tuck walked toward it with a dead man's calm and a live boy's arithmetic, doing the sums over and over:

Five charges spoiled. One chance left. One narrow gate.

*Hold*, he told the numbers. *Hold until they're all inside.*
