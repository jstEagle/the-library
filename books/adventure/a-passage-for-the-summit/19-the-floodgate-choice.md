## 19. The Floodgate Choice

The council chamber smelled of cedar smoke and wet stone, and it had gone quiet in the way a river goes quiet just before it drops over an edge.

Silas stood where they had told him to stand — on a worn brass ring set into the floor, the same brass as his compass housing, the same alloy his father must have specified twenty years ago when he drew the plans for this room. Around him, the Kept Names sat in a crescent of stone benches, old faces and young ones, Sefa among them near the bottom tier with her arms crossed tight like she was holding herself together by force.

At the center of the crescent stood Maren Vael, First of the Council, a woman past seventy with a voice that carried without effort.

"The syndicate has breached the outer ridge," she said. "Three hours, perhaps four. They come with explosives, armed men, and a traitor's map." A pause, weighted. "We have one option left."

She turned, and every head turned with her, toward the far wall of the chamber — where a relief map of the valley was carved into living rock, rivers rendered in channels of blue glass, and above the valley's throat, a mechanism drawn in silver that Silas had been staring at since they brought him in.

The floodgates.

"They were built before the Erasure," Maren said. "Before we became untraceable. Our ancestors cut them into the mountain so that if Vessia was ever found, it could not be *taken* — only drowned. The approach gorge would flood. Everything in it. The terraces, the lower orchards, the shrine houses. Two hundred years of our rebuilt lives, gone in an afternoon." Her eyes came around the room, touching each face. "And the water would take anyone standing in it."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere below them, faint through the stone, Silas could hear the river — the true river, the Ansel rising from beneath the city — moving in its sleep.

"And the alternative?" someone asked from the upper benches. An old man, his hands knotted with arthritis. "We let them in. We fight."

"We lose," Maren said simply. "They have guns and we have slings and gates. I will not dress it up. If Voss reaches the source, he takes it apart piece by piece and sells it, and everything your grandmothers bled to hide dies in a warehouse in some collector's wing." She let that sit. Then: "There is a third way. It is narrow, and it is dangerous, and it requires a pilot."

All the faces turned back toward Silas.

His stomach dropped. "I don't know your channels," he said. "I've been in this valley two days."

"You know rivers," Maren said. "That is what we are told. That is what the ledger says your father wrote about you, when you were nine years old — *the boy reads water like scripture.*" Something flickered across her face, grief or its fossil. "Elias Hartwell built the diversion channel himself. He surveyed it, he cut its first meter with his own hands, and he left instructions for exactly this day. Instructions that name you."

The words landed like cold water down his collar. "*Name me.*"

Maren lifted a folded sheet of heavy paper, sealed with wax stamped by the compass rose. "He wrote it the year before he died. He asked that it be opened only if the gates were ever needed." She crossed the chamber and held it out. "It is needed."

Silas took it. His hands weren't steady. He broke the seal and read.

---

*If you are reading this, then they've come, and I'm sorry — sorry that I'm not there, sorry that I left this weight where my child would have to lift it.*

*The diversion channel runs from the upper weir to the floodgate throat. It is three kilometers of white water through cut granite, and it has never been run, because it was never meant to be run — it was meant to be opened and abandoned, the water doing the work alone. But the gate mechanism is old, and if the sluice jams half-open, the flood stalls in the gorge and Voss walks through on the rubble. Someone has to go down the channel ahead of the water and clear the jam by hand. By hand, Silas. There is no other way. I checked for eleven years.*

*You'll want to know why I'm asking this of you and not one of ours. Because none of ours can do it. The channel reads wrong — it curls back on itself twice, and the eddy lines lie. You have to feel the lie before you see it. I couldn't teach that to people who grew up afraid of this water. But you grew up on the Ansel's worst miles pulling salvage out of currents that wanted you dead. You learned to listen to a river when it lies.*

*I won't pretend it's survivable. I'll say instead that it's possible, which is different, and that I ran worse water than this at your age with less reason.*

*Whatever you decide, decide fast. Rivers don't wait and neither do collectors.*

*— E.H.*

---

Silas read it twice. The second time his vision blurred and he read it a third time to be sure of what he'd seen.

*My child. Whoever you become.* No. That was the other letter, the one waiting downstream, unread. This one ended harder: *decide fast.*

"Well?" Maren asked.

He looked up. The whole council was watching him, and beyond them, through the high windows, he could see the valley — the terraced orchards going gold with late autumn, the slate roofs, the shrine houses with their blue-tiled eaves, the low stone walls of the lower district where children were carrying firewood in baskets too big for them. Two hundred years of a nation pretending to be weather.

"He wants me to run a channel that's never been run," Silas said, "ahead of a flood, to fix a gate by hand, while armed men shoot at me from the gorge rim."

"Yes," Maren said.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we open the gates anyway and pray the sluice holds, and if it doesn't, we die fighting in the ruins of the thing we drowned ourselves to save." She didn't flinch. "I would rather ask a stranger to risk his life than spend my people's like coins. But I am asking, Silas Hartwell. Not commanding. Your father made me promise I'd ask."

* * *

They gave him an hour to prepare, which was fifty minutes more than he wanted.

The armory was a cave off the council hall, and Odile Ferran stood in it holding a rope coil like she was deciding whether to strangle him with it or rig his harness.

"You're not going alone," she said.

"I am, actually. One boat. The channel's too narrow for two hulls, and the weight matters — Dad's notes say under ninety kilos total, boat included." He pulled the harness webbing over his shoulders. "You'd add forty."

"I'd add forty kilos of sense, which you're clearly short." She threw the rope at his chest. He caught it. "Tuck and I can take the rim trail. There's a ledge above the second curl — your father marked it. If the sluice jams, you'll need hands on the gate wheel from above, and I've run winch work on the Marrow since before you could swim."

"Odile—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked like a whip, then dropped. "Twenty years ago your father pulled me out of the Ansel with a broken arm and a busted boat, and I never paid it back because paying it back meant admitting I owed anything to anybody. I'm done with that arithmetic. You go down the channel. I go down the rim. Tuck goes where Tuck's small enough to fit, which is everywhere, gods help us." She jabbed a finger at him. "Argue and I'll tie you to the dock."

From the doorway, Tuck raised a hand. "For the record, I vote with Odile. Also I stole you a flare gun. Also I'm not sorry about either thing."

Silas looked between them — the smuggler who'd spent a book's worth of miles pretending she wasn't family, the kid who'd sold photographs to the enemy and come back anyway — and felt something in his chest that had nothing to do with fear, though fear was there too, vast and cold as the water he was about to ride.

"Fine," he said. "But when this is done, we're having a conversation about boundaries."

"Looking forward to it," Odile said dryly.

Sefa was waiting outside the armory, and she didn't argue at all. She pressed a pouch into his hand — dried herbs, bitter-smelling. "Chew this if you go under. It keeps the heart slow so the cold doesn't stop it. My grandmother used it on drowning fishermen." Her dark eyes held his. "Come back, Silas. You just got here. It would be obscene to leave already."

He almost laughed. Almost. "Keep my father's ledger safe. If I don't—"

"If you don't, I'll burn it myself and salt the ash." She kissed his cheek, quick and fierce. "Go."

* * *

The upper weir was a shelf of ancient masonry where the Ansel gathered itself before dropping into the hidden valley, and the diversion channel peeled away from it like a scar — a slot of churning green water cut into gray granite, barely wider than his boat, vanishing downhill into shadow.

The sentinels had carried his father's old survey boat up from the boathouse: a low-slung river skiff, canvas-skinned over a frame of bent ash, patched in places with resin that had yellowed with age. Twenty years old and kept ready. They had oiled the hull every season, waiting for a day everyone prayed wouldn't come. That detail hit Silas harder than the council's speech had. Somebody had believed. Somebody had maintained hope like machinery.

He loaded the skiff: pry bar, mallet, wooden wedges, the flare gun, a lantern in a waterproof case, sixty meters of line. His compass went around his neck, where it had begun to tremble against his sternum like a small live thing, needle swinging toward the gate throat as if it knew the way home.

Below, far downstream, he could hear it now — faint pops echoing up the gorge. Gunfire. Voss's advance scouts, meeting the first sentinel pickets among the terraces. Time was bleeding out of the morning.

Odile gripped his shoulder at the lip of the weir. "Second curl," she said. "The eddy lies. Trust the pull, not the look."

"That's what he wrote."

"Then for once in your life, listen to your father." Her jaw worked. "Silas. Whatever's at the bottom of that channel — you come out the other end. That's not a request. That's a debt being collected."

He grinned at her, and it surprised him how easily it came. "Yes, ma'am."

He pushed off.

The first hundred meters were the easy lie — fast water, clean lines, the skiff biting down the slot like it had been born for it. Then the walls closed in and the light went bronze and the river began to speak in a language he'd only heard in nightmares: water stacking against the left wall, folding back on itself, a hole opening under his stern that swallowed the bow and spat it sideways. He corrected, overcorrected, felt the skiff's rail kiss stone, corrected again — reading the current not with his eyes but with the seat of his pants, the way he'd learned at fourteen hauling salvage nets in flood season, the way his body remembered even when his mind was screaming.

The first curl came and he took it blind, committed, the world dissolving into noise and spray — and then he was through, into a brief calm pool, lungs heaving, alive, the channel dropping away below him in white staircases.

Above, faintly, he heard Odile's whistle — three notes, the all-clear signal they'd agreed on. She was keeping pace on the rim. Good. Good.

The second curl was where the eddy lied.

He saw it a half-second too late: the water looked like it flowed left, smooth and inviting, but underneath it pulled right, hard, into a recirculating pocket behind a granite fin. The skiff's stern swung. For one long, crystalline moment Silas hung in the air between outcomes — and then the pocket took him.

The world inverted. Cold closed over his head like a fist. The skiff rolled, and he lost the paddle, lost the light, lost up and down, tumbling in the wash cycle with granite scraping his shoulder through the jacket. *Slow heart,* Sefa had said, and later, gasping in an air pocket under the overturned hull, he fumbled the herb pouch loose and chewed a mouthful of something that tasted like pine tar and grief, and felt his panic drop away as if a hand had been laid on his chest.

*Trust the pull, not the look.*

He stopped fighting. He let the eddy carry him in its slow circle, feeling for the seam — the place where the recirculating water met the main flow and slid downstream. Every river had one. Every lie had a truth running alongside it. He found it with his fingertips first, a band of smoother, colder water, and swam into it, and the river relented, spitting him and the half-swamped skiff out into the main channel like a fisherman releasing a catch.

He hauled himself aboard, coughing, shaking, alive. Above him, three whistle notes, ragged with relief.

One kilometer left. And now he could hear the gates.

* * *

The floodgate throat was a cathedral of stone that his father had helped carve, and it was dying.

The sluice mechanism filled the gorge wall — iron wheels the size of wagon wheels, chains thick as his wrist, counterweights hanging in their housings like hanged men. Half of it was already moving; the sentinels topside had cranked the gates open per the council's order, and the reservoir above was beginning to lean its weight against the approach gorge where Voss's column was even now advancing along the old trade road.

But the eastern gate was jammed. Silas could see it from fifty meters out: the great slab hung at a drunken angle, wedged on something — debris, a fallen counterweight chain, twenty years of silt — and water was sheeting through the gap in a thin, insufficient curtain. Enough to frighten. Not enough to stop armed men who had already decided to die for a payday.

He grounded the skiff on the stone apron below the mechanism and climbed.

The wheel housing was slick with spray. He got the pry bar into the jammed linkage and threw his weight on it, and the iron groaned and gave an inch and locked again. Below and upstream, through the thin curtain of falling water, he could see them now — figures on the trade road, tiny at this distance, spreading out. A flash of glass and brass. Voss's scouts had glasses. Which meant Voss could see the gate stalling, could see the lone figure wrestling the mechanism, and would understand exactly what it meant.

Gunfire cracked off the gorge walls. Bullets sparked off the ironwork a hand's width from Silas's face, and he flattened against the housing, heart slamming despite the herb, and understood with perfect clarity that he was pinned — that every second he worked the pry bar was a second exposed, and that the flood would arrive whenever the reservoir finished leaning, and that he had maybe minutes and maybe less.

Then a flare went up from the rim above him — red, screaming, beautiful — and answering fire crackled from the heights. Odile. Tuck. The sentinels' slingers, finding their range. Covering him. Buying him seconds with their own exposure.

Silas got back on the bar.

The linkage groaned. Gave. Groaned. Through the gap in the falling water he watched the trade road, watched a figure in a pale coat stride ahead of the skirmish line with the unhurried confidence of a man who owned whatever he looked at. Bram Voss, coming to see his prize stall in the balance — coming, Silas realized with a cold drop in his gut, straight for the gatehouse road, the one path that led directly to the mechanism, directly to him.

The bar slipped. Silas slammed it home again, put his whole body into it, screaming through his teeth, and the jammed chain tore free with a shriek of iron —

— and the eastern gate dropped.

The sound was the end of the world. Water hit the approach gorge like a detonation, a brown-green wall swallowing the trade road, the terraces, the shrine houses, two hundred years of careful life erased in a roar that shook stone dust from the ceiling of the sky. The shockwave slapped the gorge and came back and nearly took Silas off the housing entirely; he wrapped both arms through the wheel spokes and held on while the flood ate the world below.

When he could see again, coughing spray, ears ringing, the approach was gone. A lake stood where the road had been. And in the middle distance, on a spur of high ground the flood hadn't reached — the gatehouse spur, the last dry path — a handful of figures clustered, stranded, furious, alive.

Among them, a man in a pale coat, looking up at the mechanism, looking directly at Silas.

Even at that distance, Silas saw Voss smile.

Then the man turned and began climbing — up the flooded spur's spine, toward the maintenance stairs his father's plans had cut into the rock, the stairs that led to the gate controls, to the manual override, to the one lever that could drain the flood and open the valley like a wound.

Silas looked at the pry bar in his hands, at the flare gun on his hip, at the long climb between Voss and the controls — a race, then, with no river to carry him, nothing between them but wet stone and iron and whatever a man was willing to become at the top of a drowning world.

He started climbing.
