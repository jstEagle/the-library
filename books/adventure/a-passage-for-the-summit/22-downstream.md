## 22. Downstream

The river in spring was a different animal than the one that had tried to kill them.

Silas felt the difference in his hands first. All winter he'd gripped tiller lines and oar handles like a man fighting something, knuckles white, wrists locked against the current's arguments. Now, three days below the ridge, his grip had loosened without his noticing. The Ansel ran high with snowmelt, brown-green and muscular, but it ran *with* them now, and there was a language to it he'd spent twenty years learning without knowing why.

"Sandbar, port side," Tuck called from the bow, where the kid had appointed himself lookout with the seriousness of a boy who had finally been given something worth guarding. "Big one. Looks fresh."

"Fresh how?"

"Like it wasn't there last month." Tuck squinted. "The water's still deciding about it."

Silas eased the skiff left. Odile, sprawled in the stern with her boots up on the cargo box — an empty cargo box, which was its own kind of miracle — cracked one eye.

"The water's still deciding," she repeated. "Listen to him. Three months on a river and he talks like a pilot."

"I talk like you," Tuck said. "That's the problem."

It was true, and they all knew it, and nobody said anything else for a while because the morning was too good to spend on talk. The banks slid past green and dripping, willows budding yellow, a heron standing in the shallows with the patience of a saint. Somewhere behind them, two days' hard travel back through the gorge, a hidden valley held a living city that did not exist. Somewhere ahead, Marrow Bend waited with its creditors and its mud and its small gray life.

Silas found he was in no hurry to arrive at either.

* * *

They carried nothing salvable. That had been the condition, and Silas had set it himself, kneeling in the council hall while the elders of Vessia watched him with eyes the color of the deep river.

"Sink it all," he'd said. "The chest, the ledger, both halves sewn back together. Let the source keep its own paperwork."

Sefa had argued — gently, in the way she argued everything, like planting seeds — that the ledger was history, that histories drowned are histories lost. But the council had already voted before Silas spoke, and when he spoke he only gave them the words they'd been waiting for: *a thing worth hiding is worth hiding completely.* His father's principle. His father's design. The compass stayed with Silas because the council agreed it had never really belonged to Vessia. It belonged to the man who built their concealment, and to whoever came after him.

Whoever came after him. That was what the eldest of the council had called Silas, pressing the brass instrument back into his hands, and he still didn't know if it was a title or a wound.

So the chest went into the pool at the river's birth, weighted with stones from the valley itself, and sank without ceremony while forty people who officially did not exist stood on the bank and watched it go. Sefa sang something low in the old tongue. Tuck cried and pretended he hadn't. Odile stood at attention like a soldier at a funeral, which, Silas supposed, was exactly what it was.

And then they'd walked out over the ridge, down through the passes Sefa's people had kept untraceable for six generations, and boarded the skiff Odile had cached in the gorge the previous autumn, and pointed the bow downstream toward the ordinary world.

Nothing salvable. Everything that mattered. Silas took inventory of it sometimes, at night, when the fire was low: one compass, one letter, one crew of two people who had no blood claim on him and would take a bullet for him anyway. It was the richest cargo he'd ever run.

* * *

The letter had been riding in his breast pocket since the cave.

He'd found it in chapter and fragment across the whole journey — pages traded away at the toll bridge, pages recovered from Voss's burned camp, a single sheet tucked inside his father's old tool roll in the shelter where the tools still smelled faintly of oil after twenty years. He'd read each piece as it came to him, hungrily, the way a starving man eats. But the last page he had not read. He'd known it was the last page by the way it was folded — twice, careful, the creases sharp, a page meant to be saved for the end of something — and he had carried it sealed through the valley, through the council, through the sinking of the chest, through the whole long cold walk out.

Odile noticed on the second morning. She always noticed.

"You're going to read that eventually," she said, not looking up from the repair she was making to the tent seam. "Or you're going to carry it until it dissolves, and then you'll be reading it one word at a time whether you like it or not."

"I'm saving it."

"For what?"

Silas watched the water. A kingfisher went past like a thrown blue knife. "I don't know. For when I'm the person it was written to, maybe."

Odile tied off her stitch and bit the thread. "Your father wrote it twenty years ago. You've been the person it was written to for twenty years. You just took the long way around to find out."

She said it without softness, because Odile didn't do softness, and that was precisely why it landed. Silas put his hand over his pocket. The paper crackled under his palm like something alive.

"That night in the flood," he said. "When you told me how he pulled you out of the wreck at Gray Landing. Why didn't you ever tell me before? All those years I was digging ditches two towns over from you."

"Because you weren't asking." She coiled the thread away. "And because a debt you announce is a debt you're collecting on. I wanted to pay it clean or not at all." She looked at him then, direct as a drawn line. "Turns out I paid it pulling your stupid body out of white water. So we're square, Hartwell. Finally, formally, square. Don't make it weird."

"It's weird."

"Yeah," she said, and almost smiled. "It's a little weird."

* * *

They camped that night on a gravel bar below the ruins of Drowned Steeple, though nothing of the steeple showed above the waterline anymore — just a brown stain of old flood, a suggestion of foundations when the light hit right. The ferrywoman was dead; Tuck had asked in the last village, and learned she'd gone in her sleep the previous winter, which everyone agreed was the only gentle way the river ever let anyone go.

Tuck cooked. The kid had discovered cooking somewhere in the winter camps and defended the discovery fiercely, producing meals from almost nothing with the same lockpick fingers that had once opened other people's boxes. Tonight it was river fish and wild onions and a flatbread that was mostly ambition, and they ate it sitting on the gravel watching the water go gold and then rose and then dark.

"You're going to Marrow Bend," Tuck said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Somebody has to settle the accounts. Sell the salvage rights, pay off what's left of the debt." Silas turned his father's compass over in his hands. In the firelight the etched rose on its cover caught orange. "It won't take long. There's nothing there for me anymore. There never really was."

"There's the boat shed."

"There's the boat shed," Silas admitted. "Which is full of my father's disgrace and my mother's absence and every year I spent being nobody's son." He paused. "I'm going to burn it, I think. The shed. Or sell it to someone who'll paint it a different color."

"Burn it," Odile said. "Painting's cowardice."

Tuck snorted fish bones into the fire. "What about after? After Marrow Bend. What happens to—" the kid gestured vaguely at the three of them, the fire, the boat, the whole improbable arrangement— "this."

Silas looked at Odile. Odile looked at the river, wearing the expression of a woman being asked to name a feeling in public.

"What happens to this," she said finally, "is that in four days I put in at Corren's Landing, where a man named Halloway owes me nine hundred crowns and thinks I forgot about him. And these two—" she jerked her thumb— "can come along or not, but if they come along they work, and if they work they eat, and if they eat they stay. That's the whole contract. That's all it's ever been."

Tuck grinned in the firelight. "Best contract I ever signed."

"You haven't signed anything."

"I'm signing it now. Verbally. Witnessed by the river."

"The river doesn't witness contracts," Odile said. "The river steals boats."

But she was smiling when she said it, and Silas sat between them in the warm dark and understood that the contract had been signed months ago — in the gorge, in the rapids, in the snow — and that everything since had just been the two of them pretending they hadn't noticed.

* * *

On the fourth morning, with Marrow Bend's smoke smudging the horizon and the current carrying them easy, Silas took out the letter.

He didn't decide to. That was the strange part. He simply reached the bend where the river widened and slowed and went suddenly, perfectly calm, mirror-still from bank to bank, and his hand went to his pocket on its own, and the fold of paper was in his fingers before any argument could be made against it.

Odile saw. She trimmed the sail — the little jury rig she'd built in the gorge and refused to call a sail because *sails were for rich people* — and steered a straight line down the middle of the calm, and said nothing, and Tuck, at the bow, turned around once, understood, and turned back to face forward, keeping watch over water that needed no watching.

Silas unfolded the page.

His father's handwriting. He knew it the way he knew his own pulse — the cramped leftward slant, the way the *r*s looked like little flags. Twenty years since he'd seen it and his eyes went straight home to it.

*My child, whoever you become.*

That was how it began. Not *Dear Silas.* Not *To my son.* His father had written it in the valley, Silas realized — the ink, the paper, the careful hand of a man writing by lamplight in a place he had chosen to die defending — and he had not known who would read it. He had not even known if anyone would. He had addressed it to a possibility.

*If you are reading this, then the compass found you, which means the river chose you, which means I am allowed to hope you are not angry with me. I am allowed very few hopes. I spend this one freely.*

*I did not abandon you. I need you to know that above all things, more than the city, more than the secret, more than my own name. I left to buy time for people who could not buy it themselves, and I stayed because leaving would have sold them anyway. Every choice I made, I made as your father first. If that sounds like a comfort instead of an excuse, then I have written it correctly.*

*The world will tell you what you are. It told me — disgraced, ruined, a liar with a trowel. The world is often loud and rarely right. What you are is this: someone the river trusted. Everything else is weather.*

*Keep the compass. Keep the names. And when you find people worth keeping — and you will, because you were always better at keeping than I was — hold on with both hands and don't count the cost. I counted costs my whole life. It bought me a grave far from you and a city that will speak my name kindly, and I would trade all of it for one more winter of your voice complaining about the food.*

*Complain about the food, child. Find someone to complain to. That's the whole of the inheritance. The rest is just geography.*

*— Your father, who was never ashamed of you, whatever else he was*

The page blurred. Silas let it.

The skiff ran on through the calm water, sail full, wake trailing long and silver behind them, and neither Odile nor Tuck said a word, and the silence was the kindest thing anyone had ever given him. When he could see again he folded the letter along its careful creases and put it back in his breast pocket, over his heart, where it had apparently been traveling all along.

"Well?" Odile said quietly, eyes on the channel.

"He says I should complain about the food."

A pause. Then Tuck, without turning around: "The flatbread last night was an abomination and I stand by it."

"There," Odile said. "Inheritance delivered. Somebody write it down."

And Silas laughed — really laughed, from the bottom of the twenty-year hole, the sound coming out of him strange and rusty and unmistakably alive — and the river carried it downstream toward Marrow Bend, toward Corren's Landing, toward whatever ordinary world was willing to hold three people who had seen a city that didn't exist and agreed, without ever saying so, to keep it between them.

* * *

They made Marrow Bend by late afternoon.

From the water it looked smaller than Silas remembered, and grayer, and sadder — the sagging docks, the salvage cranes rusting against the sky, the creditors' notices nailed to the pilings like crows. His whole childhood, reduced to a skyline you could walk past in four minutes.

He moored the skiff at the old jetty, and the three of them stood on the planks of the town that had made him, and for a moment nobody moved.

Then Tuck spotted the notice board by the ferry office and went still. "Silas."

Silas crossed to it. Pinned among the debt notices and the grain prices, one sheet, fresh ink:

**REWARD — for verified account of events upstream of the Gorge of Names, winter passage. Generous terms. Inquire at the Blue Anchor, ask for the gentleman with the scarred left hand.**

The gentleman with the scarred left hand. Silas had watched that hand sign papers in a burning camp. He had watched the river take it, or thought he had.

Behind him, Odile read the notice over his shoulder and went very quiet, the way she went quiet before a fight.

"They fished him out," she said. "Or somebody wearing his coat wants the world to think they did."

Tuck looked between them. "So do we run?"

Silas touched the compass in his pocket, and the letter over his heart, and felt the river move beneath the jetty — patient, remembering, already carrying the answer downstream to whoever needed it next.

"No," he said. "We get a room at the Blue Anchor. And then we find out who's asking."
