## 1. The Mood of the Water

The Kestrel told me things before I knew them myself. That was the whole secret of piloting, if you wanted to know it, and most people didn't. They thought it was about the wheel, the throttle, the depth gauge. It wasn't. It was about listening to a boat the way you'd listen to a woman who'd stopped bothering to lie to you.

That morning she told me the river had shifted in the night.

We were coming down off the high water above Miller's Bend, running light — six crates of dried apples, a consignment of nails, a piano nobody at the landing had paid for yet — when I felt her shudder through the wheel, a small complaint in the wood, like a dog shifting its weight before it barks. The current should have been sliding us left toward the cut bank, smooth as poured syrup. Instead it pushed right. Hard.

I eased the throttle back and stood still a moment, one hand on the wheel, one on the brass rail worn bright by twenty years of other men's hands and three years of mine. Dawn was coming up gray off the water, the kind of light that makes everything look like a promise somebody else made.

"Hollis," I called down the engine hatch. "She's telling me the bar's moved at the Bend."

A clank, a grunt, and then Hollis's head appeared in the hatchway, gray-stubbled and unimpressed. He was sixty if he was a day, built like something a blacksmith had abandoned halfway through, and he'd been listening to engines longer than I'd been alive.

"The bar's always moved at the Bend," he said. "The bar's got no principles."

"The bar's got more principles than most of my passengers. It just changes them daily." I swung the wheel over. "Take her down to half. I want to feel the bottom before I trust it."

He disappeared without arguing, which was how I knew he agreed with me. Hollis only argued when he thought I was wrong, and he thought I was wrong perhaps twice a year, which was a better record than I had with anyone else in my acquaintance.

We came into Miller's Bend at a crawl, and there it was: a fresh sandbar laid across the channel like a sleeping cat, right where the chart — my chart, hand-drawn, hand-corrected, the most valuable thing I owned after the boat herself — said there was open water. The flood upriver had spent the night rearranging the world, the way floods do, without consulting anybody.

I took her around the tail of it with six inches to spare. I know it was six inches because I could see the sand through the water, pale and close, waving at me like an old debt. The Kestrel slid over it and came out the other side clean, and I wrote the new bar onto my chart with the pencil I keep on a string, and that was that.

Three years of running this river, and I could read it like scripture. Better, maybe. Scripture doesn't shift its meaning overnight. The river does, and I kept up.

* * *

The trouble was that reading a river doesn't pay. Not the way it should. Not the way a man who can't tell a sandbar from his own shadow gets paid sitting in a countinghouse in Corliss.

I want to be clear about one thing, because people get it wrong, and the getting-wrong has cost me more sleep than the debt itself: I am good at this. I don't say it to boast. I say it because everything that happened after — every bad bargain, every hard choice, every mile of ice — started from the simple fact that I was good, and good wasn't enough, and I could not for the life of me make those two facts sit together at the same table.

The Kestrel was a hundred and ten feet of shallow-draft sternwheeler, built for rivers that big boats only dreamed about. She drew two feet of water empty, four loaded, and there wasn't a pilot on the whole system who'd take her where I took her, because there wasn't a pilot on the whole system who knew the channels the way I did. I'd learned them as a girl, running trap lines with my father, learning the water by falling in it. I'd learned her engines from Hollis, who'd come aboard drunk and stayed sober, in that order. She wasn't a pretty boat. She was a working boat, with a patched boiler and a pilothouse that whistled in a north wind, and she was mine.

Except she wasn't.

That was the part the river didn't know. The river thought she was mine. The papers said otherwise.

* * *

We tied up at Corliss landing a little past noon, and Corliss Vane's agent was waiting on the dock, which was never a good sign. Agents who want to see you send word. Agents who wait are delivering something.

His name was Prosser, and he was the kind of man who'd been designed by committee: gray suit, gray face, gray manner, with a small gold watch chain across his vest that caught the light every time he moved, which was often, because he liked to be seen moving. He held a leather folio under one arm the way a preacher holds a Bible he doesn't believe in.

"Captain Whitlock." He said it the way you'd read a name off a list. "Good run?"

"Uneventful," I said, which on the river is the highest compliment there is.

"Excellent. I wonder if I might impose on you for a few minutes. Privately."

The Kestrel was my private place. But there was no use saying so, so I took him into the saloon — the small saloon, the one I kept for business, with the good chairs and the bad coffee — and poured him coffee anyway, because my mother raised me to be polite even to bailiffs.

He didn't drink it. Men like Prosser never drink what you give them. It's a kind of power move, or a kind of cowardice, or both.

"I'll come to the point," he said, unfolding a paper with a crispness that suggested he'd practiced the unfolding. "Mr. Vane has reviewed the account."

"Has he." I kept my voice level. I've had practice. Three years of practice.

"The outstanding balance on the vessel's paper stands at two thousand four hundred dollars, plus accrued interest." He let the number sit in the air between us, the way you'd set down something heavy and then step back to watch you carry it. "Mr. Vane is prepared, in his generosity, to extend terms."

"Terms," I said.

"Payment in full within thirty days." He produced a second paper from the folio, this one already signed, already sealed, waiting only for my name at the bottom like a trap waiting only for my foot. "Or surrender of the vessel. Forfeiture, Captain. The papers transfer, the boat transfers, and your account is closed."

I looked at the paper. I looked at the coffee. I looked out the saloon window at the Kestrel's deck, at the rail I'd re-varnished myself two springs ago, at the wheel I'd steered through ice and flood and one memorable night of fire, at the name on the stern that was my boat's name and, if I was honest, about half my name too.

Two thousand four hundred dollars. I had four hundred and eleven in the strongbox, and I knew the figure the way other people know their own birthdays.

"Thirty days," I said.

"Twenty-nine, now," said Prosser, with the first thing I'd seen from him that might have been a smile. "The day is half spent."

* * *

I signed nothing. I told him I'd review the terms with my attorney, which was a lie, since my attorney was a man in Corliss who owed me money too, and whose advice on the last occasion had been to marry well. Then I walked him to the gangway and watched him go up the dock with his folio and his watch chain and his committee-designed face, and I stood at the rail a long time after he was out of sight.

The river went by below, brown and patient, doing what it always did, carrying everything toward somewhere else. A raft of driftwood turned slowly in the current, going nowhere in particular with great dignity. Somewhere upstream a steamboat whistle blew, long and low, a big boat on the big water, and I felt the Kestrel shiver under my feet — or I imagined she did, which amounts to the same thing on a boat.

Here is what nobody tells you about debt. It isn't the owing. I could have owed the money forever and slept fine. It's the being owned. Vane didn't want two thousand four hundred dollars. Vane wanted the Kestrel, because the Kestrel was the only shallow-draft boat on this stretch of river, and shallow-draft boats go where the money is when the money is up small creeks in high country, and Corliss Vane had built a fortune on being the man who got there first. I was not a debtor to him. I was a convenience. And when the convenience had served, or when the convenience could be taken cheap, the convenience would be collected.

I had thirty days. Twenty-nine.

I went down to the engine room, where Hollis was lying under the starboard cylinder with a wrench in his hand and grease on everything, including, somehow, his eyebrows.

"Prosser was aboard," he said, without looking up. He always knew. I never found out how.

"He was."

"And?"

"And we've got thirty days to come up with two and a half thousand dollars, or the boat goes to Vane."

The wrench stopped. For a moment the only sound was the river slapping the hull and the boiler ticking as it cooled, the small domestic noises of a working boat at rest.

"Two and a half thousand," Hollis said.

"Two thousand four hundred. Plus what we owe the chandlery."

"Which is?"

"Which is why I said two and a half."

He slid out from under the cylinder and sat up, and looked at me, and I looked back, and for a moment neither of us said anything, because there was nothing to say that the numbers hadn't already said. Two and a half thousand dollars was a season's good profit, if the season went well, if the water stayed kind, if nobody's cargo spoiled, if the world cooperated. The world had not been cooperating. The world had been running a long con on the whole territory for two years, and we'd all been marks.

"Freight rates are down," Hollis said finally. "Passenger trade's dead since the railroad came through the valley. And winter's coming."

"Thank you, Hollis. I'd forgotten winter."

"I'm thorough."

I sat down on the tool bench, which was against regulations, and put my face in my hands for exactly as long as it took to be tired, and no longer. Then I stood up.

"Here's what we're going to do," I said. "We're going to run the upper water."

Hollis's eyebrows, which had achieved a new altitude, climbed higher. "The upper water's dead. There's nothing up there but mud landings and one trading post and a lot of country nobody's bothered to survey."

"There's freight nobody's carrying," I said. "Small lots, high margins, landings the big boats can't touch. We're the only boat on this river that can work that water, and I'm going to work it until it gives up every dollar it's got. Thirty days. We'll be running freight the whole time, and we'll come back with the money, and Vane can take his paper and fold it into a hat."

I said it like I believed it. That's most of what a captain does, when you come down to it. I said it like I believed it, and Hollis looked at me a long moment, and then he picked up his wrench and went back under the cylinder.

"Upper water it is," he said. "I'll see to the boiler. She's been spitting a little on the starboard side."

"Thank you, Hollis."

"Don't thank me. Find money."

* * *

We cast off at first light the next morning, running upriver against the current with the paddles slapping and the pilothouse whistling its north-wind whistle even though the wind was from the south, because the pilothouse whistled when it felt like it and I'd stopped arguing with it years ago. The sun came up red off the water, which the old-timers say means rain, and the old-timers are usually right, and I didn't care. Rain meant high water, and high water meant the Kestrel could go places, and going places was the only thing I knew how to do that anybody would pay for.

I stood at the wheel and read the water. There was a seam of smooth current along the left bank where the deep channel ran, and I took it, and the boat settled into it like a hand into a glove. Behind me the saloon smelled of coffee and Hollis's pipe. Ahead of me the river bent away north into country that got higher and wilder with every mile, country where the landings were mud and the money was thin but the big boats couldn't follow.

Twenty-nine days. I'd made the number my own by then, the way you make a wound your own. I'd run the arithmetic every way it could be run, and every way came out the same: possible, but only just, and only if everything went right, and nothing ever went right, and I knew that, and I went anyway, because the alternative was to stand on a dock and watch them take the wheel out of my hands.

There's a particular kind of fear that doesn't feel like fear. It feels like clarity. Like every sense sharpened, like the world reduced to a single bright point, like the moment just before you take a bar at speed and either clear it or don't. That's what I felt that morning, standing at the wheel with the river running north and the sun coming up red. Not dread, exactly. Dread is passive. This was dread with a tiller in its hands.

I was going to save my boat. I didn't yet know what saving her was going to cost, or what I'd have to become to do it, or that the price would turn out to be the boat herself. All I knew was the channel ahead, and the seam of deep water, and the thirty days, and the feel of the wheel under my hands, which was the truest thing in my life.

The river bent. I bent with it. Somewhere up that muddy water, at a landing I couldn't yet name, a dying man was waiting with a map and a promise he wanted kept, and neither of us knew the other existed.

The Kestrel carried me toward him at eight miles an hour, and the water said nothing, and I listened to it anyway.
