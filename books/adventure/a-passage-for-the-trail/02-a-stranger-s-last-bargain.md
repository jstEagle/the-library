## 2. A Stranger's Last Bargain

Ash Hollow Landing wasn't a landing so much as a suggestion the river had once made about stopping. The bank there was a brown smear of silt and alder roots, the dock a row of pilings with ambitions above their station, and the town—if you could call eleven buildings and a church with no roof a town—sat back from the water like something waiting to be forgotten. I'd tied up there a dozen times over the years, usually to take on cordwood or let a farmer argue me down to half price for hauling his pigs. It was not, in any season, a place where fortunes changed hands.

That's what I told myself, anyway, when the man came stumbling down the bank at dusk with something bundled against his chest like a swaddled infant.

I'd been on the Kestrel's aft deck doing the accounts, which is to say I'd been staring at a column of figures that refused, no matter how many times I licked my thumb and turned the page, to show me anything except ruin. Corliss Vane's agent had been in my ear at Copper Bend two days prior, all oiled courtesy and arithmetic: *Captain Whitlock, Mr. Vane is a patient man, but even patience has a bottom line. Thirty days, or the papers change hands.* The Kestrel's papers. My boat, on paper his, and the paper the only thing that made the difference.

So when the stranger hailed me from the mud with a voice like a rusted hinge, my first instinct was to tell him I wasn't running passengers, wasn't running freight, wasn't running anything but a debt with a smokestack.

"Captain," he said, and then he coughed for a long time, the wet, tearing kind of cough that I'd heard before, on other boats, in other years, and always followed by either a coffin or a miracle. "Captain, I need—"

"You need a doctor," I said. "Town's got one. Half a doctor, anyway. He shares the building with the barber, so mind which chair you sit in."

"I need a boat." He came closer, into the lamplight spilling from my cabin window, and I got my first real look at him. Sixty, maybe older—out here the mountains age men like whiskey barrels, fast and rough. His beard was iron-gray and crusted at the corners, his eyes too bright, the fever shining out of him like a lamp behind frosted glass. He was soaked to the waist and shivering, but his hands, when he lifted the bundle, were steady. That steadiness is what made me stop and look twice. Sick men shake. Dying men who are *certain* of something don't.

"I'm not running passengers," I said.

"I'm not asking for passage." He set one boot on the bottom step of my gangway, testing it, and the whole structure groaned its opinion. "I'm asking for a promise."

"Those I sell cheaper than passage. What kind?"

"The kind you keep." He climbed the steps without waiting for permission, which should have annoyed me more than it did. There was a way men moved when they'd spent thirty years telling mountains what to do with them—the same way I moved, come to think of it—and it doesn't leave room for much deference. He stopped an arm's length away and looked at me the way a man looks over a horse he's thinking of trusting his life to. "Juniper Whitlock. Kestrel. Shallow draft, stern paddle, draws eighteen inches light. You're the only boat on this river that could do it."

"Do what?" I asked, though some animal part of me already knew, and had already started backing away from it.

He unwrapped the bundle. It was oilcloth, good stuff, wrapped twice and tied with copper wire, the way you wrap something you expect to carry through a river. Inside was a leather folio, cracked and stained, and inside that, a map.

I've seen a lot of maps in my life. I've drawn a few, bad ones, and I've been lost by several good ones. This was neither. It was hand-inked on what looked like the backs of survey sheets, sewn together at the spine with sinew, and it was beautiful in the way that only desperate work is beautiful—every line laid down with a draftsman's care, every contour annotated in a small, cramped, patient hand. Elevations. Water sources. Campsites marked with dates going back years. And across the top of the whole thing, in letters that had been gone over twice with the pen: **THE PASSAGE**.

"Where's this?" I asked, though I knew that too. The mountains it crossed were the ones that had eaten every route anyone had ever tried to cut through them. The ones the railroad men had given up on. The ones that turned back a government survey in '81 with three dead mules and a broken theodolite to show for it.

"You know where it is." He sat down on my deck box without asking, another liberty I should have resented, and the fever in his eyes banked itself down to something harder. "Nobody knows where it is. That's the point. I found it. Took me nineteen years and it took—" he gestured at himself, the whole ruined architecture of him, "—well. It took what it took."

"And you're showing me this because."

"Because I'm dying, Captain, and dead men can't walk passes." He said it the way you'd say *it's Tuesday*. No self-pity in it at all, which unsettled me more than the map did. "And because my daughter is going to cross that range this year if I have to reach out of the ground and point the way myself."

I looked at the map. I looked at him. I did the arithmetic I always do, the cold little sum that has kept me alive and afloat: a map like this was worth money. Real money. To the railroad, to the mining concerns, to any of a dozen men who'd pay in gold for a route over the range that didn't require going around it by six hundred miles of river. And I owed Corliss Vane a sum that had been breeding quietly in the dark like mice in a grain sack.

"How much?" I said.

He laughed. It turned into coughing, and I stood there feeling like a fool while he coughed, and when he could breathe again he wiped his mouth and looked at me with something that might have been disappointment.

"You'd take money for it," he said. Not accusing. Just noting, the way you'd note the weather.

"Captain Whitlock takes money for everything. It's how boats stay afloat. Ask anyone."

"I did ask." He tapped the folio with one cracked fingernail. "I've been asking about you since Copper Bend. They say you're the best pilot on the river. They say you read water like a psalter. And they say—" he paused, and the fever-light flickered, "—they say you keep your word, which is rarer than the other two."

"Who says that? I'd like to shake their hand and then bill them for something."

"My name's Crane. Elias Crane." He said it like it should mean something, and it didn't, and I let him see that it didn't. "Ash Hollow. I've been up in the high country since before you were running this river. My daughter—" and here his voice did something I didn't expect. It cracked. Not from the coughing. "Her name is Ada. She lives with her aunt in Millbrook, down past the falls. She hasn't spoken to me in six years. I wrote her a letter. It's—" he fumbled at his coat, and I watched a dying man fight his own shaking hands and lose, and finally I took the coat from him, gently, the way you'd take a rifle from a drunk, and found the letter myself.

It was sealed. It was addressed in that same patient hand. *Miss Ada Crane, care of Mrs. Harrow, Millbrook.*

"I can't go get her," he said. "I know that. I've known it since the blood started coming up. But she'll come. She'll come because she's angry, and angry people travel. And when she comes, she'll need to get where I was going, because what's at the end of that map belongs to her. Not to me. To her." He laid his hand flat on the folio. "I'm not selling you the map, Captain. I'm hiring you. Take my daughter over the range before the snows close it. That's the whole job. That's the only job."

"The snows are five months off."

"The calving season is three weeks off. You know about the glacier?"

I knew the stories. Every river pilot knew the stories—the high ice that calved in late summer, shedding its skirt of broken floes into the lakes for a few weeks, and in those weeks, if you were mad and lucky, there was a walkable way across the icefield at the head of the passage. A window. Ten days, maybe, some years less, and then it sealed again for another year.

"Nobody's crossed in nine years," I said. "The last party that tried—"

"I know what happened to the last party." He said it flat. "Two of them were mine. That's why I spent nineteen years drawing this." He tapped the map again. "Every crevasse I could find. Every safe line. It's all here. It's the difference between crossing and being crossed."

I want to tell you that I thought it over carefully. That I weighed the risks like a sensible woman, that I considered my crew and my boat and my debt and my neck, and came to a reasoned decision. The truth is faster and less flattering: I looked at the map and I saw Vane's agent's face, and I saw the bottom line in my ledger, and I saw a way out that had a smokestack on it. A route over the range meant cargo. It meant being the only boat on the far side of the mountains with the only map to the only way through. It meant, possibly, if the fates were drunk and generous, enough money to buy my papers back and tell Corliss Vane where he could store his patience.

"I'll need payment," I said. "Passage, wages, outfitting. I'm not a charity."

"The map *is* the payment. Half of whatever the passage yields is yours—cargo rights, toll rights, whatever it turns out to be worth. The other half is Ada's. That's the split. It's written in the folio, witnessed, notarized at Fort Alder two years back. It's legal, Captain. I was a surveyor before I was a fool, and I know how to make paper stick."

"And your daughter? What does she get?"

"She gets through." He said it simply, and it landed on me strangely, like a stone dropped down a well I hadn't known I had. "She gets through alive, before the snows. That's what she gets. That's all I've got left to give her."

* * *

He wouldn't come aboard that night. I offered the forward bunk—I had no passengers, and the crew could grumble—and he refused, saying the boat smelled of engine oil and he'd slept thirty years under open sky and didn't propose to break the streak. So I brought him blankets, which he accepted, and coffee, which he accepted, and a plate of Tamsin's stew, which he ate with the methodical hunger of a man refueling a machine he intended to run one more day.

He talked, that night, sitting on my gangway with the river talking behind him. Not about the pass—about Ada. About a girl who used to follow him up to the survey camps at eleven years old with a compass in her pocket and mud to her knees. About how he'd taught her to read terrain the way I read water, and how she'd taken to it faster than any apprentice he'd ever had, and how he'd repaid that by choosing the mountains over her, year after year, until one year she didn't write back.

"I told myself it was for her," he said. "The work. The money. A man tells himself a lot of things at altitude. The air's thin up there. Easy to believe anything."

"You could write to her," I said. "Explain."

"I'm writing to her." He held up the letter. "That's the trouble with explaining. It takes a lifetime, and I've got—" he looked at the river, at the dark coming down over it, "—I've got the evening."

I should have said something then. Some word about fathers and daughters, about how it's never too late, or how sometimes it is, or whatever it is people say. I didn't. I'm not built for it. I went below, checked my engine room, yelled at Hollis about a bearing he'd already fixed, and came back up to find Crane asleep sitting upright against the pilings with the map folio held against his chest and the letter in his lap, and the fever burning off him into the night air like heat off a stove.

* * *

He died an hour before dawn.

I knew it before I reached him. There's a quality to the silence around a body that you can't mistake for anything else—the river keeps talking, the wind keeps working, but the man at the center of it stops pulling, the way a stopped clock stops pulling at a room. He was still sitting up. He'd wrapped the oilcloth around the folio again and wired it shut, and he'd put the letter on top of it, weighted down with a stone from the riverbank, and he'd folded his hands over the whole arrangement like a man done with his accounts.

The half-doctor from Ash Hollow—Barber Hobbs, who did teeth on Tuesdays—came down at first light and pronounced it fever, congestion, and general ruin, in that order, and asked me if the deceased had any people. "One," I said. "Downriver. She doesn't know yet." He nodded and offered to bury him behind the roofless church for two dollars, and I paid it, and I stood at the grave with my hat off while Hobbs said the words he says over everyone, the ones about dust, and I thought about how Elias Crane had spent nineteen years drawing a map so his daughter could walk a road he'd never walk with her, and how the closest he'd come to her in six years was a letter he'd never see opened.

Then I picked up the folio and I carried it aboard.

Here is what I told myself, and I want it on the record, because a woman ought to at least be honest with her own ledger: I told myself it was salvage. The man was dead. The job he'd wanted done required a boat, and I had a boat, and the map without the boat was a dead letter, and the boat without the map was Vane's property by November. The map and the promise came as a set, like a lock and its key, and I was simply keeping the set together until the rightful owner turned up. That's salvage. That's seamanship. Any court in the territory would see it my way.

What I did not tell myself—what I made very sure not to tell myself, standing at my cabin window with the folio in my hands and the sun coming up over Ash Hollow's eleven buildings and one roofless church—was that a promise isn't salvage. It doesn't wash ashore. It doesn't get logged and sold. Either you keep it or you don't, and the man who made it doesn't get a vote anymore, and neither does the ledger.

I wired the oilcloth shut again, the way he'd left it, and put it in the strongbox under my berth, and I put the letter on top of it, and I weighed the whole arrangement down with the same stone he'd used, which I realize now was the closest thing to a prayer I've ever performed.

Then I went up on deck and gave the order to cast off, because the current was good and grief, like freight, doesn't pay for idle days.

Millbrook was four days downriver. I had a letter to deliver, a daughter to disappoint, and a debt to outrun—and somewhere behind me, though I didn't know it yet, word was already moving up the river toward a man named Vane, moving faster than any boat I owned.
