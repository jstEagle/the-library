## 3. The Girl from Ash Hollow

The letter reached Ash Hollow eleven days after my father died, which meant that the river had eleven days to decide what to do with him before I was told.

That's how I've come to think of it, anyway. The river took him and kept its counsel. The post rider took his letter and ambled. And I spent those eleven days being an ordinary person — mending a fence, arguing with the Hendersons about a boundary line, sleeping whole nights through — while somewhere upstream my father's body went cold in a room I'd never seen, among strangers who had no reason to be kind to it.

I read the letter four times on the porch, and then I folded it along its original creases, because that seemed like the only thing left of him I could still do correctly.

*If you are reading this, then I have not made the crossing myself.* That was how he began. Not *I am dying*, not *forgive me*. He wrote it like a survey note. Like terrain he'd already measured and found passable.

He said a captain would come for me. He said her name was Whitlock and she held something of his and she would know the terms. He said — and this is the sentence I must have read twenty times, the one I carried upriver like a stone in my boot — *she is harder than she looks and more honest than she wants you to believe.*

Which is a fine thing to write about a stranger and a cruel thing to write about your daughter, because it assumes the daughter needs convincing. It assumes there's a version of me that would hear *your father is dead and left his affairs to a boat pilot* and simply nod and go back to the fence.

I did go back to the fence. I finished it. Then I sold the Hendersons my half of the boundary dispute for less than it was worth, packed one bag, and started walking toward the water.

People will tell you grief makes you stupid. Grief made me precise. Every decision between Ash Hollow and that dock came clean and sharp as a struck flint, because every decision was one my father wasn't there to make, and I refused to make any of them badly. It was the only inheritance I could claim without paperwork.

* * *

The Kestrel sat at the Cordwainer Landing low in the water and ugly as an argument, all mud-colored hull and patched smokestack, with her name painted on the bow in letters someone had clearly refreshed themselves rather than pay a sign-writer. I stood on the planks and looked at her for a long moment and felt nothing but suspicion, which I recognized, even then, as my father's feeling and not mine. He'd hated rivers. He'd trusted rock and ice and the honest arithmetic of elevation. A boat, to Elias Crane, was a thing that lied about standing still.

"You'll be the girl, then."

She came down the gangplank wiping her hands on a rag, and I knew her at once, though I couldn't have said how. Red-brown hair cut short and practical, sunburned to the collarbone, eyes the flat gray-green of water over silt. She looked at me the way a pilot looks at a sandbar: measuring how much of me was showing and how much was waiting underneath to wreck her.

"I'll be Ada Crane," I said. "You'll be holding my father's map."

Something moved behind her eyes — quick, gone. "News travels."

"A letter travels," I said. "Eleven days late. News would have been faster."

"Then I'm sorry for the eleven days." She didn't say it softly. She said it like a figure entered in a ledger, and I understood immediately that this was a woman who paid her debts out loud so no one could claim she hadn't. "Your father died in the night. Fever. He was lucid until near the end. He made me a bargain and I took it, and I won't pretend otherwise."

"What bargain?"

"That's between him and me."

"It's my father."

"And this is my boat." She folded the rag and tucked it into her belt with a neatness that annoyed me more than anything else she'd done. "Miss Crane. Ada. Sit down or don't, but hear me once, because I don't enjoy repeating myself and I especially don't enjoy repeating myself to people who've just decided I'm a thief."

I hadn't decided that. I'd decided she might be. There's a difference, and I suspect she knew it, and I suspect that difference is exactly why she talked to me at all instead of having a deckhand move me along.

We sat on a bollard, the two of us, with the Kestrel's shadow across our knees like a shared blanket neither of us wanted.

"He came to me dying," she said, "and he offered me a map to a high pass — a route across the range that opens when the glacier calves, ten days maybe, once a year. In exchange I was to carry his daughter through it before the snows. Those were his terms. Not cargo. Not fare. His daughter, safe, over the top."

"And you agreed."

"I agreed."

"Why?"

She looked at me then, straight on, and I give her this: she didn't flinch and she didn't perform. "Half pity," she said. "Half arithmetic. The pass promises freight that could clear what I owe on this boat, and a man dying on my deck is bad for business either way. You want me to tell you it was nobility. It wasn't. Nobility doesn't keep a hull floating."

"My father would have liked you," I said, before I could stop it, and I saw the words land on her like something thrown.

"Maybe," she said finally. "He liked honesty more than kindness, if the map's any indication. There were notes in the margins. Warnings. One of them says *the pass keeps what it takes.*"

"I know his handwriting better than anyone alive," I said. "Or dead. Whichever applies."

"And that's the trouble, isn't it." She said it almost gently, which frightened me more than anger would have. "Because from where I sit, Miss Crane, here's what I see. A man I never met dies on my deck and leaves me a paper worth real money and a story about a daughter. And now a daughter appears, knowing things only family would know, asking after that paper. Forgive me for wondering whether grief is the costume or the face."

The river slapped against the pilings. Somewhere behind us a deckhand swore at a crate.

"You want proof," I said.

"I want to not be a fool. Proofs are optional."

So I gave her the only proof I had, the one I'd been carrying since the porch. I recited it. *"She is harder than she looks and more honest than she wants you to believe."*

For the first time since I'd stepped onto those planks, Captain Juniper Whitlock looked genuinely off balance. She turned her face toward the water and worked her jaw like a woman chewing on a word she didn't want to swallow.

"He wrote that," she said. "About me."

"Word for word. I can show you the letter."

"Don't." Sharp, then quieter. "No. Show me. Damn it." She held out her hand, and I put the letter in it, and watched her read the sentence I'd carried eleven days. Her thumb pressed flat against the paper, hard, as though testing whether the ink would hold.

"He had no call writing that," she muttered. "A man should let people stay unread."

"He also wrote that you'd try to weasel out of the promise by calling it salvage."

That got a short, unwilling laugh out of her — one bark, quickly caged. "Did he." She handed the letter back with a care that contradicted everything else about her. "Your father and I are going to have words when I die, Miss Crane. Assuming we end up in the same place, which given our respective ledgers seems likely."

* * *

Here is what I learned about Juniper Whitlock that first afternoon, watching her refuse to be sentimental: she counted everything. Not money, particularly — costs. What a kindness cost, what a truth cost, what it cost to look away. She ran her boat the way my father had run his surveys, always asking what the ground would bear.

And here is what she learned about me, though she pretended otherwise: I scared her. Not my anger. My resemblance. Every time I said something in his cadence — and I couldn't help it, I was built out of his cadences — she flinched like a woman hearing her own debts read aloud.

We negotiated on the bollard until the light went long and coppery, and I'll set down the terms honestly, because she would.

"I'll carry you," she said. "Over the pass, before the snows, as promised. But the map stays with me, and the route decisions are mine, and if I say turn back, we turn back. No daughter-of-the-dead-man override."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange you work. Deckhand's share. You haul rope, you bail, you cook if my cook's drunk, and you don't touch the wheel."

"I can read terrain," I said. "Better than most men. My father trained me since I could walk. Contour, grade, snow-load, ice-rot. Put me on a ridge and I'll tell you what's under it."

Her eyes narrowed — not in doubt, exactly. In appraisal. "Everyone says they can read terrain."

"Everyone lies about other things. Ask them their ages."

The corner of her mouth moved. It was the closest thing to surrender I'd get that day.

"Terms," she said. "Passage and protection, my command, your labor. And whatever the pass yields — freight, claims, salvage, gold if there's gold — you take an equal share. Half. Because it was his map and my risk, and because I don't want you owning me any more than you want me owning you."

"Half," I agreed, and then, because precision was all I had left of him: "Witnessed by the boat."

"The boat doesn't witness. The boat floats."

"Witnessed by the boat," I repeated, and put my hand out, and after a moment she took it. Her grip was rope-callused and dry and it closed around mine like a decision.

"One more thing," she said, not letting go yet. "Whatever's in that letter of yours that isn't about me — the parts about him, about you two, about why a prospector's daughter hasn't seen her father in however many years — I'm not asking. But if it turns out to matter, if there's something in your history that follows us upriver, you tell me before it arrives. Not after. I can steer around most rocks. The ones I can't see are the ones that sink me."

It was a fair term. It was the fairest thing anyone had said to me in weeks, and I hated it, because answering it honestly meant opening the bag I'd sewn shut.

"There's a man named Rook," I said. "In my father's margins. If he's alive, he's owed something, and my father knew it, and it's the reason I haven't seen him in six years."

Juniper studied me for a long moment. Then she nodded once, slow, like a pilot marking a channel on a chart.

"Rook," she said. "Then we'd best hope the river's kinder than the mountains."

Behind her, at the far end of the dock, a man in a gray coat who had been reading a newspaper without turning its pages for the better part of an hour folded it at last, tucked it under his arm, and walked away unhurried, as though the landing belonged to him and he was merely bored with it.

Juniper didn't see him. I did.

I said nothing. Six years of my father's silences had taught me exactly how much trouble a well-timed silence can buy — and exactly what it costs later, with interest.

I boarded the Kestrel.
