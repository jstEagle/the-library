## 5. Crew of the Willing and the Desperate

You can tell a lot about a person by how they load a boat. Some stack their gear neat as a church and pray the river doesn't disagree. Some throw everything aboard in a panic and sort it out later, which is to say never. I was raised on a third way: everything heavy goes low, everything you'll need goes close, and everything you love — well, that goes where you can watch it.

By dawn on the third day after Crane died, I had the Kestrel stripped to her bones and every crate, cask, and coil on the dock at Ash Hollow waiting for my judgment. The morning fog sat on the water like something with weight to it, and the river ran high and brown with snowmelt, pushing upstream against us the whole two hundred miles we meant to go.

Going upriver in spring is not travel. It's argument. You make your case to the current one bend at a time, and the river wins most of them.

I stood on the dock with my ledger open and did arithmetic I didn't like. Provisions for six weeks if we were lucky, four if we weren't. Cordwood enough to feed the boiler through the gorges, but not through the gorges *and* a cold snap. And hands — that was the arithmetic that kept coming up short. A boat like the Kestrel wanted four crew minimum to run her hard against floodwater: pilot, engineer, deck, cook. I had myself, and a dead man's map, and a promise I'd made mostly to a corpse.

"You're going to need me," said Hollis, from behind the woodpile, because Hollis had never once in eleven years announced himself from anywhere else. He's a big man built like something a blacksmith abandoned halfway through — all shoulders and forearms and a face that looked permanently aggrieved by weather. "I heard you bought yourself a mountain."

"I didn't buy anything. I accepted payment in paper."

"Same thing, out here." He came around the pile, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up its original color years ago. "Word's all over the landing. Crane's pass. Vane's men asking questions. You'll want an engineer who can keep a boiler alive when the cordwood's wet and the water's full of silt." He paused, which for Hollis constituted eloquence. "And you'll want one who isn't afraid of you."

"I'm not frightening."

"You're frightening when you're scared. And you're scared now." He held out his hand before I could argue. "Wages same as always. Plus whatever the pass pays out, one share."

"One share," I said, and shook it, and felt the strange lightness of having said yes to something I couldn't yet name. "You know what's up there might be nothing."

"Everything's nothing until it isn't." He picked up the nearest crate like it weighed what it looked like it should rather than what it did. "Where do you want this?"

That was the thing about Hollis. Eleven years he'd fired her boiler, and in all that time I'd never once asked him why he stayed, and he'd never once told me. Some contracts don't need reading aloud.

* * *

Tamsin found us at noon, or rather Tamsin was found, folded into the shadow of the Kestrel's stern wheel like a stowaway who hadn't gotten around to hiding yet. She couldn't have been more than nineteen, thin as a whip handle, with flour still dusting her sleeves and a carpetbag clutched to her chest like a shield.

"I can cook," she said, before I'd even finished climbing down to her. "And bake. And I can mend canvas and set a bone, my mother was a midwife, and I don't eat much."

"That last part's a lie," said Hollis, peering down from the deck. "Everyone eats much at nineteen."

"I'm not going back," she said, to me specifically, in the voice people use when they've rehearsed a speech and then thrown away the speech and kept only the voice. "The man I worked for at the Landing House — Mr. Aldous — he says I owe him three years' service for my keep, and he keeps the papers, and his brother is the constable, so there's no use talking about it. I'm not going back."

I looked at her a long moment. There's a particular look a runaway gets, somewhere past fear and into the flat country beyond it, and I knew that look because I'd worn it once myself, coming downriver at sixteen with somebody else's name stitched into my collar.

"Aldous," I said. "Big man? Rings on every finger?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"He tried to buy the Kestrel's debt off Vane last year. Wanted to fold her into his landing as a floating hotel." I spat over the rail, which is a captain's privilege and a lady's liberty, and I claim both. "All right. You cook. Wages, plus a share, same terms as everyone. But hear me, Tamsin — if Aldous comes with the constable, I won't lie for you, but I'll make them work for it. That's the best I've got."

"It's more than anyone's ever got," she said, and came aboard, and by evening the smell of real bread was rolling out of the galley, and Hollis ate four rolls and pretended he'd eaten two, and just like that we were three.

* * *

Ada Whitlock — no relation, though the shared name sat between us like a stone neither of us would pick up — appeared at first light the next day wearing her father's coat, which fit her the way grief fits everybody: badly, and everywhere.

She didn't ask to come aboard. She walked up the gangplank past me like the plank owed her passage, went to the rail, and looked at the mountains northward, where they stood white and improbable above the green.

"The map," she said. "Show me."

"Good morning to you too."

"Show me the map, Captain, and I'll show you whether you can even get where it goes." She turned and looked at me straight on. She had her father's eyes, gray as slate weather, and none of his fever. "He wrote me letters my whole childhood. Every one of them had drawings in the margins. Ridgelines. Watersheds. Where the snow sits late and where it slides early. I thought they were just — him being him. Then he dies and a stranger writes to say he left me something with a riverboat captain, and I come down here and find out it's a map worth killing for." Her jaw set. "So show me, and I'll tell you if you're about to take my father's promise up a box canyon."

I got the map.

We spread it on the pilothouse chart table, weighted at the corners with the brass engine-order telegraph handles, and Ada bent over it and went quiet in a way that made the whole boat feel quieter. Her finger moved along the inked line of the river, up through the gorge, to where the water ended at a fall, then overland — and here she stopped, and frowned, and pulled the lamp closer.

"This contour work is wrong," she said. "Or — no. It's old. He drew these slopes before the slide at Widow's Stair. The trail's moved since then; see, he has the switchbacks on the east wall, but any pack train coming through now would—" She stopped herself, looked up, caught me watching her. "What?"

"Nothing. Keep going."

"He taught me to read terrain like other fathers teach scripture. I hated it. Every fishing trip was a lesson. Every walk was 'what does that slope tell you, Ada, where does that water want to go, Ada.'" Her mouth did something complicated. "I'd give a lot to hate one more lesson."

"Well," I said, "he left you one more. It's just shaped like a mountain."

She rolled the map with more care than she'd handled anything since arriving, and handed it back, and said, "I'm coming. Not because of the promise. Because it's mine."

"Half shares," I said. "Whatever the pass yields, split with the crew."

"Done." And then, because she was her father's daughter after all: "Captain — the gorge above Two Rivers. Your boat draws how much?"

"Four foot loaded."

"Then you'll be scraping paint past the Narrows shoal, and you'll want to run it on the west side where the channel's cut deep but the eddy will try to spin you. My father drew the eddy on the margin." She tapped the rolled map against her palm. "I read it last night while you all slept."

Hollis, who had appeared in the pilothouse doorway sometime during this without either of us noticing, said, "Captain, I like her better than I like you."

"Get below, Hollis."

* * *

Loading against the current took two days, and loading against the current with Vane's man watching took considerably more out of me than freight.

Merrick arrived on the second afternoon, and I knew him for what he was before he said a word, because hired guns dress like they're trying not to be noticed and end up being the only thing on a dock that anybody notices. Gray coat, gray hat, a rifle slung casual as a yoke. He didn't board. He bought an apple from a vendor, ate it slowly, leaned against a bollard, and watched us work with the patience of a man being paid by the hour.

"Friend of yours?" Tamsin asked, bringing water up to the deckhands.

"No. And that's what worries me. Friends come aboard."

We worked anyway. That's the thing about fear — it's no excuse for bad stowage. We took on cordwood until the deck groaned, grain and salt pork and beans in oilcloth, rope and pitch and spare pine for the wheel paddles. Hollis crawled into the firebox to re-caulk a seam with the boiler cold, swearing steadily in a low monotone like a psalm with all the holy words removed. Tamsin fed us all until we surrendered. And Ada — Ada stood at the rail through both days with the map's margins copied into a little notebook, cross-checking every reach of the river ahead against her father's hand, and twice she corrected my intended line before I'd even chosen it.

"Sandbar's shifted," she said, the second evening, pointing at a long pale tongue of gravel below the landing. "Your chart shows the channel hugging the far bank. His note says the bar's been building downstream for six years. You'll want the near side, slow, and you'll want to sound it first."

I sounded it first. She was right. The far channel would have put us on the bar with half the town watching and Merrick taking notes.

"How does a girl from a farm know riverbars?" I asked her.

"How does a riverboat captain know mountains?" she said. "Somebody taught us. That's the whole difference between us and the water, Captain. Somebody taught us."

* * *

We cast off at dawn on the fourth day, cold and clear, the smoke standing straight up from Ash Hollow's chimneys like plumb lines to heaven. The Kestrel took the current the way she always did — grumbling, wallowing, then finding her stride as Hollis coaxed the pressure up and the paddles bit. Going upstream, everything is slower and louder and harder, and somehow that suits me fine. A boat fighting the river knows exactly what it is.

Tamsin brought coffee to the pilothouse. Ada stood at the glass calling depths off her notebook. Hollis answered bells before I rang them, the way he does, like the engine and him share a mind and I'm just the part that talks. And for maybe an hour, running the long green water below the gorge, with the sun coming up gold on the paddle-spray, I forgot about Vane, and the debt, and the gray-coated man on the dock.

Then I saw the smoke.

Not chimney smoke. Steamer smoke, hanging low and flat downriver behind us, moving the way we were moving, patient as arithmetic. Through the glass I made her out: one of Vane's packet boats, the *Corliss B*, riding deep and fast, and standing at her rail a gray coat and a gray hat, a rifle slung casual as a yoke, a spyglass of his own raised in something that was almost a salute.

"He can't follow us into the shallows," Ada said.

"No. She draws seven feet if she draws a foot."

"Then what's he doing?"

I lowered the glass. The gorge walls were rising ahead of us, red and gold in the morning, and somewhere up there was a shoal my dead partner-in-promises had drawn an eddy around, and somewhere beyond that a mountain that kept what it took.

"He's counting," I said. "Men like Merrick count everything. Miles, days, provisions, mistakes." I hung the glass on its hook and took the wheel. "So we'll give him plenty to count, and none of it will add up. Hollis — give me everything she's got. Miss Whitlock — notebook open. We've got a gorge to insult."

The Kestrel answered her bell, and the river narrowed, and we went up into it together — a runaway, a grieving girl, a grumbling engine-man, and a captain with more promises than tonnage — while behind us, patient as winter, the smoke of our enemies followed the bend and did not lose us.

And that night, moored in the willows with sentries posted and bread still warm from Tamsin's oven, Ada sat beside me on the roof of the texas and pointed up at the white line of the range, faint under the stars.

"Ten days of melt after calving starts," she said. "Maybe less. Father wrote it in the margin."

"Then we'd best not waste tomorrow," I said.

"We'd best not waste tonight," she said, and went below to study the map again, and I sat alone a while longer, listening to the river push at my hull like a creditor's knock, and understood that whatever waited for us in that gorge — the shoal, the eddy, the gray man counting — the true race had already started, and the mountain was winning.
