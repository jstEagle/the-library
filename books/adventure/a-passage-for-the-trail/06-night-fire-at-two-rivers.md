## 6. Night Fire at Two Rivers

I have spent thirty years around engines, and I will tell you the one truth I've learned: a machine never lies to you. It may sulk, it may seize, it may throw a rod through its own chest like a gambler turning over a bad hand, but it does not lie. People lie. Machines just break, and they break honestly, and if you listen close enough you can hear them tell you exactly why.

That is why I liked the Kestrel better than most of the people I'd shipped with. Her boiler told me her moods every morning in the pitch of her smoke and the note of her steam. That night at Two Rivers, she was telling me she felt fine. It was everything else that was lying.

* * *

We'd tied up below the confluence a little past sundown, where the two rivers came together in a wide brown braided reach and the current went lazy enough to give an old man's back a rest. Juniper wanted an early start — she'd been pushing us upstream like a woman trying to outrun her own shadow, which, knowing what I knew about Corliss Vane's men on our stern, was more or less exactly what she was doing.

The supply shed sat on the bank, maybe forty yards from the boat. It wasn't ours, properly speaking — a trapper's old drying shack we'd rented off a half-Métis fellow named Baptiste for more than it was worth — but it held half our outfit: salt pork, flour, beans, the spare boiler fittings I'd hauled clear from St. Aubin, and the lamp oil. Forty yards of open ground between the shed and the water. Remember that distance. It matters to this story.

Supper was Tamsin's beans and a cornbread that Piet ate four pieces of and swore on his mother's grave was the best thing he'd ever tasted, which tells you either a great deal about Tamsin or very little about Piet's mother. The boy was seventeen if he was a day, all elbows and appetite, with a way of looking at Juniper like she was scripture and he was still deciding whether to believe it. He'd signed on at the last landing for wages nobody honest would take, and when I asked him why, he said he had kin upriver. Everybody has kin upriver. It's the answer you give when you don't want to give the real one.

Ada Crane sat apart from the fire, as she always did, with her father's map wrapped in oilcloth inside her coat. She didn't talk much. But she watched everything, the way her father must have watched country before he ever walked it, and I'd learned by then that when Ada looked at you, you'd been *measured*, not seen. There's a difference. A man can live his whole life being seen and never once measured.

I turned in early. Old men and boilers keep the same hours: sleep when you can, worry when you can't.

* * *

I woke because the dog started screaming.

We didn't have a dog. That's how fast my mind was working — I heard an animal shrieking and catalogued the fact that we owned no animal, and then I smelled the smoke, and then I was out of my bunk and through the deckhouse door before my boots were fully on, which at my age is a feat I paid for the next morning and am still paying for now.

The shed was burning.

Not smoldering — *burning*, flames already through the roof in three places, and the light of it turned the river orange and threw shadows of all of us up against the trees like a crowd of tall thin strangers come to watch. Somebody had fed it. Lamp oil burns hot and fast, and this fire had that greedy roar to it, the sound of something drinking.

"Boat!" Juniper's voice cut across the yard, flat and hard as a caulking mallet. "Hollis, the boat — get her loose!"

She'd seen it before I had. The shed was forty yards off, sure, but the wind was coming downriver out of the north, and the sparks were going to walk across that open ground straight to the Kestrel's tarred seams and dry pine deckhouse, and a riverboat fire is not a fire, it's a funeral with a schedule. I'd seen one burn at Fort Chaudière when I was a young man. Eleven people. I can still hear it if I let myself, so mostly I don't.

I got steam up in twelve minutes, which I'll brag about until I die, because it should have taken twenty. Tamsin worked the pump like she'd been born to it, hauling river water and throwing it over the deckhouse roof while Piet and I warped the Kestrel stern-first out into the channel, fifty feet, a hundred, far enough that the falling sparks landed on water instead of wood. Juniper stayed on the bank — of course she did, she'd have stayed in the shed itself if holding the line there would've helped — directing buckets at the grass line where the fire tried to run sideways toward the timber.

And Ada — I want to set this down plain, because it was the first time any of us saw what she was made of. While the rest of us fought the fire, Ada went *into* it. Into the smoke at the shed's lean-to side, where the heat was a physical hand shoving you back, and came out dragging the map case, the survey instruments, and Elias Crane's journal box, her eyebrows singed to stubble and her voice steady as a spirit level saying, "This is all that can't be replaced."

Then the roof came down, and that was that.

* * *

By midnight the shed was a black skeleton smoking in the rain we finally got, and we stood around in the mud taking inventory of what we'd lost, which is a particular kind of arithmetic. Salt pork: gone, or near enough. Flour: gone. Half the beans, all the lamp oil, both spare lanterns, and — this is the one that made me sit down on a crate like an old man, which I am — the spare boiler fittings. My fittings. I'd carried those brass and iron pieces three hundred miles against my own chest like a woman carrying twins.

"Half our provisions," Juniper said. She said it without drama, the way she said everything, but I've known Juniper Whitlock long enough to read the small print under her voice. Half our provisions meant we bought more at the high-lakes post, and buying more meant money, and money was the exact thing we did not have. Vane held her papers. Every dollar we spent was a dollar closer to forfeit.

"Could've been worse," Tamsin said. She had soot to the elbows and a blister starting on one wrist and she said it anyway, because that was Tamsin — runaway cook, cheerful as a Sunday, and tough underneath like good bread.

"How," said Piet, "could it possibly have been worse?"

"The boat's floating," I said. "Nobody's dead. In my experience those are the only two measurements that matter." I'd been in fires where neither was true. I didn't say so. Some inventory you keep to yourself.

Juniper crouched at the edge of the burned foundation, poking through the wreckage with a stick, and after a while she went quiet in a way that made the rest of us quiet too. Then she stood up and held something out on the flat of her hand.

It was the padlock. Or what was left of it — the hasp, rather, torn free of the door, and here was the thing: the lock wasn't melted through. It was *broken*. Pried. From the inside of the frame, before the fire ever really took hold.

"Somebody opened this door," Juniper said. "Somebody unlocked it, took their time, spread the oil, and walked away. This wasn't a spark off a cookfire."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The river made its noises. Somewhere up the bank a loon called, that sound like a madman laughing through water.

"Who knew what was in the shed?" Juniper asked.

All of us. That was the trouble. All of us knew, and all of us had been ashore at various hours, and Two Rivers wasn't a town but it wasn't nothing either — a trading cabin, a fish weir, Baptiste's place a mile down, and Baptiste had a brother who drank and talked. Anybody could have sold word of our stores to anybody. Merrick's kind of anybody.

But then Piet spoke up, and I could hear something wrong in his voice, some wire pulled too tight. "Maybe it was Baptiste's brother. Maybe it was just — trappers wanting the oil. Happens all the time on the river."

"Sure," said Tamsin, easy. "Trappers who leave the oil to burn and take nothing."

Piet's face did something complicated and settled on nothing at all. "I'm tired," he said, and walked off toward the bow, and I watched him go and felt the cold start in my stomach, because I'd spent thirty years listening to machines and I know the sound a thing makes just before it fails.

* * *

He was gone by morning.

Not dead-gone. Run-gone. His bedroll still there, his tin plate washed and stacked — washed and *stacked*, which struck me as the saddest detail of the whole night, a boy washing his dishes before he deserted — and his wages untouched in the strongbox, which Juniper took out and looked at for a long time without touching.

"He ran because he's guilty," I said. We were standing at the rail watching the gray come up over the water. "Or he ran because he's scared. At my age you learn those look identical from the outside."

"You think he fired the shed?" Juniper asked. Not accusing me of anything. Just asking, the way she asks, like your answer is cargo she'll weigh honestly.

"I think somebody paid somebody," I said. "Merrick doesn't need to burn us himself. He needs one hungry boy and twenty dollars, and there's always a hungry boy. I was a hungry boy once myself. Only difference between me and Piet is nobody found me before I found my own feet."

Juniper grunted. It wasn't agreement, exactly. It was the sound she makes when a thing is true and she hates it.

Ada came up on deck then, hair still smelling of smoke, and she'd plainly heard the last of it, because she said, "You're both wrong. Or half-wrong, which is worse."

"Enlighten us," said Juniper, and there was an edge there, because Ada had been aboard nine days and was already talking to the captain like a colleague, and the strange thing was that Juniper let her.

"Piet cried at supper," Ada said. "When you mentioned the calving season. When you said ten days, maybe less. He cried and covered it with the cornbread." She looked out at the water. "My father used to say you can tell a guilty man by how still he sits and a frightened man by how much he eats. Piet ate like a man trying to fill a hole that food doesn't reach. And he asked me yesterday — twice — how many days to the high lakes, and whether there was a telegraph office there, and who we'd meet when we arrived. Guilty men don't ask who's waiting for them. Frightened ones do."

"So he was asking for someone else," I said slowly. "Reporting."

"Or being made to report," Ada said. "There's a difference, and it matters. Whoever holds a boy's family holds the boy. My father taught me that too, though I wish he hadn't had occasion to." Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under ice, and was gone. "If we find him, don't hang him. Find out who's holding the rope on his end."

"We're not hanging anybody," Juniper said, and I believed her, and I also noticed she didn't say *we're not finding him*, which was its own kind of sentence.

* * *

We buried what couldn't be saved that afternoon — or rather, we didn't bury it, we burned the last of it, because you don't leave flour and grease in a fire pit for every fox and vagrant on the river, and there's a grim economy in letting a fire finish its work. Tamsin stood over it with her hands on her hips and did sums aloud: what we had, what we needed, what the high-lakes post would charge us triple for because they always charge triple, the whole world being arranged to charge triple to people in a hurry.

"We can make it," she concluded. "Thin. Beans and rice and whatever Hollis can shoot, which given Hollis is nothing."

"I shot a duck once," I offered.

"It was asleep," said Tamsin.

"It was resting."

That got the first laugh out of anybody since the fire, and I'll tell you what I've learned about crews, since I seem to be writing the history here: a crew isn't people who like each other. It's people who've lost the same things together and decided to keep going anyway. By nightfall we'd lost a shed, half our stores, and a boy, and we were still going, and somewhere in the arithmetic of that we became something sturdier than we'd been at supper the night before. Grief does that, if you ration it right.

Juniper doubled the anchor watch. I took first shift, sitting in the deckhouse with the boiler ticking as it cooled, listening to the river and the woods and the not-silence of a country full of things watching you back. Around two in the morning I heard footsteps on the bank — one set, light, moving fast south along the game trail — and I had the rifle up and leveled at the dark before my brain caught up with my hands.

I didn't fire. Whatever it was, it was gone in ten seconds, and I sat there telling myself it was a deer, and mostly believing it.

But here's the thing I keep circling, the splinter in the thumb of this whole story: Piet's plate was washed. His wages were left. A thief takes the money. A saboteur takes payment. Piet took nothing but himself, and he went south, and Merrick's steamer was last seen anchored at the mouth of the south fork.

Ada says whoever holds a boy's family holds the boy. Well. I'm an engineer, and engineers believe in pressure systems. Somewhere ahead of us, somebody was squeezing, and Piet was just the first drop forced out of the pipe.

At dawn Juniper gave the order to cast off, and we took the Kestrel up into the narrows above Two Rivers with half our belly empty and all of our eyes open, and if the current seemed stronger that morning than any of us remembered, I expect that's because we were lighter and wearier and every pound of lost pork was a pound of margin gone. The river didn't care. Rivers never do. That's the other thing thirty years teaches you: the water is honest, and it is not on your side, and the only question worth asking each morning is whether you're good enough to be on its.

By noon we could see, far behind us and far below, a smudge of black smoke standing up off the water where no town was.

Steamer smoke.

Juniper looked at it a long time through the glass, and then she folded the glass shut and said one word — "Faster" — and I went down to my engine room, where at least the machinery only ever tells the truth.
