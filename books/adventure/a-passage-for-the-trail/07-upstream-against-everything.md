## 7. Upstream Against Everything

There is a particular sound a river makes when it has decided to kill you. Most people never learn it. Most people, if they're lucky, never stand on the deck of a shallow-draft sternwheeler at the mouth of a flooded gorge with spring meltwater running three feet over the banks and a steamer full of hired guns crawling up their wake.

I had learned the sound years ago, and I heard it that morning clear as a church bell.

The Kestrel's paddle wheel bit into water the color of strong tea, and the river answered with a low, grinding moan — the sound of whole trees traveling just under the surface, of boulders shifting their opinions, of a channel that had been a channel last week and was now a rumor. The gorge walls rose on either side of us, gray granite streaked with snowmelt, close enough in places that I could have tossed a biscuit to the rocks. The water ran fast enough that the current itself seemed to have a texture, a nap to it, like fur brushed the wrong way.

"Hollis!" I called down through the deck grating. "I want everything she has, and then I want you to find a little more."

"You want a miracle, you should've hired a preacher," Hollis's voice came up through the boards, gravel over gravel. But the engine note climbed half a tone, and the deck began to shudder in that particular way that told me my engineer was feeding the fire oil we couldn't afford to burn. That's the thing about Hollis. He'll complain about a thing right up until the moment he does it, and then he'll complain about having done it for a week afterward. It's a system, and it works.

We were three days out from Two Rivers, running light — lighter than I liked, after the fire took half our provisions, but lighter than the river could argue with. That was the whole arithmetic of the Kestrel. Every other boat on this water drew four, five, six feet. The Kestrel drew thirty inches with her bunkers full and forty pounds of sass. She was built in a shipyard that had gone under, by a designer who drank, for a route nobody believed in, and she was the finest vessel I had ever loved, which is a sentence that describes most of the bad decisions of my life.

The problem with running light is that the river knows it. A heavy boat plows. A light one skitters. And in flood, in a gorge, with the current doing eight knots against us, skittering is a thing you do on the edge of a very long conversation with the rocks.

I stood at the wheel with one hand on the brass and one hand on nothing at all, because the other was busy pointing at water.

"Bar, two points off the bow," I said. Piet, who had come back to us two days after vanishing — come back thin and silent and flinching at loud noises, and who I hadn't yet decided whether to forgive or feed to the river — stood at the lead line. He swung it, and the call came back thin: "Mark twain. Mark twain."

Twelve feet. In flood. Which meant the bar had grown since the last freshet moved it, and the channel that the charts — such as they were, and they were mostly my own pencil — said was deep on the left had likely shifted right.

"Right side," I said. "Watch your lead."

"Mark twain," Piet called again, and then, a breath later, the sound every pilot lives on: "Quarter less twain."

Nine feet. Still plenty for the Kestrel. Still plenty for anything but the thing I could see now, half a mile back around the bend we'd rounded twenty minutes ago: a long gray smear of woodsmoke hanging in the gorge air where no woodsmoke had any business being.

The Corliss Vane Line ran two steamers on this river, and the bigger of them was the *Iron Belle* — side-wheel, two hundred tons, drawing five and a half feet when she was empty and more when she wasn't, which she wasn't, because Merrick had loaded her with men and coal and, I suspected, enough rope and grapnels to make our lives interesting. She couldn't catch us. That was the one clean fact in my morning. In this water, at this stage, with this gorge, the *Iron Belle* made four knots against a current that gave the Kestrel's shallow hull less purchase to fight and less draft to fear. We were running away from them at a rate that would have been funny if it weren't so temporary.

Rivers end. That's the thing about them. You can outrun a steamer for two hundred miles, but every river eventually arrives somewhere, and so does every man with a rifle and a payroll.

"Smoke again," Tamsin said from the rail. She'd taken to standing watch on the stern when she wasn't cooking, which she mostly wasn't, because half our galley stores were ash and the other half were rationed down to a menu Hollis called "monastic" and Tamsin called "a crime." She had a spyglass that had once belonged to somebody's grandfather and a way of reporting bad news in the flat voice of a woman who has already priced it. "Same bearing. He's keeping pace. Not closing."

"He doesn't have to close," I said. "He's a clock. He's letting us run the spring out of ourselves."

Ada stood at the rail beside her, map case slung across her body like a bandolier. She'd taken to wearing her father's map that way, and I'd taken to not commenting on it. Three days upriver, and the girl had stopped looking like a mourner and started looking like something else — something I couldn't name yet, but which watched the water the way I did, and watched the smoke behind us the way I wished I didn't.

"He's right about one thing," she said. "We can't keep this up."

"I'm aware."

"Are you? Because you're smiling."

I was. It's a fault. When the water gets mean, some pilots go quiet and some pray, and I grin like an idiot, and I've never once been able to explain it to a person who doesn't feel it. There is a moment — you get it maybe twice a season if the season is kind — when the boat and the current and your own hands all agree on something at the same instant, and for the length of that instant you are not a woman with debts and dead men's promises and a gunboat on her wake. You are just a blade moving through a sheath of water, and the river, which hates everyone, hates you slightly less.

"Quarter less twain!" Piet called. "Quarter less twain!"

The bar. It had built out from the right bank in a long yellow tongue, and the flood was breaking over it in a line of standing waves like a saw's teeth. Left side was the old channel, deep last summer, but the high water had pushed a raft of logs into it — I could see the ends of them, black and patient, rolling just under the surface like a school of very large, very dead fish.

Logs or bar. That was the menu. A log through a light hull at eight knots of current is a hole. A bar is a grounding, and a grounding in flood in a gorge with the *Iron Belle* coming is a death sentence with paperwork.

Except.

I leaned out over the rail. The bar wasn't uniform. Bars never are. There — where the standing waves flattened for a boat-length, where the water went glassy and green for just a moment before piling up again — there was a scour channel. A fresh one, cut by the flood itself, winding across the bar's spine like a snake across a road. The river had dug itself a keyhole and was keeping it secret.

"Hold your lead," I said. "Piet. I need you reading every foot."

He swallowed. The boy had been half-useless since Two Rivers, jumping at hatch-covers, and now I was asking him to stand at the bow of a boat threading a needle at flood stage with the man who'd scared him out of his wits somewhere behind us. To his credit — and I made a note of it, the way I make notes of everything, for the day the ledger balances — he squared his shoulders, spat over the side for luck, and swung the line.

"Quarter less twain," he called. "Mark twain. Quarter less — quarter less *twain* —"

I took us into the keyhole.

I won't pretend I can describe what that's like to anyone who hasn't done it. The boat goes where your hands say, and your hands say what your eyes tell them, and your eyes are reading the water the way a priest reads scripture — every ripple a verse, every smooth place a promise, and somewhere in all of it a meaning, one meaning, the only one that matters, which is *this is where the deep water is, this instant, right here.* You are not steering a boat. You are agreeing with a river. And for a hundred yards, with the bar's teeth breaking white on either side close enough to spit across, with Hollis howling something up through the grating that was either encouragement or a prayer, the Kestrel and I agreed with the river all the way across.

Then the stern swung a half-point wide — a gust off the gorge wall, nothing, a breath — and the port quarter kissed the bar with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

We didn't hang up. The current was too strong to let us; it took the stern and swung it like a gate, and for three full seconds I was steering a boat that was sideways in a flooded gorge, and I will be honest with you, whoever you are: in those three seconds I saw my whole life laid out with terrible clarity, and the thing I noticed most was how short the list was of people who'd mourn me, and how recently that list had gotten longer.

Then the stern came around, the wheel answered, the water deepened, and we were through. The gorge opened a little. The bar fell behind. Piet was gripping the lead line with both hands and looking at me with an expression I couldn't read, and Tamsin said, very quietly, "Well," and Ada said, less quietly, "Do you *grin* like that all the time?" and Hollis's voice came up through the grating: "If you ever do that again, Miss Whitlock, I want it in writing first. Signed. Witnessed. Notarized."

"Noted," I said, and grinned harder.

* * *

We made the narrows above the gorge by midafternoon and tied up at a rock ledge that passed for a landing because there was a ring-bolt in the stone and a flat place to stand, and in this country that's a metropolis. The *Iron Belle*'s smoke had fallen back out of sight around the last bend, but I didn't let that comfort me. Merrick wasn't chasing us anymore. Merrick had stopped chasing us somewhere around noon, and that frightened me considerably more.

"He's pacing us," Hollis said. We stood at the rail with tin cups of Tamsin's coffee, which was less a beverage than a moral force. "Big boat, big coal bunkers. He can sit back there comfortable as Sunday while we burn everything we've got staying ahead of him. He doesn't need to catch us. He needs us tired."

"I know."

"Do you? Because we've got — " he counted on thick fingers — "call it nine days of coal at the rate you're driving her, four days of food if Tamsin keeps stretching the beans, and about two hundred miles of river between here and the head of navigation. After that, it's the portage, and the portage needs pack animals, and pack animals need money, and money is the one thing on this boat we're not carrying."

"I'm aware of our situation, Hollis."

"Are you? Because from where I stand, the situation is that we're winning a race we can't finish. Every mile we put between us and that steamer, we're also putting between us and our supplies. He knows it. That's why he stopped."

That was why he stopped. I'd known it since the smoke held steady, and hearing it said out loud didn't make it better. Merrick had done the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was simple: the Kestrel was fast and shallow and mortal. She'd outrun him to the head of navigation, maybe. And then she'd be a boat at the end of a river, out of coal, out of food, out of river, with a crew too exhausted to fight and a map worth a fortune, and the *Iron Belle* would arrive the next day, or the day after, unhurried, well-fed, and full of men.

Speed wasn't going to save us. Speed was going to spend us.

I looked up the gorge. The river came down out of the high country in a series of steps — rapids, pools, rapids — and above the last navigable reach, the canyon pinched into something the trappers called the Throat, where the water ran white and the walls came together close enough to shake hands. No steamer had ever gone above the Throat. No steamer ever would; there wasn't water enough in a wet year, and this was a wet year. But the Kestrel drew thirty inches.

Thirty inches. It's a strange thing to build a life on, a number that small.

"Hollis," I said. "If I took her up the Throat —"

"Take her up the — " He set his cup down on the capstan with the care of a man who needs to put something down before he throws it. "Juniper. The Throat's got a drop in it. Two drops. I sounded them with a pole from the rocks last spring, when we were running timber, and I'll tell you what I told the timber crew: the lower drop's got maybe two and a half feet on the crest, and the water's running so fast through the chute below it that a duck would need a contract."

"And the upper?"

"The upper I didn't pole, because the lower drop was between me and it, and I like being on this side of rapids I've insulted."

"Two and a half feet on the crest," I said. "At spring flood."

"At spring flood, which is *now*, which means it might be three, might be two, might be a hole the size of a church with a rock in the bottom of it. And even if you got over the crest, the chute below would take a boat your size and fold her like a letter. You'd need to run it with the engine wide open, dead downstream, in flood, in a slot canyon, on water nobody has ever read." He picked his cup back up. "And I'd like to state for the record that I am against it."

"Stated for the record."

"Because you're going to do it anyway."

I didn't answer, which was an answer, and he drank his coffee with the face of a man attending his own funeral and finding the eulogy inaccurate.

Here is the truth of it, and I'll set it down plainly because I didn't say it plainly to them, then or maybe ever. The Kestrel was built for exactly one thing: going where big boats can't. That was her whole reason for existing, the obsession of a dead shipwright and the inheritance of a woman who'd bought her cheap and loved her expensive. And I had been running her like a freight boat — safe routes, known channels, the long way around — because a boat you love is a boat you protect, and protection is just fear wearing work clothes. Merrick was betting that I'd keep doing it. That I'd run out the clock, mile by honest mile, until there was nothing left to run.

But the map in Ada's map case didn't go up a river. It went up a mountain. And between the river and the mountain there was a stretch of water that the Corliss Vane Line could not follow us through, not with all the coal in Colorado — a stretch of water that only one boat in the world could cross, and I happened to be standing on her deck drinking terrible coffee.

"He can't follow us above the Throat," I said.

Hollis looked at me over his cup.

"Nobody can," I said. "Not the *Iron Belle*, not Merrick, not any boat Vane can hire or build. Above the Throat, the river belongs to the Kestrel and whatever she can carry. We run it tomorrow at first light, when the meltwater's lowest, and we leave him sitting below with his guns and his payroll and nothing to shoot at but fish."

"You don't know the water."

"Nobody knows the water. That's the point. He can buy every pilot on the river and it won't buy him a draft shallow enough to follow us. For the first time since Two Rivers, we'll be somewhere his money can't reach."

Ada had come up the deck without either of us hearing her — she moved quiet as a cat with a grievance — and she stood at the rail looking up the gorge toward the Throat, where the canyon walls pinched together and the river's voice changed pitch, from a moan to something higher and angrier.

"My father wrote about the Throat," she said.

I turned. "He ran it?"

"He surveyed the portage around it, years ago, with Rook. Before the falling-out." She said the name the way you'd handle something with edges. "There's a page in the map case I haven't shown you yet. I was going to wait until we needed it."

"We need it," I said.

She looked at me a long moment — the measuring look, the one that was getting less suspicious and more appraising by the day, which I couldn't decide was better — and then she unslung the case and drew out a sheet I hadn't seen, a survey sketch in Elias Crane's cramped hand, all fine ruled lines and marginal notes, and in the corner, underlined twice, a sentence in ink gone brown with age:

*The Throat can be run at low water by a boat drawing less than three feet, but only by a fool or a Whitlock, and I have never met a Whitlock I'd trust with my daughter.*

I read it twice. Then I laughed, and it came out louder than I meant it to, echoing off the gorge walls, and Hollis looked at me like I'd finally gone all the way round the bend.

"Well," I said, folding the sketch closed and handing it back to her. "He wasn't wrong about the fool."

Above the Throat, the river went on talking to itself, high and angry and patient, and somewhere behind us, around the bend, a big gray steamer sat on the water like a promise. Tomorrow we'd go where it couldn't. Tonight, I intended to sleep like the dead — or like the soon-to-be, which amounted to the same thing, and at least the view was better.
