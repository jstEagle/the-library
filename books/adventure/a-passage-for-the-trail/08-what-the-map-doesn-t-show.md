## 8. What the Map Doesn't Show

I couldn't sleep in that bunk. Not because it was uncomfortable — though it was, a plank with ambitions — but because the boat itself wouldn't stop talking. The Kestrel creaked and ticked and settled against the current like an old woman rearranging herself in a chair, and every sound had an opinion about me being aboard.

So I got up. I took my father's map out of the oilcloth where I'd kept it since Ash Hollow, and I carried it up to the pilothouse, where Juniper Whitlock kept a lamp burning low over her charts, and I sat down at her table without asking, because asking would have meant admitting I'd been lying awake thinking about a dead man's ink.

She was there, of course. Captains are always there. She looked up from a ledger full of numbers that were clearly losing, took in the map, the hour, and my face, and said nothing, which I was learning was her way of saying *sit down, then.*

I sat.

* * *

You should understand something about my father before you understand the map. Elias Crane did everything twice: once for show and once for true. He'd write a letter to the assayer full of confident figures, and then he'd pencil the real ones in the margin, small, turned sideways, so you had to hold the page to your ear like a shell to hear them. As a girl I thought this was a game. It was only later I understood it was how a man survives his partners.

The map was beautiful. That's the first thing. He'd spent hours on it — the range drawn in fine cross-hatching, the river systems in blue that had gone tea-colored with age, the pass itself marked with a triangle and the words HIGH ROUTE — CALVES MID-SUMMER in his drafting hand, each letter a small monument. Anyone looking at it would see a prospector's treasure map and think: gold. Everyone looking at it had thought: gold.

But I held it to my ear.

There, along the glacier's edge, in letters no bigger than gnats, written sideways: *R. knows the lower survey is false. If he comes looking, tell him the corner-stone record went into the lake with the rest. Tell him I'm sorry. Tell him anything. Just don't let him have it.*

And below the triangle, in the same tiny hand, five words that I read four times before they stopped moving:

*The pass keeps what it takes.*

* * *

"Juniper." My voice came out strange. "Who's Rook?"

She didn't look up from her ledger right away. When she did, her eyes went to the map, then to me, and I watched her make the same calculation she made about everything — what it costs, what it pays, whether the two can be made to match.

"Silas Rook," she said. "He ran a trading post at the high lakes, back when I first worked this water. Half-blind now, they say. Mean as a snapped towline, they also say. Why?"

I turned the map around and pushed it across the table. "Because my father wrote him into the margins."

She put down her pen. That was how I knew it mattered; Juniper Whitlock treated her pen like a rudder — you didn't let go of it in moving water.

She read for a long time. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below us, Hollis snored like a boiler with a grievance, and Tamsin turned over in the galley, and the river went on doing whatever rivers do at night when nobody's watching — which is, I suppose, exactly what rivers do when everybody is.

"There's more," I said, and showed her the warning.

She read that longer still. Then she sat back and rubbed her eyes with the heels of her hands, and for a moment she looked not like a captain but like a woman my mother's age, which is to say tired in a way sleep doesn't fix.

"'Keeps what it takes,'" she repeated. "Your father had a poet's streak and a coward's timing."

"He wasn't a coward."

"I didn't say he was. I said his timing was." She tapped the margin note. "A man who writes *tell him I'm sorry* instead of going to say it — that's a man who's been sorry for a long time and found writing cheaper than rowing. I know the type." A pause. "I am the type, some months."

I should have bristled. Instead I heard myself say, "What do you owe? Exactly. Vane's debt."

Her chin came up. For a second I thought she'd throw me out of the pilothouse, and part of me — the part that had grown up watching adults lie beautifully — braced for it. But she exhaled through her nose and said, "Four hundred and eleven dollars, and change. Change matters at that altitude. Papers held against the hull. Thirty days when Vane's man said it, twenty-six now." She picked her pen back up, which meant the audience was over. "Why do you want to know?"

"Because you asked me to share a boat with you based on a dead man's promise, and I've been wondering if you're honest or just desperate."

"And now?"

"Now I know you're both. It's better than neither."

Something happened to her mouth then. It wasn't quite a smile. It was the place a smile goes to decide whether it's safe to come out.

* * *

We talked until the lamp burned low. Not about the claim — we didn't yet know there was a claim, only gold-shaped rumors — but about smaller, stranger things. She told me about the first boat she ever steered, a leaky scow called the *Maggie B* that she'd won in a card game she cheated at, and how she'd felt guilty about the cheating for one entire day before deciding the river didn't care how you came by your boat, only how you handled it. I told her about learning to read terrain from my father — how he'd teach me by making me close my eyes and describe the valley behind us, slope by slope, until I could walk a country backward in my head.

"That's why you stood at the rail reading the gorge," she said. "In the shallows. You weren't scared. You were *reading*."

"I was also scared."

"Both can be true. Usually are." She leaned back. "My father taught me cards and current. Yours taught you country. Between us we might make one whole person."

It was the closest thing to kindness anyone had said to me since the letter came, and it arrived sideways, wrapped in arithmetic, the way all her kindnesses did. I found I liked it better that way. Kindness offered straight-on demands gratitude, and I wasn't ready to owe anybody anything else.

I showed her how my father hid his figures — the sideways script, the marginal hand — and watched her go through the rest of the map with new eyes. We found three more notes. One gave distances between landmarks in paces rather than miles, which told me he'd measured them himself, on foot, which told me he'd walked this route more than once and wanted to be able to find it again blind. One was a tally beside the triangle: *10 days, maybe 12, never 14.* And the last one, near the lake where the river ended, was a single word underlined twice:

*Widow's.*

"Widow's Stair," Juniper said quietly. "The portage. Hollis has talked about it — says the river dies at a falls up there and everything must go overland on men's backs." She looked at me. "Your father portaged it. Alone, by the look of these figures. With surveying instruments." She tapped the margin note about the corner-stone record again. "And whatever a corner-stone record is, he dropped it in a lake rather than let Rook have it."

"Or he lied to Rook about dropping it."

"Or that." Her eyes came up sharp. "You think like a Crane."

"I'll take that as a compliment and check later whether it was one."

That got the almost-smile again. But then it faded, and she turned the map so the warning faced her, and read it aloud once more, slow, giving each word its weight: "*The pass keeps what it takes.*" She set it down. "Your father doesn't write warnings like a superstitious man. He writes like a man who's paid something already. Do you know what he lost up there?"

"No." My throat tightened. "He left when I was twelve. Everything I know of those years is letters with the true figures hidden in the margins. Whatever the pass took from him, he never wrote it plain."

Juniper nodded slowly, and I understood she was filing this away next to her own unspoken losses — the boat, the debt, whatever had taught a woman to treat hope as a line item.

"Then we find out together," she said. "And Ada — if Rook is alive, and half the stories say he is, he'll have heard your father's dead by now. Men like Rook keep ledgers too. Ours just became an entry in his."

Outside, the river muttered against the hull. Down in the gorge country ahead, somewhere past the sandbars and the shadowing smoke of Merrick's steamer, a glacier was waiting to calve, keeping its own accounts.

I folded the map along its old creases and held it a moment before putting it away — held it to my ear one last time, like a shell.

This time I thought I heard my father's voice under the river's: not *I'm sorry*, and not *gold*, but something plainer, meant only for whoever loved him enough to read sideways.

*Come careful,* it said. *Come anyway.*

I looked up and found Juniper watching me, and neither of us said anything, and it was the easiest silence I'd ever kept with another human being.

Then Hollis's boots hit the deck above, and his voice came down the companionway, rough with alarm: "Captain — light on the water. Astern. It ain't the steamer this time. It's smaller. And it ain't stopping."
