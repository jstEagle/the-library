## 9. The Portage at Widow's Stair

I've spent forty years around engines, and I'll tell you the truth about them: an engine never lies to you. It grinds when it's worn, it knocks when it's starved, it dies when you've neglected it, and it does all of this without apology or malice. A river is the same way, only bigger and wetter. People are the ones that lie.

The river lied to us for three days.

It ran smooth and green below the gorge, wide as a church and twice as quiet, and every mile of it whispered *easy, easy* the way a card sharp whispers before he lifts your watch. Juniper didn't trust it. I watched her stand at the wheel with her thumbs hooked in her belt, reading the water like a ledger she suspected of fraud, and on the third morning her suspicion paid out. The current quickened. The banks drew in. And then we came around a bend of red granite and there it was: Widow's Stair, dropping two hundred feet in three white steps, loud enough that Hollis Fairweather heard it before he saw it and felt it in his back teeth after.

The river ended there. Just — ended. All that water we'd fought upstream for nine days, going over the edge into mist, and above it the channel narrowed to a creek you could spit across.

"Well," said Tamsin, standing beside me at the rail with her arms crossed. "That's a rude surprise."

"That's not a surprise," I said. "That's a portage."

She looked at me. "A what?"

"A word that means everything comes off the boat," I said, "and goes up the hill."

* * *

Here is what a portage actually is, for those fortunate enough never to have performed one. You take a boat apart down to its bones. Engine off its mounts — and my engine, the Kestrel's heart, a thirty-horsepower Atlas I'd kept alive longer than some men keep marriages — comes out first, because it's the heaviest and the most particular. Then the boiler sections, if you're fool enough to have a boiler, which we were. Then cargo, provisions, rope, tools, the longboat we'd lashed to the roof, and finally the hull itself, hauled up rollers and skids by main strength.

Everything must go up a trail so steep the locals had named it for dead women, past a waterfall so loud you couldn't hear yourself pray, and over four miles of ridge to the still water above.

Juniper laid it out for us on the deck that first evening, chalk marks on a plank: loads, rotations, rest stops. She'd done this before, somewhere, sometime she didn't talk about, and watching her plan I understood that whatever else she was — debtor, dreamer, woman running from a man named Vane — she was also a person who knew how to move a mountain one armload at a time.

"Four days," she said. "If the weather holds and nobody breaks."

"And if somebody breaks?" Ada asked.

"Then five," Juniper said, "and we carry them too."

Ada nodded like that was a reasonable answer, which told me something about her upbringing. Crane's blood. The old man had been half-mad but he hadn't been soft.

We started at first light. I'll spare you the poetry. Portage work is not poetic. It is a rope across your shoulders and a load that weighs exactly as much as you foolishly agreed to, and a hill that does not care about your feelings, and sweat stinging your eyes until the world is a blur of granite and green. We rigged skidways from split logs. We built rollers from alder. I got the engine out with Tamsin hauling on the tackle and swearing in two languages she claimed were French and "kitchen," and when the Atlas finally swung free and settled onto the cradle we'd built, I put my hand on it the way you'd touch a sleeping animal.

"You're going to carry me up a mountain," I told it. "Behave."

"Hollis talks to engines," Tamsin informed Ada.

"I know," Ada said. "So did my father. To maps, though." She paused. "Maps don't answer either."

By the second day we found the rhythm. That's the thing nobody tells you about brutal shared labor: it sands the edges off people faster than friendship ever could. Piet — before he vanished, that was — could carry double his size in flour and sing while doing it, songs so filthy Tamsin demanded translations and then blushed at them. Juniper carried more than anyone and complained less than no one, which worried me, because I've known that kind of quiet before and it usually means a person is spending something they can't replace. Ada turned out to have a head for rigging — angles, leverage, where a skid would bind — and by the second afternoon she was directing loads, and Juniper was letting her, and neither of them remarked on it, which was its own kind of remark.

Me, I carried the engine. Two miles a day, in relays, with a team of six on the ropes. My shoulders were raw meat by the third morning. But the Atlas went up clean, and when we set it down at the top of the Stair on the evening of day three, I sat down next to it and felt something I hadn't felt since I was a young man with more back than sense.

Pride. In other people. It's a rarer vintage than pride in yourself, and it goes down smoother.

* * *

Merrick caught us on the fourth day.

I want to be fair about it: he didn't catch us because we were slow. He caught us because water runs downhill and so do steamboats, and he'd had nothing to do for nine days but follow our wake and think. His steamer couldn't follow us past the shallows below the falls — too deep of draft, like a fat man at a ballet — but Merrick wasn't the kind of man who needed to follow. He was the kind who needed only to arrive.

He came up the trail from below with two of his men, clean-shaven, unhurried, wearing a coat that cost more than my pension, and he stood at the bottom of our camp slope with his hands in his pockets like a neighbor come to borrow sugar.

"Mrs. Whitlock," he called up. "Captain."

Juniper went down to meet him. I followed, because I'm old enough to know that when a man like Merrick smiles, someone should be standing behind you.

"I see you've been busy," he said, looking past her at our scattered camp, the skids, the half-loaded longboat. "Impressive. Expensive, though. Guides, pack animals, provisions." He smiled again. "You'll be needing help with the last stretch. Lucky for you, I'm a helpful man."

"We don't need your help," Juniper said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You need the guides' help. And guides are practical people." He tipped his hat. "Good luck at the top, Captain. I do hope the weather holds."

And he walked back down the trail, whistling.

We learned what he meant within the hour. The two local guides we'd hired at the landing — brothers, decent enough men, the only souls for fifty miles who knew where the pack trails ran — were gone. Their gear was gone. One of them had left a note pinned under a stone, and the note said, in effect, *sorry, better money*, and that was the whole of it. Merrick had bought them out from under us for what I'd guess was less than he'd spend on dinner, and with them went the mules we'd arranged for, and with the mules went any hope of getting the heavy loads — engine, boiler, iron — over the ridge in time.

We stood around the cold fire pit while the news sank in. Piet looked sick. Tamsin looked furious. Ada looked at Juniper, and Juniper looked at the ridge line where the trail vanished into timber, and I watched her do arithmetic with her jaw set, and I knew the sum was coming out bad.

"So we carry it ourselves," she said finally. "All of it. Longer relays. We lose two days, maybe three."

"The snows—" Piet started.

"I know about the snows," Juniper said, and that closed the subject, mostly because nobody wanted to look at her face while it was open.

* * *

Now, here's where the story gets good, and it isn't because of anything I did. It's because of Tamsin.

She'd gone off alone that evening, the way she did when she was chewing on a problem, walking down toward the river flats below the falls where there was a wisp of smoke showing through the trees. Smoke meant people. People meant either salvation or trouble, and at that point in our journey we'd stopped much caring which.

She came back after dark with firelight on her face and an expression I can only describe as a cat that has gotten into the cream *and* arranged for the dairyman to take the blame.

"There's a family," she said. "Trappers. Name of Okafor. Father, mother, three kids, and a grandmother who is — and I say this with love — terrifying. They've got mules, they've got a sled, and they've got a grudge against Merrick."

"A grudge," Juniper repeated carefully.

"Merrick's outfit trapped their lines out last winter. Took the furs, burned the cabins up-valley. Grandmother Okafor has been waiting for him to come back through her country ever since." Tamsin grinned. "They'll haul our freight over the ridge. For a price."

"What price?" I asked, because there's always a price, and I've been poor long enough to flinch at the word.

Tamsin's grin got wider. "Stories," she said. "And labor. They want to hear about the outside — real stories, from somebody who's been places. And they want hands at their traplines before we go, because they're shorthanded and the season won't wait." She spread her hands. "I offered us."

"You offered *us*," Juniper said.

"I offered the crew of the Kestrel," Tamsin said, "as the finest collection of liars, engineers, and general reprobates ever to assemble on this river, each of whom owes the others a story a night and a day's honest work, payable on delivery." She looked around at us. "Any objections?"

I had none. I'd been carrying an engine up a mountain; a night of storytelling sounded like a spa treatment.

* * *

The Okafors arrived the next morning with four mules, a flat sled, and a grandmother who took one look at our camp, pronounced it "the work of children," and began rearranging it. Her name was Beatrice. I feared her immediately and completely, and I could tell from the way Juniper straightened up that she felt the same, and that she respected it.

The bargain was struck around their fire that night, and I'll record it here because bargains are the true history of a country. We got the mules and the sled and old Okafor's knowledge of the high trail. They got a day of our labor on their trapline, running and resetting lines up the creek — hard, cold work, and I'll admit my hands were useless for anything delicate afterward — and they got stories.

Tamsin paid first. She told them about the theaters in the cities downriver, the gaslight and the painted scenery, and then she told them true things about being a runaway cook on a riverboat, and the grandmother listened with her chin on her fist like a judge. I paid with engines — how a flywheel works, why steam wants to kill you and how to make it love you instead, and the story of the time I fixed a paddlewheeler's shaft with a fence post and prayer, both of which failed and somehow the boat made port anyway. Even Piet told one, a strange sad little tale about a sister he'd left behind, and I watched him tell it and thought, *that boy is carrying something heavier than flour.*

Ada told the last story. She told them about her father — not the prospector, not the map, just the man — how he'd taught her to read terrain by making games of it, how he'd point at a hillside and ask *which way does the water go*, and how she'd hated him for it right up until the moment she realized he was teaching her the only inheritance that couldn't be stolen. The grandmother listened to that one with her eyes closed.

When it was done, Beatrice Okafor stood, cracked her knuckles, and said: "Tomorrow we move your iron. And when that man Merrick comes through my country again—" she smiled, and it was not a comforting smile— "he will wish he had bought *me* off instead."

* * *

We crossed the ridge in a day and a half. Mules on the big loads, the whole crew on ropes for the engine, and the sled runners greased with tallow Mrs. Okafor produced from somewhere that I decided not to ask about. The trail topped out at a saddle where the wind came through clean and cold, and beyond it lay the high lakes — flat, gray-green, ringed with snow-stained peaks, and utterly still.

Still water. After nine days of fighting current, it looked like a held breath.

We brought the Kestrel's pieces down to the shore and began the reassembly, and I worked through two full days with a happiness I didn't bother hiding, because there is no feeling in this world like putting an engine back together and hearing it turn over on the first pull. The Atlas caught, coughed, caught again, and settled into its idle like a man easing into a chair, and Tamsin whooped, and even Juniper smiled — really smiled, for maybe the third time since I'd known her.

That was the evening the messenger came.

He came down the lakeshore trail at dusk, a lean half-breed boy on a tired horse, and he carried a leather folio sealed with a stamp I recognized, because I'd seen it pressed into every threatening letter Juniper had ever burned unread. Vane's mark.

He looked around our camp — at the boat, at the crew, at the mountains going purple behind us — and he asked for Captain Whitlock by name.

"She's resting," I said.

"Then she'll want to see this when she wakes," he said, and handed me the folio, and touched his hat, and rode back the way he came.

I sat by the fire with that sealed paper on my knee for a long time, listening to the Atlas tick as it cooled, watching the light die on the high lakes. Final papers, I'd have bet my pension. Forfeiture. The end of the road for the Kestrel and everyone riding her.

Behind me, Juniper's tent flap stirred. She'd seen the rider come in. Of course she had.

"Hollis," she said quietly. "What is it?"

I weighed the folio in my hand. Light, for something that heavy.

"Trouble, Captain," I said. "Sealed trouble. It's got Vane's stamp on it."

She came and stood beside me at the fire, and for a moment neither of us reached for it, and the lake held its breath, and somewhere far off — so far I might have imagined it — I thought I heard thunder, though the sky was clear as glass.
