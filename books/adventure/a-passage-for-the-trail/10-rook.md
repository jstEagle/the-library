## 10. Rook

The trading post at Mirror Lake sat where the timber gave up and the rock took over, a scatter of cabins and a long low store with a porch sagging like a tired man on a fence rail. The lake itself was the color of old bottle glass, cold enough to kill a swimmer in a minute, and it held the mountains upside down so perfectly that looking at them felt like eavesdropping.

We'd made the Kestrel's last navigable miles by noon, nosed her into the shallows below the post, and paid a boy three bits to watch her. Hollis had grumbled the whole way in about the water level dropping, the propeller kissing gravel on every turn, and Tamsin had fed us all a breakfast of fried dough and beans that Hollis declared "an insult to both." Which, from Hollis, was practically a love letter.

"We need rope," I said, ticking items off on my fingers as we came up the bank. "Rope, salt pork if it isn't green, cartridges for the rifle we don't have yet, and somebody who knows whether the Widow's Stair portage is passable this early."

"Somebody who'll tell you the truth," Ada said. "Not somebody Vane's already bought."

"That too."

The store smelled of kerosene, cured hides, and the particular staleness of places where men wait out weather they can't control. Behind the counter stood a big woman with forearms like hawsers and a name — according to the sign over her head — of MRS. QUILL, GOODS & CREDIT, THE LATTER GRUDGINGLY. She looked at our party the way she probably looked at everything: as an inventory of what might be sold to us before we understood how badly we needed it.

"Rope," I said. "Two hundred feet, good hemp. And whatever you know about the Stair."

"I know you're not the first to ask after it this season." Mrs. Quill began unspooling rope without being asked, which I took as a good sign until I caught the look that came with it. "Fellow through here ten days back. Big man, quiet boots, pays cash and asks questions like he's taking inventory himself. Wanted to know who'd come upriver since ice-out."

I kept my hands busy examining a coil so they wouldn't do anything foolish. "And what did you tell him?"

"The truth. That nobody comes upriver since ice-out but fools and freight men, and he didn't strike me as either." She tied off the coil and set it on the counter. "Then there was another one. Older. Been here since the thaw, renting the back room, drinking coffee like it's a job. Pays his tab regular, which around here makes him practically nobility."

"Where is he now?"

Mrs. Quill nodded toward the corner of the store, where a shadow detached itself from behind a rack of traps and became a man.

He was old the way driftwood is old — bleached, hardened, shaped by forces that hadn't cared about him. Tall once, stooped now, with a face full of angles that had been handsome in some decade I couldn't guess at. His eyes were the trouble. They were pale, almost colorless, and they didn't track right. One fixed on me; the other wandered somewhere over my left shoulder, inspecting the horizon behind me for threats or perhaps just company.

"You're Whitlock," he said. His voice was gravel poured slow. "Captain of the Kestrel. Shallow draft, twin screws, patched boiler. You came up the gorge against the flood, which means you're either desperate or the best pilot on this river, and I've watched enough boats to know those aren't different things."

"And you are?"

"Silas Rook."

The name landed in me like a stone dropped down a well. I heard it hit bottom. I'd read it two nights before, inked small in the margin of Crane's map, in a hand steadier than the rest: *Rook will come asking. He has cause. Don't let him make you think it's yours.*

Ada had found that note. Ada had told me. And here he was, materializing out of a corner like the answer to a question I'd been careful not to ask aloud.

Hollis shifted beside me, and I felt Tamsin go still in the way she did when she was counting exits. Only Ada didn't move. She was watching Rook with an expression I couldn't read — not fear, not anger. Recognition, maybe. She had her father's face when she did that, all the softness gone out of it and something geological underneath.

"You knew Elias Crane," I said.

"I built half of everything he ever claimed." Rook came forward, and up close I could see his hands — scarred, knuckles swollen, a surveyor's hands gone to rust. "Thirty years ago we walked into these mountains together with nothing but a transit and a shared opinion that gold was where you found it. We found plenty of nothing together. Then we found the pass."

"The map says otherwise."

"The map says what Elias wanted it to say." He smiled, and it was a terrible thing, mostly teeth and regret. "May I buy you ladies a drink? Mrs. Quill keeps a whiskey that tastes like it was filtered through a boot, but it's honest about what it is."

* * *

He spread the story out across two whiskeys and most of an hour, and I'll give him this: he didn't lie to me. Or if he lied, he lied the way the mountains lie — by omission, by letting you fill in the flattering parts yourself.

They'd been partners, Crane and Rook, prospecting the high country year after year while other men struck it rich elsewhere and laughed at them. And then, in their ninth summer, they'd found it. Not gold. Something better, or worse, depending on your appetite: a route. A walkable passage across the range through ground no one else had surveyed, the only practical crossing for two hundred miles, open a few weeks each year when the glacier calved back from its moraines.

"Elias was the dreamer," Rook said. "I was the one who could draw a straight line. We surveyed it proper. Filed nothing, because filing costs money and we were rich in everything but that. We agreed to come back the next season with capital." His wandering eye settled, briefly, on the table. "That winter I got sick. Fever took the sight out of one eye and most of the strength out of the rest of me. By the time I could walk again, Elias had gone east. Came back two years later with money I never saw the source of, and a new partner's name on nothing, because there was still nothing on paper. Just his word that we were square."

"Were you?"

"He gave me four hundred dollars and a deed to a claim he'd already allowed to lapse." Rook said it flatly, without heat, which somehow made it worse. "I took it. That's the part that shames me. I took it, because I was half-blind and half-broke and telling myself that half of something beaten was better than all of nothing. Thirty years I've lived up here telling myself that."

Tamsin, who had been pretending to study a shelf of canned goods, said quietly, "And now?"

"Now I hear a boat came up the gorge flying no colors, carrying a girl with Crane's chin and a captain with Crane's map." He turned toward me, and for a moment both eyes aligned, and the look in them was like standing at the edge of a crevasse. "So I'm going to offer you a trade, Captain Whitlock, and I want you to understand I'm not proud of it. But pride is a luxury I sold years ago, along with everything else."

"Let's hear it."

"Help me reclaim my half. Whatever the pass guards — the route, the claim, the right-of-way, whatever Elias was really doing up there all those summers — I get my share. My name on the papers, my portion of whatever it yields. In exchange, I guide you over the Stair. I know that portage better than any man alive, including the ones Merrick's already bought, because I helped cut the first trail up it. And I keep my mouth shut about your route to every soul but you."

"And if I refuse?"

He reached inside his coat, slow, and I felt Hollis tense beside me — but what came out was only a folded paper, worn soft at the creases. He didn't open it. He just laid it on the table between us like a card played face-down.

"Then tomorrow morning I walk down to the steamer anchored below the narrows, and I sell what I know to a man named Merrick. Route, timing, the condition of your crew, the fact that your engineer drinks and your cook sleeps with a knife under her pillow and your captain hasn't slept a full night since Two Rivers." He tapped the paper once. "I've spent thirty years learning that the world doesn't reward the wronged, Captain. It rewards the useful. I intend to be useful to whoever pays best. I'd rather it was you."

The store seemed very quiet around us. Somewhere outside, loons were calling across the lake, that sound like laughter run backward.

"You'd sell us to the men who'll burn us out," I said. "After Crane cheated you, you'd help the men who'd cheat everyone."

"Merrick doesn't want to burn anyone. He wants the map and the girl delivered intact — those are his instructions, I'd wager, because Vane wants leverage, not corpses. Corpses bring marshals." Rook shrugged, a dry rustling sound. "I'm not threatening to kill you, Captain. I'm offering to save you from the version of events where you don't need saving. There's a difference, and it's worth more than you think."

Ada spoke for the first time. Her voice was steady, which frightened me more than shouting would have.

"My father wrote about you. In the margins. He said you'd come asking, and he said you had cause." She leaned forward. "He also wrote: *the pass keeps what it takes.* Do you know what that means?"

For a moment — just a moment — the old man's composure cracked, and I saw underneath it something raw and ancient, grief that had been sitting so long it had fossilized.

"I know exactly what it means," he said softly. "I helped bury what it took."

Nobody breathed. Even Mrs. Quill, wiping glasses behind her counter, had gone conspicuously still.

"Tomorrow," Rook said, rising, joints crackling like green wood in a fire. "You have until tomorrow morning, Captain. Think on it. Talk to your crew. Talk to the girl." He paused at the edge of the lamplight, and his bad eye drifted toward Ada as though pulled by a current. "She has his stubbornness. God help you all."

* * *

We didn't talk in the store. We walked down to the boat, all four of us, and stood on the bank in the blue hour while the lake went pewter and the first stars came out hard and cold above the peaks.

"He'll do it," Hollis said finally. "Sell us. Men like that always do. It's not malice, Juniper, it's arithmetic. He's got nothing left to lose and he knows exactly what his information's worth."

"I know."

"So we take his deal. Half of whatever we find is better than all of nothing, and nothing's what Merrick leaves us if he catches us on the Stair."

"That's what Rook said Crane told him, once." I heard my own voice come out flatter than I intended. "'Half of something beaten.' It's how men like Vane own everything, Hollis. Somebody always takes the half."

Tamsin crouched at the waterline, skipping stones with a violence that suggested feeling. "Or we tie him up and put him on the boat till we're past the Stair. He's half-blind. What's he going to do, navigate by smell?"

"He'd do it too," Ada said. "That's what none of you understand. He spent thirty years learning these mountains with failing eyes. He doesn't need to see the Stair. He needs to remember it, and he remembers everything." She hugged her arms around herself, and for a second she looked young, terribly young, a girl who'd learned her father was dead by letter and had been running on anger ever since. "He's not lying about knowing the way. That's what makes him dangerous."

I looked out at the Kestrel riding her reflections, lantern light trembling yellow on black water, and I thought about what she was. Not a boat. A debt with a deck. Papers held by a man who'd offered to forgive everything I owed for a folded map, and whose hired gun was anchored below the narrows at this very hour, patient as winter.

Half of something beaten.

"Here's what I keep coming back to," I said slowly. "Crane cheated him. That's true, and it doesn't stop being true because Crane died penitent with a map in his fist. Rook has cause. Real cause. And I'm sailing up this mountain spending a dead man's promise like it was coin, planning to take whatever the pass guards and hand it to his daughter, and nowhere in that ledger does Silas Rook appear except as an obstacle." I turned to face them. "Maybe the honest thing is to give him his share. Not because he threatened me. Because it's owed."

Hollis stared at me. "Juniper. You're negotiating with a gun to your head and calling it principle."

"I'm negotiating with a gun to my head *and* calling it principle. Both things are happening. Welcome to my entire life."

Ada laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly swallowed — and then she went serious again, studying me with those Crane eyes. "My father let a man starve on a lapsed deed and then wrote warnings about him in the margins instead of making it right. If we pay Rook, we're paying *my father's* debt, not just buying a guide." Her jaw set. "Do it. But not his terms. Ours. A third, not a half — the crew earns shares too, and I won't have my father's sins costing Tamsin and Hollis what's theirs. And he guides us over the Stair *before* he sees a signature."

"A third," I agreed. "And he tells us what the pass took. Before we're on the ice. All of it."

* * *

I found him at dawn on the store porch, sitting with a tin cup of coffee he wasn't drinking, watching the light come up on the far peaks with his one good eye. Up close, in that gray first light, he looked less like a threat and more like what he was: an old man at the end of his money and his patience, holding the last thing he owned — a grievance — and trying to decide whether to spend it or be rid of it.

"Third share," I said, sitting down beside him without waiting for an invitation. "Split between you and the crew's fund. You guide us over the Widow's Stair starting today. And before we touch ice, you tell us what happened up there — what the pass took, and why Crane wrote warnings about it thirty years later. Full accounting, Rook. No omissions. That's the price, and it's cheaper than what Merrick would've given you and costlier than you deserve."

He was quiet a long time. The loons started up again out on the lake.

"Crane always said you river people bargained like drowning men," he said at last, "because you're used to the water deciding everything." He extended a hand, scarred and heavy, and I shook it. His grip was stronger than his body promised. "Third share. Full accounting. Done."

"One more thing." I stood, brushing off my coat. "Merrick's steamer is below the narrows. When we move for the Stair, he moves. So whatever you know about slowing him down —"

Something moved in the ruined geography of his face that might have been a smile.

"Oh, Captain," Silas Rook said, reaching for his stick and hauling himself upright, "you have no idea what an old man who's been cheated properly can do to a timetable."

Behind us, down at the narrows, a steam whistle blew — long, deliberate, and unmistakably aimed at us. Merrick knew we were here. He'd known since yesterday.

And now, so did the mountain.
