## 11. Debt Collectors

I learned to read people the way my father taught me to read contour lines — the close ones are steep, the far ones gentle, and the place where they all bunch together is where you'd better plant your feet. So when the messenger came up the trail to our high-lakes camp, I read him before he'd finished his first hundred yards: too clean for a trapper, too nervous for a surveyor, boots too good for the mud he was ruining.

"He's not here for the fish," I told Juniper.

"Nobody's ever here for the fish," she said, and kept gutting trout like a woman with nothing on her mind but supper. That was the performance, anyway. I'd been watching her long enough now to know the difference between Juniper Whitlock at ease and Juniper Whitlock arranging herself to look at ease. The second version held her knife a little too confidently.

The messenger was a lean man with a lawyer's smile and a mule driver's hands, which struck me as a man who'd made a bargain with himself somewhere along the way and lost. He stopped a respectful distance from the fire and produced a leather document case the way a priest produces absolution.

"Mistress Whitlock. Mistress Crane. I'm sent on behalf of Mr. Corliss Vane, with his compliments."

"His compliments," Juniper repeated. "He used to send those with the interest notices too."

"He did, ma'am, yes." The smile didn't flicker, which took a kind of professional discipline. "I'm instructed to deliver these into your hands personally, and to witness your receipt of them." He drew out two documents. The top one was heavy paper, sealed, the kind that costs more to make than most men earn in a week. The bottom one was thinner. Simpler.

Juniper wiped her hands on her trousers and took them.

I watched her read. I'd gotten good at that too — watching Juniper. Not because I liked her, in those early weeks. Not because I trusted her. Because my father had died trusting her, or at least died believing she was the best available instrument for his last wish, and I meant to know exactly what kind of instrument I was traveling with. A person's face when they read bad news tells you what they value. Juniper's face, reading Vane's seal, went through fury, then calculation, then something I couldn't name — a stillness, like water going glassy before it freezes.

The second paper she read twice. Then she folded both, very carefully, and put them in her coat, and said, "Tell Mr. Vane I received his compliments."

"He also instructed me to say — and I quote, ma'am, as I was paid to remember it — *'The offer has a season, like everything in these mountains. Seasons close.'*"

"I know how seasons work," Juniper said. "I've made my living on them since I was twelve."

The messenger nodded, turned his ruined boots around, and went back down the trail. Hollis, who had been watching from the woodpile with his arms crossed like a man guarding his own patience, waited until the man was out of earshot before he said, "Well?"

"Well what?"

"What did the devil send you, Juniper?"

She looked at the fire. She looked at the lake, which was doing its evening trick of turning to hammered copper. She looked at me, briefly, and whatever she found in my face made her look away again.

"Paperwork," she said. "Nothing that can't keep."

* * *

It kept for exactly one day.

I know because I counted, and because I spent that day doing what I'd been doing every evening since we'd found Rook at the trading post: going through my father's map by firelight, hunting for anything he'd hidden in it. That was our arrangement, hers and mine, though neither of us had ever said it aloud — I studied the map, she studied the mountains, and we pretended the arrangement was about the route and not about the fact that we were both waiting for the other one to break.

The map had given up its secrets slowly. First the annotations about Rook — *cheated him on the third survey, owe him more than I can pay, pray he never learns how much* — which had already arrived in the flesh at that lakeshore post, half-blind and hungry for interest on a twenty-year-old debt. Then the warning in the margin, small enough that I'd needed firelight and squinting to find it: *the pass keeps what it takes.* I'd asked Juniper what she thought it meant. She'd said, "My father used to say the river keeps what it takes. I expect mountains say the same thing in a different accent." It was the first time she'd ever mentioned her father to me, and she'd done it the way you hand someone something breakable — quickly, and without looking at them.

On the second evening after the messenger, Juniper didn't come to supper.

Tamsin covered for her the way Tamsin covered for everyone, with a running commentary about the trout's poor character and a story about a cook she'd worked under in Ash Hollow who salted the coffee on purpose to keep the crew mean. Hollis ate three helpings and said nothing, which from Hollis was a speech. But I'd seen which way Juniper had walked when she left the fire — down toward the lake, alone, with her coat buttoned to the throat, and I knew what was in that coat's inside pocket, because I'd seen the shape of it when she took the papers out that morning to look at them again.

I gave her an hour. I'm not proud of the hour. I spent it telling myself it was her business, her boat, her debt, her signature — a whole ledger of *hers* that I'd been keeping since Ash Hollow, mostly to keep from admitting that somewhere between the river and the portage at Widow's Stair, my business had gotten tangled up in hers past any hope of untangling.

Then I took my father's letter out of my shirt and went down to the lake.

* * *

She was sitting on a boulder with the papers spread on her knee, and she had a pencil — a pencil, not a pen, which told me she was still telling herself it wasn't real — hovering over the thin one. The lake was dead calm. The first stars were out over the ridge, and the glacier up-valley was making its low sounds, the ones we'd all learned to ignore and none of us could ignore anymore, like a giant turning over in his sleep and muttering about waking up.

I sat down on the next boulder without asking. She didn't tell me to leave. She didn't look up.

"Read it to me," she said finally. "The offer. I want to hear how it sounds out loud."

I took the thin paper. It was short — Vane's lawyers knew their man didn't pay for words he didn't need. I read it in the fireless dark by starlight, which is a skill my father drilled into me until I could read a bill of sale in a snow cave.

"*Final notice of forfeiture,*" I read. "*In consideration of the surrender of all documents, maps, and instruments of survey relating to the high pass, held or claimed by the estate of Elias Crane, Mr. Vane hereby releases Juniper Whitlock from all debts, liens, and encumbrances against the steamboat Kestrel, and conveys said vessel free and clear, with papers, to the said Whitlock, her heirs and assigns, forever.*"

"Forever," Juniper said. "They put *forever* in it. That's a lawyer being cruel on purpose."

"It's a good offer."

"It's a *generous* offer. That's worse." She finally looked at me. In the starlight her face was all planes and shadow, and for once there was no performance on it at all. "Do you know what I am without that boat, Ada? Do you know what I've been since I was fifteen years old? I was a deckhand on her when she was my father's. I earned my pilot's papers on her. Every debt I ever paid, I paid out of her hold. Corliss Vane didn't lend me money. He bought the right to tell me who I am, and I've spent six years telling myself the boat was still mine because my hands were on the wheel." She looked down at the paper on her knee. "He's offering to give me back my own name. All it costs is a dead man's map."

"And a dead man's daughter," I said. "Don't leave me out of the arithmetic. If you hand over the map, Vane owns the pass. And everyone who ever tries to cross it. You've seen what he does with a river. Now imagine him with a road."

"I've done the sums," she said, and her voice went flat and hard, the pilot's voice, the one she used on the river when the channel got ugly. "The map is paper. The boat is wood and iron and my whole life. A map in Vane's hands is a bad thing that happens to strangers. A boat in Vane's hands is a bad thing that happens to *me.* I've been generous with strangers my whole career. It's why I'm broke."

I could have argued. I want to be honest about this — I wanted to argue, and I had the arguments loaded, and I was angry, angrier than I'd let myself be since I'd read the letter telling me my father was dead. But sitting there in the dark with her, watching her hold a pencil over her own freedom like it was a snake she couldn't decide whether to kill, I understood something that anger would have ruined. She wasn't betraying my father. She was drowning, and someone had thrown her a rope, and the rope was tied to a monster. You don't argue with a drowning woman about the monster. You remind her she can swim.

So I took out the letter.

* * *

"I got this six weeks after he died," I said. "The letter took that long to find me because he'd addressed it to a place I hadn't lived in three years. He knew that. He wrote the address anyway. I think he was leaving me time to be angry at him before I read what he had to say."

Juniper's pencil had stopped moving.

"He wrote it in a mining camp up there," I said, and nodded at the glacier. "You can see the light of it sometimes at dusk, if the smoke's right. He was already coughing blood when he wrote it. He knew exactly how much time he was spending. And he spent a page and a half on survey notes — he couldn't help himself, the man thought in contour lines — and then he spent one paragraph on me. One. I've had it memorized since the second reading, but I'm going to read it out loud anyway, because I think you need to hear it in a voice that isn't your own."

I unfolded the paper. My hands were steady. I'd have sworn they'd shake, but they didn't.

"*Ada — if you're reading this, then a riverboat captain has kept a promise to a stranger, which is rarer than gold and worth more, and I hope you thanked her, because I raised you better than to be rude to the person who hauled your ungrateful hide over a mountain.*"

Juniper made a sound that might have been a laugh if it hadn't gotten stuck halfway.

"*You'll have heard by now that there's treasure in the pass, because there's always talk of treasure, and because I may have let the talk run ahead of me when I needed men to carry my instruments. I'm going to tell you the truth, which is what I should have led with my whole life. There's no gold up there, girl. There's something better and worse: a road. The only road through the range that the ice doesn't own for eleven months of the year, and the legal claim to it, surveyed and recorded and sitting in a strongbox with my name on the corner-stone record. Men like Vane will want it. Men like Rook will want it. They'll want it because a road through those mountains is a tollbooth on the whole world's commerce, and men like that don't see a road, they see a throat they can put a hand around.*

"*I wanted it for you so you'd never have to be poor the way your mother was poor, and never have to be owned the way I let myself be owned. But Ada — listen to me, wherever you are, whatever fire you're sitting at — the claim is only worth having if the person holding it would rather keep a promise than keep a fortune. That was always the test. That was always the whole survey. I didn't need a daughter who was rich. I needed a daughter who was free, and freedom is just a promise you've kept to yourself so long it's become a habit.*

"*If the captain who brought you through is reading this over your shoulder — and she will be, because that woman reads everything, I watched her read a sandbar for a full minute like it was a letter from a lover — then I'll say it plain, since I'm past the point where pride costs me anything. I don't care about the map. I don't care about the claim. I spent my whole life trading things I loved for things I wanted, and I'm dying alone in a cabin that smells like me, and I would give every foot of that road to have one hour of my daughter's company on it. Get her through the pass. That's the treasure. The rest is gravel.*

"*Your father, who was better at mountains than at men, and worst of all at the one man he had to be every day. E.C.*"

I folded the letter. The lake was still. Up-valley, the glacier groaned, long and low, and this time nobody could mistake it for anything but what it was — something vast beginning to move.

Juniper was looking at the water. There was a long time when she didn't say anything, and I let the silence do its work, because my father taught me that too: on a survey, you don't fill in the gaps with guesses. You wait for the reading.

"The boat," she said at last, and her voice had gravel in it. "You understand what I'd be giving up. It's not property. It's—" She stopped. Started again. "When my father died, the Kestrel was the only thing left that he'd touched. I have steered her through floodwater with my hands shaking so hard I had to lock my wrists, because letting go wasn't possible. She's not a boat. She's the last conversation I ever had with him."

"I know," I said. "My father wrote you a whole letter about what it is to trade what you love for what you want. He was an expert. He did it professionally for forty years."

That landed. I saw it land. She looked down at the thin paper on her knee, and then, slowly, she picked up the pencil — and I confess my heart went into my throat, because for one horrible moment I thought she was going to sign it anyway, and I was already composing the speech about how I'd cross the pass alone if I had to —

— and she wrote one word across the face of the offer, in letters large enough to read from across a campfire, and handed the whole thing back to me.

*No.*

"Vane's messenger," she said. "Will he come back for an answer?"

"He'll come back."

"Good." She stood, and rolled her shoulders, and when she turned to face me she was Juniper Whitlock again, all of her, the pilot and the debtor and the stubborn mule of a woman my father had bet his daughter's life on. "Then he can carry that back down the mountain. And Ada —"

"Yes."

"When this is done, when we're over the ice and the claim is filed — you don't owe me anything. Whatever your father's letter says about thanking me. You don't. I took this job for the cargo."

"I know," I said. "He knew too. That's why he chose you."

She looked at me a moment longer, and something passed between us that we didn't name then and have never named since, and then she said, "Get some sleep. We move at first light."

"Why first light? The route-finding isn't done, and Hollis says the moraine crossing needs another day of—"

"Because of that." She pointed up-valley, and I followed her finger, and I saw it: a flicker at the glacier's face, white against the dark, like a spark struck off flint. Then another. Then a sound reached us across the miles, a deep cracking boom, and a moment later the lake shivered against the shore in small, quick waves that hadn't come from any wind.

The calving had begun.

"Ten days," Juniper said quietly, watching the ice flash and boom in the dark. "Maybe fewer. God help us, the pass is opening."
