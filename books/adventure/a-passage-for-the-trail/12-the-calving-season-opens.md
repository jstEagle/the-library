## 12. The Calving Season Opens

The mountain spoke first, before any of us were awake enough to be afraid of it.

I'd been sleeping in the Kestrel's wheelhouse because the cabin had gotten crowded with gear and because the wheelhouse felt like the last honest place on the boat. Somewhere past the hour when honest people are asleep, a sound rolled down the valley — long, low, and patient, the way thunder is patient when it has all the time in the world. It didn't crack and fade the way a storm does. It ground on, like a door too heavy for its hinges being opened by something that didn't care whether you wanted it open.

I sat up with my hand already on the wheel, which tells you something about where I live in my own head.

Out through the salt-fogged glass, the peaks above the lake stood black against a sky gone the color of pewter, and along their shoulders I could see it — a flicker, a stutter, a white flash where no lightning should live. The glacier, calving. Shrugging off tons of itself into the high valley, ice the age of empires letting go all at once because the summer had finally come to collect.

The route was opening.

I sat there a while with my heart going like a paddle wheel in shallow water, and I thought: ten days, maybe. Elias Crane's map had said as much in that cramped, feverish hand — *the ice bridge stands from first calving to first snows, and no longer, and God help whoever trusts it longer.* Ten days to cross a range that had spent ten thousand years learning how to kill people who were in a hurry.

Then I went and woke the others, because misery loves company and so does terror.

* * *

By full light we were all standing on the bank looking up at the mountain like a congregation that had wandered into the wrong church.

"There," Ada said, and her arm came up steady, pointing to the notch between the two great shoulders where the ice showed blue-white even at this distance. "That's the passage. He drew it exactly." She had the map out, weighted flat with four stones against the wind off the lake, and she was looking at it the way I'd seen Hollis look at a cracked cylinder head — not with hope, exactly, but with the grim attention of a person who intends to argue with a machine and win.

"It's moving," Tamsin said. She wasn't wrong. Even from here, even to eyes that had never been above the timberline, you could see the glacier was *busy* — shedding, shifting, grinding its slow teeth. "That's supposed to be reassuring, is it? The road is visibly falling apart?"

"The road is *open*," Hollis said. He'd come up from the engine room with grease to his elbows and that expression he wore when he'd already done the arithmetic and hated the answer. "Which is more than it'll be in ten days. Or eleven. Or whenever you all finish admiring the view."

Nobody said the other thing, the thing we were all not saying, which was: and if we don't cross in time, we winter here. On this shore. With Merrick somewhere behind us and Vane's forfeiture papers folded in my coat like a knife I kept forgetting I was carrying.

I looked at the Kestrel.

She sat at the lakeshore cache where we'd tied her two days back, riding low and patient, her rails still bearing the scorch from the fire at Two Rivers that we'd never had time to sand out properly. She looked, I thought, like a dog tied outside a store, watching the door. She'd carried us four hundred miles against the current. She'd taken us where no steamer of Vane's could follow, into water so shallow the herons walked beside us. And now I was going to walk away from her and leave her to the weather and the rats and whatever Merrick's men did to boats they found unattended.

"Juniper." Ada's voice, quiet, at my shoulder. She had a way of arriving without arriving — you'd turn and she'd simply be there, the way her father must have been. "We should start before noon. The first miles are the gentle ones, and Hollis says the loads need dividing."

"I heard what Hollis says. Hollis says a great deal."

"Hollis is right a great deal," she said, and there it was — the ghost of a smile, the first one in three days. Since the forfeiture papers. Since she'd stood in the cabin doorway and read me her father's letter in a voice that never once shook, and I had sat there feeling like a coin someone had bitten and found false.

"I'm going," I said. "I didn't say I wasn't going."

"You were saying goodbye to the boat."

"I was *inspecting* the boat."

"Of course you were," said Ada Whitlock, who at nineteen had already learned to let a captain keep her dignity the way you let a drunk keep his hat on. "Inspect her thoroughly. Then come divide the loads."

* * *

We spent the morning turning the Kestrel into a mummy.

Hollis had opinions about how it was done, and for once everyone let him have them, because if anyone aboard understood what a boat needed to survive neglect, it was the man who'd kept her heart beating through flood and fire and my steering. We warped her into the shallows at the lake's eastern elbow, where a shelf of rock ran out like a pier and the water went glass-still, and we winched her bow up onto it until she was half beached, high enough that no spring swell could work her loose.

Then we stripped her.

That's the part I hadn't prepared for. You think abandoning a boat means walking away, and it doesn't — it means taking her apart with your own hands while she watches. We lifted the hatch covers and stowed them below. We unbent the awning frames, coiled the lines, packed the lamps, the tools, the spare tiller. Hollis went into the engine room with a can of grease and anointed every bright surface like a priest at a deathbed, and came out twenty minutes later with his jaw set wrong, and none of us said a word to him about it, because there is a kind of grief you don't interrupt.

The powder went last.

The salvaged charge from her magazine — the demolition stores I'd hauled aboard back at the trading post as ballast, telling myself it was prudence and knowing even then it was something else — came up wrapped in oilcloth, heavy as sin. Piet's replacement, a silent boy named Fenn who'd joined us at the post and hadn't yet learned to talk in front of me, took one end and I took the other, and we carried it up above the high-water line to a cleft in the rocks that Hollis had picked out.

"Why are we hauling explosives up a mountain?" Tamsin asked. She was balancing a crate of hardtack on her hip like a baby, which was a habit of hers that I'd stopped finding alarming somewhere around the third gorge.

"Ballast," I said.

"Juniper."

"Insurance," I said.

She looked at me, and then at the cleft in the rock, and then at me again, and whatever she saw there made her decide she'd rather carry the hardtack. Smart girl. There are questions you don't ask a captain when the answer might be a word like *scuttle*, and she'd learned to hear the shape of a question without asking it.

We covered the Kestrel with the spare canvas, lashed down over her rails and weighted with stones, until she looked less like a boat than like something the mountain had swallowed and not finished chewing. Hollis wrote the cache location in his logbook in his careful machinist's hand, and then, after a moment, added a line I pretended not to see: *Kestrel, river steamer, 62 feet. Laid up pending return of crew. All hands expect to come back for her.*

All hands. As if we were a crew of a ship that still existed.

* * *

We started climbing at one o'clock, and by two o'clock I had learned that everything I knew was useless.

That's the thing they don't tell you about leaving the water. On the river I was somebody. Twenty years of channels and sandbars and reading the brown water like a ledger — it all added up to a person, a person with a name that boatmen used with respect or at least caution. Juniper Whitlock, who could take the Kestrel through the Narrows at flood stage with her hat still on. Put me on a slope of scree with eighty pounds on my back and I was just a woman with bad boots, breathing like a bellows and lying to herself about how far the next rest was.

The trail — and I use the word generously; it was a suggestion the mountain had made to a few goats and a few prospectors, and the goats hadn't signed anything — climbed away from the lake through pine that got stunted and then got apologetic and then gave up entirely. Below us the water shrank. That was the worst of it. Every hundred yards of height we gained, the Kestrel got smaller, the white smudge of her canvas cover narrowing to a fleck, a scratch, a pale eyelash on the shore of a lake that was itself turning into a coin of blue at the bottom of the world.

I kept looking back. I told myself I was checking our backtrail, which was even true some of the time.

"Captain." Hollis, behind me, not unkindly. "You'll wear a groove in your neck."

"I'm navigating."

"By looking at the boat."

"Boats are excellent landmarks."

He grunted, which from Hollis was a paragraph. He'd known me eight years. He'd signed on when the Kestrel was new and so was my captaincy, and he'd watched me learn her and she'd watched us both, and there was no use pretending to a man like that that a person and a vessel can be separated without a sound like tearing.

Tamsin, ahead of us, had gone quiet in the way she did when the country got big. She'd run from a kitchen in Ash Hollow with nothing but a satchel and a talent for making nothing taste like something, and she'd never seen country that didn't have a fence on it somewhere. She kept stopping and turning all the way around, slow, like a compass needle finding itself.

Ada didn't look back at all.

I noticed that somewhere in the third hour. She climbed the way her father must have climbed — head up, eyes on the route, reading the ground ahead the way I read water, picking the line through the talus without seeming to decide anything, her feet just knowing. Elias Crane's blood, doing its work in her legs and her eyes while she herself still wasn't sure she'd forgiven the man for dying. Once she stopped at a cairn — an old one, tumbled, half-swallowed by lichen — and set one stone back on top with a small precise gesture, and then walked on without a word, and I understood that she was having a conversation with a ghost the whole way up and had decided the ghost could keep up on his own.

We made camp at dusk in a cirque below the ice, in the ruins of a prospector's cabin that the map had promised and the mountain had mostly kept. The walls were standing. The roof was a rumor. There was a rusted stove that Hollis pronounced *serviceable* in the tone of a man pronouncing a mule *willing*, and Tamsin got a fire going in it, and we ate beans and hardtack and a strip of dried elk that Tamsin had coaxed into edibility through what I can only assume was witchcraft.

Nobody talked much. Above us, through the broken roof, the glacier caught the last of the light and burned rose-gold, and every few minutes it spoke — that same grinding, patient thunder, nearer now, close enough to feel in the soles of your boots. Ice letting go. The road opening. The clock running.

"Ten days," Piet's replacement said suddenly — Fenn, who hadn't spoken in four days, staring up through the roof gap. Then he went red and studied his beans.

"Ten days," I agreed. "Give or take the mountain's mood."

"And if the weather takes a day from us?" Tamsin asked. "Or two? The map says storms—"

"The map says a lot of things." I looked at Ada. She had the map out in the firelight, and she was looking at the margin, at her father's cramped annotation that we'd all read and none of us had discussed: *the pass keeps what it takes.* "The map also says my father's partner is somewhere behind us with Merrick's men, so I'd rather the mountain's mood than Rook's."

"Rook's half-blind," Hollis said.

"Rook's half-blind and angry and he knows this country better than God does," Ada said quietly. "My father wrote that too. He wrote that Silas Rook could find his way through a whiteout by the smell of the stone." She folded the map with the care you'd fold a flag. "Blind doesn't mean lost. Not for a man like that."

Nobody had anything to add to that, so we drew straws for watches and rolled into our blankets, and the fire settled, and the mountain went on talking to itself above us in its old grinding voice.

I took the middle watch, which I'd rigged the straws to give me, because there was something I wanted to do that I didn't want an audience for.

* * *

At midnight I climbed out of the cabin and stood on the slope above it, and I looked back down the way we'd come.

The lake was a sheet of hammered pewter under a half moon. And there on the eastern shore, small as a toy, small as something a child might have left at the edge of a bath — the Kestrel. White canvas over her rails. Still. Patient. Tied to a country that wasn't hers, waiting for a captain who'd walked off into the mountains and left her mummified on a rock shelf like a pharaoh nobody was coming back for.

I have been afraid before. I've been afraid in floodwater and in gunfire and once, memorably, in a courtroom in Vane's home port where the judge and the plaintiff had gone to the same church. But this was a different animal. This was the fear of looking down at your own hands and not recognizing them. Because the truth — the truth I'd been circling for two days like a moth around a lamp — was that I didn't know who I was without that boat. The Kestrel wasn't my property. She was my argument. Twenty years of proof, moored and measurable, that Juniper Whitlock was worth the water she floated on. Take her away and what was left? A woman with bad boots and a dead man's map and a promise she'd made for the worst possible reasons and was keeping for better ones she still didn't fully understand.

Vane understood that. That was the whole architecture of the man. He never held your debt; he held your *name*, and the papers were just how he kept it in a drawer.

And I had walked away from her anyway. Left her roped and covered on a shelf of stone because a feverish stranger had pressed a map into my hands and asked me for the one thing that couldn't be bought back.

"You're doing it again," said a voice behind me.

Ada. Wrapped in a blanket, standing a careful few feet off, looking not at me but down at the lake. She'd come out on her watch early, or she hadn't been able to sleep, or — more likely — she'd seen me climb out of the cabin and had understood exactly what I was doing, because she was the one person aboard fluent in the language of watching something you love get smaller.

"Inspecting the boat," I said.

"From four miles away and straight up a mountain. Thorough."

"You'll go far in the inspection business."

She was quiet a moment. The glacier boomed, far off, and a seam of white light stuttered along its face and was gone.

"My father used to say," she said at last, "that a man carries his boat with him. Even ashore. Even after. He said that's why he never bought one — he said he'd never survive the carrying." She hugged the blanket tighter. "I thought it was just something he said. He said a lot of things."

"And now?"

"Now I think he was telling me how to get through this. How to get through *him*." She looked at me then, firelit from below by nothing, moonlit from above, and her face was very young and very old at once. "You haven't left her, Captain. You're just carrying her where she can't go yet. There's a difference. It took me three years to learn there's a difference."

The wind came down off the ice, cold enough to make the point of the whole trip, and somewhere below us, at the edge of the timber, something moved — a shadow detaching from shadows, low and quick, gone before I could fix it. A deer, I told myself. A fox. The mountain was full of things that moved at night and meant nothing by it.

But I stood there a moment longer, hand going to the compass in my coat pocket — Crane's compass, his last gift to me along with the map and the promise — and I watched the dark timberline and did not like the arithmetic of what I'd seen.

"Get some sleep," I told Ada. "Tomorrow we reach the ice."

"And after the ice?"

"After the ice," I said, watching the treeline, "we find out what my father-in-law the mountain has been keeping all these years. And I'm starting to suspect we're not the only ones coming to collect."

Below us, small and white and patient, the Kestrel waited on her shelf of stone. And far below *her*, down the valley we'd climbed out of, something else was waiting too — patient as the glacier, patient as the debt — and it was climbing.
