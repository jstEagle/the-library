## 13. Icefield

My father once told me that a glacier is just a river that learned patience. He said it the way he said most things — half lecture, half apology for being gone so long — and I'd nodded along without understanding, because I was eleven and rivers were things you fell into at church picnics, not things you studied.

Standing now at the toe of the Crane Icefield with my boots in two inches of slush and my breath freezing in the wool over my mouth, I finally understood him. The glacier came down out of the col like something poured and then forgotten, gray-green in its hollows, white on its shoulders, cracked everywhere with blue seams deep enough to swallow a cathedral. It was patient. It had all the time in the world.

We did not.

"Ten days," Juniper said beside me, squinting up the ice under her hand like a sailor reading weather. "Maybe eight if the warm holds. After that the bridges rot and we're climbing walls of nothing." She glanced at me. "Your father's map. How far to his camp?"

I unrolled it against my chest, weighting the corners with mittened hands. Elias Crane's fine drafting ink, the lines still sure even after everything. "Two days' travel if the route's as drawn. Three if it isn't."

"It never is," Hollis muttered behind us. He had the rope coil over one shoulder and the expression of a man attending his own funeral. "In forty years of machinery I have never once met a plan that survived contact with actual conditions."

"That's why we rope up," Juniper said.

That was how the morning went: Juniper deciding, Hollis doubting, Tamsin humming while she checked knots, Piet — no. Not Piet. Piet was three days and a burned shed behind us, and none of us said his name anymore except Ada Whitlock, alone, at night, wondering what a man must be threatened with to do what he'd done. I was learning there were a great many things I thought about alone at night. The icefield promised to add several more.

Juniper put me second on the rope. Behind her, ahead of Hollis, with Tamsin fourth and the heavy loads distributed among us like sins. She didn't explain why she'd placed me there. She just looked at me a moment longer than comfortable and said, "You've got your father's eye. Use it."

I wanted to tell her I had no such thing. My father's eye had looked at mountains the way other men looked at their children — constantly, hungrily, from a great distance. Mine had looked at ledgers and doorways and exits. I was the girl who counted the steps down from a porch. Heights and I had an arrangement: I avoided them, and they refrained from killing me.

The icefield had not signed that agreement.

* * *

The first hour was merely terrible, which lulls you. The lower glacier was a broad ramp of old snow crusted hard enough to hold a boot, and we made good time, four figures on one rope, breath smoking, the world reduced to white below and stone above and the small squeak-crunch-squeak of our own progress. I began to think: perhaps this is all it is. Perhaps the stories are exaggerated. Perhaps fear is just a rumor spread by people who never tried.

Then the snow bridge gave a sound like a giant clearing its throat, and I stopped thinking anything at all.

It held. It was six feet wide and it held, but beneath it, where the crust had sagged, I could see down — down through a lens of ice into blue dark, the kind of blue that doesn't exist anywhere above ground, the color of depth itself. A crevasse. And we were going to walk across its eyelid.

"Bridges," Juniper said, when she saw my face. "Snow fills them every winter. Some hold till August. Some hold till noon." She stepped onto the bridge without breaking stride, testing each footfall with a little stamp before committing her weight, and I understood that this was the whole art: you never fully arrived anywhere. You only ever almost did.

I copied her. Stamp, listen, step. Stamp, listen, step. By the third bridge I had stopped hearing my own heartbeat and started hearing the ice instead — or imagining I did, which amounts to the same skill. There was a hollowness under some patches, a note in the crunch like tapping a wall to find a stud. My father's map had lines penciled across the glacier, a wandering dotted thread that looked, from the valley floor, like madness. From the middle of it, I saw the logic. He wasn't drawing a straight line. He was drawing a listening line. Every bend in his route bent around something invisible.

"He heard them," I said aloud, without meaning to.

Juniper looked back. "Heard what?"

"The crevasses. Under the snow. His route bends where there's nothing to see." I crouched, put my palm flat on the crust. "But there's something to hear."

She crouched too, and for a moment the captain of the Kestrel and the daughter of Elias Crane both knelt on a snow bridge over a bottomless blue throat, listening like a pair of church mice. Then she grinned — that crooked, unsentimental grin that meant she'd revised an opinion about someone and would die before admitting it.

"Well then," she said. "Lead on, Miss Crane."

I want it recorded that my stomach dropped through the glacier when she said *lead*. I want it recorded that I nearly refused. But the rope was in my hands, and the map was in my head, and somewhere underneath the terror was a small, appalled pride, like a coal refusing to go out. So I went. Stamp, listen, step. The icefield opened ahead of us, and for the first time in my life I walked toward the high places instead of away from them, and the strange truth was that the fear didn't leave. It just changed jobs. It stopped being a wall and became a lantern.

* * *

The whiteout came at midday, out of a sky that had been innocently clear all morning.

One moment there were peaks; the next there was milk. No horizon, no shadow, no up or down except what your boots insisted on. Snow began falling sideways, fine as flour, and the temperature fell with it, and suddenly the whole world was the size of our rope.

"Close it up!" Juniper's voice came flattened, directionless. "Ten feet apart! Nobody wanders!"

Wandering is precisely what happens in a whiteout. I learned this academically, watching Tamsin drift leftward off the listening line within minutes, reeled back by Hollis swearing a blue streak that froze midair. I kept us honest by counting paces between the map's features — a rock island here, a pressure ridge there — shouting the counts to Juniper, who shouted corrections back, our voices arriving mangled and late like letters from another country.

It was during the worst of it, when I couldn't see my own boots, that the ground spoke.

A hollow note. Right where I was about to put my weight. I froze, arm up, and the rope went taut behind me, four people stopping because I stopped, and I stood there in the blind white listening until I found the edge of the hollow — a yard to the right — and traced our line around it, foot by foot, calling the count.

Later, in the tent, Hollis said, "You know what you did today?" in the tone he used for engine diagnostics. "You navigated a glacier by ear in a whiteout. Your father would've—"

"Don't," I said.

He shrugged and went back to the stove. But Juniper caught my eye across the lamplight, and neither of us said anything either, which is its own kind of agreement.

* * *

Tamsin fell on the second day.

There is no gentle way to write it, so I won't try. We were crossing a long bridge over a crevasse field the map called — in my father's tidy hand — *the Loom*, because the cracks ran parallel like warp threads. Single file. Rope tight. I was leading, reading the ice, doing everything right, and Tamsin was behind me carrying the tent pole bundle across her shoulders like a yoke, humming her kitchen songs, and the bridge simply decided, in the indifferent way of glaciers, that it had been patient long enough.

The crack was a whipcrack. The snow opened under her like a mouth. She didn't even scream — that was the worst part, the silence — just a look of pure astonishment as the world swallowed her, and then the rope snapped taut and jerked me to my knees and dragged me a body-length toward the hole before Hollis threw himself flat and Juniper drove her axe in and the whole system locked, four hearts hammering on one line, Tamsin's weight swinging somewhere below us in the blue.

"Tamsin!" Juniper bellowed into the hole. "Talk to me!"

A pause. Then, faintly, wobbling, from the dark: "I'm — hang on — I'm hanging on—" And then, absurdly, a scrap of the kitchen song, sung through chattering teeth.

Hollis laughed the way men laugh when the alternative is weeping. "She's fine. Nobody who sings is dying."

Getting her out took four hours and cost us the day, and very nearly cost more. The crevasse walls were glass-smooth; she couldn't climb; the rope ran over a burning edge of ice that frayed visibly with every minute. Juniper organized us like a drill — anchor, backup anchor, hauling line, prusiks that Hollis rigged with fingers gone clumsy from cold — and I lay on my belly at the lip with my face over the void, talking to Tamsin, keeping her voice alive in the dark, telling her about Ash Hollow, about my father's terrible handwriting, about anything, because I understood without being told that a person alone in a crevasse starts negotiating with the blue, and the blue always wins.

When she finally came over the lip — hauled, gasping, frost in her eyebrows, her left side soaked and stiffening — she looked at all of us kneeling around her and said, "Well. That bridge owed me money anyway."

Then she stopped joking, because the cold was in her clothes and starting on her bones, and the next hour was tents and stove and rubbing and sugar-water and Juniper stripping off her own dry coat to wrap Tamsin's legs, giving no order, asking no permission, just doing it, the way she did everything.

We lost the daylight. All of it. In ten days of open passage, we had spent one entire one pulling a cook out of a hole in the world.

Nobody said that last part aloud. Everybody thought it. You could hear us all thinking it in the quiet of the tent that night, five breaths and a stove tick, while outside the wind combed the icefield and the crevasses waited with their endless patience.

Tamsin, wrapped in every spare layer we owned, watched me fold the map and said, sleepily, "You knew that field was bad. You were reading it all morning. Why'd we cross there?"

"Because the alternative was worse," I said. "And because my father crossed there twice and drew it honestly." I smoothed the page. *The Loom*, in his hand. Beneath it, smaller, almost hidden in the margin: *Trust the line. Grieve later.*

"Grieve later," I read aloud, before I could stop myself.

Juniper looked over. "Sounds like him?"

"No," I said, and found it was true. "Sounds like someone who expected to be obeyed after he was dead. Which — yes. Sounds exactly like him."

She smiled at the tent ceiling. "Men who draw maps are all the same. They're just captains whose boats haven't been built yet."

I thought about arguing. Instead I lay back and listened to the glacier groan beneath us — a vast slow shifting, miles of ice settling into its own weight — and felt, for the first time since Ash Hollow, something adjacent to peace. My father had trained me without knowing it. Every summer he'd dragged me up foothills and made me name the landforms; every winter he'd written letters full of bearings and elevations I'd pretended not to memorize. I had spent nineteen years believing I'd inherited nothing from him but his stubborn chin. Turns out he'd left the important part in my hands, the way you leave a tool in a barn for a child to find years later.

The fear was still there. It would always be there; I understood that now. But it sat in the sled with the rest of the load, and I was the one holding the rope.

* * *

We moved at dawn, Tamsin riding the lightened sled with dignity and a hot brick at her back, and made the day's distance by early afternoon — hard, bright travel, the whiteout burned off, the col showing itself at last: a notch in the black skyline with the pass beyond it, and below the notch, according to the map, my father's camp.

I was reading the ice ahead — stamp, listen, step, the litany worn smooth as river stone now — when I noticed the wrongness.

Not in the glacier. Above it. On the lateral moraine, the long gray spine of rock and rubble flanking the ice, something caught the sun and threw it back. Once. Twice. Deliberate.

Metal. Or glass. Something a man carries.

I raised my hand and the rope stopped, and I pointed, and Juniper lifted the spyglass from her coat, and I watched her face do a thing I had learned to read on the river: nothing at all, which meant everything.

"Merrick," she said quietly, lowering the glass. "Five of them. On the moraine. Keeping pace." She handed me the spyglass, and through it I saw them clearly — small, distant, patient as the ice itself, moving along the high rock spine like a sentence following our paragraph.

"They can't cross the icefield," Hollis said. "Too broken. They'd need the same route we're taking."

"Which means," Juniper said, "they don't need to cross it. They just need to be waiting where we come off it." She turned and looked up at the col, then back at the moraine, and I watched her run the arithmetic of it — distances, days, the width of a chokepoint — the way she read sandbars, fast and total.

"How long before we reach the far side?" I asked.

"Tomorrow, if the weather holds." She pocketed the spyglass. "And if it doesn't, we're pinned in the open with them watching every move we make."

Behind us, faint across the miles of ice, came a sound I would remember the rest of my life: a crack like the world's largest rifle, rolling and rolling — the glacier calving somewhere in its upper reaches, shedding tons of itself into the blue, the mountain shrugging as the warmth ate at it.

Juniper's head came up sharply. She listened to the echo die, and her jaw set in a way I had learned meant a decision forming.

"We need to move faster," she said. "And we need to stop being followed."
