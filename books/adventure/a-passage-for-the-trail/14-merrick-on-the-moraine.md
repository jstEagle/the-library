## 14. Merrick on the Moraine

I've spent thirty years with my hands inside engines, and I'll tell you what I know: a machine never lies to you. It rattles when it's failing, it runs hot when it's hurting, and it tells you plain and early exactly what it intends to do. People are the opposite. People smile at you across a card table while they're loading the gun under the table, and they call it manners.

So when Juniper came down off the ridge at first light, moving quick and quiet in that way she had — like a woman who'd already decided something and was just waiting for the rest of us to catch up — I knew before she said a word that we were in trouble. Machines tell you. Juniper only ever told you after.

"Merrick," she said.

Tamsin looked up from the fire, a tin cup halfway to her mouth. Ada went still the way her father must have gone still, once, over a promising vein of rock. Piet wasn't with us anymore, and nobody said his name, but his absence sat at our fire like a sixth man every morning since Two Rivers.

"How many?" I asked.

"Five. Him and four. They're two miles back, maybe three, coming up the lateral moraine. Rook's found them a line through." She crouched by the fire and warmed her hands without seeming to feel them. "He's half-blind, but he walked this country for twenty years. His feet remember."

"Can we outrun them?" Tamsin asked.

"On the ice? No." Juniper said it flat, no drama in it, which was how you knew she meant it. "They're fresh, we're not. They've got Merrick driving them, and Merrick drives men like he means to sell them after. We could run all day and they'd close by dark."

Ada spoke from where she sat with the map spread across her knees, weighted at the corners with four stones like a little altar. "Then we don't run."

Juniper looked at her. There was something passing between those two lately that I didn't have a name for — some current running under the ordinary business of camp, the way current runs under ice. You can't see it, but you'd be a fool to drill there.

"No," Juniper agreed. "We don't run."

* * *

Now, I want to be honest about what I am in this story, because a man my age has earned the right to a little honesty. I'm the engineer. I fix things. When the Kestrel's boiler sulked, I coaxed it. When the portage mule — may he rest wherever stubborn animals go — threw our entire rope cache into a ravine, I climbed down and got it back, grumbling the whole way, because that is my function in this outfit: I do the work and I complain about it, and both halves of that arrangement keep the other honest.

But I also know about breaking things. Every man who understands how a thing works understands how it stops working. It's the same knowledge wearing different clothes.

Which is why, when Juniper laid out what she wanted, she laid it out looking at me.

"The moraine chokepoint," she said, and unrolled her own copy of the route — she'd traced Crane's map onto oilcloth back at the lake, one copy for each of us who needed it, because a map that exists in only one place can be lost or stolen, and we'd learned that lesson in blood and bacon fat at Two Rivers. "Here. The glacier squeezes between the medial moraine and the valley wall. Half a mile of single file, crevasse field on the left, rockfall on the right. Anybody coming behind us has to funnel through."

"And?" Tamsin said.

"And there's a bridge." Juniper tapped the oilcloth. "Snow bridge over a crevasse, right at the mouth of the narrows. It's holding now. It held for Crane's party, according to his notes. But it's late in the melt season, and everything under it is water going somewhere fast."

"You want to cross it and cut it down behind us," I said.

"I want to widen it," she corrected. "There's a difference. A snow bridge you can rebuild. What I want—"

"What you want," I said, "is for the glacier to eat the road behind us."

She didn't flinch. That was the thing about Juniper Whitlock that took people years to learn and enemies about a day: she did not flinch. "The powder charge," she said. "From the Kestrel's magazine. We hauled it up as ballast weight through the portage. Forty pounds of blasting powder, wrapped in oilcloth, dry as a sermon."

I should mention here that I had been carrying forty pounds of blasting powder on my back for six days, up a mountain, and had been told it was ballast. Ballast. As if I were a fool. As if a man who spent his life around engines couldn't identify a crate of explosives by its weight and its smell and the particular way it made his spine itch. I had known what it was since Widow's Stair. I had said nothing, because I had correctly guessed that Juniper was keeping it quiet for reasons, and because I figured if she wanted me to know, she'd tell me, and if she wanted me to carry it, well — I was already carrying it.

"I'll need to see the bridge first," I said. "Judge the charge. Too much and we bring the whole moraine face down, maybe bury the crossing entirely, maybe start an avalanche that takes us with it. Too little and we've wasted forty pounds of powder and told Merrick exactly where we are and what we're willing to do."

"And what are we willing to do?" Tamsin asked. Quiet. Not accusing. Just asking, the way you ask a question you already dread the answer to.

Nobody answered right away. The fire popped. Somewhere off in the white distance, the glacier groaned — that long deep sound like a ship working against its moorings, which I suppose is exactly what it was.

"We're willing to stop them," Juniper said finally. "Not kill them. If it comes to killing, we've already lost more than the pass."

"That's a fine line you're drawing," I said.

"It's the only line worth drawing."

* * *

We broke camp fast and moved. I'll give Merrick this much: he taught us urgency. Six days of being hunted will do that — turn a lazy crew into a sharp one. Tamsin struck the fire so completely that in a year you wouldn't find ash. Ada rolled the map and had it stowed before I'd gotten my pack cinched. Even Juniper, who carried grief the way other people carry a coin in the pocket — always there, mostly forgotten until needed — even she moved lighter than usual, like the decision had burned something off her.

The moraine was a nightmare of loose stone, grey ridges of rock ground fine by ten thousand years of ice, and we climbed it in the flat grey light of a day that couldn't decide whether to snow. The glacier below us was cracked like old paint, blue-green shadows in the fissures that I made a point of not looking into too long. I'm an engineer. I respect depth. Depth is where things fall and don't come back.

The chokepoint was everything Juniper promised. The ice ran narrow between the moraine spine and a wall of shattered granite, and right at the mouth of it lay the bridge — a broad sweep of snow, maybe fifteen feet across, lying innocent as a church over a crevasse that ran clear across the glacier's throat. You could see the sag in it if you knew to look. I knew to look. The middle dipped maybe eight inches lower than the edges, and along its downstream lip the snow had gone thin and grey, rotten, melting from beneath by the river that ran down there in the dark.

I probed it with the pole for an hour while the others watched the back trail. Fifteen feet of span. The crevasse underneath was wider than that — I could hear water chuckling down in it, and when I dropped a stone through a crack near the edge, it fell a long, long time before it splashed.

"Juniper," I called. She came out to me, roped, moving careful. "This bridge is the only way across?"

"For anything heavy. A man could climb down into the crevasse and back up the far side, maybe, if he was young and lucky. With packs? No. This is the door."

"Then here's what I need." I talked it through while she listened, arms crossed, wind pulling at her collar. "I don't blow the bridge — that's your instinct, and it's wrong, because a blown bridge is a message and the message is *war*, and then Merrick's got nothing left to lose and five guns to lose it with. What I do instead is undermine the near lip. Small charge, placed low, tamped right. The bridge stays standing. Looks whole. Looks *fine*. And the moment somebody puts real weight on it — a loaded man, a pack train, whatever — the near edge lets go and the whole span drops into the gap."

She frowned. "You're building a trap."

"I'm building a *delay*. There's a difference, and the difference is that a trap kills and a delay argues. They'll have ropes. Merrick's no fool; he'll rig a crossing eventually. Cost him half a day, maybe a full one, and cost him his nerve besides, because nothing teaches a man caution like watching the earth open under his boots." I spat into the crevasse, for emphasis, and heard it land somewhere far below. "Half a day is what we need to get over the shoulder and down toward the pass before the weather turns."

"And if he pushes men across anyway, fast, before the edge goes?"

"Then the edge goes under them, and they get a bath in the crevasse, and their friends pull them out, and everybody learns something." I met her eye. "It's not gentle, Captain. But it's not a grave either. Unless they're unlucky. And out here—" I gestured at the whole grey roaring emptiness of it, "—unlucky is a wide word."

Juniper stood a moment, looking at the bridge, at the crevasse, at the trail behind. Then she nodded, once, the way she did at the wheel when a channel read true.

"Do it."

* * *

I won't pretend the work was pleasant. Placing a charge in rotten snow over a bottomless crack, with the temperature falling and hunters at your back, concentrates the mind wonderfully. I dug down under the near lip, packed the charge into a chamber I carved in the old ice beneath the snow bridge — solid ice, good tamping, would throw the force sideways and shear the lip clean — and ran the fuse back thirty feet to a boulder on our side. Then I covered my workings, swept the snow smooth with a pine bough, and made the whole thing look like nothing at all, which is the finest trick engineering knows.

We crossed. All five of us, light and quick, one at a time, and I'll admit my heart was in my mouth crossing my own trap, because a man who trusts his arithmetic absolutely is a man who hasn't done enough arithmetic. The bridge held like a lamb. On the far side, Juniper posted Ada high on the rocks with the glass to watch, and we pushed a quarter mile further and waited in the lee of a granite rib, cold and miserable and pretending otherwise.

We didn't wait long. Merrick traveled like he billed: promptly.

We saw them come up the moraine a little after midday — five figures strung out, moving well, moving mean. Even at distance I could pick Merrick out. He walked like a man who expected the ground to cooperate. Behind him, four hired shapes with rifles slung and packs riding high.

They reached the chokepoint. Stopped. Merrick stood at the lip of the bridge a long time, and I could imagine him reading it — the sag, the grey lip, the width of the gap — the way I'd read a boiler plate for hairline cracks. He was dangerous precisely because he wasn't stupid. Stupid men you could outrun. Careful ones you had to out-think, and out-thinking Merrick felt like arm-wrestling a winch.

He sent one man across.

I watched it happen with my hand on the fuse and my heart doing something irregular. The man stepped onto the bridge — light, testing, then committed, moving fast the way you do when you've decided a thing is safe — and he was nearly across, nearly, one stride from the far lip, when the near edge sighed and dropped.

No bang. No drama. Just a soft whump of settling snow, a crack like lake ice in spring, and twelve feet of bridge folding away into the dark with the man still on it. He went down swinging, caught the far lip with both hands, and hung there over the green-black gulf while his companions shouted and scrambled for rope.

For one long moment nobody breathed, least of all me, because I had built that failure to happen under weight, and it had happened under almost none, and if the whole span went, if he fell—

He didn't fall. He hauled himself up over the lip and lay there gasping on the far side, alone, separated from his four companions by a gap you could have dropped a barn into, and above him — this is the part I'll remember — above him, on the rocks, stood Ada, rifle across her knees, visible, deliberate, saying nothing at all.

Merrick's voice carried across the ice, flat and clear. "Hollis!" He knew me. Of course he knew me; he'd been buying drinks for my opinions since Two Rivers. "Nice work! Very tidy!"

I touched my hat. Seemed polite.

They spent the afternoon rigging ropes. We watched them do it — the four on the near side throwing lines, anchoring to boulders, hauling their stranded man back across the gap hand over hand, a slow ant-farce of engineering conducted at the end of our spyglass. Nobody died. Nobody even bled. But when their man finally came up over the edge, soaked to the ribs and shaking, and the five of them stood together on the wrong side of the gap looking at us — looking at *me*, specifically, with the particular attention men pay to the fellow who dropped the floor out from under them — the air changed.

Tamsin felt it. "That's it, isn't it," she said quietly. "Before today, we were cargo to them. Something to intercept. Now we're—"

"Opposition," Juniper said.

"Enemies," Ada said, because she was her father's daughter and didn't dress words in Sunday clothes.

Nobody argued. Down the glacier, faint but distinct, came the sound I'd been dreading all week — a deep report, then another, the calving starting early, the ice giving up its edges to the season. Ten days, Crane's notes had said. Maybe fewer now.

Merrick raised one hand — not a wave, exactly. An acknowledgment. A promise with a glove on it. Then he turned his party and began working back along the moraine, looking, no doubt, for another way around, or another way through, or simply for patience, which was the weapon he was best with.

Juniper watched him go, and I saw her jaw set in the lamplight-grey afternoon.

"He'll find a way," she said. "Rook will sell him one."

"Then we'd best not be here when he does," I said, and shouldered the empty powder crate, forty pounds lighter and somehow feeling heavier than I had all trip.

We moved for the pass. Behind us, in the failing light, I heard the glacier calve again — long, rolling, like distant artillery — and I thought: that's the sound of the world changing its mind. Best to be on the side of the change you can live with.
