## 16. What We Carry

The storm had pinned us in Crane's old camp for two days, and by the second night the fire had become less a comfort than a congregation.

We'd dragged what was left of the mining camp's timbers into the lee of a collapsed sluice-house, stacked them into something like walls, and hung the canvas over it. The wind came off the icefield in long white gusts that made the whole shelter shudder like a living thing, but inside there was firelight, and there was Tamsin's stew — thin on meat, generous on salt pork, miraculous anyway — and there were five people who had no reason to trust each other except that they'd already trusted each other with their lives, which is a faster way to build a family than blood ever was.

It was Hollis who started it. I should have guessed it would be Hollis. He was a man who kept his feelings in the same place he kept his tools: oiled, sorted, and shown to no one. But two days of storm will pry open even an engineer.

He was turning his tin cup slowly in his hands, watching the steam, when he said, without preamble, "I've got a pension coming. Eleven years with the Consolidated Navigation Company before they folded." He said it to the fire, not to us. "Company went under owing every man jack of us back wages. I've been chasing that paper for six years. Six years of letters to lawyers who charge by the word." He took a drink. "That's what I'm carrying up this mountain. A piece of paper that says somebody owes me for my life."

Nobody laughed. It wasn't funny, but it also wasn't sad, exactly. It was just true, and true things sat differently around a fire at nine thousand feet.

"Eleven years," Juniper said quietly. "What did you do for them?"

"Kept their boats alive. Same as I keep yours alive, Captain, though yours tries harder to die." He almost smiled. "I signed on with you because you paid cash and asked nothing about my past. I stay because—" He stopped. Turned the cup again. "Because a man ought to finish what he starts. That's all. That's my whole philosophy, and it fits on a coin."

Tamsin snorted. She was sitting cross-legged with her back against the timber wall, her dark hair still damp from the snow-melt she'd been hauling for the cook pot. "A coin. That's romantic, Hollis."

"Romance is for people whose boilers don't leak."

"Fine." Tamsin set down her bowl. "Then here's mine, since we're confessing to the fire like it's a priest." She looked around at each of us, and I saw her chin come up in that way she had — the way of someone who'd practiced being unashamed of things other people thought shameful. "I ran away from a man who owned me. Not legally. Legally he was just my husband. But he owned me the way Vane owns your boat, Captain. Papers and all. And when I left, I promised myself I would never again do anything because somebody else decided I had to." She picked her bowl back up. "So I'm carrying my freedom. That's the cargo. Every mile of this trail, I'm carrying it, and I'll be damned if I set it down for anybody."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, the glacier groaned — a long, deep sound like a door opening in the bones of the world.

"Vane owns more than boats," Juniper said. It came out flat, but I'd learned by then to hear what lived under Juniper Whitlock's flatness. Flat was where she kept the things that mattered most.

"Go on, then," Tamsin said gently. "Fair's fair."

Juniper looked into the fire for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice had the careful evenness of someone walking a narrow plank over deep water.

"I was seventeen when I first steered a boat. My father's. He died owing money, same as everybody in this country dies or doesn't. The Kestrel was his, and then she was mine, and then — the spring after I lost her papers to Corliss Vane — I understood that I hadn't inherited a boat. I'd inherited a debt wearing a boat's name." Her jaw tightened. "Every run I make, every cargo, every passenger, I'm working off a sum that never gets smaller. Vane holds the papers. Vane holds me. And I have spent eleven years telling myself that as long as I could feel the wheel under my hands, I was free." She shook her head once, sharply, like a horse refusing a bit. "But I'm not stupid. I know the difference between steering and owning. I've just never let myself say it out loud."

"You're saying it now," Piet said softly. He'd been quiet all evening, hunched near the fire with his hands wrapped around his knees — the young deckhand who'd vanished during the fire at Two Rivers and come crawling back three days later, half-starved, with a story about being jumped and tied. Ada didn't believe him. Neither did I, entirely. But he was here, and he'd carried his share up the moraine without complaint, and maybe that counted for something. Maybe it didn't. The fire didn't say.

"That's what I'm carrying," Juniper finished. "The boat. Or the idea of her. I honestly don't know anymore which one keeps me going."

* * *

They turned to me last. Of course they did. I'd been hoping they wouldn't, and I'd been dreading that they might, and both at once, which is how most things felt since my father died.

"My father drew that map," I said. "In between prospecting seasons, in between the years he was gone, he sat at our kitchen table with ink and a straightedge and made something beautiful out of a place that tried to kill him. I used to think he loved the mountains more than us. Now I think—" I stopped. Started again. "Now I think he was trying to give us the only thing he had. A way through."

I pulled the map from its oilcloth sleeve, out of habit, and held it without unrolling it. "He cheated a partner to get this surveyed. He knew it. He wrote it down — Rook, in the margins, and *the pass keeps what it takes*. He spent his whole life carrying that guilt, and then he spent his last week carrying a promise instead. He gave you the map, Captain, and he gave me—" My voice did something I didn't authorize. I waited it out. "He gave me a letter I haven't finished reading yet. Because every time I start, I hear him dying while he wrote it, and I can't."

"Ada." Juniper said my name the way she called soundings — carefully, testing depth.

"He wanted me brought through the pass before the snows. That's all. That's the whole promise. Not gold. Not the claim. Just — through. Him getting me somewhere he couldn't go himself." I looked at the fire. "That's what I'm carrying. My father. All of him. The good and the cheating and the ink-stained straightedge and the letter I can't finish."

For a moment nobody spoke. Then Tamsin reached over and squeezed my wrist, once, hard, which from Tamsin was a speech.

* * *

I couldn't sleep after that. The wind had dropped to a whisper, and the fire had burned down to a red heart, and I lay in my bedroll listening to the glacier talk to itself and thinking about what I'd said.

*My father. That's what I'm carrying.*

And underneath it, quieter, the thing I hadn't said: *the claim matters less than the promise.* If we crossed the ice and stood on the far side with empty hands, my father's promise would still be kept. That was the arithmetic I wanted to believe in. Promise over profit. Love over ledger.

But lying there in the dark, I made myself do the harder sum.

If we came through with nothing — if the claim papers burned in some crevasse or blew away in some storm or proved worthless — then the route through this range belonged to whoever had the muscle to take it. It belonged to Vane. And Vane didn't want a road; he wanted a tollbooth. He'd own every wagon, every mule train, every family that ever wanted to cross. He'd own the pilots and the guides and the trappers and the towns that grew up at either end. He'd own people the way he owned Juniper — papers and all — and he'd call it enterprise, and the law would call it legal, and there would be nothing anyone could do, because the only alternative route was a dead man's map in a dead man's daughter's hands.

My father hadn't fought and cheated and sacrificed for a tollbooth. He'd drawn that map so there would be a way through that belonged to *through* — to travelers, to the crossing itself. The claim wasn't treasure. It was a fence against men like Vane.

I sat up. The fire was nearly out. Across the camp, Juniper was awake too — I could see the ember-light moving on her face, and the compass open in her palm, catching that light the way water catches it.

"Can't sleep either?" I whispered.

"Storm's breaking. Wind's shifted north." She closed the compass. "You were talking in your sleep."

"Lying awake is more likely."

"Mm." She was quiet a moment. Then: "Your father's promise. You said it plain tonight. Through the pass, before the snows. Nothing about claims."

"Nothing about claims," I agreed.

"And yet here we are, hauling survey records and corner-stone filings over an icefield like they're scripture." She glanced at me. "Does that trouble you?"

I thought about giving her the easy answer. I gave her the true one instead.

"It troubled me until tonight. Then I understood — the promise gets *me* through. The claim gets *everybody* through. He'd have wanted both. He just only had time to ask for one."

Juniper looked at me for a long moment, and something passed across her face that I'd rarely seen there — not softness exactly, but the recognition of it, like a woman hearing a language she used to speak.

"Well then," she said. "We'd better get you through, and the paperwork too."

She banked the fire, and I settled back into my bedroll, and above us the clouds were tearing open along the northern sky, showing stars, showing clear weather coming.

And that was when Hollis, who slept lighter than any man I've ever known, sat bolt upright in the dark and said:

"Smoke. Downwind. Somebody's burning green wood on the moraine."

Juniper was on her feet before he finished the sentence.

Merrick.
