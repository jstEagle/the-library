## 17. Rook's Last Climb

The storm had been worrying at the tent fly all night, like a dog that smelled something under the door. I'd slept in fits, dreaming of water — river water, lake water, the black under-ice kind — and woke before dawn with my breath frozen in the wool of my collar and a feeling in my chest I couldn't name until I stepped outside and saw him.

He was maybe forty yards off, at the edge of camp where the snow gave way to bare scree, down on his hands and knees in the gray light. Crawling. Not walking, not staggering — crawling, the way a man does when he has passed every other option and is negotiating now with the ground itself.

"Company," I said, loud enough to wake Hollis, who slept like a man with a clear conscience, which was more than any of us could claim.

Hollis came out rubbing his eyes, took one look, and said, "That's the old man from the trading post."

It was. Silas Rook, half-blind and frost-white, his coat crusted where sweat had frozen and thawed and frozen again. He'd come up the mountain behind Merrick's party, and Merrick — having used him for his eyes and his knowledge of the route — had evidently decided an old man was ballast he could afford to throw overboard.

I stood there with the wind pulling at me and did the arithmetic every captain does when something drifts into her channel uninvited. He'd threatened us. He'd sold our route. He'd have taken everything Crane left if it meant settling his own account. Turning him out would be justice of a kind, and nobody on this mountain would ever know.

But I thought about Elias Crane dying in the mud at that landing, trading the last thing he owned for a promise from a stranger. I thought about how debts work — how they don't care whether you're proud or sorry, only whether they're paid.

"Get him inside," I said.

* * *

We got him into the supply tent, wrapped in every blanket we could spare without freezing ourselves, and Tamsin built the stove up hot enough to make the canvas walls breathe. Rook shivered for two hours straight, teeth chattering out what might have been words, and then somewhere past midmorning the shivering stopped and he lay still, and for a bad quarter hour I thought we'd gone to all this trouble to watch a man die anyway.

Then his eyes opened — clouded, but finding my face by sound more than sight — and he said, "Whitlock. The boat woman."

"The same."

"You should have left me out there." His voice was gravel poured slow. "Would've saved you a burial."

"We're not buried yet, either of us. Talk or rest. Your choice."

He chose talk. Old men always do, near the end; there's nothing left to spend it on.

"Merrick took my notes and left me at the moraine," he said. "Said a blind man slows a climb. Said it kindly, too, which is worse. Cruelty with manners." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "I taught him that. Thirty years buying men cheap and selling them dear. The range collects interest, Captain. I told you that at the post. I just never believed I'd be the principal."

Ada came in with broth and knelt to feed it to him, and I watched him take it — watched something move across his ruined face that I recognized, because I'd seen it on Crane's: the look of a man being tended by a child he wronged, or failed, or both.

"You knew her father," she said. Not a question.

"I knew him better than God did, some years." Rook's head turned toward her voice. "We were partners once. Before the map, before the pass, before either of us understood what we'd found. It wasn't gold, girl. You know that by now, I expect."

"We know," Ada said.

"Surveyed right-of-way. The only road through that ice that will ever carry freight. Crane held the corner-stone record — the original survey marker documentation, filed proper, witnessed proper. Without it the claim is a rumor. With it, it's law." His breathing caught, steadied. "I spent twenty years telling myself he stole it from me. Truth is we drew straws and he won, and I never forgave him for winning fair. That's the meanest kind of grudge there is. It eats the man holding it and leaves the other fellow whole."

"And you sold us to Merrick anyway," I said.

"I did." No flinching. "Because a grudge is a debt too, and I figured the world owed me. Well." He coughed, wet and long. "The world's paid up."

* * *

Toward evening he asked Tamsin to prop him upright against the pack-saddle we were using for a backrest, and he fumbled at his coat with hands that wouldn't quite obey. Ada helped him. From the inner pocket, flat and oilcloth-wrapped, he drew a packet no bigger than a prayer book and pressed it into her palms.

"Corner-stone record," he said. "Crane sent it to me the winter before he died. Didn't trust banks, didn't trust lawyers, trusted an old partner he thought he'd wronged. Wanted me to hold it for you." His clouded eyes moved between us. "And I sat on it. Figured it was leverage. Figured someday I'd trade it for my half." He let out a long breath. "This is me trading it. Price is one thing."

"Name it," I said.

"Bury me facing the pass. Where I can see what we found, even if I never walked it." His mouth worked. "Elias and I used to sit on a ridge below the icefield and argue about what a man's worth measured. He said it was what he carried. I said it was what he owed. Turns out we were both wrong. It's what somebody carries for you, after you can't carry anything yourself."

Nobody spoke. The stove ticked. Outside, the storm had gentled into that deep mountain hush that feels less like weather than like listening.

"There's a condition on the papers," Rook went on, quieter. "Read them careful. Elias wrote a co-holder clause — promise kept, passage made. That's you, Captain. He told me once: *if Whitlock gets my girl over the ice, she's earned her name on it.* He knew you'd refuse payment. Knew you'd call it salvage. So he made it law instead of kindness." A ghost of a smile. "Cleverest thing he ever did, and he did it dying."

Ada was holding the packet like it might burn or bless, hard to say which. "Why give it to us? Why not die with it?"

"Because dead men make poor toll-keepers," Rook said. "And because Vane would have dug it out of my grave eventually. Some things want living hands." He sagged then, all at once, like a rope cut, and we eased him down, and he slept — really slept — for the first time since he'd crawled in.

* * *

He lasted three days.

Long enough, on the second evening, to ask me to sit close, and to tell me the last of it in a voice worn down to thread. About the year he and Crane had surveyed the pass, half-starved, mapping a route no one wanted because everyone was still mad for gold. About the letter he'd written Vane's people offering the route for sale, and the answer he'd gotten: *we don't buy claims, we wait out claimants.* About how he'd understood, finally, that he'd spent his whole life on the wrong side of that sentence.

"You could still be rich," I told him. "You could have held out. Named your price."

"Rich is a number, Captain. Paid is a condition." His hand found mine — cold as a mooring cleat in January, but gripping. "You gave a dying stranger shelter on a mountain because of a promise you tell yourself you didn't make. Don't think I didn't see it. Mercy's the only currency that appreciates. Everything else I ever touched depreciated." He almost smiled. "Bury me high. Face east. The pass opens to the east light first — Elias always said so, and he was right about everything except forgiving himself."

On the third morning, while the storm broke apart into hard blue sky, Silas Rook stopped breathing as quietly as he'd done most things badly and a few things well.

* * *

We buried him above the tree line, in ground Hollis picked — sheltered from wind, deep enough to hold. There was no minister and no coffin; there was a blanket, and Tamsin singing low, a hymn she claimed she'd learned in a saloon, which explained its tempo. We set stones over him, and Ada carved his name into the flattest one with her father's knife, and beneath it, after some debate, one line:

*HE OWED NOTHING HERE.*

"He'd have argued with that," Hollis said.

"That's why it's true," Ada said.

I stood a moment looking east, where the pass showed itself in the morning sun — white, indifferent, briefly open, already counting down its ten days. Somewhere behind us, Merrick was climbing with four men and a grudge. Somewhere ahead was the narrows, and the descent, and whatever waited at the bottom of a mountain for people who had given away their boat's engine charge and their provisions' margin and, in Rook's case, their last chance at profit.

I opened the oilcloth packet one more time before wrapping it back up. Legal script, faded ink, and there it was in Elias Crane's cramped hand, the clause Rook had promised: *co-holder, Juniper Whitlock, for promise kept.* My name, written by a dead man who had bet everything on a stranger's stubbornness.

Ada came to stand beside me.

"He planned all of it," she said. "Before he ever met you. He read about you in a shipping gazette — *captain known to finish what she starts.* He picked you the way you pick a channel."

"I know," I said. "I've been trying to decide whether to be flattered or furious."

"Both," she said. "He'd have wanted both."

Below us, far down the valley, a thread of smoke rose where no smoke of ours burned — thin, deliberate, a signal or a cookfire, and either way it meant Merrick's party was closer than the storm had let us hope.

I folded the packet into my coat, next to the compass, and turned to the crew.

"Break camp," I said. "We've got a canyon to reach before someone else reaches it first."
