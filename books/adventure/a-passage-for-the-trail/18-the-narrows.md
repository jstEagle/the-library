## 18. The Narrows

I have spent forty years around engines, and I will tell you the truth about them: an engine never lies to you. It rattles when it's frightened, it knocks when it's hurt, and it goes quiet when it's done. People could stand to learn a thing or two from engines. People lie even to themselves, especially on mountains, especially when they're tired and scared and pretending otherwise.

We had been pretending for three days.

The far side of the range was supposed to be the easy side — that's what Juniper kept saying, in the tone of a woman repeating a thing until it becomes true. Downhill, she said. Warmer, she said. Timber by tomorrow, she said. What she did not say, because none of us knew it yet, was that the glacier's meltwater had been busy all summer carving its opinion into the mountainside, and its opinion was a slot canyon two hundred feet deep with a creek at the bottom running like it owed somebody money.

We heard it before we saw it. A sound like the whole range clearing its throat.

"That's not a creek," Tamsin said, standing at the lip where the pines fell away. "That's a river having opinions."

"It's the same creek we crossed yesterday," Ada said. She had the map out, Crane's map, soft as cloth now from handling. "It drops. Look — the contour lines crowd together right here." Her finger traced the gorge down and down to where the lines finally loosened into something like valley. "There's no way around. The walls go up sheer on both sides for miles. This is the way down or there isn't one."

Juniper looked over the edge for a long time. I watched her do the arithmetic she does — the same arithmetic she does reading sandbars, except this time the river was vertical and she couldn't feel it through her boots.

"Rope work," she said finally. "We can rig lines along the wall where the water's cut ledges. Hollis, how much line are we carrying?"

"Enough," I said, "if nobody falls twice."

"That's the spirit."

* * *

Here is what I know about Juniper Whitlock, having worked for her long enough that working for her stopped being a job and became more of a diagnosis: she makes her decisions early and tells herself about them later. I'd seen it with Vane's agents. I'd seen it with Rook. She stands very still, says something practical, and somewhere behind her eyes a door has already closed on every other option. You find out which door later, usually at speed.

So when Merrick's party showed up on the moraine behind us that afternoon — five figures now, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who know their quarry is cornered — I watched Juniper go still, and I knew the door was closing.

"They've gained since morning," Ada said, glassing them with the spyglass. "They're not hurrying. They don't have to."

"No," Juniper agreed. "They don't." She lowered herself onto a rock, which she only does when she wants her body to make a point her voice won't. "Merrick lost two days digging himself out of that crevasse trap, and he's made them back. That means he knows this ground better than we do, or he's got better legs, or both."

"He's got Rook's knowledge," Piet said quietly. We'd picked the boy up again outside the mining camp — he'd come stumbling after us half-starved, swearing Merrick had strung him along with threats against his family, and Juniper had taken him back without a word, though I noticed she put him where she could see him. "Rook walked this pass thirty years ago. He'll know every shortcut."

"Rook's dead," Tamsin said.

"Rook's *gone*," Piet corrected. "Merrick left him on the ice. But what Rook knew walked down with Merrick."

Nobody said anything for a while. Below us the creek thundered in its slot, white and furious with snowmelt, and above us the afternoon light came slanting gold through the pines like nothing in the world was wrong.

"There's a narrows," Juniper said at last. "Where the canyon pinches. I saw it from the ridge this morning — the walls come together close enough to spit across, and there's a shelf above it where the rock overhangs." She turned and looked at me, and I felt my stomach drop the way it does when an engine makes a sound you've been pretending not to hear. "Hollis. How much powder did we haul up from the cache?"

And there it was.

When we'd abandoned the Kestrel at the lakeshore — roped her down, covered her, promised her we'd be back, the lies you tell a boat — Juniper had insisted on salvaging the powder charge from her hold. Ballast, she'd called it. Weight we might need. Forty pounds of blasting powder wrapped in oilcloth, hauled up a glacier on the backs of people who already hated their loads, and I'd grumbled about it the whole climb because I am an honest man and grumbling is honest work.

"You're not thinking what I think you're thinking," I said.

"I'm thinking the narrows is the only door out of this canyon," Juniper said. "And I'm thinking Merrick has a key and we don't want him using it."

"You blow that pinch and you seal the route for the season. Maybe forever. That's the passage, Juniper. That's the whole claim — the road, the tolls, everything Ada's father—" I stopped, because Ada was standing right there, and because saying it out loud made it worse.

"I know what it is," Juniper said.

"Do you?" I'm the only one aboard who talks to her like that anymore. Somebody has to. "Because I've watched you give things up piece by piece this whole trip — your cargo, your route, your boat — and every time you called it strategy. This one isn't strategy. This one's just burning the bridge because the bridge is worth less than our necks, and I want to hear you say it plain before I start drilling holes in rock."

She held my look. Behind her, the creek roared.

"The claim papers are in Ada's pocket," she said. "Paper doesn't need a road. Paper needs a courthouse and a witness list, and we've got both waiting in the valley. Merrick doesn't need the road either — he needs *us*, or he needs the map, and the map's memorized three ways by now. What he can't have is a season of free passage to bring his own crew through and file his own paper ahead of ours." She paused. "And yes. Partly I just want him off our backs. I'm allowed one selfish reason per mountain."

"One," I said.

"One."

* * *

We rigged the descent that evening and went down into the canyon at first light, because the melt runs highest in the afternoon and Juniper wanted the crossing done while the ledges were still dry-ish, a word I use generously. Two hundred feet of ladder-work and ledge-walking with the creek foaming below like a kettle that's given up on manners. Tamsin sang the whole way down — low, under her breath, some dockside song about a drowned sailor, which I told her was not comforting, and she said it wasn't meant to be, it was meant to keep time.

Ada crossed last but best. Something happened to that girl on the icefield — she went in scared of heights and came out with her fear filed down into a tool she uses. Her father's hands, Juniper calls them. Watching her read the rock, testing holds the way her father must have tested gravel bars, I understood Elias Crane hadn't just left her a map. He'd left her a trade, and it had been sitting in her all along, waiting for a reason.

By midmorning we stood on the south rim, all six of us, looking back across the gorge at the trail we'd descended. And then we climbed to the overhang above the narrows, and I got out the drill, and the day stopped being about travel.

The rock up there was good granite, the kind that takes a hole clean. I drilled four charges' worth while the others ferried powder up from the packs, and I'll admit my hands weren't as steady as usual. Not from fear, exactly. From the weight of it. Forty years I've broken rock — for road cuts, for mine shafts, once for a grave — and every time it's been stone standing between a man and something he wanted. This was the first time I'd ever set a charge to break stone standing between other men and something they wanted, and I found I didn't care for the difference.

Juniper crouched beside me at the last hole, tamping the charge home with the wooden rammer, careful as a woman dressing a wound.

"You could still stop," I said. "We could run for it. We've got maybe half a day on them."

"We'd spend that half day and the next ten after it looking over our shoulders. And if they catch us in the open valley—" She didn't finish it. She didn't have to. Merrick had four men and no rules; we had six people, two of them barely grown, and a stack of papers worth more than any of our lives to certain parties.

"Say it anyway," I said. "For the record. Whatever record there is."

She sat back on her heels. Below us, through the gap of the narrows, the creek ran white and endless, the same water that had carried the Kestrel's hull, that had floated every mile of freight Juniper ever fought for. She looked at it a long moment.

"I'm doing this on purpose," she said. "Not in panic. Not in anger. I'm choosing it, with my eyes open, and I'll live with it, whatever it costs." A breath. "The Kestrel was never going to survive this trip intact, Hollis. I've known that since the lakeshore. Boats are for the water they're built for, and I built my whole self for a river that ended at Widow's Stair. Better she end as a wall than rot as a memory."

"That's the most words you've ever wasted on sentiment."

"Don't tell anyone."

* * *

Merrick's party came into view on the far rim at midafternoon — five specks descending our morning's trail, unhurried, patient, inevitable. Through the spyglass I could see Merrick himself in front, tall and easy, the way a man walks when he believes the world has already agreed to his terms.

Juniper stood at the fuse with a match.

"Wait," Ada said suddenly.

Everyone froze. The girl was staring across the gorge, and her face had gone strange — not afraid, exactly. Reading.

"The ledge," she said. "Their ledge. See where it narrows, just past the slide rock? If the blast brings down the overhang, it won't just block the narrows. It'll take that section of ledge with it. They won't be able to climb past the debris for weeks — maybe not till freeze-up, when the rock settles." She looked at Juniper. "You're not sealing a door. You're erasing a stair."

"Is that a problem?"

Ada considered. I watched her consider — really weigh it, the way her father must have weighed a current, the way Juniper weighs a bar. "No," she said finally. "He chose this. He followed us over the ice knowing what we'd do if he pressed. That's not us deciding for him. That's the mountain keeping score." She stepped back. "Light it."

Juniper struck the match.

I'll tell you what forty pounds of powder sounds like when it takes a canyon wall in its teeth: it sounds like the end of something. The report went up the gorge and came back off a hundred faces, doubled and redoubled, and then the overhang above the narrows shrugged loose and came down in a single long grey roar, dust boiling up out of the slot like the mountain exhaling, and when it cleared there was no narrows. There was a jumble of house-sized stone plugged into the gorge like a cork, and the creek, insulted, was already finding its way through the wreckage in a dozen new white threads, and the ledge beyond it was simply gone — a raw scar of fresh fracture where solid rock used to hold a man's boot.

Across the gorge, five figures stood motionless on the trail. Even at that distance I could feel them staring.

Merrick took off his hat. I don't know why. Maybe respect, maybe fury, maybe just the gesture of a man acknowledging a debt he intends to collect someday. He put the hat back on, turned, and led his men back up the trail, away, small and smaller until the pines took them.

Tamsin whooped. Piet sat down abruptly on a rock like his strings had been cut. Hollis — that's me — found that his knees had opinions about standing, so he sat too.

Juniper didn't move. She stood at the rim with the burnt match still in her fingers, looking down at the place where her boat's powder had become a mountain's scar, and her face was the face of a woman at a funeral who is also, secretly, at a wedding, and cannot decide which grief to feel first.

Ada went and stood beside her. She didn't say anything. She didn't touch her, either. She just stood there, shoulder to shoulder, the way you do when words would only get in the way of the arithmetic, and after a while Juniper's hand found hers, or hers found Juniper's, and they stayed like that while the dust settled and the remade creek sang through the ruins.

"Well," I said, because somebody had to, and because I'm the honest one. "That's the Kestrel spent. Engine, hull, papers, name — all of it, for a pile of rocks and a head start." I looked at the two of them, at the whole ragged lot of us, alive and free and poorer than church mice. "Hope whatever's waiting for us down in that valley turns out to be worth a boat."

Juniper turned around. And I swear on my tools, tired and smoke-grimed and freshly widowed from the only thing she ever owned, she smiled.

"Hollis," she said, "it always was."
