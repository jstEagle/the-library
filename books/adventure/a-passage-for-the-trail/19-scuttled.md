## 19. Scuttled

I have lit fuses before. Small ones, for stumps and snags, the kind of work a river pilot does without ceremony. You light them and you walk away briskly and you don't look back, because looking back is how you lose an eyebrow.

This one I looked at for a long time.

The powder charge had come up from the Kestrel's hold in Hollis's pack, wrapped in oilcloth like a sleeping infant, hauled over Widow's Stair on the backs of men who didn't know what it was until this morning. Ballast, I'd called it when we loaded it at the lakeshore cache. Dead weight. There is a certain poetry in that which I would appreciate more if my hands weren't shaking.

The fuse ran forty feet along the canyon floor to where we crouched behind a granite shoulder, all six of us pressed into stone like letters into an envelope. Below us the narrows roared — the melt-swollen creek running brown and furious through a slot barely wide enough for a determined man to turn around in. Behind us, maybe half a mile back up the trail, Merrick's party was closing. We'd heard them before we saw them: the ring of iron-shod boots on rock carries strange in cold air, and Tamsin, who hears everything twice, caught it first.

"Six of them," she'd said, counting by ear. "Maybe seven."

"Five guns between us," Hollis said, "and two of those belong to people who've never shot at anything angrier than a grouse."

So it wasn't going to be a fight. Fights are for people with even numbers and good positions. This was going to be arithmetic.

The wall above the narrows was fractured — you could see it from fifty yards, a seam of shattered rock where some ancient frost had been patiently unbuilding what the glacier built. Crane's map had marked it. *Weak strata — avoid in thaw,* in his cramped hand, and beneath it, almost as an afterthought: *Could be brought down with intent.* The man had surveyed his own tomb and left instructions. I try not to think about whether he knew, when he inked that line, who would need them.

Ada had known first. That's the thing I keep returning to. She stood at the lip of the narrows with her father's map open against her knee and said, quietly, "He marked it for us. He must have known someone would come." And then she'd looked at me, and whatever she saw in my face made her look away, because even then — even knowing what I was about to do — I couldn't make my face behave.

Hollis rigged the charge while the rest of us hauled rocks. Not to bury it — to seat it, tuck it into the seam like a coin into a pocket. He worked with the same unhurried competence he brought to everything, wiping his hands on his thighs afterward, checking the fuse twice, three times.

"You want to do the honors?" he asked me. "It's your boat."

"It was my boat," I said. "What's down there now is ballast and memory."

But I took the match anyway, because he was right, and because there are debts you don't hand off to other people no matter how much your hands shake.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about scuttling a boat: it doesn't sound like anything. No great report, no thunderclap. The fuse hisses, the world holds its breath, and then the mountain simply decides, with the indifference of all large old things, to sit down.

The crack came first — a single flat note like the sky snapping its fingers — and then the whole face of the wall shrugged loose and went down into the narrows in a long white roar. Dust boiled up out of the slot and rolled over us, hot and mineral-smelling, tasting of copper and old stone. The creek, choked, backed up for one impossible moment and then found its voice again somewhere below, muffled, redirected, furious in a new register.

When the dust thinned enough to see, the narrows were gone. In their place: a rampart of broken granite thirty feet high, already shedding smaller stones into the churn, sealing the slot from wall to wall. Nothing was coming through that. Not today, not this season, not with ten men and dynamite of their own. The melt would cut it down eventually — next summer, maybe the summer after — but by then the claim papers would be filed and Vane's leverage would be ash.

Behind us, faint but distinct, came shouting. Merrick's voice, carrying that particular fury of a man watching his livelihood turn into gravel. Someone fired twice — wild shots, spanging off rock well wide of anything human, the gunplay of a man who needs to make noise because noise is all he has left.

We didn't run. Running felt wrong, somehow — undignified toward the dead. We stood in the settling dust, six people breathing hard, and watched the mountain finish what we'd started.

Then Piet, who had come back to us at the portage with his bruised conscience and his coerced confession, made a small sound.

"Someone's down there," he said. "On their side. I heard — after the blast. A cry."

Nobody moved for a moment. The wind pushed the last of the dust eastward. Somewhere under the rubble, muffled by a hundred tons of fresh granite, the creek kept grinding.

"We can't dig him out," Hollis said finally. Not cruelly. Just true. "Not with five of us and no tools and that slope still shedding. Anyone goes down there, they're part of the wall too."

"I know," Piet said. His voice cracked on the second word. He was nineteen and he'd set the fire at Two Rivers because men with money had convinced him he had no other choice, and he carried things, that boy, carried them like ballast of his own. "I know. I just needed somebody to say it."

I said it. I don't remember exactly what words I used. Something about how the man had chosen his company when he signed on with Merrick, something about how we hadn't started this. All of it true and none of it comforting, the way truth usually is. Ada put her hand on Piet's shoulder and left it there, and we turned up the trail, and the man under the rocks stayed under the rocks, and that is a ledger entry I will carry until I die. Not my hand on the fuse alone. Six hands. But mine held the match.

* * *

We camped that night above the slide, close enough to hear the creek working at its new obstruction, patient as a debt collector. Nobody talked much. Tamsin cooked anyway — she always cooks, it's how she prays — and we ate beans and hard bread in silence broken only by the fire popping and, once, distantly, the sound of Merrick's party retreating back up the moraine. Defeated for the season. Ruined, probably; a man like Merrick doesn't fund a second expedition out of his own pocket after losing four men's wages and a client's confidence in the same week.

I should have felt triumphant. That's what I kept waiting for — the hot clean rush of having won. We'd done it. We'd sealed the pass, saved the claim, outrun the money. Somewhere in my coat pocket were survey documents worth more than every boat on the river combined.

Instead I sat apart from the fire, at the edge of the light, and grieved.

Because here is the accounting I couldn't say aloud: the Kestrel was never just a boat. I bought her hull-deep in debt at twenty-three years old, and I paid her off plank by plank, and when Corliss Vane took her papers as collateral I told myself she was still mine, really mine, that paper was just paper. Every dollar I ever earned, every run I ever made, every sandbar I ever read like scripture — all of it went into her, or through her, or because of her. When people on the river said *Juniper Whitlock*, what they meant was *the woman who runs the Kestrel*. Take away the boat and the name was just sounds.

And now her engine sat in a cache at the lakeshore, wrapped in canvas, already rusting. Written off. Even if I lived to be ninety, even if I crawled back down there in spring with a wagon train and a prayer, no engine survives a winter in the high country wet. Hollis had said it gently, the way he says true things: *She's done, Captain. Whatever comes back for her, it won't be her.*

Scuttled. That was the word for what I'd done to the narrows, and it was the word for what I'd done to myself, and I sat there in the smoke-flavored dark understanding that grief and relief had gotten married somewhere inside me and I couldn't tell anymore which one was speaking.

"She was a good boat," said a voice beside me.

Ada. She'd come quiet, the way she does everything, and settled onto the rock next to mine without asking permission, which by then she'd earned the right to do. She didn't look at me. She looked at the fire, at the others, at the black shape of the mountains.

"She was," I said. My voice came out rougher than I intended. "Fifteen years. She leaked in the spring and sulked in the fall and once she grounded herself on a bar so solid I had to wait three days for rain, and I loved her like family. More than family, if I'm honest. Family you don't get to choose."

Ada said nothing.

That was the thing about her — she'd learned it from her father, maybe, or learned it the hard way in Ash Hollow — that silence is sometimes the only honest thing you can offer a person. Everyone else at that fire would have told me the Kestrel was just wood and iron. Everyone else would have said *you did what you had to do* or *you'll build again* or any of the hundred kind lies people use to fill the space where understanding should be. Ada just sat beside me in the cold and let the grief be as big as it actually was.

After a while she reached over and took my hand. Didn't squeeze it. Didn't say anything. Just held it, the way you'd hold a rope — firmly enough to matter, loosely enough that I could let go if I needed to.

I didn't let go.

"The promise is kept," she said eventually, so quietly I nearly missed it under the creek's grinding. "Or it will be, when the papers are filed. He'd have understood. About the boat. He gave things up too — I'm learning that. Everything he did, near the end, was trade-offs. He traded me the only way he knew how."

"He traded you well," I said.

"So did you."

And there it was, the sentence I'd been circling for eleven chapters of my life without landing on. So did you. As though the boat and the girl were the same transaction, as though giving up the Kestrel and keeping the promise were one act viewed from two sides. Maybe they were. Maybe that's what I'll believe on the good days.

* * *

Near dawn I walked down to the edge of the slide and looked at it properly for the first time. The light was gray and honest, the kind of light that flatters nothing. The rampart of stone rose out of the churning water, already wearing a lacework of spray, and beyond it the narrows continued on the far side, visible through the gap where the creek spilled over the new dam — narrowed, choked, impassable.

Somewhere on the other side of that rock, the far country waited. Valleys. Settlements. A timber office where a dead prospector's daughter could file papers that would outlast everyone in this story. Snow was coming — you could smell it under the pine, that clean metallic edge the air gets before the sky opens — and we had perhaps two days of walking to reach the valley floor before it did.

I took out the compass. Crane's compass, traded with the map, brass worn bright where his thumb had rubbed it for thirty years. It pointed, as it always did, at a pass I could no longer see and a future I couldn't yet imagine. I closed the lid.

"Well," I said to the morning, to the mountain, to the boat-shaped hole in my chest. "Nothing for it but forward."

Behind me, boots crunched on frost. Hollis, Tamsin, Piet, and Ada, falling into the loose formation we'd worn smooth as a river stone over these weeks. None of them asked what I'd decided. They'd already read it in my spine, the way I used to read sandbars — the small tells, the set of the shoulders, the angle of a body relative to its intentions.

"Two days to the settlements," Hollis said. "Three, if the snow comes early."

"Then we walk fast," I said, "and we arrive before the weather does. Ada — your father's papers. Are they dry?"

She patted her coat, over her heart, where she'd carried them since the icefield. "Dry as a sermon."

"Good. Because tomorrow, Miss Crane, you're going to walk into a land office and become the richest orphan in the territory. And I—" I stopped. Started again. "I'm going to stand behind you and watch, and I honestly don't know yet what I am after that. But I'll figure it out on the way down."

Tamsin laughed — the first laugh any of us had managed since the blast, startled out of her like a bird from cover. "Captain Whitlock, unemployed. Now there's a headline the river papers won't believe."

"They'll believe it when Vane does," I said, and found, to my genuine surprise, that I was smiling. Grief and relief, married and bickering, but both of them present. Both of them mine.

We shouldered our packs. The snow began an hour later, fat slow flakes drifting down through the pines like the mountain letting go of something, one white piece at a time — and I thought of the Kestrel, and let her go too, one memory at a time, and walked down out of the high country toward whatever a woman is when she is no longer her boat.

The answer, I suspected, was waiting in a valley full of strangers, a land office, and a spring that would come, as springs do, whether the Kestrel floated to meet it or not.
