## 1. The Man Who Owns No Address

The freight car smelled of dried fish and creosote, and Kestrel Lockridge had been counting its jolts the way other people counted sheep.

Forty-one since the last water stop. Forty-one means eleven miles, maybe twelve, if the grade held true past the headland. The couplings on this line were old—pre-war, probably, salvaged from a passenger service that had died with the coastal hotels—and they clattered in a rhythm Kestrel's body knew better than any lullaby. *Clack-clack. Clack. Clack-clack.* A bad joint at mile marker nine, a curve somewhere around the fish cannery. Kestrel had never ridden this line before. It didn't matter. Rails spoke a language, and Kestrel had been fluent since before they could write their own name.

The train slowed for the approach to Saltmere, and Kestrel did what they always did: rolled onto their stomach in the gap between crates of salted herring, pressed one eye to the rusted gap in the floorboards, and watched the ground slide by beneath them. Ballast, gray and weed-stitched. A culvert mouth, half-collapsed, dry as a sermon. A signal post leaning at fifteen degrees with its lamp long since shot out or stolen. All of it cataloged, filed, remembered. That was the work. That was the gift and the curse of a mind that refused to let a dead line stay dead.

*Saltmere Junction,* Kestrel thought. *Built 1861 as the terminus of the Meridian Coastal Extension. Passenger service withdrawn 1879 when the cliff road opened. Freight only since. Two sidings, a water tower, a coal stage nobody's used in six years. The main line to the interior was abandoned in '74 after the Vell River bridge washed out—never rebuilt, because the Meridian Railway Consolidated decided it was cheaper to ship everything around the headland by sea.*

Kestrel knew these things the way a priest knows scripture. Not because anyone had taught them, though a father's voice had planted the first seeds, but because the knowledge had grown in them like ivy on an abandoned station wall. Every closed line in the western territories lived somewhere behind Kestrel's eyes, a vast map of rust and silence that no one else had bothered to keep.

The train ground to a halt with a shriek of brake shoes that needed replacing. Kestrel counted to sixty. Then to sixty again. A brakeman's boots crunched past on the gravel, unhurried, and a door slammed somewhere up the consist. Only then did Kestrel slip from the car, drop the eight feet to the ballast in a crouch that made no more sound than a cat crossing a kitchen, and walk—casual, unhurried, a drifter with salt-stained cuffs and nowhere particular to be—out the far end of the yard.

* * *

Saltmere in the last hour of daylight was a town pretending to be more than it was. A crescent of gray stone houses above a harbor full of fishing boats. A promenade with rusting railings. Three streets of shops, two of them shuttered, the third selling rope, bait, and hope in equal measure. Gulls screamed over the fish market like creditors at a funeral.

Kestrel moved through it according to rules. Rules that had kept them alive and anonymous for eleven years, since the night they'd walked out of the Meridian yard at Corvane with a carpetbag, a father's brass watch, and nothing else.

*Rule one: never sleep twice in the same town.* Saltmere would get one night, maybe two if the weather turned, and then the coast road or a southbound freight, whichever offered itself first.

*Rule two: pay cash, pay little, and pay attention to nothing.* The man who asks questions is the man who gets remembered. Kestrel's business in any town was the business of no one: a meal, a room, a look at the station notice board, and gone.

*Rule three: never let them see your face twice.* Cap down. Collar up. Walk like a man tired from honest work, because tired men are invisible, and honest work is the last thing anyone suspects you of.

Kestrel found a boarding house on Rope Walk run by a widow with a face like a closed ledger. She took Kestrel's coin, pushed a key across the counter, and asked no questions, which was exactly what Kestrel paid for. The room was narrow, smelled of lamp oil and the sea, and had a window overlooking the harbor where the fishing fleet rocked at anchor like patient animals.

Kestrel ate bread and hard cheese standing at that window, and let the mind do what it always did in a new place: read the rails.

The coastal spur still ran, freight only, three trains a week if the tide of commerce ran high. It hugged the cliff line north of town, threading between the old sea stacks, and Kestrel had watched its cuttings slide past from the fish car with a surveyor's involuntary attention. There was something wrong with the geometry up there. Something the timetable didn't account for. A stretch where the line curved inland away from the cliffs for no engineering reason Kestrel could see from a moving train—which meant the reason was one you could only see standing still, at the cliff base, at the right hour.

And the harbor master's board, which Kestrel had read in passing on the walk up from the yard, had carried a notice in fresh paint: *EXTREME LOW TIDE FORECAST. SPRING TIDE. HARBOR AUTHORITY ADVISES CAUTION ON THE NORTH FLATS.*

Spring tide. Extreme low. The sea pulling back farther than it had in years.

Kestrel chewed the cheese slowly and told themselves it was none of their business. The coast was full of oddities. Caves, wrecks, smugglers' stairs cut into the rock by men two hundred years dead. A body could spend its whole life chasing coastal curiosities and die poor and found.

But the mind was already working. The mind was already laying the coastal spur over the old survey maps in memory, the ones Kestrel had memorized from a father's desk at Corvane, the ones with a notation in the margin that Kestrel had never forgotten and never understood: *the passage runs under, not over.* Under. Under the range. A tunnel that crossed the mountains where no tunnel was ever built—or rather, where no tunnel was ever *admitted* to being built.

Kestrel's father had gone looking for it. That was the story the family never told, the silence at the center of every Lockridge dinner. Elias Lockridge, section engineer, the finest route-finder on the Meridian payroll, had taken a leave of absence in the spring of '71 and walked into the range with a survey crew of four and come back as nothing at all. No body. No gear. No word. The Meridian paid the widow a settlement and struck his name from the seniority rolls inside a year, and Kestrel's mother had spent the rest of her short life looking at the mountains as if they owed her an explanation.

Kestrel had spent eleven years looking at rails instead. Rails didn't lie. Rails went where they were built and stopped where they were abandoned, and if you knew them well enough, they told you everything a country had ever tried to hide.

*The passage runs under, not over.*

Kestrel finished the cheese, brushed the crumbs from their coat, and went down to walk the harbor before dark, because rule four—the rule beneath all the rules—was *know your exits.* Every town, every yard, every room: know how you'd leave it, and leave nothing behind to suggest you'd ever been.

* * *

The north flats at dusk were a country of wet silver, the tide out so far that the fishing boats sat heeled over in the mud like drunks sleeping it off. The smell was kelp and old wood and the mineral breath of the exposed seabed. Kestrel walked out along the harbor wall with hands in pockets and cap low, one drifter among the dozen idlers who'd come to gawk at the strange bare sea, and let the eyes do the work the mouth never would.

The cliff line ran north from the harbor in a long rampart of dark stone, and at its base, where the spur line curved inland above, the retreating tide had peeled back a curtain Kestrel was certain hadn't been lifted in living memory. A shelf of wave-cut rock, glistening, strewn with weed and the shells of things that had no business being so far from water. And in the cliff face itself—

Kestrel stopped walking.

There was an opening. Low, wide, half-veiled in a skirt of hanging kelp and shadow, but unmistakably an opening, and—this was the part that made the back of Kestrel's neck prickle—there was masonry around it. Cut stone, dressed and fitted, weathered to the same black as the cliff but *cut*, and above it, so eroded it was nearly nothing, a lintel with letters worn to ghosts.

Kestrel stood in the crowd of idlers and did not move for a long time. The gulls screamed. The light died by degrees, the water's edge creeping back in with the patience of a debt collector.

*The passage runs under, not over.*

Elias Lockridge had been a route-finder. He had read country the way Kestrel read timetables. And if he had stood on this same shore—no, Kestrel thought, he wouldn't have stood on the shore. He'd have stood up there, on the spur, watching where the line bent inland, and he'd have asked himself the same question Kestrel had asked from the fish car: *why does the railroad flinch here?* And he'd have answered it the way Kestrel was answering it now, with the tide out and the stone showing: because the men who built the coastal spur had found something under the cliff, and had bent their line around it like a horse around a snake.

Kestrel turned away. That was the discipline. That was the whole architecture of the life: you saw the mystery, you named it, and you walked away from it, because mysteries had weight and weight left footprints, and footprints were how a man who owned no address got found.

Kestrel walked back along the harbor wall as the lamps came on, one by one, in the windows of Saltmere, and did not look north again. It took everything they had.

* * *

The trouble started at the station.

Kestrel had gone back at full dark to read the notice board—rule five, *always know what's posted*, because notices were how the world announced its intentions to people who weren't listening—and was standing under the yard lamp copying into memory a freight schedule for the southern division when the feeling arrived.

It arrived the way weather arrives, ahead of itself: a pressure, a stillness, the sense of attention settling on the back of the neck like a hand.

Kestrel did not turn. Turning was confirmation. Instead Kestrel finished reading the notice—the last item was nothing, a reward poster for a stolen horse, two hundred dollars, a bay gelding with a white sock—and let the eyes flick sideways in the reflection of the lamp's glass chimney.

A man stood at the edge of the platform light. Not a brakeman—no lantern, no badge, and brakemen didn't stand that still. Not a harbor idler—his boots were too good, city boots, and his coat was cut for a desk though he wore it like a man who'd been told to dress down and resented the instruction. He was maybe forty, lean, with a face like a folded document, and he was looking directly at Kestrel with the particular stillness of a man comparing two pictures.

Kestrel's heart kept its own timetable, and it did not speed up. Eleven years of practice. Kestrel yawned—actually yawned, a long jaw-cracking performance of a man bored past endurance—stretched, and ambled off the platform toward the dark end of the yard, unhurried, a tired workingman heading to his bed. Behind them, unhurried too, the folded face began to follow.

Kestrel gave it a hundred yards. Let him commit. Let him think the quarry was dull and slow. Then, at the gap between the coal stage and the water tower, where the shadows pooled deep as ink, Kestrel did the thing that had saved their skin in Corvane and in Harrow's Bend and in a dozen towns with forgettable names: stopped, stepped sideways into the dark, and went still.

The man came on. Passed within six feet. Kestrel could smell him—tobacco and lamp oil and something under it, paper and ink, the smell of a man who lived in offices. He stopped at the end of the coal stage, and he did something that made the cold walk up Kestrel's spine like a spider.

He took out a piece of paper, and struck a match, and read it by the matchlight. And in the flare of that match, before the flame bent and died, Kestrel saw the paper clearly: a printed notice, a column of text, and at the top of it a name that Kestrel had not seen in print for eleven years and had never once stopped hearing.

*LOCKRIDGE.*

The match went out. The man stood in the dark a moment longer, folding the paper away with the care of a clerk filing a document that mattered, and then he turned and walked back toward the station, and Kestrel stood in the coal dust with the blood gone loud in their ears.

*Rule three: never let them see your face twice.* But that was the wrong rule for this. The man hadn't seen the face. The man was working from something else. A description, maybe. A photograph—God, had the Meridian ever had a photograph? Kestrel's service record at Corvane, burned in a stove the first week of flight, but records bred records, and the Meridian Railway Consolidated was an animal that never forgot a debt.

*The passage runs under, not over.* And the Meridian had bent its line around the cave like a horse around a snake. And now, the very week the tide pulled back far enough to show the door, a man in city boots was standing in a rail yard reading a notice with the name *Lockridge* on it.

Coincidence was a currency Kestrel had never been able to afford.

Kestrel stayed in the dark until the yard was silent, until the folded-face man had gone wherever men like him went, and then moved—fast now, no more drifter's amble—back through the sleeping streets to Rope Walk. The widow's house was dark. Kestrel climbed the stairs without lighting a lamp, packed the carpetbag in four minutes by touch, left the room key on the counter with the exact fare for one night and nothing to suggest the bed had been warmer than the pillow, and stood a moment at the harbor window with the bag in hand.

North, the cliff line was a black rampart against the lesser black of the sky. Somewhere under it, behind a curtain of kelp and a century of forgetting, a door was standing open that the sea had closed on Kestrel's father.

The tide would come back in the morning. The door would shut for another lifetime. And a man with a folded face and a printed name was somewhere in Saltmere, asking questions by matchlight.

Kestrel had a rule about mysteries. The rule had kept them alive for eleven years.

Kestrel pulled the cap low, went out the back stair, and turned north along the dark cliff road, walking toward the flats while the last of the tide still held.

Some rules, it turned out, were only as good as the reasons behind them. And the reason had just walked into a rail yard carrying Kestrel's name.
