## 2. What the Tide Keeps

The tide went out so far that morning it seemed the sea had changed its mind about Saltmere entirely.

Kestrel Lockridge was on the seawall at first light, as was their habit—habit being the only address they kept—and watched the water peel back from the town like a sheet drawn off furniture in a house long closed. The harbor boats settled onto mud that had never seen sun in living memory. Gulls walked where they should have swum. Old men came down to stare and mutter about the moon, and the fishwives argued over sandworms suddenly rich for the taking, and nobody—not one soul in Saltmere—looked north along the cliff line to where the water had uncovered something that had no business existing.

Kestrel looked.

That was the trouble with rules. They worked right up until the moment they didn't.

* * *

Kestrel had four rules, though they'd never written them down, because writing things down was how a person got found. Rule one: never sleep twice in the same town. Rule two: pay cash, use half-names, and be forgettable on purpose—dress dull, speak little, let people remember your hat instead of your face. Rule three: know every dead line on the map better than the men who owned the live ones, because dead lines were where a person could move without being seen. And rule four, the oldest one, the one that had kept them alive through nine years of freight cars and borrowed bunks: never go looking for your father.

Rule four existed because Kestrel had gone looking once, at nineteen, and what they'd found instead was a syndicate lawyer in a gray suit who explained, very patiently, that Elias Lockridge had been a trespasser, a saboteur of record, and a man whose family would be well advised to grieve quietly. There had been no body. There had been no grave. There had been a sealed file and a train ticket home and the particular silence of an organization that did not need to threaten you because you understood it already.

So Kestrel mapped abandoned lines instead. It was honest work, mostly—railroads paid small fees for surveys of grades they'd abandoned, wanting to know if anything was worth reclaiming, and Kestrel gave them accurate numbers and kept copies of everything, because a private atlas of forgotten track was worth more than money to a person who lived by rule three. Nine years of it. Forty-one towns slept in exactly once. The system worked.

And then the tide went out beneath the cliffs north of Saltmere, and rule four began to itch like a healing scar.

* * *

The legend was the first thing every child in Saltmere learned, usually from a grandmother who told it while shelling peas: there was a passage under the mountains, dug before the Consolidated ever laid its main line, and it opened on the sea, and it opened only when the sea agreed. The Wreck Passage, they called it—named for the *Meridian Star*, a survey ship that broke apart on the rocks in '61 carrying the only complete charts of the tunnel's inland course. The ship drowned. The charts drowned. The mouth of the passage had not been seen since, and the town had spent two generations deciding whether it had ever existed at all.

Kestrel had heard the story twice since arriving—the second time from a dockhand who mentioned it while Kestrel was paying too much attention to the rusted stub of rail emerging from the cliff base at low tide, a detail the dockhand clearly thought nothing of but which Kestrel catalogued immediately and completely. Rail meant gauge. Gauge meant era. That stub was older than anything the Consolidated had built on this coast.

Now the tide had pulled back past it, past everything, and below the old cliff line—exposed for the first time in years, maybe decades—there was a black arch of stone perhaps eight feet high, framed in barnacled rock, breathing cold air out at the world like something sleeping with its mouth open.

Kestrel stood on the seawall and performed the arithmetic of temptation.

Going down there alone, against instinct, against rule four, into a hole in the earth that the ocean itself had been keeping shut—that was how people died. Caves flooded. Tides turned. A person could be sealed inside a mountain by six inches of water and a schedule they hadn't checked. Every competent fiber Kestrel owned said *walk away, take the noon freight east, be someone else by supper.*

But the air coming out of that arch was moving. Kestrel could see it, faintly, in the early light—a shimmer above the cave mouth, the way air shimmers over a warm rail in winter. Moving air meant another opening. Moving air meant the passage went *through*, all the way through, just like the legend said.

And somewhere on the far side of that mountain was whatever remained of Elias Lockridge's last survey.

Kestrel checked the tide table pinned inside their coat—stolen fair from the harbormaster's office two days ago, because rule three applied to information most of all. Extreme low, caused by the same lunar alignment dragging the monsoon season early off the coast. The water would stay out for perhaps five hours. Five hours to walk into a legend and walk back out.

"Against my own instincts," Kestrel said aloud, to nobody, because saying it made it feel like a decision rather than a surrender.

Then they climbed down.

* * *

The descent was worse than the cave. The cliff face at low tide was a staircase of weed-slick stone and old landslide rubble, and Kestrel went down it slowly, testing each hold, coat pockets weighted with the tools that mattered: a carbide lamp, chalk, a folding rule, a coil of good hemp line, and the little brass compass their father had left behind in a boarding-house drawer eleven years ago—the only inheritance that couldn't be seized by lawyers. The compass needle swung lazy near the cliff base, sulking against some iron in the rock, and Kestrel noted that too. Iron meant rails. Rails meant the tunnel had been meant for trains, not smugglers.

At the cave mouth, the smell hit first: cold stone, salt, kelp rot, and underneath it all something mineral and old, the breath of deep rock that has never seen weather. Kestrel stood in the arch and let their eyes adjust, and the light behind them laid their shadow long across a floor of smooth wet stone—and then across something else.

Track.

Two rails, green with age, running straight back into the dark, set on rotted ties furred with white sea-mold. The gauge was narrow—forty-two inches, Kestrel judged by eye, confirming later with the folding rule—and the rail section was a pattern discontinued before the war. This was not a mine adit. This was not a smuggler's shortcut. Someone had graded this, ballasted this, driven steel into the dark with intent, and then the world had forgotten it so thoroughly that even the tide had become its gatekeeper.

Kestrel knelt and touched the railhead. Under the green scum, the steel was sound. Not pitted through, not flaked to rust-paper. Sound.

"Who were you," Kestrel murmured, "and why did they bury you?"

The question wasn't really about the railroad. Kestrel knew that. The question was about a man who had spent his whole career chasing ghost routes—the unprofitable grades, the bypassed alignments, the lines the big roads wanted erased from the record—and who had vanished doing it. Elias Lockridge had believed, with the fervor other men reserved for religion, that the railroads' official histories were lies of omission, that whole corridors had been deliberately forgotten, and that a map of what the companies chose to abandon was a map of what they feared. He used to say it at the dinner table with his sleeves rolled up, tapping the newspaper: *They don't tear up track by accident, Kessie. Track is memory. When they kill a line, they're killing a fact.*

Kestrel had been twelve. It had sounded like a game then.

It sounded less like a game now, standing inside a fact that had been killed and buried under the sea.

* * *

Five hours. Kestrel tied the hemp line to a barnacled boulder just outside the arch—rule of cavers and cowards alike, and Kestrel was content to be both—and followed the rails in.

The tunnel swallowed daylight within forty yards. The lamp threw a circle of white that slid along wet walls cut clean and dressed with masonry in places, real work, capital work, and Kestrel's engineer's mind ran ahead of their feet the way it always did: grade felt level, maybe a half percent rising; drainage channels cut along both walls, dry now; ceiling high enough for a locomotive, or at least a heavy motor car. This had been built to carry tonnage through the range. And then abandoned so completely that no chart in any office Kestrel had ever surveyed for showed so much as a dashed line here.

Fifty yards in, Kestrel stopped, because the lamp caught marks on the wall, and the marks caught something deeper than the eye.

Survey stations. Cut into the masonry at intervals: a chiseled cross, a number, initials. Standard practice, standard form—but Kestrel read the initials and the floor of their stomach dropped away like the tide going out.

*E.L.*

Elias Lockridge. In his hand—the quick, slanted, impatient hand that Kestrel knew from a single surviving letter, from the margins of a textbook, from eleven years of memory sharpened by absence. Station 14. Station 15. Station 16, each mark fresh-cut relative to the stone around it, the chisel strokes still holding their edges where the salt spray couldn't reach.

He had been here. Not passed through—*surveyed*. He had run a full instrument line through this tunnel, station by station, and the numbers beside each cross were in his notation, the idiosyncratic shorthand he'd invented himself and taught no one, the shorthand that appeared in none of the published manuals because he'd trusted it to no one.

Kestrel pressed their palm flat against Station 17 and stood very still in the dark, listening to their own heart conduct its loud, stupid business.

He came here after. After the disgrace, after the gray-suited lawyer, after the sealed file—he had come here, to the one corridor the syndicate had buried deepest, and he had measured it. Which meant he'd known it existed. Which meant the legends were true, and the *Meridian Star*'s drowned charts pointed here, and his disappearance—his *vanishing*, eleven years ago, from a town two hundred miles inland on the far side of this very range—

The arithmetic finished itself, ugly and bright.

He had gone into these mountains. He had never come out.

Kestrel's breath fogged in the lamplight. The cold coming from deeper in the tunnel was different now—not sea-cold but mountain-cold, the still ancient chill of rock that hasn't exchanged air with the sky in a century. The rails ran on into blackness, straight as a sermon. Somewhere ahead, faintly, the air moved against Kestrel's cheek. Through. All the way through.

Turn around. That was the play. Mark the location, memorize the stations, come back with proper gear and a partner and a plan, do it like a professional instead of a haunted person stumbling into a hole because the sea happened to blink. Five hours was enough to reach Station 20 and return with margin. Anything past that was greed, and greed underground was how they wrote your name on a plaque nobody visited.

Kestrel took ten more steps anyway. Then twenty. The lamp hissed. Water dripped somewhere with the patience of geology.

At Station 19, the wall changed. The neat masonry gave way to raw blasted rock—a collapse zone, cleared and timbered long ago, the timbers now soft as bread—and beyond it the tunnel bent slightly westward, following some seam the original engineers had chosen, and Kestrel understood with a lurch that they had crossed from the known into the genuinely unknown. No chart. No second exit confirmed. Only moving air and a dead man's chisel marks marching into the dark like a trail of breadcrumbs left by someone who never expected anyone to follow.

"I'm not following you," Kestrel said to the marks, to the dark, to the man who had taught them to read grades before they could read scripture. "I'm surveying. That's different."

It wasn't different. Both of them knew it wasn't different.

* * *

What broke the spell was the light.

Not Kestrel's—a second glow, far down the tunnel where the bend swallowed everything, small and yellow and wrong. Lamplight. Oil or candle, flickering, alive. Kestrel killed their own lamp instantly and stood blind in the absolute dark, hand on the cold rail, every nerve reclassified from archaeologist to prey.

Someone else was in the Wreck Passage.

The light bobbed nearer, unhurried, accompanied by a small irregular scuffing—a shuffle-and-drag, boots and something else, a limp or a load. Kestrel pressed into a drainage recess, pulse ticking in their teeth, running the possibilities: syndicate surveyor, treasure hunter, madman, ghost. None of the options were good. All of them meant the secret was already leaking, and rule two said be gone before witnesses—

The light rounded the bend, and out of the dark shuffled a boy.

Small—twelve, maybe thirteen, starved down to whippet geometry, wrapped in a grown man's coat cut off at the knees, hair a matted salt-stiffened mop. He carried a lantern in one hand and a tin bucket in the other, and he moved along the tunnel edge with the unconscious certainty of an animal on its own territory, stepping over the third tie, ducking the low timber, avoiding the puddle—all without looking, the way Kestrel avoided the loose board in a familiar boarding house stairwell. The boy had done this walk a thousand times.

He was ten feet away when he froze.

His eyes—huge, dark, catching the lantern glow—went from Kestrel's face to the dead lamp in their hand to the chalk dust on their coat, and the bucket hit the stone with a clang that rang the length of the tunnel like a struck bell, and the boy bolted, back the way he'd come, lantern swinging wild shadows up the walls, over the timbers, across the survey crosses—

Across *E.L.*, station seventeen, eighteen, nineteen—

"No—wait—" Kestrel stumbled out of the recess, lamp forgotten, voice cracking absurdly in the cold dark. "I'm not—I'm not with anyone—"

The boy's footsteps faded. The lantern-light shrank to a spark, winked around the bend, and was gone.

Kestrel stood alone on the dead rails in the middle of a mountain, heart slamming, mind tearing itself in two directions at once. *Follow him.* The boy knew the tunnel, knew what lay ahead, knew—or might know—whether the passage truly went through. But chasing a terrified child deeper into an uncharted cave with a turning tide behind them violated every rule Kestrel had, including several they'd have to invent on the spot.

And yet.

A boy living alone in a tunnel their father had surveyed. A boy who hadn't reported a stranger, hadn't screamed for help, hadn't done anything but run—run *inward*, deeper, toward whatever shelter he had. Children who ran inward had something inward worth protecting. And children didn't find tunnels like this by accident. Someone had shown him. Or someone had been here when he arrived.

Kestrel looked down at Station 19, at those quick slanted initials cut into stone by a hand eleven years silent, and felt rule four finally, cleanly, break—not snapped, but dissolved, like a rail joint ground smooth by a million passing wheels until there was no seam left to point to.

The tide would turn in under four hours. The sensible thing was to retreat, regroup, return.

Kestrel had never once in their life done the sensible thing where their father was concerned.

They relit the lamp, checked the flame, and started walking—following the small scuffed bootprints in the sea-mold, following the spark of a stranger's lantern, following the breadcrumb trail of a dead man's chisel, deeper into the dark that the sea had kept, and kept, and finally given up.
