## 3. Admission Charged

The tide gave Kestrel ninety minutes, and the cave charged admission in cold.

Water sheeted off the rocks at the entrance in long gray beards, and the air inside tasted of salt and old iron—the smell of a shipwreck that had never quite finished happening. Kestrel stood at the threshold with the lantern raised, letting the light do its slow work across the walls, and felt the particular silence of a place that had been waiting. Not empty. Patient.

The floor sloped upward from the sea, puddled in places where the last tide had withdrawn only grudgingly, leaving pools that reflected the lantern back up at Kestrel like small dark eyes. The cliff above was the old line—Kestrel had mapped it from the freight car window on the way into Saltmere, noting how the rock strata dipped inland at an angle too shallow to be natural for a coastline this exposed. Geology made by pressure. Or by men who'd wanted something through the mountains badly enough to lie about it afterward.

Twenty paces in, the daylight died behind them, and the cave stopped being a cave.

The walls changed character. The rough natural stone gave way to cut faces—drilled and blasted, squared off with a precision that no wave had ever managed. And there, chest-high on the left wall, painted in faded white that the damp had not entirely eaten: a survey mark. A cross with a circle around it, and beneath it a string of numbers.

Kestrel's breath went out of them slowly, the way air leaves a brake line when you bleed it deliberate and controlled, because if you let it go all at once the whole system shudders.

They knew those numbers. Not the specific figures—but the hand. The *style* of the hand. Every surveyor has a signature in their numerals the way every engineer has a signature in their throttle work, and Kestrel had spent their childhood copying marks like these out of their father's field books, practicing the way other children practiced letters. The 4s were open-topped. The 7s crossed low. The 3s had flat backs, drawn fast by a man who thought faster than he wrote.

*Elias Lockridge.*

Kestrel touched the paint. It flaked under their fingertip, fine as ash. The mark was old—fifteen years old, twenty—and yet the wall around it was dry enough that the paint should have run decades ago unless someone had cut a channel above it to shed water. Which someone had. A thin groove ran along the rock six inches over the cross, invisible until you looked for it, doing its quiet work of protection without anyone to thank it.

That was his handwriting in everything, even the things he built to survive him.

"Damn you," Kestrel said quietly, to a man who had been gone longer than most people stayed anywhere. "Damn you for making me look."

The passage bent inland, following the strata, and Kestrel followed it because there was no version of themselves that could have turned back—not after the numbers, not after the groove cut careful above the mark. Their rules of invisibility, the ones they'd recited like scripture since they were nineteen years old—never sleep twice in one town, never sign a ledger in full, never let your face become familiar—those rules had been built to keep the world from asking questions. But the world had already asked this one, fifteen years ago, in Elias Lockridge's own hand, and it had waited under a cliff ever since for someone with the right name to come collect the answer.

The track began forty yards in.

It started as a glint under the lantern—a rail head catching light where nothing should have been polished. Then two rails, then ties, running dead straight into the mountain on a grade so gentle Kestrel's feet barely registered the climb. Narrow gauge. Thirty-six inches between rail centers, the gauge of branch lines and mining spurs, the gauge of lines built cheap and meant to be forgotten.

Kestrel knelt and put a palm flat against the rail. Cold, but not wet-cold. The rust was scaly near the cave mouth where the salt air reached, but here, deeper in, it was only a skin—brown, thin, flaking at the touch. Rails didn't stay like this. Rails abandoned in damp rock went to lacework inside five years. These rails had been *kept*. Oiled, or greased, or simply traveled often enough that wheels kept polishing the heads.

Someone had run traffic on this line. Recently.

Fifty yards further, the handcar sat on the track like a beetle that had died mid-stride.

It was an old inspection car, the kind section crews pumped up and down short stretches before motorcars made muscle obsolete. Its wooden deck was silvered with age, its handles wrapped in strips of leather gone black and hard. Rust had claimed the frame in orange continents, but the mechanism—Kestrel circled it once, twice, checking—was sound. The linkage moved. The eccentric was oiled. Someone had worked this machine within the season, maybe within the month.

There was a canvas tarp folded on the deck, and under the tarp: a tin cup, a coil of good rope, three candles stubbed to different lengths, and a fishhook wound with line, laid out with the neatness of someone who owned exactly these things and no others.

Kestrel straightened, lantern high, and looked down the tunnel into the dark beyond the reach of the flame.

"You keep your tools like my father kept his," they said to whoever wasn't there. "Which means I'm about to meet either a ghost or a thief."

Neither answer came. But the tunnel breathed—a faint movement of air against Kestrel's cheek, cool and steady, flowing *out* of the mountain. Caves breathe with the weather, Odile would have told her, if Odile had been anything more than a name in a syndicate file at this point in Kestrel's life. Outflow meant another opening somewhere higher. The passage went through. The legend, absurd as legends were, might be arithmetic underneath.

Ninety minutes. Maybe seventy now. Kestrel checked the tide table memorized in their head, did the subtraction, and made the decision the way they made all decisions: quickly, and with the full knowledge that they would regret it at leisure later.

They walked on.

* * *

The tunnel climbed in long easy sweeps, curving left, right, left again, and Kestrel counted paces and marked each survey station as they passed—every one in that same open-4, flat-backed-3 hand, some accompanied by dates. The earliest date was nineteen years old. The latest was eleven months ago.

Eleven months.

Elias Lockridge had disappeared eleven years ago, swallowed somewhere between Saltmere and the interior on what the railway company had called, in its single curt notice, *an unauthorized survey of no commercial value.* Eleven years of Kestrel assuming the man was dead, because the alternative—that he'd chosen to vanish, chosen it over coming home—was a weight Kestrel had spent a decade setting down and picking back up.

But eleven months ago, someone had stood at station marker 47 and written today's date beside Elias's original chainage figure. Same style. Same fast, flat-backed 3s.

Either Elias Lockridge was alive, or someone had learned to write like him well enough to break Kestrel's heart with chalk.

Kestrel was standing at marker 47, lantern held close to the wall, trying to decide which possibility hurt more, when the pebble hit them.

A small stone, thrown sidearm, struck the tunnel wall a foot from their head and cracked off into the dark. Kestrel dropped to a crouch behind the handcar's ghost—no, the handcar was fifty yards back; they dropped behind a tie pile, lantern shuttered to a sliver, heart going like a drive rod.

"Who's down there?" a voice said. Young. Cracked at the edges, the way voices are when they've been trained by shouting into emptiness. "I heard you come in. I counted your steps. There's just one of you."

"There's just one of me," Kestrel said, keeping their voice level and low, pitched the way you pitch it to spooked livestock. "I'm not with anyone."

"Nobody's with anyone." A pause. "That's the first true thing everybody says."

Kestrel almost smiled at that. Almost. "Fair. What's your name?"

Silence. Long enough that Kestrel began counting their own heartbeats to measure it. Then, from up-slope, out of the black:

"What's yours?"

And there it was—the question Kestrel had spent half a lifetime making sure no one got to ask twice. In Saltmere, at the station, someone had looked at their face too long, and Kestrel had told themselves it was nothing, that faces blurred together in port towns, that their rules still held. Now a voice in the dark was asking for a name, and Kestrel found that the rulebook, for the first time in years, had no page for this.

"Lockridge," they heard themselves say. The name came out before the caution could catch it, the way water finds the crack you didn't know was in the boiler.

Another silence. Different quality this time. The silence of someone recalculating.

"The surveyor's kid," the voice said finally, wonder flattening the crack in it. "He talked about you. He talked about you *all the time.*"

The lantern trembled in Kestrel's grip—one degree of arc, no more, but they felt it travel their whole arm. "Talked." They made their voice into iron. "Where is he?"

"I don't know." Fast, urgent, afraid of being disbelieved. "I swear it. He went up-line last winter to check the summit portal and he didn't come back, and I waited, and I waited, and the food ran out so I started fishing the tide pools, and I *waited*—" The voice caught itself, hauled itself back to some rough discipline. "But he'll come back. He always comes back. You can leave the lantern covered and sit down and wait with me, if you want. There's room."

Footsteps now, descending. Kestrel raised the lantern sliver and watched a shape resolve out of the dark: small, barefoot, wrapped in a coat built for a much larger corpse, hair cut raggedly as if by its own knife. A boy. Twelve, maybe thirteen, with eyes that had done the arithmetic of strangers many times and found the sum usually unfavorable.

He stopped eight feet away, out of grabbing range, and studied Kestrel with a frankness adults had unlearned.

"You've got his chin," the boy decided. "Same chin. He drew it on the margin of the field book once, next to the tide tables. Said it was the stubborn part."

"Elias Lockridge is alive," Kestrel said. It wasn't a question anymore. It was a load rating—the maximum weight a structure could bear—and they were testing whether their own bridge would hold it.

"He was in October." The boy shrugged, and the shrug carried eleven months of practice at not crying. "Tunnels don't eat people. He taught me that. He said the mountain's honest—it only keeps what you give it."

"And what did you give it?"

"Nothing yet." The boy lifted his chin—Elias's chin, stubborn as advertised. "I'm saving up."

Kestrel looked at him for a long moment. Barefoot in November. Coat three sizes wrong. A tin cup and a fishhook and a handcar kept oiled against the day his missing man would need it. This was what happened when you vanished, Kestrel thought. You didn't disappear. You left a hole shaped like yourself, and something small fell into it and grew around the edges.

"What do they call you?" Kestrel asked.

"Pip."

"That's not a name. That's a seed."

"It's the name I picked." Pip's jaw set. "Names you pick hold better than names you're given."

Kestrel knew that doctrine intimately. Had lived by it, in fact, under three different names in four years. Somewhere in the last minute, without signing anything, without sleeping twice anywhere, Kestrel Lockridge had acquired a dependent—or been acquired by one; the direction of ownership was not yet clear—and every rule of invisibility they owned was already compromised, because you could not walk out of this tunnel and leave a child who spoke in your father's cadences, waiting on a man who wrote dates in chalk eleven months ago and never came home.

The tide, Kestrel calculated, had perhaps fifty minutes left before the sea came back to collect its cave. Fifty minutes to get out, or be sealed in with the boy and the ghosts until the next extreme low.

"We're leaving," Kestrel said. "Both of us. Now."

Pip didn't move. "He's coming back."

"He's been gone eleven months."

"He *waited* for me once. Three days in the rain at the summit portal when I sprained my ankle. He didn't leave." Pip's voice rose and cracked wide open. "You don't get to call him gone. You weren't even *here.*"

The words landed where words like that land—in the soft tissue under the ribs, where all the old guilt was stored. Kestrel crouched, bringing their eyes level with the boy's, and made their voice as steady as rail.

"No," they said. "I wasn't here. That's the whole of my life's work, not being here. But I'm here now, and the sea is coming back in fifty minutes, and if we're inside this rock when it does, neither of us waits for anybody ever again. So you can hate me while we walk. Hate me uphill. But we walk."

Pip stared at them, breathing hard, waging some private war whose terms Kestrel couldn't see. Then the boy snatched the tin cup and the rope coil from the handcar with the speed of someone who had evacuated his life before, and jammed his bare feet into a pair of oversized boots waiting by the track, and said, sullen as any soldier reporting under protest:

"There's other people. Further up. If we're going up-line anyway."

Kestrel went very still. "Other people."

"A surveyor lady who draws maps that make her cry. And an old fellow who sleeps a lot and talks about brakes." Pip shouldered past toward the tunnel mouth, then paused, glancing back with those arithmetic eyes. "The old one knew your name already. Before you said it. He heard 'Lockridge' in his sleep, I think, the way you hear weather coming."

Behind them, far down the passage, the first whisper of the returning tide sighed against the entrance stones—soft now, patient, but gathering.

Kestrel shut the lantern to a coin of light, took one last look at station marker 47 and its impossible fresh date, and started uphill into the mountain, a stranger's rope coiled over their shoulder and their father's handwriting leading the way.

"Show me," they said.
