## 4. Three Strangers and a Handcar

The boy moved through the dark like it was a hallway in his own house.

Kestrel followed, one hand trailing the rough wall, the other keeping the lantern angled so its light fell on the boy's shoulders and not their own face. Habit. You didn't show a stranger your face in the dark; you showed them your light and let them decide what it was worth. That was rule six, or maybe rule seven. Kestrel had stopped numbering the rules years ago because the numbering implied there was a finite set, and there wasn't. The rules grew like mold in the damp corners of a life spent never sleeping twice in the same town.

"Mind the third tie," Pip said over his shoulder. "She's rotten through. I put a board over her, but the board's gone soft too."

"Third tie," Kestrel repeated, and stepped where the boy stepped.

The track ran ahead of them into the cave's throat, twin rails catching the lantern light in two thin threads that converged somewhere beyond seeing. Kestrel had walked a hundred abandoned lines and this one was different in a way that made the skin of their forearms prickle: it was *maintained*. Not well, not lately, but someone had cleared the ballast of rockfall, someone had wedged shims under the low spots. Someone had decided this track was worth keeping, and in Kestrel's experience a kept track was a kept promise, and promises in dead tunnels were rarer than gold.

"How far?" Kestrel asked.

"'Bout a quarter mile. You'll hear the fire before you see it." Pip glanced back, and in the lantern light his face was all elbows and eyes, a face built out of hunger and cleverness. "You ain't going to yell at me, are you? For bringing you?"

"Why would I yell at you?"

"'Cause Odile'll yell at *me*, and it's nicer if somebody's yelling at you instead. It spreads it out."

Kestrel almost smiled. Almost. The mouth made the shape and then remembered itself and gave it up. "I don't yell much."

"That's what my ma said about my pa." Pip said it without bitterness, the way a boy reports weather. Then he ducked under a low shelf of rock and was gone, and Kestrel followed, and the cave opened.

It opened like a held breath released. The passage widened into a chamber the size of a freight depot, its ceiling lost in black above the lantern's reach, and at its center—Kestrel stopped walking. At its center, on a shelf of natural rock above a pool of perfectly still water, burned a small cook fire, and around the fire sat two people, and beside the fire, on a section of track that ran straight through the chamber and out the far wall into darkness, stood a handcar.

Not rusted through. Not a skeleton. A handcar with oiled bearings and a mended handle and a canvas cover lashed over its bed, sitting on living track in the middle of a mountain that had swallowed it forty years ago, and it looked like a thing waiting for a crew.

The woman saw Kestrel first. She came up off her rock with a surveyor's transit case in one hand and a hatchet in the other, and she held the hatchet the way a person holds it who has thought about the question. She was maybe forty, sharp-boned, her dark hair cut short and practical, her coat a railway surveyor's coat with the brass buttons picked off—Kestrel noted the little pale circles on the cloth where the buttons used to be, the way a coat is stripped of rank.

"That's far enough," she said. Her accent was southern coastal, old money gone thin. "Pip. Come here. *Now.*"

"He's all right, Odile," the boy said, but he came anyway, sidling to stand between the woman and the fire, equidistant between his two loyalties.

The second figure rose more slowly, and Kestrel's whole body went still before the mind caught up with why. It was the way the man rose—bracing one hand on his knee, pushing up in two stages, the left leg stiff. It was the way he stood once up: weight settled back on his heels, shoulders rounded forward, the posture of a man who had spent thirty years riding the rear platform of a moving train, reading the roadbed through his boots. Kestrel knew that posture the way they knew their own heartbeat. They had grown up watching it at the end of the platform, waiting for it to turn and wave.

The old man squinted into the lantern light. He was sixty if he was a day, gray-bearded, weathered down to leather and wire. His eyes found Kestrel's face and moved across it slowly, and Kestrel watched him do the arithmetic—jaw, brow, the set of the eyes—and watched the arithmetic come out wrong, or come out right, Kestrel couldn't tell which from the old man's expression, which went carefully, deliberately blank.

"Evening," the old man said. His voice was a brakeman's voice, pitched to carry over wind and wheel-squeal without ever seeming to shout. "You came in off the tide flat."

"I did."

"Alone?"

"Alone."

The woman, Odile, hadn't lowered the hatchet. "Name," she said.

Kestrel weighed it. Rule one, the oldest rule, the one under all the others: never give your name to anyone who can write it down. But the boy had already seen their face by lantern light, and the old man was still doing his arithmetic, and something in the chamber—the handcar, the kept track, the survey marks in the dark passage behind—had already decided the question Kestrel was still pretending to consider.

"Lockridge," Kestrel said.

The hatchet did not lower. The old man, though—the old man closed his eyes. Just for a second. Just long enough. When he opened them he said, quietly, "Elias Lockridge's kid."

It wasn't a question, and Kestrel felt it land somewhere under the ribs, in the soft place where they kept their father the way other people keep a wound: covered, unexamined, aching in wet weather.

"You knew him," Kestrel said.

"Rode his trains for eleven years." The old man lowered himself back onto his rock with the same two-stage care, left leg stiff, and looked into the fire. "Amos Brant. Brakeman. Last road I worked was the Meridian Coastal, and your father was the best engineer ever ran her, and I'd thank you not to ask me anything else tonight, because some things go down easier after a man's eaten."

*Meridian.* The name sat in Kestrel's stomach like a stone. The syndicate's letter, nailed to the cave mouth that morning—*REWARD for information concerning TRESPASSERS, Meridian Railway Consolidated*—and here was a man who had worked for the Meridian Coastal, and here was Kestrel, who had come looking for a dead man's tunnel and found it occupied.

"And you?" Kestrel said to the woman. "You're the one who's been maintaining the track."

Something shifted in her face—surprise, quickly buried, then reassessment. "You noticed."

"The ballast's been cleared. Shims under the low ties. Somebody's keeping this line alive, and it isn't the boy."

"Odile Vasseur," she said. She set the hatchet down—on the rock, in reach, not away—and held out her hand instead, and her grip was dry and strong and brief. "Surveyor. Formerly of the Meridian Consolidated, Third District. Currently of nowhere, which is a promotion, if you ask me."

"Formerly," Kestrel said. "What happened?"

Odile laughed, short and without any humor in it at all. "I found something. That's what happened. You want to be careful what you find, Lockridge. Out there, finding is the most dangerous thing a person can do."

* * *

They fed Kestrel, because that was the law of shelters everywhere and caves were no exception: fish from the tide pools, dried over the fire, and a tin of beans Pip produced from a cache with the pride of a magician. Kestrel ate sitting on the rail itself, the cold iron humming faintly under them whenever the sea breathed somewhere far behind, and the four of them arranged themselves around the fire in the loose geometry of people who had not yet decided to trust one another—close enough to share the warmth, far enough to reach a weapon.

Pip sat closest to Kestrel. Kestrel noticed and didn't comment.

"So," Odile said. She had a stick and was drawing in the ash at the edge of the firelight, absently, the way a surveyor's hands draw lines even when the surveyor isn't thinking—straight, true, measured. "The tide flat. You came in off the tide flat, alone, with a lantern and a pack and no rope worth the name. Which means you didn't come for the fish. Which means you came for the passage." She looked up. "Why?"

Kestrel turned the tin in their hands. Rule two: never answer a question with the truth when a smaller truth will serve. But the fire was warm and the boy was watching and the old man had said *Elias Lockridge's kid* like a prayer or a sentence, and the smaller truths all seemed to have drowned with the tide.

"My father disappeared," Kestrel said. "Thirty-one years ago. He was running a survey out of Saltmere for the Coastal line. The company said the party was lost in a rockslide up-country. They paid my mother a settlement and sealed the file, and my mother died believing it, and I grew up believing it, and then—" Kestrel stopped. Started again, quieter. "Then I found survey marks in the second gallery back. Chiseled. *E.L., 18—*, and a datum number. My father's hand. I'd know it anywhere. He taught me lettering before he taught me to read."

The fire popped. Somewhere deep in the mountain, water moved in the dark, patient as a clock.

"That's the third gallery," Odile said. "I know the marks. I've been copying them for two months." She turned the stick over, looked at the ash on it. "Elias Lockridge's survey party didn't get lost, Lockridge. They found the Wreck Passage. The old summit tunnel—the one the company built in the forties and abandoned when the grade proved too steep for the engines of the day. The one the company has spent forty years pretending doesn't exist, because if it exists, then the Coastal route over the range was never impossible, only *inconvenient*, and every freight contract the Consolidated has signed since is built on a lie about the mountains."

"You're sure."

"I'm a surveyor." Odile's voice went flat and hard. "I was the best in the Third District, and I was fired for saying so in a report. I pulled the old grade charts, ran the numbers, and wrote that a passable tunnel crossing existed under the range, and my supervisor told me to rewrite it, and I told him to go to hell, and three days later I was dismissed for falsifying data—their word for a woman who won't. And two weeks after that, a notice went up in every station house on the coast offering reward money for information about trespassers in the cliff caves, and I understood that the company hadn't fired me. The company had *aimed* me. They let me walk out the door and then watched where I walked."

Amos Brant had been silent so long Kestrel had half-forgotten him. Now he spoke, to the fire more than to any of them.

"They've been hunting this passage longer than any of you have been alive," he said. "I ran the rear platform on the Coastal for thirty years, and twice—twice—I saw company men come through with instruments, going up-country where no line went. Both times, the crews that took them up didn't come all the way back. Men got paid off in Saltmere and told to forget the names of the stations they'd seen." He rubbed his bad knee, absently, the gesture of a man touching an old scar. "I forgot to be curious. That was my whole career, girl—forgetting to be curious. Brakemen who got curious got reassigned to lines that didn't exist."

"And you're here now," Kestrel said. "On a line that doesn't exist. Curious after all."

"I'm here because I fell." The old man said it simply. "Fell off a moving car in the Saltmere yards last spring. Shattered the knee they'd already ruined. The company pension board looked at my record and found a discrepancy—some freight I'd signed for that went missing, thirty years back, on a train your father ran. They used it to cut my pension to nothing. I was going to die in a boarding house room over a saloon, and then I heard a boy in the market talking about a cave under the cliff where the rails still ran, and I thought, well. If I'm going to die, I'd as soon die somewhere interesting." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and steady. "I didn't know whose cave it was. I swear that on the roadbed. I didn't know it was *his*."

Kestrel held the old man's gaze and felt the strange vertigo of it—this was a man who had stood on the back of Kestrel's father's trains, who had perhaps waved from the rear platform while a child stood on the platform below, and thirty-one years of Kestrel's carefully unnumbered rules had never once prepared them for the possibility that the past would be *sitting by a fire*, eating beans, with a bad knee.

"Nobody's asking you to swear anything," Kestrel said at last. "Not yet."

"Fair enough," Amos said. "Fair enough."

Pip had been quiet through all of it, hugging his knees, and now he said, "Is somebody coming after her? The company? After Odile?"

"Eventually," Odile said. "They're slow, but they're thorough. They nailed a notice to the cave mouth, which means they know someone's using the caves. They don't know who, or how many. Yet." She looked around the fire, at each of them in turn, and Kestrel recognized the look: it was the look of a person doing arithmetic with lives instead of numbers. "So. Let's say it plain, since we're all pretending we haven't been thinking it. Four people, in a hole in a cliff, on a line the most powerful railway syndicate on the coast wants sealed and forgotten. The passage runs inland—there's track, there's a handcar, and I believe—" her stick tapped the ash drawing, a line running up and up into the dark of the fire's far side, "—I believe it runs all the way through the range, to the old summit tunnel, and down the far side. If it does, it's the only way over the mountains that the company doesn't own the ends of."

"And the snows come early up there," Amos said. "Six weeks. Maybe five."

"And the company comes faster than the snows," Odile said. "So the question isn't whether we're in danger. The question is whether we're four strangers in a cave, or a crew."

The fire burned. The pool at the chamber's edge lay perfectly still, holding a broken mirror of the flames, and somewhere far down the tunnel, the rails ticked and settled like a sleeping animal.

Kestrel looked at the handcar.

It sat on the track with its mended handle and its oiled bearings and its canvas cover, and it looked like a thing waiting for a crew, and Kestrel had spent thirty-one years being a crew of one, and rule three—rule three was *never ride with strangers*, because strangers had names, and names could be written down, and written-down names could be found.

But the boy had led them through the dark without asking anything. The surveyor had been fired for telling the truth. The old man had said *his* with a crack in it.

"The handcar," Kestrel said. "Where'd it come from?"

"Second gallery," Pip said. "Under a rockfall. Took me and Amos four days to dig her out. She rides true—I checked the wheels with a level."

"You checked the wheels."

"Somebody had to."

Kestrel looked at the boy a long moment, and then at the others, and heard themselves say the thing they had not said aloud in thirty-one years, the thing that broke rule three like a rail under a runaway car:

"Then somebody should check the track ahead of her. All the way to the summit, if it goes. Before the snow, and before the company." Kestrel set the empty tin down on the rail, where the iron rang faintly, like a bell struck once, far off. "I'll run the line. But we leave before the next low tide, and we don't come back this way."

Odile's stick stopped moving. Amos Brant looked up. And Pip grinned in the firelight, gap-toothed, lit up like a lantern.

It was Amos who spoke last, quietly, into the quiet after, the old brakeman's voice pitched to carry over wind and wheel-squeal:

"Then we'd best sleep fast. Because come morning, I've got something to tell you about the last time a Lockridge ran this line—and it's not a story with an ending."
