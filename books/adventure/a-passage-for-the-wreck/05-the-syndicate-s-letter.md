## 5. The Syndicate's Letter

Odile Vasseur had spent eleven years learning to read paper the way other people read faces. A surveyor's report, a land grant, a freight manifest—each one confessed if you knew where to press. So when Pip came scrambling down from the cave mouth at first light, breathless, saying there was a man's boot print in the wet sand and something nailed to the rock, she did not panic. She climbed.

The tide was coming back in below them, filling the channel that had exposed the cave two nights past. In another day the sea would close the front door again, and whatever came next would have to come through the mountain itself. That had been their comfort. Their assumption of safety.

The notice was nailed to a driftwood post driven into the shingle just outside the entrance—driven deep, by someone with a maul and no fear of being heard. The paper was good stock, heavy, wax-sealed against the damp. It had not been here yesterday. She was certain of it. She had stood in this exact spot at dusk and looked out at the water, and there had been nothing but gulls and the smell of kelp.

She read it twice before she called the others up.

*NOTICE TO ALL PERSONS.* The letters were printed, formal, the kind of thing posted on depot walls and courthouse doors across three territories. *The Meridian Railway Consolidated offers a reward of five hundred dollars for information leading to the identification or apprehension of persons trespassing upon lands and works under its charter within this district. Trespass includes entry to abandoned tunnels, disused grades, and sealed workings. Information may be given confidentially to any agent of the company.*

Below the text, a signature block: *H. Halloway, District Agent, Meridian Railway Consolidated.* And beneath that, smaller, almost an afterthought, the way a knife is an afterthought to a fist: *All prior grants, surveys, and rights-of-way pertaining to the Wreck Passage are void ab initio and remain the sole property of the Consolidated.*

"Wreck Passage," Amos Brant said behind her. He'd come up slow, favoring the hip that forty years of braking had ruined, and he stood reading over her shoulder with his cap in his hands like he was at a funeral. "That's what they're calling it now."

"You know the name?" Kestrel asked. They had come up last, moving the way they always moved—quiet, angled, one eye on the cliff path. Odile had watched them for two days now and still couldn't fix Kestrel Lockridge's age. The face was young. The eyes did arithmetic.

"Every line has a ghost name," Amos said. "The men who built 'em call 'em something, and then the company calls 'em something else, and then nobody calls 'em anything at all." He touched the post, the nail head, testing. "This went in this morning. Before dawn, I'd say. Tide was low enough to walk the shelf until six."

"Then whoever nailed it walked out the same way," Odile said. "Or didn't walk out at all." She turned and looked into the cave mouth, into the dark where the track ran inland, where a rusted handcar sat waiting on rails her father's generation had laid. "They know someone's inside. This isn't a warning for trespassers generally. It's addressed."

Pip had gone very still. The boy did that—folded himself small and quiet as a held breath whenever grown people started talking about danger, as though stillness were a place to hide. Odile had done the same thing at his age, in a different country, in a different kind of trouble. She put a hand on his shoulder without thinking about it, and he didn't shrug it off, which told her more than anything he'd said since she'd known him.

"Five hundred dollars," Kestrel said. Not afraid. Cataloguing. "That's enough to turn every dockhand, every stationmaster, every bored deputy between here and the pass. That's not a notice. That's a purchase order."

"It's worse than that." Odile pulled the paper free of the nail—it came away with a sound like a tooth leaving a jaw—and carried it down into the cave, into the lamplight, where she could read it properly. Where she could read it like a surveyor instead of a fugitive.

* * *

She spread it flat on a crate near the handcar and weighted the corners with stones. The others gathered. Outside, the tide boomed against the cliff in long, patient strokes, the sea taking back its road.

"Look at the seal," she said. "Meridian Railway Consolidated. You know when that entity exists? Three years. The Consolidated is three years old—it's a holding company, formed to buy up failed lines and strip them for scrap and salvage rights. Before that, these same routes belonged to the Coast & Summit, and before that, half a dozen short lines." She tapped the signature. "But look at the language. *Void ab initio.* Void from the beginning. That's not scrap-salvage talk. Salvage companies don't care about the beginning. They care about the rails they can pull up and sell."

"So what does it mean?" Pip asked.

"It means the Consolidated isn't claiming this tunnel because they own it. They're claiming it because they need it to have never existed." She let that sit. Amos had gone gray around the mouth; he was looking at the floor of the cave, at the old survey marks cut into the rock, the ones in the handwriting Odile now knew belonged to Elias Lockridge. "I've seen this before," she said quietly. "In the records office at Meridian headquarters. I was fired for it."

Kestrel's head came up. "You said you were fired for falsifying a report."

"I said that's what they said." Odie laughed, short and without humor. "There's a difference between what a company says and what a company does. I learned that too." She sat down on the crate edge, because her legs wanted sitting, and told it plainly, the way she'd promised herself she never would again, to anyone.

"I worked the records division. Land surveys, right-of-way disputes, the paperwork that decides who owns what under whose ground. Eleven years. I was good at it—I could find a missing easement through forty years of conflicting filings better than anyone in the building, and everyone knew it, and everyone resented it, and that was fine. That was the job." She turned the notice over, examined the watermark in the paper. Fine stock. Expensive. "Two years ago I was assigned to reconcile the acquisition files when the Consolidated absorbed the Coast & Summit. Routine work. Except one file kept refusing to close. A tunnel. Summit crossing, somewhere in this range, surveyed originally—"

"—by the Wreck Line," Amos said. His voice had dropped low, the way men speak in hospitals.

"The Wreck Line," she agreed. "A short-haul company that went bankrupt fifty years ago after a disaster—the records disagreed about what kind. Some said a collapse. Some said a flood. One ledger entry, in a margin, in pencil, said only: *the mountain took the passage back.* And here's the thing that wouldn't reconcile. The Wreck Line's assets were sold, its rails pulled, its stations demolished. Everything liquidated. Except the summit tunnel. No demolition order. No abandonment filing. No sale. Just—silence. Fifty years of silence, renewed every decade by someone with authority, buried in a schedule of exceptions so deep I only found it because I'm stubborn and because I don't sleep well."

"And?" Kestrel said. And Odile understood: *and my father. And me. Say the part about my father.*

"And the renewal signatures changed hands four times over the decades, but the handwriting underneath—the annotations, the marginal corrections—was consistent. One person, or one family of persons, maintaining the exception across fifty years. The last annotation was dated nineteen years ago." She met Kestrel's eyes. "It was initialled E.L."

The lamp flame leaned in some draft from the sea. Nobody moved.

"Elias Lockridge," Kestrel said. Flat. Careful. A man carrying something breakable across ice.

"I didn't know the name meant anything until three days ago," Odile said. "When your friend here—" a nod at Amos "—said it out loud in this cave. But I knew the initials. I copied that file, Kestrel. Against regulations, onto my person, and I took it home, and I started asking questions I should not have asked. Within a month I was summoned to a meeting with men from legal, and they put a document in front of me and told me to sign a statement saying I had falsified survey data for personal gain. That my findings were erroneous. That the exception schedule contained nothing unusual." Her jaw tightened. "I refused. First time in my life I refused anything. So they made the refusal unnecessary—they terminated me for cause, blacklisted me with every railway concern on the coast, and had me followed for six weeks afterward. I ran. I've been running for two years, working under other names, doing field surveys for farmers and mining outfits, anyone who doesn't ask where I trained." She gestured at the cave, the track, the dark going inland. "And then I heard the legend of the Wreck Passage. Everyone on this coast has heard it. Passage through the mountains, for a price. And I thought: legends are what companies leave behind when they've finished burying something. So I came here. To the one place the Consolidated's silence pointed."

"To find the tunnel," Kestrel said.

"To find out why it matters," Odile corrected. "There's a difference. A tunnel is worth money. A secret is worth everything. And the Consolidated has spent fifty years and a great deal of money making sure this one stays buried—which means the tunnel isn't the treasure. The tunnel is the evidence."

* * *

Amos lowered himself onto the handcar bench, slowly, like a man kneeling. For a while he said nothing, and the others let him, because there was a quality to his silence that demanded room.

"I rode for the Coast & Summit," he said finally. "Thirty-one years. Brakeman, then head brakeman, then riding the caboose on the summit runs. And I'll tell you what the old heads said, because they said it the way men say things they don't want written down. There was a tunnel through the range that the company pretended wasn't there. Not collapsed—*closed*. Sealed from the inside, both portals, and the sealing crew sworn to silence, and the whole thing struck from the timetables. The story we got was structural failure. The story the old heads got, from men who'd talked to the sealing crew over whiskey, was different." He rubbed his jaw. "They said the tunnel was finished. Sound. Ready to open. And the night before the opening run, something happened inside that made the owners seal it rather than celebrate it. Nobody knew what. But the Wreck Line folded within the year, and the Coast & Summit bought the corridor cheap, and the first thing they did was re-bury every record of it. And every company since has kept the burial going. Like tending a grave."

"Or hiding a body," Pip said. He said it without drama, the way children say true things, and it landed harder for it.

"That's the arithmetic," Odile said. She stood and began pacing, because her mind worked better in motion, always had—she thought with her feet the way other people thought with their hands. "Consider what it costs to do what they've done. Fifty years of filing fees. Renewals. Bribes, probably—clerks don't maintain secret schedules out of loyalty. Then, three years ago, someone forms a new company specifically to hold this asset. Then, this season, agents start working the coast towns. Then a notice offering five hundred dollars, posted at the one cave the legend points to, within days—days—of the tide exposing it." She stopped. Turned. "They weren't watching the mountain. They were watching the sea. Because the sea is the only thing that ever opens the door."

"They knew about the cave," Kestrel said. "They've always known. They just couldn't reach it except at extreme low water, and they couldn't search it blind in the dark in a few hours of tide window. So they waited. And watched. And the moment someone found it—" Kestrel's jaw worked. "The moment *my father* found it, nineteen years ago. He came here. He surveyed it. He annotated the file. And then he disappeared, and the annotations stopped, and the syndicate let the trail go cold because the cold trail suited them fine."

"And now the trail's warm again," Amos said. "Because of us."

"Yes," Odile said. "Because of us." She picked up the notice again and held it to the light, and this time she read it not as a threat but as a document, because documents were her weapon and she intended to use it. "Here's what else this tells us. *Information leading to identification or apprehension.* Identification first. They don't know yet who's in here. If they knew, there'd be names on this notice—ours, or descriptions. Which means Halloway's people saw signs of occupation and posted this as a net. Someone local will take the bait. Someone always does. Five hundred dollars buys a fisherman's silence about having seen smoke, or a dockhand's memory of strangers buying rope. We have days. Maybe less."

"Then we move," Kestrel said. "We take the handcar and we go inland tonight."

"On what route?" Odile challenged, and immediately regretted the sharpness of it, because Kestrel flinched—not visibly, but she'd spent two years reading people professionally and she caught it anyway. "I'm sorry. I mean—we need the route. The survey marks. Your father's marks. That's the map, and the map is the only reason any of us survives what comes next."

"I know," Kestrel said. "I've been reading the marks since the second night. Give me until tomorrow. The route's in there. I can feel it. I just have to—" Kestrel stopped, and Odile watched them choose honesty over armor, watched it cost them. "I have to read it in his handwriting. And I haven't seen his handwriting since I was twelve years old."

Nobody answered that. There was nothing to answer it with.

* * *

Odile took the notice outside at midday, when the light was honest, and studied it once more while Pip gathered driftwood and Amos dozed against the crate, his breathing the ragged rhythm of a man whose body had been billing him for decades.

The paper was good. The printing was professional. But the posting—that was the tell. Anyone could print a lie. Posting it here, at risk, at dawn, meant the lie was urgent. And urgency, in Odile's experience, was the one confession a corporation could never suppress.

They were afraid. The Meridian Railway Consolidated, with its lawyers and its agents and its fifty years of buried paper, was afraid of a hole in a mountain and the ghosts walking through it.

She folded the notice into her coat, over her heart, where she used to carry stolen documents in another life. Let them hunt her for it twice.

Behind her, down in the dark of the passage, she could hear Kestrel's boots on the old ties, walking the dead line inland, reading Elias Lockridge's marks by lamplight—measuring, counting, reconstructing a dead man's intentions one chain-length at a time. Tomorrow, Kestrel had said. Tomorrow there would be a route, and a timetable, and a decision.

Odile looked north along the coast, toward the foothills just visible in the haze, already wearing the first gray suggestion of weather. The season was turning early. She could taste iron in the wind.

Six weeks, she thought. If the legends were right and the passes closed with the snows, they had six weeks to cross a range the most powerful railway concern on the continent had spent half a century pretending did not exist.

She went back down into the mountain to help pack.
