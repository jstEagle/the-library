## 6. Timetable for Ghosts

Kestrel did not sleep that night. Sleep was a habit of people who owned beds, and beds were a habit of people who owned addresses, and Kestrel Lockridge owned neither—had not for eleven years, since the morning they'd walked out of the yard at Corven Junction with a grip bag, a transit case, and their father's last letter folded against their ribs like a splint on a broken bone.

Instead they sat cross-legged in the lamplight of the cave's inner chamber, with Odile Vasseur's drafting tools spread on a flattened crate, and let the dead speak in the only language they'd ever trusted: numbers.

The survey marks were everywhere once you knew to look. Chiseled into the limestone walls in a hand Kestrel would have known blindfolded, by touch alone, the way you know your own heartbeat—*E. LOCKRIDGE, MERIDIAN SURVEY DIVISION, 1889*—and beside each mark, a station number, an elevation, a bearing. Elias Lockridge had been thorough. Elias Lockridge had always been thorough. It was what had gotten him promoted, and it was what had gotten him disappeared.

"You've been staring at that wall for two hours," Amos Brant said from his corner, where he sat wrapped in a wool blanket gone gray as ash. The old brakeman's voice came out of the dark like gravel poured slow. "Your father used to do the same thing. Stand there till the wall told him something."

"I'm not my father."

"No," Amos agreed, and there was something in the way he said it—not agreement at all, but patience, the kind a man learns watching freight cars couple at speed. "You're faster."

Kestrel didn't answer. They copied the marks onto Odile's paper instead, station by station, and began the arithmetic that was the closest thing Kestrel had ever had to prayer.

* * *

The numbers assembled themselves into a route the way rail cars assemble into a train—one behind another, each coupling a small violence, until suddenly the whole thing is moving and you can't imagine it ever having been apart.

Here was the shape of it:

From the cave mouth, a spur climbed the coastal bluff—the track under the rusted handcar, gauge intact, ballast washed but walkable. Four miles to a junction marked on no map Kestrel had ever seen, where the spur met the old Meridian trunk grade abandoned in '91 when the company went bankrupt building it. From there the grade ran inland through the foothills, rising on a steady two-percent climb—Kestrel could read the cut slopes in the survey notes, could feel the gradient in their teeth the way other men felt weather in their knees—up the Vell River valley, over a trestle the notes called Bridge 47, through a series of switchbacks cut into the shoulder of Mount Cerrin, and then—

And then the summit tunnel. Two thousand nine hundred feet of it, bored straight through the spine of the range, never finished, never officially opened, its existence stricken from every timetable and every record after the Consolidated bought the Meridian's corpse at auction. The notes ended at the portal. But the grades continued on the far side in Odile's stolen records—the ones she'd been fired for finding—and they descended the eastern slope toward a river town called Ferrow's Landing, forty-one miles beyond the mountains as the crow flew, and crows, Kestrel thought, were welcome to it. Trains didn't fly. Neither would they.

"Say it out loud," Pip said. The boy had crept close sometime in the night and now sat hugging his knees, chin on them, eyes enormous in the lamplight. He'd been doing that all evening—drifting nearer, then away, like a moth testing whether the flame was safe. "Say how long it takes."

"It doesn't take anything yet." Kestrel kept their eyes on the paper. "It's just ink."

"Say it anyway."

Kestrel looked up. The boy had soot on his face and a chipped front tooth and the particular hunger of someone who had learned that wanting things out loud was how you got them taken away—and wanted this one out loud anyway. Something in Kestrel's chest shifted, some plate grinding against another plate, and they heard themselves say:

"Six weeks. Maybe five if the weather holds. Walking the grade, clearing rockfalls as we find them, pushing or hauling the handcar where the rails will bear it. We'd need food for the whole crossing because there's nothing on that line but ghosts. We'd need rope, lamp oil, tools. And we'd need to be through the summit tunnel before the first snow seals it—or before the monsoon floods the low passes, whichever comes first, and I've seen monsoons come early off that coast."

"Five weeks," Pip repeated, tasting it.

"Six," Kestrel said. "Always plan for six."

Odile Vasseur stood at the chamber's edge with her arms crossed, and she had been silent so long that when she finally spoke, it landed like a spike driven home. "The Consolidated sealed the western portal in '94. I found the work order. A crew out of Saltmere, paid cash, no paperwork. They collapsed the first hundred feet with black powder and posted men to watch it for a season."

"Then we don't go in the west portal."

"There is no other portal."

"There is," Kestrel said, "if my father's numbers are true." They turned the page toward her. "Look at station 340. Ventilation adit, driven from the south face of Cerrin, three hundred feet below the summit ridge. Every long tunnel has one—you know that better than I do. You can't bore two thousand feet of mountain without somewhere for the smoke and the bad air to go. The Consolidated sealed the door everyone knew about. Nobody bothers sealing a hole halfway up a cliff face that isn't on any drawing they kept."

Odile took the page. Her eyes moved across the figures, and Kestrel watched the disgraced surveyor do what surveyors do—check, recheck, distrust, verify. Her lips moved with the arithmetic. The lamplight caught the scar through her left eyebrow, some old wound she'd never explained and Kestrel hadn't asked about.

"The adit might be open," she admitted at last. "Might be a chimney full of rubble. Might be a bear den. There's no way to know without climbing to it."

"So we climb to it."

"And if it's closed?"

"Then we dig. Or we turn around." Kestrel looked down at the pages covered in their father's hand, and the truth rose in them like water in a flooded shaft, unstoppable and cold. "But I didn't spend eleven years learning every dead line in this country to stop at the last one."

* * *

Dawn came into the cave the way tide comes into a harbor—gray, incremental, inevitable. Light reached the inner chamber in a thin blade across the floor, and by it Kestrel saw the others clearly for the first time as something other than complications.

Amos Brant, sixty if he was a day, hands like cured leather, sitting up now despite the stiffness in his hips, watching Kestrel with an expression the engineer couldn't parse and didn't want to try. The old man knew things. He'd said the surname like he'd been carrying it twenty years, waiting to set it down somewhere. That conversation was coming. Kestrel could feel it standing behind them like weather.

Pip, asleep finally, curled against the cold iron of the handcar like it was a large and ugly mother. The boy had ridden rails alone through two winters. Kestrel knew exactly what that cost, because Kestrel had paid the same fare, just older and with more practice pretending it hadn't.

Odile, awake, always awake, sharpening a pencil with a knife in small precise strokes, her records stacked beside her in oilcloth bundles tied with the neatness of a woman whose life had come apart once already and who intended to keep what remained of it in order.

Three strangers. Three reasons to vanish. And one passage through a mountain that the most powerful railway syndicate on the coast had spent years trying to erase.

Kestrel stood and walked to the cave mouth.

Outside, the sea lay flat and pewter-colored under a sky the color of unpolished steel. Far out, past the headland, clouds were stacking themselves along the horizon in tall white towers—the coast's oldest warning, the mountains' distant landlord collecting rent. Monsoon weather, or its herald. Six weeks. Maybe less.

Kestrel had rules. Eleven years of them, worn smooth as rail steel from daily use. Never sleep twice in the same town. Never travel with anyone whose name you know. Never want anything enough that losing it could stop you. The rules had kept Kestrel alive through bankruptcies and manhunts and one bad winter in the northern yards that still showed up in dreams. The rules were simple, and simple things survive.

But here was the flaw in the design, the defect in the rail, the thing Kestrel had known since the moment they'd seen their father's name chiseled in this stone: the rules were built to keep a person moving, and moving was not the same as going anywhere. Eleven years of mapping dead lines. Eleven years of cataloging everything the railway world had abandoned—including, though Kestrel had never put it in those words, Kestrel Lockridge.

And here, in a hole in a cliff, were four people the world had also written off, and a route through the range drawn in a dead-or-disappeared man's hand, and a clock running in snow and rain.

Kestrel turned back to the chamber. Amos was watching.

"Well?" the old brakeman said.

"We leave in three days." Kestrel heard their own voice and was surprised by how certain it sounded, how much it sounded like someone else—a father's cadence surfacing in a child's throat after all these years. "Three days to provision. Odile, you'll draft the route clean—I want two copies, and we split them between us so no single loss kills the knowledge. Amos, you know rolling stock and you know these hills from the brake van side; you'll tell me what the grade will do to us before it does it. Pip—" the boy's eyes snapped open at his name, wary as a struck animal— "you know this coastline better than any of us. Where does a person buy rice and lamp oil in Saltmere without being remembered?"

Pip blinked. Then, slowly, the grin arrived, the chipped tooth and all. "There's a widow on Tanner's Lane sells stores out her back door. She's deaf as a post and half-blind and she hates the railroad company somethin' awful."

"Then we're in business."

"And me?" Odile said quietly. "What am I to you, engineer? You've assigned us all like crew positions."

Kestrel met her eyes. The honest answer was *I don't know*, and the truer answer was *I don't want to know, because knowing makes you responsible*, and both of those answers belonged to the man who'd walked out of Corven Junction eleven years ago.

"You're the surveyor," Kestrel said. "I'm the engineer. Between us we're going to take a train line that doesn't exist across a mountain that isn't supposed to be passable, and we're going to do it before the snow comes down. That's what you are to me."

Odile studied them for a long moment. Then she nodded once, sharp as a stamp on a document, and picked up her pencil.

* * *

They worked through the day, and the cave filled with the strange industry of a departure. Odile drafted at the crate, her pen whispering. Amos inventoried their combined possessions with a brakeman's economy—rope, forty feet, bad in places; a block and tackle; two lanterns and half a gallon of oil; a pry bar; a hatchet; Odile's instrument kit, which she guarded like a child; tinned food for perhaps ten days among four mouths, which meant hunger for six weeks unless they resupplied, and there was nowhere on a dead line to resupply. The arithmetic was bad. Kestrel wrote it down anyway. Bad arithmetic honestly faced beat good arithmetic imagined.

In the afternoon Kestrel walked the spur track with Pip, testing rails, and the boy talked the whole way—about the widow on Tanner's Lane, about the tide pools, about a seal he'd named Mr. Bristles who owed him a fish. Kestrel mostly listened. Listening was cheaper than answering, and answering risked the kind of attachment the rules forbade.

But near the cave mouth, Pip stopped and pointed at the handcar. "We're really taking her? All the way?"

"As far as she'll roll."

"My da said handcars are the honestest machines ever built. What you put in is what you get out." The boy paused, and his voice dropped, careful. "Was your da an engineer too? On the trains?"

Kestrel looked at the rusted machine, at the boy's face, at the whole trap of the question. Every instinct said deflect. Deflect, diminish, move along, stay a stranger.

"He was," Kestrel said. "He was the best there was. And one day he drove a train into these mountains and didn't drive it out again."

Pip absorbed this with the gravity of the young. "So we're finishing his run."

Something turned over in Kestrel's chest, heavy and warm, like a firebox being lit after years cold. "I suppose we are."

* * *

On the second evening, Odile finished the copies. She laid them side by side on the crate—two identical sheets, ink still sharp, the route rendered in her precise hand: elevations, distances, water sources, hazard notes, the ventilation adit circled twice. She slid one across to Kestrel.

"Your father's figures checked against mine," she said. "Where they disagree, I've noted it. They disagree in only one place." Her finger stopped at a point high on the western slope, above Bridge 47. "His notes call for a water tower and wood camp at mile thirty-one. My records show the Consolidated dismantled it in '92. If anyone's living up there, they're squatters. If anyone's watching that stretch of line, that's where they'd watch from."

"Noted." Kestrel folded the map along Odile's creases and slid it inside their coat, against their ribs, where the last letter had lived for eleven years. It fit there as if the space had been waiting.

That night—for the first time in longer than Kestrel could calculate—they slept. Not well, and not long, but truly, deeply, in the same place as other breathing people, and woke without shame at dawn.

Which was why Kestrel was awake, and outside, and looking north along the cliff path, when the rider appeared.

One horseman on the coast road, moving fast, too fast for a farmer, dressed in city black that even at a mile looked wrong against the salt-and-driftwood palette of Saltmere. He did not slow at the town's edge. He rode straight through it, and Kestrel watched him stop at the station house, dismount, and nail something to the board beside the platform—watched the paper flap white in the morning wind like a signal flag.

Behind Kestrel, footsteps on the stone. Odile, then Amos, following the engineer's stare.

"What is it?" Pip asked, scrambling up.

Kestrel's mouth had gone dry. Because the rider, remounting now, turning his horse back toward the town, had lifted his head as he passed the headland—and for one instant, across a mile of brightening air, Kestrel would have sworn the man looked directly at the cave mouth. At them.

"Pack everything," Kestrel said quietly. "We leave tonight."
