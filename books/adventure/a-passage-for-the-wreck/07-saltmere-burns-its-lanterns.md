## 7. Saltmere Burns Its Lanterns

Pip had learned to count the ways a town said *someone's coming*.

In Saltmere, it was the dogs first. They went quiet all at once, like someone had drawn a curtain over the harbor. Then the gulls lifted off the fish sheds in one gray sheet and did not come back down. Then—this was the strangest part—the lanterns. Saltmere burned its lanterns every night, strings of them along the quay and up the cliff stairs, little orange eyes against the dark so the boats could find their way home. Tonight they went out one by one, street by street, as if the town were closing its own eyelids.

Pip sat up on the salt crate where he'd been pretending to sleep and watched the darkness eat the harbor.

He'd been living in the cave for eleven months by then, and he knew every sound it made: the drip at the back wall that kept time better than any clock, the groan of the old handcar when the tide breathed on its rails, Amos snoring like a landslide with opinions. He knew which footsteps were seals on the rocks above and which were Odile coming back from her night walks with her notebook full of numbers nobody wanted anymore. So when the footsteps came down the tunnel at an hour when nobody should be walking, Pip was already awake, already flat behind the rusted handcar, before Kestrel's voice found him in the dark.

"Pip." Just his name, low. Kestrel never wasted words after sundown. "Lanterns are going out."

"I saw. From the mouth."

"Get the others. Quiet as you can."

Quiet as you can turned out to mean shaking Amos's shoulder until the old man surfaced from sleep with a curse half-formed and then, seeing Kestrel's face, swallowing it whole. It meant Odile already dressed, already rolling her charts into a oilcloth sleeve, because Odile slept the way cats do—one ear open for the world's bad news. It meant gathering everything they owned in the world into four bundles and two hands' worth of tools, and it took less than four minutes, and Pip thought later that this was the saddest arithmetic he knew: how fast a home could become luggage.

"They're here," Kestrel said, when all three stood in the black of the tunnel. "Two men came off the evening packet. I watched them from the dunes. They went to the harbormaster first, then the tavern. Asking about trespassers. Asking about a girl."

"A girl," Odile repeated flatly.

"Brown hair, sixteen or so, answers to—" Kestrel paused.

"Nobody," Pip supplied. "She answers to nobody."

Amos made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't gotten so heavy on him lately. "Then we'd best make sure she keeps answering to it. Where's the spur?"

"Behind the cliff line. My father's marks show it running inland from a loading ledge half a mile north. Coastal spur, abandoned nine years when the quarry shut." Kestrel's voice had that quality Pip was still learning to read—not fear exactly, more like a blade being drawn slowly. "Track should be intact under the sand drifts. The handcar will run."

"The handcar," Amos said, "is older than sin."

"The handcar," Kestrel said, "is the only thing in this cave that goes anywhere."

* * *

They moved through the last hour of dark like ghosts who hadn't yet been told they were dead.

Kestrel went first, of course. Pip had noticed that Kestrel always went first—not because of bravery, though there was that too, but because Kestrel trusted no path they hadn't tested with their own feet. The tide was out, and the wet sand below the cliff held their footprints only briefly; the sea was already coming back to erase them, patient as a debt collector. Pip walked where Kestrel walked, stepped where Kestrel stepped, and felt something he couldn't name yet—a feeling like being a word in somebody else's sentence, but a word that belonged there.

Odile carried the survey rolls. Amos carried the tools and, despite everyone's objections, most of the food. Pip carried nothing but himself and the small tin box he kept hidden inside his coat, the one thing he'd brought to the cave and never shown anyone. He put his hand over it now, through the fabric, the way you'd touch a scar to remind yourself it was yours.

The loading ledge appeared out of the dark like a shipwreck standing up. Rusted bollards, a crane arm bent at the neck like a hanged man, and beneath the drift sand—the rails. Two lines of them, running north along the cliff base and then curving inland through a cut in the rock, swallowed by night.

Kestrel knelt and swept sand off the railhead with the side of a hand, then touched the steel the way other people touched relics. "Cold," Kestrel murmured. "Good. No trains through here in years, but the steel remembers weight. It'll hold us."

"You talk to track," Amos said.

"Track listens better than people."

They wrestled the handcar up from the tunnel mouth—it took all four of them, grunting, the wheels shrieking once before Odile stuffed rag into the bearings—and set it on the rails facing inland. Pip took the near handle. Kestrel looked at him a moment in the starlight, something unreadable passing across that closed face, and then took the far handle without a word. Which was, Pip understood, permission.

"Amos, you ride and listen. Odile, watch our back trail. We pump steady, not fast. Fast makes noise, noise makes light, light makes us dead." Kestrel swung up onto the platform. "Questions?"

"Yeah," Pip said. "Where does this line go?"

Kestrel almost smiled. Almost. On Kestrel's face it registered as a slight loosening around the eyes, which anyone else would have missed entirely. "Nine miles to the junction at Cinder Flats. After that, we'll see."

"We'll see," Odile echoed softly. "The motto of the doomed."

"No," Kestrel said. "The motto of the living is *keep moving*. *We'll see* is just what hope sounds like when it's trying not to wake anybody."

* * *

Pumping a handcar through the dark was the closest thing to flying Pip had ever done.

That was the truth of it, though he'd never have said it aloud while Amos was wincing at every jolt. The handles rose and fell, rose and fell, and the little car gathered speed along the dead rails, and the wind came up off the sea and hit his face with salt and cold, and the stars wheeled overhead, and the cliff rushed past on his left like the wall of the world. His arms burned. His lungs burned. He didn't care. He was *moving*, really moving, not running-from moving but running-toward moving, and for the first time in his life the motion had other people in it.

When he'd run away from the mill town—when he'd jumped the freight at Harrow Junction with nothing but the tin box and a lie about his age—he'd thought being alone was the same as being free. Eleven months in the cave had taught him otherwise. Alone wasn't free. Alone was just alone, a boy talking to dripping walls, counting tides, waiting for a life to start without having the nerve to go find it. But this—four fugitives on a stolen-from-nobody handcar, fleeing men with badges and ledgers, racing the sunrise up an abandoned coastal spur—this was freedom with witnesses. This was a story he'd get to keep.

"Ease off," Kestrel called across the handles. "Curve ahead. Feel it in the rail."

Pip felt it—a faint shiver coming up through the iron before the curve even showed. He marveled at that. The track was talking to them. He pumped lighter, matching Kestrel's rhythm, and the car took the bend smooth as water finding its level.

"How'd you know?" he panted.

"Know what?"

"That the curve was there. Before we saw it."

Kestrel was quiet for a few pumps. Then: "My father taught me. Every rail has a memory. Weight leaves a signature in the steel. You learn to read it, you're never surprised." A pause. "He also taught me never to ride a line I haven't walked. I'm breaking that rule tonight. Remember that, if this ends badly—I broke my own rules for you people."

"Best rules are the ones worth breaking," Amos called from the rear platform, and Pip heard the grin in the old man's voice, and something in his chest went warm and dangerous.

* * *

Dawn caught them at Cinder Flats, and dawn was the wrong color.

Pip noticed it before anyone said anything—the east horizon gone the yellow of old teeth, the sea haze thickening instead of burning off. Odile stood on the junction platform, a rotted timber affair with one sign still legible—CINDER FLATS, 4 MILES TO QUARRY BRANCH—and stared south with her head cocked like a bird hearing weather.

"That's not fog," she said.

"It's monsoon haze," Kestrel said. "Off the coast. Weeks early."

"Monsoon." Amos spat over the rail, an old habit from drier country. "Rain season. Means flooded passes."

"Means every low route out of these mountains closes early," Kestrel agreed. "Which means the syndicate won't be the only thing chasing us. The weather will be hunting too."

Pip looked from face to face and saw the same calculation happening in each one, fast and silent, like telegraph clicks. He was learning to read that too. These people spoke in glances the way he used to speak in silence.

"So we go high," he said.

Three heads turned. He hadn't meant to say it out loud. Heat crawled up his neck.

"The summit tunnel," he went on, stubborn now that he'd started. "The one your father surveyed. The one in the marks. If the low passes flood, then the way through is the way *up*. Isn't it? Isn't that why we're all here?"

Silence. The wind moved the haze. Somewhere down the dead branch line, a rail ticked as the sun warmed it.

"He's right," Odile said at last, quietly, as if admitting to a crime. "It's the only crossing that doesn't depend on the weather holding. Only on us beating the snowline."

"And the snowline is six weeks out," Amos said. "If we're lucky."

"If we're lucky," Kestrel repeated, and Pip heard the weight settle onto those words like freight onto a bridge—tested, measured, accepted. Kestrel looked at him then, really looked, and whatever passed across that guarded face this time, Pip decided privately to call it respect. "You've been reading the marks."

"Some of them. The ones in the open tunnel. I copied them all in my book." He hesitated. "I figured somebody ought to know them. In case."

"In case of what?"

"In case the person who was supposed to know them didn't come back."

The words hung there. Pip hadn't meant them cruel, but he saw them land somewhere deep in Kestrel anyway, saw the flinch that got mastered before it finished arriving. For a moment neither of them breathed. Then Kestrel nodded, once, slow, like a switch being thrown.

"All right," Kestrel said. "Cinder Flats to the quarry branch. Quarry branch climbs to the old maintenance camp at Mile Twelve. We rest there today, move at dusk. From here on, we're climbing."

* * *

They were a mile up the quarry branch when Pip heard the sound that stopped his heart.

A whistle. Far behind, down toward the coast—long, short, long. Not a train whistle. Trains didn't run out here anymore. It was a signal whistle, the kind railway men used, the kind Amos still carried on a lanyard around his neck, and it came again, and this time a second whistle answered it from further north, up the coast, ahead of them.

Amos went gray. "Flankers," he said. "They've split. One party behind, one working up the coast road. They're not searching for us anymore, boy. They're herding."

"Can they hear us?" Pip whispered, though the question was foolish—they could hear themselves, the squeak of the handcar carrying in the morning stillness like a scream.

"Not yet. But sound runs downhill to the sea, and we're pumping uphill of them." Kestrel's eyes were fixed on the grade ahead, where the branch curved into a cutting choked with broom and gorse. "There's a siding past that cutting. We push the car in, cover it with brush, and walk. From here to the camp, we're pedestrians."

"Walking," Pip said. "All the way up?"

"All the way up." Kestrel swung down and laid a hand briefly on the handcar's rail—an odd gesture, almost affectionate, and Pip filed it away with all the other small contradictions that made up the person who owned no address. "She's earned her rest. So have we. But not for long."

They hid the car in the siding as the whistles sounded again, closer now, and began to climb on foot into the hills, four figures in the bracken with the monsoon haze rising off the sea behind them like a held breath. Pip climbed last, and at the top of the cutting he turned once and looked back.

Far below, tiny with distance, lights were moving along the coastal road. Lanterns. Burning in daylight.

Saltmere had put its lanterns out. The syndicate had lit theirs. And somewhere between the two, Pip understood, was the life he'd stumbled into—dark behind, bright hunters, and nothing ahead but a mountain, a dead man's marks, and the strange, terrifying, wonderful knowledge that for the first time in his life, when he ran, he would not be running alone.

He turned and climbed.
