## 8. The Bridge That Remembers

Amos Brant had crossed the Vell River forty-one times in his working life, and he remembered every one of them the way a man remembers the faces of women he'd loved and lost. He knew the trestle before he saw it — knew it by the sound of the water changing under the grade, by the way the rails began to sing a different note as they approached the gap. A bridge was never silent. Even a dead one. Even one that hadn't carried a train in twenty years still hummed its old song if you knew how to listen.

He listened now, standing at the edge of the cut with his bad knee aching from three days on the handcar, and what the bridge sang back to him made him close his eyes.

"That's not good," Odile said beside him. She had her surveyor's squint on, the one that measured things without instruments.

"No," Amos agreed. "It is not."

The trestle came down out of the fog like the skeleton of some animal that had lain down in the river gorge and died there. Sixty feet of creosoted timber on stone piers, spanning the Vell where it ran fast and green-white over its boulders, swollen already with the first rains pushing up from the coast. The track still sat on top of it — that was the miracle and the trap of it. Rails intact, ties mostly whole, the line running straight and true across the span as if inviting them onward. But beneath the deck, Amos could see what the years had done. Two of the stringers on the far side had rotted through at the cap beam, sagging like broken ribs. The third pier from the north end had shifted, knocked off plumb by some flood or freeze years ago, and the whole structure above it leaned a degree or two to the east, held up now more by habit than by engineering.

"Can we take the handcar across?" Pip asked. The boy was crouched at the rail's edge, peering down into the gorge with the fearless appetite of the young. "Looks solid enough on top."

"Looks," Amos said, "are what bridges use to kill brakemen."

Kestrel had come around the other side of the cut, walking the near abutment with their head tilted, reading the structure the way Amos read it but younger, faster, with better eyes. They crouched, touched the rail, laid two fingers against it like taking a pulse.

"Stringers are gone on the north third," Kestrel said. "Deck'll hold a man walking careful. It won't hold four hundred pounds of handcar rolling across at speed."

"Then we walk it," Odile said. "Carry the gear across in loads."

"And then what?" Amos asked her. He didn't mean it unkindly, but the question needed asking. "You walk across, you're on the far side with no car, no way back, and every mile ahead of you is uphill. That handcar is the difference between making the summit tunnel before snow and crawling there on your hands." He looked down the line, south and west, where the rails curved away toward the low country. "Or we detour. Drop south to the Meridian mainline junction at Corben's Mill, cross on the iron bridge there, come back up the valley road."

"Corben's Mill is syndicate territory," Kestrel said flatly. "There's a section house there. There are men there. We've seen the notices."

"I know what's at Corben's Mill," Amos said. "I helped build the yard there in '09."

"Then you know why we can't go through it."

He did know. And so the choice stood in front of them the way the river stood below: repair the crossing, or walk into the arms of the men who wanted them found. There was no third option. There rarely was, in Amos's experience. Life offered you bad timber or bad company, and you picked your rot.

* * *

They spent an hour arguing about it, which was an hour Amos thought they could ill afford, and then Kestrel did what Kestrel always did when the argument wore itself out: went quiet, walked the ground alone, and came back with a decision that sounded like it had been theirs all along.

"We fix it," they said. "The north third. Three stringers need sistering — we scab new timber alongside the old, bolt it through, re-shim the pier cap where it's settled. Odile, you tell me what load the repaired span will carry. Amos—" and here Kestrel turned to him, and something in their face made Amos's chest tighten, because it wasn't trust exactly, but it was the next thing down from trust, the thing trust grows from if you water it, "—you've spliced trestles before?"

"Spliced, shored, burned down, and rebuilt," Amos said. "I was a bridging man before I was a brakeman. Before my knees quit."

"Then you're our engineer for the day."

The timber was the problem. You couldn't sister rotten stringers without sound wood, and there was nothing sound within miles except — Amos pointed it out with some satisfaction — the trestle itself. The old line had been built with a maintenance spur and a stacking yard just south of the crossing, and though the stacks were long since collapsed into blackened heaps, the bottom courses of the pile, buried and shaded, had kept. They pried free a dozen beams of dense, tar-black old-growth fir, hard as iron, and dragged them down to the gorge edge, and the work began.

It was brutal work, and Amos loved it, and hated how much he loved it.

His body had been betraying him for a decade — the knee first, then the shoulder, then the slow dimming of his hearing in the left ear, all the wages of thirty years riding the rods and hauling brake wheels in weather that would kill a mule. But his hands still knew this work. His hands knew how to read a bolt hole by feel, how to seat a drift pin with two strikes instead of five, how to tell by the sound of a hammer blow whether the wood behind it was solid or hollow as a lie. He worked the far span while Kestrel and Odile rigged the lifting lines, and Pip — the boy was tireless, a stray dog of a child who had decided these four strangers were his pack and would hear no argument about it — ferried tools and bolts and water up and down the bank until Amos told him to sit down before he fell down.

By late afternoon the first stringer was sistered and bolted, and the second was half-lifted, and the light was going amber and long across the water, and Amos allowed himself — foolishly, he would think later; he would think it many times — to believe they might finish before dark.

The trouble was the cap beam on the third pier. Where the pier had shifted, the cap had cracked along a check that ran its whole length, and the bearing surface under the new stringer ends was uneven. It needed shimming — hardwood wedges driven in to take the load true — and the only way to reach it was to go out onto the pier itself, past the broken railing, and work hanging over the drop.

"I'll rig a boatswain's chair," Odile started, but Amos waved her off.

"Chair takes an hour to rig and an hour to position. I can do it in ten minutes with a belt line and a prayer." He was already knotting the rope around his waist, testing it against a stanchion. "I've hung off worse than this over faster water. This river's barely moving."

The Vell, green-white and roaring over its stones, disagreed with him silently.

Kestrel caught his arm as he stepped up onto the deck rail. "Take the safety line too. Clip it short."

"You worried about me, Lockridge?"

"I'm worried about the schedule."

But their hand stayed on his arm a moment longer than the schedule required, and Amos grinned at them, gap-toothed and old, and went out over the water.

* * *

Hanging above the Vell with a wedge mallet in his fist, Amos Brant felt twenty-five years old again, and that should have been his first warning. Age comes back to collect the moment you forget it's owed.

The work itself went clean. He drove the wedges home one after another, feeling each one bite, feeling the load transfer through the new timber into the old pier, the whole structure settling with a groan and then a silence that meant *right*. Below him the river threw up its cold breath. Above him the sky went from amber to violet. Behind him, on the deck, he could hear Pip chattering at Kestrel about something — the boy talked constantly, a stream of questions and observations that would have driven a lesser man to violence but that Amos found he'd grown fond of, the way you grow fond of rain on a roof.

The last wedge was seated wrong. He felt it — a high note in the hammer blow, a sponginess under the strike — and instead of backing off and resetting it, the way any sensible sixty-year-old man would have done, he leaned further out over the drop to get a better angle, and he drove it again, harder.

The crack in the cap beam, the old check that ran its length, chose that moment to finish its business. It let go with a report like a rifle shot, a chunk of hardwood the size of a coffin lid shearing away under his feet, and the world tilted.

He had one instant — one clean, bright, terrible instant — to understand what was happening. The belt line snapped taut against the stanchion. The stanchion, rotten at the base the way everything on this bridge was rotten, did not hold. And Amos Brant, who had ridden the tops of boxcars through hurricanes, who had walked moving trains in ice storms with a lantern in his teeth, who had survived forty-one crossings of the Vell River and a thousand crossings besides, went off the third pier of the Wreck Passage trestle and fell.

The water took him like a fist.

Cold — cold beyond anything, the kind of cold that stops being temperature and becomes opinion, the river's opinion of him, immediate and contemptuous. It rolled him under, slammed him against a boulder with a force that whited out his vision, spat him up long enough for half a breath, and took him again. He heard — or dreamed he heard — shouting, thin and far away, the voices of people who were not yet done needing him. His left leg had folded wrong somewhere between the pier and the water; he could feel it flapping at the knee like a loose coupling. His hands, those faithful hands, found a root growing from the bank — willow, deep-rooted, greedy for water — and closed on it, and held.

The current pulled. The root held. Somewhere in between, the old brakeman hung on, coughing river water and blood, and refused with the whole stubborn remainder of his strength to be taken.

* * *

They got him out by making a chain of themselves — Kestrel flat on the bank with the rope around their chest, Odile gripping Kestrel's ankles, Pip hauling on the line hand over hand, all of them screaming encouragement or curses, Amos couldn't tell which and didn't care. When his boots finally scraped mud and his body came up over the lip of the bank like a landed fish, he lay on his back in the wet grass and laughed, a horrible wheezing laugh full of river, because he was alive, and because his leg hurt so much that laughing was the only response that made sense.

"Broken?" Kestrel demanded, kneeling over him, hands moving down the limb with surprising gentleness.

"Fibula, maybe. Knee's wrenched. Nothing that hasn't happened before." He tried to sit up and the river's opinion of him asserted itself again, the whole gorge spinning. "Ask me tomorrow."

"It's not funny," Pip said. The boy's face was white and streaked with tears he didn't seem aware of. "You went under and you didn't come up and I thought—" He stopped, jaw working.

Amos reached up and gripped the boy's collar, pulled him close, forehead nearly touching forehead. "Listen to me, Pip. I've been falling off things for fifty years. I'm very good at it. The trick is landing near a root." He released him and lay back, looking up at the first stars. "How's the bridge?"

A pause. Then Odile, dry as August: "The bridge is fine, Amos. The bridge is *better* than fine. Your wedges took the load perfectly. Whatever you hit on the way down, the structure didn't miss it."

"Then it was worth it," he said, and meant it, and slept.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of the old maintenance shed, and Amos lay propped against his bedroll with his leg splinted from scrap lumber and his ribs wrapped tight, listening to the others argue in low voices by the fire.

He heard most of it through the pain-haze. Odile saying the pace would halve with him limping, that they had perhaps five weeks of safe weather and no margin left. Kestrel saying nothing for a long time, and then saying, quietly, "Nobody gets left behind. Not on my line." And Pip saying, fierce and certain, "We carry him. I can carry him. I'm stronger than I look."

Amos stared up at the canvas of the shed roof, at the old shadows of rafters long since fallen, and felt something move in his chest that had nothing to do with cracked ribs.

He had spent thirty years belonging to a railroad, and the railroad had belonged to a company, and the company had used him up the way it used up everything — timber, men, rivers — and discarded him the same way, with a pension letter and a watch fob and no goodbye. He had told himself he was done belonging to anything. That was the luxury of the old: you got to stop hoping.

And now here was a disgraced surveyor calculating his weight in gear ratios, a runaway girl-child swearing she could carry him, and a stranger with his father's eyes declaring that nobody got left behind on a line that didn't even exist anymore.

"The bridge remembers," Amos murmured to himself, too low for the fire to hear. Old brakeman's saying: a bridge remembers every load it ever carried, and gives back what it chooses. Tonight it had chosen to give him back. He intended to be worth the choice.

Beyond the firelight, upriver, the Vell ran higher than it had that morning. He could hear it in the dark, a new note in its old song, deeper and more urgent. Rain coming down from the coast, days early. Water finding its way.

In the morning, he thought, they would put him on the handcar and push him inland, and he would ride the dead line toward the summit like freight, like cargo, like a debt being hauled to settlement. And somewhere ahead of them, he was suddenly sure of it, the way he'd been sure of bridges all his life — someone else was on that line too. The rails had been singing to him all day, and underneath the wind and the water, faint as a ghost's footsteps, another song: steel singing in the distance, carrying a weight that wasn't theirs.

The mountain pass was not empty. And whatever was coming down it toward them, they would meet it with one man lame, one map, and four days' less time than they'd planned.

Amos closed his eyes and let the river sing him to sleep.
