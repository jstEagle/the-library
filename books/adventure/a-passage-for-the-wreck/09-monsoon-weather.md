## 9. Monsoon Weather

The receiver had come off a syndicate supply wagon three days out of Saltmere, and Odile Vasseur still felt the theft in her palms like a stain that wouldn't wash. She'd told herself it was salvage—abandoned property, technically, the wagon unattended while its driver drank himself stupid at a crossroads tavern—but she knew better. She had spent eleven years as a surveyor for the Meridian Railway Consolidated, and she knew exactly what belonged to whom, and she had taken this anyway, because the alternative was walking blind through country she didn't know, and Odile Vasseur would rather steal than be ignorant.

She had the set up on an overturned crate in the lee of a cut-stone culvert, its brass fittings polished with her thumb until they gleamed like small promises. Amos Brant sat nearby on a folded tarpaulin, his bad hip propped on his good one, whittling a stick into nothing. Pip was supposed to be asleep under the handcar's canvas, which meant Pip was listening to every word with eyes shut and ears wide open. Kestrel was out on the grade somewhere, walking the rails ahead with a lantern hooded to a coin-slit of light, reading the line the way other people read scripture.

Odile turned the dial with two fingers, slow as a surgeon.

Static first—the oceanic hiss of the coastal band, all that water talking to itself two hundred miles behind them. Then a fragment of dance music from some hotel orchestra in Port Calloway, thin and tinny, a ghost of a world where people still danced. Then, cutting across it, the clipped voice of the Meridian weather service, broadcasting on the open channel the way they always did before dawn, when they thought no one but station agents was listening.

"—repeat, advisory for all coastal districts. A depression forming two hundred miles southeast of Cape Verren has intensified overnight. Barometric readings falling faster than seasonal norms. Expect it to make landfall as a full monsoon system within seventy-two hours, possibly less. All low-lying passes, river crossings, and cuttings are advised to expect—" a crackle, a swallow of static "—flooding well above historical levels. This is not a normal season. I say again, this is not a normal—"

Odile turned it down. Not off. Down. As if the voice were a fire she could bank.

"Well," said Amos, not looking up from his stick. "That's a fine how-do-you-do."

"It's early." Her voice came out flat, professional, the voice she'd used in drafting rooms for a decade. "Monsoons don't reach the coast proper until mid-season. This one's ahead by weeks. The forecaster sounded frightened."

"Forecasters don't frighten easy."

"This one did."

Amos set down the stick. He was sixty if he was a day, hands like tree roots, a face weathered down to its essential geometry. He had ridden brakes on her father's—no. Not her father. On *Kestrel's* father's trains, back when the Lockridge name had meant something besides absence. He looked at her now with the particular patience of a man who had watched weather kill people before and expected her to do the arithmetic properly.

"So talk me through it," he said. "You're the map woman."

Odile pulled the satchel over and drew out the route sheet she'd been building since Saltmere—a working document, pencil on butcher paper, amended nightly. It was not her father's map; that treasure lived folded against Kestrel's ribs, shown to no one, guarded like a heartbeat. What Odile had was the skeleton around it: every pass, every crossing, every drainage between here and the summit tunnel, plotted from memory and from the fragments of survey record she'd memorized before Meridian burned her career for knowing them.

She spread it on the crate beside the receiver and weighted the corners with stones.

"Here's what we have," she said. "Three ways over the range before we reach the dead line and the summit tunnel. Three, assuming the tunnel itself holds, which is its own prayer." She touched the westernmost line. "Vell Gap. River crossing, then a climb through a granite notch. Doable in dry weather. In flood, the Vell comes up twenty feet in an hour—I've seen the high-water marks myself, old survey pegs with scum lines on them like bathtub rings. If the monsoon hits the headwaters, Vell Gap is gone."

"Flooded or washed out?"

"With a bridge, flooded. Without one, the whole approach grade goes with it. Either way, closed."

Amos grunted. She moved her finger east.

"Cutter's Pass. High route, gravel bed, no major crossings—but it funnels. One road in, one road out, and the drainage cuts across it in six places. Six creeks, all of them flash-prone. In a normal monsoon you might thread it between storms. In an early violent one?" She shook her head. "You'd be gambling your life on the timing of six separate walls of water. And Meridian knows Cutter's Pass. It's on their freight maps. If they're hunting us, that's where they'll wait."

"That leaves the middle route."

"The middle route is why we're carrying everything on our backs instead of riding the handcar." She tapped a dotted line that climbed through country so broken the surveyors who'd mapped it had left notes in the margins—*unreliable grade*, *slip zone*, *do not attempt after rain*. "Hollow Fork. No bridges worth the name, no funnel, no freight value, which means Meridian barely remembers it exists. But it's shale. Shale and rain are enemies. Every hour of storm increases the odds of a slide closing it by—" she caught herself doing the thing she hated, the precise number offered as comfort, and made herself say the honest version instead "—by a lot. By enough."

"So the arithmetic," Amos said slowly, "is that the weather doesn't just chase us. It closes doors behind us."

"Worse than that." Odile heard her own voice go quiet, and hated that too. "It closes doors *ahead* of us. Look." She took the pencil and marked the coast, then drew arrows inland, fanning out along the river valleys. "A monsoon isn't a wall of water moving toward you. It's a sponge wrung out over the whole watershed at once. The rain falls on the mountains the same night it falls on the coast. Everything drains downhill simultaneously. Which means the low passes drown first, then the mid-level routes get cut by slides as the slopes saturate—and the saturation keeps working downward for days after the sun comes out. We don't have to outrun the storm. We have to outrun the *consequences* of the storm, and those move slower and last longer."

Pip's eyes were open. Odile pretended not to notice.

"How long," Pip said, from under the canvas, in the too-casual voice of a child performing sleepiness, "how long do we actually have? For Hollow Fork. Before it goes."

Odile did the calculation she'd been doing since the first word of the broadcast, the one that sat in her chest like swallowed lead. Rainfall rates. Soil absorption curves she'd once drawn for a Meridian report nobody read. The elevation of the Fork, the dip angle of the shale beds, the seepage history of the springs along it.

"Four days of grace after landfall," she said. "Five if we're lucky. Landfall inside seventy-two hours. So call it seven days, total, from tonight, to get through Hollow Fork and onto the dead line gradient before the mountain starts shrugging its shoulders."

"And the dead line itself?"

"The dead line climbs. Climbing is good. But it crosses three spring-fed cuttings on the way up, and those will run like rivers by the time we reach them." She met Amos's eyes. "We'll be wading. In meltwater and storm runoff, uphill, in the dark, with an injured man."

"I've been waded before," Amos said. "Didn't care for it."

* * *

Kestrel came back an hour before dawn, smelling of wet iron and rail grease, and Odile watched the news land on that guarded face the way she'd watch a load settle onto a trestle—looking for the point where the structure started to sing.

They broke camp in the grey half-light and pushed on, because there was nothing else to do. The handcar clattered along the abandoned coastal spur, its wheels singing their one note over joints rusted open, and the sky to the southeast had begun to do something Odile had only seen twice in her life and never wanted to see again: it had turned the color of a bruise going deep, green-black at the horizon, stacked with cloud that didn't drift so much as *build*, layer on layer, like something being assembled with intent.

By noon the air had weight. Not heat exactly—pressure. The kind of pressure that made old joints ache and horses refuse fences and men go quiet without knowing why. Amos rode the handcar's rear seat with his eyes closed and his bad leg stretched out, and Odile knew he wasn't resting. She'd seen brakemen listen to track before. He was feeling the rail for the tremor of another train, some ghost of traffic, some sign that the world beyond their little stolen machine was still running on schedule.

At midafternoon they stopped at a water tower long dead, its tank holed with rust, and Odile climbed the ladder to check the receiver one more time. The coastal band was chaos now—station after station breaking in, Port Calloway ordering small craft ashore, a fishing fleet radio operator with a cracked voice reporting seas already running wrong for the season, and underneath it all, faint as a pulse, the Meridian advisory, updated:

"—landfall estimate revised. Twelve hours. Possibly ten. All interior districts should anticipate heavy precipitation beginning within thirty-six hours. Repeat: the system has accelerated—"

She stood on the tower platform with the wind beginning to worry her collar and looked south, and there it was, far off but unmistakable: a smudge on the sea's edge that was not cloud and not land, a darkness with rain hanging from it in visible gray curtains, miles of them, like the skirts of something enormous walking toward shore.

Ten hours. She had given them seven days. The mountain had just taken two of them away while she stood watching.

She climbed down and told them plainly, because she had learned long ago that people could carry almost any truth if you handed it to them whole instead of letting them find pieces of it in your face.

Kestrel listened without expression, which Odile was learning to read as the loudest expression there was. Then Kestrel walked to the edge of the clearing, stood looking at the southern sky for a long moment, and came back changed—not calmer, but *focused*, the way a lens focuses, scattered light pulled to a burning point.

"Then we stop being careful," Kestrel said. "From here to Hollow Fork we travel straight through. No scouting detours, no wide berms around bad trestles—we assess fast and commit. And when the rain catches us—" a pause, the smallest hitch, there and mastered "—when it catches us, we keep moving anyway. Wet and miserable beats buried."

"And nights?" Pip asked. "We can't see in the dark."

"We'll have to learn."

* * *

The rain reached them at dusk the next day, a full day ahead of even the revised forecast, and it did not arrive so much as *land*—a single colossal drop hitting the canvas over the handcar like a thrown stone, then another, then a sound like the whole sky unzipping.

They were in the foothills by then, the coast already a memory behind the first ridges, and Odile stood in the doorway of an abandoned section house—walls of vertical board, roof mostly honest, a stovepipe hole breathing cold air—and watched the world drown.

It came down in ropes. In sheets. The ditch beside the track filled while she counted to a hundred. The trackbed itself, that faithful ballasted spine they'd been following for days, began to shine and then to run, water finding the low side of every tie, braiding, gathering, going somewhere with purpose. Somewhere upslope a hollow note began, steady as a bell: water entering the culvert under the fill, and the culvert answering.

Amos got a fire going in the old stove with wood that fought him, and the four of them crowded into its orange circle, steaming, silent. Outside, the dark was absolute except when lightning opened it—white flashes showing hills running with silver veins, showing the rain itself as a kind of falling geography, ranges and valleys of water.

Odile got out the butcher paper and, by stove light, began amending the route sheet. Crossed out Vell Gap—no, she'd crossed it out yesterday, in ink of the mind. She crossed out Cutter's Pass formally now, drawing a clean line through it, and the act felt like closing a coffin. That left Hollow Fork and the dead line and the summit tunnel, one thread left of the three she'd woven, and every hour of this rain was fraying it.

She became aware that the others were watching her work. That Pip had crept close enough to read upside down. That Kestrel was looking at her with an intensity that had weight and direction, and that Amos was looking at Kestrel looking at her, and that something in the room had gone taut as a guy-wire in wind.

"You've done this math before," Kestrel said quietly. "Flood timing. Saturation curves. Slide probabilities. That's not surveyor general knowledge, Odile. That's specialist knowledge. Somebody taught you hydrology the way they taught me gradients."

The fire popped. Rain hammered the roof like a demand.

"There's a lot somebody taught me," Odile said. "And a lot I taught myself that I shouldn't have. And a report I wrote, eleven years ago, about a certain tunnel under this range, that said things Meridian very much did not want said." She kept her eyes on the paper, on the clean coffin-line through Cutter's Pass. "I was fired for it. Formally for falsifying data. Actually for telling the truth. And there are parts of that story—" her pencil stopped "—parts that touch your father's route, Kestrel. Parts that touch other things too. Things Amos knows, and I know, and you don't."

The silence stretched. Water gurgled in the culvert outside, patient as digestion.

"Tomorrow," Kestrel said at last, in a voice carefully emptied of everything, "we walk. Tonight—" and here the voice did something almost human, almost soft, before the guard came back up "—tonight it seems we're sheltering together regardless."

Odile nodded and bent over the map, and beneath the drumming of the rain she felt the truth rising in her chest like floodwater, finding every crack, refusing to be held back much longer.
