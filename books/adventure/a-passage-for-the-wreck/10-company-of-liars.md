## 10. Company of Liars

The rain came up the valley like something with intent.

Kestrel heard it before they saw it—a hiss in the pines above the dead line, a sound like a thousand small animals turning over at once—and then it arrived, not falling so much as arriving sideways, driven hard off the coast by a wind that smelled of salt and rot and distance. They had been making good time on the old spur grade, the handcar rattling along rails that still held their gauge if not their dignity, when Odile pointed east at a wall of gray where there should have been sky.

"Shelter," she said, and that was all anyone needed to hear.

The culvert was half a mile on—an arched stone throat bored through a shoulder of granite where the old engineers had refused to go around. It was dry inside, mostly, though a thin sheet of water slid down the far wall and pooled in a dark mirror that reflected their lantern like a second, drowned company. Kestrel backed the handcar under the arch, threw the brake lever, and stood listening to the storm hammer the hillside above them as if the mountain itself were being shaken awake.

They had been traveling four days since Saltmere. Four days of pumping the handcar through cuttings choked with alder, past signal posts stripped of their arms, past stations reduced to foundations and one leaning water tower that Amos had saluted without any apparent awareness he was doing it. Four days of Pip talking constantly, Odile mapping constantly, Amos humming hymns with the wrong words, and Kestrel saying almost nothing, because saying nothing was the oldest skill they owned.

But the rain had closed the world down to the size of this stone room, and stone rooms had a way of asking questions.

* * *

Pip built a fire at the mouth of the culvert—small, shielded behind a wall of split ties, smokeless enough if you didn't look too hard. The boy had a talent for fires the way some people had a talent for lying: instinctively, gratefully, as though fire had been waiting for him his whole life. He crouched over it now, feeding it pine knots, his face lit orange from below so that he looked like a figure carved on a church roof.

Amos sat against the handcar with his bad shoulder propped on a rolled blanket. The fall at the Vell trestle had left him stiff and yellowed along one side, and he moved now the way old machinery moves—with deliberation, each motion costing what it cost. He'd refused to talk about the trestle beyond "bracing failed, I read it wrong," which Kestrel knew was a lie, because Amos Brant did not read timber wrong. Forty years of brakeman's hands did not misjudge a chord. But there are lies you let stand because the truth would only make the injured man smaller, and Kestrel let that one stand.

Odile spread her charts on a flat tie-end and weighted the corners with stones. She worked even now, in lamplight, redrawing sections of the route from memory, checking them against the survey marks Kestrel had copied from the cave walls. Her pencil moved in short, certain strokes. Watching her work was like watching someone pray in a language only they understood.

"So," Pip said, to the fire more than to anyone. "Are we going to do it or not?"

"Do what?" Kestrel asked, though they suspected.

"The true-telling." Pip looked up. "Amos says every crew that runs together longer than a week either tells the truth or starts keeping two sets of books. And we've got six weeks of mountain ahead of us and no week behind us yet."

"That's not how books work," Odile said mildly, not looking up from her chart.

"It's how people work," Amos said. His voice came out low and gravelly, worn smooth at the edges. "Boy's right. I've seen crews come apart over less than what we're carrying." He turned his head, slow, until his eyes found Kestrel across the firelight. "Seen it come apart over exactly what we're carrying."

The rain filled the silence for a while. It drummed on the stone arch overhead and ran in bright threads down the culvert mouth, and somewhere above them a limb cracked under the weight of water and went down the slope with a long tearing sound.

"All right," Kestrel said.

They hadn't decided to say it. The word simply arrived, the way words do when a person has been holding a door shut for years and the hinges finally give. Kestrel sat down on the rail bed, back against the cold ballast wall, and pulled their knees up, and looked at the three faces around the fire—the boy hungry for story, the surveyor pretending she wasn't listening, the old man watching with an expression Kestrel could not yet name.

"You first," Kestrel said to Odile. "You've been carrying paper since Saltmere. I've watched you check your coat pocket eleven times a day."

Odile's pencil stopped.

For a moment Kestrel thought she would refuse—that she would fold herself back into that cool, precise distance she wore like a drafting apron. But then she set the pencil down parallel to the chart's edge, squared it, and looked into the fire.

"My last assignment for the Coast & Interior Survey," she said, "was the Meridian right-of-way through the Kessel Divide. Reconnaissance for the new summit crossing. I was good at my work. I want that understood—I was very good. I found things other surveyors walked over." Her jaw tightened. "One of the things I found was a tunnel. An older tunnel, pre-consolidation, on a line that officially never existed. The records called it a drainage adit. It is not a drainage adit. It runs clean under the range for nine miles with a gradient of less than two percent, and somebody built it in secret and then buried every trace of having built it."

"And you reported it," Kestrel said.

"I reported it." A short, bitter breath that was almost a laugh. "I wrote the most careful report of my career. Diagrams, elevations, bore samples. I sent it up the chain and waited for the commendation." She picked up the pencil again, turned it once between her fingers. "Three weeks later my director called me in and showed me my own report with a correction letter attached. My findings had been amended. The tunnel was reclassified as unstable, impassable, structurally compromised. Sealing recommended. My signature was still on it."

"They falsified your report," Pip said.

"I falsified nothing. That was the elegance of it. They let me keep my signature and took everything else. When I objected, I was informed that a surveyor who could not distinguish competent rock from incompetent rock was a liability to public safety, and I was dismissed with two weeks' pay and a letter of reference so carefully worded it might as well have been blank." She looked up, and her eyes in the firelight were hard and bright and furious in the way of people who have been made to doubt their own instruments. "I spent a year trying to prove the original data existed. Then I stopped trying to prove it and started trying to find it. Which is how I ended up in a sea cave reading survey marks off a wall."

"Whose marks?" Kestrel asked quietly.

Odile met their eyes. "You know whose."

"Elias Lockridge," Amos said.

The name dropped into the culvert like a coupling pin dropping into a socket—everything after it held together differently.

* * *

Kestrel had not spoken their father's name aloud in eleven years. They had carried it the way you carry a splinter too deep to draw: aware of it always, working around it, letting the flesh close over the shape of it. Other people said *your father* and Kestrel said nothing, and the nothing had grown so thick around the name that hearing it spoken plainly, in Amos's flat brakeman's voice, felt like a hand reaching into their chest.

"You knew him," Kestrel said. Not a question. It had been sitting in Kestrel's sight for days now, unexamined, the way you can ride past a broken rail for miles before you admit you saw it.

"I rode with him," Amos said. "Eleven years ago. Last run."

The fire popped. Pip had gone very still, the way animals go still.

"He was a road engineer," Kestrel said. Each word placed like a foot on uncertain ballast. "He surveyed for the Consolidated before the consolidation. Then he didn't anymore. Then—" Kestrel's hands had closed into fists without permission. "Then there was an accident on the Vell division. A washout. A freight went into the river at Miller's Bend. Eleven cars. Two men died. The inquiry blamed the roadbed assessment, and the roadbed assessment was signed Elias Lockridge, and he was gone before the ink dried. No body. No note. Just gone, and the syndicate lawyers happy to have a dead man to blame."

"I know," Amos said. "Because I was on that train."

The rain roared. Kestrel heard it from a great distance, the way you hear weather through the walls of a house you're leaving.

"Brakeman, rear platform, Miller's Bend local freight," Amos went on, and his voice did not waver, and somehow that was worse than wavering would have been. "Your father rode the cab that night because he didn't trust the roadbed and wanted eyes on it himself. He'd filed three warnings about that stretch. Three. All buried. So he rode the train to see for himself, and I rode with him because Elias Lockridge was the kind of man you rode with when he asked, and he asked."

"And?" The word came out of Kestrel like something torn. "And what, Amos? Did the roadbed fail? Was he right about the fill? Tell me the engineering, tell me the truth of the ground, because that part I can hold—"

"The ground was fine," Amos said. "That's the thing nobody ever said out loud. The ground was fine. That fill would have held a hundred years. What failed was a switch. Somebody lined the main over onto the river siding two miles back, and by the time your father saw the points coming at him in the dark there wasn't a braking distance in the world long enough." He looked into the fire, and the firelight moved in the deep seams of his face. "I saw the switch lamp. Green, where it should have been red. I told the inquiry the lamp was green. They wrote down that the lamp was green. And then the finding came back anyway: roadbed failure, engineer's assessment defective, case closed."

Kestrel was standing. They had no memory of standing. The culvert seemed smaller than it had been, the stone pressing in, the water-mirror on the far wall throwing their reflection back at them doubled and trembling.

"You let them hang it on him," Kestrel said. "You stood in that inquiry room and you let them hang it on a man who wasn't there to defend himself, and then you went on working for them. Twenty more years. I've seen your service record, Amos. You retired with a pension and a watch."

"Yes," Amos said.

"Why?" It came out ragged. "Why would you—"

"Because the morning after the inquiry, a man from the Consolidated's legal office came to my boarding house and sat across my kitchen table and ate my eggs and told me about my daughter." Amos's voice stayed level, but his hands, Kestrel noticed, had gone white-knuckled on the blanket roll. "My girl was nineteen, working a telegraph office in Corvane. He described the building. Described her desk. Told me accidents happen on railroads, Mr. Brant, terrible accidents, and witnesses to green lamps have been known to remember red. And I had buried a wife already and I could not—" For the first time his voice caught, a wheel flanging on a joint. "I could not bury her too. So I remembered red. God forgive me, I remembered red, and I kept remembering it for twenty years, and when I got my pension I started walking lines. Walking every dead line in the territory, looking for one thing."

"The tunnel," Kestrel whispered.

"Elias told me about it once, drunk, the year before. Said he'd found a door under the mountains that the company didn't know it owned, and that someday it would matter, and that when it mattered he'd need men who weren't afraid of the dark." Amos lifted his head, and his eyes were wet and steady. "I figured if he was alive anywhere, he'd be near that door. And if he was dead, then the least I owed him was to find out why."

* * *

Nobody spoke for a long time. The storm beat its fists on the mountain. Pip fed the fire without being asked, mechanically, the way people do when the alternative is thinking.

It was Pip, in the end, who broke it—but not with noise. With quiet, which from Pip was more startling than shouting.

"Perrin," the boy said.

Everyone looked at him.

"My real name. Since we're doing this." He sat cross-legged by the fire, chin up, doing bravery with his whole body. "Perrin Ashe Vole. My mother kept a station inn at Dunmark Junction till the Consolidated bought the junction and burned the inn for the insurance—or that's what everyone says; I was eight. She sent me to an uncle in Saltmere who sold me to a fish cannery within the month. I ran the winter I turned twelve. Been living free since." He shrugged, elaborately casual, and entirely failing at it. "Pip's just what the cannery called all the small ones. Easier than learning names for kids you were going to work to death anyway."

Odile reached over without ceremony and put her hand on the back of his neck, briefly, the way you'd touch a machine you were calibrating. Pip leaned into it maybe half an inch and pretended he hadn't.

"There," Pip said. "Now everybody's ruined. Fair's fair."

But it wasn't fair, and it wasn't finished, because Kestrel was still standing with their father's name ringing in their skull and twenty years of arithmetic rearranging itself into a shape they couldn't yet read. If the switch had been lined—if the accident was murder dressed as geology—then the syndicate hadn't merely hounded Elias Lockridge out of his career. They had tried to kill him. And when he vanished instead of dying, they had spent eleven years making sure the record stayed buried, sealing files, amending reports, nailing reward notices to cave mouths.

And the man who could have stopped it—the one living witness to the green lamp—had chosen his daughter's life over the truth, and had carried that choice so long it had grown into his spine.

Kestrel should have felt rage. Some part of them did, banked and black, coals at the bottom of a stove. But what rose in them, what frightened them, was something colder and more precise: the understanding that Amos Brant had done exactly what Kestrel Lockridge had done for eleven years. Had survived by disappearing. Had let a lie stand because the truth cost more than he could pay.

We are the same, Kestrel thought, and hated it.

"You should have told me at the cave," Kestrel said. Their voice came out flat, engineered, level as a grade. "The first night. Before I pumped a handcar a hundred miles with you on it. Before I—" They stopped. Before I started thinking of you as mine.

"Would you have taken me along if I had?"

"No."

"There it is, then." Amos nodded slowly, as if Kestrel had confirmed a timetable. "I told you when the price of the truth was lowest, and the price was still too high. That's not a defense. That's just the fare I paid."

"The fare *you* paid." Kestrel laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "My father paid it. Whatever happened to him—wherever he went—he paid it alone, with the whole world believing he killed two men through his own incompetence. Do you know what that does to a child? Growing up as the daughter of a ghost and a murderer? Every town we landed in, I learned to give a different name before I learned my times tables. Because of what *he* carried. Because of what *you* helped make him carry."

"Kess," Pip said softly.

"Don't." Kestrel held up a hand, and the boy subsided.

Odile had risen at some point and stood at the edge of the firelight, charts forgotten, watching Kestrel with the expression of a woman reading an instrument that has begun to move in a way the manual doesn't cover.

"The map," she said quietly.

"What about it."

"It's in your coat. Your father's route, reconstructed from the cave marks. The only copy in the world." Her eyes held Kestrel's. "And you're standing between it and the rest of us, and none of us knows what you're going to do, and I think you don't either."

Kestrel's hand had gone to the coat pocket without their permission. Through the wool they could feel the oilcloth wrapping, the folded sheets, the weight of eleven years of a man's handwriting. Everything he was. Everything they were.

The rain slackened—not stopping, but easing, the storm spending itself against the higher country, moving on up the range ahead of them like something being driven. In the lull, far off down the dead line, faint beneath the dripping of the pines, Kestrel heard a sound they had been trained their whole life to hear and dread: the far-off knock of steel on steel. Someone, somewhere behind them, walking the rails with a spike maul or a track gauge, checking the line.

Or hunting it.

"I need air," Kestrel said.

"Kestrel—" Odile began.

"I need *air*." They took the map out of the pocket—just took it out, held it, feeling all three of them watch it the way starving men watch bread—and put it back, and turned, and walked out into the rain into the dark of the cutting, alone, with the whole storm of the coast at their back and the black shoulder of the mountains ahead.

Behind them, muffled by stone and water, they heard Amos say quietly, "Let them walk. They'll come back or they won't. Either way, they'll do it as themselves."

Kestrel walked. And the map rode against their ribs like a second heart, beating out of time with their own.
