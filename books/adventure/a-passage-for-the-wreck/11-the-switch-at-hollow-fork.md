## 11. The Switch at Hollow Fork

Kestrel left before dawn, when the rain had thinned to a whisper and the others slept in the lee of a fallen water tower that smelled of creosote and rust. They did not leave quietly. That was the point. Boots on gravel, the creak of the pack straps, the deliberate scrape of a lantern hook against iron—enough noise to be heard by anyone who cared to listen. If Amos Brant woke and called out, Kestrel would not answer. If Odile stirred, she would understand. She had lied on paper too; she knew what it was to have your name written into something false.

But it was Pip's breathing Kestrel listened for as they stood in the dark, one hand on the tower's cold flank. The boy slept curled against Amos's side like a burr, his face slack with a trust he had no business giving anyone. Kestrel had known him four days. Four days, and already the boy said *we* when he talked about where they were going.

*We* was how it started. *We* was how men ended up buried under trestles with their names struck from company ledgers.

Kestrel turned and walked east along the dead spur, toward the junction the old maps called Hollow Fork, and did not look back.

* * *

The map rode against Kestrel's chest inside an oilcloth sleeve, and every step pressed it there like a second heartbeat. Their father's map. Drawn in that cramped, confident hand Kestrel had first seen two weeks ago on the cave wall—survey marks, elevations, a route through the range that the Meridian Railway Consolidated had spent twenty years pretending did not exist. Kestrel had copied it onto fresh paper in Saltmere, working by lantern light while the tide came back in below, and the copy was good. Good enough to lead them through. Good enough, if it fell into the wrong hands, to lead the syndicate straight to everything: the tunnel, the passage, the proof of what their father had found before he vanished into the mountains and out of the world.

So Kestrel took it. Not because they needed it—Odile had memorized half the bearings already, because of course she had—but because taking it was the only sentence Kestrel could still say clearly.

*I don't forgive you.*

That was what they'd told Amos last night, after the truth came out in the dripping dark of the shelter. Amos had been there. On the last run. The night the brakes failed—or were made to fail—on the down grade above Corren Gorge, the night Kestrel's father went into the gorge with his engine and eleven cars of freight and never came out. Amos had been the brakeman walking the train. He'd seen the coupling pins. He'd known they'd been pulled, and he'd signed the company report anyway, the one that called it weather. He'd been paid to forget, and he had forgotten beautifully for nineteen years, right up until he saw Kestrel's face in the cave and said their surname like a prayer.

*I don't forgive you.* And Amos had nodded, old eyes wet in the lantern light, and said, "I know. I'm not asking you to." And then he'd said the thing that made leaving necessary: "But you'll have to need me anyway, girl. That's how it works."

*Kestrel,* not *girl*. Another thing to resent. He'd known their father. He'd earned the familiarity and forfeited it in the same breath.

The rain stopped an hour past first light. The spur climbed away from the coast through cuttings choked with fern, and the world went green and dripping and utterly silent except for Kestrel's own breath. This was the country Kestrel loved best—the abandoned grade, the rails rusted to lace, ties soft as bread. No schedules here. No syndicate. Just the arithmetic of decay, which never lied to anyone.

They walked all morning and did not think about anything, which was its own kind of lying.

* * *

Hollow Fork announced itself before Kestrel saw it: the smell of coal smoke, faint and wrong, riding the wind down the valley. Then the sound—a rhythmic clanking, metal on metal, patient as a clock. Kestrel went off the grade and up into the alder scrub, belly-down on a ridge of slate, and looked.

The junction sat in a bowl where three lines met and two of them died. A wye turnout, a water crane gone to ruin, a signal gantry draped in ivy. And camped beneath the gantry, sheltered from the wet: six men, a mule, and a handcar mounted on a flatbed wagon. Canvas tents. A cook fire burning green wood so the smoke would read as mist from any distance. Syndicate discipline. Meridian Railway Consolidated, section crew or worse—and nobody sent a section crew this deep into a dead line unless they were looking for something. Or someone.

Kestrel lay in the wet fern and counted. Six visible. One at the far switch, tapping the points with a hammer, checking the throw. Two at the fire. Three moving gear between tents. A seventh might be sleeping. The mule carried panniers—wire, tools, maybe dynamite. They weren't surveying. Surveyors didn't carry blasting powder up dead spurs.

They were sealing something. Or preparing to seal it. Which meant the syndicate had guessed the route too, or intercepted enough of it, and Hollow Fork sat square across the path Odile had plotted from her memory of the bearings.

Which meant Kestrel had just walked alone into the exact place the family should not split up.

The thought arrived without mercy, wearing Amos's voice: *You'll have to need me anyway.*

"Damn you," Kestrel whispered, to the old man, to the map, to the whole crooked architecture of other people.

* * *

The problem was the switch. The main line through Hollow Fork ran north toward the summit approach, and the syndicate patrol was camped directly on it. But there was a second way—the old quarry branch, a hairpin of track that left the junction a quarter mile south and rejoined beyond the ridge. It was steeper, rotten, unmarked on anything newer than thirty years. Kestrel knew it existed only because their father's map showed it, a thin penciled line annotated in that cramped hand: *Q.B. — passable, walk it first.*

Walk it first. Fine. Kestrel could walk it first, circle back, bring the others around the long way tonight while the patrol slept. All of which required getting off this ridge without being seen, and required knowing whether the quarry branch was passable or had fallen into the Vell gorge sometime in the intervening decades.

Kestrel slid backward down the slate, found a game trail, and moved south through the trees with the low sun breaking apart in the branches. The branch line appeared through the brush—an embankment, then a cutting, then the first curve of the hairpin hugging the gorge wall. The rail was there. Rusted, but there. Kestrel walked it, testing ties with each step, cataloguing defects the way other people breathed. Washout at fifty yards, passable on the ballast shoulder. Deadfall at two hundred, climbable. Then the cutting narrowed, the gorge dropped away sheer on the left, and Kestrel stopped.

Ahead, the embankment had slumped. Not collapsed—slumped, a long sagging bow of slipped fill, the track bowed sideways but continuous, a tightrope of rusted steel over forty feet of air. Walkable, maybe, if you trusted two corroded rails with your life. For a loaded party with an injured man? No. Not Amos, not with his ribs strapped and his hip the way it was.

Unless the fill was shored. Unless someone who understood earthworks spent a day on it with timber and stone—

Which was Odile's trade. Which meant telling Odile. Which meant going back.

Kestrel stood on the bowed rails with the gorge breathing cold air up at them and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like anger, exactly how thoroughly the trap had closed. Not the syndicate's trap. The other kind. The kind made of people.

"You can resent your family," their father used to say, some evening long ago, in a kitchen that no longer existed anywhere but in Kestrel's head, "and still set a plate for them. That's not weakness. That's just what a crew is."

Kestrel had hated that saying for nineteen years. It had waited all this time to be true.

They turned back north along the branch, and that was when the whistle blew.

* * *

Two notes, sharp, from the direction of the junction. Then voices. Kestrel dropped flat behind a granite boulder as three men came down the main line at a jog—one carrying a rifle, one carrying wire cutters, one carrying nothing but authority in the set of his shoulders. Kestrel knew that silhouette. Broad hat, narrow hips, the rolling gait of a man who'd spent years riding coaches. The agent from Saltmere. The one who'd nailed the notice to the cave mouth and asked the harbormaster questions with a smile that never reached his eyes.

They stopped twenty yards short of Kestrel's boulder, at the point where the quarry branch met the main line, and the agent crouched and touched the ties.

"Wet prints," he said. "Fresh. Somebody walked this branch within the hour."

"Could be a deer track," offered the man with the wire cutters.

"Deer don't wear boots." The agent rose and scanned the tree line, and Kestrel pressed themselves into the granite's shadow and became, by long practice, part of the landscape—breath shallow, hands still, mind running the geometry of escape. The trees to the east were open fern, no cover for eighty yards. The gorge wall to the west was vertical. South lay the bowed rails over the drop. North lay the junction and five more men.

The agent took his time about it. That was the worst part—watching him decide, watching his gaze travel the fern line inch by inch, a man doing careful work. Kestrel's hand found the map through the oilcloth, through the shirt, against the sternum. If they ran, they'd be shot at. If they stayed, they'd be found. There was a third option, the one Kestrel's whole life had been built on: vanish now, sacrifice the circuit back to the others, go to ground alone, come at the tunnel from a different angle entirely. Alone again. Safe again. Invisible again.

The agent stepped off the ballast and into the fern.

And then the valley filled with sound.

It came from the north—from the junction—a long, descending moan of steam-whistle, impossible, absurd, a locomotive note in a country where no locomotive had run in thirty years. The agent spun. Down the main line, around the curve, came a handcar pumping hard, four figures on it, and riding the rear platform with a lantern held high and a brakeman's lever in his grip, an old man in a patched coat, hauling the whistle-cord of the handcar's signal horn for all he was worth and shouting in a voice cracked with age and pure railroad theater:

*"Special! Special on the main! Clear the board, boys, clear the board!"*

The three men froze, staring. And Kestrel, whose heart had stopped and now restarted with a violence that hurt, understood in one flashing instant what Amos Brant had done. He'd heard Kestrel leave—he'd been awake the whole time, of course he had, he'd been a brakeman for forty years and brakemen sleep like cats—and instead of following, instead of arguing, he'd loaded the others on the handcar and rolled them straight down the main line toward the syndicate camp, because the one thing guaranteed to pull every eye in a valley was a train where no train should be.

Pip was pumping. Odile was pumping. And Amos stood at the rear with the horn, playing conductor on a ghost train, buying sixty seconds with his own body.

The agent forgot the fern. The three men turned and ran for the junction, and Kestrel came up out of the shadow and ran the other way—south, along the branch, back toward the bowed rails and the others coming on. Fifty yards. A hundred. Behind them, shouts, the crack of the agent's voice trying to organize panic into pursuit, and Amos's horn sounding again, closer now, brazen, magnificent.

The handcar burst around the curve of the main line as Kestrel reached the junction's southern switch, and for one suspended moment the two parties saw each other across forty feet of shining wet rail—Kestrel standing on the ballast, the syndicate men scrambling from their tents, and the handcar flying past with Pip's face lit up like a festival lamp and Odile grimly hauling and Amos raising one hand, not in greeting, in *instruction*, pointing hard at the southern switch with two fingers.

Throw the switch. Bring them onto the branch. Get off the main line before the patrol got organized.

Kestrel threw it. The bar was stiff with thirty years of rust and it fought like a living thing, and Kestrel put their whole fury into it—every ounce of resentment and grief and unwilling love—and the points slid over with a bang that sounded like a door closing. The handcar rattled through onto the quarry branch, Amos leaping off at the tail-end as it passed, hitting the ballast, rolling, coming up bad on his bad hip with a grunt Kestrel heard clear across the distance.

"Push!" Amos roared at the car. "Get it round the hairpin, I'll spike the switch!"

He limped to the switch stand, pulled a spike maul from somewhere in his coat, and drove a spike between the point and the stock rail to lock the turnout—buying minutes, maybe ten, before the syndicate could force it. Then he turned and looked at Kestrel across the rails, chest heaving, rain and sweat on his face, and neither of them said anything at all.

There was nothing to say that hadn't been said in the lantern light last night. He had betrayed a dead man's memory and saved a living one's life in the same hour, and both facts were true, and they would stay true forever, the way a broken rail stays broken no matter how well you shim it.

Kestrel broke first. "The branch is out past the hairpin—embankment's slipped. We'll have to shore it."

"I figured you scouted it." Amos shouldered the maul. His voice was steady, but his hands weren't. "Your daddy always scouted twice."

"Don't." The word came out sharp enough to draw blood. "Don't talk about him."

Amos nodded slowly, accepting the blow like a man who'd budgeted for it. "Fair," he said. "Fair enough." He started up the branch after the handcar, then paused. "You took the map."

Kestrel's hand went to their chest before they could stop it.

"Good," Amos said. "Keep it close. And Kestrel—" He didn't turn around. "Next time you walk out on us, walk louder. I nearly didn't hear you go."

* * *

They caught the handcar at the top of the hairpin, where Odile had already halted it and was pacing the bowed rails with her surveyor's eye, muttering elevations. Pip stood on the car, breathless, glowing, vibrating with the morning.

"Did you see?" he burst out at Kestrel. "Did you see us come around the curve? Amos said we'd have maybe one chance to make them look the wrong way and—" He caught Kestrel's expression and faltered. "You're mad."

"I'm not mad."

"You are. You've got your mad face."

"Pip." Kestrel looked at him—at the boy who'd said *we*, who'd pumped a handcar into a gun camp because an old man asked him to—and felt the resentment in their chest shift, grudgingly, to make room beside itself for something else. "Thank you. All of you. Now get off the car and start gathering timber. We've got an embankment to build and maybe nine minutes of head start."

Beyond the ridge, faint but distinct, came the sound of the syndicate's mule braying, and a man's voice shouting orders, and the ring of a hammer on locked iron.

Amos settled himself on a tie with a groan, maul across his knees, and looked up at the grey sky where the storm was gathering again. "Nine minutes," he said. "I've shored worse with less."
