## 12. Up the Dead Line

The handcar died at the foot of the mountain, and Pip thought that was fitting, somehow. It had carried them this far—two hundred miles of rusted coastal spur and forgotten branch line, pumping through the dark with Amos calling the grades and Odile watching the rails—but the dead line ahead of them had no rails worth pumping. Just ties gone soft as bread, and a grade that climbed like it held a grudge.

They left it in a cutting choked with alder scrub. Kestrel stood over it a long moment, one hand on the cold iron of the frame, the way you'd touch a horse you had to turn loose.

"It got us here," Pip said.

"Things get you here," Kestrel said. "That's all they're for."

Pip didn't say anything to that. He was learning which of Kestrel's sentences were doors and which were walls, and that one was a wall, so he let it be.

They redistributed the load in the grey light before dawn. The rope went on Kestrel's back because Kestrel said rope was life and life went where the route decisions were made. The tools—crowbar, sledge, two shovels wrapped in oilcloth—went across Amos's shoulders until Odile took one shovel away from him without asking, and Amos let her, which told Pip how bad his ribs still were. The food, what there was of it: hardtack, salt pork gone white at the edges, a tin of condensed milk Pip had been saving since Saltmere and would probably keep saving until it killed him or saved him. Odile carried the maps and instruments in a leather case she slept with curled against her chest like a child.

And Kestrel carried the map. The real one. Folded into eight sections inside a waxed envelope, and never once opened where anyone could see.

Pip watched it go up the mountain on Kestrel's back and thought about how a piece of paper could weigh more than a man.

* * *

The grade was called the Devil's Staircase on the old survey charts, Odile said, though she admitted she'd made that up herself once, years ago, for a report nobody read, and the name had stuck the way lies stick when they're better than the truth. It rose through switchbacks cut into the mountainside, each one tighter than the last, the embankment crumbling at its outer edge into a drop that the fog filled up completely, so that walking it felt like walking along the rim of a bowl of milk.

Pip counted his steps. That was a thing he did. In the cave, waiting out tides, he'd counted everything—the drips, the bats, the seconds between Amos's snores—and counting kept the fear in a box with holes punched in it, small enough to breathe. So he counted steps. A hundred steps, rest. A hundred more.

By the third switchback the rain started. Not the monsoon yet—that was still down on the coast, doing whatever monsoons did before they arrived, gathering itself like a debt collector—but a thin, needling rain that came sideways off the peaks and found every gap in a coat, every seam, every place where a boy's body leaked heat.

"Snow above us," Kestrel said, squinting up through the murk. "This is just its shadow falling early."

"Cheerful," said Odile.

"I'm an engineer. We don't do cheerful. We do timetables."

Amos laughed, then stopped laughing fast, hand pressed to his side. "Boy," he said to Pip, "you want to hear about the winter of '81? Now *that* was weather."

"You've told me," Pip said.

"I'll tell it better this time."

But he didn't get far into it, because the grade steepened and even stories cost breath, and after that they climbed in silence except for boots on rotten ties and the wind worrying the alder scrub like a dog with a bone.

Pip counted. Ninety-one. Ninety-two. His legs had gone strange, heavy and light at once, like they belonged to someone else who was letting him borrow them. Ninety-five. He'd been hungry before. Hungry was familiar; hungry was just the cave with extra scenery. But this was past hunger into something else, a hollowness that rang when he moved, and the rain had gotten inside him somehow, a coldness sitting behind his sternum that no amount of moving touched.

Ninety-eight. Ninety-nine.

He lost count somewhere around four hundred, and that frightened him worse than the drop beside the trail. Counting was the box. If the count went, the fear got out.

"Pip." Odile's voice, close. When had she fallen back to him? "How are your feet?"

"Wet."

"That wasn't the question."

"They're fine," he said, and then the trail tilted, or he tilted, and the milk-white fog came up and met him halfway.

* * *

He didn't faint, exactly. He remembered later that he hadn't fainted, and being proud of it in a small, stupid way. He'd sat down. Just sat down on a tie at the edge of the switchback, meaning to rest for one second, and the one second had turned into a country he couldn't walk out of. The rain fell on his face and he watched it come down out of nothing and thought: I should stand up now. And his body answered like a telegraph office after hours. No operator. Line closed.

Then hands were under his arms, and the sky swung, and Kestrel's face was above him, rain running off the brim of the cap, eyes narrowed in that way that meant calculations were happening.

"When did you last eat?"

"This morning."

"Ate. Not chewed."

Pip tried to remember. There had been hardtack. There had been—had there been? The days had gone soft at the edges since the bridge, since Amos fell, since everything became climbing and cold and the sound of Amos breathing wrong at night.

"He's been giving me his share," Amos rumbled, arriving like weather. "Little fool thinks I haven't noticed."

"You have?" Pip said, mortified.

"Boy, I've been a brakeman thirty years. I notice weight distribution. It's my whole trade." He crouched, wincing, and pressed his own ration tin into Pip's hands. "Eat."

"You need—"

"I need you upright, and that's the same thing right now. Eat."

The salt pork was terrible. It was the best thing Pip had ever tasted. He ate half before his stomach clenched and refused the rest, and Odile wrapped the remainder in cloth and put it in her pocket without comment, and Pip understood that it would come back to him later, in installments, the way she did everything—precisely, secretly, with more kindness than she'd ever admit to.

Kestrel crouched in front of him, back turned.

"Get on."

"What?"

"On. You're not walking the next mile on those legs, and we're not camping in the open at this elevation with wet wood and a storm coming down off the summit. Get on."

"I can walk."

"You can't. I know, because I've been you. Fifteen years old, alone on a dead line, refusing help because help is a debt and debts mean people and people mean staying." Kestrel glanced back, and there was something in their face Pip had never seen—not softness exactly, but the memory of softness, worn smooth like a rail tie. "I stayed invisible so long I nearly disappeared entirely. Don't start practicing."

Pip got on.

It was humiliating for about forty steps, and then it wasn't. Kestrel carried him the way Kestrel did everything—with terrible efficiency, hands hooked under his knees, his arms around their shoulders, adjusting for the grade without breaking stride. Pip felt the map's envelope pressing against his chest where the pack rode, that paper heart beating against his spine, and he thought: *this is what it weighs.* Not the paper. The carrying.

The others rearranged themselves around them without being asked. Odile ranged ahead, reading the grade, calling out loose ballast and washouts. Amos brought up the rear, slow but steady, humming something under his breath—an old brake-song, work-chant rhythm, half a tune Pip had heard him hum in the cave a hundred times. It wasn't much of a song. It kept time with Kestrel's footsteps anyway.

Somewhere in the middle of the fifth switchback, Pip stopped being embarrassed and started being something else. He was twelve years old when he'd run, and he'd been carrying himself ever since—his own bags, his own silences, his own name folded up small in his pocket like the map. Nobody had ever carried him. He'd assumed nobody would. Carrying was for children and cargo, and he was neither; he was a survivor, and survivors traveled light.

But riding on Kestrel's back through the needle-rain with Amos's song keeping time, Pip discovered the other thing, the thing nobody told you: sometimes the strong ones needed something to carry, too. Kestrel's grip was steadier with him aboard. The set of their shoulders had changed since the cave, since the letter, since the group became a group—less like a man traveling alone, more like a locomotive that had finally been given cars to pull. Purpose ran through them like current through a line.

*Found family means being carried sometimes,* he thought. *And carrying sometimes. And neither one is a debt.*

He must have dozed, because the next thing he knew the rain had thickened, gone slushy, ticking against his hood with a sound like sand, and the world had turned white at the edges.

"Sleet," Odile called back. "We're crossing into it."

"How far to the portal?" Amos asked.

"Survey says the gradient tops out at the shoulder, tunnel mouth on the north face. Two miles as the crow flies. Maybe five the way this line wanders."

"And the snowline?"

Kestrel's voice came from beneath Pip, flat and even. "We're standing in it."

* * *

They camped at dusk in the lee of a rock cut, if you could call it camping—a tarp strung between the cut wall and a leaning tie, everyone packed together for warmth, no fire because firewood was wet and smoke was a beacon and both of those facts were fatal in different ways. They ate cold. They drank water Odile had caught in the tarp's sag, tasting of canvas. Amos's breathing was bad again, a whistle in it now that hadn't been there yesterday, and Pip lay listening to it and doing arithmetic he hated: *if Amos gets worse, we carry him. If we carry him, we're slower. If we're slower—*

"Stop that," Odile murmured from beside him. She could read minds. Or maybe she just did the same arithmetic.

"Stop what?"

"The sums. They always come out wrong this time of night. Do them at dawn, when the numbers behave."

Pip almost smiled. Then, in the dark, Amos spoke.

"Boy's asleep?"

"No," Pip said.

"Hm." A pause, filled by wind. "When I was your age, I got carried once too. Off the top of a tender at Marrow Junction, after a coupling let go. Engineer reached down and hauled me up one-handed without even stopping the train. Never learned his name. Thought about it every day since, though." Another pause. "People think the railroad's about freight. It ain't. It's about who reaches down."

Pip looked across the dark at the shape of the old man. "Did you reach down? For anyone?"

The silence went on long enough that Pip thought he wouldn't answer. Then, quiet, almost lost in the wind:

"Once, I didn't. That's why I'm here."

Nobody asked what he meant. Everybody knew. It hung in the wet air between them, unexploded, and Kestrel turned over with their back to the group, and Odile closed her eyes, and the sleet ticked on the tarp like a clock counting down something none of them wanted to name.

* * *

Dawn came the color of dishwater, and they climbed.

The last miles were the worst, because hope made them careless. Every bend, Pip expected the portal—black rectangle in the grey rock, shelter, an ending to the climbing—and every bend gave them only more grade, more switchback, more sleet turning to true snow now, fat flakes that came down without wind, straight and silent, like the sky was deciding something.

It was Odile who saw it first. She stopped dead on the trail ahead, and Pip, riding on Kestrel's back again—he'd faltered twice more that morning and stopped pretending otherwise—felt Kestrel's stride change, feel the whole body go alert.

"Well?" Amos puffed.

Odile pointed.

Through the snowfall, maybe half a mile up the final straight of grade, the embankment ran into the mountainside itself. And set into the rock, barely visible, a darkness. A mouth.

The portal.

Nobody cheered. Pip thought later that was the strangest part—they'd walked five days up a dead line through sleet and hunger and a dying man's whistling lungs, and when salvation appeared they just stood there in the falling snow, too spent for joy, looking at it the way you look at a door you're afraid might be locked.

Then Kestrel shifted Pip's weight and started walking again, faster, and the others followed, and the snow came down harder behind them, filling in their footprints, erasing the dead line mile by mile, cutting them off from everything below.

Pip watched it happen over Kestrel's shoulder. Watched the white close over the trail like water closing over a sunk thing.

"We can't go back," he said.

"No," Kestrel said. "That's rather the point."

They reached the portal as the light failed. Up close it was bigger than it had looked—twenty feet of timbered arch, the beams black with age but sound, the masonry work of men who'd expected their tunnel to outlive empires. Snow had drifted against the sill in a smooth ramp. And beyond the arch: darkness, going back and back, breathing out air that smelled of stone and old iron and, very faintly, of smoke.

Pip smelled it at the same moment Kestrel did. Felt them stop.

"Smoke," Odile whispered. "Fresh."

Kestrel set Pip down gently on the drift, took the lantern from the pack, and struck it lit. The flame stood up steady in the tunnel's exhale.

"Someone's inside," Kestrel said quietly. "And recently."

The light reached only a few yards into the black. Beyond it, the tunnel waited, two miles of it, and somewhere in the middle of that dark, a collapse—and now, apparently, company.

Kestrel looked back at them once—at Odile with her maps, at Amos swaying on his feet, at Pip standing in the snowdrift with the tin of condensed milk still unopened in his pocket.

"Stay close," Kestrel said, and stepped through the arch, into the mountain, and the dark took them one by one.
