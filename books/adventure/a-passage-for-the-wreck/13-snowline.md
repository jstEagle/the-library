## 13. Snowline

Odile Vasseur had spent eleven years learning to read mountains the way other people read newspapers—casually, constantly, with an eye for the small print that killed you. So when she stopped on the switchback below the summit and looked back down the dead line, she knew what the white haze on the wind meant before she could name it.

"Snow," she said. Not a question. A diagnosis.

Kestrel turned. Behind them the gradient fell away in long, rusted switchbacks, the rails they'd climbed all day glinting wet and black between the ties. Below that, the foothills they'd crossed that morning were already softening at the edges, the rain that had chased them since Hollow Fork turning to something thicker, slower, more deliberate. The clouds had come down off the peaks to meet them, and the whole world above four thousand feet had become a gray room with no walls.

"How far to the portal?" Amos asked. He was strapped into the improvised litter they'd rigged from the handcar's bed and two pack frames, and he asked it the way he asked everything now—flat, patient, a man who had made his peace with being cargo.

"Three miles of track, then the cut," Odile said. She pulled the map from the oilcloth sleeve inside her coat, though she didn't need to. She had the father's survey marks memorized to the chain. "Three miles, and then we're either through or we're not."

"Optimist," Pip said. The girl had Odile's spare coat wrapped around her like a robe, sleeves rolled six times, and her teeth were chattering in a rhythm she was clearly trying to disguise as speech.

"Surveyor," Odile corrected. "It's a different disease."

They climbed. There was no alternative worth discussing and so nobody discussed it. That was the thing about the dead line, Odile thought, planting her boots on ties that hadn't felt a wheel in thirty years—it permitted no debate. The grade was twelve percent in places, brutal, built by men who had believed the mountain owed them a right-of-way and had died proving otherwise. The rails themselves were the only mercy: they gave purchase where the ballast had washed to gravel, and Kestrel walked them like a tightrope, one foot ahead of the other, reading the ties for rot the way Odile read contours.

She watched Kestrel's back and thought, not for the first time, that she had misjudged this person badly in the cave. She had taken the silence for arrogance—the silence of a man who owned no address, no name on any ledger, no history he'd admit to. But eleven days on the dead line had taught her the truth: the silence was load-bearing. Kestrel carried things in it. A father. A grudge against Amos Brant that had not thawed so much as been set aside, like a tool you couldn't afford to drop but couldn't bear to hold. A map drawn in another man's hand, folded against Kestrel's chest, that Odile suspected was the only document in the world that mattered to them.

And now snow. She did the arithmetic again because the arithmetic was all she had that couldn't be lost or stolen. Six weeks to the summit pass, Kestrel had said in the cave, reading the old survey dates. They had used four. The monsoon had eaten the rest. Every low pass between them and the far valley was filling with brown water even now—she'd heard it in the stolen receiver's chatter two nights ago, dispatchers shouting at each other about washouts on the Coast Line, the word *unpassable* passing down the wire like a contagion. The summit tunnel was the last door still open in the wall of the range.

Behind them, the first flakes arrived. Not the shy experimental flakes of a mountain's first snow but fat, committed ones, falling with the vertical seriousness of the monsoon's cold upper deck. Within twenty minutes the switchbacks below had gone the color of old bone. Within forty, the track they'd climbed was gone—not hidden, *gone*, erased as if the mountain had reconsidered and unmade the railway out of spite.

"Retreat's closed," Amos observed from the litter, watching it happen. He sounded almost satisfied. "Good. I never did like having options. They just give you time to make the wrong choice slower."

Pip laughed, a short bark of a sound, and then looked ashamed of it. Odile didn't. She understood the laugh completely. There was a horrible freedom in a door shutting behind you—the same freedom, she supposed, that had come with the letter that ended her career. *Miss Vasseur's report contains irregularities.* After that, no options. After that, only forward.

Her own report. Her falsified report. She had signed away the tunnel's existence to keep it out of Meridian's hands, and it had cost her everything except the one thing she'd been trying to protect, which was the knowledge itself. And now here she was, carrying that knowledge up the mountain on her own two feet, about to hand it to a hole in the rock and pray the hole went all the way through.

The cut appeared as the light failed—not gradually but suddenly, a black slot in the white hillside, timbered at the mouth with beams gone silver-gray, and above them, cut into the rock in letters that had once been painted gold:

**SUMMIT TUNNEL — 2 MILES 1,340 YARDS — MERIDIAN CONSOLIDATED**

"Somebody was proud of this once," Pip said.

"Somebody was paid to be proud of it," Odile said. "There's a difference, and it's usually about four dollars a day."

Kestrel stood at the portal a long moment without going in. Odile came up beside them and understood why. The air coming out of the tunnel was moving—slow, steady, a breath drawn from somewhere deep in the mountain, and it was warmer than the air going in. Warmer meant open. Warmer meant the far portal was drawing air through, which meant the tunnel was not a dead tube but a passage, throat to throat, the mountain's two mouths connected.

"It breathes," she said.

"It breathes." Kestrel's voice was strange—hope held at arm's length, examined, not quite embraced. Odile recognized the posture. She'd worn it herself for eleven years. Hope was the most dangerous thing a surveyor could carry into the field; it made you see grades that weren't there, bridges that would hold. "Two miles and a quarter to the far side. If the air's moving the whole way—"

"Then the way forward exists." She finished it for them, because someone had to say it out loud, and she watched the word *forward* land on all of them like a weight being set down. Even Amos, gray-faced in the litter, let his head tip back against the frame and closed his eyes.

"And the way behind," Odile added, because she was the one who carried the bad arithmetic, "is closed. Nobody follows us up that grade tonight. Nobody follows us up it ever, if this keeps falling."

They made camp in the portal's mouth, just inside the timbering, where the mountain's breath kept the worst of the cold off. Kestrel got a fire going in a ring of broken ballast—small, screened, more warmth than light. They ate cold beans and hard bread and the last of the smoked fish from the river camp, and nobody talked much, because the talking would come later or it wouldn't, and right now the body wanted its due.

Odile sat with her back against a timber and did what she always did at the end of a day: she surveyed. Not the land—the people. Pip had gone from feral to merely sharp-eyed in eleven days; the girl was asleep in seconds, curled against Amos's litter like a cat against a warm stove, one hand still gripping the strap. Amos was awake and watching the fire, and when he caught Odile looking at him he said, quietly, "She snores. You'll want to know that before you're in a tunnel with her."

"Noted."

"She's a good kid under all that noise. Whatever her name is." He said it carefully, the way you set down something fragile, and Odile let it pass. Pip's real name was Pip's to give or keep. Everyone in this company was a ledger of withheld entries.

Kestrel sat apart, as always, map on their knees, not reading it—just resting a hand on it, the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping animal. The firelight made the old ink glow faintly brown. Odile thought about what that paper was. Not a map, really. A man. A man who had walked this same portal thirty years ago with a transit and a notebook and a name his child still wouldn't say aloud, and who had walked into the mountain and out of the world, and left behind nothing but chain measurements and a child who had learned to never sleep twice in the same town.

She opened her mouth to say something—she didn't know what, something about the breathing air, something about tomorrow—and Kestrel spoke first.

"Someone's been through."

Odile went still. "What?"

Kestrel tilted their head toward the tunnel mouth, toward the floor of it, where the firelight only reached a few yards. "Come look. Bring the lantern."

The floor of the tunnel was old ballast, and old ballast tells stories if you know its grammar. Odile knew it. She knelt where Kestrel pointed, lantern low, and read.

Footprints. Not theirs. Boot prints, several sets, pressed into the fine drift-dust that thirty years of mountain breath had laid along the tunnel floor—and here was the thing that made her stomach tighten: the prints were crisp. Edges sharp. Dust not yet resettled into them. Days old, not months. Weeks at the outside.

"Four sets," she said. "Maybe five. One set's smaller—your size, Pip's, or near it. They came in from the far side." She traced the direction with a finger. "And they were carrying weight. Look—these drag marks here, between the steps. Something heavy. A crate, or a stretcher."

"Meridian?" Amos had hauled himself up on one elbow, and his voice had lost its ease.

"Could be a maintenance crew from the far valley. Could be hunters. Could be—" Odile stopped. She was a surveyor; she dealt in what was measurable. "Could be anyone. But they went *through*, Kestrel. Whatever's two miles in, it didn't stop them."

Kestrel crouched, lantern held low, and for a moment their face in the light was the face Odile imagined on the father in the old photographs Amos had described—a face doing arithmetic it didn't like. "Two miles in," Kestrel said. "That's where your father's notes flag trouble. Chain 11,000. He wrote *rock working* in the margin. Underlined it."

"Rock working means rock moving," Odile said. "It means he heard it settling. It means he knew."

"And someone crossed recently. Over or through a collapse at chain eleven thousand." Kestrel stood. The lantern light made their shadow climb the timbering, huge and wavering. "So either the collapse isn't as bad as his notes feared—"

"Or someone cleared it," Amos said. "And clearing a collapse takes tools, timber, and time. And Meridian Consolidated has all three, and has wanted this tunnel for years, and just lost us somewhere on the wrong side of a snowed-in grade."

Nobody said the rest of it. Nobody had to. Odile finished it inside her own head, in the cold professional voice she used for the worst numbers: *If the syndicate's men are through the tunnel, they are between us and the far valley. If they cleared the collapse, they intend to keep it cleared—for their own use. And if they find our fire's smoke or our prints in the dust, they will know exactly what came up the dead line behind them.*

She looked back down the mountain, out through the portal, and the snow was coming down thick and silent and total, filling the cut, filling the switchbacks, filling the whole gray world. The retreat was closed. The way forward existed. And somewhere in the dark two miles into the mountain, in the space between those two facts, someone was already there.

Pip stirred in her sleep and murmured something that might have been a name, and might have been a place, and the mountain breathed its long slow breath through the portal, in and out, in and out, like something enormous waiting to see what they would do.

Kestrel banked the fire low and took the first watch, the map folded away against their chest, and Odile lay down in the breathing dark and did not sleep, and did not sleep, and did not sleep.

* * *

Two miles into the mountain, by the arithmetic of the chain, the collapse waited. Odile fell asleep at last against Amos's litter and dreamed of her report—the clean white page, the lie she'd written in her own best drafting hand—and woke once, deep in the night, certain she had heard, far down the tunnel, the ring of iron on stone. A pick, or a bar. Rhythmic. Patient. Working.

She lay listening until the sound resolved into her own pulse, or didn't, and the snow fell on and on behind them, sealing the world they'd come from, and ahead of them in the black throat of the range, whoever had crossed before them was two miles away, or one, or close enough now that she could almost believe she heard them breathing.
