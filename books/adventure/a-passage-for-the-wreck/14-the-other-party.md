## 14. The Other Party

The dark in the tunnel was not empty. Kestrel had known that for half a mile before any of them said it aloud.

It was the way the sound moved. Their own footsteps came back wrong—too many heels striking stone, too much breath that wasn't theirs. Odile heard it first, of course. She stopped without announcing it, hand up, and the four of them stood in the black with their lamps hooded and listened to the mountain breathe.

"Two," Amos whispered at last. "Maybe three. Ahead and to the left, past the fall."

"You're sure?"

"I spent thirty years listening to trains I couldn't see," Amos said. "Men are easier. Men cough."

Kestrel raised their lamp a fraction, unhooded it, and let the light spill down the bore. The tunnel ran straight as a hymn here, the old summit grade, rails still bedded in ballast though nothing had rolled over them in forty years. Two hundred yards on, where the collapse had been reported—a shoulder of fallen rock choking the passage floor to ceiling—the light found something else.

A fire. Small, mean, banked low, but unmistakable. And around it, shapes.

"Don't," Odile said quietly.

But Kestrel was already walking forward, lamp held high, and behind them they heard Pip's breath catch and Amos mutter something that might have been a prayer or a curse, and then all of them were following, because that was the thing about a family, even one three weeks old and built out of wreckage—it followed you into stupid places and made them slightly less stupid by being there.

* * *

There were six of them.

Six men in grey wool and canvas, syndicate-issue from the stitching, gathered around a fire so small it was less warmth than suggestion. Their gear was piled against the tunnel wall in the neat, defeated stacks of people who had stopped expecting to need it quickly. Two lay wrapped in blankets, shivering in a rhythm too deep to be sleep. One sat apart with his head in his hands. The rest looked up as Kestrel's light hit them, and Kestrel watched six faces do the same arithmetic at once: strangers, armed with nothing but a lantern, standing between them and the way home.

Then the man nearest the fire stood, and Kestrel knew him.

Saltmere. The station platform, three weeks and a lifetime ago—the man who had asked about freight schedules with a little too much interest, who had looked at Kestrel one second too long, whose face had been the first crack in twenty years of invisibility. He'd worn a better coat then. Now he wore the same coat under a layer of rock dust, and he was holding his hands out from his sides in a gesture that was either surrender or an invitation to be reasonable.

"You," he said.

"Me," Kestrel agreed.

Behind Kestrel, Amos shifted his weight, and Kestrel knew without looking that the old brakeman had put himself between Pip and the fire. Good. That was one arrangement Kestrel didn't have to manage.

"My name is Dree," the man said. "Corvin Dree. Meridian Railway Consolidated, survey division." He said it like a man reciting his own eulogy, the words worn smooth from use. "You're Lockridge."

"Not the name I'd have picked."

"It's the name your father used." Dree's voice didn't rise. That was the thing Kestrel remembered from Saltmere and saw confirmed now—he wasn't a shouters-and-wavers sort of man. He was the kind who wrote things down. "Elias Lockridge, engineer, last employed Meridian Coastal Division, departed service under circumstances the company does not discuss. You have his chin. And his habit of standing where the exits are."

Kestrel became aware that they were, in fact, standing where the exits were—one eye on the collapse, one on the dark stretch behind them—and hated how legible they apparently were.

"There are four of us," Kestrel said. "We're passing through. We don't want anything you have."

"We don't *have* anything." Dree laughed, and there was no humor in it, only exhaustion ground fine as flour. "Look at us, Lockridge. Look at us properly."

Kestrel did. It would have been easier not to.

The two men under blankets weren't sleeping. One had a boot off and a foot wrapped in rags gone brown at the edges. The other simply shook, steadily, the way a man shakes when the cold has gotten inside the machinery of him and started running on its own power. The one sitting apart had a surveyor's transit across his knees, cradled like a child, and he hadn't looked up once. The youngest of them—barely older than Pip, though Pip would have fought anyone who said so—stood at the edge of the firelight holding a pick handle with both hands, knuckles white, trying very hard to look like a threat and mostly looking like a boy pretending the dark wasn't full of teeth.

"We came through the western adit eleven days ago," Dree said. "Company records said the tunnel was clear. Company records were thirty years old and written by optimists. We got two miles in and the mountain came down ahead of us. Then it came down behind us, smaller, near the ventilation gallery—we dug through that one, lost most of our blasting powder doing it, which is why we couldn't touch this one." He nodded at the collapse. "So we've been sitting here. Waiting for the company to send word. Or rescue. Or anything."

"And?"

"And nothing comes." He spread his hands. "You've seen the weather coming up the coast. So have we, on the receiver we carried in. When the monsoon makes landfall, every drainage on this side of the range runs backward into the low galleries. This tunnel floods from the bottom up. We've done the arithmetic." His eyes went to Odile, standing just past Kestrel's shoulder with her arms crossed. "Your surveyor has done the arithmetic. I can tell by her face."

Odile said nothing, which was answer enough.

"So," Dree said. "Here we are. Six men of the Meridian Railway Consolidated, sent to find a tunnel the company officially denies exists, trapped in it, freezing, waiting to drown. And here you are. The trespassers we were dispatched to locate and remove." He rubbed a hand over his face, and in the firelight Kestrel saw that it wasn't a hard face underneath. It was a tired one, a clerk's face, a man who had expected a career of ledgers and had gotten a mountain instead. "I confess I've lost track of which of us is supposed to be threatening whom."

* * *

They withdrew fifty yards down the bore to talk, because you didn't negotiate in front of your own frightened people, whichever side they were on.

"They're lying," Amos said flatly. "It's a trap. They let us get close, and then—"

"With what?" Odile cut in. "Two sick men, a boy with a stick, and no powder? Amos. Use the engineering brain you're so proud of. If they wanted to take us, they'd have needed to take us before we saw them, and they didn't, because they *can't* move. Listen to that foot. That man isn't walking anywhere."

"They're syndicate," Amos said. "That's enough."

"It was enough three weeks ago," Odile said. "Before we were freezing too."

Pip hadn't spoken since the firelight. Now the girl—because that's what Pip was, whatever the name said, a girl of maybe fifteen doing a boy's job of surviving—spoke from the shadows where she'd tucked herself, arms around her knees.

"The young one," she said. "With the pick handle. He was crying when you turned away. I saw."

Nobody answered that. The silence did its own answering.

Kestrel stood a little apart, lamp hooded, looking back up the tunnel toward the distant orange smudge of the enemy's fire, and tried to hold two truths in their hands at once, which was a skill nobody taught you and everybody needed.

Truth one: these were the people hunting them. These were the men whose notice on the cave wall had put a price on Odile's head, whose questions in Saltmere had burned the group out of the last safe town on the coast, whose employer wanted the Wreck Passage sealed and everyone who knew of it silenced. If Kestrel walked away and let the tunnel take them, every problem on this journey got simpler. Simpler was the currency Kestrel had traded in for twenty years. Simpler was the whole religion of the man who owned no address.

Truth two: the boy with the pick handle was crying. And the man with the ruined foot had wrapped it with the same kind of care Kestrel's father had taught them to wrap a journal bearing—tight enough to hold, loose enough to live—and somewhere behind those facts was a truth Kestrel had been dodging since Saltmere: the syndicate was not a monster. It was a building, and buildings were full of rooms, and the rooms were full of men, and most of the men were just following the corridor they'd been pointed down, carrying out orders written by people who would never stand in a flooded tunnel and do the arithmetic themselves.

Dree had said *your father*. Not the company's phrasing. A man's phrasing.

"You're going to talk to them," Amos said. It wasn't a question.

"I'm going to look at the collapse first," Kestrel said. "I don't negotiate with anyone until I know what we're negotiating *about*. Maybe they're lying about being stuck. Maybe the fall is passable and they're sitting on the far side of it waiting for us to be fools."

"And if it's true? If they're truly sealed in?"

Kestrel thought about their father's handwriting on the cave walls, sixty miles back and forty years gone. Thought about a man who had mapped lines nobody rode, for reasons nobody thanked him for, and who had vanished into a mountain with all his reasons still in his pockets.

"Then we'll know what kind of people we are," Kestrel said, "before we decide what to do about it."

* * *

The collapse was worse than honest work and better than hopeless, which was the narrow band where Kestrel did their best living.

The fall had come off the eastern wall—a slab the size of a chapel, riding a fault line, bringing half the rib down with it. But it had come down *forward*, into the bore, and the crown above it still held arch. Kestrel played the lamp along it for a long time, reading the rock the way Odile read maps and Amos read couplings: where the pressure wanted to go, where it could be persuaded to go instead, where the mountain had already made up its mind.

"Timber and labor," Odile said beside her. Kestrel hadn't heard her come up; the woman moved quietly for a person who talked so much. "Three days with enough timber. Five without. We have neither."

"They might have timber."

"They might have *nothing*, Kestrel. That's rather my point."

Below them, at the toe of the fall, the air moved. Cold, wet, tasting of iron and deep stone—but moving. Which meant the tunnel wasn't fully choked. Which meant that somewhere beyond the slab, the mountain was breathing through some other throat, and if air could pass, then given time and timber and stubbornness, so could everything else.

Including water. Kestrel kept that thought to themselves.

They walked back toward the fire, and Dree rose to meet them, and the standoff resumed—but changed now, the way a thing changes when both sides have measured the same wall.

"Well?" Dree said.

"It's real," Kestrel said. "You're sealed in. The crown will hold if nobody's foolish, but you're not digging through that with pick handles and hope. You need timber, shoring, and somebody who knows what they're doing. You have none of the three."

"And you do."

"I have a surveyor who can design the shoring in her sleep. I have a brakeman who's forgotten more about load-bearing than your whole division ever learned. I have—" Kestrel paused, and decided against counting Pip as an asset in front of armed men. "I have hands."

Dree looked at Kestrel for a long moment. In the firelight his face did something complicated—hope and suspicion wrestling in a small room, neither winning.

"What do you want?" he asked finally. "Because I know what my orders say. I know what the company wants done with people who find this tunnel. And I'm telling you plainly, Lockridge, so there's no misunderstanding later: I am not authorized to promise you anything except that I'll write down exactly what happens here, and I'll sign it."

"That," Kestrel said slowly, "might be the most honest thing anyone from Meridian has ever said to me."

"It's not honesty. It's exhaustion." Dree almost smiled. "Ask your terms anyway. My men are dying by inches and the water is coming, and I find my principles have developed holes."

Kestrel looked past him—at the boy with the pick handle, who had set it down at some point and didn't seem to have noticed; at the man with the ruined foot, watching with the flat patience of the badly hurt; at the one cradling the transit, who still hadn't looked up, and who now, quietly, without raising his head, said:

"He's fair, Mr. Dree. The father was fair. I worked a season under him on the coast spur, before." A pause. "Before everything."

The tunnel was very quiet. Somewhere far off, water dripped with the regularity of a metronome counting down something none of them wanted named.

Amos had gone stiff as a tie-plate. Kestrel felt it more than saw it—the old man beside them, suddenly a stranger again, wearing an expression Kestrel couldn't read in the bad light and didn't try to.

"Terms," Kestrel said, dragging their eyes back to Dree. "Tomorrow. After I've slept on it, and after you've told me everything your company knows about Elias Lockridge. Starting with why you were sent to find his tunnel, and ending with what happened to him."

Dree's face went carefully blank—the blankness of a man deciding how much of the truth he could afford.

"That," he said, "may take longer than the shoring."

"Then we'd best start early."

And behind Dree, unnoticed by nearly everyone, the young one with the pick handle picked it back up—not as a weapon, Kestrel noticed, but the way a child holds a railing—and asked, in a voice cracked from disuse:

"Sir? Is it true they came up from the sea cave? Is it true there's a way through that the old surveys missed?"

Dree didn't answer. But his silence, Kestrel thought, had a shape to it. And the shape was *yes*.
