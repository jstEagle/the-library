## 15. Terms in the Dark

The dark in the tunnel had a texture to it, Odile thought. Not the clean black of a closed room but something granular, alive, pressing against the lantern light like silt against a lamp glass. She had spent eleven years of her life making darkness legible—reducing mountains to contour lines, reducing the unknowable interior of the earth to numbers a clerk could file—and here was the thing her maps had never once admitted: that the earth did not care to be legible. That it could close its throat whenever it pleased.

The collapse lay forty feet ahead of them, a spill of shattered rock and splintered timber where the old roof had let go sometime in the decades since the last train passed. It filled the bore from wall to wall, rising toward the crown of the tunnel in an untidy heap. Beyond it—somewhere beyond it—the passage continued toward the summit and the far valley and everything they had crossed half a range to reach.

And between Odile's little group and that heap of stone stood six men of the Meridian Railway Consolidated, holding three lanterns, two rifles, and one very cold expression.

Kestrel Lockridge stood at the front of their own party with the particular stillness Odile had learned to read over the past weeks of hard travel. It was not calm. It was arithmetic. Kestrel's whole body became a ledger when danger came near—assets on one side, liabilities on the other, and every living soul entered as a line item.

"Say it again," said the syndicate man. Halloway. He had a voice like gravel poured slowly, and he kept his rifle angled down at the ballast, which Odile noted was either courtesy or calculation. "Slowly."

"Shared labor," Kestrel said. "You want through this collapse same as we do. You've been digging at it for—" they glanced at the syndicate camp, the bedrolls, the empty tins stacked against the wall, "—four days? Five? And you've moved maybe a yard of rock. Because you're digging blind. No survey. No sense of how the mass sits, what's load-bearing, what's loose. Every hour you spend moving the wrong stone is an hour you might be burying yourselves."

Halloway's jaw worked. Behind him, his men shifted. They were younger than their leader, most of them, and they had the look of men who had signed on for escort work and found themselves entombed instead. One of them—a boy with chapped lips and frost-nipped ears, the youngest of them—kept glancing back into the deeper dark behind the collapse, as though the mountain might exhale.

"You're the Vasseur woman," Halloway said, turning his head slightly without taking his eyes off Kestrel. "The one they cashiered out of the Coast Division."

Odile felt the words land like a hand on the back of her neck. "I'm a surveyor," she said. "What I am besides that is none of Meridian's business, since Meridian made certain it wasn't anymore."

"They say you falsified a report."

"They say a great many things." She lifted her chin. In the lantern light she could see the syndicate men properly now: hollow-cheeked, wet-booted, the youngest one shivering in a coat meant for a bigger man. Men following orders, she thought, and freezing like everyone else. The thought did not make her trust them. It only made her understand them, which was more dangerous. "Here is what I know, Mr. Halloway. That mass above your heads has been settling for thirty years, and every pickaxe blow you strike redistributes the load. You are not clearing a tunnel. You are negotiating with a landslide, and so far you have been losing."

One of the younger agents muttered something. Another elbowed him quiet.

"And your offer," Halloway said, "is that we dig together. Your people and mine. And then?"

"Then everyone walks through. Everyone keeps quiet about everyone else. We were never here. You were never here. The Consolidated gets no report of trespassers, because there were no trespassers, because this tunnel does not exist. Isn't that how your company prefers its history?"

Something flickered across Halloway's face—not amusement exactly, but the ghost of it, the way a dead line still carries a faint hum if you put your ear to the rail.

"And if we take your labor and then keep your equipment and your lives anyway?" he asked. "Six of us. Four of you, and one of those a cripple on a stretcher."

From the shadows behind Kestrel came a dry voice. Amos Brant had insisted on being carried forward for the parley, propped on the stretcher they'd lashed from a handcar plank and two pack frames, his splinted leg stretched before him like cargo. His face was gray with pain and he spoke anyway, slow as syrup, each word placed like a chock under a wheel.

"Mister Halloway," he said, "you ever ride a brake shoe down a four-percent grade in the rain?"

"I never rode anything."

"No," Amos agreed. "I could tell by the question." He shifted, winced, went on. "I rode the Summit Line for nineteen years. Ran these very miles, boy, before you were weaned. There is a ventilation shaft a mile back the way you came, and another ahead past the collapse, and I know where every one of them daylights on the mountain, because I helped blast half of them. You put hands on us, and you'll wish you'd died honest in the rockfall instead."

It was mostly bluff. Odile knew it was mostly bluff; Amos could barely stand, and the shafts he named were guesses dressed in authority. But Halloway couldn't know that, and neither could his men, and in the dark a bluff weighed the same as a fact.

Silence held for a long moment. Somewhere far off in the mountain, water dripped with the patience of geology.

"The terms," Halloway said finally. "Lay them out proper."

* * *

They negotiated by lantern light, sitting on overturned crates and ballast stones like delegates at some absurd underground congress. Odile laid it out because no one else could: the terms of physics, which were less negotiable than any contract.

"The collapse is roughly forty feet of fill, mixed rock and old timber, mounded against the north wall—which means the south haunch is carrying most of the load, and that's where we work. Not from the top. From the bottom and the south side both, cutting a gallery through, timbering as we go. You have timbers?" She nodded at their camp. "You must. You've been propping your own workings."

"Some," Halloway admitted.

"We'll need all of them, plus whatever sound wood we can tear from the old lining farther back. The gallery wants to be five feet wide, seven high—enough for a man with a pack, nothing more. Narrow is strong. Every foot of width you add doubles the roof span and quadruples your risk."

"And who directs the work?" Halloway asked. His eyes went to Odile. "You?"

"I engineer the shoring. I set the timber, I call the sequence, I decide when a section is safe and when it isn't. When I say stop, everyone stops—including you. Including your rifles." She let that sit. "But the direction of the dig itself, the reading of the ground—that belongs to Amos Brant. He knows this tunnel better than any man alive, and he knows rock, having been under it his whole working life. He directs from the stretcher. He doesn't move; his leg is broken and we haven't the means to do more than splint it. But his eyes and his mouth work fine."

Amos gave a wheezing laugh. "First job I've held in twenty years where the company carries me to work."

Halloway looked at his own men. Odile watched him do the arithmetic she'd already done: four days of futile, dangerous digging against a chance of actually getting through; the reward money waiting somewhere beyond the mountain versus the very real possibility of dying short of it. Syndicates paid widows badly, if at all. She'd read enough of their internal correspondence, before they'd burned her career for knowing too much, to know how the Consolidated valued a field agent's life: as a line item, depreciating.

"Labor shared," Halloway said at last. "Passage shared. Silence both ways." He spat to one side, sealing it in the manner of men who trusted spit more than signatures. "But my men keep their weapons."

"And we keep ours," Kestrel said evenly, "and nobody points anything at anybody unless they mean to die shortly after. Agreed?"

Halloway hesitated, then nodded once.

"One more term." Odile raised a finger. "The boy works topside only. No gallery work."

"He's yours?"

"He's ours," she said, and heard how it sounded, and did not correct it.

Pip, hovering at the edge of the lantern light with a crowbar nearly as tall as himself, opened his mouth to protest. Odile caught his eye and shook her head, a small precise motion, and to her mild surprise he closed his mouth again. The boy was learning. They all were.

* * *

The work began within the hour, because the mountain did not grant extensions.

Odile established the gallery mouth first, marking the cut lines in chalk on the collapsed face while Amos, stretchered up onto a flat crate within shouting distance, talked her through what he could see and she could feel. She'd forgotten, in the years since her disgrace, how much she loved this—the conversation between a mind and a mass of stone, the way the mountain answered questions if you asked them correctly. A tap of the bar here: hollow, dangerous, a slab hanging on habit. A probe there: solid, married to its neighbors, trustworthy. The collapse was not chaos. It was a sentence written in a language she could read, and it said: *enter low, enter narrow, and do not wake me.*

"South haunch first," Amos called. "Cut the toe back two feet, then set your first frame before you go another inch. Timber's no good after the rock comes down, girl. It's only good before."

"I know, old man."

"Knowing and doing are different animals. Knowing pulls the plow; doing holds it."

She almost smiled. Almost. Her hands were busy.

The gallery grew by inches. Kestrel worked beside her, tireless, hauling broken stone back along the bore in a canvas sheet, their breathing steady as a metronome. The syndicate men worked too—Halloway had detailed three of them to the dig and posted two at the rear with lanterns and rifles, which everyone pretended was security and everyone understood was fear. The youngest one, the chapped-lipped boy whose name turned out to be Ellery, proved unexpectedly useful: small enough to worm into the tight places between slabs, quick-fingered, and possessed of the particular courage of someone who had not yet learned what he should be afraid of.

"Careful there," Odile told him, as he reached past her chalk line toward a wedge of stone that looked invitingly loose. "That one's holding up its neighbor. Pull it and you pull the argument down on both of you."

Ellery withdrew his hand as if the rock had bitten it. "How can you tell?"

"Thirty years of asking rocks questions and getting answers. Most of them rude."

He grinned at her—grinned, in the middle of a mountain's throat—and Odile found herself unsettled by it. He was somebody's son. Somebody, somewhere, had taught him to grin like that, and here he was spending it on the Consolidated's errand, wedged into a collapsing tunnel for wages that would buy his mother a decent coat.

She put the thought away. Sentiment in a gallery got people killed. But it stayed put away the way a live coal stays lit under ash.

By what Odile judged to be late afternoon—they had lost the sun days ago and reckoned time now by hunger and lantern oil—the first frame stood. Two legs and a cap beam, hemlock salvaged from the old lining, wedged and keyed until Odile could lean her full weight against it and feel it answer like a living thing. Seven feet of gallery open behind it. Thirty-three to go.

"Frame's true," she called to Amos.

"Course it is. I supervised." But his voice had gone thinner through the day. She glanced back and saw Pip crouched beside the stretcher, feeding the old brakeman water from their dented flask, one small hand resting on the man's shoulder as though holding him to the world. Amos's color was wrong. The break had been two days ago and the leg was angry about it; Odile had seen the shine of fever starting in his eyes when he thought no one was looking.

They could not afford it. None of them could afford anything, least of all sickness, and yet here it was, itemized on the ledger with everything else.

* * *

They rested in shifts, and the shifts were where the watching happened.

Odile took the middle watch, wrapped in her coat against a chill that lived in the tunnel like a tenant, and used the hours to study the enemy. Halloway slept like a man accustomed to sleeping anywhere and trusting nothing: on his side, boots on, rifle in the crook of his arm. The two sentries changed without speaking. Ellery slept curled near the gallery mouth, closest to the work, as though the dig itself were warmer than the company.

Kestrel sat awake across the bore, sharpening a pry bar's edge with a whetstone, the small rasping sound filling the dark. After a while they rose and came to sit beside her, uninvited, which by now was its own kind of invitation.

"You saw it too," Kestrel said quietly. Not a question.

"Halloway's counting our tools when he thinks we're asleep. And the big one—Voss, the one with the scar—he walked the gallery twice today measuring it with his stride. He's thinking about whether five feet wide fits an ambush."

"It does," Kestrel said. "Barely."

"So we assume the truce lasts exactly as long as the rock does. The moment we're through, the terms expire." Odile turned her lantern down to a coal-glow. "Unless."

"Unless?"

"Unless we become worth more to them through than stopped. Think like a clerk, Kestrel. Halloway reports the tunnel sealed, the trespassers scattered or dead, and collects whatever a sealed report collects. Or Halloway reports the tunnel *open*, traversed, and full of witnesses who will talk unless treated generously—and suddenly silence is a commodity he has to buy rather than enforce." She tapped her temple. "We hold shares in the same secret. That's the only collateral either of us has."

Kestrel was quiet a long moment. The whetstone had gone still. "You've thought about betrayal a great deal."

"I was married to the railway for eleven years, darling. Same thing."

A breath of laughter, quickly smothered. Then Kestrel said, "Amos is worse tonight."

"I know."

"Fever?"

"Starting. If it climbs much further he stops directing and starts raving, and then we lose the only man who knows where the shafts daylight." She rubbed her eyes with thumb and forefinger. "So we dig faster. That's the whole strategy. Everything always comes down to digging faster than the trouble."

Kestrel looked back toward the stretcher, where Pip had appointed himself night nurse and lay curled against Amos's good side like a cat against a stove. "And if the trouble digs faster than us?"

Odile did not answer immediately. Far down the bore, behind them, toward the portal and the weather outside, she thought she heard something that might have been wind—or might have been water, finding the lower grades, running the old ditch lines, doing what water always did. Doing what the monsoon had promised to do.

"Then we learn to swim," she said.

* * *

Dawn, if such a word applied two miles inside a mountain, brought the second shift of work, and with it the first crisis of the alliance.

Voss, the scarred syndicate man, struck a bad blow. Odile saw it happen the way one sees an accident begin—in perfect, useless detail. He was cutting at the gallery face where the chalk line curved, and he swung the pick high and hard, and the sound it made was wrong: not the dull knock of bonded stone but a bright, ringing *crack*, the sound of a tooth breaking.

"Down!" Odile shouted. "Everyone out of the gallery—*now!*"

They scrambled. The slab came down two seconds later, a half-ton of ceiling rock that would have made Ellery a footnote, slamming into the fresh-cut floor and splitting the cap beam of her first frame like a knuckle. Dust rolled out in a brown wave. When it cleared, the gallery mouth was choked to half its height and the frame leaned drunkenly, one leg kicked out from under it.

Nobody was hurt. Everybody was shouting.

"You said it was marked!" Voss was on his feet, dust-white, pointing at the chalk. "You said inside the line was safe!"

"Inside the line was safe for a *careful* man!" Odile advanced on him, and she was not a large woman but she had spent a decade giving orders to crews of fifty and it showed in her spine. "You swung high where the line said low. You put your whole shoulder into a hanging slab. I don't set lines to decorate the rock, I set them because the rock reads differently above them, and if you ever—" she jabbed a finger at his chest, "—*ever* swing above my chalk again, I will walk out of this tunnel, and you and your rifles can negotiate with the mountain on your own. Do you understand me?"

For a moment she thought he would swing at her. His fists were half-closed, his jaw bunched, and behind him his comrades watched with the flat attention of men deciding whose side history would record them on.

It was Halloway who ended it. He crossed the camp in four strides and put one hand on Voss's shoulder—not gently, not violently, just with weight. "The lady sets the lines," he said. "Company rules. You follow the engineer or you explain to me why the tunnel's still blocked." A pause. "Apologize."

Voss looked at Odile. Something moved behind the scar and the anger—something tired, something that remembered being a man who built things instead of broke them. "Apologies," he said, gravel-voiced. "Won't happen again."

"See that it doesn't." She turned away before he could see what the near-miss had cost her, and knelt at the broken frame, and began assessing.

Amos's voice floated from the stretcher, hoarse but level. "Reset the leg on the left. Key it with that flat stone, the one by your knee. Cap beam's cracked but it'll carry if you sister it—there's sound timber forty feet back, third row of the old lining, I marked it in '09 and I'll wager it's still there." A cough. "And Vasseur."

"Yes, old man?"

"Good shout. Half a second slower and we'd be short one boy." He didn't say which boy. He didn't need to. Odile looked at Ellery, dust-caked and shaking, being steadied by Pip of all people—Pip with a hand on the older boy's arm, saying something too quiet to hear, and Ellery nodding—and felt the strange vertigo of watching enemies' children comfort each other in the dark.

The alliance was a rope bridge, she thought. Every plank rotten, every anchor doubtful, and the river rising underneath. But rope bridges carried people across. That was the entire trick of them: they held precisely as long as everyone stepped carefully, and everyone stepped carefully precisely because they knew it might not hold.

By midday the frame was reset, sistered, stronger than before. By evening the gallery stood at fourteen feet. Twenty-six remained.

It was during the last shift of that day, while Odile chalked new cut lines by lantern light, that Ellery came to her—sideways, apologetic, a boy carrying a message he hated.

"Beg pardon, ma'am. Mr. Halloway says—says to tell you the water's come up in the lower bore. Back toward the portal. Ankle-deep this morning." He swallowed. "It weren't ankle-deep yesterday."

Odile straightened slowly. Behind her, the mountain dripped its patient count. Somewhere below them, in the dark under the dark, the monsoon was climbing the grades, filling the ditches, testing every culvert, looking—as water always looked—for the way through.

Twenty-six feet of rock between them and the summit. And now, at their backs, a rising sea with a timetable of its own.

She picked up the chalk and drew the next line, and made it low.
