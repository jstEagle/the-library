## 16. Water Finds a Way

Amos Brant had spent forty years listening to trains, and in all that time he had never heard anything that sounded like the mountain dying.

It came up through the rock first — a low, wet groan, the sound of a joint being wrenched slowly apart — and then, fainter, from somewhere below and behind, a hiss like a thousand small boilers losing pressure all at once. He lay on his stretcher against the tunnel wall, hip packed in ice-cold mud because there was no ice, and he listened the way he'd once listened for a dragging brake shoe two miles back in the dark, and he knew what the mountain was telling them.

Water. Coming up the line behind them.

"You hear that?" he said.

Odile Vasseur crouched over her lamp at the edge of the shoring gallery, chalk in one hand and a broken surveyor's rule in the other, and she didn't look up. "I've been hearing it for an hour."

"That's not the wind."

"No," she said. "It's not the wind." She drew one last mark on the rock, straightened, and turned to face the long dark of the tunnel — the collapsed section behind them, the two miles of dead line ahead, the ventilation shaft they'd already scouted and found too narrow to take more than one body at a time without rope and nerve. "It's the monsoon. It's made landfall. And it's found us."

* * *

They had been in the mountain three days.

Three days since the bargain struck in the dark with Halloway's frozen syndicate men — shared labor for shared passage, shovels and timbers traded back and forth like currency between people who did not trust each other further than the length of a shovel handle. Three days of Odile's shoring going up timber by timber, of Amos lying on his back directing the bracing of a collapse he could not walk to, his voice carrying down the gallery the way a brakeman's voice once carried down the length of a freight train. Three days of Kestrel Lockridge working beside the syndicate men in silence, saying nothing, forgiving nothing, but hauling timber with the rest of them because the mountain did not care who had signed which letter.

And now the mountain was sending its bill.

Amos had felt it coming in his hip before he'd heard it in the rock — the old break aching the way it ached before hard frost, before the kind of weather that killed men who thought they were already through the worst of it. He'd broken it thirty years ago on the Meridian's Coast Division, riding the rear platform of a cut of hoppers down the Vell grade, and it had never once lied to him about weather. It was lying to him about nothing now.

"Get the gear up," he called, and his voice cracked on the second word, and he hated that. "Everything you want to keep, get it up off the floor. Now."

Pip came running out of the side gallery with the boy's arms full of rope coils, eyes wide. "How high's it going to come?"

"Son, I've seen a culvert the size of a church door fill to the crown in eleven minutes." Amos pushed himself up on one elbow, and his hip screamed, and he let it scream because the pain kept him honest. "This is a tunnel two miles long with a grade falling back the way we came. The water doesn't have to be deep. It just has to be faster than we are."

Kestrel was already moving. That was the thing about Lockridge that Amos kept having to relearn — the stillness, the watchfulness, the way they cataloged every stranger and every exit like a man memorizing doors in a house he intended to burn — and then, when the moment came, no hesitation at all. Kestrel had the packs stacked and the map — the map, the father's map, wrapped in oilcloth — slung high across their back before Amos finished his sentence.

"How far to the shaft?" Kestrel asked Odile.

"Half a mile. Maybe more, if the gallery's flooded."

"And the shaft itself?"

"Vertical, sixty feet, then a crawl. One at a time. With the rope."

Everyone looked at the rope. There was one rope. A hundred and twenty feet of half-inch hemp that Amos himself had spliced back together at the trestle over the Vell, back when the crossing had cost him his hip and very nearly the rest of him. It was a good rope. It was the only rope. And there were nine of them — four of the cave party, and five of Halloway's men, if you counted the youngest, a boy named Teller with a chapped face and a way of looking at Amos like Amos was a man in a story his grandmother had told him.

Halloway was looking at the rope too. Amos saw it. He'd spent forty years watching men decide things before they knew they'd decided them — watching a hogger's hand drift toward the throttle, watching a tramp's weight shift on the ladder of a moving car — and he watched it now. Halloway's eyes went to the rope, then to the water sounds rising from behind, then to his own men, counting them the way Amos had counted them a hundred times in the last three days. Five against four. One rope.

"Mr. Halloway," Amos said, pleasantly, into the silence. "You're doing arithmetic."

Halloway didn't deny it. That was the thing about the man — he had a kind of battered honesty in him, the honesty of someone who had stopped pretending to himself somewhere back on the far side of the mountains. "I'm doing what you'd do," Halloway said. "Nine people. One rope. Sixty feet of shaft. Somebody's last."

"Reckon I have done it," Amos said. "Reckon I'm the only one here who has." He swung his legs off the stretcher, and the pain went through him like a coupler slamming home, and he stood up anyway, because there were things you did standing. "Thirty years ago I rode the brakes for the Meridian. Coast Division. You know what that was like — forty cars of ore down the Vell grade and nothing between you and God's judgment but a brake wheel and your own two arms."

"We know the division," one of Halloway's men said quietly.

"Then you know the name Lockridge."

The word went through the group of them like current through a rail. Halloway's jaw tightened. Kestrel, at the far end of the gallery, went very still — the stillness of a held breath.

"Edmund Lockridge was the best engineer that division ever ran," Amos said. "And one night in October of — of the year I don't like to say — he took a cut of empties down the grade in weather no man should have been out in, because the dispatcher told him there was a passenger special behind him and the special had lost her brakes on the summit side, and if Edmund didn't get down and hold the junction, that special was coming through Hollow Fork at speed with a hundred souls aboard." Amos's voice was steady. His hands were not. He let them shake. "I was the brakeman on Edmund's train. And I was drunk. And when he called for the hand brakes on the rear platform, I was sitting in the caboose stove trying to get warm, and I didn't hear him, and by the time I heard him it was two cars too late, and the train came into the junction four minutes after the special had already gone through — four minutes, and it didn't matter, it never mattered — but Edmund Lockridge was running her so hard to make those four minutes that she jumped the rails on the curve below the junction, and he went into the Vell with her, and they never found enough of him to bury."

The water hissed in the dark behind them. Nobody moved.

"They said it was his fault," Amos said. "The inquiry. They said he was running too fast for the conditions. They said it because saying that kept the dispatcher clean, and the dispatcher was the syndicate's cousin, and the syndicate has been cleaning up after itself for a hundred years. And I let them say it. I stood in that inquiry room and I kept my mouth shut about where I'd been sitting, because I was afraid of losing my job, and I have been paying for that mouth for thirty years, and I will still be paying for it when the water comes through this tunnel and washes what's left of me out to the sea." He looked at Halloway, and then at the rope, and then at all of them. "So here's my arithmetic, Mr. Halloway. Edmund Lockridge gave four minutes and his life for a hundred people he never met. The least I can do with what's left of me is see that nobody in this tunnel dies fighting over a rope."

* * *

The water came faster than any of them wanted to say out loud.

They could hear it now without trying — a rushing, swallowing sound, the sound of a river being born in the dark — and the air in the tunnel had gone cold and wet and tasted of stone. Odile had the shaft rigged in forty minutes: rope anchored to a sound timber she'd set into a natural fissure, a loop tied every arm's length, a system for hauling the packs up first and the injured second and everyone else in the order that kept the strongest at the bottom to hold the line.

"Order of ascent," she said, chalk in hand, treating it like a survey because treating it like a survey was the only way her hands stayed steady. "Packs first. Then Amos — he gets hauled, he doesn't climb. Then Teller—"

"The hell you say," said one of Halloway's men, a big slab-faced fellow called Dree. "Our men go last. That's the deal. You go first, we hold the rope, and we'll see who's standing when it's done."

"And if you don't hold the rope?" Odile said. "If you climb up and cut it loose once you're at the top?"

Dree didn't answer. The silence answered for him, and everyone heard it.

It was Teller — the youngest, the chapped-faced boy who had spent three days hauling timber beside them without complaint — who broke it. "We're not doing that," Teller said. His voice shook. "Mr. Halloway. Tell him."

Halloway looked at his men. He looked at the rope. He looked, for one long moment, at Kestrel Lockridge, and whatever passed between the two of them in that dark gallery, Amos thought, was older than this tunnel and would outlast it.

"We hold the rope," Halloway said. "All of us go up on one line or none of us do. That's the terms. That was always the terms."

"And if the water's faster than the line?" Dree said. "Somebody's last, Halloway. Somebody's always last."

"Then the last man gets my place in the order," Amos said.

They all turned to him.

"I'm the heaviest thing in this tunnel that isn't timber," Amos said. "I'm the slowest, I'm the most broken, and I've had thirty years more of life than that boy there." He nodded at Teller. "I go last. I hold the bottom of the rope while the rest of you climb, and I come up when it's empty, and if the water takes me, it takes the one man here who's already been dead since October of the year I don't like to say."

"You're not dying in here," Kestrel said.

It was the first thing Lockridge had said to him directly in days — since Hollow Fork, since the map, since the walking out alone. Amos looked at the young man — because that's what Kestrel was, whatever the face said; young, under all that iron — and he saw Edmund in the set of the jaw, and it hurt worse than the hip.

"Your father said something like that once," Amos said quietly. "Standing on the rear platform with the rain coming sideways and the grade falling away under us. I said, *Edmund, we're not going to make it.* And he said, *Amos, we're not dead yet, so we're making it.*" He managed something that was almost a smile. "So we're making it. All of us. Now get up that rope."

* * *

The packs went up first. Then Odile went up the line like she'd been born to it, hand over hand, quick and exact, and her voice came down the shaft telling them the top was clear. Pip went next, light as a cat. Then two of Halloway's men. Then Kestrel, who paused at the bottom of the rope with one hand on it and looked back down the tunnel — down the long black throat of the dead line, toward the sound of the water — and Amos saw the young man listening for something in it, some signature, some pattern, the way a good engineer listens to a locomotive.

"It's going to hit the gallery before we're clear," Kestrel said. Not a question.

"Likely," Amos said.

"Then we don't stop for anything. Not for the packs if they foul the line. Not for anything." Kestrel looked at him a moment longer. "You hold the bottom. You come up last. And when you get to the top, Amos Brant, you and I are going to finish a conversation."

"Yes, sir," Amos said, and meant it, and Kestrel went up the rope into the dark.

Teller climbed. Dree climbed, red-faced and silent, not looking at anyone. Halloway went up second to last, and at the bottom of the shaft, holding the rope's tail, Amos felt the line go light and knew he was alone in the tunnel with the water.

He could see it now. Far down the black, a gray gleam, a moving shine — the flood front coming up the grade, knee-high and climbing, carrying sticks and silt and one wheel of a handcar, spinning. The sound of it filled the whole world. It sounded, Amos thought, like the sea had finally come inland to collect a debt.

He put his hands on the rope and climbed.

Sixty feet. He'd climbed worse — once, at Ashcombe, up a moving grain train in the rain, hand over hand over forty cars — but that was thirty years and two good legs ago. His hip tore at every pull. The rope burned his palms. The water rose beneath him, and he felt it take his boots, then his knees, cold as a grave and moving like a living thing, and above him Odile's voice came down the shaft — *climb, old man, climb* — and he climbed.

The water took his waist as his hand found the lip of the shaft. Fingers closed on his collar — Kestrel, and Teller, and Halloway, all of them hauling together — and they dragged him out onto the stone like a landed fish, and below them the flood came up the shaft after them, rising, rising, and stopped six feet down as the air in the dead line compressed and held it, groaning, like a held breath.

They lay on the stone in the crawl's dim lamplight, nine people, gasping, dripping, alive. Every pack was lost. The map was safe on Kestrel's back. The rope hung down the shaft, its tail in the flood, and no one had cut it, and no one had fought over it, and Amos Brant lay on his back on cold stone and laughed until his ribs hurt, because after thirty years it was finally, finally paid — not forgiven, he knew better than to ask for forgiven, but paid, the debt settled in the only currency the mountain accepted.

Then the laugh died in his throat.

Because from up the crawl — from ahead, from the direction of the surface, from the high shoulder of the range they had been clawing toward for two hundred miles — came a sound that did not belong to water.

Voices.

And a lantern, swinging down the shaft toward them, held by a man they had never seen before — a man in a railway coat with a Meridian button, who looked down at the nine of them huddled in the dark and said, in a voice that carried no apology at all:

"You're a long way from anywhere. And you're about to be a long way further — the storm's taken the portal. There's no way out but through."
