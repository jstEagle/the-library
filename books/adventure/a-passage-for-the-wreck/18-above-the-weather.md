## 18. Above the Weather

The shaft spat them out into a world that did not believe in the storm.

Pip came up last, hauled by the wrist through the last yard of iron rungs, and lay flat on stone that was dry—actually dry, dust-dry, warm from some buried heat of the mountain—and stared straight up at a sky so blue it looked painted on. Below them, muffled by two thousand feet of rock, the monsoon went on with its work. He could feel it as a tremor in the ground, a low animal grumble, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"Off the rim," Kestrel said hoarsely. "Move off the rim."

Pip rolled sideways instead of standing, crab-walking on his elbows until his boots found grass. Actual grass, short and wind-flattened, growing in the seams of an old roadbed. He hadn't seen living green in four days. It seemed obscene somehow, after the dark and the water and the boy who had drowned holding a line so the rest of them could climb.

He didn't look at the shaft mouth. He made himself not look at it.

They had come up in a row, one after another, like things being born: Odile first, then Halloway, then the two surviving syndicate men—one of whom had simply sat down at the top of the ladder and put his face in his hands and not moved since—then Amos on the litter they'd rigged from a section of shoring plank and two pack straps, then Kestrel, then Pip. Nine had gone into the tunnel below the collapse. Six had come out. One of those six was barely a man anymore, just a wet gray shape that shook.

Pip counted them twice anyway. It was a habit now. Counting was how you knew you were still real.

Kestrel stood at the edge of the shelf with their back to him, hands on hips, looking down-slope at where the weather lived. From up here the storm was a country of its own—a vast gray-white territory filling every valley to the west, boiling slowly against the mountainsides, shot through with flickers of lightning that lit it from inside like a lantern behind cloth. Rain fell out of it in visible curtains, whole moving walls of water marching across the foothills they'd crossed three days ago. Somewhere under all that were the flooded passes, the drowned lower tunnel, the handcar they'd left on the dead line, the whole drowned geography of everything they'd survived.

Above the storm line, the air was thin and clean and cold enough to bite. The sun stood high and indifferent over all of it.

"We're above it," Pip said, mostly to hear his own voice. "We're actually above the weather."

"For tonight." Kestrel didn't turn around. Their voice was scraped raw from smoke and shouting and the taste of the tunnel; everyone's was. "Weather climbs too. Everything climbs eventually."

* * *

The shelf they'd emerged onto was an old construction camp, or what remained of one. Someone had built here once—forty years ago, sixty—when the summit tunnel was a promise instead of a grave. There were foundation stones laid out in rectangles, half-swallowed by turf. A rusted stove pipe stood upright out of a mound of rubble like a periscope. And along the contour of the slope, half-hidden in the grass, ran the graded bed of the old summit spur, smooth and level and going nowhere, a road built for trains that had never been allowed to run.

Odile walked it end to end while the others collapsed, reading the ground the way she read everything, lips moving, fingers ticking off distances. She came back with her jaw set in the way that meant she'd found both good news and bad news and was deciding which to say first.

"There's a cutting two hundred yards east, sheltered on three sides. Stone walls, part of the original works. We can get a fire in it if we burn green wood and keep the smoke low—the cloud deck will hide us anyway." She paused. "And there's water. A snowmelt seep coming out of the rocks. Clean, I think. I'll check it."

"Good," Kestrel said.

"That's the good news," Odile said.

Nobody asked for the bad. Nobody had the strength. They gathered what there was to gather—Amos's litter, the packs, the single coil of rope that had come up the shaft with them, the thing that had nearly gotten them all killed and had saved them instead—and moved east along the ghost road with the sun on their backs and the storm growling below like a dog chained to the world.

* * *

The cutting was exactly what Odile had promised: a slot of sheltered stone with a floor of packed gravel, open only to the south, where the view dropped away forever. Pip helped drag Amos in on the litter and got his first proper look at the old brakeman in full light, and had to work hard to keep his face still.

Amos Brant had always been big—not tall so much as dense, a man built like freight, all beam and ballast. Now he looked deflated, emptied, his left leg splinted with survey stakes and strapping from his own coat, his face the color of the ash in a dead fire. His eyes were open, though, tracking the sky, and when Pip knelt beside him he managed the ghost of his old crooked grin.

"Well," Amos rasped. "Look at that. Blue sky. Thought I'd dreamed it."

"You didn't dream it," Pip said. "It's real. We're above it. Above the whole storm."

"Above the weather." Amo's grin widened a fraction. "Your papa used to say that. Did I ever tell you? No—course I didn't." Something moved behind his eyes, some old grief surfacing and being pushed back down. "When we'd crest a summit grade ahead of a front, he'd stand up in the cab window and shout it down the platform. *Above the weather, boys!* Like we'd outrun God himself." He coughed, a long terrible sound. "Man loved being higher than the rain."

Pip didn't know what to say to that. He never knew what to say when Amos talked about Kestrel's father, because every time he did, Pip watched Kestrel's shoulders change—watched them go rigid and far away, like a person listening to a sound from another room. So he just squeezed the old man's hand, which was the size of a dinner plate and cold as rail iron, and went to help Odile with the fire.

* * *

They burned green scrub and the dry planks of an old signal post someone had carried up the mountain forty years ago and never used. The smoke rose thin and white and was immediately shredded apart by the wind, and Odile pronounced it invisible against the cloud, and nobody argued.

Halloway sat apart, as he had sat apart since the shaft. The other two syndicate men had surrendered themselves completely—had, in fact, begun helping without being asked, hauling wood, taking turns at the water seep, doing the small obedient tasks of men whose organization had ceased to exist somewhere back in the flooding dark. But Halloway sat on a flat stone at the edge of the cutting with his ruined boots off and his blistered feet steaming in the sun, cleaning a pistol he no longer pointed at anyone, and watching Kestrel Lockridge with an expression Pip couldn't read. Not hatred. Not quite surrender either. Something more like a man doing arithmetic he didn't like the answer to.

Pip brought him a tin cup of melted snow-water because Odile told him to, and because he was trying—trying hard—to be the kind of person who could do that.

Halloway took the cup. Looked at it. Looked at Pip.

"You know what I was before this?" Halloway said suddenly. His voice was quiet, pitched under the wind. "Railroad police. Twenty-two years. Chased hoboes off boxcars. That was the whole job. Chasing hungry men off empty cars." He turned the cup in his fingers. "Then Meridian bought the line and the job got bigger. Bigger and bigger. Until one day I'm climbing a mountain to seal a hole in the ground and drown strangers in the dark, and I can't remember which order the orders came in. Which ones I agreed to and which ones just... arrived."

Pip stood there holding nothing, saying nothing.

"The boy," Halloway said. "The young one. Rennie. He held that line because I told him to hold the line." He drank the water in one long pull, all of it, as if it might be the last clean thing he'd ever be given. "Tell your people I know what that costs. That's all. Just tell them I know."

Pip walked back to the fire with the empty cup and didn't tell anyone anything, because he didn't yet know if it was a confession or a trick, and because his chest hurt in a place that had nothing to do with climbing.

* * *

Evening came on slow and enormous. Up here the sunset lasted an hour, the light going gold, then copper, then a bruised violet, and the sea of storm-cloud below caught all of it and burned like the surface of another planet. Pip sat on the lip of the cutting with his knees drawn up and watched it, and for a while—for almost a whole hour—he thought about nothing at all. It was the first time since Saltmere that his head had been quiet. The tunnel had taken the noise out of him. Or maybe the height had. Maybe you had to get above the weather before your own weather would lift.

Behind him, the camp sounds: Odile muttering over her reassembled instruments, correcting the map by lamplight. The two ex-syndicate men snoring already, sleeping the dead sleep of the spent. Amos breathing in long whistling pulls, asleep or pretending. Kestrel sitting alone by the fire, feeding it sticks one at a time, each one placed with the same exact care they gave to everything—timetables, knots, other people's lives.

Pip counted the camp again. Five of theirs. Three of the others. Eight alive on the roof of the range. He knew the number of the ones who weren't, too, though he kept that number somewhere he didn't look.

He got up and went and sat down next to Kestrel.

For a while neither of them spoke. The fire ticked. Far below, lightning walked silently through the cloud-sea, lighting it up in great soft pulses, thunder arriving minutes late like news from a distant war.

"I used to think," Pip said, "that if I ever got out—if I ever got anywhere—I'd know what to do next. You know? Like the getting-out was the hard part, and after that it'd be obvious." He pulled his jacket tighter. The cold up here was honest cold, dry and clean, nothing like the wet killing cold of the tunnel. "But I don't. Know what's next. I keep trying to picture tomorrow and it's just—blank. White. Like fresh snow."

Kestrel fed a stick to the fire. Said nothing. Kestrel was very good at saying nothing; Pip had learned that it wasn't the same as refusing to talk. It was more like leaving a door open.

"So I've been thinking about after," Pip said. "After the descent. After Ferrow's Landing, or wherever we end up. And I wanted to ask—" His throat tightened, which annoyed him. He was fourteen; he'd slept in a cave for a year; he'd climbed out of a drowning tunnel. He could ask one question. "Are we going to stay together?"

The stick in Kestrel's hand stopped halfway to the fire.

"Because I know how you are," Pip rushed on, before the silence could close. "I know the rules. Never sleep twice in the same town. Own nothing they can trace. Be nobody, go nowhere twice. I've heard you say it. And it kept us alive, I know it did, it's why we're sitting on top of a mountain instead of at the bottom of one. But that was when it was just—" he gestured vaguely, "—people running. And now it's not. Now it's Amos, who can't walk a descent without being carried. Now it's Odile, who'll want a drafting table and a door that locks. Now it's me. And I just—" 

He stopped. Made himself finish it.

"I don't want to learn everybody's name and then lose them at a station platform. That's all. That's the whole thing. If we're going to split up, I'd rather know now, so I can start practicing not caring."

The words came out harder than he meant them, and he flinched at his own voice, but he didn't take them back.

Kestrel set the stick down. Looked into the fire for a long time. The light moved on their face, and Pip watched them the way he'd learned to watch them over these weeks—reading the small telltales, the signals along the line. The jaw working slightly. The thumb rubbing the callus on the opposite palm, where the rope had burned it in the tunnel.

"Pip," Kestrel said finally.

"Yeah."

"When I was sixteen, my father told me the only inheritance he had to give me was a timetable. He said: *learn every line, ride every road, and never let any one place learn you back.* Because places that learn you are places that can report you. Places that can grieve you." Kestrel's voice was level, careful, the engineer's voice, the one that reported conditions on the line ahead. "I have lived by that for eleven years. It has worked. Do you understand? It has *worked*. No syndicate has ever built a file on me thicker than a single page. I am, officially, almost no one."

"But—"

"But." Kestrel picked up another stick and did not feed it to the fire. Just held it. "But a page is not nothing. And pages accumulate. And there is a ledger in Ferrow's Landing, and a branch line with rotted ties, and an old man on a stretcher who cannot run, and a surveyor who has already started drawing the repairs in her head—I've seen her doing it, she thinks nobody notices. And there's you." The stick turned over in Kestrel's fingers. "There's you, asking me to stop."

Below them the storm flashed, a whole silent country of light.

"And I cannot answer you," Kestrel said. "That's the truth. I've been trying to answer you since Saltmere, and I can't. Every rule I have says one thing. Every—" a pause, a breath, "—everything else says another. And I don't know yet which one of those is me. I genuinely don't. I've been nobody for so long that I'm not sure who shows up if I stop."

Pip nodded slowly. It wasn't the answer he wanted. But it was an answer, and it was true, and he was learning—slowly, painfully, up here above the weather—that true answers were worth more than comfortable ones.

"Okay," he said. "Then don't answer tonight."

"No?"

"Answer on the descent." Pip looked out at the burning clouds, at the far blue ridges already catching the last light, at whatever waited down there in the green. "You've got a whole mountain to decide on. That seems fair. Even timetables give you a junction eventually."

Something happened to Kestrel's face then—something small, quickly controlled, but real. Almost a smile. The closest thing to one Pip had seen since the tunnel.

"A junction," Kestrel repeated. "Where you either commit to the main line or take the branch."

"Exactly."

"All right." Kestrel finally fed the stick to the fire. "On the descent, then."

* * *

Pip woke once in the night, in the cold hour before dawn, and lay listening.

The storm was still down there, murmuring in its sleep. The fire had burned to a red eye, banked and tended. Odile slept sitting up against her instrument case. Amos breathed. And Kestrel—

Kestrel was awake. Sitting at the lip of the cutting with the map spread across their knees, weighted at the corners with stones, the old survey paper silver-pale in the starlight. Not reading it. Just looking at it, the way you look at a face.

Beyond Kestrel, down-slope, at the very edge of the firelight, Halloway was also awake, also sitting, also looking at the map—or at Kestrel, or at the space between them where the whole future of the passage hung unburned.

And far to the north, so faint Pip might have imagined it, down along the dark shoulder of the range where the syndicate's scattered remnant had fled—a light. A single point of orange, bobbing. Moving. Climbing.

Someone else was coming up the mountain.
