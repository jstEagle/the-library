## 19. Descent Into the Green

Halloway had spent eleven years learning to read men in bad light—twitches of the jaw, the way a hand drifted toward a coat pocket, the particular silence a liar kept. He had never once been taught to read weather, and now the sky was doing all the talking.

It came down in ropes. Not rain as he understood it, not the gray drizzle of the coast or the honest thunderstorms of the plains he'd crossed as a boy, but something vertical and total, a curtain drawn between him and everything he had ever been certain of. The jungle below the tunnel's mouth swallowed the five of them and gave back only sound: water on leaves, water on stone, water filling every hollow thing in the world.

He walked at the rear because no one had told him to and no one had told him not to, and that absence of instruction was itself a kind of sentence. Behind them, somewhere up in the white roar of the pass, the tunnel was drowning. Somewhere behind that, Meridian Railway Consolidated's district office was waiting for a report he would never file. He turned his collar up against the wet and tried to remember what he was for.

* * *

The first night down they sheltered in the ribs of a water tower that had been abandoned so long it had become furniture for the forest. Vines had climbed its legs like ivy on a corpse; the tank above them held six inches of standing water and a family of frogs who objected to visitors. Odile Vasseur built a fire anyway, small and shielded, because she said the alternative was fever, and fever killed more survey crews than avalanches ever had.

Halloway sat apart, the way a man sits when he hasn't been invited but hasn't been sent away. He watched the girl—the one called Pip—turn her boots over by the fire and pick leeches off with a knife blade, flicking each one into the flames with the flat efficiency of someone who had done it many times before. She caught him watching.

"You'll want to check your socks," she said. "They get in through the lace holes. You don't feel them till they're fat."

He checked his socks. There were three. He burned them too, and the girl nodded at him across the fire, once, like a man acknowledging another man's work.

Kestrel Lockridge said nothing to him at all. That was fine. Halloway had come down out of the mountains intending to arrest these people, and instead he had watched the youngest of Meridian's agents—a boy named Teller, nineteen years old, who wrote letters home to a mother in Corvane—hold a shoring line until the water took him. Halloway had heard the line go. He would hear it again tonight, and tomorrow night, and he suspected for years.

Amos Brant lay on a stretcher of lashed poles near the fire, his leg splinted, his breathing the slow laboring bellows of an old machine still running out of habit. He was awake, though. He was always awake. He caught Halloway's eye and said, without preamble:

"Teller. What was his first name?"

"Josiah." It came out of Halloway before he could decide whether to spend it.

Brant nodded slowly, looking into the fire. "I rode with a Josiah once. Good name. Gets used up too fast."

That was all. But it was the first time anyone on this side of the rope had asked Halloway a question whose answer mattered to nobody's strategy, and he found he could not sleep afterward, lying under the dripping tower legs, turning it over. *What was his first name.* Eleven years of reading men, and the old brakeman had read him in four words.

* * *

The dead line, on the far side of the range, was a different animal than it had been on the near side. On the coastward approach there had been track bed, ballast, the skeleton of a grade you could follow. Here the jungle had eaten everything. The grade existed as a suggestion—a long green rampart running downhill through the trees, its cut faces slumped into the forest, its culverts blown out and streaming. Where trestles had stood there were stumps of pilings wearing crowns of fern, and the rail itself appeared and vanished like a thought: sometimes buried, sometimes arched free of the mud in rusted curves, sometimes simply gone, sold for scrap forty years ago by some crew whose names were lost even to Odile's obsessive records.

Odile navigated by those records and by instinct, walking ahead with her notebook in an oilskin sleeve, calling out hazards in a clipped voice. "Washout, ten yards, keep to the high shoulder. Culvert's gone here—cross single file on the upstream lip." Kestrel moved beside her, and Halloway, watching them, understood that he was seeing two people speak a language he did not know. They read the ground the way he read faces. They saw failure coming in the shape of a slope.

On the third morning the grade simply ended.

Not washed out—*removed*. A whole section of hillside, perhaps eighty yards of it, had let go and slid into the valley below, taking the roadbed with it, leaving a raw brown scar steaming in the rain. Below the scar, the valley floor was a river where no river should be, brown and fast, carrying whole trees.

"We go around," Kestrel said.

"Around is two days through that," Odile said, gesturing at the jungle, which was less a place than a condition. "Or we cross the slide while it's fresh. Fresh slides firm up in about a week. After that it's mud to the waist."

"So we cross today."

"So we cross today."

Halloway looked at the scar. It was steep, featureless, and moving—not visibly, not the way water moves, but with that slow granular creep he had seen once in a mine tailings pile outside Duncarrie, three days before it ate a mule team. He opened his mouth to say something prudent and realized, with a strange vertigo, that he had no standing to say it. He was not their officer. He was not their prisoner either, though Brant had tied his wrists the first night out of the tunnel and Odile had untied them the second morning with the comment that feeding a prisoner slowed everyone down. He was cargo. Cargo does not vote.

But Kestrel looked over at him anyway. Looked at him the way you look at a tool you're deciding whether to trust.

"You've crossed bad ground," Kestrel said. Not a question.

"Tailings, scree, one icefall." Halloway heard his own voice, flat and official, and hated it. "Nothing like that."

"Rules are the same everywhere. One at a time. Don't stop. If it starts going, throw yourself downhill and swim—don't try to run uphill, the slide's faster and it panics people into it." Kestrel tested the surface with a boot, then a knee, then lay flat and spread weight across forearms, listening. Whatever was heard satisfied them. "Pip first. Lightest. Then Odile. Amos goes last with me hauling the stretcher on a line. Halloway—" A pause. The rain ticked on the leaves. "You're between Odile and me. If anyone slips, you're close enough to catch and heavy enough to anchor."

It was, Halloway understood, a test dressed as tactics. Put the hunter in the middle of the herd and see what he does when the herd starts to fall.

He crossed the slide with his heart slamming and his eyes fixed on the brown water below, and when Pip went down halfway across—one boot punching through a crust, the whole slope giving a low grinding sigh beneath them—it was Halloway's hand that shot out and closed on the girl's wrist, and Halloway's weight that dropped flat and held, and Halloway's arm that ached for two days after. Pip scrambled up, spat mud, and said, "Thanks," in the tone of someone adding a debt to a ledger already complicated.

Kestrel said nothing. But that evening, when they made camp, Kestrel handed him a strip of dried fish without being asked, and in the economy these people ran on, that was a treaty ratified.

* * *

He began, without meaning to, to be useful.

It started small. He had a habit from his agency days of noting distances and times, and one evening he recited the day's figures aloud—hours marched, estimated miles, the bearing they'd held—and Odile looked up from her notebook with an expression he couldn't classify and said, "You pace count."

"Everyone at the agency paces count."

"Your counts match mine within a tenth of a mile." She wrote his figures in the margin, next to her own, and thereafter she checked them against each other every night, and it became a ritual neither of them named: the disgraced surveyor and the syndicate man, comparing numbers by firelight like two clerks who distrusted each other's ledgers and trusted arithmetic.

Then it was the river crossings. He knew knots—agency knots, ugly and strong—and he rigged the ferry line at the second washout faster than Kestrel could have, and Kestrel watched his hands work and said, "Where'd you learn that?" and Halloway said, "Before the agency. I worked deck on a coastal freighter for two seasons," and discovered as he said it that he had not thought about those two seasons in nine years, and that thinking about them now felt like opening a door onto a lit room in a house he'd believed condemned.

Then it was the leeches, the fevers, the food. He knew which palms held drinkable hearts—he'd learned that from a deckhand from the islands—and he cut three in one afternoon while the others slept off a march, and when Pip woke and found them lined up like little green canteens, the girl looked at him with open suspicion and drank one anyway.

"You're trying to make us need you," Pip said.

"Yes," Halloway said. Because it was true, and because he was tired of lying, and because he suspected—correctly, as it turned out—that honesty unsettled these people more than any lie he could construct.

* * *

On the sixth night, Brant sent Pip to fetch water and beckoned Halloway closer to the fire with two fingers.

"I knew your type of outfit, back in the day," the old brakeman said. His voice was gravel in a tin cup. "Meridian's nothing new. There's always a company, and there's always men who do its remembering for it. Men who know which files to pull and which witnesses to visit." He shifted the splinted leg, wincing. "I was one. Different company, same work. I did things on behalf of men who never learned my name, and I told myself it was a living."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you've got the look. The look of a man whose reasons just ran out on him." Brant poked the fire. "You stopped walking like an agent about four days ago. Agents walk like they own the ground. You walk like you're borrowing it."

Halloway sat with that. The rain came down through the canopy in fat intermittent drops, striking the fire and hissing. Somewhere upslope a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"The letter," Halloway said finally. "The notice at the cave. It wasn't a reward notice. Not really. Rewards are public—they want witnesses coming forward. That notice was a warning to the field teams. It meant the syndicate already knew the passage was found, and the reward language was cover." He rubbed his face. "I helped draft the field orders, Brant. Three years ago. Seal the tunnel, retire the records, handle discoverers per discretion. I wrote the phrase *per discretion*. I sat at a desk and invented polite words for what happened to Teller in that tunnel."

"And now?"

"Now I'm walking through a jungle with the people I was ordered to erase, carrying their stretcher gear, and I can't make myself want anything else." He laughed, short and joyless. "Eleven years reading men. Never once read myself until this week."

Brant studied him for a long time. Old eyes, firelit, missing nothing.

"Kestrel's father," Brant said, "was the only honest man I ever rode with, and I betrayed him, and I've got twenty years of penance left to serve. You've got less to answer for and more time to answer it in. That's not absolution. That's arithmetic." He closed his eyes. "Go sleep. We move at first light, and the grade gets worse before Ferrow's Landing."

* * *

The eighth day, the land changed character entirely. The dead line abandoned its mountain grades and dropped toward the river country in a series of long descending curves, and the jungle thickened from forest into something tropical and suffocating—ferns tall as men, trees buttressed like cathedral pillars, air so wet it was nearly liquid. The rain slackened to a steady warm drizzle and then, unbelievably, stopped altogether for two hours in the afternoon, and the sun came through the canopy in solid golden shafts, and steam rose off everything, and for a little while the world was almost beautiful.

Halloway walked point that afternoon. No one assigned it to him; he simply took it, the way a man takes a chore in a house he's begun to think of as his. Ahead of him the old railbed curved along a bench above the river, and the river below was swollen and yellow-brown and carrying the monsoon's first real load, and he stood a moment watching it and felt something turn over in his chest.

He had served the syndicate because the syndicate was order. That was the truth he'd built his life on—someone had to hold the map, someone had to keep the lines running, and the men who did it were necessary even when the work was dirty. Order against chaos. He had believed it the way you believe in gravity: without examination, without mercy.

And now he stood on a dead railroad in the middle of nowhere, guarding four fugitives and a corpse's memory, and he felt—for the first time since Teller went under the water—something other than dread. Purpose, maybe. Or its poor cousin, usefulness. These people had taken him in not out of mercy but out of arithmetic, and somehow the arithmetic had become something warmer, and he did not have a word for it, and he suspected the word, when he found it, would cost him.

Behind him, Odile called his name. Not *agent*, not *you there*. His name. Just his name, the way you call a colleague.

"Halloway. Bearing check—we should pick up the branch line junction inside the hour if your counts are right."

"They're right," he said. And then, because the transformation demanded testimony: "There's a bridge. An iron one, on the branch line, maybe four miles past the junction. I surveyed it two years ago—Meridian was pricing the branch for abandonment. It was rated for light traffic then. It'll be worse now, but it stands, and it's the only dry crossing of the Vell between here and Ferrow's Landing."

Odile wrote it down. Kestrel, twenty feet back with the stretcher ropes over one shoulder, looked at him for a long moment across the green distance.

"You surveyed our escape route," Kestrel said. "For the people hunting us."

"I surveyed a lot of things," Halloway said. "I didn't used to ask what they were for."

Something passed over Kestrel's face—not forgiveness, nothing so generous, but a recalculation, a figure carried to a new column. "Then lead on," Kestrel said. "And Halloway—if there's a syndicate party ahead of us on that branch line, I want to know before they know. Can you do that?"

He could. He thought of the pursuit party that had cornered them at the washout, scattered now, some surrendered, some perhaps reporting back. He thought of district offices and field orders and the phrase *per discretion* in his own handwriting. He thought of Josiah Teller's mother in Corvane, waiting for a letter that would arrive in a stranger's careful script.

"I can do that," Halloway said, and took the point, and led them down into the green toward the sound of the river and whatever waited on the far bank of it.
