## 21. Last Light on the Down Grade

The rain had been falling for three days without conviction—thin, gray, more mist than storm—and Kestrel had learned to read it the way other men read faces. This was not the monsoon's true voice. That was still out there, somewhere over the coast, gathering itself for the final argument. What fell now was only the range exhaling, wringing itself out before the season turned.

They came down the dead line in single file, five of them and a stretcher that was no longer a stretcher but two poles and a tarp slung between Halloway and Odile, Amos riding it with the grim patience of freight. The grade dropped through cuttings choked with fern, past mileposts whose numbers had been painted over by moss until they were just pale ghosts of arithmetic. Kestrel walked point with the map folded into their coat—not their father's map anymore; that was ash on the wind above the snowline, and what remained lived in Kestrel's head, which was where it had always really lived.

"Water's up," Pip said, from behind. The boy's voice had changed since the tunnel. It was quieter now, and it carried further.

Kestrel stopped at the lip of the cutting and looked down, and felt the old cold pleasure of recognition—the engineer's cold pleasure, the one that came before every disaster worth naming.

The Vell's southern fork ran along the grade here, or rather beside it, because the line had been built on a shelf above the river and the river had spent forty years arguing about it. Below them, where the track curved to cross a dry ravine, the embankment had let go. Not recently—the washout was old, a raw brown scar where the ballast had slid away and taken thirty yards of roadbed with it, leaving the rails cantilevered out over nothing like the ribs of something that had died mid-stride. But the ravine beneath was no longer dry. Meltwater from the high country churned through it, brown and fast, maybe four feet deep, full of the season's first debris.

And beyond the washout, on the far shelf, stood six men with rifles.

Kestrel did not move. Behind them, the file halted, and Kestrel heard Odile set her end of the stretcher down with exaggerated care, the way you do when you want everyone to know you are not running.

"That's far enough," called the man in front. Kestrel knew the voice before they saw the face: Verrick, the Saltmere agent, the one who'd nailed the notice to the cave mouth and asked questions in the tavern with a smile like a ledger balance. He looked worse than he had in Saltmere. They all looked worse. The mountain had been working on all of them equally.

"You're a long way from your territory," Kestrel called back.

"We've been a long way from everything for nine days." Verrick lowered his rifle slightly—not sheathing it, just letting his arm remember it was tired. "Meridian wants the survey records. We were told you burned them."

"Then Meridian heard right."

"Meridian doesn't believe it." He shrugged, an apology made of shoulders. "I don't much care what Meridian believes anymore, but I've got orders, and the man who delivers me your map—or your testimony that there is no map—goes home to a pension instead of a blacklisting. You understand how that arithmetic works. I've watched you work it yourself."

Kestrel understood it fine. That was the trouble with Verrick: he was honest the way a scale is honest. It made him dangerous, but it also made him predictable, and predictability was the one currency Kestrel had never run short of.

Behind them, low, Odile murmured: "We can't go back up. The sleet's closed the switchbacks—we'd freeze before dark."

"And we can't fight six rifles," Halloway said flatly. He'd set down his end of the stretcher and stood with his hands visible, a man who had crossed a line somewhere in the jungle and could not uncross it. "I'm telling you as one of them: they'll shoot if we turn. Verrick won't order it unless he has to, but Pell will." He nodded at a wiry man on Verrick's left, young under the mud, finger resting along his trigger guard with the tenderness of a man holding the last thing he trusted.

"I know," Kestrel said.

And the truth was, Kestrel did know. Had known since the moment they'd seen the washout scar, because Kestrel had been reading this line for two days now the way a priest reads scripture, and this stretch of the Down Grade was in the survey notes in their father's hand, annotated in that cramped pencil script: *Culvert at Mile 41 washed out '09. Rebuilt on fill, not masonry. Cheap work. Will fail again.*

*Will fail again.* Their father had written it like a prophecy, and prophecies, Kestrel had learned, were just engineering observations made by people who paid attention.

Because the washout scar was old—but the ravine below it was new. The meltwater had undercut the fill beneath the rebuilt section. Kestrel had seen it that morning, walking the curve ahead of the others: the ballast breathing, a slow subsidence, the rail sagging a half-inch where it should have been true. A rail could hang over air forever if nobody touched it. Fill could not. The crust over the culvert was a drum skin stretched across a hollow, and it would hold a man walking soft, probably hold two.

It would not hold six men running.

Kestrel looked at the light. Late afternoon, the sun already gone behind the western shoulder, the ravine filling with blue shadow. Another hour and the whole cutting would be dusk. And dusk was the thing. Dusk was always the thing.

"All right," Kestrel called to Verrick. "You want the truth? Come take it. But my people put down nothing while you're pointing guns at a stretcher. Your man there—" a nod toward Pell, "—keeps that rifle slung or we talk all night."

Verrick considered. Kestrel watched him consider, watched the arithmetic move behind his eyes: darkness coming, a wounded man, five exhausted ones, orders that required a document rather than a corpse. "Pell. Sling it." The rifle went up. "Now walk it out. Slow."

"Not yet." Kestrel crouched, ostentatiously, and began unrolling the oilcloth packet at their feet—the empty one, the decoy they'd carried since the fire above the snowline, because Kestrel Lockridge had survived twenty years on the principle that you never showed anyone an empty hand when a full-looking one cost nothing. "Map's wrapped. I'll bring it across myself. One condition: your men stay on that side until I've handed it over. My people have been hunted through a mountain range by your company. They'll not stand close to six rifles while I play courier."

"Reasonable," Verrick said, and Kestrel could hear him deciding he'd won. That was the beauty of it. Men who thought they'd won stopped looking at the ground.

Kestrel straightened, turned, and walked back up the grade to the others, unhurried, and knelt beside Amos's stretcher as if adjusting the tarp.

"The culvert," Kestrel breathed. "Under the washout. The fill's hollow—it'll carry weight walking, not running. Maybe not even that, if the water's chewed it further since morning."

Odile's eyes flicked to the crossing, to the sag in the rail, and Kestrel watched the surveyor in her confirm it in half a second. "It's a lie with a body count attached," she whispered.

"It's a lie with *no* body count attached, if I build it right. Listen." Kestrel kept their hands busy with the tarp straps, eyes down, voice pitched under the river noise. "I cross alone carrying the packet. I stop mid-span—I tell them the wrapping's knotted, make a show of it. Get them impatient. Get them onto the span. When the third man's on, the fill goes, and everyone on it goes into four feet of water."

Pip's face had gone white. "Into—"

"Four feet of water in a ravine with a gravel bed and a sandbar fifty yards down. Cold, wet, humiliated, alive. And the span behind them is gone, so the rest stay on their side, and we keep walking, and by the time they've climbed out and stopped shaking, we're a mile into the jungle dark and they've got no line to follow because—" Kestrel tapped their own temple, "—the map is ash and memory, and memory doesn't leave footprints."

"And if it goes early?" Odile said. "If it takes the first man?"

Kestrel looked at the crossing again. At the sag. At the water. Felt the weight of it settle where all such weights settled, just under the sternum, next to the ghost of a drowned stranger in a ventilation shaft who would be nineteen years old forever.

"Then I've killed a man for a piece of oilcloth," Kestrel said quietly. "So I need it to go exactly when I say. Which means I need to know exactly how much it'll carry." They glanced at Odile. "Fill failure under load. Give me your worst estimate and then give me your best."

Odile stared at the span for a long moment, lips moving, doing the silent arithmetic of a woman who had once falsified a report to protect a tunnel and had been paying for it ever since. "Walking load, spaced—six men, maybe. Running, bunched—two. Three if the water's undermined the north abutment like I think it has." She looked at Kestrel. "You're asking me to bet lives on my guess."

"I'm asking you to bet them on the best guess on this mountain. There isn't another one."

Amos, from the stretcher, spoke for the first time. His voice was gravel and rust, but it carried. "Your daddy used to say a brakeman's whole job is knowing exactly how much a thing can take before it lets go. Cars. Couplings. Men." His eyes found Kestrel's, and there was no judgment in them, which after Hollow Fork was its own kind of forgiveness. "He'd have done it. And he'd have stood on the span himself till the last second to be sure."

"That's what scares me," Kestrel said. "That's exactly what scares me."

* * *

They moved at dusk.

Kestrel walked out onto the span alone with the oilcloth packet under one arm, boots finding the sleepers by feel, each step placed with the deliberation of a man crossing ice. The rail sang faintly underfoot—a thin harmonic, the note a rail makes when it is honest. Underneath, where the ballast had sagged, the rail's song went flat. Kestrel marked the spot. Three sleepers past the sag, the fill felt solid again. The hollow was bounded. Good. A stage needs edges.

Halfway across, Kestrel stopped and knelt, and began to struggle with the oilcloth knot—a knot tied that morning specifically to look difficult, tied by a woman who had once faked a survey report and knew precisely how lies were built: strong enough to bear inspection, weak enough to fail on schedule.

"Trouble?" Verrick called from the far side.

"Wrapping's swollen. Rain got into the knot." Kestrel picked at it, spat on it, swore with theatrical frustration. "Give me a minute."

"You've got until full dark," Verrick said. "Then we come across and take it."

*There it is.* Kestrel kept their head down so no one could see what moved across their face. "Come ahead, then. Just mind the middle—ballast's loose there, single file."

Single file. Spaced loads. Every word a load-bearing lie.

Verrick came first, naturally—he would, he needed the document in his own hand—and he walked carefully, as instructed, a man trusting the ground because the man on the ground had told him to. Then Pell, slinging his rifle as ordered, because Verrick had ordered it and because the boy was too tired to imagine why a collapsing railway's collapsed crossing might matter. Then a big man named Coyle, whom Kestrel remembered from the tunnel, the one who'd shared his tobacco with the youngest agent, Rennie, before the water took him. Coyle walked heavy. Coyle worried Kestrel.

Three on the span. Two still on the far bank, one holding the rear. Amos, Odile, Pip, and Halloway waited back up the near grade, invisible in the cutting shadow, and Kestrel prayed—without any god in particular, just to the mountain, which had been listening all week—that they'd have the discipline to hold.

Kestrel stood, packet in hand, and stepped backward off the hollow section onto the sound fill at the near edge, and raised the oilcloth high.

"Here's your truth, Verrick!" they called. "Come and get it!"

And Verrick—tired, cold, certain of his victory, a man who had spent nine days in the wet chasing ghosts and finally saw one standing still—took three running steps.

Pell followed him. Of course Pell followed him; the boy had been following people his whole life, that was the entire architecture of a man like Pell.

Coyle hesitated—one heartbeat, God bless him, one heartbeat of a big man's native caution—and that heartbeat saved him, because the fill let go under the second pair of running boots with a sound like a giant clearing its throat, a deep subterranean *whump*, and the middle of the span simply ceased to exist. Rails, sleepers, ballast, forty years of cheap rebuild, all of it poured down into the ravine in a brown avalanche, and Verrick and Pell went with it, into the water, with a double splash that echoed off the cutting walls like applause.

For one endless second the world held very still.

Then Verrick surfaced, coughing, rifle gone, hat floating past him like a small boat abandoning ship. Pell surfaced a moment later, spluttering, and Kestrel's heart, which had stopped somewhere around the *whump*, lurched back into motion. Both of them. Both moving. The current took them sideways, tumbling, and Kestrel was already moving along the near edge, shouting:

"Sandbar! Fifty yards down, left side—swim left! LEFT!"

Pell caught the eddy first, dragged himself onto the gravel bar gasping, on hands and knees, alive, gloriously, stupidly alive. Verrick came in a moment later, hauled out by Coyle, who had scrambled down the far abutment and thrown a length of rope—his own pack rope, Kestrel noted, the man had sacrificed his gear without being asked—and the two of them lay on the bar coughing while the ravine roared where the span had been.

The two agents on the far bank stood frozen, rifles half-raised, staring at the gap where the crossing had been, and Kestrel stood on the near edge with the empty oilcloth packet still raised overhead like a lantern, and laughed—actually laughed, a wild, cracked sound that surprised even them.

"There's your truth!" Kestrel shouted across the water. "There's the whole survey, Verrick—paper and water and a hole in the ground! Tell Meridian whatever you like! Tell them the mountain ate it!"

"You could have killed us," Verrick rasped, from the sandbar. There was no fury in it. That was the strangest part. There was something almost like awe.

"I could have," Kestrel agreed. "I built it so I wouldn't. Walked you onto it soft, spaced you out, aimed you at the sandbar. Ask your surveyor, when you find him—that crossing failed the way I wanted it to fail, and not one boot-width otherwise. That's the difference between us, Verrick. You break things to finish a job. I broke a bridge to end one." Kestrel lowered the empty packet. "Go home. All of you. The passage's closed, the records are ash, and there's nothing left on this mountain worth a pension."

Verrick sat on the gravel bar in the last gray light, soaked, hatless, a man watching his orders float away downstream, and slowly, deliberately, he shook his head—not refusal, Kestrel realized. Surrender. The particular surrender of a tired man who has been given a story he can actually report.

"Rennie's death gets written up as a cave-in," he said quietly. "Yours too, if anyone asks. The Lockridge party, lost in the tunnel collapse. Clean file." He looked up, and for a moment the ledger-balance smile returned, worn thin but recognizable. "That's the only mercy I've got to trade. Take it."

"Taken," Kestrel said.

They turned and climbed into the cutting, and the four shadows rose around them—Amos on his stretcher, Odile with the map that wasn't a map folded safe against her chest, Pip wide-eyed and vibrating, Halloway standing with his ruined company's rope coiled over his shoulder like a man carrying the last piece of a house he'd moved out of.

Nobody spoke until the ravine was well behind them and the dark had come down soft and total, and then Pip, walking beside Kestrel in the black, said, "You built a trap that couldn't kill anybody. Out of a broken bridge."

"I built a bridge that couldn't hold a lie," Kestrel said. "Same thing, most days."

Ahead, far below through the trees, a light appeared—a single yellow point, steady, human. Then another. Lamplight on water. A river town, down at the bottom of the dark, where the dead line met the living branch and the living branch met the sea.

Pip grabbed Kestrel's sleeve. "Is that—"

"Ferrow's Landing," Kestrel said, and found, to their considerable alarm, that their voice had gone rough. "Station light. They keep it burning all night for late trains." A pause. "Somebody down there is expecting somebody."

The five of them stood on the grade in the rain-washed dark, looking down at the light, and Kestrel understood that the chase was over, the mountain was crossed, and the only thing left to survive now was staying.
