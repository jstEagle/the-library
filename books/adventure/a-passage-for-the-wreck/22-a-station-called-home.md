## 22. A Station Called Home

The river announced Ferrow's Landing before the town did.

For two days they had followed it down through the drowned green country, the monsoon rain falling now not in sheets but in a steady, patient gray, as if the sky had decided to live here and meant to stay. The Vell's tributary ran high and brown beside the old branch line, carrying whole trees past them like matchsticks, and Kestrel walked the ruined grade with a surveyor's eye and felt something they had not felt in fifteen years of sleeping in freight cars and never twice in the same town.

They felt useful.

The grade was wrecked—washed out in three places, culverts plugged with debris, one long stretch where the ballast had simply slid into the current like sugar off a table—but it was *readable*. Every failure had a cause, every cause a fix. Kestrel could see the line repaired in their mind's eye, could see the little trains running again, could see the town at the end of it before they ever reached it.

"There," Amos said from the stretcher-sled Halloway and Pip took turns dragging. The old brakeman's broken leg was splinted with tunnel timber and bound with Odile's petticoat, and he had complained about the petticoat for forty miles, which everyone understood was his way of saying thank you. He raised one gnarled hand toward the bend ahead. "Smoke."

It wasn't smoke. It was mist lifting off the river where the valley widened, and above it, perched on a shelf of higher ground the flood hadn't touched, a water tower, a grain elevator, a row of tin roofs going silver in the rain. A depot with a lamp already lit against the gray afternoon, though it was barely four o'clock. A town that had turned its lights on early because the weather asked it to.

Ferrow's Landing. Kestrel had never been there. That was the point. That was the whole point.

They stopped at the edge of the last washout, fifty yards from the first house, and stood in the rain like people at a border.

Pip broke the silence first, because Pip always did. "What do we say?"

"The truth," Odile said. She had washed the tunnel mud out of her hair in a stream that morning and tied it back with a strip of signal flag cloth, and she looked, for the first time since Saltmere, like a woman who had once held a position and might hold one again. "Mostly."

"Mostly," Halloway repeated. The syndicate man stood slightly apart, as he had for six days, hands empty, coat gone, hat gone, looking less like an agent of the Meridian Railway Consolidated than like a man whose employer had died and left him no instructions for grief. "They'll ask what I am."

"You're a railroad man," Amos said. "Ain't you? You surveyed lines for a living. That's a railroad man. Rest is details."

Halloway looked at him for a long moment. "That's generous."

"I'm told I'm dying of generosity," Amos said. "Leg's killing me. Might as well spread it around."

Kestrel said nothing. They were looking at the depot lamp, and thinking about ledgers.

Every station on every line Kestrel had ever ridden kept a ledger—the night agent's book, names and times and business, signed by every soul who passed through and wanted a bunk in the waiting room or work on the section gang. Fifteen years, and Kestrel Lockridge had signed none of them. Had given aliases, or nothing, or had slept in the freight yard rather than put a name down anywhere a name could be found. A name was a handle. A handle could be pulled. Kestrel's father had taught them the trade and their mother had taught them the fear, and between the two lessons they had built a life with no address, no ledger line, no mark anywhere that said *this person was here*.

The Meridian syndicate had hunted them across half a range anyway. So much for invisibility.

"Kestrel." Odile's voice, quiet. "You're doing the thing where you leave without leaving. I've watched you do it for three weeks. Your feet are pointing at the hills."

Kestrel looked down. Their feet were, in fact, pointed at the hills.

"They'll come looking eventually," Kestrel said. "Meridian. Even with the records burned, even with Halloway's report lost in the river. Men like that don't stop. They just wait for the price to drop."

"Then let them find us working," Odile said. "Five railroad people, hired legal, on a branch line, repairing a washout for wages. You know what that looks like to a syndicate man? It looks like nothing. Nobody hides in plain sight better than someone with a timecard."

Amos laughed, a wet, delighted sound. "She's got you there, Lockridge."

And Pip—Pip just looked at them. Seventeen years old, real name finally spoken aloud on the far side of the mountain three nights ago, thin as a rail joint and steady as one, and looking at Kestrel with the particular patience of someone who has already decided and is only waiting for the rest of the world to catch up.

Kestrel turned toward the town.

* * *

The station agent at Ferrow's Landing was a woman named Marta Ferrow, sixty-odd, granddaughter of the man the town was named for, and she met them on the depot platform with a shotgun that she did not point but also did not put down.

"Five of you," she said. "Walking in out of the monsoon on the dead branch. One of you on a stretcher. One of you wearing a Meridian field coat under that mud." Her eyes went to Halloway, who had indeed kept the coat, because it was the only coat he had. "Talk fast."

"Marta Ferrow?" Kestrel said.

"Who's asking?"

"Nobody, yet. That's sort of what I came about." Kestrel set down their pack, slowly, and turned so she could see the tools hanging from it—the gauge check, the level, the worn brass track gauge that had belonged to their father. "Your branch line's dead because the Consolidated abandoned it eight years ago when they built the mainline up the other side of the valley. But your town didn't die, which means somebody's still moving grain and lumber by road, and road freight costs you triple in wet season. Am I close?"

Marta Ferrow's shotgun lowered perhaps two inches. "Go on."

"That grade can be brought back. Three washouts, two plugged culverts, and the trestle over Miller's Slough needs half its stringers replaced—I saw the rot from the far bank. I'm a track engineer. The woman behind me is a surveyor who can stake the whole alignment in four days. The old man is a brakeman with forty years on mountain grades who will supervise your crews and complain constantly. The boy learns anything you put in front of him inside a week. And the man in the Meridian coat—" Kestrel paused. "The man in the Meridian coat knows exactly how the Consolidated thinks, which is worth more out here than another pair of hands."

"And what do you want?"

"Wages. Shelter. And our names in your ledger, if you keep one."

Something shifted in the woman's face. "You want to be *written down*?"

"I want to be written down," Kestrel said, and heard their own voice come out steadier than they expected, "in ink. Where anyone can find me."

* * *

Marta Ferrow kept the ledger in the depot office, under glass on the agent's desk, a fat canvas-bound book gone soft at the corners, thirty years of names and times in a dozen different hands. She opened it to the current page while the others waited in the doorway, dripping, and dipped a pen that needed a fresh nib, and said, "Name and business."

Kestrel picked up the pen.

Their hand did not shake. That surprised them. They had imagined this moment, in the way you imagine things you believe will never happen—on cold nights in boxcars, in the dark of the summit tunnel with water rising, walking down through the green country—and in every imagining their hand had shaken. But it didn't. It moved the way it moved over a track diagram: sure, deliberate, trained.

*Kestrel Lockridge.*

Not an alias. Not a false name borrowed off a grave marker in some town they'd already left. Their own name, the one their father had shouted across rail yards and their mother had written on school papers, the name the syndicate knew and the name the legend of the Wreck Passage would carry now whether Kestrel wanted it to or not. Signed in full, in a stranger's book, in a town they intended to see again tomorrow.

Below it: *Business — track repair, Ferrow's Landing branch.*

Odile signed next, then Amos, dictating his letters because his writing hand was busy holding his leg still, then Pip, who wrote their true name carefully, like practicing it, and then Halloway, who hesitated longest and wrote smallest, and when he finished, Marta Ferrow read all five entries, closed the book, and said:

"Room over the livery's dry. Work starts when the rain lets up. And Mr.—Halloway—you'll sleep where I can hear you snore."

"Yes, ma'am," Halloway said, and something in his shoulders came down half an inch, like a man setting down weight he'd forgotten he was carrying.

* * *

The rain did not let up for nine days, and the work started anyway, because Marta Ferrow was that kind of woman and the town was that kind of town.

Kestrel learned the shape of Ferrow's Landing the way they'd once learned the shape of closed lines: by walking it, cataloging, memorizing. The grain elevator with its crew of six. The church whose basement flooded every year and whose congregation rebuilt the sandbag wall every year with the cheer of veterans. The section house, roofless, that would become the tool shed. The children who followed Pip around like ducklings within two days, because Pip could whistle any bird call in the valley and made up calls for birds that didn't exist.

And Kestrel learned the washouts. Walked them in the rain with Odile staking behind, arguing happily about drainage the way other people argued about politics. On the fourth day they found the old culvert under milepost eleven, collapsed, invisible, exactly the kind of failure that killed repair crews who trusted a smooth surface—and Kestrel stood over it in the downpour and thought about a different culvert, a different collapse, men in gray coats standing on it in the rain while Kestrel called out the warning that saved their lives, and thought: *this is what the knowledge is for.* Not running. Not hiding. This.

On the ninth day the rain thinned to mist, and the sun came through sideways and gold, and the whole valley steamed like a locomotive at rest, and Amos sat on the depot platform with his leg propped on a crate and pronounced judgment.

"Gonna hold," he said. "Grade'll take a month. Trestle'll take two. And this town—" he gestured with his pipe at the wet silver roofs, the steaming fields, the children racing a hoop along the plank walk, "—this town's gonna be fine."

"You said that about the Vell trestle," Odile said.

"The Vell trestle *is* fine. I'm the one that fell off it."

They laughed, all of them, there on the platform, and Kestrel stood a little apart with a mug of coffee that Marta Ferrow had made strong enough to strip paint, and watched the light move on the river, and did the arithmetic they had been avoiding for fifteen years.

Running had cost everything and bought nothing. Every town left before dawn, every name burned after use, every line mapped and abandoned—and the syndicate had found them anyway, had chased them over a mountain, had cost a young agent named Ellery Voss his life in the dark of the Wreck Passage, a death Kestrel carried now like a stone sewn into the lining of their coat. Staying, by contrast, cost a signature. One line of ink. And bought a roof, and wages, and a boy learning telegraphy from the depot key, and an old man teaching brakemen's calls to anyone who'd listen, and a surveyor drawing the branch line's restoration on proper paper with proper instruments, humming.

Stopping no longer cost more than staying. That was the whole discovery. That was the entire secret, and it had taken a sea cave and a mountain and a flood to learn it.

Pip came out onto the platform and stood beside them, not speaking, which was unlike Pip, and after a while Kestrel understood why. The boy was holding the depot's copy of the timetable—not the ghost timetables Kestrel had reconstructed from their father's marks, but a real one, printed, current, for the branch line that did not yet run.

"First train's scheduled for spring," Pip said. "Marta says if the line's done, she'll drive it herself. She says—" Pip stopped, and grinned, and started over. "She says the engineer should ring the bell. Tradition."

"She say who the engineer should be?"

"She said whoever signs the ledger gets the seat."

Kestrel looked at the page in the boy's hand, at the printed times for trains that existed only as stakes and stringers and a trestle half-built over Miller's Slough, and felt something settle in their chest like a spike driven home.

"Well," Kestrel said. "I did sign the ledger."

Down the platform, Amos began telling the children about the Wreck Passage, already improving it, already giving himself a bigger part, and Odile called out corrections from the tool shed, and Halloway—former agent of the Meridian Railway Consolidated, current man with a hammer and a nail keg—was repairing the depot's fence in the gold evening light like a man who had finally been given honest instructions.

The mist was coming off the river. Somewhere up-valley, the first spring-grade survey stakes caught the last of the sun.

Kestrel drank the terrible coffee, and stayed.
