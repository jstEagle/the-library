## 1. Cargo and Rumor

The Vessarin River is a long gray argument between two mountain ranges, and I have spent eleven years losing that argument gracefully, one bend at a time.

My plane is called the *Magpie*, which is generous. A magpie at least has the decency to be whole. Mine is a forty-year-old bush hauler held together by rivets, prayer, and a welding job in the left wing strut that Sefa in Port Liss did for free because she said watching me fly it was "a form of entertainment she didn't want to lose." There is a patch on the fuselage where the original paint gave up and I painted over it with whatever was in the hangar, so now there's a rectangle of institutional green sitting in the middle of a body the color of old rope. Passengers point at it sometimes. I tell them it's camouflage. Nobody has ever believed me, and that's fine. The *Magpie* doesn't need to be believed in. She needs to be fed, flattered, and occasionally forgiven, like everyone else I've ever loved.

That morning I loaded her at Port Liss in a drizzle fine enough to qualify as weather only by technicality: crates of antibiotics bound for Ferrow Bend, a bolt of canvas for the ferryman there, insulin for Old Ansa up at Kiln Landing — packed in ice, packed in straw, packed in my own anxiety, because Ansa is seventy-something and stubborn as riverstone and will not come downriver to see a doctor even if you carry her aboard bodily. I know this because last spring I tried carrying her aboard bodily. She bit me.

"You're late," Sefa said, ticking crates off her manifest with a pencil worn down to a stub.

"I'm early," I said. "You're just punctual."

She snorted. Sefa communicates almost entirely in snorts and manifest corrections, and after eleven years I can hold entire conversations with her without either of us saying a word. That's the arrangement I've built my life around: people who don't ask questions, delivered goods that do all the talking. Medicine says *I thought of you*. Canvas says *I remembered what you mentioned once, months ago, when you were drunk*. It's a language. It keeps everybody at exactly the distance where they wave when you land and don't notice when you go.

"News?" I asked, because news is cargo too, and lighter than insulin.

Sefa leaned against the crate of antibiotics and lowered her voice out of habit rather than necessity; nobody else was on the dock but a heron with opinions. "Survey boats coming up from the delta. Government ones, or wearing government shirts anyway. Measuring things."

"Rivers measure themselves. That's their whole personality."

"They were asking about the source." She said it lightly, the way you'd mention rain coming. But her eyes stayed on mine a beat too long, and I felt the familiar cold slide down under my ribs, the one that lives there rent-free. "Nobody asks about the source, Ivo. The source is a rumor with a waterfall attached."

"Then they'll be disappointed tourists."

"Mm." She stamped my manifest, which meant the conversation was over, which meant it wasn't. "Ansa wants you to stay for supper at Kiln. She's making the eel thing."

"The eel thing that tastes like regret?"

"She knows you'll say yes."

I said yes. Of course I said yes. This is the trap of the Vessarin: every stop is warm. Every dock has someone who saved you a plate, a kid who runs alongside the pontoon shouting your name like you're something worth shouting about, an old man who wants you to look at his boat engine and listen to his story about the war he may or may not have fought in. Eleven years of warmth, banked stop by stop, enough to keep a person alive through any winter.

And then I'd take off, and the relief would hit like altitude — that clean thinning of obligation, the whole river unspooling behind me with its supper invitations and its survey questions, and nobody anywhere knowing who I used to be. Not even me, most days. That's the trick I pulled off, the one thing I'm genuinely good at besides landing on water shorter than sense allows: I made myself into a man with no before. Ivo Sandoval ferries medicine and gossip. Ask him about anything earlier than eleven years ago and he'll show you the horizon and change the subject to your engine.

* * *

Ferrow Bend first. The ferryman, Doss, met me on his deck with the canvas already half-unrolled across his shoulders like a cape, because Doss has never once waited for a delivery to be handed over when he could instead make it a small theatrical event.

"For the roof," I said.

"For the roof," he agreed gravely, as though we'd concluded a treaty. Then, while I signed his book: "They say the flood took the Kessin footbridge out. Whole thing, gone downstream like it owed somebody money."

"Floods collect debts. It's why I don't keep any."

"You keep one with me. You still owe me a story about how you learned to land in fog."

"I learned by landing in fog badly, several times. Story's over. It's short because I'm short on dignity."

He laughed, and his grandkids came running down the bank to watch the takeoff, which is the best part of any stop — the moment when children look at the *Magpie* like she's a miracle instead of a compromise. I let them believe it. Kids should get at least one miracle before the river starts charging interest.

Kiln Landing next, higher up, where the water goes from brown to tea-colored and the hills start crowding in close like eavesdroppers. Ansa was waiting on her porch with the eel thing already smoking, and I ate it, and it tasted exactly like regret, and I had seconds. She talked about her knees, the price of salt, a girl downriver who'd run off with a barge cook, and then, casually, the way Sefa hadn't quite managed:

"Boats above the gorge yesterday. Big ones. Survey lights at night, sweeping the banks like they're looking for something that ran away."

I kept chewing. "Looking for what?"

"If I knew that, boy, I'd have told them myself and charged them for it." She refilled my cup. Her hands shook, but her eyes didn't, and Ansa's eyes have missed nothing on this river since before I was flying it. "Whatever it is, it's upstream. And everything upstream comes down eventually. Water always tells."

I flew out of Kiln at golden hour with the light doing that thing it does on the Vessarin, turning the whole valley to hammered brass, and I let myself feel the relief arrive right on schedule. Supper eaten. Insulin delivered. Questions deflected. The day balanced perfectly on the scale: warmth taken, warmth given, nothing carried over. Tomorrow I'd run the upper stretches — the little strips, the floating clinics, the places so far off any map worth printing that my fuel stops are the closest thing they have to a postal service. Routine. Rhythm. The whole beautiful boring architecture of a life built specifically so that no single day could ever corner me and ask me who I am.

The *Magpie* disagreed with the plan, as she does. She developed a stutter in the number-two cylinder somewhere over the narrows, a hiccup like a laugh she couldn't suppress, and I put us down on the wide water below Halloway's Rock to check the plugs while the light died. That's the other thing about the river: it doesn't care about your schedule. You land when it says, you camp when it says, and you learn to find the comfort in a tarp and a thermos and the sound of current going about its business, indifferent to governments, surveys, names, and pasts.

I was elbow-deep in the cowling by full dark, swearing at a spark plug with real feeling, when the storm came down the valley.

Not forecast. Not gradual. One minute the stars were out over the ridge; the next the air turned the color of wet slate and the wind arrived sideways with a sound like the whole river clearing its throat. I got the tarps over the engine, got the tie-downs doubled, got myself wedged in the lee of Halloway's Rock with the *Magpie* groaning beside me like an old woman in a bad mattress, and then the sky simply opened and poured the mountains into the water.

I've flown weather worse than that. I've slept through weather worse than that. But I lay awake most of the night listening to the Vessarin rise, because rivers that rise that fast are rivers that are moving things — logs, bridges, boats, fences, the occasional memory nobody asked to have returned. Sefa's voice came back to me with the rain: *everything upstream comes down eventually.*

Toward morning the storm blew itself out over the ranges, and the world came back rinsed and dripping, and the river had climbed six feet up the bank and gone the color of strong coffee with grounds in it. I walked the shoreline with my coffee while the *Magpie* dried out, checking my tie-downs, checking the driftwood line, doing the arithmetic every river pilot does after a flood: what's missing, what's broken, what's new.

What's new was snagged in the roots of a fallen alder twenty yards downstream, riding the slack water in slow circles.

At first I took it for a crate off some swamped barge — square, heavy-looking, dark. But barges don't lose chests with lead lining showing through split seams, and they certainly don't lose chests with a crest stamped into the lid, a crest I knew the way you know a scar you don't talk about: a circle broken by three lines, like a sun interrupted mid-sentence.

Karavel.

I stood there with my coffee going cold and my heart doing something complicated, and I looked upriver, and downriver, and at the empty gray water, and at the chest turning patiently in the eddy like it had been waiting all night for someone with a rope and a bad history.

The river had finally collected my debt. Everything upstream comes down eventually — and apparently, so does everything buried.
