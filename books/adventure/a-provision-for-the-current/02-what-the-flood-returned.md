## 2. What the Flood Returned

The storm came up the valley like a debt collector, and I did what I always do when the Vessarin gets ideas above its station: I tied everything down twice and sat in the hangar drinking bad coffee while the sky tried to renegotiate my lease.

Her name is the *Kestrel*, my plane, though "plane" is generous some mornings. She's a forty-year-old bush freighter with a patchwork skin, an engine that starts on prayer and profanity in roughly equal measure, and a cargo hold that has carried vaccines, wedding dresses, contraband coffee, one very angry goat, and—until this week, as it turned out—one stowaway I hadn't met yet. But I'm getting ahead of myself. The night of the storm, the hold was empty except for a crate of penicillin I was too tired to unload properly, and I went to sleep in the loft above the hangar listening to the river eat the bank behind my strip.

The Vessarin floods every spring. It's not dramatic; it's administrative. The water rises, takes a fence or two, rearranges somebody's dock, and settles back down looking pleased with itself. Old-timers say the river remembers where things used to be. I'd always taken that for the kind of poetry people invent when they live somewhere flat enough that nothing else happens.

I woke to sunlight and silence, which on the river means either beauty or damage. Usually both.

The bank behind the hangar had lost a good three meters of mud. My mooring posts stood in open water like men waiting for a bus that isn't coming. And snagged in the roots of the drowned willow—the big one, half-fallen already these past two years—was a chest.

Not a crate. Not a drum. A chest, iron-banded, lead-lined by the weight of how it sat in the water, low and stubborn, refusing to bob. It looked less like something the flood had found and more like something the flood had been asked to deliver.

I should have left it. Let me be clear about that, because everything afterward traces back to the fact that I didn't. A pilot who ferries medicine and gossip along two hundred kilometers of river learns one rule early: what the water gives back belongs to whoever it belonged to before, and if you don't know who that was, it belongs to nobody, and nobody's business stays nobody's business. I've watched men ruin themselves over salvage. There was a fisherman up at Kettle Bend who pulled a drowned outboard motor out of a eddy and spent four years fighting a customs claim over it until he drank himself onto the wrong side of a weir.

But it was sitting there with its bands green with age and its lid just barely cracked, and the current was worrying at it like a dog with a bone, and I thought: if I leave it, it'll wedge under someone else's dock, and then it'll be their problem, and they'll come asking me about it anyway because I'm the one with the radio and the reputation for knowing things.

So I waded out. Knee-deep, then thigh-deep, cursing the cold, and got a rope around it, and hauled. It took twenty minutes and cost me a boot. The river gave it up grudgingly, the way old men give up secrets.

It was heavier than it had any right to be. Lead sheathing under the wood—I could see it where a corner had split—and the wood itself was dark, dense, some hardwood I didn't recognize, kept from rotting by whatever was inside keeping the water out. And stamped into the lid, pressed deep enough to survive decades underwater, was a seal.

A tower. A bridge beneath it. A star over both.

I knew that seal the way you know a face from a photograph of a place that no longer exists. Everyone along the river knows it, the way everyone knows a ghost story. Karavel. The nation that got erased. Not conquered, not renamed—*erased*. Thirty-some years ago, before my time, the treaties were voided, the maps redrawn, the archives sealed, and the name struck from every atlas between here and the coast. You can still meet Karaveli people. They're shopkeepers and boatmen and grandmothers now, citizens of wherever they happen to stand, and if you mention the old country they look at you the way people look at a photograph of a house that burned down with everyone inside.

Nobody stamps things with that seal anymore. Nobody *can*. The stamping of it would be, depending on which border official you asked, either a crime or a hallucination.

I sat on the bank with my ruined boot and my dripping trousers and looked at that seal for a long time. The morning was doing its ordinary work around me—kingfishers, the smell of wet clay, Sefa's dog barking at nothing two docks down—and I felt the particular cold that has nothing to do with river water. Fear arrives first, always, before curiosity. Fear is the body's cartographer; it draws the map of trouble before the mind has finished reading the terrain. And my body was telling me, plainly: *someone made this chest, someone hid this chest, someone will come asking questions about this chest, and questions are the one cargo you cannot jettison.*

I opened it anyway. Of course I opened it. I am many kinds of fool but I have never once been the patient kind.

The lock was old brass, and age had done my work for me; the keyhole was a suggestion rather than an obstacle. Inside, packed in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, were documents. Dozens of them. Deeds, letters, a ledger with columns of figures in a hand so fine it might have been engraved, and bound at the bottom, wrapped separately in waxed canvas, a single folder thick as a brick.

The paper smelled of river and time. Most of it was water-stained at the edges but the lead lining had done its job; the hearts of the pages were dry. I spread a few on the hangar floor and read by the open doors while the floodwater slid past outside, brown and busy, minding its own business again as if it hadn't just changed mine.

Most of it meant nothing to me. Land grants. Shipping manifests. A child's drawing of the tower-and-bridge seal, done in charcoal, kept carefully, which broke something small in my chest that I decided not to examine. But the folder at the bottom—the brick—was different. Its first page bore the same seal, full size, and beneath it a heading in formal script:

*Provision for the Current.*

I laughed. Out loud, alone, at a dead nation's paperwork. Because that's what I'd been calling my life for years without knowing I was quoting anything—a provision for the current, a little set aside against hard times, enough to keep moving, never enough to stop. Somebody had written that phrase across the top of a treaty folder and hidden it in a lead box, and the river had held it all this time, and then given it to me, of all people, the way you'd hand a lit match to a man soaked in kerosene.

I couldn't read the whole document. Half of it was in Karaveli script, flowing and strange, and the other half was legal language in trade-tongue that seemed designed to be unreadable even when it wasn't in a dead alphabet. But certain words surfaced like fish rising. *Headwaters.* *In perpetuity.* *Source.* And once, underlined twice in faded ink: *the source shall remain.*

I put it down. I picked it up. I put it down again and walked to the hangar door and looked at the river and smoked half a cigarette I didn't remember lighting.

Here is what I know about the source of the Vessarin: nothing, officially. It comes down out of the high country past the last surveyed rapids, through country the government maps mark with the surveyor's honest confession—blank space and a dotted line and the words *unverified*. unofficially, everybody knows something. That the upper river belongs to no one worth bribing. That people live up there who don't appear on censuses. That the reason the maps are blank is not that nobody's bothered, but that everybody who bothered came back wrong—quiet, or rich, or silent, or not at all.

And here is what I know about myself, which is related: I have a name I don't use, a history I don't discuss, and a talent for being exactly nowhere when officials start asking exactly the right questions. I built this life the way you build a wall in a flood season—fast, imperfect, one sandbag at a time. Medicine drops. Gossip ferrying. Be useful, be cheerful, be forgettable. Every stop warm, every departure a relief. That's not freedom, whatever I tell myself at altitude. That's just running with better scenery.

And now the river had delivered a sealed box of other people's erased history to my doorstep, stamped with a ghost, containing words like *in perpetuity*, and my first coherent thought—not my noblest, I'll grant—was:

*Nobody can ever know I have this.*

My second thought was worse: *Somebody already knows.*

Because chests don't float upstream. That thing had been hidden—sunk, buried, cached—in the river country sometime in the last thirty years, by someone who expected to come back for it, and the flood had torn it loose and handed it to the one pilot on the Vessarin with a past worth hiding. Coincidence is a word I stopped believing in around the same time I stopped using my real name. Things surface when they surface for reasons, and the reason is usually that someone, somewhere, has started digging.

I rewrapped the documents. All of them, even the child's drawing, especially the child's drawing. I restacked them in the chest, closed the lid, and considered the logistics of sinking it back into the river, which lasted approximately four seconds before I imagined some dredging crew or customs skiff hauling it up with my rope still knotted around it. My rope. My knots. I cut the rope off and burned it in the stove, which felt absurd and necessary, like burning a confession.

Then I dragged the chest into the back of the hangar, behind the spare propeller and the crates of engine oil, and threw a tarp over it, and stood there feeling like a man who has agreed to hide a fugitive on the strength of a handshake.

The light was going gold outside. Down at the docks, Sefa was resetting his mooring lines and shouting cheerfully at his dog, and the world was performing ordinariness at full volume, and I almost believed it. Almost. Then I noticed the boat.

It was a long way off, coming down from the north—from upstream, from the direction of blank spaces on maps. A survey launch, by the cut of it, government-grey, with a mast-mounted instrument pod and too clean a hull for working water. It wasn't stopping. It wasn't even slowing. It was just passing, steady and deliberate, the way a man walks past your window when he's checking whether you're home.

I stood in the shadow of my hangar door and watched it go, and the cold came back, the one fear draws on the map.

That night I slept badly, which is to say I slept in the *Kestrel*'s pilot seat with the chest's key—brass, old, warm from my pocket—in my fist, and dreamed of towers and bridges and stars, and woke at dawn to a sound I couldn't place until I was fully awake and my heart was already going.

Someone was in my cargo hold.

I came down the ladder with a wrench in my hand and found the tarp thrown back, the lid open, and a girl—sixteen maybe, river-thin, furious, soaked to the knees—standing over my fugitive chest with her hands full of the child's charcoal drawing, crying like the drawing was a person and the person had come home.

"That's mine," she said, before I could say a word. "That's my grandmother's. Who are you?"

Which, under the circumstances, struck me as the funniest question anyone had asked me in years.
