## 3. The Girl Who Claimed It

I had been in the cargo hold for two days, which is a personal record, and I want it noted that I am very good at small spaces. I learned from the best, which was my grandmother, who spent eleven years in a room the size of a wardrobe and came out of it still able to laugh at bad jokes. Compared to her, a crate of bandages and a tarpaulin is practically a ballroom.

The plane smelled like engine oil and dried fish and something sweet underneath it that I decided was hope, because I needed to believe this pilot carried something besides cargo. I had watched Ivo Sandoval load that hold at dawn in Tern Hollow, arguing with a dockhand about the weight of a medicine chest while scratching behind the ear of somebody's dog, and I had thought: *this one notices things*. A person who argues about weight distribution is a person you can trust with your life at four thousand feet. That was my whole logic. It has held up better than most of my plans.

I had not planned on the flood-chest.

I heard them talking about it through the floor — the pilot and the river man at the last stop, their voices coming up through the boards like water through a hull. *Karavel.* My grandmother's word. The word she said the way other people said a dead husband's name. I had spent sixteen years hearing about a country that did not exist anymore, and here were strangers saying it out loud over coffee like it was weather.

So when we landed at some mud-flat strip with a name I didn't catch, and Ivo climbed out to argue with a man about mooring lines, I did what any reasonable stowaway would do.

I opened the chest.

* * *

It was heavier than it looked, lead-lined, cold as a grave plate even though the hold had been warm all day. The seal on the lid was a bridge over three waves, struck into the metal deep enough that I could read it with my fingertips with my eyes shut. I have done that since I was six years old. Grandmother pressed my hand to a carving of it once — she kept a broken door panel under her bed like other grandmothers keep photographs — and said, *Learn it by touch, little bird. Eyes can be fooled. Hands remember.*

Hands remember. I had the seal under my palm and my eyes were burning and I told myself firmly that I did not cry, that crying was for people whose grandmothers got to die in their own country, and then I cried anyway, quietly, into the crook of my elbow, because I am honest with myself if nothing else.

Inside: oilcloth bundles. Papers. A tin box rattling with something small. And on top, folded twice, a letter addressed in a hand I knew better than my own — because she taught me mine.

*To whoever the river gives this to,* it began.

That's when the hatch opened above me and daylight fell in along with the pilot.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being caught: it goes faster than you think. One second you're holding contraband and your grandmother's handwriting; the next second there's a shadow in the doorway and a voice saying, "Well," in a tone that could mean anything from *murder* to *lunch*.

I looked up. Ivo Sandoval stood in the hatch with the sun behind them, so I couldn't read their face, only the shape of it — lean, weathered, hair going grey at the temples in a way that suggested worry rather than age. Their hand wasn't on a weapon. That seemed like a good sign, or a very bad one, depending.

"I can explain," I said.

"Can you." They climbed down into the hold, unhurried, which unnerved me more than shouting would have. Shouting I knew how to handle. "Because from up there it looked like you'd been living in my airplane for two days eating my emergency biscuits."

"The biscuits were stale."

"They're emergency biscuits. Stale is the point." They crouched across from me, and now I could see their eyes, and they weren't angry. They were doing arithmetic. I know that look; I've worn it myself when counting coins against a fare. "Two days. Through the storm crossing. You didn't make a sound."

"I'm quiet."

"You're more than quiet. There's a fuel gauge in the tail that reads wrong and I've flown past it a hundred times without noticing. You found it." They nodded at the corner where I'd scratched a tally mark next to a cracked dial, a habit of Grandmother's — *mark what lies, little bird, so you don't trust it twice.* "Who taught you planes?"

"Nobody taught me planes. Somebody taught me machines lie." I lifted my chin. If I was going to be thrown out of an airplane's life, I was going to do it with my spine straight. "My grandmother. Nadia Karaveli-Vess. She was a pilot too, before. Before they erased it."

Something moved behind Ivo's face. Not surprise exactly. More like a door opening onto a room they'd boarded shut.

"That's not your name," they said. "Nadia. You gave it to the dockhand at Tern Hollow as Nadia something-else. I checked the manifest."

"You checked the manifest for a stowaway you didn't know you had?"

"I check everything. It's kept me alive." They looked down at the open chest between us, at the letter in my hands, and their voice changed — went quieter, careful, the way you walk on ice. "And that's not yours either."

"It is," I said. "It's mine more than it's anyone's. Look at the seal. Look at the letter. *To whoever the river gives this to* — well, the river gave it to me. My grandmother waited her whole life for something from Karavel to come back. She died in March. In April the river coughs up a chest with her country's crest on it, and you're going to tell me that's coincidence?" I heard my own voice crack and hated it and kept going anyway, because Grandmother always said the truth sounds broken when it matters most. "She used to say the Vessarin keeps accounts. Everything the water takes, it returns eventually. With interest. This—" I put my hand flat on the lid, on the bridge and the three waves, "—this is the interest."

For a long moment Ivo didn't say anything. Then they sat down properly, cross-legged, like we were two kids on a floor instead of a criminal and a fugitive in a flying box, and asked the question I hadn't expected:

"What was her name? Your grandmother. Before."

"Nadia. Just — Nadia. She dropped the rest. Said names were luggage and she'd already lost the country they belonged to." I swallowed. "I took hers. When she died. It felt like the only thing left to carry."

Ivo looked at their own hands then. I'll swear to this: they looked at their hands like the hands belonged to someone else, someone they were keeping safe somewhere, or hiding from. And they said, almost to themselves, "People do that. Drop the name. Pick up another one. Tell themselves the old one drowned."

"Did yours drown?"

Their head came up fast. For a second I thought I'd blown it — whatever fragile thing was forming in that hold, I'd put a boot through it. But they didn't throw me out. They laughed instead, short and dry, no humor in it at all.

"You're sixteen?"

"Fifteen and a half."

"God help me." They rubbed their face. "Yes. Mine drowned. Or I held it under until it stopped kicking, which amounts to the same thing." They reached across and turned the letter toward themselves, reading upside down, lips moving slightly. Then: "*To whoever the river gives this to.* You know what that means? It means whoever packed this didn't know who'd find it. They trusted the water. That's either faith or desperation, and in my experience they weigh the same."

"So you believe me. About it being Karaveli. About it being — ours."

"I believe the seal. I believe the letter's old. I believe you've been touching that crest like it's a saint's bone." They paused. "What I don't believe is that nobody else will come looking. Chests like this don't surface quietly. Floods wash things downstream, but paper travels upstream faster — rumors, questions, men with clipboards. Someone made this chest. Someone sealed it. Someone knows what's inside, or knows enough to want it."

"Then we get there first."

Ivo stared at me. "There?"

"Wherever it says." I flipped through the oilcloth bundles, careful now, gentler than I'd been. Maps with rivers I half-recognized wearing names I didn't. A tin of wax cylinders. And a page covered in Grandmother's alphabet — the Karaveli script, the one she taught me on the backs of receipts because teaching it openly was still illegal in three provinces — heading upstream, toward the source, toward a word repeated three times like a heartbeat: *provision. Provision. PROVISION.* "Whatever they hid, they hid it at the source. And whatever a provision is, my grandmother spent forty years waiting for it, so it's worth reaching."

"You don't know what you're asking."

"I know exactly what I'm asking. I'm asking you to fly upstream with a stowaway and a box of dead people's paperwork toward something dangerous." I met their eyes. "You were already going. Don't tell me you weren't. I've been in your hold for two days — you flew past two landing strips you could've stopped at, and you didn't. You flew on. Whatever's in that chest scared you, and whatever scares Ivo Sandoval is worth chasing, because a person that careful doesn't scare easy."

They were quiet long enough that I heard the river below us, slapping the pontoons, patient as a creditor.

"You're a menace," they said finally. "You're a fifteen-and-a-half-year-old menace who eats stale biscuits and breaks into sealed evidence."

"Yes."

"And if I say no? Put you off at the next town, hand you to the constable, wash my hands?"

"Then I'll stow away on someone else's plane, and they'll be worse at checking manifests than you are, and probably worse at flying, and I'll die, and it'll be on your conscience." I shrugged. "Or you take me, and I earn my seat. I'm good with engines. I'm good with locks. I read Karaveli script, which you clearly don't, because you've been staring at that letter for five minutes like it owes you money."

Another silence. Then Ivo did something I didn't expect at all. They picked up the letter, folded it along its original creases with a precision that hurt to watch — like they were handling a wound — and held it out to me.

"Your grandmother's," they said. "Yours by inheritance. But the chest stays locked between stops, and you tell me everything she taught you. Every word of that script. Every story. Understood?"

"Understood." I took the letter. Our fingers didn't touch, but something did — some agreement older than either of us, the kind Grandmother would have called a covenant and Ivo probably called a liability.

"And Nadia." They said the name carefully, testing whether it fit in their mouth. "If we're doing this, you sleep in the cabin, not the hold. I'm not running a ferry for ghosts."

* * *

We took off an hour later, into a sky the color of unpolished pewter, heading upstream against the current the way Grandmother always said her people traveled — home, always home, even when home was a rumor. I sat in the cabin this time, in an actual seat with an actual strap, the chest lashed beside me like a passenger. Ivo flew with their jaw set and their eyes moving constantly, mirror to gauge to sky, and once, thinking I wasn't watching, they glanced back at me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere.

Later I'd learn that expression meant *I know how this ends for people I let close.* Later I'd learn a lot of things. But that afternoon I just pressed my palm flat against the chest lid, felt the bridge and the three waves bite into my skin, and watched the river braid itself silver below us, carrying us both toward the source.

Behind us, far back down the valley, a black speck rose from the last town and turned our way.

I decided not to mention it yet. Grandmother always said: *tell people bad news when it's certain, little bird. Until then, let them have the sky.*
