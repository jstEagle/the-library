## 4. The Cartographer's Debt

There are three reliable ways to find me in Port Sable, and Ivo Sandoval knew all of them, which is the kind of intimacy that should have been flattering and was instead deeply inconvenient.

The first is the harbor office, where I still hold a surveyor's license that the Guild would very much like me to surrender voluntarily. The second is the chapel of the Tide-Mother, where I do not pray but do sit in the back row on Sundays because the acoustics are good for thinking and the congregation is too pious to gossip about a woman drinking from a flask shaped like a hymnal. The third is the Rusted Compass, and it was there, at half past noon on a Tuesday so humid the flies had given up, that Ivo found me.

I was three fingers into something cheap and honest when the door opened and let in a slice of river light and one bush pilot wearing the expression of a person who has recently been robbed by geography.

"Marta," Ivo said.

"Ivo." I raised my glass an inch. "You look terrible."

"You look drunk."

"It's Tuesday. One of us is behaving appropriately." I slid my boots off the opposite chair—not out of courtesy, but because Ivo would sit down anyway, and I preferred to choose the moment rather than have it chosen for me. "What did the river do to you?"

Ivo sat. Ordered nothing. That alone told me this wasn't a social call; Ivo treats bars as churches treats saints, with genuine devotion and regular attendance. Ordering nothing meant the visit was business, and business with Ivo Sandoval historically ended with someone's life getting complicated. Usually mine.

"I need you to look at something," Ivo said, and set a canvas-wrapped bundle on the table between us.

"Is it money? Please tell me it's money. I owe the harbormaster eleven weeks of dock fees and he's started leaving notes on my boat written in increasingly large handwriting."

"It's not money."

"Then I'm already disappointed, but go on."

Ivo unwrapped it, and I will be honest with you, reader, or whoever ends up reading this testimony: I was prepared to be unimpressed. Ivo has brought me things before—a cracked compass that pointed at sentiment instead of north, a fish that Ivo swore was a new species and turned out to be a very old trout. My professional opinion has been solicited on everything from salvage law to whether a certain mechanic was flirting with a certain pilot, and my track record on all fronts is embarrassing.

But when the canvas fell away and I saw the chest—lead-lined, salt-scoured, its lid stamped with a crest I had spent six years trying to drink out of my memory—I put down my glass.

Not set it down. Put it down. There is a difference, and bartenders know it.

"Where," I said, "did you get that."

"The river gave it back."

"The river doesn't give things back. The river takes things and occasionally lends them." I leaned closer despite myself. The crest was worn but legible: a circle broken by a vertical line, with three stars above—the seal of Karavel, the Provisional Republic of Karavel, the nation that existed on paper for forty-one years and then existed nowhere at all, erased by treaty, by cartography, by the particular cruelty of deciding that a place stops being real if enough important people agree to stop drawing it.

I had drawn it. That was the joke the universe had been telling for six years. I had drawn Karavel onto maps with my own hands, and then I had watched other hands draw it off again, and I had signed the correction sheets, because signing them was the price of keeping my license, and keeping my license was the price of feeding myself, and I have never once claimed to be a brave woman. Competent, yes. Thorough, famously. Brave, no.

"Ivo," I said carefully, "do you know what you're holding?"

"A locked box with a dead country's stamp on it."

"A locked box with a *live* country's stamp on it." I glanced toward the bar. Noon crowd: two dockhands arguing about rope, the bartender polishing a glass that had been clean since Monday, nobody listening. Nobody ever listens in the Rusted Compass; that's why I love it. Still, I lowered my voice. "Karavel wasn't just erased. It was *unmade*. Treaties voided, records burned, borders reassigned across three neighbors who all swore up and down they'd never wanted the territory and then fought over every kilometer of it. And the upper Vessarin—" I tapped the table, once, hard. "The upper Vessarin was the whole reason. The headwaters. The provision."

Ivo went very still. Ivo does stillness well; it's a pilot thing, I think, the ability to stop moving entirely while everything behind the eyes keeps working. "What provision?"

"That's the part nobody wrote down. Or rather, everybody wrote it down and then everybody burned their copies. When the Commission came through in '61 to redraw the basin, there were references—survey memos, a shipping manifest, one very nervous clerk who'd had too much wine and too much conscience in the same evening. Something was secured at the source of the Vessarin. Legally secured. Documents, instruments, I don't know—a treaty, a deed, a trust. Something that made the headwaters belong to somebody, and somebody wasn't any of the three nations doing the erasing." I shrugged, and hated how casual I could make my shoulders look while my heart hammered. "Then the clerk drowned, the memos were reclassified, and I was informed that continuing to reference Karaveli sovereignty constituted a breach of professional standards. I breached. They discredited. End of story."

"That's not the end of story," Ivo said. "That's the middle. What happened after?"

"After?" I laughed, and it came out with the right amount of bitterness because I've practiced. "After, I mapped the lower tributaries for a fertilizer company until the fertilizer company folded, and then I mapped nothing for anyone, and now I owe the harbormaster eleven weeks of dock fees. You know the after. You've been watching the after for six years, mostly with pity, which I resent."

Ivo didn't say anything to that, which was worse than pity. Ivo pushed the chest an inch toward me.

"Open it," Ivo said.

"It's locked."

"You're a surveyor. Open it."

"I'm a *discredited* surveyor. It's different. It comes with fewer tools and more self-loathing." But my hands were already on the lid, because six years of drinking is a long time to spend pretending you don't miss the work, and here was the work, sitting on a sticky table in the Rusted Compass, wearing the seal I'd betrayed. The lock was a Karaveli field mechanism—I recognized the make, triple-tumbler, designed for expedition chests that might spend seasons in rain. I'd opened dozens. My fingers remembered before my pride could object.

It didn't open. Of course it didn't open. The final tumbler sat stubborn, and when I tilted the chest I heard papers shift inside—heavy stock, many pages—and felt something in my chest shift correspondingly.

"There's a second key," I said. "Or a code. Field chests like this took two turns: the surveyor's key and the commissioner's. Redundancy against theft." I looked up. "Ivo. Where exactly did the river give you this?"

"Below the Kettle Rapids, after the flood. Wedged in a root mass with a dead man's pack strap still on it."

I sat back. The dead man's pack strap was almost certainly sixty years old, which meant the chest had been in the river—or buried near it—for six decades, which meant whoever had hidden it had hidden it well, and the flood had undone in one night what three nations had spent lifetimes ensuring.

And then I thought the thought I should have thought first, the thought that turned my stomach over and made the cheap honest liquor curdle into something expensive and dishonest.

"Ivo," I said, "who else knows?"

"Nobody. Nadia. You."

"Nadia being...?"

"Stowaway. Long story. She claims the chest."

"Everyone claims the chest. Here's who else knows: the Meridian Consolidated Survey Group." I watched Ivo's face for recognition and got it. "Ah. You've seen the barges."

"I've seen barges. Didn't read the names."

"Read them next time. MCSG has been buying up mineral rights along the whole basin for two years—quietly, shell companies, the usual. Three months ago they posted a notice of intent with the regional authority: hydrographic survey of the upper Vessarin, feasibility assessment for a retention structure at approximately"—I closed my eyes, because I could see the coordinates, I would always see the coordinates, I had drawn that bend of the river with such loving precision that the map lived behind my eyelids—"kilometer mark 340. A dam, Ivo. At the source. Which means either they don't know about the provision, or they know exactly about the provision and intend to drown it under ninety meters of concrete before anyone can produce it."

"And the fleet?"

"Left Port Anselm last week. Six vessels, if the harbor gossip is accurate, and harbor gossip is accurate roughly as often as I am sober, so call it four to eight. Survey barges, a dredge, security contractors. They'll move slow against the current, but they're moving." I finally picked my glass back up, purely for something to do with my hands. "Here's the arithmetic. If that chest contains what I think it contains, it's worth more than every barge in that fleet, and there are people upstream, off the record, who have kept their heads down for sixty years waiting for exactly this to surface—and there are people downstream who will burn a village to keep it buried. You are currently the only person standing between those two groups, and you are standing there with a locked box, a stowaway, and a plane held together by prayer and spite."

"That's not fair. There's also duct tape."

"*Ivo.*"

"What do you want me to say, Marta? That I'm scared? Fine. I'm scared. Now tell me what you know about the channels above the Kettle, because the charts end at 320 and I need to be at 340 before those barges are."

I should mention, for the record, that I said no. In my head, I said no with tremendous eloquence. I laid out the case for no: I am disgraced, I am unreliable, I am eleven weeks behind on dock fees and nine years behind on courage, and the last time I put my name on something true, it cost me everything, and I have rebuilt a small, shabby, survivable life out of the wreckage, and I would like to keep it, thank you, please drive through.

What I said out loud was: "The channels above the Kettle aren't on any chart because the Karaveli pilots navigated by memory and stone cairns, and the cairns are still there if the floods haven't taken them. I can reconstruct maybe seventy percent of the route from my old field notes. The other thirty percent we guess, and guessing on the upper Vessarin gets you killed in approximately nine different ways, several of them creative."

"So that's a yes."

"That's a yes," I said, and finished my drink, because a woman should mark the occasion. "But understand what you're buying. I'm not coming along for adventure. I'm coming because I signed the correction sheets, Ivo. I drew Karavel off the map with my own hand, and I have been apologizing to myself about it every day for six years in a language I don't respect. This chest is the only apology that counts. If there's a provision at that source—if there's *paper* at that source—then I intend to see it delivered, even if it kills me, and given the state of my liver, that's a real possibility, so plan accordingly."

Ivo looked at me for a long moment. Then, quietly: "The chest won't open without a second key. You said a commissioner's turn. Do you know who holds it?"

"No," I said. "But I know who would. There's a man in Port Anselm named Reyes—customs division, supposedly, though his paperwork has always smelled of something stronger. He was at the Commission hearings in '61. He was the one who read my breach into the record." I stood, wobbling only slightly, and began wrapping the chest back in its canvas with hands that had stopped shaking the moment I'd decided. "And Ivo—one more thing. The notice of intent MCSG filed? It lists a liaison officer coordinating between the fleet and the regional authority. I pulled the name from the registry two months ago when I was bored and bitter."

"And?"

"Reyes. Same man." I tucked the bundle under my arm. "So whatever's in this chest, the government already knows it's worth sending soldiers after. We leave tonight, or we don't leave at all."

* * *

We left at dawn, which is to say I left at dawn, which is to say I was carried, semi-ambulatory and richly hungover, aboard a plane that smelled of engine oil and secrets, by a pilot who asked no questions and a stowaway girl who looked at me like I was a legend, which is the most dangerous way to be looked at by anyone under twenty.

The girl—Nadia—had the chest in her lap and her chin up, and when she caught me staring at the crest she said, "It was my grandmother's," with the flat certainty of someone who has decided a fact cannot be argued with, and I, who had spent six years learning that every fact can be argued with by the right people with the right stamps, believed her instantly and completely.

"Hold on tight," Ivo called from the cockpit, as the river unspooled below us, brown and patient, carrying everything it had ever taken toward a sea that never asked where it came from.

I pressed my forehead to the window and watched the last charted kilometers fall away beneath us, and I thought: whatever happens next, at least I'll die off the map, going somewhere I was never supposed to go, carrying something I was never supposed to carry.

For a disgraced cartographer, it was the finest heading I'd ever flown.
