## 5. Agent Reyes

The trouble with a secret is that it doesn't stay in the chest. It leaks. It gets into your coffee, your sleep, the way you check the mirror of your own plane three times before takeoff like the past might be crouched in the baggage compartment between the spare prop and the tarp.

I'd had the chest four days when the government arrived, which in my experience was about three days longer than governments usually took to ruin a good thing.

I was at Ferro Landing, topping off fuel and lying to the fuel man about my route—north, I said, routine circuit, nothing upstream—and he nodded with the particular blankness of a man who has been paid by someone else to listen. That should have been my first clue. My second clue was the seaplane tied at the far dock: government grey, engines too clean, sitting in the water like it had never once been asked to earn its keep.

My third clue was leaning against my wing.

He hadn't changed much. Taller than memory made him, or maybe memory had kept him small so he'd be easier to carry. Same crooked smile, the one that always looked like an apology arriving early. He wore a badge on his belt now instead of a rifle sling, and a jacket that had never been rained on.

"Ivo Sandoval," he said, and the name landed wrong, like a key cut for a different lock.

"That's me," I said. "Who's asking?"

"You know who's asking, Ash."

* * *

His name was Tomás Reyes. Had been, anyway. Twelve years ago we'd both answered to other names in a place that no longer appeared on maps either, though for less poetic reasons than Karavel's. We'd flown together for six months out of a strip that officially did not exist, running people and ammunition and, once memorably, a pregnant goat, through a corridor that three separate governments insisted belonged to them. Then the corridor closed, the operation burned, and everyone scattered into whatever life they could invent. I invented mine. Apparently he'd been issued his.

"Amnesty," he said. We were in the shack I rented by the month at Ferro, and he was looking around it with an expression I didn't care for—the expression of a man cataloguing evidence. "Full pardon. Clean papers under any name you want. A stipend. You hand over the chest and everything in it, and the last twelve years stop existing."

"They already stopped existing," I said. "That was the arrangement."

"The arrangement expired." He sat down on my only chair like he owned it, which, given his current employer, maybe he did. "Four days ago a flood on the Vessarin delivered a lead-lined chest stamped with the seal of a nation that was legally dissolved before you were licensed to fly. Do you know what happens when a country is erased, Ash? The paperwork doesn't burn. It gets filed. And every few decades somebody goes digging through the files, and everybody who ever touched them gets very nervous."

"So nervous they send you."

"So nervous they send me," he agreed, "because I told them I knew where to find you." He turned his hands palm-up, an old gesture, half surrender, half shrug. "It was that or let them send someone who'd never shared a tent with you. Someone who'd read your file and think they understood you."

"And do you? Understand me?"

"I understand that you kept the chest instead of reporting it." His voice stayed gentle, which from Reyes was worse than shouting. Gentle meant he'd already decided how this ended and was just being kind about the scenery. "I understand that you went to Marta Okonkwo, of all people, and asked her what the seal meant. I understand there's a girl in your cargo hold who isn't listed on any manifest."

The shack got very quiet. Somewhere outside, the river kept doing its indifferent work.

"Nadia came to me," I said. "She's not cargo."

"No. She's a complication. So is Okonkwo, whose surveyor's license was revoked for falsifying maps, and whose testimony would be worth exactly nothing except—" he leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a second the badge disappeared behind the boy I used to know, "—except she's right, Ash. About the source. About what's buried up there. I've seen enough of the file to know the provision is real, and I'm telling you as a friend, not an agent: if that treaty surfaces, it doesn't just embarrass a ministry. It un-erases a country. There are men whose entire careers were built on that erasure. They will not lose gracefully."

"Then why are you here? If it's hopeless?"

"Because I'm hoping you're smarter than you were at twenty-two." He stood, straightened a jacket that didn't need it. "You have until the fleet passes the second narrows. Three days, maybe four. After that, the operation goes live, and my offer expires, and what comes upriver after me won't make offers."

At the door he stopped, back to me, and said quietly: "You look good, Ash. Flying suits you. It always did—that was the one thing we agreed on, remember? Everything else we argued about."

"I remember arguing about the goat."

He laughed—a real one, brief and startled out of him—and then he was gone across the mud, and I stood in my shack holding a pardon I didn't want, feeling like a man who'd been offered a full meal on the condition he never taste anything again.

* * *

Nadia was waiting in the plane, folded into the navigator's seat with her knees up, pretending she hadn't heard every word through the tin wall. She was fifteen and a terrible pretender.

"He knew your name," she said. "Your real one."

"He knew an old one."

"What's the difference?"

I checked the fuel gauge I'd already checked. "About twelve years and a lot of paperwork."

She unfolded herself and stood in the aisle, arms crossed, and I saw her grandmother in the set of her jaw—the same stubborn architecture, the same refusal to be moved along. "Marta says the chest has a second lock. One that needs something from Karavel itself. Something my grandmother carried out when she left." Her chin lifted. "If you give it to him, it dies. Whatever's inside dies in a filing cabinet, and the village at the source drowns under a reservoir, and nobody will even know its name to mourn it."

"You've known about the village for two days and you talk like it's yours."

"It *is* mine." No waver. Fifteen years old and certain as bedrock. "Grandmother drew it for me a hundred times. Every channel, every bend. She said someday the river would bring it back, and I'm what the river brought."

Outside, the government seaplane lifted off the water in a clean, expensive arc and turned north. Toward the narrows. Toward the fleet.

I looked at the girl, and at the chest lashed down in the hold like a sleeping animal, and at the horizon where the past was flying away to report on me. Loyalty and truth sat in my cockpit like two passengers fighting over the controls, and I realized with a dull, sinking clarity that I'd already chosen, somewhere between the goat and the girl, between the corridor and the river. I just hadn't admitted it yet.

"Get Marta," I said. "Tell her to bring every chart she ever drew from memory. We leave before dawn."

Nadia grinned and jumped down onto the dock, and I climbed into my seat and put my hand flat on the instrument panel, the way you'd steady a horse.

"Hold together," I told the plane. "One more impossible thing."

Somewhere north, past the second narrows, barges loaded with dynamite were already moving against the current. And for the first time in twelve years, I was moving faster.
