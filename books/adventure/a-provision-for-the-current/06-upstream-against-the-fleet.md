## 6. Upstream Against the Fleet

I have been drunk in a great many boats, but I had never before been sober in one I was afraid of, and I want it on the record that the second condition is considerably worse.

The *Stubborn Magpie* — Ivo's plane, not this tin coffin of a riverboat we'd rented from a man who charged us triple because he could smell desperation the way sharks smell blood — sat behind us at Corren's Landing, grounded by a cracked strut and Ivo's insistence that flying into fog so thick you could chew it was how pilots became statistics. So: river. Upstream. Four of us, one outboard engine with a cough like a consumptive uncle, and behind us, gaining, three survey barges with floodlights and corporate money and, as of yesterday morning, armed escorts.

"Left," Nadia said.

We were in fog so dense that the river had ceased to exist as a concept. There was only gray, and the sound of water doing something complicated off to our right that I did not want to think about.

"You're sure?" Ivo asked, hand already on the tiller.

"She said left," Reyes said from the bow, where he'd appointed himself ballast and lookout, two jobs he performed with the grim competence of a man who had once done worse things for worse reasons.

"I heard her," Ivo said. "I'm asking if she's sure."

Nadia stood at the front beside Reyes, chest strapped to her back like a turtle shell she'd decided to grow into, thirteen years old and steering four adults through a drowned world on the strength of a dead woman's memory. "My grandmother ran this channel every dry season for thirty years," she said, in the flat voice she used when someone questioned her and she wanted them to feel stupid about it later. "She said the Karaveli called this stretch the Needle's Eye because there's exactly one way through, and if you can't find it, the river finds you instead."

"And the left fork is—"

"The left fork is the way through. The right fork is what the maps show." She turned and looked at me, and there was something in her face older than thirteen, some inheritance that had skipped her mother and landed on her whole. "You drew those maps, didn't you? The wrong ones?"

The question went through me like a fishhook. "I drew the ones I was paid to draw," I said.

"Yeah," she said. "That's what I thought." And she turned back to the fog. "Left."

Ivo swung us left.

* * *

Here is what I have never told anyone sober, and what I am telling you now because the fog has made me honest and because nobody reading this will ever be in a position to do anything about it:

Twenty-two years ago, I mapped the upper Vessarin. Not the polite version — the real one. I was young, I was brilliant, and I believed that a map was a promise: that the world deserved to be known accurately, and that knowing it accurately would protect it. I spent eleven months in the headwaters. I found channels no government boat had ever entered. I found the village — or its predecessor, anyway; villages move like hermit crabs when they need to — and I put none of it on paper. I filed a report saying the upper river was unnavigable above the Serrat confluence, choked with falls and shifting gravel bars, useless for anything but tragedy.

It was the best lie I ever told, and it cost me everything, because the company I worked for sent a second team to check my work, and the second team found the truth, and rather than admit their first surveyor had falsified data, they declared me incompetent, struck my name from the registry, and redrew the river themselves. Correctly. Thoroughly. With annotations for dam sites.

So when I say I know what it means to draw a map that kills a place, understand that I am not speaking hypothetically. I have held the pencil. I have felt the weight of it.

Which is why, when Nadia said *left*, and Ivo took us left, and the fog peeled back just enough to show me water I recognized — water I had lied about — I put my head in my hands and laughed until Reyes asked if I needed medical attention.

"It's still here," I managed. "The channel. They never blasted it, because they never found it, because—" I wiped my eyes. "Because my lie held. Twenty-two years, and my lie is still holding."

"That's good, right?" Reyes said carefully, the way you talk to someone laughing on a boat.

"It's the only good thing I've ever done," I said, "and I did it drunk, and it got me ruined, and it saved a village I've never seen. Make of that what you will."

* * *

The barges caught up to us at midafternoon, or rather their noise did — a low industrial thrum rolling up the river valley like weather. We'd tied off in a side slough under overhanging alders, engine cold, and watched through the branches as the lead barge came around the bend below us, floodlights cutting useless cones through the mist, a loudhailer crackling announcements to an audience of trees.

*"This is a licensed hydrographic survey operation. All vessels upstream of the Serrat confluence are required to identify themselves and heave to for inspection."*

"Heave to," Ivo murmured. "Charming. Very maritime law of them."

"They can't see us," Reyes said. But his hand was inside his jacket, on whatever he carried there, and I noticed that Ivo noticed, and that neither of them said anything about it, which is its own kind of conversation.

"They don't need to see us," I said. "They have sonar and a paid copy of the official charts. The question is whether their charts know about the Needle's Eye."

"Do they?" Ivo asked.

"No. Because the official charts are based on the second survey. My false report. The company corrected my *errors* downstream, where it mattered commercially, but up here—" I shrugged. "Up here they simply accepted that the river becomes impossible. Why spend money verifying a dead end?"

"So we're hiding inside your lie," Ivo said. There was no judgment in it. If anything, there was something like admiration, which I found more unsettling than judgment would have been.

"We're hiding inside the only true thing I ever did," I said, "which was lying."

The barge ground past below us, close enough that I could read the stenciled name on its hull — *Vessarin Development Consortium, Vessel 3* — and see a man on deck scanning the banks with binoculars, his gaze sliding across our alder screen without catching. Nadia had gone very still, one hand on the chest, and I realized she wasn't afraid of being found. She was afraid of the chest being found. Of it being taken and opened by people who would read a treaty as a problem to be managed.

After a long time the thrum faded upstream, and the fog closed again, and Ivo exhaled like a diver surfacing.

"Nadia," Ivo said. "When we get to the next junction — the one your grandmother called the Split Tooth — how far ahead of them does it put us?"

"Half a day," Nadia said. "Maybe less if their pilot doesn't know the water. He'll be reading depth soundings. I'll be reading the river."

"How can you read a river you've never run?" Reyes asked.

She looked at him with genuine puzzlement, as though he'd asked how she knew her own name. "Because she told me about all of it. Every night. For years. I used to think she was just an old woman talking nonsense to keep a child quiet." She patted the chest. "Turns out she was leaving me a map. It just wasn't drawn on paper."

I caught Ivo's eye across the boat. We were both thinking the same thing, I suspect: that this was what inheritance actually looked like. Not crests and seals and legal documents, but a girl who had memorized a drowned nation without knowing she was memorizing it, and who now carried it upstream in her body while the men with sonar groped along behind her.

* * *

We ran the Split Tooth at dusk, and I will describe it briefly because I have decided to be honest in this account even where honesty makes me look foolish:

It is a channel perhaps nine meters wide, formed where a rock spine splits the river like a broken incisor, and the water moves through it with the specific fury of a crowd trying to exit a burning building through one door. The official charts mark it as rapids, impassable. What the charts do not mark is the eddy line on the left third of the channel, where the current folds back on itself and runs smooth as poured glass, if — and only if — you enter at the exact seam between the two flows, which is invisible from upstream and exists only in the knowledge of people who learned it from other people going back further than any chart.

"Seam's just past the dark rock," Nadia said, calm as a woman ordering tea. "Grandma said aim for where the water looks angry but sounds quiet."

"That's not a sentence that means anything," I said.

"It means everything," Ivo said quietly. "Aim for where it looks angry but sounds quiet. That's the seam. You can hear the difference." And Ivo took us in.

I have run bad water on five rivers in my life, always as a passenger, always praying. This was different. This was watching someone thread a needle at speed in failing light while a thirteen-year-old called the marks, and the strange thing — the thing I keep returning to — is that somewhere in the second half of the channel I stopped being afraid. Not because the danger lessened. Because I understood, suddenly and completely, that I trusted these people. Ivo's hands. Nadia's voice. Even Reyes, hauling on the line I'd made him rig, calling depths in a voice that never wavered, a man whose loyalty I still did not trust and whose competence I no longer doubted.

We shot through the gap and the river opened beyond it into a long slow reach wrapped in evening fog, and Nadia whooped — actually whooped, thirteen years old for one full glorious second — and then composed herself, because she was Nadia.

"The fleet will lose six hours at the Tooth," she said. "Their pilot won't try it in the dark. He'll wait for daylight and soundings."

"Then we don't stop either," Ivo said.

"We have to stop sometime," Reyes said. "People sleep. Engines cool."

"People sleep," Ivo agreed. "Engines cool. But we sleep in shifts, and we cool the engine while drifting, and at first light we're through the marshes before they've finished arguing about the Tooth." Ivo looked around the boat at us — at the disgraced cartographer, the runaway agent, the girl with a nation on her back — and something crossed Ivo's face that I would call tenderness if I didn't know better. "Nobody signed up for this. Anybody who wants out, the next landing's a day's walk from a rail spur."

Nobody moved. The engine ticked. Somewhere upstream, thunder muttered, or blasting — it was getting hard to tell the difference.

"Well then," Ivo said. "Marta. You know these waters better than any living person, including the dead ones. Where do we hide a boat overnight?"

And I — sober, ruined, twenty-two years late — picked up the chart I had redrawn in secret during our second night aboard, the one with my lies burned off it and the truth inked in, and I pointed to a place on the river that appears on no map but mine.

"There," I said. "An oxbow behind the old ferry crossing. The Consortium's charts show it silted shut. It isn't. I made sure of that too."

Nadia grinned at me, sharp and bright. "Another lie that's holding?"

"No," I said, and found, to my considerable surprise, that I meant it. "That one's the truth. From here on, everything I give you is the truth. I'm told it's habit-forming."

Ivo pushed the throttle forward, and the *Magpie*'s replacement — a boat we had named, over Nadia's objection, the *Reluctant Consensus* — climbed up onto plane, and the fog took us, and behind us, faint through the gray, we heard the first blast of the fleet's survey charges echo down the valley like the river clearing its throat to speak.

Nadia's hand found the chest. "They're getting closer to it," she whispered.

"Yes," I said. "So we'd better get there first."
