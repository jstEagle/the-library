## 7. The Village That Isn't on Maps

The channel Nadia found wasn't on any chart, which was the point.

Marta had gone quiet an hour ago, her old surveyor's instincts fighting her new faith in a teenage girl with river mud to the knees. Ivo held the plane low over the water, floats kissing the current where the Vessarin split into three braids that every map said didn't exist. Fog sat on the water like something sleeping.

"Left fork," Nadia said again. "Then when you see the leaning cedar, you cut hard right and hold your breath."

"Hold my breath," Ivo repeated.

"The rocks don't care about your feelings, pilot."

Ivo cut hard right. The plane shuddered through a gap Ivo could have sworn was narrower than the wingspan, and then the fog opened like a curtain being drawn by polite hands, and there it was.

A village. Terraced gardens climbing the valley walls, stilt houses along a slow green lagoon, smoke rising from cookfires in threads so straight it looked like the air itself had agreed to behave. Canoes. Laundry. A woman beating a rug on a dock, who froze mid-swing when she saw them.

Nobody spoke for a long moment. Then the woman dropped the rug, cupped her hands around her mouth, and shouted a word that made Nadia grab the window frame with both hands.

"That's Karaveli," she whispered. "That's—she said *come in*, she said it the way Gran said it."

* * *

They moored at a dock built from timber so old it had gone silver, and the village came down to meet them the way water comes downhill—gradually, then all at once. Old men with river-stone faces. Children who materialized from behind barrels. A man carrying a fish he seemed to have brought purely as an excuse.

Nadia stood on the dock shaking, and Ivo understood why before anyone said anything: everyone here had the same jawline she did. The same set of the shoulders. She'd spent her whole life being the only one of whatever she was, and now she was standing inside a mirror made of people.

An elderly woman pushed through the crowd. She walked with a cane but used it mostly for emphasis, jabbing it toward Nadia like a fencing master calling a point.

"You have Yeva's chin," the woman said. "And Yeva's terrible manners, arriving by air without sending word."

"You knew my grandmother?"

"I grew up three houses from her." The woman's voice cracked on the second sentence, and she covered it by whacking Ivo's shin lightly with the cane. "You. Pilot. You brought her home. You will eat until you cannot move, and then you will answer questions."

"I really can't stay—"

"You will eat *until you cannot move*."

Ivo looked at Marta, hoping for backup. Marta was already following the smell of bread.

* * *

The meal lasted four hours, or possibly six; time went strange in the long house, moving at the speed of stories instead of minutes. Nadia sat pressed between two cousins she hadn't known existed that morning, laughing at a story about her grandmother stealing a boat at fourteen, and Ivo watched her across the fire and felt something complicated happen in their own chest.

This was what Ivo had spent eleven years not having. Not because it was unavailable—every stop along the river offered some version of it, warm kitchens, names remembered, chairs saved—but because Ivo kept leaving before it could take root. Every departure had felt like freedom. Sitting here, watching a girl get claimed by a whole village in a single evening, Ivo finally recognized the feeling correctly: it had never been freedom. It had been the specific loneliness of a person who mistakes a running start for wings.

"You're staring," Marta murmured, sliding onto the bench with a bowl of something involving fish and a criminal amount of butter.

"Observing."

"You're brooding with your eyes open. There's a difference and it's about forty percent more pathetic."

"Thank you, Marta."

"Anytime." She tore off a piece of bread. "The elder—the one with the cane, Sefa—she asked me about the chest. I told her what's in it. Or what we think is in it." Marta's voice dropped. "Ivo, they've known about the dam. They've known for months. They sent word downstream and nobody printed it, nobody answered, because officially this village doesn't exist. You can't flood a place that isn't on a map. It's very tidy."

Ivo looked around the long house. The children asleep in piles like puppies. The old men arguing softly about net weights. An entire community that had survived erasure by becoming invisible—and invisibility, Ivo knew better than almost anyone, works fine right up until someone decides to bulldoze the fog.

"They can't fight a survey fleet with canoes," Ivo said.

"No. But they might fight it with paper. If the chest says what Nadia thinks it says." Marta turned her bowl in her hands. "There's a thing I should tell you. When I mapped the upper river, I found signs of settlement here. Old terraces, stone markers. I reported them as uninhabited ruins. It got me promoted." Her jaw worked. "It got them erased a little harder."

"Marta—"

"Don't forgive me yet. I'm still deciding if I deserve it." She stood, took her bowl to the wash basin, and came back looking older. "Sefa wants to see the seal tonight. Properly. In front of the council."

* * *

The council turned out to be seven elders arranged around a table older than most nations, lit by lamps that smelled of rendered fish fat. Sefa presided with her cane laid across her knees like a scepter. The lead-lined chest sat between them all, and the room regarded it the way people regard a coffin that might be occupied.

"Open it," Sefa said.

Ivo worked the lock—the first one, the one that had yielded to the key sewn into the chest's lining. Inside lay oilcloth bundles, and beneath them, a second lock: brass, small, shaped like a river delta.

"There's another key," Nadia said. "Gran wore it on a cord. She was buried with it."

Silence. Then a boy near the door—sixteen maybe, all elbows and urgency—said, "Grandmother Sefa has Yeva's things. From the estate sale. She bought the whole box."

Sefa closed her eyes briefly, as if praying for patience with her own sentimentality. "I could not let them sell her ring," she said defensively. "Or the cord."

She sent the boy—his name was Tomas, and he ran everywhere the way other people walk—to fetch it. He returned breathless, holding a small tin. Inside, among a cracked hairbrush and a wedding photograph of strangers, lay a cord with a brass key.

Nadia picked it up with both hands, the way you lift something that might be alive.

"It doesn't feel right to turn it," she said. "Not without everyone. Not without—" She looked at Ivo. "Not without knowing what happens after."

"What do you mean?" Marta asked.

"If it's a treaty, if it gives the source back to us—then we stop being invisible. All of us. Everyone in this room becomes a name on a document that governments can read." Nadia's voice wobbled and steadied. "Gran chose disappearing. She chose it for sixty years. And I'd be undoing it."

The elders looked at each other. Somewhere outside, a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

Sefa spoke last, as eldest, and her voice was dry as driftwood and just as hard to break.

"Yeva chose disappearing so that we would survive. Survival was the mission, not the hiding." She tapped the chest once with her cane. "Hiding was only ever the method. If the method has stopped working—if barges are coming upriver to drown us—then we change methods. That is not betraying her. That is finishing her work."

She looked around the table. "We open it at the source. Where the provision was buried, where it belongs. At dawn, with the pilot's plane, because the river route takes four days and I am eighty-one." A pause. "If the pilot agrees."

Every face turned to Ivo. Seven elders, one mechanic, one disgraced cartographer, one girl holding a dead woman's key, and a whole village's future balanced on whether a bush pilot would fly one more impossible errand.

Ivo thought about saying no. Thought about the fog downstream, the fleet, the badge Reyes wore, the eleven years of careful nothing. Thought about how the safest life was the one where nobody knew your name, and how that life had a floor made of ice, and how Ivo had been hearing it creak for years.

"Yeah," Ivo heard themself say. "Dawn. But we take the long channel out—Nadia navigates. And somebody feeds me first, because I fly badly hungry."

The relief in the room was so loud it took a moment to hear the other sound underneath it: a low engine note, far off, wrong for these waters. Too steady. Too big.

Tomas, who had gone to bar the doors, came back pale.

"There are lights on the river," he said. "Downstream. A lot of them."

Marta was already moving for the door. "That's not possible. The rapids—no barge makes those rapids before—"

"They're not coming by barge," Ivo said quietly, already calculating fuel, weight, dawn light. "Reyes flies too."
