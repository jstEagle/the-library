## 8. The Provision

The source of the Vessarin is not the thundering thing the maps promised. Marta's old charts, the ones she'd drawn before anyone thought to ruin her, showed a great cataract bursting from a mountainside, and I'd spent half my life assuming she'd flattered her own work. What we found instead was a spring—a wide, patient upwelling in a bowl of black stone, so clear you could count the pebbles forty feet down, so cold it made your teeth ache to look at it. The river didn't begin here. It *condensed*, the way a decision condenses out of a long silence.

Nadia stood at the water's edge with the chest in both arms, and for once she wasn't performing certainty. She just looked small, and wet, and fifteen.

"It's supposed to open here," she said. "Grandmother was very clear. 'At the water that remembers.' I used to think it was poetry."

"Everything's poetry until it's instructions," Marta said. She'd been sober four days now, which meant her hands were steady and her temper was not. She knelt beside the chest and ran her fingers along the seam where the lead lining met the wood. "There. See how the seal's recessed? It's not decoration. It's a keyhole."

I looked at the crest stamped into the metal—the double river and the star, Karavel's sign, a country that had been unwritten so thoroughly that even my atlas, which trusted nothing, had no record of it. My hands wanted to shake. I let them. There's no point pretending fear away; it just goes underground and digs.

"You should do it," I told Nadia.

She shook her head. "You found it. The river gave it to *you*." Then, catching my expression: "That's what Grandmother would say. She believed rivers choose people. She said that's why they drown the ones who lie."

"Cheerful woman," Marta muttered.

But Nadia held the chest out to me, and there are moments when refusing costs more than complying, so I set my palms against the seal and pressed, and turned, the way you'd turn a stubborn valve on an engine that's been sitting too long.

The mechanism inside was beautiful and vindictive. Something clicked, then ground, then clicked again, and the lid rose with a sigh of cold air that smelled of wax and iron and, faintly, of cedar—of a room somewhere that no longer existed.

No gold. No jewels. Nadia made a sound like she'd been struck.

What came out instead was paper. Dozens of documents wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord gone brittle as autumn grass, and beneath them a copper cylinder, stoppered, heavy as a child. Marta unwrapped the first document with the tenderness she usually reserved for carburetors, and I watched her face do something complicated.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you magnificent dead people."

"What is it?" Nadia's voice cracked straight down the middle.

Marta held the page up to the grey morning light. Her hands, steady for four days, began to tremble anyway. "It's a treaty. Ratified. Signed by Karavel's chancellery *and* by the Vessarin Compact—the three-nation authority that drew the current borders. Granting the headwaters of the Vessarin—" she tapped the bowl of black stone around us, the spring, the whole patient heart of the river— "to the Karaveli people. In perpetuity." She looked up. "Not ceded. Not leased. *In perpetuity.* And the Compact countersigned. Which means every government downstream has been living on stolen property for thirty years, with the receipt buried at the scene."

The wind came off the water and moved the reeds, and nobody said anything for a while. Nadia sat down hard on a rock, holding one of the letters like it might fly away. I understood her. I've spent years carrying papers of my own that could unmake me, and there is a particular vertigo in holding proof of something everyone agreed to forget.

"They erased the country," I said slowly, working it through, "but they never erased the treaty. They couldn't. So they hid it."

"And hoped the river would keep its mouth shut," Marta said. "Which it did. Until the flood." She was already spreading documents on a flat stone, cataloguing, cross-referencing, the surveyor waking up in her like a lamp turned on. "Signatures, seals, ratification dates, boundary descriptions—it's complete. This isn't a claim. This is a *verdict*."

Nadia laughed, or sobbed, or some hybrid of the two that teenagers have patented. "We did it. We actually—we got here first. We *won*."

I remember thinking: don't say it out loud. In my experience victory announced aloud is an invitation, and the world RSVPs fast.

We were still reading when the fog upstream thinned and I heard the engines.

You learn to hear engines the way farmers learn to hear weather. These were diesels, twin-screw, running heavy—barges, plural, coming down from the north cut where no barges had any honest business being. I climbed the rocks above the spring and looked back along the channel, and there they were: three hulls in formation, grey-green, flying the survey pennant of the Meridian Water Authority, and riding the lead barge's deck like a figurehead stood a man I knew by his silhouette before I knew him by anything else.

Reyes.

He'd found us. Of course he'd found us—Ivo Sandoval flies a plane with a radio and a mouth, and Marta Okonkwo buys fuel from people who sell information cheaper than diesel. I'd known this day was coming since the chest came out of the floodwater. Knowing doesn't help. Knowing is just fear with better paperwork.

"We need to move," I called down. "Now."

"Move where?" Marta demanded, arms full of treaty. "This is the source. This is the whole point. If we run, we're couriers. If we stay, we're witnesses."

"Witnesses to what?"

"To whatever they do next."

Nadia had already dragged the chest behind the black stones, out of sightline from the water. Good instincts. Bad odds. The barges were closing at a speed we couldn't match on foot through bog, and the only aircraft within two hundred miles was mine, parked on the shingle six miles downstream where I'd left it with Junie guarding it and muttering about her fuel lines.

The lead barge swung toward the bank with the confidence of something that has never once been told no. Ropes went over. Boots hit gravel. And Reyes came up the path between the reeds with six armed men fanned behind him and his badge on a lanyard around his neck, displayed the way you'd display a hunting license.

"Ivo," he said. His voice hadn't changed. That was the worst part. Ten years, a government, a war's worth of distance, and he still said my name like we were sharing a cigarette on a night shift. "You look terrible."

"You look employed."

A flicker of the old grin, quickly filed away. His eyes went past me to Marta, to Nadia, to the flat stone with its spread of documents, and I watched him do the arithmetic and arrive at a number he didn't like. "So you opened it."

"So you knew what it was."

"I knew there was a reason the Authority suddenly cares about the upper river." He said it lightly, but his men had stopped walking, and I noticed none of them had looked directly at the documents. Soldiers know contraband when they see it, and they know when the contraband might be legal. "Ivo. Last offer, and it's real this time. Amnesty. Full pardon, new certification, your name cleared wherever you want it cleared. All you have to do is hand over the chest and forget the source exists."

"And the girl's inheritance?"

"Is a historical curiosity."

"And the village downstream? The one your fleet's dam is going to put under forty feet of water?"

Something moved behind Reyes's eyes—quick, suppressed, like a fish under ice. He'd read the same reports I had. He'd done the same math. "The dam provides power for two million people," he said, reciting it, and hearing himself recite it.

"The treaty provides the river to the Karaveli," Marta said, stepping forward with a document held high like a lantern. "Countersigned by your own Compact. You're not standing on Authority land, Agent. You're standing on a trespass."

For a moment—one long, suspended moment, with the spring murmuring behind us and the fog lifting off the water like breath—I thought we'd won it right there. I thought the badge would blink, the man underneath would surface, the way he used to surface when the orders got dirty and we'd sit up all night arguing about lines we wouldn't cross.

Then a radio crackled on the lead barge, and Reyes's jaw tightened, and whatever surfaced in him went back under.

"Secure the site," he said, and his voice was a stranger's. "Confiscate all materials. Nobody leaves."

His men started forward. Nadia grabbed my sleeve. Marta gathered the treaty to her chest like a child. And above the black stone bowl, over the water that remembered everything, the first drops of rain began to fall on thirty years of buried truth—and on the six of us who'd carried it into the light, with nowhere left to run and nowhere left to hide.

Reyes met my eyes one last time. What I saw there wasn't cruelty.

It was a man drowning, reaching for whoever was nearest.

"I'm sorry, Ivo," he said. "Orders changed this morning. Nobody's going home the way they came."
