## 9. The Dam Breaks First

I've been drunk in a lot of places. I have never before been drunk with purpose, and let me tell you, it's a different animal entirely.

We'd spent two days at the village doing what Marta Okonkwo does best, which is arguing with paper. The treaty was real—Ivo had cracked the chest open like an egg and there it was, wax seals and all, a document older than most of the governments currently pretending the river belonged to them. My job was to make it legible to the modern world: coordinates verified against my old survey notes, boundaries cross-referenced, the whole thing translated from Karaveli legal archaism into something a tribunal clerk wouldn't sneeze at.

The trouble with being good at your job is that people believe you when you tell them what you've found. And the trouble with telling people is that someone always tells the wrong people.

It was Nadia who heard it first—a low thrum through the water, felt in the soles of your feet before you heard it in your ears. She came running up from the landing beach with her braids flying and her face gone pale as birch bark.

"Barges," she said. "Three of them. Coming around the Sorrow Bend."

I got my field glasses on them from the ridge above the village, and I'll be honest with you, my hands were not steady. Three flat-bottomed survey barges, riding heavy, cranes folded on their decks like sleeping insects. And on the lead barge, stacked in neat rows under tarps that weren't doing a very good job of hiding anything: blasting charges. Enough of them to rearrange a canyon.

"They can't," Nadia said. It wasn't a question, exactly. It was more like a prayer she didn't believe in. "The treaty—"

"The treaty is a piece of paper," I said, "and they are holding dynamite. Paper has never once won that argument."

Ivo stood beside me at the ridge, arms crossed, watching the barges with the expression of a person doing arithmetic they don't like. Behind us, the village was already stirring—word travels fast in a place where everyone lives one loud voice from everyone else. Old women were pulling children indoors. Men were hauling canoes above the waterline. A Karaveli elder named Sefu, who had taken one look at our chest and known exactly what it was, appeared at my elbow with the calm of a man who had been waiting for this day for sixty years.

"How long?" he asked me.

"Until they're in position? Half a day. Until they fire?" I shrugged. "Depends how much paperwork their conscience requires. Judging by the cargo—" I nodded at the charges visible even from here, "—not much."

Sefu nodded slowly. "Then we move tonight."

"Move where?"

"Up." He pointed at the high terraces behind the village, the switchback paths carved into the cliffside, goat tracks and stairways his people had maintained since before anyone thought to map them. "The flood will take the houses. It cannot take us."

That was the part that made my stomach turn, if you want the truth. Not the barges. Not the charges. The fact that this man had a plan ready, polished by generations of practice, for the day the outside world finally remembered they existed and tried to erase them again. You don't build escape routes into your village unless you expect to need them.

* * *

They started blasting at dusk.

Not the dam itself—not yet—but the narrows three kilometers downstream, where the fleet had decided the river needed convincing to cooperate. The first explosion rolled up the valley like thunder with a grudge, and the second came before the echo of the first had finished dying, and by the third the water in front of the village had gone strange—pulling back from the banks in long, sucking breaths, then surging forward brown and confused.

"They're not surveying," Ivo said. We were on the beach, loading the last of the elders' chests into the plane's cargo hold—Ivo had flown her downriver twice already, ferrying the oldest and youngest to the high ground past the falls. "Surveyors measure. This is construction. They've skipped every step between 'arrive' and 'destroy.'"

"Orders change," said a voice behind us, and I turned around fast enough to pull something in my neck.

Reyes stood at the edge of the beach, and he looked wrong. That's the only way I can put it. When we'd met him at the source, he'd been pressed and official, badge gleaming, orders in his breast pocket like a loaded gun. Now his jacket was unbuttoned, his badge was gone entirely, and there was mud to his knees. He looked like a man who had jumped off his own ship.

"Your orders changed," Ivo said flatly. "Or you did."

"Both." He came closer, hands open, palms out—the universal gesture of *please don't hit me yet*. "The directive came through an hour ago. Not 'secure the site.' Not 'negotiate.' 'Clear the zone.'" His jaw worked. "By any means. The company doesn't want witnesses to the flooding, Ivo. They want the valley empty before the water arrives, and they want it empty in a way that can't be traced back to them."

"And you came here because?"

"Because I've read those orders before." His voice dropped. "Different country. Different uniform. Same words. And last time, I followed them." He looked at Ivo for a long moment, and whatever passed between them had thirty years of history in it, none of which I was invited to. "Last time, I told myself it was necessary. I'm done lying to myself. Give me a boat."

Nadia appeared out of nowhere, the way teenagers do, as if she'd been standing behind Reyes the entire time listening. Maybe she had. She looked him up and down with the particular contempt only a sixteen-year-old can manufacture from thin air.

"You're the agent," she said. "The one who wanted to steal our chest."

"I'm the one who's about to spend all night hauling your grandmother's neighbors out of the water," Reyes said. "You can hate me while I do it or after. Your choice."

Something flickered across her face—grudging respect, maybe, or just the recognition of another person carrying too many names. She grabbed a rope. "Take the stern."

* * *

I won't give you a tidy account of that night, because there wasn't one. There was no order to it, no clean sequence of events. There was black water full of debris, and lantern light swinging on poles, and the screaming of a river that had been kicked awake, and somewhere in the middle of it, four people and a dozen villagers in leaky canoes doing the arithmetic of human life against current speed.

The fleet had blown the lower narrows wide, and the river, given permission, took everything it had ever wanted. The water rose faster than Sefu's projections—faster than mine, and I'll carry that professional humiliation to my grave. By midnight the lower terraces were gone. By two in the morning the water was chewing at the stone foundations of the meeting house, and we were running trips between the collapsing docks and the high path, canoes overloaded, engines flooded, paddles breaking.

Marta Okonkwo, disgraced cartographer, drunk, coward—these are titles I've worn. That night I wore a different one: ferryman. I poled until my palms split. I hauled a ninety-year-old woman named Ama out of a window in the meeting house as the wall gave way behind her, and she weighed nothing, nothing at all, like carrying a bundle of dry sticks, and she thanked me in Karaveli, a language I don't speak, and I understood every word anyway.

Reyes worked like a man paying off a debt with interest. He went into the water four times that I saw—four times into current that would have killed any of us, and came up each time with someone in his arms. On the third trip he brought out a boy, maybe eight years old, blue-lipped and limp, and Ivo did compressions on the dock stones while Nadia counted rhythm out loud, her voice cracking but never stopping, and the boy coughed up half the Vessarin and lived.

On the fourth trip, Reyes didn't come up.

Ivo went in after him. No hesitation, no discussion—just boots off, knife between teeth, and gone into the black. Nadia and I held the canoe against the current and waited, and I did the thing you're not supposed to do, which is count seconds. At forty seconds I started composing the sentence I'd have to say to the village. At seventy, they surfaced together, twenty meters downstream, tangled in a fishing net, Ivo dragging Reyes by the collar toward the shallows with the stubbornness of a bush pilot who refuses to lose cargo.

We got Reyes breathing on the dock. He coughed, rolled over, vomited river water, and laughed—a wet, broken sound.

"I had it," he rasped.

"You absolutely did not," Ivo said.

"I had it under control."

"You were drowning, Tomás."

And there it was. A name, dropped into the night like a coin into a well. Reyes went very still. Nadia looked between them. I pretended very hard to be checking a rope knot.

"That's not my name anymore," Reyes said quietly.

"No," Ivo agreed. "Neither is yours mine. But some nights, the old ones fit better."

He didn't argue. He lay on the dock planks, shivering, staring up at a sky too clouded for stars, and after a while he said, to no one in particular: "The fleet's going to blow the upper channel at dawn. The one that feeds the falls. When that goes, the high terraces flood too. Everyone we moved tonight—we moved them into the water."

I sat down hard on the dock. "Then we need the water to go somewhere else."

"We need the charges to go somewhere else," Ivo corrected, and stood up, wringing river out of their sleeves, and looked at me with that expression I've learned means *I have a terrible idea and you're going to help me.*

"Nadia," Ivo called. "How well do you know the channels below the Sorrow Bend?"

She grinned, and it was not a nice grin. It was the grin of a girl whose grandmother had taught her every secret the river kept, and who had just been handed a reason to use them.

"Well enough to lose three barges in the fog," she said.

* * *

Dawn found us on the water again, which felt deeply unfair. Somewhere behind us the village climbed higher, carrying what couldn't be replaced and leaving what could. Ahead of us, through river mist thick as wool, the survey barges sat anchored in the narrows, their cranes silhouetted against a bruise-colored sky, their crews presumably eating breakfast and congratulating themselves.

In our canoe: me, Ivo, Nadia, and a government agent with no badge and a borrowed paddle, steering toward three hundred pounds of explosives with nothing but a teenage girl's memory of drowned channels and a mechanic's contempt for other people's equipment.

"This is piracy," I observed.

"It's salvage," Ivo said. "Those charges are abandoned property. Abandoned onto us."

"That's not what salvage means."

"It is tonight."

Nadia raised her hand, pointing left, where the mist hung lowest over water that looked identical to every other water. "Channel runs there. Two feet deep at dawn tide. Barges draw six."

Ivo smiled—the real one, the rare one, the one that makes the whole stubborn enterprise seem briefly worth it.

"Then let's introduce them to the bottom," they said, and dug the paddle in, and we went into the fog after the fleet.
