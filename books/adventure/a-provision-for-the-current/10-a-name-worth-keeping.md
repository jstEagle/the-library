## 10. A Name Worth Keeping

The radio in my plane had never worked properly. I want that on the record, since the record is the whole point of this story. For eleven years it crackled and hissed and swallowed half of what anyone said to me, and I'd come to think of it as a feature. A radio that works is a radio that can be answered.

Marta fixed it in an afternoon, kneeling in the mud with her sleeves rolled up and a soldering iron she'd liberated from the survey fleet's own wrecked barge, muttering curses in three languages and inventing a fourth. "Your wiring's a war crime," she told me.

"It's held together by loyalty," I said.

"It's held together by *insulation tape*, which is not the same thing."

But when she finished—when the static cleared and the dial actually found stations instead of just finding ghosts—I sat in the pilot's seat with my hands on my knees and understood that there was no going back to being a rumor. A working radio meant a voice that carried. And I had something to say.

Nadia sat on the wing, legs dangling over the propeller hub, still wringing river water out of her braids from the night before. The flood had taken three houses and the old meeting hall, and given back nothing but silt and one drowned goat we did not speak of. But everyone was alive. Everyone. Even Reyes, who stood at the edge of the village green looking like a man who had been rescued against his will and was deciding whether to be grateful or file a complaint. He'd chosen, in the end. When the second blast came down the gorge and his own crew chief gave the order to let the lower channels flood rather than abort the survey, Reyes had waded into the current after a Karaveli grandmother without hesitation. Whatever else he was, whatever badge he wore, he had been my comrade once, and the current had remembered it before he did.

I keyed the mic.

"This is—" and here I stopped, because the next word was the hard one. Eleven years of *Ivo Sandoval*, bush pilot, ferryman of medicine and gossip, and underneath it all a name I'd buried so deep I sometimes dreamed in it and woke up not knowing whose dream I was having.

Nadia swung down off the wing and stood beside the open cockpit door. She didn't say anything. She'd gotten good at that—knowing when a silence was a door you hold open versus a door you lock.

"This is Sera Voss," I said. "Late of Karavel. Late of a great many places. Broadcasting on open frequency, all bands, and I would take it as a personal kindness if somebody out there is recording."

Somebody always is. That's the first law of rivers and radios both.

* * *

We read the treaty four times over three days.

The first time, Marta read it, because she had the surveyor's cadence, every clause landing like a stake driven into ground: *the headwaters of the Vessarin, from the source spring to the falls of Terevin, are granted to the people of Karavel and their descendants, in perpetuity, irrevocable under the concord of three crowns.* The provision wasn't treasure. It was paper. It was paper that had waited sixty years in a lead-lined chest for someone stubborn enough to drag it into daylight, and it turned out that stubbornness was the one inheritance Nadia's grandmother had left in abundance.

The second reading went to the village elders, who listened standing up, the way Karaveli listen to things that matter, and afterward an old woman named Bess took Nadia's face in both hands and said, "You have your grandmother's chin," which made the girl cry in a way no flood ever had.

The third reading went out over my radio, and this is where the witnesses came in. Three nations' worth of them, because the concord required signatures from all three, and it turned out that when you broadcast a legal document in plain language on an open band, the world develops ears very quickly. A judge in Corvane replied by return transmission. A clerk in the Ossuary Territories asked for coordinates and got them. And from the third nation—the one whose survey fleet was even then rusting in the shallows below the falls, its dam project dead in the water along with its financing—there came, after a long and satisfying silence, a message consisting of a single word: *Acknowledged.*

"Two syllables," Marta said, reading it over my shoulder. "Sixty years of erasure, undone in two syllables."

"They were very busy syllables."

The fourth reading was Reyes's. He insisted. He stood in front of the whole village with his badge in his pocket instead of on his chest—he'd mailed it back, or claimed he had; with Reyes you check—and read the treaty aloud in a voice that cracked exactly once, on the word *perpetuity*. Then he took out a notebook and wrote, in his neat government hand, his testimony of everything that had happened: the orders, the blast, the flood, the choice he made in the water. He signed it. He handed it to me.

"You'll need corroborating witnesses," he said. "And you'll need someone who can testify to what the ministry knew and when. That's me. That's been me for a while now, apparently."

"What happens to you?" Nadia asked.

He looked at the river for a long moment. "I imagine I'll find out. But I'd rather find out on the right side of it." He almost smiled. "Besides. Someone has to keep you people out of actual prison, and Marta's already banned from two jurisdictions."

"Three," Marta said proudly.

* * *

The testimony was typed on Marta's portable typewriter, which she'd dried out over the cookfire like a woman resuscitating a drowned child, and it ran to nineteen pages. Flood, blasting, orders, rescue, treaty, names. When it came time for signatures, Marta signed *Marta Okonkwo* with a flourish she hadn't earned back yet but was clearly practicing for. Nadia signed *Nadia Emmerich-Karaveli*, having decided overnight that hyphenates were for people with nothing to lose, and she had plenty to lose now, which she informed us was the entire point.

Reyes signed his full name, rank and all, crossed out the rank, and initialed the crossing-out.

Then the pen came to me.

I looked at it for longer than a pen deserves. Somewhere behind me the village was rebuilding—a hammer ringing somewhere, children hauling planks, the smell of woodsmoke and cut timber and the particular green smell of a river running free above a gorge that would not, now, be plugged. My plane sat at the edge of the green with its radio antenna strung up like a flag. Everything I had built out of leaving was right here, asking me to stay, and staying had always been the thing I was worst at.

*Sera Voss.* I wrote it. Slowly, letter by letter, like learning handwriting again. It felt less like signing a document than digging something up—carefully, the way you excavate a foundation, checking for live wires. Sixty years of Karavel erased; eleven years of me erased; and here we both were on the same page, un-erased by the same pen.

"There," Marta said. "That wasn't so bad."

"My hands are shaking."

"That's called being alive. You should try it more often."

But here is the thing I learned in the writing of it, and it took the whole nineteen pages to learn: the name on the page was mine, but it was not the name of the person who had flown the medicine runs, who had hauled a stowaway out of a cargo hold and pretended to be annoyed about it, who had watched a drunk cartographer put down the bottle and pick up a compass. That person was Ivo Sandoval. Not a mask. Not a hiding place. A name I had built plank by plank the way the village was rebuilding its hall, and it had held in weather that would have sunk anything older.

So when the clerk's copy needed a final signature—the one that would go to the judges, the archives, the three crowns—I signed *Ivo Sandoval* underneath *Sera Voss*, and drew a small line connecting them, the way you'd draw a route on a chart.

"Sandoval?" Reyes said, reading upside down. "Not Voss?"

"Voss is where I'm from," I said. "Sandoval is who I am. Turns out you're allowed to have both, if you do the paperwork."

Nadia grinned at me like I'd finally passed a test I hadn't known I was taking. "Took you long enough."

"I've been busy drowning a dam."

* * *

They ask me now, sometimes—the clerks, the journalists who arrive by the same barges that came to break us, the occasional lost academic—why I don't go back to flying. Why a bush pilot with a working radio and a famous name doesn't take contracts, take money, take the easy sky.

Because the plane stays. That's the answer I give, and it's true, though not the whole truth. The whole truth is that I grounded it myself, the morning after the last signature, and told Nadia she could have the wings for a schoolhouse roof if the village wanted them, and she looked at me with such horror that I laughed for the first time in weeks.

"I'm joking," I said. "The plane flies. But it flies *from here* now. Medicine runs, mail, gossip. Same as always. Just—" I gestured at the green, the hammering, the smoke, the impossible village that wasn't on any map until it insisted on being on all of them. "Just with a home field."

Marta was already drawing the new charts. Proper ones, surveyed clean, the upper river mapped at last with the source marked and labeled—not *Terra Incognita*, not *Provision Site*, but its true name, the Karaveli name, which means, roughly translated, *the place that gives and cannot be owned*. She cried a little doing it. She claims it was the ink fumes.

And Reyes stayed too, at least through the hearings, sleeping in the rebuilt meeting hall and being terrible at carpentry and slowly, grudgingly, becoming the kind of neighbor who shows up with lumber and opinions. The ministry sent one letter summoning him home. He used it to light the cookfire. Nadia kept the ashes in a jar, "for historical purposes," she says, and I have stopped asking.

Last evening we all sat on my wing—old habit, new meaning—and watched the river run silver toward the falls, unbroken, unbought, going wherever it pleased.

"So," Nadia said. "What do we do tomorrow?"

Marta snorted. "Tomorrow? We rebuild a village, fight three governments, survive the rainy season, and teach this girl to fly."

"I already know how to fly."

"You know how to *crash slowly*. There's a difference."

I leaned back against the cold metal and listened to them argue, and thought about the chest the river had coughed up, and how I'd spent eleven years believing the current only ever took things away. It returns things too, if you wait. Names. People. Home.

The radio crackled behind us—someone downstream, wanting medicine, wanting news, wanting the world. I reached over and answered.
