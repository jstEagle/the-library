## 1. The Optimists' Arithmetic

The tornado was a good half mile wide and Elodie Eastwick had it exactly where he wanted it, which was to say he had it in front of him and not behind him, and if you knew anything about chasing you knew that the second condition was the only one that mattered.

"East of the cell," he said into his phone, one hand on the wheel, eyes on the wall cloud. "It's turning left. It's going to turn left and I'm going to be sitting on its shoulder like a traffic cop."

"You always say that," said Denny Okafor, on the other end, from the safety of a weather office two hundred miles away. "And then you come back with hail dents."

"This time I'm telling you. Look at the hook echo. The whole meso is rotating like a figure skater." He eased off the gas, let the truck settle into the gravel shoulder's rhythm. "This is the one that pays for the year. Footage like this goes to three networks minimum. Maybe four."

"What about the—" Denny started.

"Don't say their name during a chase," Elodie said. "Bad luck."

"It's not luck, it's interest—"

Elodie hung up on him, which was rude, but the tornado was beginning its left turn right on schedule, dragging its debris funnel across a soybean field like a finger through flour, and there was no room in the cab for both awe and arithmetic.

He'd been reading storms since he was nineteen, since the summer his father died and grief needed somewhere loud to go. Eleven years of it. He could read a sky the way literate men read a page: the green-black bruise of hail shafts, the beaver's tail of inflow cloud, the way a wall cloud would tighten and drop and tighten again like something deciding. He was good at this. That was the problem with Elodie Eastwick — he was genuinely, verifiably good at the one thing he did, and it paid just well enough to keep him doing it, and cost just enough to keep him borrowing.

The truck was a ten-year-old diesel with two hundred thousand miles and a roof rack carrying three cameras worth more than the truck itself. It had been financed by the Verrin brothers, who had also financed the cameras, the fuel card, and the repair after last spring's hail event, and whose names lived in a leather ledger that Elodie tried very hard never to think about as a ledger, because ledgers were what other people kept.

The tornado crossed the county road a quarter mile ahead of him.

Later, reviewing the footage frame by frame in a motel room that smelled of mildew and burnt coffee, he would see exactly where he made the call: the moment he decided the rope-out was happening faster than the storm's forward motion, that he could slip south behind it, that the road ahead — the one crossing the field, the dirt one, the one he couldn't quite see the far end of — was passable. He would see himself make the call the way you watch a stranger in old home video, someone with your face but none of your hard-won wisdom, and he would understand that the mistake wasn't ignorance. It was confidence. Ignorance you can teach. Confidence you can only outlive.

The road ended at a creek crossing hidden by six feet of corn.

He felt the front wheels drop before he saw the water. Then the world went brown and weightless and the airbag punched him so hard in the sternum he heard his own ribs answer, and when the truck stopped moving it was nose-down in the creek bed at forty-five degrees, engine screaming steam, cornstalks draped over the hood like funeral flowers, and somewhere above him the tornado was roping out into the evening sky, beautiful and indifferent, having taken nothing from anyone today except Elodie's entire future.

* * *

He climbed out through the passenger window because the driver's door was against the bank. His hands were shaking. Not from fear — he catalogued that honestly, standing in the creek water up to his shins — but from the specific tremor of a man doing math he doesn't want to finish.

The cameras were fine. Of course they were. He'd spent real money on cases. The footage card in his breast pocket held eleven minutes of the best tornado video of his career, shot from inside the circulation's outer band, and it was worth maybe eight thousand dollars to the right buyer, and the truck was worth negative fourteen, and the debt —

He sat down on the creek bank and let himself do the math, because not doing it had never once made it smaller.

Eleven thousand, four hundred. That was the principal the Verrins would say he owed, though the number they'd written down had a way of growing between visits, fed by fees whose names changed each time — a documentation fee, a risk adjustment, once memorably a *weather contingency*, which even Elodie had to admire for nerve. With the truck totaled and the gap insurance lapsed because he'd missed a payment in June, the hole went from deep to structural.

His phone buzzed. Denny.

"I saw the radar," Denny said. "You okay?"

"Ribs are mad. Truck's dead."

A pause on the line, long enough that Elodie could hear Denny choosing words. "You want me to come get you?"

"No. I know where I have to go."

Another pause. "Elodie. Don't sign anything."

"They don't make me sign things anymore," Elodie said. "That's the thing people don't understand about them. Signing is for strangers."

* * *

The Verrin Brothers Lending Company occupied the ground floor of a converted feed store on the east side of Harrow Falls, behind a façade painted a color that might once have been yellow and had settled into something more honest. There was no sign saying LENDING COMPANY. There was a sign that said VERRIN BROS., FINE GOODS & PROVISIONS, which had been true for approximately nine months in 1998 before the brothers discovered that lending was fine goods too, and the best provision of all was cash.

Inside, it looked like what it pretended to be: shelves of hardware, coils of rope, a cooler of soda pop, a rack of work gloves. And in the back, past the counter where a radio played big-band music low enough to be a secret, a small office with two desks facing each other, because the Verrin brothers liked to conduct business side by side, like a doctor and a nurse, or a priest and an altar boy.

Casper Verrin was the older by four years and the larger by fifty pounds, a broad soft man with a round pink face and the unshakeable cheerfulness of a man who has never once been surprised by bad news because he causes most of it. Milo Verrin was thin, quick, wore glasses he didn't need, and did all the actual numbers, which he did with visible pleasure, the way some men do crosswords.

They were both behind their desks when Elodie walked in, wet to the knee, ribs strapped, and they both lit up.

"There he is!" Casper spread his arms as if greeting a son home from war. "There's the storm king! Milo, look at him. Walked in here out of the weather like a man coming home."

"He looks like a man who drove a truck into a creek," Milo said pleasantly, without looking up from a paper in front of him. He didn't need to look up. They'd known about the truck before Elodie had finished climbing out of it; a farmer down the road had a nephew who texted.

"Creek found the truck," Elodie said. "Road was washed out."

"Mm." Milo turned the paper around and slid it across the desk. It was a printout of the insurance declaration page, and someone had circled, in red ink, the lapse notice date. "June fourteenth. You missed a payment in June, Elodie. One payment. Sixty-one dollars and forty cents." He shook his head with genuine sorrow. "Sixty-one dollars. That's the whole story right there, isn't it? Empires fall over sixty-one dollars."

"The gap coverage would've covered the difference," Casper said, leaning forward, elbows on the desk, voice dropping into the register of a confessor. "You'd have walked away clean today. Clean! Instead—" he gestured at Elodie's soaked trousers, his strapped ribs, the general ruin of him, "—instead we're all here, together, working on it. Which is fine. We like working on it. We're optimists, the three of us."

"I need a number," Elodie said.

Milo smiled and pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward himself, and Elodie watched him write, and understood that the number had existed for hours, maybe days, and the writing was theater. But it was good theater. Milo wrote numbers the way Casper hugged: warmly, and with total control.

"Truck's collateral, so that's settled — we take the wreck, sell it for parts, call it even on the vehicle. That leaves your balance." He wrote a figure. "Carried forward. Plus the season's advance we gave you in April, which you'll recall was against earnings that are now, regrettably, hypothetical." Another figure. "Plus service costs." A third figure, larger than the first two combined, delivered without a flicker.

Elodie looked at the total. It was a number with a shape. Not a number you paid; a number you lived inside, like weather.

"That's not what we agreed in April."

"We agreed in April you'd have a season," Casper said gently. "Seasons end, Elodie. Some of them end in creeks." He stood, came around the desk, and put a heavy warm hand on Elodie's shoulder, and the terrible thing, the thing Elodie could never explain to anyone who hadn't felt it, was that the hand was genuinely kind. Casper Verrin liked him. That was the horror of the Verrin brothers: they weren't wolves in suits. They were friendly men who happened to eat people, and they enjoyed the eating the way other men enjoyed fishing — patiently, skillfully, with respect for the fish.

"Here's what we're going to do," Casper said. "Because we believe in you. Genuinely. You've got an eye. Milo says it, I say it. You read sky better than anybody in three counties, and a man with an eye is never really broke, he's just early."

"Thirty days," Milo said, capping his pen with a click that sounded final as a bolt. "We carry the balance thirty days. You bring us a plan that services it — real money or real collateral — and we restructure, and everybody stays friends."

"And if I don't?"

Casper's smile didn't move but something behind it did, a shift like light changing on water. "Now why would you ask a question like that on such a nice day? Look outside. Sun's coming out. Tornado weather's over for the week." He patted Elodie's shoulder twice, companionably. "Thirty days. We'll do lunch when you've thought. I'll buy."

* * *

Outside, the sun was indeed coming out, gilding the puddles in the lot, and Elodie stood by the wreck of his truck on the flatbed tow and felt the dread settle into him properly — not the spike of the crash, but the slow cold pool of it, the kind that lasted.

Thirty days. Eight thousand dollars of footage, sold fast, maybe covered a third of the service costs alone. He had no collateral. He had a rented room, a camera bag, and a reputation for being lucky that had just expired.

He drove Denny's borrowed car back along the river road toward town because it was the only road, and that was how he came around the bend below Miller's Bend and saw what the flood had done to the far bank — a whole slice of hillside slumped into the water like a sleeping man rolling over, trees tipped, dark earth exposed in a raw wound forty yards wide.

He almost didn't stop. He should not have stopped. Every instinct of a man with thirty days said keep driving, go sell the footage, start the begging.

But the surveying work was posted on the county board — flood damage assessment, per diem plus mileage — and Elodie knew banks. Knew how rivers ate them and what they gave back. There was money in disaster, if you were the one holding the clipboard instead of the shovel.

He parked and walked down to the slump, boots sinking in the fresh mud, and stood looking at the exposed strata of the hillside: topsoil, clay, gravel, and then — near the waterline, where the river had done its finest surgery — a corner of something that was not soil, not root, not rock.

Dark wood. Banded in iron. And on the visible face, half-scoured of a century of mud, a stamped impression in faded wax-red: a crest he didn't recognize, a tower and a star, and beneath it letters worn nearly smooth.

Elodie crouched. The river moved past him, patient, carrying everything it had ever taken somewhere downstream, and the chest sat in the wound it had opened, waiting to be found by exactly one person.

He looked over his shoulder at the empty road. Then he took out his multi-tool.
