## 3. A Dead Nation's Signature

The university archive smelled like cold paper and old ambition. Elodie Eastwick stood at the reference desk at nine in the morning, still smelling faintly of river mud, wearing his one clean shirt, and lying to a librarian with a straight face.

"Genealogy," he said. "Family history. My grandmother came from somewhere in the old Kessavian territories."

It was a good lie. It had the right texture—boring, personal, the kind of request that made archivists want to go back to their coffee. The librarian, a young man with wire-rimmed glasses and the posture of someone who'd never been chased by anything faster than a deadline, nodded and pointed him toward the eastern stacks.

"Kessavian holdings are in the restricted annex. You'll need to sign in. No pens, no bags, gloves for anything pre-dissolution."

"Dissolution," Elodie repeated, testing the word.

The librarian's eyebrows went up slightly. "You don't know the history?"

"Grandmother didn't like to talk about the old country."

That was true of a lot of grandmothers, apparently, because the librarian accepted it without question and led him back through corridors of shelving into a room where the light came in high and yellow through frosted windows, and the silence had weight to it, like snow.

The annex held four long tables and perhaps six thousand documents. Elodie signed the ledger—*E. Eastwick, genealogical research*—and felt a small private irony at writing his real name into a book of lies.

He started with the atlases.

Kessavia appeared in them the way a person appears in old photographs—present, then fading, then gone. In the 1898 survey atlas it was a full nation: a wedge of highland territory wedged between two larger neighbors, its escarpment drawn in confident hatching, its capital—*Vessik*—marked with a star. In the 1911 edition the star was smaller. In the 1919 edition the name was printed in italics, the cartographer's equivalent of a footnote. By 1924 the space where Kessavia had been was labeled simply: *DISPUTED TERRITORIES — ADMINISTERED.*

Twenty-six years. A country with a thousand years of history, erased in a single generation.

Elodie pulled the 1919 atlas closer and looked at the escarpment. The coordinates from the ledger, which he had memorized and then burned the paper they were written on, sat somewhere along that hatched spine of rock. He let his finger hover over the place without touching it. Superstition. He was not a superstitious man, but he had learned that some things you don't point at until you understand what they are.

* * *

The histories were worse than the atlases, because the histories had gaps shaped like answers.

He worked through the morning, building the story the way he'd build a storm—reading the pressure drops, the wind shifts, the things not said. Here is what the official record offered:

Kessavia had been a mountain nation, poor in arable land, rich in three things: timber, minerals, and a legal tradition so old and so meticulous that neighboring countries had historically hired Kessavian jurists to settle their own disputes. The royal house—the Aldermont line—had ruled for nine centuries. The nation's wealth in its final decades had come less from what it dug out of the ground than from what it held in trust: escrow accounts, inheritance claims, disputed estates from a dozen borders' worth of conflicts. Kessavia was, in essence, the region's safe-deposit box. Everyone's gold slept under Kessavian law.

Then, in the winter of 1918–1919, three things happened in quick succession.

First: a border incident. A munitions depot in the neighboring nation of Ostrava—forty miles from the Kessavian line—exploded with a force that registered on instruments in three countries. Two hundred dead. Ostrava's government, within a week, produced evidence that the depot had been sabotaged by Kessavian agents, and demanded reparations that would have gutted the Kessavian treasury for fifty years.

Second: the Kessavian court, in what historians called the *Winter Audits*, opened its own investigation—and found, in its own records, evidence that the sabotage had been real. That agents of the Aldermont crown had done it. That the order had come from very high up.

Third: the records burned.

The national archive at Vessik caught fire on the night of February 3rd, 1919, and burned for two days. Every document relating to the Winter Audits was destroyed. So were the escrow ledgers. So were, apparently, a great many other things, because when the smoke cleared, the Kessavian government resigned en masse, the Aldermont heir abdicated a throne that no longer had a nation under it, and the neighboring powers moved in to administer the territory "temporarily"—a temporary arrangement that had now lasted a hundred years.

The history books disagreed about everything except the fire. Some said the royal family burned the records to hide their guilt. Some said they burned them to destroy evidence that would have bankrupted half the aristocracies of Europe, guilty and innocent alike. One monograph, self-published and barely literate, insisted the fire was set by Ostravan agents to erase proof the sabotage had been *staged*—a false flag to give Ostrava pretext for annexation.

Nobody agreed on what was hidden. Everybody agreed something was.

Elodie sat back and looked at the ledger's memory in his head. The wax seal, unbroken until his knife. The careful hand. The clause he'd read four times on the riverbank, by matchlight, his heart doing something embarrassing in his chest:

*...to the lawful bearer, upon presentation at the place designated, and upon claim entered before the first snowfall following the centenary of dissolution, all provisions, holdings, and obligations of the Aldermont trust shall devolve entire...*

First snowfall following the centenary. The centenary was this year. A hundred years since the dissolution. Which meant the window was open *now*, and would close when the first snow fell on the escarpment—and the escarpment, he knew from a lifetime of storm work, caught its first snow early. Sometimes October. Once, in his grandfather's day, September.

He did the arithmetic the way he did all his arithmetic lately, in terms of days and dollars. Sixty days, maybe fewer, to get up a river and up a mountain and stand at a set of coordinates and claim an inheritance from a country that had been legally dead for a century.

It was insane. It was also, possibly, the only arithmetic in his life that currently balanced.

* * *

He found the legal material in the afternoon, and it was the legal material that scared him.

Because here was the thing about Kessavia: the neighbors had dissolved the nation, but they had never quite managed to dissolve the *law*. The treaties administering the territory contained carve-outs—clauses the negotiators had probably considered harmless housekeeping at the time. Property held in trust under Kessavian law remained under Kessavian law. Claims of inheritance, properly documented, could still be entered. There was even, in one dusty treaty annex, a provision for a *claims commissioner*—an office that had stood vacant for a hundred years, but which the paperwork insisted still existed, in the way that old paperwork insists on anything.

Which meant the ledger in his coat wasn't a curiosity. It was a *live instrument*. Somewhere in the tangle of international law, a door had been left unlocked for a hundred years, and he was holding the key.

And doors that stay unlocked for a hundred years are usually unlocked on purpose.

He copied references until his hand ached—no pens allowed, so he did it in pencil, filling a small notebook with citations he'd have to come back for—and by late afternoon he had a working theory with three legs: the trust was real; the claim was real; and the reason the window had never closed was that everyone who might have closed it had spent a century making sure no claimant ever reached the door.

He was re-reading the treaty annex for the fourth time when he noticed the archivist.

She was at the far end of the annex, at a desk he'd have sworn had been empty all day, doing something with a card catalogue and a magnifying glass. Fifties, maybe, grey hair cut short and practical, a cardigan the color of wet slate. She had the stillness of people who work with old things—the particular patience of someone who knows that everything in the room has already waited a hundred years and can wait a hundred more.

He might not have noticed her at all, except that when he looked up, she was already looking at him.

Not staring. Worse than staring. She had the expression of a woman doing a small calculation and arriving at a number she found interesting.

Elodie went back to his treaty.

Ten minutes later she was at his table. No announcement, no footsteps—he'd have sworn the floorboards hadn't creaked. She set down a slim volume beside his notebook with a soft click.

"You'll want this," she said. "The 1921 claims register. It lists every inheritance petition filed against the Aldermont trust in the first two years after dissolution. Forty-one of them. All rejected. All rejected on the same grounds—*documentation insufficient*."

He looked at the book, then at her. "I'm doing genealogy."

"Mm." She pulled out the chair across from him and sat, unhurried, as if the room belonged to her, which it probably did. "Your grandmother's family. Which you've been researching for—" she glanced at the sign-in ledger, "—six hours. During which you've requested the dissolution treaties, the Winter Audit correspondence, the Vessik fire commission report, and the claims procedure annex. That's not genealogy, Mr. Eastwick. That's due diligence."

His pulse did something slow and cold. "I don't think we've been introduced."

"Maren Kolb. Senior archivist." She said it the way other people said their rank. "I've worked this collection for thirty-one years. Do you know how many people have requested the claims procedure annex in that time?" She didn't wait for him to guess. "Four. Two were law students. One was a very confused man looking for his uncle's war medals. And you."

"Maybe I'm a law student."

"Law students take notes with pens they've smuggled in. You follow the rules. You're not here to learn something." Her eyes went to his notebook, then to his face. "You're here to *verify* something. You already have a document. You're checking it against the record."

The silence stretched. Elodie had spent his life reading skies—knowing when a green tint meant hail, when a sudden calm meant the tornado had cut off and was cycling again. He read her the same way. She wasn't hostile. She wasn't reporting him to anyone, or she'd have done it already. She was *interested*, and interest from a person like this was more dangerous than hostility, because hostility you could walk away from.

"Suppose," he said carefully, "I was verifying something. Hypothetically."

"Suppose you were."

"Is there anything in the record about the trust's obligations? Not the holdings. The *obligations*."

Something moved behind her eyes—there and gone, quick as a cloud shadow. "Why do you ask it that way?"

"Because everything I've read today talks about what the trust *holds*. Nobody talks about what it *owes*."

Maren Kolb was quiet for a long moment. When she answered, her voice had dropped, though there was no one else in the annex to hear it.

"Because the holdings were burned, Mr. Eastwick. But the obligations were never written down at all. They were kept in a separate ledger—*the Provision*—held by the Aldermont family personally. It was never in the Vessik archive. It didn't burn with the rest, because it was never there." She leaned forward. "The fire commission spent two years cataloguing what was lost. You know what they concluded? That the fire was *incomplete*. That documents which should have been in the archive were missing *before* the fire. Someone cleaned house first, then burned the empty rooms."

The annex was very quiet. High above them, the yellow light had gone amber with the late afternoon, and somewhere a radiator ticked like a cooling engine.

"The Provision," Elodie said.

"If that's what you're holding," Maren said, "then you're not holding an inheritance. You're holding a *liability* that a dead royal family spent its last days making sure no one would ever have to account for." She stood, smoothing her cardigan, the conversation apparently over. "The register is yours to read. Forty-one claimants, Mr. Eastwick. Read what happened to the ones who got closest."

He read.

The thirty-ninth claimant was a woman named Ilse Vantorre, who in 1920 filed documentation so complete that the administering powers couldn't reject it on procedure. Her petition was denied on a technicality discovered *after* filing. She appealed. The appeal record ends with a note that Ms. Vantorre withdrew her claim for reasons of health.

The fortieth was a shipping magnate who died before his hearing. The forty-first was a name that had been struck through so thoroughly the ink had gone through the paper.

Elodie closed the register and sat in the amber light, and the thrill that had been carrying him all day—the *treasure*, the *debt*, the *way out*—curdled slowly into something with a different taste. Not fear exactly. The specific cold you feel when you realize the storm you've been chasing has turned and is now chasing you.

When he looked up, Maren Kolb was back at her desk at the far end of the room, magnifying glass in hand, apparently absorbed in her cards. But as he packed his notebook—as he signed out, *E. Eastwick*, the last name in the day's column—she spoke without looking up.

"The annex closes at six. But I'm here most evenings, Mr. Eastwick. If your hypothetical document raises further questions."

It wasn't an offer. Or it was, but offers from people like Maren Kolb came with hooks in them, and he could feel this one set already, just under the skin, the way a barb sets when you pull the wrong direction.

"Thanks," he said. "I'll probably be back."

"I know," she said, and turned a page.

Outside, the evening was clear and cold for early autumn, and the first stars were out over the university rooftops, and Elodie stood on the steps doing the one piece of arithmetic he'd been avoiding all day: the ledger said *first snowfall*. Maren Kolb said *liability*. Forty-one claimants said *don't*.

And somewhere across town, the Verrin brothers were smiling at somebody, counting somebody's days, and Elodie had twenty-six of them left.

He turned up his collar and walked into the dark, feeling the way he always felt in the last hour before a storm broke—certain it was coming, certain it was bad, and already too far in to turn around.
