## 4. Terms of Collection

They found me on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays are when the world collects.

I was at the docks doing the arithmetic of ruin—counting what the insurance man might believe, subtracting what he wouldn't—when I saw the Verrin brothers' car glide into the lot like a well-fed animal choosing a spot in the sun. It was a long silver thing, older than both of them combined and better maintained than anything I'd ever owned. They stepped out in matching linen jackets, one cream, one bone, and the whole dock seemed to lean toward them the way grass leans before weather.

"Eastwick!" called Dov Verrin, the older by four years and the louder by a factor of ten. He spread his arms as if we were family reunited at a wedding. "You're a hard man to find. No, that's not true. You're an easy man to find who makes himself hard. There's a difference, and the difference is what we're here to discuss."

His brother, Ash, said nothing. Ash never said much. He had a face like a closed ledger and a habit of standing slightly behind and to the left, which in my experience was where the interest accrued.

"Dov," I said. "Ash."

"You look thin," Dov said, taking my hand in both of his, warm and dry and firm, a handshake like a receipt. "Grief does that. Or diet. Which is it?"

"Insurance paperwork."

"Ah. The truck." He clucked his tongue, genuinely sorrowful, or genuinely good at seeming so—the two were indistinguishable in him, which was the whole art of the man. "That was a fine truck. You drove it like a man who loved it. And lost it like a man who didn't love himself. We watched the footage, you know. The chase company sells it. You cut under the mesocyclone on a failing road with a wall cloud rotating at your six o'clock. Magnificent stupidity. Ash, wasn't it magnificent?"

"Stupidity," Ash said.

"Both things," Dov agreed. "Come. Walk with us. The water's high, and I like to see what it takes."

So we walked the dock, the three of us, and I understood within the first thirty steps that this was not a social call, and within the first fifty that it was worse than a collection call. This was a courtship.

"Thirty days," Dov said. "That's what we gave you. You're what—eleven days in? Twelve? And already the river has handed you a gift. Isn't that remarkable. Ash, tell him what the river handed him."

"A chest," Ash said.

The word went through me like a hook through the gill. I kept walking. I kept my hands in my pockets, where they could do no damage.

"I don't know what you—"

"There's a man," Dov said pleasantly, "who works the fuel barge, third slip. Chatty fellow. You bought diesel from him the morning after the flood, and you had mud on you from the north bank, which is interesting, because the north bank is where nothing of yours is, and you had a hand truck, which is interesting, because a hand truck is a tool for moving things too heavy to carry, and you had—" he paused, savoring it, a man unwrapping a candy he'd been saving—"a look on your face. Ash, tell him about the look."

"Like a man who'd found something," Ash said.

"Like a man," Dov said, "who'd found something."

The river moved beneath us, brown and heavy with the season's rain, carrying whatever it carried. I thought about the chest in my rented storage unit, wrapped in a tarp under a tarp, and about the ledger inside it, and about the coordinates I had memorized and then written down and then burned the paper, and about how none of that mattered, because Dov Verrin was looking at me with the patient delight of a man who had already won and was enjoying the part where I found out.

"Okay," I said. "I found something in the bank collapse."

"Did you."

"A chest. Old. Sealed. Stamped with some dead country's crest."

"Kessavia," Ash said.

I stopped walking. That was a mistake and I knew it the moment I did it, because Dov's smile widened by a precise, calibrated millimeter, the smile of a fisherman feeling the line come tight.

"You know the name," he said.

"Everyone knows the name. It's on the old survey maps."

"It is on nothing," Dov said cheerfully. "That's the point of erasure. But we know it. We know a lot of things, Eastwick. It's our business. Lending is a business of knowing. You want to know the difference between us and a bank? A bank lends you money and hopes you pay. We lend you money and *know* you'll pay, because we know everything there is to know about you, and what we don't know, we buy." He gestured at the fuel barge, at the gulls, at the whole gray morning. "The chest. Open it for us."

I looked at him. He looked back with those warm, hooded eyes, and I understood the shape of the trap I was standing in, and it was this: if I said no, I confirmed the chest was worth something. If I said yes, I handed them the thing before I knew what it was worth. And if I lied—if I told them it was junk, ballast, river trash—they would smile and thank me and know I was lying, and they would *enjoy* knowing it, and the knowing would sit in the ledger of our relationship like a debt accruing interest I couldn't see.

"There's nothing in it but paper," I said. "Old paper. A ledger of some kind. Coordinates. It's an inheritance claim, maybe, from before the country folded. Probably worthless. Probably the claim's expired, or the land's been parceled out a dozen times, or—"

"Or it hasn't," Ash said.

"Or it hasn't," I admitted.

Dov had stopped walking. He stood at the rail now, looking out at the brown water, hands folded on the rail like a man at church. "Do you know what we do, Eastwick? Really? People think we break legs. We've never broken a leg. Legs are for people who've run out of imagination, and we have never once run out of imagination." He turned. "We fund things. That's all we do. We find a man with a truck and a talent for reading weather, and we fund the truck. We find a man with a chest and a dead nation's crest, and we fund the *expedition*. We are not your enemies, Elodie. We are your most devoted fans. We believe in you the way no one else in your life has ever believed in you: with money."

"I don't need—"

"You need everything." He said it kindly, which made it worse. "You need a truck. You need fuel. You need permits, gear, a guide who knows the escarpment, food, rope, insurance for the people who'll break. You've done the arithmetic—I can see it behind your eyes, you do it constantly, it's why you're thin. The arithmetic says you cannot get up that mountain alone. The arithmetic says you have eleven days of thirty and nothing coming in. The arithmetic says the legal window closes at first snow, and first snow on the high escarpment is—" he glanced at his brother, who said, "Six weeks," and Dov said, "Six weeks, thank you, Ash, six weeks, and you have no truck and no money and no crew, and every day you wait, someone else is reading the same flood reports you are."

He let that sit. Gulls argued over something in the shallows.

"Here is our offer," Dov said. "We fund the whole thing. Everything. Top to bottom. You lead it—you're the one with the claim, the one with the name on the ledger, whatever the paper says. In return: sixty percent."

"Sixty." I laughed, and it came out wrong, too high. "Of what? A claim that's probably worthless?"

"Of whatever's there. And—" he held up one finger, and the finger was the whole man, the whole family, the whole warm smiling apparatus of them—"total disclosure. You show us the ledger. Every page. You tell us what it says, where it points, what you find. No surprises, Eastwick. Surprises are how partnerships die, and we don't want to die, and we'd hate for you to."

I stood at the rail and felt the nausea come up through me, slow and specific, the particular sickness of lying to people who *liked* it. That was the thing nobody understood about the Verrins. Menace was easy. You could brace against menace. But these two collected liars the way other men collected stamps, and they were gentle about it, and the gentleness was the trap. Every word I spoke now was being weighed on a scale I couldn't see, and the scale was calibrated by people who had spent forty years weighing exactly this.

"Total disclosure," I said.

"Every page."

"And if the claim's worthless? If it's expired, if the land's gone, if it's all ash and old wax?"

"Then you owe us nothing for the expedition," Dov said, magnanimous, radiant. "We'll have spent it on your dream, and you'll still owe us the original debt, which we'll continue to carry at our usual generous terms, because we believe in you." He smiled. "But it isn't worthless."

"No?"

"No. Because if it were worthless, you'd have shown it to us by now. A man hides what's precious, Eastwick. You've been hiding it for eleven days. You've slept badly for eleven days—I can see it. You've been to the storage yard twice, and the second time you checked the lock twice. You memorized the coordinates and burned the paper, which was smart, and also told us everything, because only precious things get burned." He pushed off the rail and straightened his jacket. "So. Sixty percent. Total disclosure. Our man on the boat—Ash will arrange the supplies, the fuel, the permits. And in exchange, you get up that mountain before the snow, and you come back a free man, and we all live happily in each other's debt forever."

"And if I say no?"

Ash moved, slightly. It was nothing—a shift of weight, a recalculation of the space between his body and mine—and it was everything.

"Then we wish you luck," Dov said warmly. "Truly. We'd hate to see you fail. But luck is expensive, Eastwick, and yours is currently financed by us."

I looked at the river. I thought about the ledger in the dark of the storage unit, and about the second line of the heir's entry, the one I hadn't told anyone about, the one that had made my hands shake—the name of the claimant, which was not a name from a century ago at all, but a name that was still in the phone book, still on a sign in a town I could drive to in a day. A living heir. Which meant a living claim. Which meant the window wasn't just open; it was *waiting*.

And I thought: they don't know that. Dov knows the chest, the crest, the coordinates. He does not know the name. The name is the one thing I have that they don't, and the moment I show them the ledger, it stops being mine.

"Deal," I said. "Sixty percent. Total disclosure. But I read you the ledger myself—my copy, my hands. You don't take it, you don't photograph it, you don't have it out of my sight. That's my price on top of yours."

Dov's eyebrows went up, genuinely delighted. "Look at him, Ash. Negotiating. Eleven days from drowning and he's negotiating." He took my hand again, both hands, warm as a banked fire. "Done. You read. We listen. We're very good listeners."

We shook on it. Ash shook too, briefly, his grip dry and exact, and in the half-second our palms touched I had the distinct, unshakable feeling of being *read*—flipped through like a document, every page, every margin note—and I understood that whatever I was hiding, he had already guessed the shape of it, and was simply waiting, with the patience of a man who owned all the time in the world, for me to hand it over myself.

They walked back to the car. Dov waved at the fuel barge man, who waved back, and I understood that too—the informant hadn't been bought so much as *kept*, the way you keep a lamp lit in a window.

That night I went to the storage unit and unwrapped the chest and read the ledger again by lamplight, all of it, twice. The coordinates. The seal. The heir's name, inked in a hand that had pressed too hard, as if the writer had been afraid or hurried or both. And beneath the name, in a different ink, older and browner, a single line I'd translated on the third night and hadn't slept since:

*Let the claimant understand what he claims.*

I sat with it a long time. Then I did the thing I'd been avoiding: I took out a map and a pencil and started making a list of people desperate enough, skilled enough, and broken enough to say yes to a trip up the escarpment before first snow. Because I couldn't do it alone—that arithmetic was closed—and because a crew was cover, witnesses, and in the worst case, people between me and the mountain's answer.

The first name on the list was a guide who'd been banned from the escarpment for taking clients up a route that killed two of them. The second was a cook who'd been asked to leave three kitchens for reasons involving explosives. The third was a university archivist who had watched me a little too closely across a reading room table, and who I suspected was already packing.

I wrote them all down. Outside, the first hard rain of the season came down on the storage roof like a countdown.

Six weeks to first snow. Eleven days of thirty. And somewhere in a silver car across town, two cheerful men were telling each other exactly how much I'd lied to them tonight—and laughing.
