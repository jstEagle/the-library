## 5. Crew of the Desperate

The trouble with hiring desperate people, Elodie had learned over thirty years of chasing weather and dodging creditors, was that they lied beautifully. Desperation was a finishing school. It taught a man to smile with his whole face while calculating exits.

He needed three liars. He'd found them in four days, which he considered either efficiency or a symptom.

* * *

Tomas Reyes came first, because Tomas was easiest to find if you knew where disgraced guides went to drink, and Elodie knew, because for two years after his own father's bankruptcy he'd been drinking in the same one.

The Anchorline sat at the end of a rotting pier on the Graywater, a bar that smelled of creosote and old arguments. Tomas occupied the same stool he'd occupied since the incident—everyone called it "the incident" now, even Tomas, with a certain grim pride—and when Elodie sat down beside him, the bartender set out two glasses without being asked. That kind of place.

"You're the storm man," Tomas said. "Eastwick. You totaled your truck in April."

"News travels."

"The Verrins like their debtors' failures publicized. It's advertising." He turned his glass a quarter-turn, not drinking. "What do you want?"

"A guide up the Kessavia Reach. Past the falls, past the old waystations, to the escarpment headwall."

Tomas laughed once, without humor. It sounded like a rockfall starting. "Nobody guides the Reach anymore. Nobody's guided it since I—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "Since the incident."

"I know about the incident. The Wexler party. Two clients lost above the treeline in a whiteout you said wouldn't come."

"They said it wouldn't come," Tomas corrected quietly. "I said turn back. They were paying me to say yes." He finally drank. "So you've come to the only guide stupid enough to have learned that lesson, hoping I'll say yes again."

"I'm hoping you'll say yes for the right reason this time."

"And what reason is that?"

Elodie laid the folded paper on the bar—not the ledger itself, never the ledger itself, but a page he'd copied in Maren Kolb's archive under the pretense of checking citations: coordinates, the Kessavian seal drawn from memory, and a single line from the inheritance clause. *To the claimant who presents himself before first snow.*

Tomas looked at it for a long time. Longer than a man looks at coordinates. A man who'd spent his life reading mountains looked at those numbers like they were a sentence being read aloud at his trial.

"That's the upper Reach," he said. "That's the country nobody comes back from rich."

"People have tried."

"People have died trying. Rich is different." He pushed the paper back. "Who else knows?"

"One other person. An archivist."

"An archivist." Tomas said the word like it was a species of rodent. "And the men who own your truck debt?"

"Don't know the route. Yet."

Tomas nodded slowly, and Elodie watched him do the arithmetic—the same arithmetic every desperate man does, weighing the weight of what he owes against the weight of what might be found. Elodie knew the sum came out wrong. He also knew Tomas would go anyway.

"My fee is half up front," Tomas said. "And when we're above the waystations, my word is law. If I say the mountain says no, the mountain has spoken. No argument, no appeal."

"Agreed."

"Why?" Tomas asked suddenly. "Why you? Why this? Men don't climb into dead countries over paperwork unless something's eating them."

Elodie gave him the truth, or the shape of it. "Something is. It has teeth and it smiles a lot, and there are two of them."

Tomas almost smiled back. "Then we understand each other. I'm drinking with mine."

* * *

Petra Ilves he found where she'd said she'd be, which was a kitchen, because Petra Ilves was always in kitchens—it was the one room where her particular talents were legal.

She ran the grill at a roadhouse outside Marrow Junction, a woman built like a doorstop with forearms that could crack walnuts by reputation alone. When Elodie walked in, she didn't look up from the flat-top.

"If you're here about the health inspection, the rat lives in the walls, not the food."

"I'm here about a job. Cook and crew, six weeks, river and mountain."

"Six weeks pays nothing anywhere. What's the real job?"

Elodie glanced at the lone customer, an old man asleep over his coffee. She followed his glance, sighed, and jerked her chin toward the back. In the kitchen's blue fluorescent hum, she crossed her arms and waited.

"There's a vault in the escarpment," he said. "Kessavian. Sealed a century ago. We need it opened."

"We need," she repeated. "We already got a guide, then. So you need someone to open things."

"I'm told you can open most anything."

"I'm told a lot of things." But her eyes had changed—sharpened, gone somewhere else. Somewhere with a mining contract in it, though Elodie didn't know that yet. "Who told you about me?"

"A man at the quarry office owed me a favor. Said you handled the blasting when the company stopped paying for licensed men. Said the charges always went exactly where you wanted and never where anyone expected."

"He talks too much." She picked up a spatula, put it down again. "Kessavian vaults. You mean the Reach country. The old nation."

"You know it?"

"Everyone from upriver knows it. My grandmother used to sing songs in Kessavian. Didn't know what they meant. Just that you didn't sing them loud near government men." She wiped her hands on her apron, a gesture that meant decision made but not yet admitted. "Terms. I cook, I carry, and when we hit stone that needs persuading, nobody stands behind me and nobody asks how I learned. And I want double shares."

"Double shares of what? There may be nothing in the vault but dust."

"Then I want double shares of dust." Her mouth twitched, almost a smile. "When do we leave?"

"Ten days. Before the water drops and before the snow."

"Snow's early this year," she said. "Everyone upriver says so. The birds went south in August." She said it lightly, but Elodie felt it land like a forecast.

* * *

Maren Kolb invited herself on the fourth day, which was fitting, because everything about Maren arrived uninvited and acted like it owned the premises.

He found her waiting outside his rented room at the Ferryman's Rest, sitting on the porch rail with her coat buttoned to the throat despite the mild morning, a leather satchel across her knees like a shield.

"You copied page forty-one of the inheritance registry," she said, before he'd cleared the steps. "You didn't sign the access log correctly. You wrote 'genealogical inquiry.' People doing genealogical inquiries don't photograph documents with their phone under their jacket."

"I was going to return it."

"No, you weren't. You were going to run upriver with a forged provenance and hope no one checked the seal impression against the archive's reference collection." She stood. "Which, incidentally, is fine work. The forgery. Whoever made your ledger knew the Kessavian chancery hand intimately. That's the part that should worry you."

Elodie kept his face still, which took effort. "You've seen my ledger?"

"I've seen enough of it. Enough to know three things." She held up fingers. "One: it's genuine period work. Two: it names an heir through a line that officially has no descendants. Three: it is one of three copies, and the other two are accounted for—or were, until 1971, when one vanished from a government repository during an inventory that your friends the Verrin brothers' father helped conduct."

The morning seemed to get colder. "You've done a lot of homework on my finances."

"It's my job to know where documents go and whose hands they pass through. Documents are like rivers, Mr. Eastwick. Everything upstream ends up downstream, and the interesting stuff sinks." She adjusted the satchel strap. "I'm coming on your expedition."

"You're not—"

"I'm coming on your expedition," she repeated, with the patience of a person explaining gravity, "because if that ledger is the copy I think it is, then its disappearance is a matter of record I have spent eleven years trying to close, and I will not let some storm-chaser with a debt problem lose it in a crevasse. Also, you'll need someone who can read the chancery hand when you find the vault records, because you can't, and Reyes can't, and your cook certainly can't."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I report the existence of an unregistered Kessavian inheritance claim to the Ministry of Records, which triggers a seizure order, which means you climb the escarpment behind a government team instead of ahead of your own." She smiled then—a small, precise, terrible smile. "I'd rather be useful than be obeyed. I find people tell you more when they think you're on their side."

"And are you? On my side?"

"I'm on the side of the record," Maren said. "Where that puts you depends entirely on what you do next."

* * *

They met as a crew for the first time on the ninth day, in the back room of the Anchorline, because Elodie figured if the meeting went badly, at least everyone would be drunk enough to survive it.

He spread a chart of the Reach on a table scarred by decades of bottle rings. Tomas stood with his arms crossed, radiating the specific resentment of a professional forced to share a boat with amateurs. Petra ate sunflower seeds and cracked the shells with her molars like small executions. Maren sat straight-backed, notebook open, writing down everything, including, Elodie suspected, the things people didn't say.

"Four days upriver to the last navigable water," Elodie began. "Then nine days on foot to the escarpment headwall. We move light, we move fast, and we move quiet. Nobody's name goes on any manifest. As far as the world knows, we're a survey crew checking flood damage."

"Flood damage," Tomas said flatly. "In October."

"Government surveys happen in October all the time."

"Government surveys don't hire cooks who used to blow up quarry faces." Petra shrugged at his look. "I ask around too, storm man."

"The point," Elodie said, "is that we're invisible until we're not. Questions?"

"Yeah." Petra spat a shell into her palm. "What's actually in the vault? Because I've been telling myself gold, but gold doesn't need a genealogist."

"It needs someone who can read what's written inside," Maren said smoothly.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer that keeps us all alive," Maren said. "Documents attract attention. Attention attracts men with boats. The less each of us knows, the less we can sell."

The silence that followed had edges. Petra looked at Elodie. Tomas looked at the chart. And Elodie understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that he had assembled a crew bound together by exactly one thing: each of them was lying to the others, and each of them suspected it.

It should have frightened him more than it did. Instead he felt the old pull, the storm-chaser's pull—the sense of standing in air that hadn't decided yet whether to be calm or catastrophic.

"Tomas leads on the mountain," he said. "Petra handles anything that needs opening. Maren reads anything that needs reading. I handle money, supplies, and the route. Anybody has a problem with anybody, you bring it to me, not to each other. We leave tomorrow at first light, high water or not."

"High water's the point," Tomas muttered. "Low water shows the rocks."

"One more thing." Maren closed her notebook. "Before we commit—I want to hear him say it." She looked at Elodie with those archival eyes that filed everything and forgave little. "Say why you're going. Not the debt. The real reason."

Elodie looked around the room: the disgraced guide who'd stopped trusting his own judgment, the cook who flinched at the word *contract*, the archivist who'd invited herself aboard a lie. His crew. His desperate, cracked-mirror crew.

"Because somebody sealed a door a hundred years ago," he said, "and paid a fortune to keep it shut, and I want to know what costs that much to bury."

It wasn't the whole truth. It was close enough to hold the room.

Petra cracked another seed. Tomas uncrossed his arms. Maren wrote something down—three words, maybe four—and Elodie would spend the whole river journey wondering what they were.

Outside, the first wind off the northern hills rattled the pier pilings, carrying the smell of snow from a sky still stubbornly blue.

Winter was early this year. The birds had known since August.
