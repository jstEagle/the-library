## 6. Departure at High Water

The river had opinions about our departure, and none of them were welcoming.

Maren Kolb stood on the dock at Graywater Landing with her collar up and her notebook zipped inside her coat, watching the water run brown and fast with everything the autumn storms had shaken loose upstream. The *Perdika*—Elodie's boat, bought with borrowed money and named, she suspected, for a fish that gets eaten—sat low in the current, riding heavy with fuel drums, rope, canned food, and Petra Ilves's alarming collection of cookware that doubled as demolition equipment.

Three weeks since the archive. Three weeks since Elodie Eastwick had walked into her reading room with a forged university affiliation and a question about a country that no longer existed, and she had let him walk back out believing she hadn't noticed.

She had noticed everything.

"You're staring at the water like it owes you an apology," Petra said, hauling a crate of blasting caps past her on a hand truck. The Estonian moved like someone who had learned early that hesitation was a luxury. "It doesn't. It doesn't owe anybody anything. That's the whole problem with water."

"I'm estimating flood stage," Maren said.

"You're deciding whether to get on the boat."

Maren didn't answer, which was its own answer, and Petra grinned and rolled the crate up the ramp.

The truth was that Maren had been deciding things for three weeks, and every decision had come back the same. Her grandfather's name sat in the dissolution records like a stone at the bottom of a well: *Aldous Kolb, Undersecretary for Territorial Settlement*, one of eleven signatures that had erased Kessavia from the map. She had grown up with that name on the spine of the family Bible and never once asked what it had signed. Now she was going up a river in storm season to find out, and the only person who could take her there was a debt-ridden storm chaser with a ledger he thought she hadn't seen.

Elodie appeared at the top of the ramp with a manifest under his arm and the particular cheer of a man performing competence. "Ms. Kolb. You're early. I like early."

"You like early because early people are easier to manage."

"I like early because late people make me nervous." He handed her the manifest. "Crew, supplies, route. Full disclosure, as discussed."

She read it standing in the rain, which he probably expected her to refuse to do, and she read all of it, which he probably didn't expect. Fuel: adequate. Food: adequate. Route: the Serein to the second confluence, then the Kess channel, then the escarpment approaches. It was a good route. It was also, she was nearly certain, a slightly wrong route.

"Where did you get the coordinates for the channel turn?" she asked.

"Off the ledger."

"Mm." She handed the manifest back. "The ledger you found in a riverbank."

"The same."

"And you didn't think a document that's been sitting in the mud for a hundred years might have, say, shifted its opinions?"

Elodie smiled at her. It was a good smile—warm, self-deprecating, calibrated to make a person feel clever for distrusting him. Maren had spent eleven years in archives learning to read documents, and documents were just people who couldn't lie as well.

"I had it verified," he said.

"By whom?"

"A professional."

"A professional forger, you mean."

The smile stayed exactly where it was, but something behind it recalculated. She watched it happen—the fractional pause, the weight shifting off his heels. He was good. He was genuinely good. But he had learned his craft on men like the Verrin brothers, who wanted to be deceived, and she had learned hers on dead men who had no choice in the matter.

"I don't know what you're implying," he said.

"Neither do I, yet. That's why I'm coming." She stepped past him onto the ramp. "I'll take the bow watch. I get seasick in the cabin."

* * *

They cast off at noon into weather that couldn't decide what it wanted to be. The sky to the north was the color of wet slate, and the wind came off the escarpment in long cold pushes that flattened the water and made the pines along the banks hiss like something boiling.

Maren took the bow as promised, wedged behind the rail with her notebook, and watched Elodie run the river. He was better at it than she'd expected. Storm chasers, she had decided in the archive, were gamblers with a scientific vocabulary, but Elodie read water the way she read marginalia—with attention, with respect, with the constant assumption that it was trying to tell him something. He took the Serein's braided channels without hesitation, choosing currents by some arithmetic of surface texture she couldn't follow.

Petra cooked in the galley and said things through the hatch that were either jokes or threats, depending on the listener. Tomas Reyes sat at the stern with his charts and said almost nothing at all, which Maren had learned was his way of saying plenty. The disgraced guide had taken one look at her on the dock and given her a nod of recognition—not of her, specifically, but of what she was. Another person with reasons. The boat was full of them.

By the third day they had left the last of the farm country behind and entered the gray corridor of the Kess channel, where the banks rose into clay cliffs and the river ran deep and cold and quiet in a way that felt less like peace than like holding its breath.

That was the night Maren decided to stop pretending.

She found Elodie at the stern, past midnight, smoking a cigarette he'd been rationing since Graywater. The rain had stopped and the clouds had torn open just enough to show a few stars, and the escarpment stood somewhere ahead of them in the dark, unseen but present, the way a creditor is present in a debtor's thoughts.

"You sleep at all?" he asked, without turning.

"I sleep fine. I sleep in archives." She leaned on the rail beside him. "I want to show you something."

He watched her take out the notebook, wary but curious, and she opened it to a page she'd been carrying for three weeks like a loaded weapon.

"Three copies," she said. "The ledger. When the dissolution commission finalized the settlement in 1919, the inheritance provision was executed in triplicate. Standard practice for a trust of that size. One copy to the claimant's line, one to the territorial archive at Vess, one retained by the commission itself." She turned the notebook toward him. In the lamplight from the cabin, her pencil sketch showed a fragment of a commission docket she had photographed eleven years ago and never understood until a stranger started asking about Kessavia. "*Provision executed in three parts. Distribution per annex.* Three parts, Eastwick. So the question I've been wanting to ask you since the dock is: which part do you have?"

Elodie didn't answer right away. He looked at the sketch for a long time, and then he looked at the river, and then he looked at her, and Maren watched him make the same calculation she had made in the archive: *what does this person know, what will they do with it, and can I survive them knowing it.*

"The chest was sealed," he said finally. "Official seal. Commission stamp. The coordinates are—"

"The coordinates are what they are. I'm not saying your copy is worthless. I'm saying it's one of three, and the other two didn't wash out of a riverbank, which means someone had them, which means someone *has* them, which means somewhere on this river or on that mountain there are people who know exactly what your piece of paper says." She closed the notebook. "And here's the part that should keep you up at night, since you don't seem to sleep anyway: the commission's copy was never accounted for. When the archives burned in '21, the fire report lists what was destroyed. Your ledger isn't on it. Neither is the commission's copy. Only the Vess copy. So two copies survived that fire, officially, and one of them is in your pocket, and you found it in the mud."

The cigarette burned down between his fingers. Somewhere ahead, thunder muttered along the escarpment like a clearing throat.

"You've known since the archive," he said. "Since the first day. You let me walk out of there."

"I let you walk out of there because you were going to walk out of there, and I wanted to know where you'd walk."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have." She turned to face him fully. "So here's mine, since we're trading: my grandfather signed the dissolution. Aldous Kolb. His name is on the same commission that issued your ledger, and my family has spent a hundred years not talking about it, and I have spent eleven years in Kessavian records trying to figure out what he actually put his name to. That's why I invited myself onto your boat. Not for the inheritance. For the *record*. Whatever your vault holds, it holds the truth about what my grandfather did, and I will trade you everything I know about the commission to get at it."

The wind came down the channel and pushed the boat against its anchor line, and the hawser creaked, and neither of them moved.

Elodie stubbed out the cigarette and was quiet for a while. When he spoke, his voice had lost the performance entirely, and what was underneath was tired and sharp and younger than his face.

"The ledger names an heir," he said. "Direct line. It gives coordinates and a legal window—first snow, like you said. And it's the only thing standing between me and two men who smile too much and collect in blood. So I need it to be real, Kolb. I need it so badly that I can't afford to wonder if it is. And you just made me wonder."

"Good."

"*Good?*"

"Doubt is the only verification tool that doesn't lie to you." She zipped the notebook away. "I don't trust your copy, Eastwick. I don't trust your route, which follows it. And I don't trust you, because you lied to me in my reading room with a forged affiliation and a rehearsed biography, and it was *good*—the biography had a dead mother and a fishing accident and everything. But I watched you read the flood surveys at the landing, and you read them like a man who wants the truth even when it costs him. So here's my offer: we're both liars, we're both going up the same river, and neither of us can do this without the other. Wary alliance. Full disclosure going forward. You check my grandfather's signature, I check your coordinates, and when we find out which of us has been played, we find out together."

Elodie looked at her for a long moment, and then he laughed—short, unwilling, real.

"You know what the worst part is," he said. "I believe you. And I've been believing people professionally for two years, and it's the most expensive habit I have."

"Then break the habit selectively. Believe me about the three copies. Verify everything else."

"Same terms."

"Same terms." She put out her hand, and he shook it, and his grip was cold from the rail and honest in a way his words hadn't been yet.

* * *

The storm arrived two days later, and it arrived like a debt.

Maren felt it before she saw it—a pressure in her ears, a flatness to the light, the birds going silent along the banks all at once, as if the whole valley had been told to be quiet. Elodie felt it too. She watched him come out of the cabin, look north, and start giving orders in a voice stripped of all its charm.

"Tomas, take us into the leeward cut. Petra, everything loose gets lashed twice. Kolb—" he paused, and something passed across his face that might have been the beginning of trust or the end of it, "—you're on the charts. You have the best memory for the channel of any of us, and we're going to need it, because we're not stopping."

The storm hit them an hour later in the narrows above the second confluence, rain coming sideways, the river rising around them with an appetite that made the water level climb the banks in visible increments. Maren called the channel from memory—she had spent three nights with the survey maps until she could see them with her eyes closed—while Elodie ran the boat and Tomas watched the water ahead and Petra lashed and cursed and cooked, because Petra cooked through everything, as if feeding people were her form of prayer.

They came through the worst of it at dusk, hull scraped, fuel drum dented, everyone soaked and silent with the particular exhaustion of having been, briefly, completely in the river's power.

And that was when Tomas, at the stern, said quietly: "We have company."

Maren turned. Far back down the channel they had just survived, running the same water they had just run, was a boat. Gray hull, no lights, no markings, holding a course through the narrows that no stranger could have held on the first try.

They knew the channel. They knew it the way Tomas knew it, the way she had taught herself to know it—from the surveys.

From *their* surveys.

Maren looked at Elodie, and Elodie looked at the gray boat, and she saw him arrive at the same arithmetic she had: three copies, two unaccounted for, and a route that only five people in the world were supposed to know.

"Petra," Elodie said, very quietly, "how much fuel do we have?"

"Enough," Petra said, "if we don't sleep."

The gray boat ran on behind them, patient as interest accruing, and the escarpment rose ahead out of the last light—black, enormous, and waiting.
