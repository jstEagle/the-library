## 7. The Salvage Crew

Tomas Reyes had guided eleven expeditions up the Vantre and buried two clients, and he could tell you the exact moment a trip went from trouble to catastrophe. It was never the rapids. It was never the weather. It was the moment somebody on the boat stopped looking forward.

He noticed it on the third morning, poling the shallows above Corben's Bend while Elodie slept off his watch in the bow and Petra argued with the outboard's carburetor like it owed her money. Maren Kolb stood at the stern with her binoculars raised—not scanning the banks for landmarks, not checking the water. She was looking back downriver.

"You'll wear a hole in the air doing that," Tomas said.

She lowered the glasses without hurry. "Thought I saw smoke."

"Campfires. Fishermen. This stretch has a dozen homesteads."

"Mm," she said, in the tone of a woman filing something away rather than accepting it.

Tomas went back to his pole and told himself the cold in his stomach was just the river spray. He'd spent nine years reading water for a living and three years reading people after the license scandal took that living away, and both skills agreed on one thing: when a client looks over her shoulder twice before noon on day three, you are being followed.

* * *

He raised it with Elodie at the midday halt, quietly, while Petra stripped and cleaned the fuel filter twenty feet downstream—out of earshot, though Tomas no longer trusted distance to mean anything.

"There's a boat behind us," he said. "Shallow draft, diesel, running dark at night. I've caught its engine twice through the fog. Once last night, once the night before."

Elodie crouched by the fire, feeding it twigs, and didn't look up. "You're sure it's not a homesteader?"

"Homesteaders don't run dark. Homesteaders don't hold position for an hour and then fall back when we stop." He kept his voice level. "Somebody's pacing us. Somebody patient."

Elodie poked the fire. Sparks climbed. "Harrow & Finch," he said finally. "Salvage outfit out of Drenmark. They work the big rivers, insurance recoveries, wreck clearance. If the Verrins talked to anyone about funding this trip—and they would have, they talk to everyone—it'd be them."

"So we're racing them."

"We're being *paced* by them. There's a difference. Racing means they want to beat us to the vault. Pacing means—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening.

"Pacing means they already know where we're going," Tomas finished.

Elodie said nothing, which was its own kind of answer. That was the thing about Eastwick that Tomas had learned in six days of working for him: he wasn't stupid, and he wasn't careless, and he wasn't even reckless in the way people said. He was a man who did arithmetic fast and hated the sums, so he did them alone and late at night and came to breakfast wearing the answer like a clean shirt.

"Who knew the route?" Tomas asked. "The actual route. The stops, the camps."

Elodie looked at him then, and Tomas watched him do the arithmetic right there, in daylight, and hate it. "I planned it on the boat. First night. Everyone was aboard."

Everyone. Four people, one river, and a route spoken aloud under the stars like a secret shared is a secret kept.

"I'm not accusing anyone," Elodie said.

"No," Tomas agreed. "You're just going to sleep with your boots on from now on."

* * *

They made the Narrows that evening—a limestone throat where the Vantre dropped twelve feet in half a mile and the walls closed to thirty yards. No room to pass another boat. No room to be passed. Tomas ran it at dusk because he wanted the light low and flat, wanted to see every rock's shadow lie to him honestly, and they came through clean, whooping, Petra slapping the gunwale like the boat was a horse that had jumped a fence.

And then, camped below the Narrows in a gravel bowl with high ground on three sides, Tomas heard the diesel again.

Not behind them anymore.

Ahead.

He lay awake in his bedroll listening to it, a low industrial mutter threading through the sound of the rapids upstream, and he understood with a sick, settling clarity what pacing meant. Harrow & Finch hadn't been following their route. They'd been following their *schedule*. Somewhere ahead, below the next set of falls where the river widened into the long lake called the Widow's Mirror, the salvage crew would be waiting at the only place a boat could put in for twenty miles.

He got up and woke Elodie without a word, just a hand on the shoulder, and pointed upstream toward the sound. Elodie listened for a long time. His face in the firelight was very still.

"They're not chasing us," Elodie whispered. "They're herding us."

"To the lake."

"To the lake." He rubbed his eyes. "Okay. Okay. We don't sleep. We run the lake tonight, in the dark, before they're set. Petra can run the engine blind if I navigate. You know the water."

"The Widow's Mirror in the dark is suicide."

"The Widow's Mirror at dawn is a trap. Pick our suicide."

That was the arithmetic again, done fast, hated, worn like a shirt. Tomas found, to his own disgust, that he nodded.

* * *

They broke camp without fire, loaded in silence, and pushed onto black water under a sky scoured of moon by cloud. Petra ran the engine at half throttle, her hands reading the vibration the way a blind woman reads a face. Elodie knelt at the bow with a shuttered lantern, opening its slit only when Tomas called a bearing, and between calls they moved by sound—the river widening around them, the walls falling away, the water going glassy and deep and cold.

For an hour it worked. For an hour Tomas let himself believe they'd slipped the leash.

Then the searchlight hit them.

It came off the northern shore, a white blade across the water, and with it the diesel roar swelled to full voice—a broad-beamed salvage tug, forty feet of riveted steel with a push-bar welded to her bow, swinging out of a cove where she'd been idling with lights doused. Waiting exactly where Tomas would have waited.

"Tomas!" Elodie's voice cracked across the dark. "Hard to starboard!"

Petra slammed the throttle home and the boat heeled, engine screaming, and the searchlight swung to track them like a hand reaching for a coin rolling off a table. A voice came over the water through a megaphone, amplified and almost friendly:

"*Vessel on the lake! Cut your engine! This is Harrow & Finch Salvage operating under a recovery writ—you are obstructing a licensed operation!*"

"He's lying," Maren shouted from the stern. "There's no writ covers this water—I've read the riparian charters—"

The tug's bow rose as she throttled up.

Tomas understood one heartbeat too early what was happening: they weren't trying to board. Steel against wood, forty tons against four, and the push-bar coming on at ramming speed with all the plausible deniability of a navigation accident. He grabbed Petra's collar and hauled her down as the bar struck.

It caught them aft of midships. The world became noise and cold. The hull opened along a seam with a sound like a giant tearing paper, and the river came in with the particular violence of water that has been waiting. Tomas hit the deck, lost his grip on Petra, saw her go over the side into the black—

—and then Maren was there, throwing a coiled line with a fisherman's accuracy that Tomas would think about later, for weeks, wondering where an archivist had learned to throw like that. Petra's hand shot out of the water and closed on it, and Maren braced against the stern post, boots skidding, and Tomas crawled and got a fistful of Petra's jacket and together they dragged her aboard, coughing, half-drowned, alive.

The tug backed off. Its searchlight held them for a long moment—cataloguing, Tomas thought, counting survivors, assessing damage—then snapped off. The diesel receded into the cove and died to a mutter. In the sudden dark, the megaphone voice came once more across the water, cheerful as a shopkeeper:

"*No hard feelings, friends! Fuel and repairs available at the south shore landing—standard rates!*"

Then nothing but the river, and the cold, and the sound of their own boat drinking itself to death.

* * *

They grounded her on the shingle before she sank, beached her stern-first in the shallows with a foot of water sloshing in the bilge. Petra sat wrapped in every dry blanket they owned, shaking, teeth chattering around curses in a language Tomas didn't know. Elodie stood waist-deep in the freezing lake holding a lantern inside the hull, looking at the tear.

"Not the prop," he said. "Not the rudder. They hit us exactly where it costs the most time to fix and the least to sink us." He climbed out, dripping. "They wanted us crippled, not dead. Dead men don't turn back; they just become a search party and questions. Crippled men quit."

"Or slow down," Tomas said. "Slow enough to be overtaken on the portages."

Elodie wrung out his sleeves. His hands were steady, which Tomas noted the way you note a dog that doesn't growl at thunder. "We've lost the hull integrity for the upper gorge. And half our diesel went down with the leak."

"How much left?"

"Enough to reach the head of navigation. Not enough for mistakes."

Maren crouched by the fire she'd finally allowed, feeding it slowly, watching the dark southern shore where the tug's riding lights now glowed like a town that had never existed. "They knew the Narrows schedule," she said. "They knew we'd camp below it. They were positioned at the Widow's Mirror hours before we launched tonight." She turned and looked at each of them in turn, firelight moving on her face. "That's not surveillance. That's a timetable. Someone gave them our route and our pace."

The silence stretched. The river filled it.

"Elodie planned the route," Tomas said, because someone had to say it and it might as well be said plainly, the way you'd point at a rock in the water. "Spoken aloud. First night. All of us present."

Petra's head came up sharply. "You're saying it was one of us."

"I'm saying a steel tug doesn't guess."

Maren's eyes stayed on Elodie. "You lied to the Verrins about what the ledger says. I've known that since the dock. Men who lie to creditors make enemies with budgets." It wasn't an accusation, quite. It was worse—an explanation offered generously, the way you'd offer a coat to a man you suspected of theft.

"And you," Elodie said, not blinking, "knew the ledger was one of three copies and mentioned it only when it suited you. Archivists don't get searchlights either, Maren. But governments do."

Tomas watched the two of them across the fire, each perfectly composed, each holding their cards close enough to warm their hands on, and felt the old familiar exhaustion of the guide whose job is to keep the rope team roped. Petra was staring into the flames, and her face—her poor, honest, exhausted face—did something complicated. Something guilty-shaped.

He filed it away. He was getting good at filing things away on this river.

"Nobody's solving this tonight," he said aloud, standing. "We've got a holed boat, half a tank, and a hundred miles of gorge. Whatever's rotten in this crew, it'll keep till morning. Cold keeps everything."

Elodie nodded slowly, and for just a second, looking at the fire, his composure slipped and Tomas saw underneath it: a man doing arithmetic he couldn't finish, arriving at a sum he didn't want, suspecting—correctly, though none of them knew it yet—that the cheapest person on this expedition had sold them all for less than the price of a life.

Across the lake, the tug's riding lights went out one by one, like eyes closing.

Sleep well, Tomas thought at the darkness. You've earned it. Somebody paid for it.

He checked the mooring line twice, then sat with his back against the holed hull and his broken license in his breast pocket and kept the first watch, listening to the river, waiting to hear which of his companions stirred in the night—and what, exactly, they'd whisper into whatever device or darkness was listening back.
