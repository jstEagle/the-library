## 8. Ledger of Small Betrayals

Petra Ilves had cooked for prison work crews, for a fishing trawler that ran eleven weeks without port, and once, catastrophically, for a wedding of four hundred people in a hall with one working stove. She had learned to read a room through its stomach. You could tell everything about people by how they ate—what they hoarded, what they left, who waited to be served and who took.

So she knew, three days before anyone said anything, that the crew had started eating differently around her.

Tomas still took seconds, but he watched her ladle them out now, his eyes doing arithmetic she didn't like the shape of. Maren ate precisely, completely, nothing wasted, nothing extra—she'd always done that—but lately she finished and then sat with her spoon across her bowl like a gavel waiting to fall. And Elodie. Elodie had stopped joking about her coffee. That was the worst sign. A man who stops complaining about your cooking has decided you might not be around long enough for it to matter.

The river carried them north through country that was getting meaner by the mile—narrower banks, grayer water, hills shouldering in close like men crowding a card table. The Harrow & Finch boat hadn't shown itself since the collision, but Petra felt it the way you feel a tooth you're going to lose: not pain exactly, just a constant awareness of something rotten waiting to happen.

She washed the pots in river water gone cold enough to ache and told herself, for the hundredth time, that she'd made the right call. That there was no version of this where she didn't make it. That Jan was down in the Verrin-leased mines with four years left on a contract he'd signed at nineteen because their mother was dying and the signing bonus was the size of a miracle, and that contracts like that didn't end early except two ways—death or buyout—and Jan wasn't the dying kind. He was the kind who kept working. Who smiled in the letters even when the letters got shorter. *Sister, don't worry. Sister, the air is fine. Sister, I bought a coat.*

Nobody bought a coat. Not down there. They said they did so you'd stop sending money.

Petra dried her hands on her apron and looked upriver at Elodie's back, hunched over the ledger he thought none of them saw him reading, and understood that she had maybe an hour before someone else forced this conversation on worse terms than the ones she could choose herself.

She'd been a cook long enough to know: you serve the bad news first, while they're still hungry enough to listen.

* * *

She caught him alone at dusk, at the woodline where the bank rose toward the first real slopes of the escarpment. He was feeding the fire the way he did everything—efficiently, absently, half his attention always on the horizon. She set two cups of coffee down between them, sat on the log opposite, and drank before he did, which was a message in itself. If it was poisoned, she was going first.

"Elodie." His name came out rough. She cleared her throat. "I sold our route."

He didn't move. That was the thing about Elodie Eastwick that had fooled everyone including, apparently, himself—he looked like a man who reacted. Loose, grinning, all weather. But right now he went absolutely still, cup halfway to his mouth, and she saw the other man underneath, the one who totaled trucks on purpose and pried open dead nations' chests and lied to the Verrin brothers' faces with sweat on his palms and his voice level.

"To whom," he said. Not a question. He already knew.

"Harrow & Finch. Before we launched. Through a man at the fuel dock named Ostrov." She made herself keep going, steady as a metronome, because if she stopped she'd start apologizing and apologies were just noise with wet eyes. "Coordinates of the launch, the route upriver, the resupply points, the timeline. Everything but—" 

"The ledger." He set the cup down. "You never mentioned the ledger."

"No." She held his eyes. "I want you to hear me say it plainly, because I know how this sounds otherwise. I gave them the route. I did not give them the reason. As far as Harrow & Finch knows, we're a salvage outfit chasing flood-scour deposits like a dozen others this season. I told them there was nothing aboard worth more than the boat. I told them you were broke and stupid and the trip was a long shot for insurance scrap." A breath. "I may have undersold you slightly."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not a smile. The ghost of where a smile would go if this were any other night.

"How much," he said.

"Eight hundred." She didn't flinch from the number; she'd decided not to flinch from anything tonight. "Half up front, half on delivery of proof we'd gone upriver past the falls. It's already spent. Every coin of it. My brother had four years left on a Verrin mining contract, and the buyout price is twelve hundred, and I had four hundred, and now I have eight hundred more and a debt to a different kind of man." She turned her cup slowly on her knee. "Ostrov doesn't collect money, Elodie. He collects favors. I've traded one cage for a smaller cage, and I knew that when I did it, and I did it anyway, because my brother is twenty-three years old and coughing black, and I am not the person who lets that happen while she cooks stew on a river."

The fire popped. Down at the boats, Tomas's lantern swung as he checked the lines, a small yellow pendulum counting out the seconds she had left.

"You understand the problem," Elodie said quietly. "The collision. They nearly took Petra—I mean, they nearly took *you*—off the stern. They cost us a third of our fuel. If they'd boarded instead of rammed, they'd have found the ledger, because you'd have handed it to them along with the rest of the manifest."

"I never put it on the manifest."

"That's not the point and you know it." He rubbed his face with both hands, and for a moment he looked young and exhausted and exactly as broke as she'd described him to Ostrov. "The point is I can't verify anything you say anymore. You understand that too. You could be telling me the truth right now, or you could be running a second play for Ostrov, or for whoever's actually behind Harrow & Finch—and don't look surprised, I've been doing that math since the collision. Somebody hired that boat knowing our route before we ever launched. Now I know why. What I don't know is whether you were the leak or just the first one caught."

"Then maroon me." She said it flat, and meant it, mostly. "There's a trading post thirty miles back downriver. Tomas can run me there tomorrow morning and be back in two days. You lose a cook and gain a crew that sleeps soundly. Or you keep me, and every night you wonder. Those are your options. I've laid them out honest, which is more than I did for Ostrov, and more than most people in my position would do, because most people in my position would wait until you found out some other way and hope the finding-out killed somebody else."

Elodie looked at her for a long time. The firelight did things to his face that made him hard to read, and she suspected he knew it, and used it.

"Why tell me now?" he asked. "Three days after the collision. Why not at the dock? Why not never?"

Because I couldn't eat, she almost said. Because I watch people eat for a living and I couldn't stand being watched back. Because Jan sent a letter at the last port and it was four lines long and the handwriting had gotten worse.

"Because the next stretch of river is the dangerous part," she said instead, "and I refuse to be the reason somebody dies up here twice. Once was business. Twice would be character."

* * *

He didn't answer right away. He got up, walked to the water's edge, stood there with his hands in his pockets looking at the escarpment going blue and then black against the last light. Petra stayed on her log and drank her coffee and let him have the silence, because she'd learned that men like Elodie did their deciding in silence and their talking afterward, and interrupting the process only restarted it.

When he came back, he crouched by the fire instead of sitting, forearms on knees, close enough that she could see the calculation running behind his eyes like water under ice.

"Here's what I believe," he said. "I believe you told me the truth, because liars with a second play don't confess—they manage. They feed you a little truth wrapped around a bigger lie and let you feel clever for catching the small part. You just handed me the whole ugly thing with no wrapping on it. That's either honesty or the best performance I've ever seen, and I've been performed at by professionals this month." He fed a stick to the fire. "But belief isn't trust, and I'm short on both, so here's what happens now."

"Say it."

"You stay. You cook, you climb, you carry your share, and you spend your guilt productively. Because guilt is useful, Petra—it's the only fuel that burns hotter the more of it there is. When we hit the escarpment and somebody needs to go into a bad spot first, that's you. When rations get thin and somebody takes the smaller portion without being asked, that's you. When we need someone to hold a charge while the fuse runs, that is absolutely you." He glanced up. "I'm not punishing you. I'm investing. There's a difference, and the difference is that I expect a return."

"And if I'd rather take the trading post?"

"Then Tomas rows you down tomorrow and I'll write you a reference that says you're the finest camp cook on the river and no trouble at all, and we'll both know I'm lying, and it'll cost me nothing." He shrugged. "But you won't. You'll stay. Because eight hundred coins bought Jan four fewer years in that mine, and the only way that trade pays off is if whatever's at the top of this mountain is worth enough to keep you clear of Verrin country for good. You didn't sell us out for money, Petra. You sold us out for a door out. You're going to want to see what's behind this one."

She hated him a little, right then. Hated him for being right, and for making mercy sound like arithmetic, and for understanding her better than she'd authorized. It was the same warm menace she'd heard in the Verrin brothers' voices at the fuel dock, cheerful men quoting terms, except Elodie's terms were ones she could survive.

"One condition," she said.

"Of course there is."

"When this is over—if there's anything worth taking—you help me get Jan out. Not money. Out. You know people who break contracts. I've seen the way the Verrins talk to you; you know how those machines work. Whatever the inheritance turns out to be, a piece of it buys my brother's freedom, or I start wondering out loud, very loudly, about what else I might have mentioned to Ostrov."

Elodie laughed—a short, startled bark, genuine as a snapped bone. "Blackmail. From my own cook."

"Former informant," she corrected. "Get the titles right."

"Done." He put out his hand, and she shook it, and his grip was dry and strong and entirely without warmth, which she respected. Deals sealed in warmth dissolved in cold. This one would hold.

* * *

She was scrubbing the pot again—there was always another pot—when Maren materialized at the edge of the firelight with her arms crossed and her spoon-gavel expression firmly in place. Of course she'd heard. The woman heard everything; she probably heard the river thinking.

"He's keeping you," Maren said. Not asking.

"He's investing," Petra said. "His word."

"Mm." Maren studied her the way she studied documents, looking for the forgery. "For what it's worth, I think you told him the truth. And for what it's also worth, I think you're wrong about one thing. You said you never mentioned the ledger." Her voice dropped, quiet and terrible. "But Harrow & Finch didn't ram us to stop a salvage run, Petra. Salvage rivals race each other. They don't try to drown each other's crews at night. Someone told them there was something aboard worth killing for. So either Ostrov sold your information twice—to us and to someone who knows what we're really carrying—or the leak isn't finished leaking."

Petra's hands went still in the cold water.

Downriver, far behind them in the dark, she imagined she could hear an engine, patient as a collector.

"We launch at first light," Maren said, and walked away, leaving Petra alone with the fire, the pot, and the sudden, sickening understanding that her confession had solved nothing at all—that somewhere ahead on the escarpment, the second betrayal was already packed and waiting, and nobody knew whose it was.
