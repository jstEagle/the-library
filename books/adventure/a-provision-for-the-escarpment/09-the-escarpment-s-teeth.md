## 9. The Escarpment's Teeth

The river ended the way most good things end: without ceremony.

One morning it was carrying us, and by noon it was a brown ribbon threading away below us through a gorge we'd never see again, and the boat—Petra's ugly, beloved flat-bottomed thing that had survived a collision, a leak, and Petra's cooking—was beached on a gravel shelf with its nose in the willows like something that had died mid-drink.

Tomas stood at the base of the escarpment and did not say anything for a long time. That was how I knew it was bad. Tomas Reyes talked the way other men breathed; silence from him was a weather event.

"Two days to the treeline," he said finally. "Three if the switchbacks are washed out. Then the scree field, then the shoulder, then—" he pointed at a gray notch high above, so far up it seemed more idea than place, "—that."

"That," I said, "is where the ledger says to go."

"The ledger," Maren said behind me, "that one of you is lying about." She said it lightly, the way she said everything, like a woman commenting on the price of eggs. Nobody rose to it. We'd been three weeks on the water together and we'd learned the trick of letting her barbs pass through us like cold current.

The escarpment didn't look like rock from below. It looked like a wall built by something with more patience than mercy—a thousand feet of layered stone running north and south as far as the eye could follow, streaked black with old water, patched white with the first snow of the season clinging to ledges no goat would trust. The Kessavians had cut their road into it a century ago, and the mountain had spent that century unpicking their work stitch by stitch.

"Weather window?" I asked Tomas.

He squinted at the sky with the professional suspicion he reserved for everything except women and card games, which he trusted completely and wrongly. "Four days. Maybe five. After that—" He shrugged. "You've seen what autumn does down here. Up there it does it faster, and sideways."

Five days. The legal window closed at first snow—not the dusting on the heights, but the real snow, the snow that closed the passes and turned the escarpment into a place where dead men waited politely until spring. If we weren't at the vault and back down before it, everything—the river, the collision, Petra's betrayal, my lies, all of it—would have been spent on nothing.

"Then we climb today," I said.

* * *

The old Kessavian road was a marvel, or had been. It switched back and forth across the face of the escarpment in long patient diagonals, cut into the living rock, walled on the outside edge with fitted stones taller than a man. A hundred years ago you could have driven a wagon up it. Now the walls were tumbled, the surface was a riverbed of loose shale, and every few hundred meters a section of road simply wasn't anymore—slid off into the void, leaving gaps we had to traverse on hands and knees along ledges the width of a coffin.

The mountain collected its toll early and without negotiation.

The first hour took Petra's spare cookpot, which bounced twice and vanished into the gorge with a sound like a struck bell, diminishing. The second hour took my dignity, when the ledge under me crumbled and I slid ten feet on my back before Tomas caught my pack strap and held me over the drop while I found purchase with my boots. He didn't even strain. He just stood there, planted like fence posts, saying, "Slowly, jefe. Slowly. The mountain has all day and neither do we."

By the third hour we'd stopped talking entirely. Conversation costs breath, and breath was currency we were hoarding. The world narrowed to boots, stone, the next handhold. Behind me I could hear the others breathing—Petra's steady diesel-engine rhythm, Maren's lighter and quicker, Tomas's deliberate as a metronome—and I found I could tell who was struggling by sound alone, the way you learn a boat's creaks.

Maren was struggling. She hid it well, the way she hid everything, but her breath came shorter each hour and she'd stopped making observations about geology, which for Maren was the equivalent of screaming.

We made the treeline by late afternoon, a ragged fringe of wind-bent pines growing out of the rock like hair on a knuckle. Beyond them the slope opened into the scree field, a mile of gray rubble rising toward the shoulder, and above that the notch, and above that clouds moving with intent.

"Camp here," Tomas said. "Scree in the dark is how people die stupid."

Nobody argued. We pitched the tents in the shelter of the last trees, and Petra produced a stove and a pot of the barley-and-fat gruel that had become our entire existence, and we ate watching the light die on the wall of rock above us. Somewhere up there, half-buried, was a door. Somewhere beyond it was whatever the Verrins really wanted, whatever Finch feared, whatever Maren's grandfather had signed his name to. It felt absurd that it should be so close and so far at once—that a nation's whole buried truth should sit behind a few thousand feet of broken stone.

"Snow tonight," Maren said, nodding at the heights. The dusting on the ledges had thickened since noon. "Up there, anyway."

"Dusting," Petra said. "Not the snow. The window holds."

She said it like a prayer. We all let her.

* * *

The second day was the scree field, and the scree field was where the mountain started collecting in bone.

It looked walkable from below. That was the trap of it—gray rubble sloping at a reasonable angle, a highway of loose stone leading straight toward the shoulder. But scree doesn't hold. Every step sank six inches and slid two, and the slope was studded with boulders the size of houses perched on foundations of gravel, waiting, patient as creditors, for some vibration to give them permission.

We crossed it roped together, twenty feet apart, moving in the slow diagonal that Tomas called reading the slope and I called walking on eggshells the size of a county. By midday we were on the upper third, the river invisible now, the world reduced to gray stone and white sky, when the mountain cleared its throat.

It was small, as avalanches go. A boulder the size of a stove, undermined by meltwater somewhere above us, let go with a crack like a rifle shot. Tomas shouted the word every climber knows in every language—"Rock!"—and we pressed ourselves against the slope, heads down, while it passed us thirty feet out, gathering two smaller companions, bouncing in great silent arcs that would have been almost beautiful if beauty hadn't been beside the point.

It was the second rock, the one none of us heard coming, that changed the arithmetic.

I saw it only in peripheral vision: a chunk of shale the size of a bread loaf, tumbling out of the sun, tracing a line that ended precisely where Maren Kolb stood with her head down against the slope, unaware, her hood up, her world shrunk to her own boots.

There was no time to shout. There was barely time to understand. And then Tomas moved—not away from the rock, which was the sensible direction, but across it, two long strides on unstable ground, and he put his body between Maren and the falling stone and got his forearm up.

The sound was wet and wrong. The kind of sound you feel in your teeth.

Tomas went down onto one knee, and Maren, knocked sprawling by the impact of him, scrambled back and stared at his arm hanging at an angle forearms are not designed for, and for a moment nobody said anything at all. The mountain, having taken its due, went quiet again, indifferent as a tax office.

"Wrist," Tomas said through his teeth. His face had gone the color of the scree. "Both bones. I heard them." He tried to laugh and it came out as a grunt. "Good news, jefe. It's not my rope hand."

"It's your climbing hand," Petra said, already beside him, already cutting away his sleeve with the same knife she'd once held to a man's throat over cards. The arm was swelling as we watched, a smooth terrible bloom rising under the skin. She splinted it with a tent stake and strips of shirt, quick and competent, and Tomas made sounds through his clenched jaw that he clearly hoped we'd interpret as commentary rather than pain.

"Why," Maren said. She was still on the ground. Her voice had lost all its archness; it was bare, unarmored, almost young. "Why did you—you didn't have to—"

"You were going to get hit," Tomas said simply, as if that settled it, as if the mathematics of it were obvious. Maybe to him they were. "Also, you owe me now. Big. I collect after the summit." The grin cost him visibly.

We camped early, in the lee of a boulder field, because Tomas couldn't grip and the roped work above the shoulder would need everyone whole. He sat propped against stone with his splinted arm cradled like something rescued from a fire, and refused the first dose of the pain tablets, and then accepted the second, and by dusk was telling a long rambling story about a mule named Bishop that may not have been true and certainly wasn't relevant, and we let him talk because talking was how he held the fear down.

I walked out onto the scree alone while the others slept in shifts.

The sky had gone hard and clear, the way it does before it means business, and the stars were brutal with altitude. Above us the shoulder caught the moonlight, and above the shoulder the notch was visible now as a genuine gap in the ridge, black against blacker sky. One day's climbing. Maybe less, if the mountain allowed it, and the mountain had been sending invoices all week.

Behind me, gravel shifted. Maren came and stood beside me, arms crossed against the cold, and for a while neither of us spoke. Two liars, sharing a view.

"He saved my life," she said finally. "And I still don't know if any of you are telling me the truth about why we're here."

"Same," I said.

That earned a short laugh, surprised out of her. "Elodie Eastwick. Do you know what I think? I think you're not a liar at all. I think you're worse. I think you're a man who tells himself the truth so rarely that when he does, he can't recognize it." She looked up at the notch. "My grandfather signed an order that erased a country. Everyone in my family has spent a hundred years pretending it was paperwork. I need to know what the paperwork was covering. That's my whole story. You can believe it or not."

"I believe it," I said. And found, to my surprise, that I meant it—which frightened me more than the rockfall had.

Below us, faint in the dark, I thought I heard something that might have been an engine, far downriver, or might have been wind working the gorge. I decided it was wind. I was getting good at deciding things were wind.

* * *

The third morning there was snow on our tents. Not the dusting on the heights—an inch of real snow, wet and heavy, the kind that falls when the season stops asking permission. It lay on the scree like a shroud laid out ready, and it turned the footing from bad to treacherous, every stone slicked and every hollow hidden.

Tomas stood looking at it, flexing his splinted hand inside its wrapping, testing what remained of his grip. His face was gray with more than pain.

"One day to the notch," he said. "If we push. If the weather holds another day, maybe two." He turned and looked at each of us in turn, and what I saw in his eyes was not fear of the mountain. It was the look of a guide doing arithmetic he didn't like. "But somebody carries my pack, and somebody ties my knots, and if it starts snowing hard up there—" he nodded at the clouds stacking themselves against the ridge like furniture being moved into place, "—we don't come down this year. Understand? The window isn't closing. For some of us, it may already be shut."

Petra spat and started repacking. Maren pulled her hood up. And I looked at the notch, and thought about doors, and debts, and the specific weight of a wrist bone breaking in the service of a lie, and I said:

"Then we push."

Above us, the first flakes of the next storm were already sifting down through the still air, fine as ash, falling without hurry, as though the mountain knew exactly how much time it had left to collect.
