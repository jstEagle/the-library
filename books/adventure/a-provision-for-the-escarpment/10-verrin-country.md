## 10. Verrin Country

Maren Kolb had spent eleven years in archives, and she had learned to read a room the way she read a marginalia: not for what it said, but for what it had been made to say. The camp on the gravel bar said *we are four tired people who trust each other*. The truth was written in the smaller script—Petra checking the fuel drums twice before sleeping, Tomas cradling his splinted wrist like something borrowed, Elodie smiling at jokes he wasn't listening to.

And now there was a fifth element in the composition. A boat.

She saw it first as a smudge of woodsmoke downstream, wrong in every particular. Their own fires were small and buried; this one stood up straight and confident, a column of gray against the rain-hung sky. Then the sound reached them across the water—the lazy putter of an engine being run at half its capacity, the mechanical equivalent of a man whistling on your porch.

Tomas came up the bank first, wrist tucked against his chest, and said nothing, which from Tomas was a shout.

"Company," Petra said. She had a knife out. Maren noted that Petra always had a knife out these days, since the night on the river, since her confession, since Elodie had looked at her for a long ten seconds and then simply handed her back the matches. Guilt had made her quick. Or maybe quickness had been there all along, and guilt had only sharpened it into visibility.

The boat rounded the bend with the unhurried authority of something that owned the river and was merely lending it out. It was bigger than theirs, flat-bottomed, canvas rigged over the midsection, and standing in the bow with one hand on the rail was a man who managed to look both relaxed and entirely awake, the way certain animals do.

"I know him," Elodie said quietly. He'd come to stand beside Maren without her hearing him approach, which she was beginning to suspect was a habit rather than an accident. "That's Doss Verrin."

"There are three of them?"

"Two brothers and a cousin who does the walking. That's the cousin." Elodie's smile did the thing it did when he was frightened—went wider, warmer, more convincing. Maren had catalogued that expression early. It was his tell, though she doubted anyone who loved him would ever notice, because no one who loved him would be watching for it. "Doss doesn't collect. Doss delivers."

"Delivers what?"

"Whatever you least want."

* * *

Doss Verrin stepped onto the gravel bar like a man arriving at a family picnic. He was perhaps forty, round-faced, with the kind of pleasant, unlined features that make cruelty invisible until it's already finished with you. He wore a good coat that had been rained on and didn't care. Two crewmen stayed with the boat; he came alone, hands visible, palms slightly out, a walking demonstration of harmlessness.

"Miss Kolb," he said, before anyone introduced her. "The archivist. We keep files too, you know. Ours are just better funded." He beamed at her, then at the others, taking inventory the way a man counts spoons. "Reyes. Ilves. And Eastwick, looking thinner than his last statement of account. Good. Worry burns calories."

"What do you want, Doss?" Elodie asked.

"Want? I've brought *supplies*. Look at this." One of the crewmen handed ashore two crates and a wrapped bundle. Doss set them down with the tenderness of a man laying flowers on a grave. "Rice, salt pork, lamp oil, dry socks. Cold up top already—we've had reports. And a medical kit, because Cousin Aldous heard about the wrist and insisted. He's sentimental that way. We all are. Family business."

Tomas stared at the medical kit the way starving men stare through restaurant windows.

"You're supplying us," Petra said flatly.

"We're *investing* in you. There's a difference, and the difference is interest." Doss crouched by the crates, arranging them, giving everyone time to think about interest. "Now. Some housekeeping matters, and then I'll let you eat."

He produced an envelope. Not paper—an oilskin wallet, the kind surveyors use, built to survive weather and neglect. He held it out to Elodie with both hands, almost ceremonially.

"Message from my brothers. They ask me to convey three items, and they ask me to convey them accurately, because accuracy is a form of respect."

Elodie took it. Maren watched him work the wax seal—she caught herself noting the seal, filing it, everything filed—and unfold the single sheet inside. She watched his face while he read, which meant she missed nothing: the small contraction around the eyes at the second line, the stillness at the fourth.

"Read it aloud," Doss suggested pleasantly. "Or don't. My brothers said either way. They said, and I quote, 'He'll understand that we know, and knowing is the message.'"

Elodie read it silently. Then, because he was Elodie, and because Maren was learning that his recklessness had a grammar all its own—he read it aloud.

"*Item one. We know the ledger you carry is not the only ledger.*"

The gravel bar went very quiet. The river kept talking behind them, indifferent.

"*Item two. A second copy exists, damaged but legible, and its coordinates differ from yours. We mention this not to alarm you but to save you embarrassment at altitude.*"

Maren felt the ground tilt under her, the same tilt she'd felt in the archive when the dissolution order had first resolved itself under her magnifier. So it was true. Her grandfather's signature at the bottom of a nation's death certificate, and somewhere above them, proof of what the death had been for. She had told them—partially—in the whiteout that hadn't come yet, no—she corrected herself; she had told them on the river, partially, that she suspected copies. But *differing coordinates*. Someone had altered one of them. Possibly the one in Elodie's pocket. Possibly the one leading all of them up a mountain in the teeth of winter.

"*Item three,*" Elodie continued, and here his voice did something she'd never heard it do. It went careful. "*The discount for honesty expires at first snow. After that, standard terms resume. You know what standard terms are, Elodie. You've been paying them your whole adult life.*"

He folded the paper along its original creases. His hands were steady. Maren admired that and distrusted it in equal measure.

"Standard terms," Doss repeated warmly, as if resuming a conversation from a week ago instead of delivering a sentence that had just redrawn the map of everyone's future. "You see, my brothers have a philosophy. Most lenders bet on repayment. Very limited imagination. My brothers bet on *outcomes*. They don't much care whether you pay in money, Elodie. Money is just one currency among several, and frankly not their favorite. Money is boring. Money can be printed, stolen, devalued. What can't be devalued is leverage."

"Say what you mean," Petra said.

"I thought I was." Doss spread his hands. "Very well, plainly, since Miss Ilves prefers it. My brothers funded your expedition. My brothers also funded Harrow & Finch. Both boats, both crews, both maps. They are not choosing between you. They are *watching* both of you, and whichever one comes down off that mountain holding something valuable, well—" he smiled with genuine pleasure, "—that's the one they own."

* * *

The silence afterward had texture. Maren had felt silences like it only twice before: once in the archive basement when the lights failed, once at her father's funeral when the relatives stopped pretending.

It was Tomas who broke it, which surprised her. "You gave Harrow & Finch our route," he said to Doss. "That night collision. They knew where we'd anchor. Petra didn't sell that."

"No," Doss agreed cheerfully. "Petra sold them the route *before* we sold it to them. Redundancy, Mr. Reyes. Any competent operation builds in redundancy." He turned to Petra with an apologetic little bow. "No offense intended. Your brother's contract has been adjusted, by the way. Reduced term. Consider it a bonus for services rendered, or an investment in your continued cooperation—I find those are usually the same thing."

Petra's knife hand opened and closed. Elodie moved—not toward Doss, but fractionally toward Petra, a half-step, the geometry of a man putting himself in the line of something. Maren filed that too.

"And if we refuse the supplies?" Elodie asked.

"Then you climb hungry, and hungry climbers make poor decisions, and poor decisions void warranties." Doss shrugged, a whole philosophy contained in the motion. "Take the supplies. Refuse the supplies. My brothers' position is identical either way: they hold paper on every person on this gravel bar except—" he paused, savoring it, turning to Maren with bright curiosity, "—well. Except Miss Kolb. Who owes nobody anything. Which raises the interesting question of why she's here."

"My reasons are mine."

"Of course. Of course." He said it the way people say *of course* when they intend to find out anyway. "Though I will note, professionally, that a woman who walks away from a university post, a pension, and a named fellowship to chase a dead country up a frozen river is either a romantic or a debtor. And romantics are rare."

"The second copy," Elodie said. "How do they know it exists?"

Doss's smile widened by a degree. It was, Maren realized, the real answer to the question, delivered in the negative space: *because someone told them*. Because the second copy had been found—or placed—or reported—by someone whose reports reached the Verrins before they reached the crew. Because somewhere upstream or downstream or inside the expedition itself, information flowed in directions none of them had authorized.

"First snow," Doss said instead, checking a pocket watch he didn't need—the sky was doing the telling. "That's the deadline, friends. Honesty discount. Full disclosure of whatever you find up there, shared amicably, terms renegotiated in good faith. After that—" he clicked the watch shut, "—standard terms. And standard terms, Elodie, you know better than anyone. Standard terms are how a man ends up owing his hands."

He walked back to the boat. At the rail he paused, turned, and offered one last gift with the air of a man dropping coins in a beggar's cup:

"The chest. When you're done with it. My brothers want the chest. Whatever's inside, whatever you decide about ledgers and inheritances and dead nations—the chest comes home to us. Consider it collateral. Consider it sentiment. Consider it whatever helps you sleep." He tapped the rail twice. "Safe climbing."

The engine started. The boat slid back into the rain-gray river and diminished, unhurried, confident, owned.

* * *

They didn't speak for a long time after. Petra distributed the rice and salt pork because food is the only argument that settles anything, and they ate sitting apart on the gravel like strangers at a station, watching the water.

It was Elodie who finally talked, and he talked to the river, not to any of them.

"I did the arithmetic wrong," he said. "From the very beginning. I thought this was a debt story. Find the inheritance, pay the debt, walk free. Clean transaction." He laughed, short and hollow. "But they never wanted money. Thirty days—that was never a deadline for repayment. It was a leash length. Every extension, every cheerful loan, every 'no rush, Elodie, family business'—they weren't lending me capital. They were buying *proximity*. Getting closer. Making sure that whatever I found, they'd be standing near it when I found it."

"They bet on both boats," Maren said.

"They bet on every boat." He turned the oilskin wallet over in his hands. "They probably bet on the archivist too, if they could figure out how. They don't care who finds the vault. They care that *someone* finds it, and that whoever it is owes them. The inheritance isn't the prize. The inheritance is the *hook*. Whatever's up there—gold, papers, ghosts—they want a debtor holding it. A man who owns something valuable and owes everything else. That's not a creditor. That's a farmer."

Nobody answered. Upstream, the escarpment had come out from under its cloud collar for the first time in two days, and even from here, even at this distance, Maren could see snow on the high switchbacks. White as chalk. White as the messages in the waystation walls they hadn't reached yet.

"First snow," Tomas said quietly, flexing his splinted hand. "Up there, that's not a deadline. It's a door closing."

"Then we climb faster," Petra said.

Elodie stood, brushed gravel from his knees, and looked at the mountain the way Maren had seen him look at a storm cell on the horizon—with that terrible, luminous appetite that had totaled one truck already and might yet total them all.

"One thing Doss got wrong," he said. "He said everyone on this bar owes the Verrins except Maren." He glanced at her, and for once the smile was gone, and what was underneath it was younger and more afraid. "Everyone on this bar owes *somebody*. That's why we're all here. The question isn't who's bought. The question is—who's reporting."

He picked up the medical kit, tossed it gently to Tomas, and shouldered his pack.

"Sleep fast," he said. "We move at first light."

Above the treeline, the snow was already falling.
