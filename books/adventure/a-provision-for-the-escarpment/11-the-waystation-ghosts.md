## 11. The Waystation Ghosts

Tomas Reyes had spent eleven years guiding people through mountains and had learned one rule above all others: the mountain decides when you stop. Not the map. Not the schedule. Not the man with the ledger and the deadline.

So when the snow squall came down the escarpment like a gray curtain being drawn across the world, he didn't argue with it. He pointed at the notch in the ridge—half a mile of bad ground—and said, "There. That's where we sleep."

"There's nothing there," Elodie said.

"There's something there."

He was right, which was a relief, because his credibility had been running thin since the wrist. The break was six days old now, splinted with a tent pole and Petra's duct tape, and it ached in a way that made his handwriting illegible and his temper short. He'd been guiding with one good hand, doing everything half as fast and twice as carefully, and he hated every minute of it. A guide who couldn't use both hands was a passenger with opinions.

But he could still read terrain. And the notch in the ridge wasn't natural.

* * *

The waystation sat into the mountainside like it had grown there, which in a sense it had—cut stone on the lower courses, timber above, the whole structure built to shed avalanche and shrug off wind. It was maybe forty feet long, half sunk into the slope, with a low door that you had to duck through and a roof of overlapping slate that hadn't lost a single piece in a century.

Tomas went in first, because that was the job, because even with a busted wrist nobody else was going first.

Inside: cold air, dry air, the particular stillness of a space that had been waiting. His headlamp swept across plank floors, a long stone hearth, iron brackets where lamps had hung. Bunk platforms along one wall, big enough for twenty people if they'd slept shoulder to shoulder.

"Kessavian," Maren said from the doorway. She didn't sound surprised. She sounded like a woman confirming an entry in a catalog she'd memorized years ago. "Relay stations. They ran a supply line over the escarpment—grain going up, ore coming down. There were supposed to be nine of them."

"And this is number what?"

She ran her light along the wall until it caught something carved beside the door frame. "Seven."

Petra whistled from outside. Elodie just stood in the doorway letting the wind push at his back, looking at the dark inside like it might look back.

"Well," he said finally. "It beats a tent."

It did beat a tent. That was the strange part. Tomas had sheltered in worse places—caves, culverts, a decommissioned fire lookout with a wasp nest in the mattress—and this was better than all of them. Solid. Dry. Almost warm, once Petra got the hearth going with split planks she pried from a collapsed woodshed out back.

They ate. Rice and sausage, tea brewed strong because the altitude made weak tea taste like apology. The storm worked itself into a fury outside, and the waystation took it without complaint, and for about an hour they were five people having dinner instead of five people running out of time.

Then Elodie turned his headlamp toward the wall.

"Tomas," he said quietly. "Come look at this."

* * *

The chalk covered every wall.

Not graffiti—not exactly. Writing, hundreds of lines of it, in hands that ranged from careful schoolroom script to the shaky scrawl of someone writing by feel. Names. Dates. Sentences. Some of it was practical—*water source behind station, take the left switchback, do not trust the ice bridge after thaw*—advice passed from party to party like a relay baton. But most of it was not practical at all.

*Maria Voss, age 9, going to her aunt in Corvenne.*

*We were told we could take one bag each. I took my mother's sewing box. I don't know why. She would have taken the photographs.*

*Day 12 on the mountain. The baby is well. The baby is well. The baby is well.* Written three times, the repetition less like emphasis than like prayer.

*If anyone reads this: we had a house. We had a street. We had names for all the dogs.*

Tomas read standing still, turning slowly, his light moving down a century of farewells. This had been the last stop before the pass—the last roof between the refugees and whatever waited on the other side of the border that no longer existed. People had known it. You could feel it in the writing. These weren't messages home; these were messages *forward*, thrown at a future none of them trusted to remember them.

"They knew," Maren said softly. She'd stopped near the far wall, where the chalk grew denser, more desperate. "When Kessavia was dissolved, the population was given seventy-two hours to declare allegiance or leave. Most of them left. Through here." She touched the wall, lightly, the way you'd touch a grave marker. "This was the exit door of an entire country."

"How many?" Petra asked.

"The records say forty thousand crossed the escarpment in eighteen months. The records also burned, so." She shrugged, and the shrug didn't work; her shoulders wouldn't perform indifference. "Forty thousand is the number that survived the burning. Make of that what you will."

Elodie had gone quiet in the way he did when he was calculating something. Tomas had watched him do it for weeks now—that flat stillness, eyes moving like abacus beads. Usually the calculation was about money. Tonight it looked different. Tonight it looked like a man reading a room full of ghosts and wondering which of them had signed his name to something.

"We should sleep," Tomas said, because somebody had to say it. "Long day tomorrow. The pass approach eats slow climbers."

Nobody moved for a moment. Then Petra laughed—a short, startled sound—and pointed at the bunk wall.

"Look at this one."

* * *

It was low on the wall, at the height of a child's hand, and it wasn't words at all. It was a drawing.

Chalk, faded but stubborn: a mountain rendered in the confident wrong proportions of a kid who drew what things meant rather than what things looked like. Switchbacks. A stick-figure line of people climbing. And at the top, circled twice, a shape that stopped Tomas's breath for a second.

A door. Round-topped, set into the mountain itself, with a design on it—a circle with rays, or a wheel, or—

"A seal," Maren whispered. She crouched, careful of her knees, and brought her light close. "That's the Kessavian crest. The sun-wheel. It's on the ledger's wax stamp. It's on—" She stopped herself.

"It's on the chest," Elodie finished.

Below the drawing, in a child's uneven letters: *PAPA SAYS THE DOOR IS WHERE THE MONEY SLEEPS. BUT ANNA SAYS IT IS WHERE THE BAD THING IS LOCKED. I THINK THEY ARE BOTH RIGHT.*

Silence. The stove ticked. Wind pressed against the slate roof like a palm testing a lid.

"That's a joke," Petra said. "Right? Kid making up stories."

"Kids repeat what adults say when adults think they're asleep," Tomas said. He knew this from experience, though not the kind anyone wanted to hear about. "Somebody told that child two different stories about that door. Which means somebody argued about it. Loudly enough to be overheard."

Maren was still crouched, still staring. "The official history says the royal treasury was seized by the provisional government during dissolution. Inventoried, auctioned, dispersed. There's no vault. There was never any vault."

"And yet," Elodie said, "here's a hundred-year-old drawing of one, done by a kid whose family walked past it."

"Or heard about it," Tomas said. "Or invented it. Children draw dragons too."

"Do children draw dragons with the national seal on them?" Maren stood, brushing chalk dust from her knee, and looked at Elodie with an expression Tomas couldn't fully read—part challenge, part warning. "Your ledger gives coordinates. My grandfather's government swore there was nothing to find. One of those documents is lying, Mr. Eastwick, and I've spent my whole life assuming it was mine."

Elodie held her gaze for a long moment. Then he looked back at the child's drawing, at the little circle of people climbing toward the circled door, and something crossed his face that Tomas had never seen there before. Not greed. Not calculation.

Doubt.

"Sleep," Tomas said again, firmer this time. "All of you. Whatever that door is, it'll keep till morning. Ghosts always do."

* * *

Tomas didn't sleep.

His wrist saw to that—it throbbed in time with his pulse whenever he lay still, a dull metronome counting down something he preferred not to name. So he lay on his bunk near the hearth and listened to the others breathe, and watched the firelight move across the walls, and read the chalk by flicker instead of headlamp, which somehow made it worse. In firelight the messages looked fresh. Like the ink—or the dust—hadn't dried yet.

Around what he guessed was midnight, he heard Petra get up.

She moved quietly—he'd give her that—but a cook learns to hear people in a kitchen, and a guide learns to hear people in the dark, and Petra Ilves was neither sneaky nor subtle no matter how hard she tried. She crossed to the far wall, the one with the densest writing, and stood there for a long time. Then she knelt. Then she stayed kneeling.

Tomas gave it ten minutes before he got up too, cradling his splinted arm, and joined her at the wall.

She didn't startle. She just glanced at him and went back to reading. Her light was fixed on a single line, low on the stone, written small and pressed hard enough to snap the chalk:

*I am staying. Someone must be from here. If the country is gone then I am the country. — E. Sund, blacksmith, Kessavia forever*

"He stayed," Petra said. Her voice was rough. "Everyone else walked. This guy stayed."

"Maybe he had a reason."

"Maybe he was a fool."

"Those are often the same reason."

She huffed—not quite a laugh—and wiped her face with the heel of her hand, fast, like killing a mosquito. Tomas pretended not to see. That was the other thing guides learned: when to watch and when to grant privacy.

"My brother writes me letters," she said, after a while. "From the contract camp. They let him write one page a month. He uses it to tell me he's fine." She stared at the blacksmith's name. "Every letter says fine. Eleven letters. Fine, fine, fine. You know what that means?"

"I know what that means."

"Yeah." She swallowed. "These people wrote on a wall because there was nobody left to write to. No address. No post office. Just—whatever came up this mountain next." She put her fingers near the chalk but didn't touch it. "And we're walking up their road to dig up their country's grave and check if there's anything in the pockets."

Tomas said nothing. There was nothing useful to say. He'd guided rich tourists and desperate ones, and the desperate ones always talked at night, always, because night stripped the daytime armor off, and the best thing a guide could do was stand there and be a wall to talk at.

"You ever lose anybody?" Petra asked suddenly.

"Yes."

"To the mountain?"

"No. To paperwork." He almost smiled. "Worse mountain. Steeper."

This time she did laugh, wet and brief, and then she got up and went back to her bunk, and Tomas stayed at the wall a while longer.

He read the messages until the fire burned low. *Going to Corvenne. Going to Halvard. Going wherever they'll have us.* A hundred exits from a country that had been erased so thoroughly that its own survivors' grandchildren would grow up thinking it was a fairy tale. And threaded through all of it, appearing every few feet like a refrain, variations on a single theme:

*Remember us.*

*Say we existed.*

*Tell them we were real.*

Tomas Reyes was not an educated man. He'd learned three languages from trail talk and one from a girlfriend, and he'd forgotten most of his school lessons except how to read weather and how to read people. But he understood, lying there in a dead nation's guesthouse, that he was reading the only history those people had been allowed to keep. Not ledgers. Not archives. Chalk on a wall, written by lamplight by people with one bag each and seventy-two hours.

And somewhere above them, up past the switchbacks, past the pass approach, past whatever the weather decided to allow—there was a door. A child had drawn it. A child had written that some people called it money and some people called it a locked bad thing.

Tomas thought about Elodie's face when he'd read the drawing. Thought about Maren's voice saying *one of those documents is lying*. Thought about the Verrin brothers' cheerful voices on the satellite phone, and Finch's salvage boat shadowing them, and the simple arithmetic of the thing: four separate groups of armed, motivated, indebted people all climbing toward the same door, each convinced it held their salvation.

Doors that valuable didn't hold treasure. Tomas had seen what happened to villages that found gold. Treasure was just trouble with a shine on it.

Whatever was behind that door, somebody had hidden it behind a dead country's corpse and a mountain's teeth, and everybody who remembered why was a name on a wall.

He fell asleep sometime before dawn, his splinted arm propped on his chest, dreaming of chalk dust falling like snow.

* * *

Morning came blue and brutal-clear, the storm spent, the escarpment glazed in rime. The pass approach stood exposed above them—two thousand feet of switchbacks stitched up a face of gray rock, the top lost in streamers of wind-blown snow.

Tomas stood outside in the cold, working feeling back into his fingers, and studied the route while Petra melted snow for breakfast. From here he could see most of it: the traverse, the couloir crossing, the final ramp below the summit ridge. Two days, maybe three, if the weather held and nobody broke anything else.

Behind him, inside, he heard Maren call out—sharp, odd.

"Elodie. Come here. Now."

Tomas turned. Elodie was already moving, and Tomas followed him in, and they all crowded to the far wall where Maren's light was fixed on a section of chalk that hadn't caught Tomas's eye in the dark.

It was newer. Not new—but newer. The chalk was brighter, the hand steadier, and the message was in three languages, Kessavian and two neighbors', as if the writer had wanted to be understood by whoever came next, from whichever direction.

*TO WHOEVER CLIMBS PAST THIS POINT: THE VAULT WAS OPENED IN MY GRANDFATHER'S TIME. WHAT WAS TAKEN FROM IT SHOULD HAVE BEEN LEFT. TURN BACK. SOME PROVISIONS ARE MADE BY REFUSING THEM.*

Below it, a date. Forty-one years ago.

And below that, smaller, in a different hand, added later:

*We did not turn back either.*

Nobody spoke. Outside, the wind picked at the slate roof, patient as a creditor.

Elodie reached out and traced the older message with one finger, not touching the chalk, just following its line in the air, and when he turned around his face had gone very calm—the specific calm, Tomas thought, of a man who has just realized exactly how much trouble he is in and is deciding, right now, whether to say so.

"Pack up," Elodie said. "We climb today."

Tomas looked at the warning one more time. *Turn back*, it said, in three languages, written by someone who hadn't.

He picked up his pack with his one good hand, and followed.
