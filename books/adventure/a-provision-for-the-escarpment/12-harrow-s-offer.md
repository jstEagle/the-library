## 12. Harrow's Offer

Finch found him at the worst possible hour, which was probably the point.

They'd made camp in a fold of the escarpment where the river's last tributary pooled against black stone—a shelf of flat ground barely wide enough for three tents and a cook fire. Above them the mountain went up in earnest now, switchbacks stitched into the rock face like sutures, and the first snow of the season sat on the high shoulders like something waiting to be shrugged down onto them. Elodie had sent Petra and Maren up the trail at first light to scout the third switchback, which had crumbled into scree sometime in the last geological week, and Tomas was sleeping off his wrist with the help of half a bottle of the medicinal brandy he pretended wasn't for himself.

Which left Elodie alone, mending a boot sole with waxed thread, when the gravel behind him shifted and there was Finch—standing at the edge of camp with his hands visible and empty, the way a man does when he wants you to know he could have been holding a gun and chose not to make the point.

"Mr. Eastwick." Finch was maybe fifty, built like a man who'd spent thirty years lifting salvage instead of weights, with a face that had been around salt water long enough to stop pretending it hadn't. He wore better gear than anyone on Elodie's crew and wore it badly, creased wrong, like clothing bought by someone else. "Mind if I sit? Your guide isn't here to throw me off the mountain, and I promise I'm heavier than I look."

"You've got a boat full of men two bends downstream," Elodie said. He didn't put down the boot. "You don't need an invitation."

"I've got a boat full of men who are cold, wet, and starting to ask questions about their hazard pay." Finch lowered himself onto a boulder with the care of someone whose knees had opinions. "Harrow included. My partner is not enjoying this expedition, Mr. Eastwick. Harrow enjoys nothing. It's why we work."

"You're a long way from your boat for small talk."

"I am." Finch studied the fire, then the tents, then the thread pulling through the boot leather with what looked like genuine professional interest. "You stitch a sole like a man who's been poor his whole life. That's not an insult. It's the only kind of competence I trust."

Elodie tied off the knot and set the boot down. "Say whatever you climbed up here to say. They'll be back within the hour."

"They'll be back within the hour," Finch agreed pleasantly. "So let's use it."

He reached inside his coat—not fast, telegraphing every inch—and produced a leather folio, which he set on the ground between them without opening. Then he folded his hands and looked at Elodie with the frank patience of a man about to quote a price.

"Harrow & Finch will buy your claim. Entirely. You walk away today, we carry you down to the river ourselves, you keep your crew intact, your guide keeps his reputation prospects marginally less dead, and your creditors—" a small pause, delicate as a scalpel, "—are paid. In full. By us. Today, by wire, before you're even back at the dock."

Elodie kept his face still. Inside, arithmetic started running whether he wanted it or not—the Verrin number, the interest, the thirty days that had become twenty-two, the specific weight of a debt that collected in blood. Paid in full. The words had a physical pull, like current.

"How much," he said.

"A figure you won't believe until I show you." Finch opened the folio. Inside was a single sheet, bank letterhead, a number written in ink so confident it might as well have been engraved. Elodie read it twice. It was more than the debt. It was more than the debt plus the expedition plus five years of his life at storm-chaser rates. It was, in fact, roughly what a man like Elodie would need to never be poor again, padded generously enough to make the point that the padding itself was part of the message.

"That's not a buyout," Elodie said slowly. "That's a ransom. You only pay ransom when you think the thing you're buying is dangerous."

Something moved behind Finch's eyes—quick, quickly controlled, but Elodie had spent years reading skies and people were just weather with worse manners. He'd seen it. The flicker of a man whose script had skipped a line.

"It's a business transaction," Finch said.

"No." Elodie leaned forward, elbows on knees, and watched Finch the way he'd watch a wall cloud: patiently, looking for rotation. "A buyout is what you offer a competitor. A finder's fee is what you offer a fool. This—" he tapped the paper, "—is what you offer a man standing on something you're afraid of. So. What's in my vault, Finch?"

"There is no vault."

"There's a vault. You've known the route since before I did—that's the part I keep chewing on. Somebody sold you our line, fine, but somebody also told you where the line *went*, and they told you before I ever opened that chest. Which means whoever's paying you didn't need my ledger. They needed me. A disposable debtor with a legitimate claim, walking up a mountain on their behalf." He let that sit. "The Verrins bet on both boats. I figured that out days ago. What I haven't figured out is why the second boat is willing to spend this much to sink the first one."

Finch was quiet for a long moment. Downriver, water worked at stone, patient as everything else on this mountain. When he spoke again, the pleasantness had thinned enough to see through.

"Do you know what Harrow & Finch actually does, Mr. Eastwick? Not the brochure version. We recover things people want lost. Ships that sank carrying cargo no insurer will name. Documents from wrecks that were 'accidentally' scuttled. We are very good at finding what powerful parties prefer stays drowned, and we are paid twice—once to find it, once to lose it again properly." He turned the folio over in his hands. "I have been doing this for twenty-six years. And in twenty-six years, I have developed a nose. Not for treasure. Treasure smells like nothing. But there's a smell certain jobs have. Certain *clients*. And this job—" he exhaled through his nose, "—this job reeks of it."

"Of what?"

"Of men who burn archives." Finch said it flatly, no drama, which somehow made it worse. "Our client didn't hire us to retrieve an inheritance. You don't pay this number for paper with a dead nation's crest on it. You pay this number because you know what the paper *proves*, and you need it to prove nothing. Ever. To anyone." He met Elodie's eyes. "Whoever sealed that vault didn't hide money up there, Eastwick. Money gets spent. Money gets forgiven. Whatever they hid, they hid it because spending it would be safer than letting it be found—and the people who've kept that secret for a hundred years are still alive, still organized, and still paying. I know because I'm one of the people they pay."

The wind came down the escarpment and pushed the fire sideways. Elodie thought about Maren's grandfather and the dissolution order, about burned records, about a scandal so large a country had to be erased to contain it. He thought: *she knows*. He thought: *of course she knows, she's known since the archive*.

"And if I refuse?" Elodie asked.

"Then I'm obliged to inform you of the terms of refusal." Finch's voice stayed level, but his hands had gone still in a way that cost him effort. "My client's instruction, verbatim: if the claimant cannot be purchased, the claimant is a liability, and liabilities above the treeline in October have historically resolved themselves." He paused. "I am telling you this because I want you to take the money, Mr. Eastwick. Not because I'm sentimental. Because I've read the file—or the pieces of it I could get—and I would rather pay you than bury you, and not mainly for moral reasons. Burying people creates paperwork. Paperwork creates questions. Questions are the entire enemy of my profession."

"You're warning me off and offering me a fortune in the same breath."

"I'm offering you the only exit that doesn't end with you in the scree field." Finch stood, joints announcing themselves, and nudged the folio an inch closer with his boot. "Take it to your crew. Take it to your God. I'll be at the second bend until tomorrow's light. After that, my instructions change, and I stop being the reasonable one on this mountain. There's a man on my boat named Corver who's been getting paid extra to be unreasonable, and winter is coming down faster than either of us would like."

He turned to go, then stopped, half-facing the fire.

"One more thing. Free of charge, because I like you, and liking people is a weakness I can't afford twice." His voice dropped. "Your archivist. Kolb. Ask her what her family name buys her on this mountain, and ask her why a woman who hates her grandfather's signature needs the *originals* and not just the truth. Truth redeems. Originals trade. Think about which one she came for."

Then he was gone, down the switchback with the sure-footedness of a man who'd climbed out of wrecks for a living, and the mountain swallowed him the way it seemed prepared to swallow everything.

* * *

Elodie sat with the folio for a long time.

He read the number again. He did the arithmetic again, honestly this time, the way he did wind loads before a chase—no optimism, no rounding in his favor. The number cleared the Verrins. It outfitted a new truck. It put a roof over ten winters of chasing without a single contract signed in blood. It was, objectively, the best deal anyone had ever offered him, and it had arrived wearing the face of a frightened man.

That was the thing he couldn't shake. Greed he knew how to carry; greed was just hunger with paperwork. But Finch hadn't smelled like greed. Finch had smelled like a man standing downwind of something burning, offering Elodie money to walk the other way before the smoke reached them both. Twenty-six years of salvage, and the veteran of a thousand drowned secrets had climbed a mountain in bad boots to beg a stranger to quit.

You didn't pay that kind of money for gold. Gold had a price. Whatever was in the vault had a *consequence*, and consequences were the one commodity that appreciated.

He heard them coming before he saw them—Petra's voice first, arguing happily with Maren about load-bearing charges, Tomas's laugh trailing after despite the wrist and the brandy. Elodie slid the folio into his coat, feeling its weight settle against his ribs like a second ledger, and stood to meet them.

Maren read him immediately, the way she always did, her eyes going sharp and narrow. "Someone was here."

"Finch," Elodie said. "Alone. He offered to buy us out."

Petra snorted. "How much?"

"Enough." He hesitated, and hated himself for hesitating, and decided that on a mountain full of liars he could afford one clean sentence. "And he told me the client isn't after treasure. He thinks the vault holds proof of something worth killing a century to protect. And he said—" Elodie looked at Maren, and made himself finish it, "—he said to ask you what your family name buys you up here. And why you need the originals instead of the truth."

The argument about charges died in Petra's mouth. Tomas stopped moving. Above them, far up the black face of the escarpment, the first flakes of the real snow began to come down—fat, slow, unhurried, like ash falling upward—and Maren stood very still in the middle of camp with the weather gathering behind her eyes.

"We need to talk," she said. "All of us. Before that becomes a blizzard."

Because it was going to. Elodie could read a sky, and the sky had just changed its mind about them.
