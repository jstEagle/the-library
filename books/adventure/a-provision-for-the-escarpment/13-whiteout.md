## 13. Whiteout

The storm didn't arrive so much as it was always there, waiting above the treeline for them to climb into its mouth.

Petra Ilves felt it first in her teeth—a pressure change she'd learned to read in her grandmother's kitchen on the coast, where weather came off the water like a debt collector. She stopped on the switchback and spat into the wind to see which way it bent.

"Elodie." Her voice came out flat and certain. "We have maybe two hours."

He looked up from the map he was pretending to read. Two days of climbing had stripped the optimism off him, leaving something leaner underneath that Petra found easier to trust. "Two hours until what?"

"Until the sky comes down." She nodded at the west, where the clouds had gone the color of wet slate and were stacking themselves against the escarpment like freight cars coupling. "That's not weather. That's a wall."

Tomas, cradling his splinted wrist against his chest, said nothing. He'd said very little since the rockfall—since the moment his good hand closed on Maren's pack strap and hauled her back from the edge while a boulder the size of a stove went past where she'd been standing. His face had settled into an expression Petra recognized from mining camps: the look of a man recalculating whether the mountain was worth it.

Maren Kolb stood apart from all of them, wind tearing at her hood, watching the storm build with an expression Petra couldn't name. Not fear. Something more like recognition.

"We need shelter," Maren said. "Real shelter. A tent won't hold through this."

"There's nothing real above the treeline," Tomas said.

"There's one thing," Elodie said.

They all looked at him. He'd been holding out on them again—Petra could tell by the way he wouldn't quite meet their eyes—and she felt the old anger rise in her chest, the anger she'd been carrying since the night she'd confessed to selling their route and he'd looked at her with those calculating eyes and decided to keep her anyway. Mercy as an investment. She understood the transaction. She still hated it.

"The waystation," Elodie said. "Kessavian survey markers put it half a mile east of the ridge line. If it's still standing."

"If," Tomas said.

"It's been buried under snow for a hundred years," Maren said. "Snow doesn't tear things down. Snow preserves them."

* * *

They made the waystation with forty minutes to spare.

It was exactly as unremarkable as a building designed to be forgotten should be: a low stone structure set into the slope, its roof collapsed at one corner, its door—a slab of Kessavian ironwood gone gray as bone—still hanging on hinges that had outlived the nation that forged them. The seal was carved above the lintel, worn nearly smooth. Petra ran her glove over it and felt the ghost of some animal she couldn't identify, its jaws open around something round.

Inside, they got the fire going with Petra's last three sticks of dynamite-grade determination and half a bottle of stove fuel, and then the storm arrived and erased the world.

She'd been in whiteouts before. This one was different. This one sounded personal—the wind came down the escarpment and hit the waystation like it had a grudge, screaming through the gap in the roof, driving snow through every crack until the interior filled with a fine, stinging haze. Within an hour you couldn't see across the room. Within two hours you couldn't hear yourself think.

They rigged the tarp over the broken corner and lost the fight within twenty minutes. They wedged Tomas's pack frame into the gap instead, and that held, mostly, and the temperature inside dropped to something just barely compatible with staying alive.

The rations lasted one day.

Not because there weren't enough—there had been enough, barely, calculated by Elodie with the same arithmetic he applied to everything, the arithmetic of a man who'd learned that hope is a currency and budgets accordingly. But the cold burned calories like a furnace, and by the second morning Petra caught herself doing sums she didn't want to do: how long a person could last without food if they didn't move, if they stayed in their bag, if they let the fire die and slept.

Nobody said it out loud. That was the etiquette of starvation. You did the math privately and lied about your hunger publicly, and everyone knew everyone was lying, and the lies were a kind of warmth.

By noon of the second day, the lying stopped working.

* * *

It was Maren who broke first, which surprised Petra. She'd had the archivist pegged as the type who would ration her secrets the way she rationed everything else—in precise, defensible portions.

But hunger does strange bookkeeping. It makes you spend what you've been hoarding.

They were huddled around the stove, all four of them, close enough that their shoulders touched, because touching meant sharing heat and sharing heat meant living. The wind had found a new note, higher, almost conversational, like it was tired of screaming and wanted to talk.

"My grandfather signed it," Maren said.

Petra looked up. Elodie didn't move. Tomas, half-asleep in his bag, opened one eye.

"The dissolution order," Maren said. "Kessavia. 1923. There were eleven signatures on the final instrument. Aldous Kolb was the seventh." She said it the way you'd recite a confession you'd rehearsed so many times it had worn smooth, lost all its edges. "I've spent nine years in archives. I can tell you the weight of the paper it was written on. I can tell you the ink batch by its oxidation pattern. I cannot tell you why he signed it, because no record survives that says why anything happened. They burned it all. And my family has spent a century being the people who burned it."

The stove ticked. Wind worried the tarp.

"You said you were here for the historical record," Elodie said quietly.

"I said what I needed to say to get on this boat." Maren's laugh was dry as ash. "You lied about the ledger's contents. I lied about my reasons. I told myself we were even."

"And now?"

"Now we're starving in a dead country's waiting room, and I find I don't have the energy left to be careful." She pulled her knees up, wrapped her arms around them. "Here is what I actually believe. My grandfather wasn't a monster. That's the part nobody wants to hear—that the men who dissolved a nation and burned its records were mostly ordinary, tired, frightened bureaucrats who were told the alternative was worse. I've read his letters. He was kind to his dog. He paid his gardener above market rate. And somewhere in that vault on this mountain is the reason a decent man put his name to an erasure, and I need to see it. Not for history. For him. Either he was complicit in something unforgivable, or he was covering for something unbearable, and either way my family has been paying interest on a debt we never understood." She looked at Elodie. "You wanted to know why I invited myself onto your expedition. That's why. I'm not here for treasure. I'm here for the terms of the contract."

Petra found herself staring at the fire. Because she knew about contracts. She knew about signing things you didn't fully understand, about debts that grew in the dark, about how a person could wake up one morning owing more than they'd ever agreed to owe.

Her brother had signed a mining contract at nineteen because the recruiter bought the family dinner and spoke kindly to their mother, and the contract had clauses in it that took Petra four years and a bribed notary to fully translate. By then it didn't matter what the words said. What mattered was that Joss was underground, and the company owned the next eleven years of his life, and the only thing she'd found that could buy him out early was money she didn't have—which was why she'd sold a stranger's route to Harrow & Finch, and why she was sitting in a blizzard eating the arithmetic of her own guilt.

"He'd understand," Petra heard herself say.

Everyone looked at her.

"Your grandfather." She shrugged, feeling the words come out of her like heat leaving her body, impossible to call back. "The kind ones are always the ones who sign. Nobody asks the monsters to sign. Monsters aren't trusted with paperwork." She fed a sliver of kindling into the stove. "My brother signed a contract because a man was nice to our mother. Kindness gets used. That's what it's for, sometimes."

"That's a bleak theology," Tomas said from his bag.

"I'm Estonian. We specialize."

Something that might have been a laugh went around the circle, thin and involuntary, and Petra realized that was what the confession had purchased—not absolution, not understanding, but laughter. In a whiteout, in a dead nation's waystation, with the rations gone and the storm showing no interest in ending, Maren Kolb had spent her most guarded secret and bought them all ten minutes of being human together.

Confession as survival. Petra turned the phrase over in her head and found it was true in a way she hadn't expected. Not the content of the confession—the act. You told the truth for the same reason you shared body heat. Because alone, you froze.

* * *

Elodie spoke last, near dark, when the wind had dropped to a mutter and the snow-haze in the room had settled into something almost peaceful, glittering in the stove light like the whole world had been ground down to powder.

"I'll tell you what I know," he said. "Since we're spending everything."

"Don't—" Maren started.

"No. You're right, Maren. Whatever's in that vault, we're all going to see it together or not at all, and I'm done deciding alone what people can bear." He stared into the stove. "The ledger names an heir. Me, technically—it names whoever holds it, which is a lawyer's trick, but the effect is the same. It promises an inheritance claim that clears debts. That's what it says." He paused. "What it doesn't say is what the inheritance *is*. And I've been lying to myself about how much that bothers me, because I needed it to be money. I needed it so badly I stopped asking questions. Debt does that. It makes you grateful for any door marked EXIT, and you stop checking what's behind it."

"Finch knows what's behind it," Tomas said.

Everyone turned. Tomas sat up slowly, wincing, his splinted wrist held against his chest like something borrowed and breakable.

"On the ridge, before the rockfall. He wasn't talking to me—he thought I was asleep in the lee of the rocks. He was on the radio. I heard four words." Tomas's voice was gravel and exhaustion. "*Before someone else opens it.*"

The stove ticked. Outside, the wind gathered itself for another run at the roof.

"Opens what?" Maren asked.

"A vault doesn't get hidden that well," Tomas said, "unless what's inside is worth hiding. And a salvage man doesn't pay expedition wages to beat tourists to a treasure chest." He looked at each of them in turn. "Whatever your ledger leads to, Eastwick—somebody with money believes it's not gold. Somebody with money believes it's the opposite of gold."

Petra watched Elodie's face in the stove light and saw the exact moment the arithmetic changed—saw him take the new number into his equations and come out with a total he didn't like. She'd seen that look once before, the night she'd confessed, when he'd weighed her betrayal against her usefulness and chosen investment over justice.

But this time there was no calculation behind his eyes. Only the arithmetic itself, cold and final, and beneath it something that looked almost like relief—as if part of him had known all along and had finally been given permission to admit it.

The wind hit the waystation like a fist. Somewhere above them, under six feet of new snow, the true entrance waited with whatever it had kept for a hundred years.

And the storm, Petra thought, was going to end eventually.

That was the terrible part. Storms always ended.
