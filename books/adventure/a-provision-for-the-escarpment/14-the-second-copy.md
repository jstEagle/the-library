## 14. The Second Copy

The dead man had been waiting a long time, and he'd done it neatly.

Tomas found him—or what was left of him—on the third morning after the waystation, in a shallow overhang two hundred meters off the route Maren had plotted from Elodie's ledger. Petra spotted the cairn first: a stack of stones too deliberate to be rockfall, half-swallowed by lichen. She kicked it apart expecting nothing and got a boot heel, then a shinbone, then the rest of him arranged with something like dignity against the back wall of the overhang, wrapped in canvas that had gone stiff as bark.

Nobody said anything for a while. The wind did the talking.

"Kessavian," Maren said finally. She was crouched at the edge of the shelter, not quite entering it, the way people stand at the threshold of churches they don't belong to. "Look at the buttons. Military pattern, but the crest has been cut off every one. He didn't want to be identified even by his own uniform."

"He cut them off himself," Tomas said. "You can see the knife marks. Fresh cuts, once. Not worn."

Elodie stood where he'd been standing since Petra shouted, which was nowhere in particular, doing arithmetic he didn't like. A man on this mountain. Decades ago—the canvas, the desiccation, the lichen said decades at minimum. On this mountain, on *this* line, within a day's walk of where Elodie's coordinates put the vault.

"You came up here," Elodie said quietly, to the dead man, because it was easier than saying it to anyone living. "You had a copy."

Maren's head turned. Slowly. He watched her arrive at the same thought he'd just landed on, watched her face do the thing her face did when she was filing something she intended to use later.

"We should search him," she said.

* * *

They searched him with more care than they'd searched their own packs. Petra worked the canvas wrappings loose while Tomas stood watch on the slope—not for Harrow & Finch, who hadn't been seen since the blizzard broke, but because Tomas couldn't stand still anymore with a wrist splinted and a summit he'd already decided not to reach. Maren took notes. Of course she took notes; she took notes the way other people breathed.

The man carried almost nothing. A tin cup. A folding knife worn to a sliver. And inside the last layer of canvas, tucked against his chest where a person keeps the thing that matters most—

A ledger.

Smaller than Elodie's. Water-damaged along one edge so badly that the cover had delaminated into gray felt, but the wax seal fragment clinging to the spine was unmistakable: the double-headed eagle of Kessavia, wings spread over a mountain. The same seal that had been pressed into the riverbank chest that started all of this.

Elodie didn't touch it right away. That was the strange part, the part he'd remember later—he stood there with his hands in his pockets like a man at an auction who'd suddenly realized the lot was cursed.

"You open it," he said to Maren.

"Why me?"

"Because if it's bad news, I want it delivered by someone who enjoys that sort of thing."

Something flickered across her mouth that wasn't quite a smile. She drew a knife, slit the wax residue, and opened the ledger flat on a spread tarp with the tenderness of a surgeon and the speed of a woman who knew paper could die.

The pages were warped but legible. Kessavian script, dense columns, the same format as Elodie's copy—and Maren read Kessavian the way she read everything, fast and out loud only when she wanted witnesses.

"Provision schedule. Vault inventory summary. Inheritance terms." Her finger stopped. "Coordinates."

The wind picked up. Somewhere below them, scree ticked down a chute like a clock winding itself.

"Read them," Elodie said.

She read them. Twice, because the first time nobody believed her, including her.

They were wrong. Not wildly wrong—close enough that both sets pointed at the same general shoulder of the escarpment, close enough that a careless comparison would never catch it. But the bearing differed by four degrees and the elevation by nearly forty meters, and forty meters on a face like this was the difference between a wall and a void.

"That's my copy," Elodie said. "Mine says—" He recited them from memory, because he'd been reciting them in his sleep since the river. Maren checked his ledger against the dead man's, page by page, and when she looked up her expression had gone very still and very interested, the look of an archivist who has just discovered a forgery in a collection she trusted.

"They differ," she said. "Someone altered one of these."

"Not altered. *Made.*" Elodie heard his own voice come out flat and cold and was grateful for it. "Altered means you take a real document and change it. This is a whole second document. Someone sat down with the original and produced a copy with different numbers in it. Two copies. Deliberately."

Petra, who had been quiet through all of it, said: "Three."

Everyone looked at her.

"Maren told us on the river. Before you decided we were all family." Petra's voice was careful, stripped of its usual kitchen-table warmth. "There are three copies. So now we have two, and they disagree, and the third is wherever the Verrins keep things they don't want us counting."

"And the question," Maren said softly, "is which one our friend here carried."

They all looked at the dead man again. He lay in his canvas shroud with his cut-off buttons and his tin cup, patient as furniture, and Elodie felt the ground do something underfoot that had nothing to do with geology.

Because here was the thing he kept circling and couldn't land: the dead man had died *short of the vault.* Short of it by however much—a day, an hour, a storm. But short. If his coordinates were true, he'd been climbing toward the right place and run out of life before he reached it. And if his coordinates were false—if he'd carried the forged set—then he'd died walking confidently toward a wall, or a drop, or nothing at all, sent up this mountain by someone who wanted him spent and gone.

Either way, somebody had used him.

And either way, the same somebody might have used Elodie.

* * *

They made camp in the overhang because the weather was turning again and sentiment doesn't insulate. Petra buried the man properly, stacking the cairn higher than she'd found it, and said something in Estonian over it that none of them asked her to translate. Then they sat around the stove with the two ledgers side by side on the tarp between them, and the conversation happened that Elodie had known was coming since Maren read the numbers aloud.

"So," Tomas said. His splinted wrist rested in his lap like a small animal he was minding for someone else. "Which one is yours?"

"What kind of question is that?"

"The honest kind." Tomas wasn't angry. That was the worst part. Tomas sounded tired in the marrow, a man three weeks into a trip he'd taken to outrun disgrace and finding disgrace had taken the same route. "Where did your copy come from, Elodie? A chest in a riverbank. Washed out of collapsed ground after a flood. Convenient timing—you needed a miracle that week, and there one was, sealed and stamped and waiting."

"I pried it open myself. The seal was intact. The wax was old."

"Wax can be old and still lie."

"Maren examined it," Elodie said, and immediately regretted it, because Maren went quiet in a way that made everyone turn.

"I examined it," she admitted. "The seal is genuine. The wax is period-correct. I'll swear to both." She paused, and Elodie watched her choose precision over comfort, because that was who she was. "But a genuine seal proves the document was made by someone with access to a genuine seal. It does not prove the document was made a century ago. And it does not prove the numbers were copied faithfully."

Silence. The stove hissed. Above them the escarpment held its snowline like a threat.

"Think about it from the outside," Maren continued, gentler now, which somehow made it worse. "A debtor. Deep in debt to men who collect in blood. Who finds, in the exact week his creditors give him thirty days, a treasure map to a nation's lost inheritance. What are the odds?"

"The odds are I got lucky."

"The odds are you got *chosen.*" She let that sit. "The Verrins have a dock informant. We know that—they learned about the chest through him. But what if the informant isn't how they found out? What if the informant is how the chest found *you?* Placed. Floated loose at the right bend of the river, in front of a drowning man with a truck to replace and a talent for finding things other people lose."

Elodie laughed. It came out wrong, short and ugly. "That's insane. You're telling me the Verrins engineered a flood."

"No. I'm telling you floods happen every spring, and people who think in ledgers notice patterns. Maybe they didn't place it. Maybe they just recognized it when their informant reported it—and instead of taking it, they let you keep it. Funded your expedition. Bet on you." She tapped the dead man's ledger without touching it. "Or maybe someone else placed it entirely, and the Verrins are chasing a map they believe because you believe it. There are too many hands on this document to know whose fingerprints are whose. That's the point of a forgery. It's designed to be believed by the person it's fed to."

"And the dead man?" Petra asked. "Who fed him?"

"Same hands, maybe. Different decade. Different expendable climber." Maren's voice dropped. "He cut the crests off his own buttons. That's a man hiding from Kessavia's enemies—which means he believed the inheritance was real, and believed claiming it would make him a target. He was an heir, or thought he was. And someone gave him a map."

"Or someone gave him *your* map," Elodie said, "and yours is the forgery."

"Yes," Maren said simply. "That's also possible. Possibly likelier. Your copy surfaced recently. His has been in the ground for fifty years. Documents age toward truth."

"Not always," Elodie said. "Sometimes they age toward whatever the forger wanted, and everyone who could contradict them dies on a mountainside."

It hung there between them: the possibility that the honest-seeming artifact was the trap precisely because it seemed honest. Elodie looked at his ledger—the one he'd slept with, gambled with, lied to the Verrins about—and felt it change weight in his memory. Same weight. Different object entirely, maybe. A thing built to move him, the way bait is built to move fish, and no fish ever caught the angler's hand in its mouth.

Tomas spoke into the silence. "I'm not going on."

"Tomas—"

"No. Listen." He held up the splinted wrist as evidence, though they all knew the wrist wasn't the reason. "One of these maps is a lie. Maybe both. I've got a broken wrist, we're four days from the window closing, and I'm supposed to follow numbers that two documents can't agree on, up a mountain that already collected Petra's blood and my bone. I'll take Maren's injured and the gear cache down to the waystation. Anyone who wants to follow a maybe-forgery can follow it without me."

"It might be real," Elodie said. "The discrepancy might mean nothing. Scribes err. Copies drift."

"Scribes err by a letter," Tomas said. "Not by forty meters and a bearing. Somebody moved those numbers on purpose, Elodie. The only question left standing is whether they moved them toward the vault or away from it—and which of us they moved them *for.*"

He started packing. Nobody argued, because there was nothing to argue with. Petra glanced at Elodie, and he saw the question in her face before she asked it, saw her weighing the same arithmetic he was: follow the debt-map or turn back, trust the drowned document or the buried one, and either way walk forward knowing that somewhere behind all of this stood a pair of cheerful brothers who had never once expected repayment in money.

Elodie looked at the two ledgers lying together on the tarp, one fresh from a river and one old as his father, disagreeing by four degrees.

Then he looked up at the escarpment, at the shoulder of rock where the true numbers—or the false ones—converged, and made the only decision left that was actually his.

"We triangulate," he said. "Both sets. The vault has to sit where the truth overlaps—and if there's no overlap, then we'll know exactly what kind of lie we've been climbing."
