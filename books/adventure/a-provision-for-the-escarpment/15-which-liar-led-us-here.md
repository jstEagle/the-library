## 15. Which Liar Led Us Here

The dead man's ledger lay open between us on the waystation floor, and nobody would touch it.

We'd carried it down from the ledge where we found him—or what was left of him, a leather satchel of bone and waxed canvas tucked behind a cairn some previous traveler had built, or maybe he'd built himself, a man stacking stones against the fact of his own death. The second copy. Water-stained, frost-bitten, but legible where it mattered, and what mattered was this: the coordinates were wrong.

Not Elodie's coordinates. His said the vault sat in the eastern face, above the third switchback, where the scree funneled into a chute. The dead man's said west, past the waystation, into country no sane route would take you. One of them was forged. And one of us had been carrying the forgery since the river.

Maren Kolb had spent eleven years in archives learning to read documents the way doctors read faces—the flush of authenticity, the pallor of fraud—and she knew before she finished the first page that she didn't know. That was the worst part. She could tell them nothing certain, and certainty was the only currency anyone had left to spend.

"Say it plainly," Petra said. She stood with her back to the wall, arms crossed over the chalk messages, as if the refugees' century-old handwriting might offer testimony. "One of these books is a lie. Whose?"

"Mine's not a lie." Elodie's voice came out flat and careful, the voice of a man walking on ice he already knew was thin. He hadn't slept. None of them had, really, but it showed on him like weather. "I pulled that chest out of a riverbank with my own hands. I didn't write it. I didn't alter it."

"You didn't have to." Maren heard her own voice and was surprised by how steady it was. Eleven years of saying *the record shows* had trained her for exactly this room. "You fished it out of a flood. Floods move things. Rivers deliver. The question isn't whether you wrote it—it's whether someone wrote it *for you to find*, and whether they knew exactly how badly a man in your position wants to believe in buried money."

Elodie looked at her then, and she watched him do the arithmetic she'd already done: the Verrin brothers, cheerful as Sunday, who'd offered to fund an expedition at sixty percent; the dock informant; Harrow & Finch waiting on the water before Elodie had even bought rope. A man drowning in debt is not a hard man to bait. You don't sell him the map. You let him find it, so he'll trust it with his life.

"You think they planted it," Elodie said slowly. "The chest."

"I think," Maren said, "that the Verrins lend money to people who can't pay, and I've been wondering since day one what they actually collect. Money runs out. Men don't—not if you spend them right. An expendable debtor with a map and a deadline will climb any mountain you put in front of him."

"That's—" He stopped. Ran both hands through his hair. "That's insane. They gave me thirty days. Why send me up here? What's on this mountain worth that theater?"

"Ask the dead man," Petra said, nodding at the satchel. "He climbed it too. Forty years ago, maybe fifty, from the look of the gear. Somebody sent *him*."

Silence took the room. Outside, the wind worked at the waystation roof with patient fingers, prying loose whatever the last century had left unfastened. Tomas sat apart near the cold stove, his broken wrist splinted against his chest, and Maren realized he hadn't spoken since they'd come down off the ledge. He was looking at the two ledgers the way a guide looks at a river he no longer trusts—reading water that had changed overnight.

"Tomas," she said.

"Don't." He held up his good hand. "Don't put me in the middle of this. I'm already in the middle of this. That's the problem."

"Then say which side of the middle you're standing on."

He laughed, short and without humor, and got to his feet with the care of a man whose body had become an argument against him. "You want to know what I know? I know routes. I've spent twenty years learning which maps lie, because a lying map doesn't kill you on paper—it kills you on the mountain, three days from anywhere, when the switchback it promised turns out to be a cliff face with a nice drawing on it." He nodded at Elodie's ledger. "Your book says east. East is a chute. I've been looking at that chute since yesterday, and chutes like that funnel rockfall, and rockfall doesn't care whose signature is on your paper. The other book says west, around the shoulder, through country that at least *exists*." He paused. "But here's what I also know. The dead man followed the western route. And the dead man is dead."

"The dead man broke his leg in a rockfall and froze," Petra said. "That's not the map's fault."

"No," Tomas agreed. "It isn't. But I've got a busted wrist, Maren's hands haven't warmed up properly in two days, and every hour we stand here arguing, winter's coming down that ridge like it's being paid by the hour. So somebody tell me—which liar led us here? Because I'm not following either one of you until I know."

*

Maren stepped outside because the walls had gotten too small for the truth.

The waystation clung to a shelf of the escarpment like something the mountain had decided, temporarily, not to swallow. Below, the world fell away in gray terraces to the tree line and beyond it the river, invisible now, just a seam of cloud. Above, the peaks wore their first snow like a warning delivered in advance. First snow. The legal window. Somewhere under all that white was a door, and somewhere behind the door was whatever had killed a nation, and Maren had come four hundred miles to see it because her grandfather's name was on the dissolution order like a stain that forty years of scrubbing hadn't lifted.

She'd told them that much in the blizzard. Proof. Redemption. She hadn't told them the rest—that if the documents proved Kessavia's rulers engineered their own catastrophe, then her grandfather hadn't signed away a guilty nation. He'd signed away an innocent one. That he'd known, maybe. That the family name wasn't dirty; it was *complicit*. There were versions of the truth where redemption meant burning everything she'd come to find.

And now there were two ledgers, and one of them was a forgery, and she couldn't help noticing how convenient it all was—for everyone. If Elodie's copy was fake, the Verrins had spent a debtor like a match. If the dead man's copy was fake, then someone had climbed this mountain decades ago to plant a false trail, which meant the real secret was worth killing for across generations. Either way, the truth was a weapon, and Maren had spent her whole career around weapons that looked like paper.

Footsteps behind her. She knew the gait before she turned—Elodie walked like a man perpetually braced for a bill.

"If mine's the forgery," he said, without preamble, "then the Verrins knew about Kessavia before I did. Before the chest ever washed out. Which means the flood, the timing, the whole thing—"

"—was a delivery," Maren finished. "Yes."

"And if theirs is the forgery?"

"Then someone wanted the vault found by whoever followed the eastern route, and wanted anyone following the western route to die in the scree with a false map in their bag." She hugged her arms against the wind. "Either way, Elodie, we weren't led here by accident. We were *routed*."

He stared out at the white. "Finch tried to buy me off. Back below the treeline. Offered me more than the claim was worth to walk away." He said it like a confession, and she understood why—he should have told them sooner, and they both knew it, and telling her now was a down payment on a debt of honesty he'd been running since the river. "He wasn't scared I'd find gold. He was scared I'd find whatever it is."

"Which means Finch knows what it is," Maren said. "Which means the people who hired Finch know. Which means at least three parties on this mountain are betting on different doors." She turned to face him. "So here's my question, and I need the truth, because I'm about to bet my grandfather's name on the answer. When you opened the chest on the riverbank—before the seal, before the ledger—what did you feel?"

Elodie blinked. "What kind of question is that?"

"A document examiner's question. Forgeries are made by people who want something. They're efficient. They cut corners. But the chest—the seal, the wax, the way the ledger was wrapped—" She'd turned it over in her mind a hundred times since the ledge. "Whoever made that chest wanted it believed. Desperately. That's not how you forge a trap. That's how you forge a *prayer*. Someone made that thing hoping it would reach the summit someday. Hoping someone would finish what they couldn't."

Elodie was quiet a long moment. "Or you're seeing what you need to see. Your grandfather. Your name."

"Yes," Maren said simply. "Probably. We're all doing that. Petra's buying her brother, Tomas is buying his reputation, you're buying your life back, and I'm buying a dead man's honor. Nobody climbs this mountain for free." She looked back toward the waystation door. "The question was never whether we're liars. It's whether our lies point the same direction."

*

Inside, the vote happened without anyone calling it a vote.

Tomas spoke first, and he spoke like a man laying down cards. "I'm taking the injured back down. My wrist is useless for climbing, Maren's half-frozen, and I'll not drag two cripples up a chute full of rockfall on a map one of you might be carrying by mistake." He said *cripples* gently, including himself. "Petra, you keep the charges. If the door's real, you're the only one who can open it anyway."

"I can walk," Maren started.

"You can walk *down*," Tomas said. "Down is honest work. Up from here is faith, and I'm out of faith. Twenty years guiding, and I'll tell you the one rule that kept me alive: when the map disagrees with itself, the mountain wins. Go home."

"I'm not going home." Petra hadn't moved from the wall. "My brother signs his contract renewal in six weeks. If I come down empty, he goes into the deep seams, and the deep seams are where men stop coming back up. Whatever's behind that door, it's worth more than my life, because my life is currently worth nothing to anyone but him." She looked at Elodie. "I sold your route once. I won't sell anything again. But I'm going up."

"Maren?" Tomas asked.

Every eye came to her, and Maren felt the old archivist's vertigo—the moment when the record contradicts itself and you must choose which source to trust, knowing the choice makes you part of the record. Two ledgers. Two doors. Her grandfather's ghost on one side, a stranger's bones on the other.

She thought about the chalk on the walls behind her. *Remember us. We were here. The snow took the road.* A hundred refugees, lied to by their own government, writing their names on stone because stone was the only place left that would hold them. Whoever forged whichever ledger had counted on people like her—people who needed the truth so badly they'd follow any document that promised it.

Maybe that was the trick. Maybe that was always the trick.

"I go up," she said. "East. Elodie's route." She saw Tomas's jaw tighten. "Because the dead man's map is the one that was *carried*—carried means it was meant to be found, and things meant to be found are the oldest traps in the archive. But Elodie's chest was *hidden*. Sealed, sunk, buried in a bank for a century. Archives run on one law, Tomas: the hidden thing is the true thing. People hide what's real. They only display what they want believed."

"The dead man hid his copy behind a cairn," Petra pointed out.

"The cairn was built *after*," Maren said. "By him. To mark his grave, not to hide the book. He wanted it found eventually. That's different." She turned to Elodie. "Well? It's your map. Say something."

Elodie looked at the two ledgers a long time. Then he picked up his own—the one from the river, the one that had cost a truck and a debt and four lives' worth of trouble—and closed it, and held it against his chest like something warm.

"West was the dead man's route," he said finally. "East is mine. And I've been broke, chased, shot at, and lied to since the day I pulled that chest out of the mud, and every single mile of it has pointed east. If the Verrins planted it, they planted it well enough to fool a river, a flood, and me, and I don't have the luxury of doubting the only thing I've got left." He looked around the room. "Three of us go east at first light. Tomas takes the injured down. Anybody who thinks I'm the liar—now's the time to say it to my face."

Nobody said it. That was somehow worse than if they had.

Tomas began packing in silence, movements economical, a guide dismantling a camp he no longer believed in. At the door he stopped, wrist cradled against him, and looked back at the three of them standing in the chalk-light of the refugees' last words.

"When you find that door," he said, "remember that it was hidden by people who were very good at hiding things. And ask yourselves why a nation that wanted its treasure found sealed it in a mountain and burned all its own records." He pulled his hood up. "Dead men leave maps. Guilty men leave instructions. Try to know which one you're following."

Then he was gone into the gray, and the wind filled the space where his voice had been, and Maren stood with the two liars she'd chosen, watching the first real flakes of the season drift past the doorway like ash falling upward.

Above the escarpment, the sky was clearing. Clearing, Petra said, was worse than storming—a clear sky up here meant the temperature was falling like a debt called due, and the cold would make the rock brittle and the charges unpredictable and every hand clumsy.

At first light, they went east, toward the chute, following a map that might be a forgery, written by a nation that might be guilty, toward a door that someone, somewhere, had gone to great lengths to make sure they would never—or always—find.

Halfway up the first pitch, Maren looked back once. Far below, tiny against the gray, Tomas's party was descending into the trees.

And far above, at the head of the chute where Elodie's map promised a door, something caught the early sun and flashed—once, twice—like glass. Or a lens. Or a signal.

Someone was already there.
