## 16. The Vault Door

The dead man's ledger lay open between us on a flat slab of granite, its water-swollen pages pinned down with stones, and for the first time since the river gave me that chest I felt like I was reading it instead of being read by it.

"Again," Maren said. "Slowly."

I traced the coordinate line with my finger. "His copy puts the entrance at the third switchback above the waystation. Mine says the fourth." I looked up at her. Her lips were cracked from the wind and she'd stopped bothering to hide how cold she was, which somehow made her more formidable, not less. "Same bearing. Different elevation. One of them is wrong."

"One of them is *edited*," she said.

Petra crouched at the edge of the slab, chewing the last of a ration bar she'd been making last two days. "Or both are wrong," she said around the crumbs. "Or both are right, and the door is between them."

I stared at her. She shrugged.

"I blow things up for a living. Things that want to stay hidden get put in the middle. Nobody guards the edges."

Maren pulled out her notebook—the one she'd been keeping since the university, dense with her small precise handwriting—and began sketching. The escarpment rose above us in gray terraces, the snow line maybe two hundred meters up and falling by the hour. The sky had been bruising all morning, clouds stacking themselves into something patient and heavy.

"The discrepancy is forty meters vertical," Maren said. "If someone altered your ledger to send claimants to the wrong elevation—"

"They'd send them somewhere plausible," I said. "Not off a cliff. Somewhere you'd search. Somewhere you'd waste days."

"And the fourth switchback," Petra said, "is a scree field."

We all looked at it. It fanned across the mountainside like something spilled, a hundred years of rockfall funneled into a gully, boulders the size of carts sitting on top of rubble the size of fists. Nobody would dig through that. Nobody would even try.

Nobody except people with nothing left to lose and a deadline made of weather.

* * *

It took us until midafternoon to work our way along the base of the scree, and what we found there I will describe as carefully as I can, because I have thought about it every day since.

There was a wall under the rubble.

You couldn't see it standing back—you saw only scree, an accident of geology—but when Petra and I climbed into the field itself and started moving stones, the pattern emerged. The rocks weren't random. They were *arranged*, or they had been once, tumbled and settled but originally placed, a deliberate burial done by hands that wanted the mountain itself to do the finishing work. And beneath the largest boulder, wedged where no flood or frost could have put it, was a slab of dressed granite with carving on it.

I cleared the grit away with my bare hands. My fingers went numb before I finished.

A seal. Circular, maybe a meter across, cut deep enough to survive a century of stone grinding over it: a river running into a mountain, and above the mountain, a crown with no king's head under it. The same crest that had been stamped into the chest. The same crest from the child's drawing on the waystation wall—Petra had copied it into her own notebook, and when I glanced over at her she already had it out, comparing, her breath coming fast.

"That's it," she said. "That's exactly it. Same crown, same river. He drew it from memory. A kid who'd seen this door."

Maren stood below us, not climbing, and I realized she was crying without any movement in her face at all, just the wet tracks freezing in the wind. "My grandfather signed the order that erased them," she said quietly. "And they hid their proof under a mountain and let children memorize the door." She wiped her face with the back of her glove. "Go on. Open it."

"Not yet." I sat back on my heels. The seal was set into a frame, and the frame was set into a wall, and the wall ran back into the scree farther than I could follow. This wasn't a hatch. It was a threshold. "Doors this well-hidden were hidden for reasons. We do this properly."

"Properly," Petra repeated. "Meaning explosives."

"Meaning you find the seam without bringing half the gully down on us."

She grinned, and for a moment she was just Petra again, the cook who kept us fed and alive, before the guilt and the mining contracts came back into her eyes. "Give me an hour."

* * *

She took three hours, and they were the longest of my life, because while she worked the sky finished deciding.

The clouds came down the valley like something with intent. The temperature dropped so fast that the sweat from my climb froze in my collar, and by the time Petra called us over, our breath was hanging around us in clouds that didn't disperse. First snow. Not the legal deadline—not yet, not technically—but the mountain's own announcement of what was coming, and how little time it intended to give anyone.

"It's a plug," Petra said. She'd mapped the seams with chalk stubs—her own chalk, I noticed, saved from the waystation walls, though she didn't explain why and I didn't ask. "They didn't build a door you open. They built a door you *unseal*. Granite plug, seated in a throat of fitted stone, packed with lead around the edges. No hinges, no lock. You break the lead seal and the plug lifts—or it did, a hundred years ago. Now?" She patted the stone almost affectionately. "Now it needs persuasion."

"How much persuasion?"

"Small charges. Shaped. In the lead seams, not the granite. If I do it right, the plug cracks loose and the wall stands. If I do it wrong—" She spread her hands. "We're under two hundred tons of scree, Elodie. If I do it wrong, they never find this door again, which, honestly, might be what the Kessavians would have preferred."

Maren had climbed up beside me. "Can you do it right?"

Petra looked at her for a long moment. "I've blown my way out of worse places for worse reasons," she said. "Yes."

We retreated to the slab where the dead man's ledger still lay pinned. Petra wired her charges with hands that didn't shake—she'd told us once she'd been shaking the day the mine collapsed, and hadn't shaken since, because after that there was nothing left to be afraid of—and ran the wire back to us behind a boulder that had seemed adequate when I chose it and seemed very small when I crouched behind it.

"On three," she called. "Cover your ears and pray to whatever's listening. Probably nobody. Pray anyway."

The blast was smaller than I expected—a flat double crack, more felt than heard, a punch of pressure and a cough of dust rolling out of the scree field. Stones rattled downslope. Somewhere above us, the mountain shed a few tons of its loose skin, and I pressed my face against the granite and waited for the sound to stop.

When it stopped, there was silence, and then Petra shouting, and then we were running.

* * *

The plug had cracked clean along the lead seams, just as she'd promised, and come apart in three great pieces that leaned against each other like drunks holding each other up. Behind them: darkness, and cold air flowing out of it, air that had been sealed in there for a hundred years and smelled of stone and dryness and something else—something faintly sweet, like old paper, like a library.

A throat of fitted stone ran back into the mountain. Not far—I could see the end of it, twenty meters in, where the passage opened into a wider dark. The walls were carved. Even in the failing light I could see the carvings: the river, the mountain, the crownless crown, repeating, and between them, lines of text in Kessavian script that none of us could read.

Maren could read some of it. She stood at the threshold with her notebook open, comparing letterforms she'd spent years studying in burned archives and fragmentary records, and her voice when she spoke was steady in the way of someone holding something heavy with both arms.

"It says the same thing three times. It's a warning formula—they used it on legal documents. *Let the one who enters be bound by what he finds.*" She looked at me. "Elodie. Do you understand what that means? This isn't a treasure vault. It's a *registry*. They sealed records here. Records with legal force."

"Then we go in, verify the claim, and get out before the storm."

"Yes," she said. But she didn't move, and neither did I, and Petra stood behind us with her hand resting on the broken plug as if steadying a horse.

The sky above the escarpment had torn open in the west—one band of hard gold light, the sun going down through a gap in the clouds—and it fell across the scree field and lit the carved seal at our feet, and for about ten seconds the whole entrance blazed like something consecrated. The river in the carving caught the light and looked like it was running. The crown looked like it was waiting.

It was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen, and every instinct I owned—the ones that had kept me alive through tornadoes and debt collectors and a winter mountain—was screaming at me not to take another step.

"Doors this well-hidden," I said, mostly to myself, "were hidden for reasons."

"You've said that twice now," Petra said.

"I'm going to keep saying it until it stops being true."

It didn't stop being true. I knew that then, standing in the last of the light with the cold pouring out of the mountain like a held breath finally released. Whatever was in there had been worth killing a nation over. Worth erasing from maps. Worth sending a child's drawing down through a century so that someday, someone, might find it again—or might be found by it.

I clicked on my headlamp. Maren clicked on hers. Petra struck a flare and held it high, and the red light washed the carved walls blood-colored, and the passage swallowed our shadows behind us as we stepped through.

Twenty meters in, the passage opened, and our lights found the vault.

And the vault, in that first unguarded second before any of us understood what we were looking at, was full of light—hundreds of small reflections winking back at us from the dark, like stars, like a sky underground.

Petra raised the flare higher.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, Elodie. That's not treasure."

She was right. It wasn't.

It was paper. Thousands upon thousands of documents, sealed in wax and glass, stacked floor to ceiling in the dark—and every one of them, as far as our lights could reach, was looking back at us.
