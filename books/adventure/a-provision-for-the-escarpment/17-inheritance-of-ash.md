## 17. Inheritance of Ash

The cold came out of the vault before anything else did.

It rolled up the shaft like breath from a cellar, carrying the smell of stone and old paper and something faintly sweet, like wax left too long in a closed room. Petra's charges had cracked the seal cleanly—she was good at her work, whatever else she was—and now a black rectangle gaped in the scree where a century of rockfall had hidden it. The sky above us had cleared to that hard, rinsed blue that means the temperature is about to fall off a cliff.

"Air's fine," Maren said, holding her lantern into the opening. "Old air. Stale, but it moves."

"Of course it moves," Elodie said. "Doors this well-hidden were hidden for reasons."

Nobody laughed. That was the trouble with our crew by then: we'd all stopped pretending we were on an adventure.

I went first. Not because I was brave—I had established over nineteen days on this mountain that I was not brave—but because the ledger named me. *Heir and claimant.* Whatever waited down there had been waiting for someone with my name in their book, and it seemed rude to make it wait longer.

The passage sloped down maybe thirty feet and opened into a chamber the size of my totaled truck's garage, if my garage had been built by people who expected to be remembered forever. Kessavian work: fitted stone without mortar, walls carved in that dense, interlocking script Maren could read and I could only admire. Lantern light moved across it and the carvings seemed to shift, which I decided was a trick of the flame and not worth thinking about.

And in the center, on a table of black stone—

No gold.

I want to be honest about that moment, because honesty is the only currency I have left. For one full second, standing in a dead nation's vault with three witnesses behind me, I felt the specific vertigo of a man who has climbed a mountain on a promise and found the promise empty. My debts have a way of doing arithmetic without my permission. Sixty percent to the Verrins, minus Tomas's medical costs, minus the boat, minus Petra's cut, and what remained would have bought back my truck and maybe a winter of not being afraid. I stood there doing that math against an empty stone table and felt the mountain laugh at me.

Then Maren pushed past me with her lantern held high, and I saw what was actually on the table.

Documents. Dozens of them. Sealed in oilcloth, stacked in cedar boxes that had done their job so well the paper inside was still supple. Ledgers. Correspondence bound in ribbon. A slim volume with a cover stamped in gold leaf—the Kessavian crest, the same one that had been staring up at me from a riverbank chest since September.

Maren's hands were shaking. I had never seen them shake. She read the first page of the gold-stamped volume, and then she sat down on the floor of the vault of Kessavia like her legs had been repossessed.

"Maren," I said.

"You should read this," she said. "You especially."

* * *

Here is what the ruling family of Kessavia did, according to their own hand, in their own archive, sealed against exactly the kind of finder I turned out to be:

In the spring of the last year of the nation, the granaries of the eastern provinces failed. Not from weather. From policy. The Chancellor's council had diverted the grain reserves—sold them abroad through shell brokers to cover debts of their own, private debts, gambling debts dressed as statecraft—and when the famine came, they needed someone to blame before the starving figured out who to blame.

They chose Vellmark, the neighbor across the river. They forged correspondence showing Vellmark agents poisoning the wells. They burned their own border villages and called it Vellmark cavalry. And when the war came—the short, ugly, pointless war that killed perhaps forty thousand people and ended with both nations broken—the Kessavian crown surrendered its own country rather than let the truth surface. Dissolution by suicide. A nation that erased itself to bury a lie.

The documents proved all of it. Provenance chains. The shell brokers' real ledgers. Orders in the Chancellor's own hand. And—this was the part that made Maren sit down—a signed confession by the Chancellor himself, written the night before he took poison, addressed to whoever came next. *To the heir,* it began. *If you are reading this, you are the person I have chosen to carry what I could not.*

"Heir," Maren said. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the way voices go when they're holding something heavy very still. "Not beneficiary. Heir. As in inheritor."

"I noticed," I said.

Because I had. I was reading faster than she was now, skimming the correspondence, and the picture assembling itself was worse than the confession. The documents didn't just prove the crime. They were the crime's continuing machinery. Three foreign governments had bought Kessavian silence in the aftermath—there were receipts, actual receipts, payments routed through banks whose names I recognized from newspaper scandals. Two of those governments presumably believed these papers had burned with everything else. The third had spent a century making sure no one went looking.

"This is why Finch wanted us off the mountain," I said. "This is why the Verrins funded two expeditions. Nobody climbs a glacier for grain ledgers, Maren. Somebody hired somebody who knows what's down here."

"The Verrins don't know governments," Maren said. "The Verrins know money. Someone told the Verrins what to want."

"And that someone—" I looked around the vault, at the carved walls, at the careful, patient architecture of concealment, "—has been waiting a hundred years for a legal window and a desperate man with a claim. The inheritance isn't the documents. The inheritance is *them*. Whoever holds this becomes the person who can prove three governments paid to hide a famine they helped bury. You don't own that. It owns you."

Petra had come down the passage and stood at the edge of the lantern light, arms crossed, saying nothing. She was good at saying nothing. But when I glanced at her she said, quietly, "My brother's contract. The mining company. It's Vellmark-chartered." She let that sit. "There are people alive today whose grandparents starved because of this paper. And people alive today whose grandparents got rich keeping it buried."

"That's the provision," I said. "That's what the escarpment provides. Not gold. Culpability. Clean title to a hundred years of other people's sins."

Maren laughed—one short, terrible note with no humor in it. "My grandfather signed the dissolution order. I've spent eleven years trying to find out whether he was a monster or a man following procedure. Now I know he was neither. He was a janitor. They handed him a broom and told him the mess was already old." She pressed her palm flat against the confession. "And you're the heir. Legally. Which means every government, every bank, every descendant of every broker in these pages has exactly one problem between them and permanent silence."

She didn't finish the sentence. She didn't have to. The problem was standing in the vault holding a lantern.

* * *

We catalogued anyway. Habit, or greed's long shadow, or just the need to do something with our hands while the horror finished dressing itself as vindication. Because that's what it was, underneath—the vindication part. Everything checked out. The coordinates, the seal, the legal mechanism Maren had traced through a century of dormant-claim law. I had been right about all of it. The ledger was genuine, the claim was valid, the window was real. If I carried these documents down the mountain and filed them before first snow, I would legally become the heir of Kessavia, with standing to sue governments, sell secrets, or ransom silence.

Every path from that sentence ended somewhere I didn't want to stand.

Sell them, and I was the broker—just another shell company with a pulse, moving other people's atrocities toward the highest bidder. Publish them, and the first thing that happened was the third government's people arrived, politely, permanently, and everyone who'd touched the paper started dying of accidents. Bury them again, and I'd spent twenty-two days and Tomas's wrist and Petra's soul climbing a mountain to become a better-class of janitor than her grandfather.

"There's a fourth option," Petra said, reading my face the way she read fuse lengths.

"Don't," I said.

"Burn it."

"In here?" Maren stood up. "Elodie, think. This is history. Ugly history, but the refugees' families, the Vellmark dead—they deserve—"

"They deserve the truth," I agreed. "And the truth gets them nothing but a lawsuit their grandchildren will lose, and gets us graves. There's a version of this where I publish and it matters, Maren. It requires lawyers, protection, ten years, and me staying alive, and we both know how long I stay alive once the wrong people read a shipping manifest." I picked up the Chancellor's confession. Light paper. Heavy as ballast. "But that's a decision for daylight. Right now we have a descent to survive and a legal window that closes at first snow, and I am not deciding the fate of four nations in a hole in a mountain."

Which was when Petra, who had gone back up to check the weather, put her head down the passage and said, in the tone of a woman reporting a rockfall:

"We have company. Boats' worth. Coming up the switchbacks."

* * *

I climbed out of the vault into air that had turned to iron. Below us, strung along the escarpment trail like beads on a wire, came torches. More than one party. The lead group moved with the unhurried competence of professionals; behind them, a second cluster moved with the cheerful chaos I would have known anywhere, because I owed it money.

Maren came up beside me, wind pulling at her hood. "How?"

"The advance man knew our route in chapter ten," I said. "The Verrins bet on both boats. And Finch—" I watched the torches converge below, "—Finch tried to buy me out because he knew what was here. Everyone's been climbing toward this door. We just got here first."

"So what do we do?" Petra asked. "We could seal it. Another charge, collapse the passage—"

"No." I found I was smiling, and it wasn't a nice smile, but it was mine. "No more hiding things in mountains. That's how we got here." I looked down at the torchlight crawling toward us through the gathering dark, and above it at the first thin veils of cloud sliding in off the western peaks, early, too early, snow-clouds with bad manners and excellent timing.

First snow. The window closing. Every claimant, creditor, and killer on the mountain at once, and me standing at the door holding the only complete set.

"Everyone arrives tonight," I said. "Let's not keep them waiting."
