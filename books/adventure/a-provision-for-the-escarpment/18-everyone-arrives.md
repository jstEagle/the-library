## 18. Everyone Arrives

I heard the dogs before I saw the men.

That was the first wrong thing. There are no dogs on the escarpment—nothing up here but rock, wind, and the occasional goat with poor judgment—and the sound of barking carried up the switchbacks in that flat, echoing way sound behaves above the treeline, arriving from everywhere and nowhere at once.

Maren Kolb had spent eleven years in archives learning to read silence. The silence coming off the vault mouth behind me was a particular kind: Elodie Eastwick standing in a doorway he'd just blown open, holding a satchel of documents that could start wars, saying nothing at all. Petra Ilves crouched by her charges with her hand still on the detonator box, like a surgeon who hadn't been told the operation was over. And Tomas Reyes was three days gone down the mountain with a broken wrist and my conscience tied to his pack straps.

The barking came again. Closer.

"Elodie," I said.

"I hear it." He didn't turn around. He was looking into the vault the way you look at a thing you've already decided to stop looking at, which is to say he couldn't stop. "How many sleds does it sound like?"

"Two. Maybe three."

"Harrow & Finch travel light." He finally turned, and his face did something complicated and then settled on nothing, which is his version of screaming. "The Verrins don't."

* * *

They crested the last switchback within the hour, and they came in the order I would have predicted if I'd let myself predict anything: first the Verrins' advance man, Dov, whom we'd last seen at the supply cache with his cheerful message about expiring discounts, and behind him six men in matching oilskin who moved like they were being paid by the step. Then, strung out along the ridge like punctuation, the Harrow & Finch crew—four of them, roped together, professional in the way that made the Verrins' men look like what they were.

And between the two groups, walking alone, wearing a city coat at nine thousand feet: Finch himself.

Snow had started an hour ago. Not the blizzard yet—the real one was still stacking itself up against the western peaks, a gray wall with ambition—but the fine, dry, preliminary snow that falls when the mountain is clearing its throat. It settled on everyone's shoulders equally. That seemed important. I couldn't have said why.

"Well," said Dov Verrin—he was the younger one, the one who smiled more, which meant less—"you found it."

"We found a door," Elodie said. He was standing slightly downhill of the vault mouth, which put him between the documents and everyone, and I watched him do it deliberately, shifting his weight until the geometry of the standoff arranged itself around him. "Whether there's anything behind it worth the walk is another question."

Dov laughed. His brother Renn, older, quieter, did not. Renn looked past Elodie at the carved seal, half-emerged from scree now that Petra's charges had peeled the mountainside open, and something crossed his face that I recognized because I'd seen it in mirrors during bad weeks in the archive: hunger wearing patience as a disguise.

"You're bleeding," Renn observed, to no one in particular. His eyes stayed on the door.

"It's the altitude," Elodie said. "Everyone's bleeding up here. It's very fashionable."

* * *

Let me tell you what I knew at that moment, because knowing things and being able to use them are different skills, and I had spent my whole life confusing them.

I knew the ledger in Elodie's satchel was the third copy—or rather, the copy that had traveled under three identities, altered once, authenticated twice, and carried up this mountain by a man who owed money to people who had funded both sides of the race to find it. I knew the second copy lay wrapped in oilcloth in Tomas's abandoned camp two thousand feet below, water-stained but legible, its coordinates disagreeing with ours in ways that had nearly ended us at the fault line of trust. I knew my grandfather's signature sat at the bottom of the dissolution order that had erased Kessavia from every map in the civilized world, and that I had come up this mountain telling myself I wanted redemption when what I wanted, if I was honest, was ammunition.

And I knew, watching Finch pick his way across the scree toward us with his hands visible and empty, that he was afraid. Not of the guns. Of the door.

"Mr. Eastwick." Finch stopped ten feet away, close enough to talk, far enough to be polite about it. Snow gathered in the collar of his coat. He was maybe fifty-five, built like a filing cabinet, with the eyes of a man who reads other people's mail for a living. "You look terrible."

"The mountain will do that."

"The mountain," Finch agreed. "May I ask you a professional question?"

"You may ask."

"When your people opened that vault—" he nodded at the dark seam behind Elodie, where Petra's charges had done their work and the cold air of a sealed century breathed outward, smelling of stone and dust and something faintly chemical, like old film—"did you find gold?"

Elodie paused exactly long enough for everyone to notice.

"No," he said.

Finch closed his eyes briefly, the way a man does when a diagnosis confirms the worst. "Then I'll make you an offer, and I want you to understand it comes with respect. Walk away. All of you. Leave the documents in the vault, collapse the entrance, and every debt you carry—every obligation, every claim, every grudge anyone holds against any member of this expedition—disappears. Paid in full. By me. No questions, no receipts, no follow-up." He smiled, and it was the worst smile I've ever seen, because it was genuine. "You'd be rich, Mr. Eastwick. Richer than whatever's in there could make you, once you divide it six ways and pay the brothers their sixty percent."

"And why," Petra called from beside the detonator box, her accent flattening the words into stones, "would a salvage man pay that much for nothing?"

"Because it isn't nothing," Finch said, without looking at her. "Because I've read enough of the record to know what Kessavia buried, and I know who else has read it, and I know that whoever walks down this mountain carrying those papers is going to spend the rest of a much shorter life being paid, or hunted, or both." He turned back to Elodie. "I'm offering you the only exit that exists."

* * *

Here is what happens to time in a standoff. It doesn't stop. It thickens.

The snow fell. The Verrins' six men stood in their loose arc with rifles slung—not raised, never raised, because the Verrins preferred their violence deniable and their witnesses comfortable. The Harrow & Finch crew stood behind their employer with weapons that were better maintained. Petra stood with her thumb resting on a detonator box connected, she assured us all earlier, to secondary charges she'd seeded in the scree slope above the vault mouth, and I believed her, because Petra Ilves had never once lied to me about explosives. People, yes. Blasting caps, never.

And Elodie Eastwick stood in the middle of all of it with a satchel strap across his chest, doing arithmetic.

I've watched him do this before, on the truck radio during chases, when the storm splits and he has thirty seconds to choose a fork. He goes very still. His jaw works slightly, like he's chewing the numbers. Everyone else sees a man frozen; what's actually happening is a man running futures at speed, discarding most of them, wincing at the cost of the survivors.

"You funded them too," Elodie said suddenly, to Dov. Not a question.

Dov spread his hands, delighted to be caught. "We funded everyone. We're lenders, Eastwick. Lending is our whole personality." A ripple of laughter from his men, trained laughter, the kind that gets people promoted or disappeared. "Harrow & Finch needed capital for the season. You needed an expedition. We saw no reason the escarpment shouldn't enjoy a little competition. Competition improves outcomes."

"Whose side wins?"

"Whoever brings us the documents." Dov's smile didn't move but everything behind it did. "Or whoever burns them, honestly, we've discussed it, Renn and I—we're flexible. What we can't have is ambiguity. Ambiguity is bad for business. So here's the quote, final, in front of witnesses so nobody feels cheated later: you give my brother and me exclusive possession of those papers, and your debt dies today. Every cent. We'll even throw in the truck."

"You totaled the truck," Petra said.

"We improved the truck," Dov said. "Into its next phase."

* * *

Renn Verrin spoke then, for the first time, and the temperature dropped faster than the snow could account for.

"The archivist," he said, looking at me.

I kept my face still. Eleven years of reading dead men's silences is good training for surviving live ones.

"Maren Kolb. Granddaughter of Aldous Kolb, signatory to the Dissolution Accord." He took a step toward me, and two of his men adjusted their positions without appearing to move. "Your family name has been rotting for a hundred years, Miss Kolb. Those papers in the satchel would either redeem it or bury it deeper—I genuinely don't know which, and neither do you, because you haven't read the complete set. Has she?" he asked Elodie. "Read the complete set?"

"She's read enough," Elodie said.

"That's not what I asked." Renn's voice stayed level, almost gentle. "Has. She. Read. The complete set."

"No," I said.

Everyone looked at me. Even Finch. Especially Finch.

"No," I repeated, and stepped forward, out from behind Elodie's shoulder, because if this was going to be a negotiation over my grandfather's crimes then I was going to stand in it with my whole body. "I've read fragments. Enough to know that whatever's in there, it isn't treasure. Enough to know that the nation that wrote these documents chose to be erased rather than answer for them. And enough to know that every person standing on this mountain right now is calculating how much of that erasure they can sell." I looked at Renn. "Including you. Especially you. You didn't fund two expeditions to recover paper. You funded them to control who gets hurt by it. That's not lending. That's arson insurance."

For a moment—one full breath, two—nobody moved, and the snow came down through the silence like static, and I understood that I had just done something either very brave or very stupid, and that up here the difference mostly depended on the weather.

Renn smiled. It was the first time I'd seen him do it, and I understood immediately why Dov handled the smiling. Renn's smile was a tool with a specific function, the way a crowbar is.

"Well," he said. "Now we know where everyone stands."

* * *

It was Finch who broke the geometry, and he broke it by doing the unthinkable: he sat down. On a rock, in the snow, in his city coat, like a man waiting for a train.

"Nobody is shooting anybody," he announced, to the mountain in general. "Not up here. You discharge a rifle at this altitude in a loaded snowpack and you bury the vault, the documents, and yourselves. Everybody knows it. That's why everybody's still talking." He looked up at Elodie. "So let's talk. Final offers, all around, and then Mr. Eastwick decides, and we all live with it, or die with it, whichever the weather prefers."

"The Verrins offer freedom," Dov recited, cheerfully, like a carnival barker. "Debt cleared, plus the truck, plus safe passage down."

"Finch & Harrow offers wealth," Finch said. "Enough that none of you ever work again. In exchange, the documents come with us, intact, and disappear forever. Some things should stay buried. I'm offering to be the grave."

"And the archivist?" Renn asked softly. "What does truth offer?"

Everyone looked at me again. I felt it like a change in pressure.

I thought about my grandfather's study, and the locked drawer I'd picked open at nineteen, and the single sheet inside: not orders, not confessions, just a list of names in his handwriting, forty names, each with a small cross beside it, and no explanation anywhere in his papers of what the crosses meant. I had spent twenty years building a career on the assumption that the crosses meant guilt. Standing in the snow, I realized I had never once considered they might mean grief.

"Truth offers nothing," I said. "That's the point of it. It's the only thing on this mountain that isn't for sale, which means it's the only thing here with any value left." I turned to Elodie. "But I'm not asking him to sell anything. I'm asking him to decide what kind of man walks down this mountain. Because whoever holds those papers when the snow stops—that's who they become. Forever. There's no second ledger for that."

* * *

Elodie Eastwick went still.

Not the arithmetic stillness. Something else. I'd seen it once before, in the waystation, when Petra found the child's chalk drawing of the vault door—a round seal, a smiling sun with rays, drawn by a hand that had never seen the actual thing—and Elodie had stared at it for a long time and then walked outside and stood in the dark for an hour. I'd thought then that he was grieving strangers. Now I understood he'd been rehearsing.

He reached up slowly and lifted the satchel strap over his head.

Every gun on the mountain became very interested in him. Six Verrin men, four Finch men, Petra's thumb whitening on the detonator, Finch rising off his rock like a puppet pulled upright.

"Easy," Elodie said, to all of them, in the mild voice of a man discussing weather. "Nobody's going to do anything stupid in a snowpack. Finch said so, and Finch is the only honest person here."

"Eastwick," Renn said, and for the first time his voice had an edge on it, a real one. "Think carefully."

"I've thought about nothing else since the river gave me that chest." He held the satchel in both hands, in front of him, where everyone could see it. Snow landed on the canvas and didn't melt. "Sixty percent, Dov. Full disclosure. Freedom. Wealth. Redemption. Graves." He looked at each of them in turn, cataloguing, the way he catalogued storm cells. "You all quoted prices. Nobody asked what the goods actually cost."

"They cost nothing," Dov said. "Paper, Eastwick. It's just paper."

"Yeah," Elodie said. "That's what worries me."

Behind him, through the broken doorway of the vault, the wind shifted and began to moan across the opening—a low, resonant note, like breath across a bottle, like the mountain humming through a century of held air. The real storm was close now. I could see it eating the western ridgeline, coming for all of us with the indifference of a creditor.

Elodie looked at the satchel. Then at me. And he asked me a question without words, the way people do when they've stopped trusting language, and I answered it the same way, and I will never write down what passed between us, because some provisions are made in silence or not at all.

"Last chance for offers," he said aloud. "Anyone?"

The snow thickened. Nobody breathed.

"Good," said Elodie Eastwick, and reached into his coat pocket, and brought out something small, and struck it against his fly.
