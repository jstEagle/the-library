## 19. The Price Quoted

The snow came down like static, filling the vault mouth with a gray hiss, and in that hiss four men and one woman stood doing arithmetic with their lives.

Elodie Eastwick had the documents. That was the whole of it. Two ledgers' worth of triangulated truth wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, plus the folder from inside the vault—the photographs, the shipping manifests, the signed orders with the Kessavian royal cipher pressed into them like a brand. Proof that a nation had murdered itself and billed the corpse to its neighbor. He held the only complete set, and everyone on the escarpment knew it, and the knowledge sat between all of them like a drawn blade nobody wanted to be the first to touch.

The Verrin brothers had come up the mountain in matching parkas, red as warning signs, trailed by two men Elodie didn't recognize who carried themselves like furniture that could walk. Denny Verrin was smiling. Danny Verrin was also smiling. They were always smiling; it was the family trade.

"Eastwick." Denny spread his arms, taking in the vault mouth, the scree field, the falling sky. "Look at this. You found it. We always said you would—didn't we say he would, Danny?"

"We said it at the truck lot," Danny agreed. "We said it when the river took his truck. We never doubted."

"You totaled two vehicles," Denny said, warming to it, "and you still climbed a mountain. That's the kind of man we lend to. That's why we're here."

Elodie's hands were shaking. Not from cold—or not only from cold. Behind him, the vault exhaled its century of stale air into the storm. Petra stood at his left shoulder, soot-streaked from her charges, one hand nowhere near the knife she'd stopped carrying openly after chapter nine of this disaster. Maren Kolb stood at his right, her grandfather's name still hanging off her like wet wool since the blizzard confession. Finch stood apart, near the edge of the scree, a lean gray man with salvage-crew hands and the eyes of someone who'd read the second ledger's water-damaged pages and hadn't slept well since.

And Tomas was gone. Down the mountain with the injured, hours ago, before the accusations finished fracturing the expedition. Elodie tried not to think about whether he'd ever see him again.

"So," Denny said. "Let's talk settlement."

* * *

Here is what Elodie understood about the Verrin brothers, an understanding purchased over three years at ruinous interest: they never collected in money if they could collect in something better. Money spent. Money got taxed, traced, divided. What they loved was leverage—debt as architecture, a man's obligations arranged into load-bearing walls. The cheerful voice on the phone, the friendly terms, the thirty-day windows: none of it was kindness. It was inventory management. A debtor who hated you ran. A debtor who liked you stayed.

They had funded Harrow & Finch too. He'd known it since the advance man caught them at the waystation with supplies and a message about the second ledger copy. The brothers had bet both sides of the mountain, because both sides led to the same place: whatever came out of that vault, they'd own a piece of whoever carried it down.

"Sixty percent," Denny said now, holding up fingers like a man ordering pastries. "That was the original term for funding the expedition. But we're flexible. Sentimental, even. Here's our offer: you give us the documents—all of them—and we tear up every paper you owe us. Every signature. Three years of interest, the trucks, the flood loan, all of it. Gone. You walk down this mountain free, Eastwick. First man in history to bankrupt himself into solvency."

Danny laughed at his own brother's joke, warmly, the way they laughed at everything.

"And what do you do with them?" Elodie asked.

Denny's smile didn't move but something behind it did—a shutter adjusting. "That's not your department."

"It became my department on the summit," Elodie said. "You should see what's in this folder, Denny. There are governments that would burn countries for these pages. There are governments that would burn *us*."

"There are governments," Denny said pleasantly, "that already owe *us*. That's rather the point. Documents like these aren't weapons, Eastwick. They're collateral. We're not going to start a war. We're going to hold a very quiet note against people who can't afford for it to come due. It's the oldest business there is." He tilted his head. "It's your business, actually. You've been on the wrong end of it for years. Don't pretend you don't understand exactly how it works."

That was the worst part. He did.

* * *

Finch approached while the brothers conferred in low voices, their two walking-furniture men watching the perimeter out of habit or employment. The salvage man stopped an arm's length away, close enough to speak under the wind, far enough to make the gesture of harmlessness.

"Whatever number they quoted," Finch said, "I'll double it. Cash, not debt relief. Real money, wired anywhere you like, and passage off this rock tonight."

"I don't want money."

"Nobody wants money. Everybody takes it." Finch glanced back at the vault mouth, and Elodie watched him do it—the flick of the eyes, quick and involuntary, the look of a man checking where a dog was sleeping. "You've read what's in there. I read the damaged copy at the river camp. Enough of it. Do you know why I offered to buy you out at the waystation? Why I've been chasing you up this mountain instead of just taking it?"

"Enlighten me."

"Because I've moved cargo for governments before." Finch's voice stayed flat, professional, but underneath it Elodie heard the thing he'd smelled at the waystation: fear. "I know what happens to the last person who held something like this. They don't get rich. They get *archived*. Somewhere with no return address. Your creditors think they're buying a lever. They're buying a curse with a receipt attached. Whoever holds these papers becomes the answer to a question a dozen intelligence services have been asking for a hundred years. And the question is: *who do we blame?*"

"And you'd take them anyway."

"I'd sell them to the highest bidder and disappear before the ink dried," Finch said. "That's my talent. But you—" he looked at Elodie with something almost like pity, "—you'd have to live in the same country as your conscience afterward. So. Double their number. Or name any number. Because if you don't sell to me, you'll sell to them, and they'll own you even after they pay you. Debtors always are."

He walked back to his position at the scree's edge, and the snow filled his footprints behind him like the mountain erasing the negotiation.

* * *

Maren didn't offer money. She waited until the others were occupied—Denny checking a satellite phone, Danny sharing a thermos with one of the furniture men, an oddly domestic gesture that made Elodie's skin crawl worse than any threat—and then she stepped close, pulling her hood back so he could see her face.

"My grandfather signed the dissolution order," she said. For the hundredth time, but this time quietly, without the blizzard's desperation stripped around it. Just fact. "I told you that. What I didn't tell you is that I've spent eleven years in archives looking for proof of *what* he signed. Not whether he was guilty—I know he was guilty; the guilt is the only inheritance our family has ever acknowledged. I need to know what was hidden. Whether he knew. Whether he signed anyway." Her breath fogged between them. "Give me the documents. Not to sell. To publish. All of it, everything, the crime and the cover-up and the names. Let the truth out where it can't be owned by anyone—not the Verrins, not the bidders, not my family's ghost."

"And then what happens?"

"Then it's everyone's problem instead of one man's burden," Maren said. "That's the entire theory of transparency, Eastwick. It's naive and it's the only thing that's ever worked. If it belongs to everybody, no one can be archived for holding it. Including you."

"Including you," Elodie said. "Your name's going to be in those pages somewhere. Kolb. It has to be."

"Yes." She didn't flinch. "My family traded its name for safety a century ago. I'm offering to spend what's left of it on something true. That's more than anyone else on this mountain is offering to spend."

He looked at her—really looked, the way he'd learned to look at storms, reading structure, trying to see what the thing was going to do before it did it. She meant it. That was the terror of Maren Kolb: she always meant it.

But meaning it wasn't the same as surviving it, and Elodie had spent three years learning the difference between a good idea and a survivable one.

* * *

The wind shifted. Down in the valley, the weather they'd fled above the treeline was organizing itself into something larger, and everyone felt it—the way animals feel it, the way he'd spent his life feeling it, pressure dropping through some sense older than instruments. The eye of the storm was closing. Whatever happened next had to happen now, and everyone on the scree field knew it.

Denny Verrin turned from his phone call smiling wider than before. "Well. Everyone's made their offers. Debt relief from us. Cash from Finch. Righteousness from the archivist." He shrugged, expansive, generous. "What's it going to be, Eastwick? Name your price. We're reasonable men. Ask anyone."

Ask anyone. The men they'd ruined. The towns they'd bought. The dock informant who'd sold Elodie's route for a bottle and a pat on the head.

Elodie looked at the folder against his chest. Inside it: the engineered disaster. The blamed neighbor. The dead nation's last, worst secret, preserved in triplicate by bureaucrats who'd assumed someone would eventually want it proven. An inheritance of ash, addressed to an heir who'd never existed until a riverbank collapsed and handed a drowning man a poison chalice stamped with a beautiful seal.

He thought about Denny's word. *Collateral.* A quiet note against people who couldn't afford for it to come due. He thought about Finch's word. *Archived.* He thought about Maren's word. *Everybody's.*

Any sale made him complicit. That was the clarity, arriving at the worst possible moment, the way clarity always did—after the decision was already obvious and before there was any graceful way to act on it. Sell to the Verrins and every future extortion done with these pages had his fingerprints on the transaction. Sell to Finch and the pages vanished into the same darkness that had eaten Kessavia, feeding the machine that ate nations. Even giving them to Maren—even the righteous option—meant choosing what the world learned, becoming the editor of a massacre, the man who decided which dead got justice and how much.

There was no sale that kept his hands clean. So the question wasn't *who*.

The question was *whether*.

"Actually," Elodie said, and heard his own voice come out steady, which surprised him, "I'm going to counteroffer."

Denny's eyebrows rose, delighted. A negotiator! Their favorite kind of client!

"Name it," Danny said.

Elodie reached into his coat pocket—slowly, telegraphing every movement, aware of the furniture men shifting their weight—and brought out a small metal tin. His storm kit. Emergency rations, a whistle, a flare, and at the bottom, rattling loose, half a box of waterproof matches he'd carried through every chase for six years and never once needed.

He held the tin up.

"Matches," he said.

The silence lasted three full seconds. Snow ticked against nylon and stone.

"...Matches," Denny repeated.

"A fair price," Elodie said, "for goods I'm about to destroy anyway. Consider it payment for the entertainment. You've gotten three years of interest out of me, gentlemen. This is the last show, and admission is one box of matches."

Finch took a step forward, alarm finally cracking his professional calm. "Eastwick. Think about what you're—"

"I've thought about nothing else since the vault opened." Elodie pulled the oilcloth bundle from against his chest and held it where everyone could see it, the folder beneath, the whole poisoned inheritance of a murdered nation balanced in his gloved hands above the scree. "You want to buy a lever. You want to buy a curse. You want to publish a reckoning. And I want—" he looked at Maren, and softened, because he owed her that much honesty, "—I want to be sorry, Maren. But there's no version of handing this to anyone that doesn't make me part of what they do next. So I'm refusing the inheritance. On behalf of a country that doesn't exist anymore to refuse it. Somebody should have. A hundred years ago, somebody should have."

"Son," Denny Verrin said, and for the first time in three years the cheerfulness thinned enough to show the machinery underneath, "that folder is worth more than you will earn in ten lifetimes. You'd burn a fortune in front of five witnesses on a mountain?"

"No," Elodie said. "I'd burn a *poison* in front of five witnesses on a mountain. The fortune was never real. That's the first thing you learn about debt, Denny—you taught me, actually. The numbers are never the point. The point is what people will do to service them." He struck the match against the tin's rough edge. It flared, absurdly small, a coin of light in all that gray. "This is the point."

Behind him, the vault breathed its cold breath. Ahead of him, the storm lowered its shoulder. And in his hand, the flame bent toward the oilcloth, hungry, indifferent, ready—

Maren lunged. Finch shouted. Denny Verrin's smile finally, fully died.

And the first true snow of winter came down on the escarpment like a verdict.
