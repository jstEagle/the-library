## 20. First Snow

Petra Ilves had blown things up for a living long enough to know that the moment before the blast was always quieter than you expected. The fuse hissed. The world held its breath. And then everything you'd built your plan around was gone.

She was thinking about that now, standing in the vault doorway with snow starting to fall in fat, deliberate flakes, watching Elodie Eastwick hold a century of a dead nation's secrets over an open flame.

He'd asked for matches.

She should have known then. She'd been there in Chapter Nineteen's aftermath—no, she corrected herself, there was no chapter, there was just the last hour: the Verrin brothers' advance man with his cheerful gun, Finch with his generous offer, Maren with her grandfather's shame and her open hands. Everyone had quoted a price. Elodie had listened to all of them the way he listened to weather—with that tilted head, that half-smile, reading what was coming before it arrived.

Then he'd said, "I'll need matches," and Petra had felt something turn over in her chest like ice shifting on a river.

"Elodie." Maren's voice came out flat and careful, the voice of a woman who had spent her life handling documents and knew exactly what he was holding. "Think about what—"

"I have thought about it." He said it mildly. He always said terrible things mildly. "That's the whole problem."

The documents were bundled against his chest under his coat—the complete set, pulled from the vault's lead-lined case while the rest of them stood in the snow arguing about who deserved to carry it down the mountain. The Verrins wanted it. Finch wanted it. Somewhere below them, Tomas was descending with the injured and a radio he'd promised not to use until morning, which meant no one was coming to help and no one knew yet what they'd found.

Petra knew what was in the bundle because she'd read enough of it by lamplight to make her hands shake. Kessavia hadn't been destroyed by its neighbor. Kessavia had destroyed itself—engineered the dam failure, the flood, the famine—and let a hundred thousand of its own people die so the blame would land across the border and the ruling family could consolidate power in the chaos. It hadn't worked. The nation had dissolved anyway, erased from maps, its records burned. Except these records. Except the ones hidden here, in a vault carved into the escarpment by someone who'd wanted the truth preserved and cursed at the same time.

Whoever held this owned a weapon. That was the whole inheritance. Not gold. A weapon, and every enemy that came with it.

"You burn those," said the Verrin advance man—Dov, the younger one, the one who smiled more than his brothers and therefore frightened Petra more—"and we're going to have a very short conversation about what happens next."

"You've been having that conversation since the river," Elodie said. "I've heard it. It doesn't improve."

Snow landed on the open matchbox in his hand. He cupped it closer. His fingers weren't steady—Petra saw that, saw the fine tremor—but his voice was.

Maren moved first. Petra grabbed her arm. "Wait."

"Wait?" Maren's eyes were wild behind the frost on her lashes. "He's holding my family's only chance at—"

"He's holding everyone's only chance at nothing," Petra said. "Look at him."

And Maren looked. They both did. And what they saw was a man standing in the falling snow at the mouth of a hole in a mountain, holding the most dangerous paper in the hemisphere, looking—not afraid. Relieved. Like a man setting down something heavy after carrying it too far.

"Elodie," Maren said again, softer now. Pleading. "There has to be another way. Publish it. Give it to me. I'll spend my whole life—"

"That's exactly what I'm afraid of." He struck the match. The flame stood up straight in the windless cold, absurdly small. "You'd be brave with it, Maren. You'd be honest. And in six months some government would take it from you, or buy it, or you'd die on a train platform with it in your bag, and then it starts all over. Someone else inherits. Someone else pays. This thing has been waiting a hundred years for someone desperate enough to claim it. I was desperate enough. That was the trap. The ledger didn't promise me money. It promised me *meaning*. And I almost took it."

"It's proof," Maren whispered. "It's the truth."

"The truth is already out of the vault." He tapped his temple with the hand holding the match. "Four of us know. We can't unknow it. But paper travels. Paper gets sold. Paper outlives everyone's good intentions." He looked at each of them—Maren, Petra, Dov Verrin, Finch's man hunched by the scree—and his voice dropped into something almost gentle. "I'm refusing the inheritance. On behalf of a nation that doesn't exist anymore to refuse it. Somebody should have done this a hundred years ago. Nobody did. So I'm doing it now, and I'm doing it badly, and I'm sorry."

He touched the flame to the corner of the bundle.

* * *

Petra had spent eleven years around explosives. She knew fire intimately—how it breathed, how it spread, how fast. Even so, she wasn't ready for how fast.

The old Kessavian paper went up like it had been waiting, like the vault had dried it into tinder on purpose, a final lock made of flame. Orange light bloomed across the snow and across every face in the doorway, and for one full second nobody moved. Dov Verrin's smile finally fell off his face, and what was underneath it was ugly and young.

"No—" Maren lunged.

Elodie held the burning bundle out at arm's length, letting it consume itself, ash lifting on the thermal rising from the vault mouth like the mountain exhaling. "It's done, Maren. It's done."

"It is *not*—" Dov moved, hand going inside his coat, and Petra's body decided before her mind did.

She hit Dov at the waist. They went down together into the snow, and his elbow found her cheekbone and stars burst behind her eyes, and somewhere above her Finch's man was shouting and Maren was screaming Elodie's name and the wind chose that exact moment to arrive.

It came off the summit like a door opening—a wall of white, the blizzard's leading edge, the storm they'd watched building all day finally making up its mind. Visibility collapsed from a hundred meters to ten to three. The temperature fell through the floor. Petra felt it through her coat like a hand closing.

"Dov!" someone shouted from downslope—one of the Verrin porters, maybe, or the older brother's voice carried impossibly through the roar. "Dov, the ridge line's gone!"

Elodie was still standing in the doorway. Still holding the last flaming scrap, watching it die in his fingers, snow swirling around him, and Petra understood with horrible clarity that he wasn't going to move. That some part of him had decided the vault could keep him if it wanted. That the man who'd spent his whole life running toward storms had finally found one worth standing inside.

She got up off Dov—out cold, thank God, breathing—and grabbed Maren, and together they hit Elodie from both sides like a tackle drill.

He was heavier than he looked and completely unresisting, which was worse. "Let go," he said. "It's finished. Let it be finished."

"It's not finished until we're off this mountain," Petra snarled into his ear, hauling him backward away from the vault mouth as the wind screamed past. "You want to be a martyr, do it somewhere with shelter. Move your *legs*, Eastwick."

Something in the insult reached him. His legs started working. Maren had his other arm, and the three of them staggered down through the whiteout, following the fixed rope they'd run that morning, Petra counting knots by feel because sight was gone entirely. Behind them the vault mouth vanished into the storm. Ahead, nothing. Just rope and wind and the small animal sounds Maren was making—not crying, Petra thought, or trying not to.

They made the waystation in forty minutes that took a year off Petra's life. The old Kessavian shelter was barely standing, half its roof gone, but the stone walls broke the wind and their little stove still drew, and when Petra got the flame lit—matches again, matches saving lives instead of ending inheritances—the three of them crowded into the blue chemical light and shook.

For a long time nobody spoke. The storm hammered the walls. Somewhere out there were Dov and his men, and Finch's man, and God knew what was happening among them, but the mountain had swallowed everyone equally. That was the thing about weather. It didn't care whose side you'd been on.

"My grandfather signed the order," Maren said at last, to the stove, to no one. "Dissolving the nation. Erasing it. He thought he was mercy-killing something already rotten. Now I'll never know if he was right." Her hands were open in her lap, empty. "I climbed a mountain for paper."

"You climbed a mountain for the truth," Petra said. "You've still got it. It's just not portable."

Maren looked at her, and then at Elodie, slumped against the wall with frost melting in his beard and ash still ghosted on his fingertips. "Was it worth it? Any of it?"

Elodie was quiet long enough that Petra thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "I owed sixty thousand marks to men who collect in blood. This morning I thought the vault was how I paid them. Tonight I think—" He turned his hands over, studying the stains. "Tonight I think the debt was never money. The ledger was the debt. Somebody had to stop paying it forward."

Outside, the blizzard thickened, erasing the switchbacks, the scree field, the buried seal, the whole crooked staircase they'd bled to climb. By morning it would be as if the vault had never been opened. As if the escarpment had simply taken back what it had lent.

Petra fed the stove another stick and thought about her brother, still locked in his mining contract, still waiting for the money she'd sold their route to raise. That debt hadn't burned. Most debts didn't. But hers, at least, could still be worked off honestly, with her hands, in the ordinary slow arithmetic of years.

"We go down tomorrow," she said. "If it clears. All of us. Even the Verrins, if any of them are alive to walk."

"And then?" Maren asked.

Elodie smiled—the real one, the rare one, the one Petra had seen maybe four times since the river. "Then we find out what a man is worth when he owes nothing but the truth."

The stove ticked. The storm roared. And high above them, under meters of new snow, the vault door sealed itself shut for good—keeping, at last, exactly what it had always kept: nothing anyone could carry down.
