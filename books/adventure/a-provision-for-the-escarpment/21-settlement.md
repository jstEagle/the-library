## 21. Settlement

The Verrin brothers' office smelled of coffee and lemon polish, the way it always had, as if menace could be laundered out of a room by a good housekeeper. I sat in the same chair where they'd given me thirty days. The chair hadn't changed. I had.

Dess Verrin poured three cups without asking. His brother Renn sat behind the desk, hands folded on the blotter like a man at church. Between them, on the desk's scarred oak surface, sat the chest.

It looked smaller here than it had on the mountain. That was the trick of it — everything from up there shrank when you brought it down into the world of ledgers and interest rates and men who smiled while they counted your fingers. On the escarpment it had been an object of destiny. Here it was a box with a broken hasp and river mud still dried in its seams.

"You look terrible," Dess said cheerfully, handing me my cup. "Mountain agree with you?"

"About half."

Renn laughed. It was a good laugh, warm, genuine in the way that made it worse. "He's joking," he told his brother. "See, he's joking. That's the Elodie Eastwick we lent money to."

I drank the coffee. It was good coffee. They'd always had good coffee, good chairs, good manners. The Verrins had built their business on the principle that people would forgive almost anything if you never once raised your voice, and in eleven years of lending at rates that should have been criminal, no one had ever proven them wrong.

"So," Dess said, settling into the chair beside mine, close enough that our knees nearly touched. "Walk us through it."

"Through what?"

"The mountain." He spread his hands. "You went up with four people and came down with two and a half — Tomas's wrist doesn't count as a whole person anymore, poor bastard — and you come back carrying nothing but an empty chest and a story about how there was nothing inside worth taking." He smiled. "We funded an expedition, Elodie. We'd like the expedition report."

"There's not much to report. The vault was empty."

"Empty."

"Of anything valuable. There were documents. Paper. Old paper." I shrugged. "Water damage, mostly illegible. I burned what was left for warmth on the way down."

Renn's smile didn't move but something behind it did, some small recalculation, an abacus clicking over one bead. "Burned it."

"It was cold."

"It was *cold*," Dess repeated, delighted. "Did you hear that, Renn? He burned a century-old national archive because it was cold."

"I also burned it because whoever claimed those documents inherited Kessavia's crimes along with its assets, and I've got enough creditors." I looked at Renn. "No offense."

"None taken," Renn said. "We're very proud of our reputation."

Here is what I had learned on the mountain, in the whiteout, in the vault doorway with snow coming down like the sky settling its own accounts: the Verrin brothers did not lend money. That was the surface of what they did, the way a fishing lure is the surface of a fish. What they actually did was buy options on people. Every loan was a contract on a future version of you — the version that would be desperate enough, or brave enough, or stupid enough to do whatever needed doing when the note came due. They hadn't sent me up the mountain for sixty percent of an inheritance. They'd sent me up the mountain to find out what was up the mountain, using a man whose death would cost them nothing and whose survival might cost them everything they wanted to know.

And Harrow & Finch had been their second bet. And Petra's betrayal had probably been visible to them before it was visible to me. I had been a chess piece so thoroughly owned that both sides of the board were theirs.

But they didn't know about the seal.

* * *

Finch had found me first, two days after we came off the escarpment, in the river town of Lowmarsh where the expedition had dissolved into its component miseries. He'd bought me dinner — good steak, which I ate with the particular ferocity of a man who'd spent six weeks eating Petra's trail rations — and made his offer across the tablecloth with the weary patience of someone who'd explained this to simpler people.

"Whatever you brought down, I'll pay triple what the Verrins are owed. Cash. Tonight. You walk away clean, you keep your truck — the new truck, I'll throw that in too — and you never think about Kessavia again."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I go back to my clients and tell them the documents are in play, and things get less pleasant for everyone." He'd turned his glass slowly on the cloth. "Mr. Eastwick, I've read the fragments my people recovered from the man who died on the mountain forty years ago. I know what family kept those records and why. You don't want to be the last person holding them. Believe me. There are governments that would burn this whole valley to un-know what's in that vault."

"I burned them," I said.

He'd studied me for a long moment, the way you study a card player who just went all-in on a hand you can't read. Then he'd laughed — short, unhappy — and paid for dinner, and left, and I'd watched him go knowing he didn't believe me either, and knowing it didn't matter, because belief wasn't the currency here. Proof was. And there was no proof left in the world except what lived in my head, and I intended to take that to my grave with the door locked.

Now, in the Verrins' office, Renn opened the chest.

It was empty. Of course it was empty. I'd gone through it on the river, twice, making sure. The false bottom I'd pried loose outside Lowmarsh, alone, at night, with Petra standing watch not because she didn't trust me but because she understood why I needed her to.

"There's nothing here," Renn said.

"Nope."

"We funded—"

"You funded a salvage operation. Salvage law's clear: the salvor gets salvage value. Salvage value of an empty chest is an empty chest." I set down my cup. "But."

Both brothers went still. It was remarkable, watching it happen — two large cheerful men becoming, for one second, absolutely motionless, like herons deciding whether the water held anything worth striking.

"But," Dess prompted.

I reached into my coat pocket and set it on the desk between us.

The seal of Kessavia. Bronze, the size of a dinner plate, the crest worked in relief — the escarpment itself, stylized, with the sun rising behind it and the old motto ringing the rim in a language maybe four hundred people alive could still read. It had been bolted to the inside of the chest's lid, hidden under a leather facing, and whoever had packed the ledger a century ago had either not known it was there or had known exactly, and had meant it as a message for whoever came after: *this nation existed. This nation was real. Prove otherwise.*

To a government archivist it was evidence. To a historian it was a relic. But to collectors — and I had spent three evenings in Lowmarsh's one decent hotel lobby making phone calls, and Maren had spent three evenings confirming what the calls turned up — to collectors of erased nations, of dead flags and defunct currencies and the ephemera of countries that had been not conquered but *edited*, it was the crown jewel. The last surviving great seal of Kessavia. Maren's appraisers had used words like *irreplaceable* and *auction record* and, once, quietly, *obscene*.

"That's—" Dess started.

"The great seal," I said. "Bronze, original, provenance documented well enough to satisfy anyone who isn't looking for a reason to doubt it. There are three collectors who've made written offers. One of them is in Zurich. One of them I'm not going to name because his lawyers asked me not to. And one of them is a museum foundation that will make the sale public, which means everyone will know the Verrin brothers settled a debt with the seal of a murdered country, which — and I mean this respectfully — is exactly the kind of story you two enjoy being in."

Silence. The coffee pot ticked as it cooled.

Renn picked up the seal. He turned it over in his big careful hands, reading the motto, tracing the escarpment with one finger, and I watched him do the arithmetic — because the Verrins were, above all else, arithmeticians, cheerful ones, and the numbers here were very good indeed. Better than good. My debt, with the creative interest they applied, came to a figure that had haunted me for a year. The seal's low estimate was four times that.

"You're overpaying," Renn said finally.

"I'm buying my life back," I said. "That's not the same market."

Dess laughed — the real laugh, the one that came up from somewhere honest — and clapped me on the shoulder hard enough to rock me in the chair. "Oh, I've missed him. Renn. Have I mentioned I've missed him?"

"Every day since he defaulted," Renn said dryly. He set the seal down, aligned it precisely with the edge of the blotter, and pulled a sheet of paper toward him. "A settlement in kind. Full satisfaction of principal and interest. No further claims by either party." His pen hovered. "You understand that if this thing turns out to be a forgery, the debt revives. With penalties."

"It's not a forgery."

"How do you know?"

"Because a forger would have made it beautiful. This thing's ugly. It's got repair marks, it's been dropped, somebody patched a crack in the rim with lead a hundred years ago. Real objects get damaged. Forgeries stay perfect." I shrugged. "Ask your appraiser. Ask three of them. I'll wait."

They did ask. It took nine days, during which I slept fourteen hours a night in a rented room above a chandlery and ate breakfasts that involved eggs, plural, and slowly stopped flinching at the sound of wind. On the ninth day I came back to the office and the papers were waiting, signed, and Dess shook my hand with both of his, and Renn slid the settlement copy across the desk, and that was it.

Eleven years. One signature. An empty chest and a dead nation's name.

"Pleasure doing business," Dess said at the door, and meant it, and that was the most frightening sentence I'd heard in weeks.

* * *

Maren published in the spring.

Not the documents — those existed now only in the memories of five people, one of whom was Finch, and Finch had retreated so completely after the mountain that his firm's offices in the capital listed a new tenant by February. Not the vault's contents, not the proof of what Kessavia's ruling family had done, not the names or the mechanisms or the machinery of blame that had drowned a nation and nearly drowned the four of us. She'd seen what publishing that would do. Governments would move. Descendants would litigate. Two nations that had built a century of uneasy peace on the official version of events would find the foundation cracked, and Maren Kolb, who had spent her life wanting to redeem her grandfather's name, had stood in the blizzard above the treeline and chosen the harder redemption: the truth about herself, told privately; the truth about Kessavia, buried with the mountain.

What she published was the chalk.

Forty-one pages, photographed in situ before the weather took the waystation walls for good. The messages refugees had scrawled a hundred years ago while climbing the same switchbacks we'd climbed, waiting out the same storms, leaving their names and their destinations and their griefs on the plaster for whoever came next. *Anna Weissl, going to her sister in Corvane, if God wills.* *The mill is lost. The children are well.* *Whoever reads this, we were here. We were a country once. Remember us kindly.*

She'd titled the book simply *The Waystation Walls*, and she'd dedicated it *to the heirs, wherever they are*, and when I wrote to ask her whether she regretted holding back the rest, she answered with a single line I've thought about more than any sentence I've ever read: *History without ammunition is the only history that lets people sleep.*

Petra went north. Her brother's mining contract had been voided in the chaos — the company that held it turned out to have been one of Finch's subsidiary interests, and when Finch folded his tents, the contract folded with him — so her confession on the mountain had, in the end, purchased exactly what she'd sold our route to buy. She'd shaken my hand at the Lowmarsh dock and said, "You're the only man I ever betrayed who ended up glad of it," and I'd said, "Don't make a habit of me," and she'd laughed and boarded the northbound barge and I haven't seen her since, though she sends postcards from places with unpronounceable names.

Tomas's wrist healed crooked. He guides again, out of the eastern valleys, and charges extra for parties who mention treasure.

* * *

I bought a used truck with the small advance Maren insisted on paying me for consulting on the photographs — I'd been there, I could confirm the walls were real, and she wanted one witness who wasn't an academic. The truck is nothing special. It doesn't chase tornadoes so much as follow them at a respectful distance, the way you'd follow a dangerous dog you'd once loved.

People ask, sometimes, at fuel stops, at the diners where chasers trade radar screenshots like baseball cards, what happened to me. Where I went for a season. Why I came back thinner and quieter and paying cash for everything. I tell them I took a job in the mountains. It's even true.

Some nights, parked on a ridge with a supercell building green-black on the horizon, I take out the stub of chalk I keep in the map pocket — the one I picked up off the waystation floor without thinking, the way you pick up a shell from a beach — and I hold it, and I don't write anything anywhere, because there's no wall that needs it. But I hold it anyway. It's the closest thing I own to a receipt.

Because here's the provision I made, the one nobody drew up and nobody countersigned: I owe nothing to the Verrins, nothing to Finch, nothing to any bank or brother or board. But I owe the truth to the mountain, and the mountain collects forever, and I pay gladly, every day, by keeping my mouth shut and remembering.

The storm ahead rotates, tightens, drops a funnel into a wheat field where it has no business being, and I start the truck and drive toward it, because that's the work, and the work is simple, and simple is what I traded everything for.

Behind me, in the mirror, the road I came up is already gone.
