## 1. The River Keeps What It Is Owed

The Serein had lost eleven feet since Bram Vane arrived, and it intended to lose more before the season was done.

He could read its retreat the way other men read weather — in the mud lines climbing the pilings like tide marks on a bathtub, in the smell of exposed silt that was half river and half something older, in the way the dredge barge *Mule* sat lower every morning against her mooring lines as if she were embarrassed to be seen floating on so little water. The mining concern that had hired him called it "seasonal drawdown" in their paperwork. The deckhands called it the river dying. Both were correct enough.

Bram called it work, which was his word for everything, and had been for five years now.

"Vane." Pellit, the shift foreman, came down the catwalk with his hard hat pushed back and his face arranged into the expression of a man about to deliver bad news he'd rehearsed twice. "Company wants another pass at the north channel before they pull us out."

"The north channel's picked clean. I've been through it four times."

"They want a fifth."

"They want a fifth because the assay office is bored," Bram said, "and because I bill by the pass." He said it without heat. It was true, and both of them knew it, and there was a certain comfort between men who could say true things to each other at six in the morning over bad coffee.

Pellit grinned. "You're a cynical man, Vane."

"I'm an honest one. There's a difference, but it's mostly spelling." He drained the coffee, made a face at it — the concern bought coffee by the ton and treated it accordingly — and pulled on his gloves. "Fine. Fifth pass. But when we come up with nothing but river clay and old boots, I'm invoicing them for the boots too."

The *Mule*'s dredge was a claw on a chain, essentially — a great iron mouth on a lattice boom that bit into the riverbed and vomited silt onto the sorting screen, where men with rakes and patience looked for ore color in the gray slurry. It was not romantic work. Bram had done romantic work once, or near enough — survey diving off the Amber Coast, a season cataloguing a drowned temple with a team who quoted poetry at each other over dinner. He'd left before the second season. He didn't talk about why, and after five years nobody asked, which suited him fine. Dredging didn't ask questions. Dredging just wanted your hands steady and your back willing, and in exchange it let you stop thinking, which was the entire point of it as far as Bram was concerned.

He ran the winch himself. He always did. Foremen learned quickly that Vane would take any job on the barge except the one where he stood around watching somebody else do it.

The morning went the way mornings went: bite, lift, dump, sort. Clay, gravel, a length of rope gone to black leather, half a wagon wheel, the promised boot. By midday the sun had cooked the mudflats into something like bread crust, and the crew had worked up the loose, profane cheer of men who knew the job was nearly over. Two more passes and the concern would tow the *Mule* downriver to winter moorings, and Bram would take his pay and move on, because moving on was what he did. He'd dug for hire across three continents and had never once stayed long enough to learn whether a town's second summer was better than its first.

It was on the sixth bite of the afternoon that the claw came up heavy.

Bram felt it before the gauge told him — a drag in the cable, a wrongness in the rhythm, the winch motor dropping a note like a singer hitting a step they hadn't expected. He eased the throttle back and brought the load up slow, and the whole barge leaned toward the boom as if leaning in to look.

"That's no clay weight," Pellit said, coming up beside him.

"No," Bram agreed. "That's a box weight."

The claw broke the surface trailing water in ropes, and hanging half-spilled from its jaws was a chest.

Not a crate. Not a drum. A chest — iron-banded, oak beneath the iron though the oak had gone the color of drowned things, and stamped on every visible face with bronze plates gone green-black with forty years of river. The largest plate sat on the lid, and even caked in silt, even furred with weed, the shape of it was unmistakable: a seal. A circle, a bridge of seven arches spanning a stylized current of water, and above the bridge a star.

Bram stopped moving.

This requires some context, because Bram Vane did not stop moving. In five years he had been washed out of two camps by flood, walked out of one by war, and talked out of a third by a woman who'd turned out to be married; in all that time he had never once stood still when there was somewhere else to be. Restlessness wasn't a habit with him. It was a load-bearing wall. He moved the way rivers moved — not from choice but from structure.

But he stood there, gloved hand still on the winch lever, and looked at the seal, and did not move.

"Vane?" Pellit said. "What is it?"

"You know what that is?"

"A chest."

"I know what the seal is." His voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's voice played back to him. "Seven-arched bridge. Star over the water. That's Vessia."

The name landed on the deck of the *Mule* the way names like that land — nobody spoke for a moment, and the ones who were young enough shifted uncomfortably, because Vessia was not a country anymore and had not been for forty years, and there were places where saying the name aloud was less like history and more like swearing in church.

Vessia had been erased. Not conquered exactly — conquered countries got to keep their names on maps as provinces, as territories, as footnotes. Vessia had been dissolved. A treaty signed by three governments and none of its own people had unmade it in a single sitting: borders redrawn, records seized, the language struck from official use, the very name removed from atlases with the brisk efficiency of a censor's pen. Forty years on, you could stand in the middle of where Vessia had been and find no marker, no monument, nothing but new place-names painted over the old ones like a lie told often enough to become signage.

And here was its seal, dredged up out of the Serein, green with river years, staring up at Bram Vane from the sorting screen like a face at a window.

"It's probably nothing," Pellit offered, in the tone of a man hoping to be reassured. "Government stores, maybe. War surplus. They shipped everything in sealed chests back then."

"Maybe." Bram knelt beside it. Up close the bronze plates showed tooling under the grime — fine work, deliberate work, the kind of stamping that came from a mint or a chancery rather than an army quartermaster. And along the lid's edge, half worn away, a line of lettering in a script that hadn't officially existed in four decades. He brushed mud from it with his thumb. The letters were Vessian. He couldn't read them — almost nobody alive could, outside of grandmothers and smugglers — but he recognized the shapes the way you recognize a song from another room.

"Company property," Pellit said, without conviction. Salvage law on the Serein was a tangle of charters and customs older than either government that claimed the river, but the mining concern's contract was simple: anything of value the dredge brought up belonged to the concern, minus Bram's percentage, which was small.

"Sure," Bram said. "Company property. A Vessian state seal on company property. On a river that never touched Vessia." He straightened. "Where's the nearest map that shows where Vessia used to be, Pellit?"

"There isn't one. That's rather the point of the whole business, isn't it?"

"Exactly." Something was happening in Bram's chest that he distrusted intensely. It felt like curiosity, but bigger than curiosity — curiosity the way a spark is bigger than a match head. Five years of channel after channel, contract after contract, town after town, and he had kept the engine of himself running precisely so that nothing could catch. Now something had caught, and it was in the shape of a dead nation's stamp, and he found that his hands wanted to open this chest more than they had wanted anything in years.

They opened it the careful way, because Bram was a professional and professionals don't crowbar things that have waited forty years underwater. Pry bar at the hasp, cloth padding under the iron, Pellit and two deckhands hauling while Bram guided. The lid groaned, shed a curtain of black water, and came free.

Inside: silt, of course. Waterlogged straw gone to dark jelly. And beneath it, shapes.

A lead case, soldered shut, the kind used for documents meant to survive. A bundle wrapped in oilcloth and tarred canvas, tied with wire. A smaller box, iron-bound, fitted inside the larger one with a precision that suggested the larger chest had been built around it. And wedged in the corner, wrapped in waxed leather, a journal — a real paper journal, bound in boards, and when Bram lifted it with both hands as gently as if it were a sleeping animal, the cover fell open to the first page and the handwriting hit him like cold water.

He knew that handwriting.

He had grown up under that handwriting. On shopping lists and margin notes and the flyleaves of books he'd been given and then watched being packed away. Tall, narrow letters, forward-leaning, impatient — the hand of a man who wrote fast because he thought faster and resented the ink for keeping pace.

Elias Vane. His father.

Bram had not spoken his father's name aloud in five years. He had not spoken to the man at all in eight, and had built those five years out of that eight with the diligence of a mason. Elias Vane was the famous archaeologist, the celebrated one, the man whose name opened doors Bram preferred to keep closed — doors to institutions, to funding, to a whole world of people who said *oh, you're Elias Vane's son* in voices that made Bram want to dig holes in places with no forwarding address. He'd dropped the surname from his working papers, gone by initials, by *B. Vane, salvage and dredging*, and told himself it was practicality. It was exile, and he'd furnished it comfortably.

And now his father's handwriting was dripping river water onto the deck of the *Mule*, forty miles from anywhere Elias Vane had any business being.

"Vane." Pellit's voice, careful. "You've gone white as the screen. You know whose book that is?"

"My father's," Bram said. The words cost something. He paid anyway. "My father wrote this."

The deck went quiet around him. Even the dredge seemed to idle softer.

The journal's pages were fused with damp, and he didn't dare force them — not here, not with twenty pairs of eyes and a company contract that said everything in this chest belonged to men who filed paperwork. He closed it. Wrapped it back in its waxed leather. Stood there a moment with his hands flat on the chest's rim, feeling the iron bands cold through his gloves, feeling the whole weight of the thing — wood, water, years — and underneath that weight, faintly, like a pulse, the sense that he was holding a door that had been waiting a long time for someone to knock.

"What's in the rest of it?" Pellit asked quietly.

"Documents. Records, maybe. State records, if that seal means what I think it means." Bram looked up at the sky, doing arithmetic he didn't want to do. "The concern will want this logged, assessed, and claimed by nightfall. Which gives me until nightfall."

"Until nightfall for what?"

"For me to decide what kind of man I am." He almost smiled. "Or at least what kind of thief."

Pellit laughed, then saw Bram wasn't joking, and stopped.

Here is what Bram did next, and he did it without letting himself think, because thinking was a luxury for men whose fathers were strangers: he took the journal. Slid it inside his coat, against his ribs, where the warmth of him could start undoing forty years of river. The chest itself he left — let the company log it, weigh it, argue over it. Let them have their bronze-stamped box and their salvage percentages. But the handwriting was his. Whatever else the treaty governments and the mining concerns and the dead might claim, a man's hand was his own, and Bram Vane had spent five years refusing his father's name and discovered, standing knee-deep in the Serein's last honest water, that he could not refuse his father's ink.

He worked the rest of the shift in silence. He ate supper in silence. And when full dark came down and the camp lamps swung on their wires, he took a lamp of his own and walked out past the drying racks to the mudflats, where the river lay shrunken and gleaming under the stars, and sat down on an old piling with the journal in his lap.

For the first time in five years, Bram Vane stopped moving.

He sat while the moon came up. He sat while the night birds started their arguments in the reeds. He peeled the journal's pages apart one at a time, patient as a surgeon, and read by lamplight what his father had written in a hand that leaned forward like a man walking into wind.

Survey notes. Soundings. Coordinates for a stretch of the Serein that Bram knew like his own palm — and marked, in his father's precise, impatient script, with a single underlined word:

*Meridian.*

And below it, dated eleven days before the date Bram had learned, three years late and by accident, of his father's death:

*If anyone finds this, the wreck is real. So is everything they sank with it. Come at low water, and come before the dry season takes the river — or don't come at all.*

Bram looked up at the black ribbon of the Serein, running low and getting lower, and understood that he had just stopped moving for the first time in five years only to discover that he had, in fact, six tides to get somewhere.

He needed a hydrographer. He needed one specific hydrographer, and she was going to be insufferable about it.

"Damn you, old man," he said softly, to the journal, to the river, to the star over the seven-arched bridge. It was the first thing he'd said to his father in eight years, and it was, he suspected, exactly how their conversations had always gone.

Then he stood up, tucked the journal inside his coat, and started walking toward the lights of town — moving again, finally, but for the first time in five years, toward something instead of away.
