## 2. Six Tides

Odile Sarn lived in a house that leaned.

Not dramatically — it wasn't the kind of lean that made insurance men write letters. It leaned the way an old sailor leans against a rail: comfortably, permanently, with the patience of something that had decided long ago where it meant to stand and saw no reason to stand anywhere else. The whole structure canted a few degrees toward the river, as if it too were listening for water.

Bram had been coming here since he was nineteen and knew nothing, and he was thirty-four now and knew slightly more, so he didn't knock. He let himself through the gate with its hand-painted sign — TIDE TABLES DRAWN WHILE YOU WAIT, NO REFUNDS — and found her on the back porch, bent over a drafting table the size of a coffin lid, arguing with a river gauge.

"You're wrong," she said, without looking up.

"Good morning to you too."

"The gauge at Kiln Bend is reading high. It's always reading high. Some idiot tied a goat to it in 1988 and the anchor plate never sat right again." She tapped her pencil twice against the paper, a small percussive punctuation mark, then looked up. Her eyes were the pale gray of old chart paper, and they went straight past his face to the canvas duffel slung over his shoulder, because Odile Sarn had never in her life met a bag she didn't want to inventory. "You look like a man carrying something he shouldn't have."

"I look like a man who's been walking since dawn."

"You look like both. Sit down. There's coffee, if you don't mind it tasting like punishment."

He sat. The porch boards gave their familiar complaint. From here you could see the whole gray-green width of the Serein River doing what it did every morning of the world: shrinking. It was early in the dry season still, but the mud banks were already showing their ribs, long pale stripes of exposed silt like the marks on a prisoner's wall, counting down the days.

Bram set the duffel between his boots and didn't open it. Not yet. First he wanted to hear himself say it out loud, in front of the only person whose opinion could make or unmake him.

"I pulled a chest out of the river yesterday."

Odile's pencil stopped.

"A bronze-stamped chest," he went on, "with the seal of Vessia on it. Out of the contracting channel below Harrow Shoals. And inside—" He stopped, because inside was not a thing you summarized. Inside was coin-stamps and seal-matrixes and a civil document bearing a treasury countersignature that hadn't legally existed in forty years, and inside, folded into a oilcloth sleeve like a letter nobody had ever meant him to find, was a survey journal in handwriting he'd spent half his life trying to forget.

"Vessia," Odile said. She said it the way you'd say the name of a dead friend. Then she stood up, crossed the porch in three strides, and held out her hand. "Show me."

* * *

She handled the chest the way other people handled infants and unexploded ordnance, which is to say with total attention and no sentiment whatsoever. She turned it under the light. She read the stamp. She opened it — the outer lock had surrendered to Bram's pry bar with almost insulting ease; it was the inner chamber, a sealed bronze coffer nested in the heart of the thing, that kept its own counsel — and she went through the contents piece by piece while Bram drank coffee that did, indeed, taste like punishment, and watched her face do things faces weren't supposed to do.

When she got to the journal, she stopped.

"This is a hydrographic survey," she said quietly. "Soundings. Current tables. Bottom composition by station." She looked up. "This is *good* survey work. This is better than good. Who—"

She read the signature block. Bram watched her get there. He watched the exact moment she understood, because Odile had a face built for hiding things and it betrayed her anyway, one muscle at a time.

"Elias Vane," she said.

"He signs his work now," Bram said. "Forty years too late for me to care."

"That's a lie," Odile said, not unkindly, "and we'll come back to it. But first—" and this was the thing about her, this was why he'd walked eleven miles before sunup instead of going to any of the four harbormasters or two lawyers who would have taken his money and given him nothing — "first we're going to find out how much time you have. Because I can tell you already, from the color of that mud line on your chest, that you don't have much."

* * *

What followed was three hours of the most beautiful arithmetic Bram had ever seen, and he included in that assessment every card trick and every rigged dice game he'd ever watched run money across three continents.

Odile worked the way water worked: patiently, along the path of least resistance, wearing down whatever opposed her. She laid out her charts — twenty years of them, the Serein basin drawn and redrawn in her own hand, each dry season recorded, each flood year noted in margins gone soft with handling. She cross-referenced the gauge readings against rainfall records from three stations upstream. She consulted a battered almanac with a broken spine and muttered at it like a colleague. She sent Bram out to the shed twice, once for the brass dividers and once for a bottle of plum brandy that she said was for medicinal purposes and then medicinally drank half of.

And slowly, on a fresh sheet of vellum, the river's future took shape.

"Here's your wreck," she said at last, tapping a point below Harrow Shoals where her soundings showed a deep scour hole hugging the old channel wall. "The Meridian went down in the flood year — your father's journal has the position to within a boat-length, which frankly offends me, because I've surveyed that reach nine times and never flagged it. She settled into the scour, deep enough that forty years of dredging crews sailed right over her heads. Lucky ship."

"She's not lucky yet."

"No. But here's the luck you do have." Odile drew a line along the channel profile. "The dry season drops the Serein about a meter and a half over the next six weeks. That scour hole stays wet — it's fed by springs, see, here and here — but everything around it goes to mud. Which means for a short window, your wreck isn't a diving job. It's a digging job. You can walk to her. You can work her with ropes and buckets and strong backs instead of pumps and bells. For a salvage crew, that window is a gift from God."

Bram felt the hope rise in him like the river in spring, and hated himself for it, because Odile's face hadn't changed and Odile's face always changed when there was bad news coming. She delivered bad news the way she drew coastlines: accurately, and with visible regret about the rocks.

"But," she said.

"There it is."

"But the same drop that exposes her also strands her. Look." The pencil moved. "Six weeks from now, the approach channel above the shoals goes to eighteen centimeters. Less. You couldn't float a coracle over it. Anything you pull out after that point has to go out overland, through country with no roads worth the name, and anything left *in* the wreck gets buried in silt until next flood year — and next flood year, my friend, the dredging contracts start again, and some mining concern's dragline takes her apart for ballast stone without ever knowing what she was."

"So there's a window."

"There's a window." Odile put down the pencil. She laced her fingers together and looked at him across the drafting table, and Bram braced himself, because he knew that look. It was the look she'd given him at nineteen, when he'd told her he wanted to learn hydrography and she'd said fine, but first you'll learn that the river does not care about you, and neither do I until you prove you care about it.

"Six tides," she said. "Spring tides back up the estuary this time of year — weak ones, but real. Each one pushes usable water up past the shoals for a few hours. High enough to bring a barge in light, work the site, and get out. Six of them between now and the closure. After the sixth, you're done. Whatever's still down there belongs to the mud until the river changes its mind, and the river" — she gestured at the view, the shrinking gray water, the patient exposed ribs of bank — "has never once changed its mind for anybody."

Bram looked at the sheet of vellum. Six neat tide symbols marched down the margin in Odile's small precise hand, each one annotated with a date, a height, and a window of hours. They looked like a row of headstones. They looked like a staircase.

"Six tides," he said.

"To reach her, raise what matters, and clear the shoals on the way out. And that assumes weather, and that assumes nobody contests you, and Bram—" she paused, and chose her words with the care she chose soundings, "—a find like this doesn't stay quiet. Chests with national seals on them are like corpses with jewels on them. Sooner or later somebody comes asking who's been grave-robbing, and they won't ask nicely, and they will have more boats than you."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I've watched you walk away from every hard thing in your life that had a name attached to it. You dug your way across three continents rather than be Elias Vane's son. Now his handwriting is sitting on my table and you're about to need everything he knew, and I want to know, before I commit one hour of my remaining eyesight to this — are you in? All the way in? Because six tides doesn't leave room for a man to change his mind halfway."

The porch boards ticked in the heat. Down on the river, a kingfisher hit the water and came up with nothing, and tried again, because that was the whole of the arrangement between birds and rivers.

Bram looked at the journal. His father's hand. He remembered that hand from a hundred childhood suppers — the pen moving, always moving, the man more present to his ink than to his son. He had spent five years making sure he never signed anything that connected them. No joint credit. No inherited commissions. Dug under other men's names, took pay under initials, let people assume Vane was a coincidence.

And now the name was the map. The name was the only map.

"I'm in," he said, and heard, to his surprise, that it came out steady. "All the way. Draw me your tides."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon. By the time the light went amber, the plan had bones.

Tide one: reconnaissance. Confirm the wreck, confirm the hold, place markers. Tide two and three: open the hold and shift the light cargo — documents first, Odile insisted, over Bram's instinct for the coin. "Paper burns, paper rots, paper is the only thing in that hold that can't wait," she said, and Bram thought of the registry fragment he'd found in the chest and didn't argue. Tides four and five: the heavy lift, the treasury, run out on a barge at high water. Tide six: reserve. "Always keep a tide in your pocket," Odile said. "A salvage man without a reserve tide is a gambler who's bet the house. And I've seen your house, Bram. It's a bedroll."

"It's a very good bedroll."

"It's a disgrace. Speaking of which, you'll sleep here tonight, the lean-to's dry, and tomorrow you'll need a crew, and you can't pay one, which means you'll need fools, and I unfortunately know where all the local fools berth."

"Tack Merrow," Bram said. "Still running the Narrows?"

"Still running everything. If it fits in a boat, Tack has smuggled it, and if it doesn't fit, he's smuggled it in pieces." She began rolling the vellum, careful, deliberate, and then paused with her hands flat on the chart. "There's one more thing. Before you recruit anyone, before you spend a single one of your six tides — you should understand who else can count."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning the Serein Company gauges the river at Kiln Bend, and the Serein Company reports to the treaty government, and the treaty government erased Vessia so thoroughly that they struck the word from the school atlases. A nation doesn't get erased by accident, Bram. It gets erased on purpose, by people who check their work." She fixed him with those chart-gray eyes. "Somewhere downstream there is a filing cabinet with your wreck in it, and a man whose job it is to notice when the file moves. The day that chest surfaced, the clock started twice. Once for the tide." She rolled the chart closed and tied it with string. "And once for them."

Bram walked out onto the leaning porch and looked downriver, toward the shoals, toward the drowned ship, toward the horizon he'd spent his whole life treating as an exit.

The water was low and getting lower, and somewhere beneath it lay the proof that his father had spent his last silent years doing the one thing Bram had never let himself do: caring about something enough to sign it.

Six tides. He counted them the way Odile had written them, one through six, and found that dread and resolve, in the end, felt almost identical in the chest — the same tightness, the same heat. Only the direction differed.

Behind him, Odile called through the door: "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we go find your fools."

Downstream, far past the bend where the river slipped out of sight, a lamp was burning in a harbor office, and a clerk was comparing gauge readings against a shipping manifest, and frowning.
