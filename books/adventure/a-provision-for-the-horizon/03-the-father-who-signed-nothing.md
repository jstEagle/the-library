## 3. The Father Who Signed Nothing

Bram did not open the chest again that night. He told himself it was prudence — a man alone in a dredging camp with a sealed national treasury had no business poking at locks by lamplight — but the truth was simpler and less flattering. He was afraid of what was inside.

Not the gold. Gold he understood. Gold had rules, weights, prices, buyers. You could hold gold and feel nothing but the arithmetic of it. It was the other things he feared: paper, ink, handwriting. Things made by people.

He slept badly on his cot in the survey shack, the chest three feet away under an oilcloth, and dreamed of rivers that kept changing their minds about which way they flowed.

* * *

In the morning he made coffee over a spirit stove and laid the chest's contents out on his drafting table like a surgeon laying out instruments — or evidence. The bronze seal of Vessia, a mountain cradling a star, stared up at him from the lid. Forty years erased from every map, and here was its stamp, bright as the day some clerk had pressed it into wax.

The coins came first because they were easiest. He counted them without counting them, hands moving on habit while his mind drifted. Vessian crowns, minted in a city called Vel Sarnen that no longer appeared on any chart sold south of the treaty line. Then ingots, then plate — candlesticks, a christening cup, the small domestic silver of a government that had packed its soul into a ship when the treaties were signed and never gotten it back.

And then, wedged beneath a tray of coin in a way that suggested someone had put it there deliberately, hurriedly, a book.

Oilskin-wrapped. Survey-sized. The spine cracked in the particular way of a notebook carried daily for years.

Bram knew before he opened it. Some part of him knew from the weight of it, from the way the oilskin was tied with the double hitch his father used for everything — mooring lines, bootlaces, the knot of a man who trusted rope more than people.

He untied it anyway.

The handwriting hit him like cold water. *Elias Vane*, in letters small and mercilessly precise, each numeral drafted as if it would be audited by God. His father had written grocery lists this way. Bram had once found a note in the old man's kitchen reading *milk, 2; bread, 1; tell B. the river is not his enemy, only his employer* — and had thrown it in the stove, and had thought about it for five years.

The journal began:

*Day one. The Meridian went down where the charts say she didn't. I have spent eleven months proving the river lies, and I am pleased to report the river is a better historian than the men who drew her maps.*

Bram sat down slowly.

Page after page. Soundings. Star shots. Cross-bearings taken from church spires and dead trees and a chimney that "will fall within two winters, so let this entry stand as its monument." His father had spent — how long? Years — running the river in secret, triangulating the true position of the wreck of the Meridian, the salvage ship that had carried Vessia's treasury and civil registry out of the country ahead of the treaty commissioners and had conveniently sunk within sight of the border, ending all questions.

Conveniently. Bram turned pages faster. Elias Vane did not use the word *conveniently* lightly.

*The wreck lies in the old channel, forty feet deeper than the modern chart admits. The river has been burying her like a cat buries a crime. Whoever finds her will find not merely gold but the registry. I am certain of it now. They did not sink a treasury. They sank the census.*

There was a foldout map bound into the back — hand-drafted, gorgeous, the kind of work that made Bram's chest hurt with an admiration he resented like a debt. Every reach of the river annotated. Every sandbar dated. And in the corner, in his father's smallest script, a single line:

*Six tides of exposure in a dry year. Plan accordingly.*

* * *

"You look," said Odile Sarn, arriving at noon with a crate of instruments and no invitation, "like a man who has been arguing with a ghost."

"I don't argue with ghosts. Ghosts win."

"Then you've been losing to one." She set down the crate, saw the journal, and stopped moving entirely, which for Odile was the equivalent of shouting. "Is that—"

"It's his hand."

She crossed the shack and looked over his shoulder for a long moment. Odile had taught Bram hydrography back when he was nineteen and furious at everything except water. She had known Elias too — had, Bram now remembered with a strange lurch, exchanged letters with him for years, letters Bram had assumed were about tide tables.

"He sent me soundings," she said quietly. "Every spring. No return address. I thought he was being careful." Her mouth twisted. "I thought he was being *paranoid*. Turns out those are different professions."

"Odile." Bram kept his voice level. "How long has he been dead?"

She looked at him the way she looked at a gauge reading that contradicted every other gauge. "You didn't know?"

"How would I know? We hadn't spoken since—" Since the fight. Since Bram had walked off a dig in Corvane territory rather than work under the Vane name, and his father had said, in that maddening even tone, *Very well. Sign your own name to your own work, then,* and Bram had said the thing he could never take back, and neither of them had written. Five years of silence, maintained with the discipline of two stubborn professionals.

"A year ago this month," Odile said. "Heart, they said. At his house on the Reach. There was a notice in the surveyors' gazette. Small one. He'd have hated a large one."

Bram heard himself say, "I don't read the gazette anymore," and heard how thin it sounded, and hated it.

"No," Odile said gently. "I imagine you don't."

* * *

Here is what Bram remembered, though he would not have called it memory — memory implied he'd kept it somewhere on purpose:

He was nine, and his father was teaching him to take a bearing, and young Bram got it wrong four times in a row, and on the fifth try got it right, and Elias Vane said nothing. Not *good*. Not *well done*. Nothing. Just took the instrument back and moved to the next lesson, and Bram had stood there holding his correct answer like a gift returned unopened.

That was the whole father, wasn't it? A man who signed nothing — no warmth, no praise, no letter, no goodbye. A man whose love, if it existed, was filed somewhere Bram was never given clearance to read.

Except.

Except the man had spent his final years secretly documenting a drowned nation, alone, in defiance of a treaty government that had every reason to want the Meridian to stay buried. Except he had tied this journal in oilskin and hidden it in a chest that the river itself had been keeping, and the river had given it up to — of all the dredges on all the miles of water — his son's chain.

Except there was a lawyer's letter tucked in the oilskin too, which Bram had been avoiding the way you avoid a tooth you suspect is bad. He took it out now. Broke the seal.

*Mister Bram Vane,* it began, in the bloodless copperplate of estate law. *We write regarding the estate of Elias Vane, deceased fourteen months past. Per the testator's instruction, we were to make contact upon your thirty-fifth birthday or upon your discovery of certain materials, whichever came first. As our inquiries indicate you have recently recovered items from the River Ossen near Kiln Bend, we consider the condition met.*

*The testator has left you the whole of his estate: the house at Graywater Reach, his instruments, his papers, and a sum held in trust. He left instructions that we attempt contact annually. Our records show seven such attempts. All were returned unopened.*

Seven. Seven envelopes sitting in a lawyer's office, addressed in that merciless hand, coming apart in a drawer. Bram set the letter down very carefully, the way you set down something when the alternative is throwing it through a wall.

"Bram," Odile said.

"Don't."

"He was dying for a year before it took him. He had time to write you directly. Instead he wrote a *condition* into his will. Discovery of materials. He engineered it so you'd find him, Bram. So the finding would be yours."

"That's worse," Bram said. "That's so much worse. That's — he couldn't just *write*—"

"He couldn't just die, either, apparently. He turned his death into a survey." Odile picked up the journal, weighed it in her hands like a core sample. "You're angry."

"I'm *furious*."

"Good. Anger means you loved him. Grief is just anger that's finished its paperwork." She set the journal down. "Read the rest of it. All of it. Then decide who you're furious at, because I've met the treaty office, and I promise you they're worse company than a dead man."

* * *

He read until the lamp burned low.

The journal was half survey, half confession. Elias Vane had begun, Bram gathered, as a hired consultant — hired, he now saw with a chill, by a firm whose name had been blotted out with ink so thorough it could only have been done by the author himself. Early entries were dry, professional. Then, somewhere around the third season, the entries changed.

*I asked today why the client wants the wreck located if they intend to leave it buried. The answer was a raise. I have accepted the raise. I intend to keep working and stop asking questions, which I understand is how respectable men sleep.*

Then, two pages later:

*I did not stop asking questions. God forgive me, I never once stopped.*

The last third of the journal was a different document altogether. Positions verified three ways. Photographs referenced. Names — commissioners, ministers, the architects of the Vessian dissolution — listed with dates and sums beside them, in columns that read like a ledger of sin. And threaded through it all, the map, refined season by season, converging on a single set of coordinates in the old channel below Kiln Bend.

Coordinates that matched, to the fathom, where Bram's dredge chain had snagged a bronze-stamped chest.

He closed the book. His hands were not quite steady.

The anger was still there — good, hot, useful anger at the man who had chosen riddles over words, conditions over conversation, seven returned envelopes over one honest letter. But underneath it, rising like water under a hull, was the other thing. The thing he had refused for five years, then for fourteen months, then for one long night beside a sealed chest.

His father had died alone in a house full of secrets, and the nearest thing to a mourner at the funeral had probably been the lawyer.

And the lawyer's letter had a postscript. Bram had missed it on the first pass, his eyes skidding off anything in copperplate. He found it now.

*P.S. — The testator anticipated interest in the estate from parties other than yourself. He instructed us to inform you, should you ever present yourself, that "the fleet will come dressed as law." We confess we do not know what this means. We hope you do.*

Bram looked up at Odile. She was watching him with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"When does the dry season start biting?" he asked.

"Six tides," she said. "Maybe seven if the rains upstream run late. After that, the channel drops and the wreck might as well be on the moon." She paused. "Why do you ask like that?"

"Because my father's lawyer says a fleet is coming," Bram said, "and I've just realized the chest I pulled out of the river isn't treasure to everybody. To somebody, it's evidence." He stood, and the anger settled in him at last into something with a shape and a direction, which was the most dangerous form grief could take. "Get your instruments, Odile. We're going to need to know exactly how much water is over that wreck — and exactly how little time we have."

Outside, far downstream, so faint it might have been imagination, something with engines was already moving against the current.
