## 4. Salvage Law and Other Fictions

The gunboats came upriver on the third morning after the chest, and Bram knew them before he could see them, because the herons left.

He was sitting on the porch of Odile Sarn's stilt-house with a cup of coffee that had gone cold an hour ago and the Vessian chest open at his feet, working through Elias's journal by lamplight he didn't need anymore because the sun had been up for two hours and he hadn't noticed. That was the thing about the journal. It ate time. His father's handwriting — that narrow, impatient slant he'd spent thirty years trying not to recognize — marched across page after page of soundings and sketches and marginal notes in three languages, mapping a river bottom most men would swear held nothing but silt and old regrets.

*Meridian lies eleven fathoms off the Gray Bend cut, hull intact, cargo manifest appended.* And then, smaller, pressed so hard into the paper it had nearly torn through: *They will come for this. Let them find nothing but my name, which they already took.*

Bram had read that line maybe forty times.

Now the herons lifted off the shallows in a single gray sheet, wheeling downstream, and Odile came out onto the porch with her ears doing what her ears always did before her mouth caught up.

"Engines," she said. "Two. Heavy. Running against the current like they've got somewhere to be."

"Corvane?"

"Who else owns gunboats on this river?" She squinted south, where the bend flattened out into a mile of brown water and heat shimmer. "I told you six tides. I didn't tell you you'd have company for them."

"You said the river was dying."

"The river is honest. It's the people floating on it you want to worry about." She looked down at the chest, at the bronze stamp worn soft by forty years of water — the double horizon of Vessia, the nation that had existed on maps right up until the maps were redrawn around it. "You should decide what you're going to do when they ask you what you found."

"I'm going to lie."

"Mm." Odile sat down heavily in her chair, joints announcing themselves. "And how good are you at lying, digger? Because from where I sit, you're a man who once returned a mining concern's overpayment to the penny. Twice."

"That was different."

"It always is." She poured herself coffee from the pot he'd abandoned and drank it cold without complaint, which was the most Odile thing about her. "Here comes your different now."

* * *

The Corvane Salvage Fleet arrived the way money arrives: loudly, and with the assumption that everyone had been waiting.

There were two gunboats, shallow-draft, twin-screwed, their hulls painted the flat industrial green of every Corvane operation on three continents, and behind them a barge crane riding low with equipment, and behind *that* a third vessel that wasn't armed or working at all — just white superstructure and polished rail, the kind of boat that existed so somebody could stand on it looking important. A man did stand on it, actually, hands clasped behind his back, wearing a linen suit in weather that would kill linen.

The lead gunboat swung wide and put its bow toward Odile's stilt-house with the casual insolence of something that had never once been told no. Wake slapped the pilings. The chest, open at Bram's feet, rocked gently on its own shadow.

"They'll board," Odile said. "Let me do the talking."

"No."

"Bram."

"You talk too much like a hydrographer." He stood, closed the journal, and slid it into his coat, which fit badly over it and made him look, he suspected, exactly like a man hiding a journal. "They're going to read me either way. Better they read me wrong."

"And how do you plan to be read?"

"As boring." He managed half a smile. "It's the one performance I've rehearsed my whole life."

The gunboat's boarding party came up the ladder without asking — four men in Corvane green with the particular slack competence of hired muscle, and behind them, last, a woman who was neither slack nor muscle.

She was perhaps fifty, gray going on silver at the temples, built like someone who'd spent her life on boats and intended to die on one. She wore a plain working jacket with captain's bars stitched on by hand rather than issued, and she climbed the ladder the way Bram hauled himself out of a pit: efficiently, without wasted motion, already reading everything. Her eyes went to the heronless shallows, to the mooring lines, to Bram's badly fitting coat, and stayed there a half-second longer than politeness allowed.

"Captain Ilsa Rehm," she said. "Corvane Salvage Fleet, Upper Reaches contract." She didn't offer a hand. "You're dredging for the Kessel Mining concern. You pulled something Tuesday night."

Not a question. Bram kept his face at its long-practiced setting of mild professional vacancy.

"Pulled a lot of things. River's full of them. Mostly mud."

"Mud doesn't make a man stop working." She walked past him along the porch, unhurried, and stopped at the empty space where the chest sat — because it still sat there, because moving it would have meant admitting it mattered, and because some stubborn animal part of Bram wanted her to see it and understand nothing. "Bronze-stamped chest. Pre-treaty sealwork. My sonar crew logged your winch load at four hundred pounds and your reaction time afterward at zero. You stood in that water for eleven minutes, Mr. Vane. We have the thermal imaging."

Well. So much for boring.

"Vane," she repeated, tasting it. "Any relation to Elias Vane?"

The name went through him like a winch hook through a palm. He'd had five years of practice not flinching at it and he flinched anyway, some small tell around the eyes, and he watched her file it away with the neatness of a woman keeping books.

"Distant," he said.

"Mm." She turned back to the river, hands in her pockets, and for a moment she seemed less like a corporate captain and more like someone standing watch at dawn, which was somehow worse. "Mr. Vane, I'll be brief, because we both have work. This reach of the Meridian's course falls under salvage concession 114-C, granted to Corvane by the regional authority. Anything recovered between the channel markers belongs, by law, to my fleet. That includes objects of historical significance, which are — I'll note — legally required to be surrendered to the concession holder within seventy-two hours of recovery."

"The river isn't charted under any concession past the second marker," Odile said. "I surveyed it. The channel markers washed out in the spring flood and nobody replaced them, because nobody with money cared about this stretch until—"

"Until now," Rehm agreed pleasantly. "Concessions are funny things, madam. They expand to fit the interest."

"That's not law, that's appetite."

"Yes," Rehm said. "I've never found a meaningful difference."

Bram almost laughed. He didn't, because laughing at Ilsa Rehm felt like laughing at a landslide, but there was something in him that recognized her — the flat honesty of a person who had stopped pretending their work was anything other than what it was. He'd met a hundred men in this trade who dressed theft up as science. She wasn't bothering.

"What do you want?" he asked.

"Three things." She held up fingers. "One: whatever you recovered, surrendered, receipted, no charges filed for the delay. Two: your continued employment. Kessel's contract is void as of this morning — Corvane bought it. Your dredge, your crew's wages, all of it now flies our colors. You're a good hand, Vane; my paymaster says you find things other people's instruments miss. There's a place for you."

"And three?"

She looked at him then, fully, and the pleasantness drained out of her face like water out of a cracked hold, leaving something older and wearier underneath.

"Three is the finder's fee." She reached into her jacket and set a folded paper on the porch rail, weighting it with a brass weight she produced from the same pocket, the way a surgeon lays out tools. "Standard schedule. Ten percent of assessed value for verified location of a protected wreck. Which means if you know where the *Meridian* sits — and your father spent the last decade of his life telling anyone who'd listen that it does, though nobody listened until his survey notes started surfacing — then you can walk away from this river a rich man. Legally. Cleanly. With Corvane's gratitude and my personal word that no one looks too hard at how you came by what you came by."

"And if I don't take it?"

"Then you're a private citizen in possession of disputed cultural property, obstructing a licensed concession, on a river you don't own, holding documents that may or may not belong to a dead man's estate that may or may not have been settled properly." She said it the way Odile read soundings: depth by depth, no drama, just the bottom coming up. "Corvane has lawyers, Mr. Vane. Excellent ones. They will bury you in paper for years, and while they bury you, my divers will be in the water. You'll lose slowly, which is the worst way to lose."

The porch was quiet except for the river. Downstream, the white boat's important man had taken out binoculars and was studying the treeline as if Vessia might come walking out of it.

Bram looked at the fee paper. Ten percent. Of a national treasury. He did the arithmetic without wanting to and got a number with more zeroes than his whole life had earned, and hated himself a little for how loud the number was.

"You knew my father," he said.

"I met him twice." Something moved behind her eyes — not guilt, exactly. Inventory. "Once at a hearing where he accused my company of erasing a country, and once on this river, four years ago, when I offered him this same arrangement and he threw the paper in the water."

"What did he say? Exactly."

Rehm considered whether to answer. Then, apparently deciding that honesty cost her nothing here: "He said, 'You can't buy what was never for sale, Captain, and you can't drown what floats.' Then he made me tow his skiff two miles because his engine had died and he refused to admit it."

Despite everything, Bram's mouth twitched. That sounded like Elias Vane precisely — principled right up until the moment principle became inconvenient, then shamelessly practical.

"He was a difficult man," Rehm said.

"He was a stranger."

"Then you're grieving a stranger, and it'll be easier than the alternative." She picked up her brass weight, tucked the fee paper's corner so it wouldn't blow away, and moved to the ladder. At the top of it she paused, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped into a register meant only for him, quiet under the engine noise.

"One more thing, off the record. The client behind this contract isn't Corvane. Corvane is the shovel. Somebody else is paying for the hole, and they are paying *extremely* well, and in my experience clients who pay that well aren't paying for treasure." She looked back at him, and for one unguarded second she looked tired — tired the way he felt, tired down in the keel. "Treasure keeps. Secrets don't. Whatever your father found, Mr. Vane, somebody wants it to stay drowned, and they have already paid for that outcome once. Ask yourself why a dead surveyor's house burned in March."

Bram's blood went cold and slow. "What house?"

Rehm held his gaze a moment longer, and he understood that she had just handed him something she shouldn't have — a splinter worked loose and passed across the table, for reasons he couldn't yet read. Maybe conscience. Maybe strategy. Maybe just the ancient mariner's superstition that you don't leave a drowned man unmentioned.

"Seventy-two hours," she said aloud, resuming her corporate voice like a coat. "The fee stands. The law stands. The river, unfortunately, also stands, and it's dropping fast — your hydrographer friend could tell you that better than I. Six tides, I'd guess, before this wreck is sealed in mud until the next flood decade." She stepped down onto the gunboat's deck, then paused once more. "Oh — and Vane. If you do decide to run for it: don't use the Gray Bend cut at night. That's where my picket boat sits."

She descended. The gunboat backed away with the same insolent grace it had arrived, wake slapping the pilings, and the little flotilla reformed downstream and began, methodically, to drop anchor buoys across the widest part of the channel — claiming the river the way a spider claims a room, one line at a time.

* * *

Odile waited until the engines faded before she spoke.

"Well," she said. "That went terribly."

"She gave us the picket position."

"She gave you the picket position she *wanted* you to have. Or she made a mistake, or she's playing some game three boards deep, or — and I'd bet on this one — she's a professional who's just realized her client is the kind of client that gets professionals killed, and she's hedging." Odile refilled her cup, thought about it, and added the cooking brandy instead of milk. "Either way, you noticed what she did, yes? She told you the law is hers, the river is hers, the clock is hers, and then she handed you a legal exit priced at ten percent. That's not an offer, digger. That's a test. She wants to know if you're a businessman or a believer."

"Which am I?"

"Judging by the fact that you haven't touched that paper?" Odile smiled into her cup. "A believer. God help us. Believers need crews."

Bram looked at the fee paper fluttering gently on the rail, at the anchored gunboats lying fat and certain on the dying river, at the chest with its patient bronze horizon. Seventy-two hours. Six tides. One picket boat at Gray Bend — real or bait, true or trap. And somewhere upstream or downstream, a burned house that had been his father's, holding whatever Rehm's client had paid good money to erase.

He picked up the paper, weighed it in his hand for a long moment — and then folded it into his pocket next to the journal, because a man would be a fool to throw away the only piece of Corvane paper with a signature on it.

"I need a pilot," he said. "Someone who knows this river below the markers, someone who isn't in anybody's registry, someone who can move four hundred pounds of bronze past a picket boat at night and charge me an amount I'll hate."

Odile snorted into her brandy. "You need Tack Ferrow. And Tack Ferrow needs paying, and Nen Ozu needs convincing, and you—" she pointed the cup at him, "—need to stop standing on porches looking noble and start moving, because the tide went out twenty minutes ago, and Captain, that leaves five."
