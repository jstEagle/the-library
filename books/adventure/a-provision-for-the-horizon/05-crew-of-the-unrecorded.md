## 5. Crew of the Unrecorded

Bram had a rule about asking people for things: never ask anyone who could afford to say no.

It wasn't cynicism so much as arithmetic. A man with options weighs them. A man with nothing left to lose says yes quickly, works hard, and doesn't run to the authorities at the first sign of trouble — because the authorities had already done whatever they were going to do to him, years ago, and it hadn't gone well.

So when he sat down that evening in Odile Sarn's cluttered survey office above the chandlery, and she asked him who exactly he imagined was going to help him steal a nation out of a riverbed, he said: "People the law forgot."

Odile looked at him over her spectacles for a long moment. She was sixty-one, built like a mooring post, and had spent forty years measuring rivers that nobody thanked her for measuring. Her office smelled of ink, lamp oil, and the particular damp that got into everything within ten miles of the Vessa.

"You'll want Nen," she said finally. "And you'll want Tack, God help you. And you'll want me, though I'm too old for this and I intend to complain the entire time."

"I hadn't asked yet."

"You were working up to it. You have your father's face when you're working up to something. He'd stare at the middle distance and breathe through his nose." She took off her spectacles and polished them on a rag that had once, possibly, been white. "Sit down, Bram. Let's talk about how much trouble you're planning, so I can decide whether to be part of it or merely watch it from a safe distance."

* * *

Nen kept a stall in the fish market — not selling fish, but mending the nets, which was work nobody wanted and everybody needed, and which meant she heard every conversation on the waterfront without ever being part of one. She was small, quick-fingered, somewhere between thirty-five and fifty depending on the light, and she had been the senior archivist of the Vessian Royal Registry before the registry itself had been declared never to have existed.

You cannot be fired from an institution that officially has no history. So instead, Nen had been *unhired*. Her credentials became forgeries by decree. Her twenty-two years of service became trespass. The treaty government had not arrested her; that would have acknowledged her. They had simply erased her, the way they'd erased everything else, and let her walk out into a world where her life's work was legally a rumor.

Bram found her at the stall just after dawn, mending a net with fingers that moved like they were reading something.

"Madam Nen?"

"Nobody's called me madam in eleven years." She didn't look up. "Fishmonger's boy, if you're here about the Kessler order, it's not ready."

"I'm not the Kessler boy."

"Then you're either revenue, police, or trouble." Now she looked up. Her eyes were pale gray and entirely unimpressed. "You're too dirty for revenue, too young for police. Trouble, then."

"A little of both." Bram set the bronze-stamped chest lid fragment on her table — he'd pried it loose the night before, keeping the chest itself hidden — and watched her whole body go still.

Her hands stopped moving. For three full seconds the only sound on the waterfront was gulls and the slap of water against pilings.

"Where," she said quietly, "did you get that."

"Dredging the Vessa, two miles below the old ferry crossing."

"That's the Meridian's route."

"It is."

She picked up the fragment the way other people held infants. Her thumb traced the stamped seal — the double tower of Vessia, crowned with the river-wreath — and Bram saw her lips move faintly, reading the maker's mark the way he'd read depth soundings.

"They said it all went down with the ship," she murmured. "The registry, the treasury, the civil rolls. Forty years of 'regrettable loss.' Convenient loss. Loss that closed the book forever." She set the fragment down with exaggerated care, as if it might bruise. "What do you want, digger?"

"A crew. Six tides. I need someone who can read what's in that wreck — catalogs, seals, locks, provenance. Someone who can prove what it is, to whoever needs proving."

"And why me? There are archivists in the capital. Honest ones, with licenses."

"Because a licensed archivist can't touch this. Anything they certify gets seized under salvage law before the ink dries. I need someone with nothing the law can take away." He hesitated, then said it plainly, because she'd earned plainness: "I need someone the treaty already ruined. Because they can't ruin you twice."

Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile, but its distant cousin.

"That," she said, "is the most insulting honest thing anyone's said to me in a decade." She began rolling down her sleeves. "I'll come. But understand something, Bram Vane — I know that's your name, your father's name was on half the correspondence I archived — I'm not coming for treasure. I'm coming because if that registry is down there, it holds four hundred thousand names. People the treaty declared never born. My mother's name is in it. My brothers'. If those pages exist, then we existed, and there are men upstream who would burn the river itself to keep saying otherwise." She stood, brushed net-mending twine off her skirt, and looked at him levelly. "So we're not stealing a treasure, Mr. Vane. We're stealing a witness. Try to keep up."

* * *

Tack was harder.

Not because he was reluctant — Tack had never been reluctant about anything in his life, which was precisely the problem. Bram found him in the back room of the Salt Larder tavern, where Tack lived in a state of cheerful fugitive equilibrium, playing cards with two men whose faces suggested they also preferred not to be described.

"Tack Ferrow."

"Bram Vane!" Tack spread his arms wide, delighted, cards still fanned in one hand. "The dredger! Sit, sit. Are you buying? You look like a man buying."

Bram sat. "I need a pilot. Downriver, past the Corvane checkpoint at Gray Bend, at night, with cargo that doesn't exist on any manifest."

"Sounds illegal."

"It is."

"Sounds *fun*, is what I meant." Tack gathered the cards, apologized extravagantly to his companions in a way that committed him to nothing, and steered Bram toward the corner. Up close he was lean and sunburnt, with a grin that had clearly gotten him out of more trouble than it had gotten him into, and a scar through one eyebrow he touched whenever he was thinking, which was often. "Corvane's posted watchers at Gray Bend. Gunboat, chain boom, inspection lanterns. You know that?"

"I know."

"And you still want to run it."

"With you at the tiller."

Tack's grin widened, then, interestingly, faded into something more careful. "Here's my question, Vane. Everybody knows Corvane Salvage rolled into town last week like they own the water. Everybody knows they've got papers. Papers beat people, in my experience. So what makes you think your cargo's worth dying over?" He leaned forward. "Because I'll run anything. That's not virtue, that's just what I am. But I'd like to know what I'm running *for*, since apparently I'm sentimental now in my old age."

Bram considered lying to him and discarded it. Tack had the look of a man who could smell a lie the way sharks smell blood — professionally, without malice.

"There's a wreck down there," Bram said. "The Meridian. And aboard it is proof that a country existed. Names, records, a treasury. Corvane's been hired to make sure none of it surfaces. I mean to surface all of it."

Tack whistled softly. "Vessia."

"You knew?"

"My grandmother was Vessian. Kept saying grace to saints who weren't on any calendar anymore. Used to tell me the river remembered even when the maps forgot." He shrugged, suddenly embarrassed by his own sincerity, and recovered with the grin. "Anyway. Nobody's ever paid me in *history* before. What's the split?"

"Even shares among crew. Minus expenses."

"Expenses being?"

"Repairs, bribes, and whatever it costs to bail me out when this goes wrong."

"So, everything." Tack stuck out his hand. "I'm in. One condition — when we inevitably get shot at, I get to say I told you so."

"You can say it now, if you like."

"No, no." Tack shook his hand briskly. "Savor it. Anticipation's half the pleasure."

* * *

Odile joined last, formally, which for Odile meant she made Bram sit through a two-hour briefing on seasonal water levels before she agreed to anything.

"The dry season comes early this year," she said, tapping her charts. "Upstream tributaries are already down nine percent. By my figures — and my figures are better than yours, don't interrupt — the wreck site will be workable for six tides. Six low-water windows. After that, the channel drops below four feet and nothing bigger than a canoe reaches it, including Corvane's divers, including us. It becomes a mud flat with secrets in it until next spring."

"Six tides," Bram repeated. It sounded worse aloud.

"Six tides. Which means your romantic little expedition needs hydrography, soundings, current tables, and someone who knows where the sandbars move, because they move, Bram, they always move, and every year some confident young man learns this fact permanently." She fixed him with a look. "I'm sixty-one. I have a bad knee. I will complain constantly. And I'm coming, because I spent thirty years charting that river and I'll be damned if Corvane's gunboats get to rewrite my charts by force."

Bram felt something loosen in his chest that he hadn't known was tight.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me. Thank me at the end, if we live." She rolled up her charts. "Now. Nen, Tack, myself. Four counting you. A barge, a diving bell we'll have to beg or borrow, and six tides against a fleet." She paused at the door. "Your father would have hated this plan. Too reckless. Not enough contingency." A rare, crooked smile. "He'd have come anyway. Go home and sleep, Bram. Tomorrow we start sounding the channel, and you'll want your strength, because I intend to work you like a rented mule."

* * *

They gathered that night on Tack's barge, the *Marta B.* — a flat-bottomed, deeply unglamorous workhorse of a vessel that Tack defended as though she were a thoroughbred. Nen arrived with three crates of her personal papers, salvaged over eleven years from a country that insisted they were fiction. Odile arrived with instruments. Bram arrived with the chest, wrapped in sacking, and set it on the deck planks between the lantern and the river.

For a while nobody spoke. The Vessa moved past in the dark, broad and patient, carrying its secrets the way it carried everything — without complaint, without hurry.

"So," Tack said at last, feet up on the rail. "Four people the world's finished with, one barge, one impossible deadline, and a chest full of a dead country." He raised an imaginary glass. "To the unrecorded."

"To the unrecorded," Nen echoed, and this time it was almost a smile.

Odile snorted, but she raised her hand too.

Bram looked around the lamplit deck — the disgraced archivist, the grinning smuggler, the complaining hydrographer — and felt the strange, cautious warmth of it settle over him. Five years he'd worked alone, hiring out his shovel and keeping his name out of every ledger. He'd told himself it was freedom. Standing here, he suspected it had only ever been loneliness with better posture.

"We should open it properly," Nen said suddenly. "Before we commit. If the seal's intact, there may be requirements — provenance protocols, funerary seals. Vessian chests of state weren't simple strongboxes."

"It opened fine," Bram said. "Outer lid, anyway. Pried it at the site."

Nen went very still. "*Pried* it."

"With a crowbar."

She closed her eyes briefly, as a physician might upon hearing how a patient had treated a wound. "Mr. Vane. Show me the inner lock. Now. Before you put a crowbar anywhere near it, show me the inner lock."

Bram lifted the sacking. Lantern light fell on the chest's second lock — the small one, recessed beneath the main hasp, the one he'd assumed was decorative. It was bronze, worn smooth, worked into the shape of a pair of cupped hands holding something long since corroded away.

Nen leaned over it and did not touch it. Her voice, when it came, had gone quiet and careful, the voice of a woman standing in a church.

"That's not a lock," she said. "That's a *question*. Funerary work. It opens for bloodline — for a token worn by the house that sealed it. To anyone else, it's just bronze." She looked up at Bram, and the lamplight made her gray eyes seem almost silver. "Which raises the real question, digger. Who sealed this chest? And why does a drowned nation's treasury care who's asking?"

Behind them, far upstream, a gunboat's searchlight swept the water once, twice, and went dark.

Tack lowered his feet off the rail.

"Well," he said. "That's our cue, isn't it."
