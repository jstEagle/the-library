## 7. Downstream of the Law

Tack Merrow had run contraband past checkpoints in four rivers, two canals, and one flooded quarry that technically belonged to a monastery, and she had a rule for all of them: the water tells you where the law is sleeping.

The Corvane checkpoint at Gray Bend was not sleeping. It was wide awake, fat, and lit up like a festival, which meant somebody upstream had already sold their names.

She stood at the tiller of the *Kestrel's Debt* — her barge, her debt, her name for it, because the moneylender in Port Sallow had laughed when she'd tried to register it under anything else — and watched the lanterns strung across the narrows like a necklace of small yellow lies. Two gunboats sat moored bow-to-bank, their searchlights sweeping the water in slow, patient arcs. A third hung back in the shadow of the bend itself, dark, which was the one that would kill you.

"They've got a chain," Odile said beside her, low. The old hydrographer had a spyglass to her eye and the tone of a woman reading a weather report about the end of the world. "Floating boom, just under the surface, port side. They'll raise it for traffic they like."

"Then we'll be traffic they like," Tack said.

Bram Vane came up from below decks carrying the chest's weight in his shoulders even though the chest itself was lashed in the hold under forty sacks of river gravel. He'd been down there an hour, checking the lashings, checking them again, checking them a third time with the particular devotion of a man who had recently learned that everything he cared about fit inside one bronze-bound box.

"They flagged every barge out of Sallow since dawn," he said. "How do you know our papers won't hold?"

"Because our papers are beautiful," Tack said. "I paid a man named Ferro eleven silver to make them beautiful. Ferro has never once been caught, which is either a testament to his skill or proof that he works for Corvane, and I've decided not to think about which."

Nen looked up from the crate she'd been using as a desk, archivist's ink still on her fingers. "That's not reassuring."

"It's not supposed to be reassuring. It's supposed to be true." Tack grinned at her. "Reassuring is extra."

The plan was simple, which Tack distrusted on principle. Simple plans were what other people made so that someone like her could be blamed when they came apart. They would run the checkpoint openly — papers, fees, a hold full of gravel and one very boring cargo manifest listing dredging equipment and salt fish. The chest would be under the gravel. If the Corvane inspectors wanted to dig through forty sacks by lantern light, well, that was what bribes were for, folded into the manifest folder like pressed flowers.

What the plan did not account for — what nothing accounted for, really — was the way Bram kept looking at the hatch.

"You're going to get us killed staring like that," Tack told him quietly, while Odile went forward to argue with the river about currents. "A man who looks at the thing everyone wants is a man who tells everyone where the thing is."

"I know how to keep my face still."

"You know how to keep your face still when you're digging. This isn't digging. This is being dug." She turned the tiller a spoke, feeling the current lean against the hull like a tired animal. "Rule of the river, Vane: the cargo doesn't matter. The man who wants the cargo matters. Watch him, not it."

"And who's watching me?"

Tack considered that. "Me, apparently. Gods help us both."

* * *

They came into the lantern light an hour past midnight, riding the ebb, and Tack put on the performance of her life, which was saying something, because her life had mostly been performances with occasional intervals of running.

"Barge *Kestrel's Debt*, out of Port Sallow, bound downriver with dredging gear and salted herring!" she called across the water, in the flat bored voice of a woman who had made this trip nine hundred times and hated eight hundred of them. "Papers coming across!"

A launch put out from the gunboat with four inspectors aboard, and the lead one — young, wet behind the ears, wearing a Corvane sash that hadn't learned to hang right yet — climbed aboard with the exaggerated care of a man who did not own these boots and knew it.

"Purpose of cargo?"

"Dredging. The mining concern upriver wants the channel deepened before the dry season kills it." Tack handed over the folder with the exact degree of reluctance that suggested mild annoyance and nothing worth finding. "You're welcome to look. Mind the herring barrels. Number three leaks."

The inspector flipped pages. His lamp swung. Behind him, two more inspectors were already moving along the deck toward the hold, and Tack felt the familiar cold slide down her spine, the one that meant the odds had shifted without changing shape.

"Forty sacks of ballast gravel," one of them called back. "Want me to open them?"

"Do I look like I want to open forty sacks of gravel?" the inspector said, and there it was — the flicker, the glance between them, the little dance of men deciding whether effort was worth their pay. Tack started breathing again on the installment plan.

And then the third inspector, the quiet one at the rail, said: "Open them."

He was older. He wore no sash. He had the stillness of a man paid not by the inspection but by the result, and when he looked at Tack, he wasn't looking at a smuggler. He was looking at a delivery.

"Ballast gravel," Tack said pleasantly. "From the Sallow pits. It's gravel."

"Open them."

* * *

Later — much later, when her hands had stopped shaking and the river had carried them clean past the last lantern — Tack would reconstruct those ninety seconds the way she reconstructed everything: as a series of small mercies arranged by a god with a sense of humor.

The first mercy was the herring barrel, number three, which leaked exactly as advertised. It chose that moment to let go properly, flooding the deck boards with brine and dead fish smell, and the young inspector stepped in it to the ankle and swore with real feeling, and in the half-minute of chaos that followed, Odile appeared at the gangway holding the manifest folder and a second folder, thicker, stamped with the mining concern's actual seal — a document she had spent the entire voyage preparing, because hydrographers, Tack was learning, believed in redundancy the way priests believed in prayer.

"Perhaps this will save you the trouble," Odile said, in the voice of a woman who found the entire century mildly disappointing. "Channel survey authorizations. Depth orders. You'll want to note the seal number; your supervisor will ask."

The older inspector didn't look at the documents. He looked past them, at the hold, at the gravel, and Tack saw him weighing something — not evidence, but value. What was down there versus what it would cost to take it here, in the light, with witnesses.

Then the second mercy arrived, and it arrived from the most unlikely direction imaginable: Bram Vane walked out of the shadows at the hold ladder, wiping his hands on a rag like a man interrupted at honest work, and said, loudly, "If you're going to open the gravel, open it. We've another forty sacks under those, and the tide won't wait for any of us."

It was perfect. It was so perfectly indifferent, so magnificently bored, that the older inspector's certainty wavered — because guilty men hid, and this man was offering shovels.

But Tack saw his eyes. They never went to Bram's face. They went to the hatch, once, quick as a fish taking a fly, and then back, and in that flicker she read the whole letter: *he knows*. Not suspected. Knew. Knew there was something under the gravel, knew its shape, maybe knew its weight, and was choosing — choosing — to let it pass.

Why?

The answer came to her with the cold clarity of river water in the wrong season: because he didn't need to take it here. Because a chest pulled from a dying river was worth a finder's fee, but a chest delivered to Gray Bend was worth whatever Ilsa Rehm had promised him. Because the checkpoint wasn't a net. It was a funnel. Everything they passed tonight was being counted, weighed, and reported downstream to a woman who didn't need to catch them — only to know where they were going.

"Gravel checks out," the older inspector said suddenly, stepping back from the hold. "Move along. You're holding up the channel."

The young inspector blinked. "Sir, we haven't—"

"Move *along*."

* * *

They ran dark after that, no lamps, Tack steering by the sound of the water against banks she knew the way other people knew their mothers' voices. Nen sat on the chest itself, literally sitting on it, arms wrapped around her knees, as if proximity could protect it. Bram stood at the bow doing the one thing Tack had told him not to do, which was stare at the hatch.

At the first bend with cover, Tack finally let go of the tiller long enough to pour herself a finger of the terrible plum brandy she kept for emergencies, and then poured a second finger and drank both, because one emergency had happened and she could feel the second one circling.

"He let us through," she said.

Bram turned. "What?"

"The quiet one. No sash. He opened the gravel question and then closed it himself, and he never once looked at you like a stranger." She held up the empty cup. "Men don't do favors for strangers. He knew what we're carrying, Vane. And he waved us through anyway, which means somebody wants us moving — wants us easy to follow, or easy to find later. Rehm doesn't have to catch us at a checkpoint. She just has to know where we sleep."

Odile had come aft, and the old woman's face in the starlight had gone very still, the way it did over a chart showing a reef nobody had charted before. "The boom was raised before we signaled," she said slowly. "I noted it. I assumed incompetence. I no longer assume incompetence."

"So we weren't lucky," Nen said. "We were *allowed*."

"We were allowed," Tack agreed, and heard her own voice come out flatter than she intended, all the exhilaration draining out of it and leaving something colder underneath. She'd come up the river three days ago feeling like the old days — the wind, the dark water, the game of it. Somewhere between the lanterns and the leaking herring, the game had stopped being a game. You could outrun a law. You couldn't outrun a ledger.

Bram was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had that new hardness in it, the one that had arrived with the journal and his father's name and hadn't left. "Then Rehm doesn't want the wreck. Or not only the wreck. Any salvage crew could take gold off a dead ship — that's work, that's risk, that's shares. But she let the chest pass." He looked down at the hatch, at the thing beneath it. "She wants *this*. Whatever's inside it. Which means whoever's paying her knows what's inside it too."

"Vessia's treasury," Nen whispered. "Everyone knows that story."

"No." Bram shook his head slowly. "Treasure sits in wrecks for forty years without anyone sending gunboats. Something in this chest scared someone enough to buy a fleet." He met Tack's eyes. "How far to the next safe anchorage?"

"Six hours, if the river behaves."

"And if it doesn't?"

Tack smiled, and it was the smile she used for passengers she liked, which was worse than the one she used for passengers she didn't. "Then we find out what else Ferro's eleven silver bought."

She took the tiller again and leaned into the current, and the *Kestrel's Debt* slid downstream through the dark, past the sleeping law, carrying forty sacks of gravel, one impossible chest, and five people who now understood — each in their own way, each at their own cost — that they were no longer running *from* something.

They were being herded *toward* it.

Behind them, far back at Gray Bend, a single dark lantern kindled on the third gunboat, brief as a blink, and went out.
