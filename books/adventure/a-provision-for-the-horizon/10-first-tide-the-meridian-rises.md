## 10. First Tide: The Meridian Rises

The river dropped like a held breath let go.

Bram felt it before he saw it — a change in the current's voice against the hull, the water going from conversation to whisper as the Gray Reach shallowed overnight. He was awake for all of it, which surprised no one aboard the *Marrow*, least of all himself. He had slept perhaps two hours in stretches, and each time he woke it was with his father's journal open on his chest, the handwriting swimming up at him in the dark like fish rising to feed.

"Low water in forty minutes," Odile said from the stern, where she had been sitting since before dawn with her instruments laid out on an oilcloth like a surgeon's tray. She did not look up. She never looked up when she delivered numbers, as though the numbers might be embarrassed to be seen arriving.

"You're sure?"

"I am never unsure about water, Bram. I have been unsure about people my whole life and look what that got me." She tapped her ephemeris. "Forty minutes. Perhaps thirty-five if the wind holds out of the east."

Tack came up from below decks rubbing sleep out of one eye and holding a tin mug of something that claimed to be coffee. "If we're going to die today," she said, "I'd like to do it caffeinated."

"We're not going to die."

"That's exactly what a man says right before I have to swim somewhere cold." She drank, grimaced, drank again. "Where is Nen?"

"Reading," Bram said. "She's been reading since midnight."

Nen was indeed reading — curled in the bow of the barge with three of Elias Vane's survey journals stacked around her like sandbags, her finger moving down columns of depth soundings and bearing marks as though she were praying along them. When Bram came forward she looked up, and there was something in her face he hadn't seen before. Not fear. Something older than fear.

"The soundings," she said. "Your father took them himself. Look at the dates — he came back every dry season for eleven years. Eleven years of standing in this same river, measuring it, writing it down, and never once telling anyone why."

"He told me why once," Bram said. "He said he was mapping silt deposits for a canal concern."

"And you believed him?"

"I believed he was boring." Bram sat down across from her, the deck timbers warm under his palms despite the early hour. "That was easier. A boring father you can leave without hating yourself."

Nen closed the journal gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping child. "He wasn't boring, Bram. He was careful. There's a difference, and it cost him everything."

Bram didn't answer. The river answered for him, dropping another handspan, and somewhere downstream a gravel bar that hadn't seen air in a decade broke the surface with a wet gleam, blinking like something newly born.

* * *

They saw the wreck at half past low slack, and Bram would remember afterward that nobody spoke.

The Meridian lay on her starboard side in the long elbow of the Reach, canted into the old channel where the current had scoured a basin around her and then, over forty years, filled it back in to her gunwales. Now the river had withdrawn like a tide going out on some impossible shore, and she stood revealed — black ribs above the mudline, a forest of exposed frame timber running her length, weed hanging off her in banners that dripped and steamed in the morning sun. Her stern castle rose maybe twelve feet above the flats, its carved railings furred with mussel shell, and along her flank, just visible where the mud had slumped away, ran a line of bronze fittings gone green as garden statues.

"She's bigger than the drawings," Tack said finally. Her voice had lost all its irony, which from Tack was roughly equivalent to a religious experience.

"Drawings lie by omission," Odile said. "She's a hundred and sixty feet if she's an inch. Treasury vessel, not a trader. You don't build a hundred and sixty feet of ship to carry cloth."

Bram waded toward her before anyone could stop him, which was fortunate, because no one tried. The mud took him to the knee, then the shin, sucking at his boots with the greedy patience of everything in this river. The smell hit him halfway out — river rot and old iron and, underneath it, something mineral and strange, like a struck flint. He had dug on three continents. He knew every smell the ground kept. This one he didn't know, and his heart went ahead of him like a dog off its lead.

The hull rose over him. Up close the timbers were enormous — oak, black as bog-wood, still sound where the mud had held them. His fingers found the bronze fitting and traced it: a stamped plate, and on the plate, worn but legible, the double-key seal of Vessia.

He stood there with his hand on it and found, to his considerable irritation, that his eyes were burning.

Five years he had kept moving. Digs in Kessarin, dredging contracts up and down three river systems, a bunk wherever the work was, a name shortened and muddied so no one would ask whose son he was. He had built a whole life on the principle that stopping was how you drowned. And now here he stood, stopped, ankle-deep in the exact mud his father had measured for eleven lonely years, and the only thing he could think was: *you were here. You stood right here.*

"Bram!" Odile's voice, sharp. "The hold! Check for the hold!"

He wiped his face with the back of his wrist, professional again, and moved aft along the exposed flank. According to the journal — according to the *map*, the map that had started all of this — the Meridian's treasury hold sat beneath the stern castle, accessed through a cargo hatch on the port side, sealed with a double bulkhead. If the mud had protected the hull the way the silt maps suggested —

There. A hatch coaming, broken open by decades of shifting, but behind it, when he leaned into the dark and held his lamp high, the unmistakable geometry of an inner bulkhead: riveted iron, unbreached, a door set flush in it with a lock plate the size of a dinner platter.

And on the lock plate, even crusted with river growth, the shape of the seal — the same double key, but larger, worked in relief, with a socket at its center shaped like nothing Bram had ever seen except, he realized with a falling stomach, like the impression in wax on the chest they'd pulled from the shallows upstream.

"It's here," he called back, and his voice cracked on the second word, and he decided not to care. "The hold's intact. Iron bulkhead, sealed door. It's here, all of it."

For a moment the crew just stood in the shallows and looked at him, four people on a leaking barge in the middle of nowhere, grinning like fools. Even Odile smiled, which she did approximately once per fiscal year. Tack whooped and threw her cap into the water, then had to go retrieve it, which dented the drama somewhat.

Nen alone did not cheer. She had come out onto the flats and stood looking up at the wreck with her arms wrapped around herself, and when Bram reached her she said, quietly, "My grandmother used to sing a work song about the Meridian. The children thought it was about a star." She touched the hull the way you touch a grave marker. "It was about this. All along it was about this, and we sang to it not knowing."

* * *

They spent the next hour doing what Bram's hands knew how to do even when the rest of him was somewhere else entirely: measuring, sounding, marking. Odile paced the waterline drop with her staff and called out figures; Tack rigged lines from the barge to the stern castle so they could haul equipment out across the flats when the water returned; Nen cross-referenced Elias's journals against the visible structure, checking his bearings, finding them good, finding them — Bram watched her realize it — finding them *loving*. Every measurement taken twice. Every hazard noted in the margin in his father's small, patient hand.

"He knew someone would come," Nen said. "Not just anyone. Someone who'd check his work. He wrote this for a digger."

"He wrote it for anyone," Bram said. "He never once wrote anything for me."

"No," Nen agreed, not unkindly. "But he wrote it *like* you."

Bram had no answer for that, so he busied himself with the hatch, clearing silt from the threshold with a hand trowel, working by habit while his mind turned over what Nen had said like a stone in a pocket.

Which is why he heard it first.

A sound under the river's murmur — rhythmic, metallic, faint. Not current. Current doesn't keep time.

He went still, trowel in the mud, head cocked. "Odile. Quiet."

The crew stilled. The sound came again. Tap. Tap-tap. Pause. Tap.

"Tack," Bram said. "What taps underwater?"

Tack's face went through several expressions in rapid succession, none of them pleasant. "Pneumatic line signal. Or—" She stopped.

"Or?"

"Or a cutting torch chisel. Low pressure, working slow." She was already moving toward the barge, all business now, irony abandoned like the cap she'd fished out of the river. "You hear it on wrecks sometimes. Salvors mark their cut lines by tapping the hull — lets the diver on the surface track progress without coming up."

"Progress toward what?"

Tack looked at him, and then at the wreck, and then at the far side of the Meridian, beyond which the river bent away north toward Corvane's checkpoint at Hallow Ford.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you absolute sons of — they're already here."

* * *

They crossed the flats at a run, which the mud resisted with every trick it had. Bram went up the stern castle's exposed railings hand over hand, boots slipping on mussel shell, and hauled himself onto the weather deck, which was a wilderness of silt and collapsed decking and forty years of bird nests. From there he could see over the wreck's starboard side, down the length of the hull, to where the river bent north.

Two boats. Low, gray, unmarked except for a stencil number. Moored in the deep channel on the far side of the Reach, casual as fishermen, and between them and the hull, a float line ran taut into the water with small buoys spaced along it — a dive line, anchored, in active use.

And rising from the water near the hull, thin and silver in the morning light, a thread of bubbles. Steady. Unhurried. Working.

"They're cutting from the starboard side," Bram said when the others reached him, breathless and mud-slicked. "Opposite flank. They're going through the outer hull toward the hold."

"How long?" Odile demanded. "How long until they breach the inner bulkhead?"

Bram did the arithmetic he didn't want to do. Iron outer plating, forty years of river growth over it, a slow chisel working at low pressure to stay quiet. Then the inner bulkhead — heavier, but a determined crew with proper gear wouldn't be stopped by it, only delayed. "A day," he said. "Maybe less if they've got more divers rotating than we can see. Maybe two if the growth slows them."

"And the tide?"

Odile consulted her watch, then the sky, then some private ledger she carried behind her eyes. "Water starts rising within the hour. Full flood by evening. Next true low water — next exposure like this — tomorrow night. That's our window to work the hatch openly." She paused. "That's also their window. Everyone works better in the dark, and they know we're here now."

"Do they?" Tack asked. "Know, I mean. Or are they just salvaging blind and we got lucky?"

As if in answer, the bubbles at the far side of the hull stopped.

The silence was worse than the tapping. Four people on a dead ship's deck, holding their breath, watching a line of bubbles that wasn't making any more bubbles.

Then, from the gray boat in the channel, a figure stood up — too far to see a face, close enough to see the gesture: a hand raised, unhurried, and swept in a slow arc toward the Meridian's stern. Toward them. Not a wave. An acknowledgment. *We see you. We've always seen you. Take your time.*

"Rehm," Bram said.

"You can't know that," Odile said.

"I can. That's exactly how she'd say hello." He remembered the salvage fleet's captain at the dock at Corvane Landing, the finder's fee and the threat delivered in the same breath, the way she'd looked at the chest — not at him, at the chest, the way a banker looks at collateral. "She's not rushing because she doesn't have to. She thinks we're trapped. One barge, no guns, and a wreck she can take whenever she likes."

"Is she wrong?" Tack asked.

Bram looked down at the hatch coaming below them, at the iron door behind it, at the socket in the lock plate shaped for something he didn't yet possess. Somewhere in a sealed house upriver — in a house someone had already torn apart looking for it — was the thing that fit.

"Not yet," he said. "But she will be. We need that token, and we need it before the next low water. Which means somebody has to get into my father's house past whatever tore it apart last time, and back, inside two days."

"I'll run it," Tack said immediately.

"You don't know the house."

"You don't know the checkpoints between here and there. Rehm's fleet sits on the river like a cork in a bottle. Anybody going upstream gets boarded, searched, and invited to reconsider their life choices." She grinned, and it was her river grin, the one that meant trouble had already been priced in. "So we don't go by river. There's a cart road through the Karst hills — two days hard driving, one day back if the axle holds. Give me Nen."

Bram blinked. "Give you Nen?"

"She's read every page of those journals. If there's a hiding place in that house, your father will have left it marked the way he marks everything — twice, patiently, for someone who checks his work. That's not me. That's her." Tack shrugged. "Also she's the only one of us who can read whatever language he hid things in."

Nen looked up at the mention of her name, still standing with her palm flat against the wreck's railing, and Bram watched her weigh it — the house of the man whose books she'd slept beside, the risk, the road. He watched her decide.

"I'll go," she said. "But Bram — if the token isn't there, if he spent it or lost it or it was taken—"

"Then we open that door another way."

"There is no other way. That's what funerary work means. It isn't a lock, it's a promise. It opens for blood or it doesn't open." She said it gently, the way she said most hard things, and climbed down toward the barge to pack.

Below them, in the northern channel, the bubbles began again. Tap. Tap-tap. Patient as erosion.

Odile put a hand on Bram's shoulder. For once she looked at him directly, her pale hydrographer's eyes narrowed against the glare off the water.

"One tide," she said. "We confirmed it exists. Now comes the part where we keep it."

Bram looked north, where the gray boats sat quiet as knives in a drawer, and then south, where the river waited to rise and cover everything again.

"Then we'd better be quick," he said, "about learning what my father was quick about his whole life — keeping what matters hidden from people with boats."

Tack was already untying the skiff.
