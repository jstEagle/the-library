## 11. Ilsa Rehm's Ledger

Ilsa Rehm kept her accounts the way her mother had kept a kitchen: everything in its place, everything written down, nothing wasted. Her ledger rode in a oilcloth case inside her coat, against her ribs, where a softer woman might have carried a letter from a lover. It was not a book of money, exactly. It was a book of what things cost.

*Two gunboats, fuel, four days: 640 marks.*
*Informant at the Karst ferry: 30 marks, paid in salt, because salt could not be traced.*
*One hydrographer, captured: pending.*

She wrote "pending" and then sat with her pen above the page for a long moment, listening to the rain come off the river in sheets against the wheelhouse glass, and did not write anything else for a while.

The hydrographer was seventy-one years old. She had learned this an hour ago, from the woman herself, who had announced it in the tone of a person reciting their own eulogy and finding it satisfactory. Odile Sarn sat now in the chartroom of the *Corvane Pride*, wrapped in a blanket some deckhand had fetched her because her teeth were chattering, drinking tea that Rehm had ordered made properly, with the good leaves, because — and here was the trouble, here was the whole trouble — because Rehm could not seem to stop treating prisoners like guests.

"More tea?" she asked.

"I'm an old woman, Captain, not a leaking hull." Odile held out the cup anyway. "But yes."

Rehm poured. Her second-in-command, Vosk, watched from the doorway with the expression of a man who had seen his captain feed gulls off the stern and disapproved of it on principle. Vosk disapproved of everything that did not appear on an invoice.

"You understand," Rehm said, sitting down across the chart table from the old woman, "that this is not a kindness. You're a lever. That's all. I pull you, and Bram Vane gives me the wreck, and everyone goes home with their accounts balanced."

"Ah," said Odile. "So you're the kind who needs to say it out loud. That's worse, you know. The ones who say it out loud are the ones still arguing with themselves."

"I'm not arguing with myself."

"No," Odile agreed pleasantly. "I am. You've just mistaken me for your conscience, which is flattering but inaccurate. I'm much older and much more stubborn." She sipped her tea. "This is good tea. Where does a salvage captain get tea like this?"

"Corvane pays for the good leaves."

"Corvane pays for everything, I imagine."

"Corvane pays for everything," Rehm said, and heard, in her own voice, the flatness that Odile had been fishing for. She closed the ledger. "You were Bram Vane's teacher."

"I had the misfortune of teaching a boy who only ever wanted to learn the parts I couldn't teach. Rivers, mostly. Where they've been and where they intend to go. I taught him arithmetic and he used it to argue with me about silt." Odile's eyes crinkled. "You want to know where he'll be at the next tide. I can tell you for free: he'll be where the river tells him to be, and the river has never once been where Corvane's charts say it should be."

"I have charts."

"You have *invoices*," Odile said, "bound in leather. I've seen your kind of chart, Captain. Every sandbar marked as of the survey date, every survey date three years old, every channel drawn the way it was when the mapmaker got paid. The Meridian doesn't sit in your chart. It sits in the river's memory, and the river has been keeping that memory for forty years, and it does not take corrections from men with invoices."

Rehm let the old woman talk. It was, she told herself, technique. Let the prisoner fill the silence and they'd hand you everything. But Odile Sarn filled silences the way a river fills a channel — completely, and on her own terms — and somewhere in the middle of a digression about the difference between a flood and a freshet, Rehm realized she was enjoying herself, and that this was a problem, and that she was going to have to put it in the ledger.

*One hydrographer, captured: pending. Note: talks too much. Note: so do I, when I'm tired.*

* * *

The client's message came at dusk, by the sealed courier packet that arrived twice a week from upriver, and Rehm read it alone in her cabin with the lamp turned low, the way she read all the client's messages, because they read better that way. In low light you could almost pretend the words were someone else's.

The client did not sign its name. The client never signed its name. The packets bore the seal of a shipping concern, Halloway & Marsh, which had offices in four cities, no ships anyone could find, and a stationery budget that would have made a cardinal weep. Halloway & Marsh wanted the Meridian. Specifically, Halloway & Marsh wanted one item from the Meridian, and the rest — the gold, the plate, the crown regalia of a country that had been erased from the maps with the tidy efficiency of a clerk striking a line — the rest was to be *recovered and dispersed through standard salvage channels*, which was Halloway & Marsh's way of saying: melt it, sell it, scatter it, and make sure that by the time anyone asks, it is no longer a thing but a price.

And the one item. The registry.

Rehm had known, in the way you know a load is overloaded before the crane arm groans. She had known since the second packet, when the client's instructions had shifted from *secure the wreck* to *secure the wreck's contents, with priority given to the civil registry, which is to be destroyed unopened, and confirmation of destruction is to be notarized*. She had read that sentence four times. *Destroyed unopened.* Not seized. Not archived. Not returned. Destroyed, and the destruction witnessed, because the client wanted proof, and proof meant paper, and paper meant that somewhere in a government office a man whose grandfather had signed the Treaty of Ashford wanted to be certain that the last list of Vessian names — every citizen, every family, every child entered into the record of a nation that had officially never existed — would never again be a list, but ash.

She had taken the contract anyway. That was the part she kept circling, at night, with the lamp low. She had read the terms and she had signed, because the fee was the largest of her career and because her fleet was eleven boats and two hundred wages and a dry dock with a roof that leaked, and because — she had told herself this, in the voice she used for telling herself things — because salvage was salvage, and a wreck's contents belonged to whoever pulled them up, and she had never once in nineteen years asked where a cargo came from. Cargo had no history. That was the beauty of it. That was the whole profession: you met the world at the moment it lost something, and you did not ask how it lost it, and the not-asking was not cowardice, it was *neutrality*, it was the clean line between her work and other people's sins.

*Destroyed unopened.*

She got out the ledger and wrote, in her small even hand: *Registry, civil, Vessian: one item. Client instruction: destruction. Not salvage. Not recovery. Destruction.* And then she sat looking at the word *destruction* until the lamp guttered, because she had written it down and now it was in the book, and the book was the one place where she did not lie.

Her mother had kept accounts the same way. Flour, salt, lard, the money owed to the widow Dresch, the money owed *by* the widow Dresch, written in the same hand with the same honesty, because her mother had said — Rehm had been nine, and had never forgotten it — *a ledger is the only friend who'll tell you the truth about yourself, and if you lie to it, you'll never find another.* Her mother had kept a shop in a river town that had changed hands four times in her lifetime and had belonged, officially, to no nation at all, and had belonged, unofficially, to whoever held the bridge, and her mother had paid every holder of the bridge and recorded every payment and never once called it anything but what it was: *bribe, bridge toll, forced.* The ledger did not care who won. The ledger only cared what was true.

Ilsa Rehm had left that town at sixteen with a boat hook and her mother's arithmetic, and she had built a fleet, and she had told herself for nineteen years that the fleet was neutral, and now she sat in her cabin with a word in her ledger that was not neutral, and the lamp guttered, and she did not sleep.

* * *

In the morning she brought Odile Sarn breakfast. This was, Vosk's face made clear, unprecedented. Prisoners on the *Corvane Pride* got hard bread and river water and a bucket, and were grateful for the bucket.

"Eggs," said Odile, examining the plate. "Poached. Captain, are you fattening me for something?"

"I'm keeping you alive. You're worth more alive."

"Everything is worth more alive. That's the whole trouble with the world." Odile cut into the eggs with the deliberation of a woman who intended to enjoy them out of spite. "You're going to make your offer this morning. I can tell. You've got your negotiating face on. It's a good face. Very bank-manager. But you've also brought me eggs, and no one brings eggs to a negotiation they feel clean about."

Rehm sat down. She had rehearsed the offer on the walk over — the wreck for the woman, clean trade, no harm done, Vane gets his teacher back, I get the hold, everyone's accounts balance — and she delivered it now in the tone she used for quoting rates, and Odile Sarn ate her eggs through the whole of it and did not interrupt once, which Rehm slowly understood was worse than any interruption.

When it was done, Odile dabbed her mouth with the napkin and said, "May I ask you a question? Not about the trade. About you."

"You may ask."

"Your client. The one whose seal is on those packets that come upriver twice a week. Do you know who they are?"

"I know what they pay."

"Yes," said Odile. "I imagine you do. I'm asking if you know who they *are*."

Rehm was quiet for a moment. Through the wheelhouse glass she could see the river running high and brown with the last of the wet season, and the far bank where the reeds stood thick, and somewhere down that river a wreck full of dead nation, and somewhere beyond that a boy she'd offered a finder's fee and a threat in the same sentence, who had looked at her with eyes like his father's and said nothing at all.

"They're Halloway & Marsh," she said. "A shipping concern."

"Halloway & Marsh has no ships."

"They have contracts."

"They have *one* contract," Odile said, "and I would wager my pension — which is modest, but I'm fond of it — that the contract is not with Halloway & Marsh at all. I'd wager it's with the ministry that administers the Ashford territories. The treaty government, Captain. The one that signed Vessia out of existence forty years ago and then spent forty years making sure no one remembered there'd been anything to sign away." She leaned forward, and her voice, which had been pleasant all morning, went quiet and precise, the voice Rehm imagined she'd used on students who were about to fail. "They didn't erase a country with soldiers. Soldiers are expensive and they leave witnesses. They erased it with paper. And now there's a chest in a river full of the paper they couldn't find, and you are the paper's escort to the furnace. That's your cargo. Not gold. *Ash.*"

"You don't know my contract."

"No," Odile agreed. "But I know you've been sick since the second packet. I can see it. It's in your face when you think I'm not looking. You've been keeping books for nineteen years, Captain, and somewhere in the last week your own ledger started telling you the truth about yourself, and you've been bringing me eggs ever since." She sat back. "Now. About your offer. The answer is no."

"No?"

"No. Not because I'm brave — I'm seventy-one, bravery is for people with more future — and not because I think you'll hurt me, because you won't, you'll feed me eggs until the dry season ends and then apologize. I say no because Bram Vane will trade the wreck for me. He'll do it in a heartbeat. He'll hate himself while he does it and he'll do it anyway, because that's the kind of fool I trained him to be, and your client knows it too, which is why you took me instead of shooting at him." She folded her hands. "And I will not be the reason a nation's grave gets robbed twice. So you'll need to find another lever, Captain. Or — and I say this with all the affection of an old woman who liked your tea — you'll need to decide that you are not, in fact, the kind of person who pulls levers."

Rehm stood. She gathered the plate, the cup, the napkin, with more care than the task required, and at the door she stopped, without turning around.

"The registry," she said. "If it's destroyed, and you're right about who wants it destroyed — then the forty years didn't happen. The erasure finishes. There's no record there was ever anything to record. Do you understand what that is? That's not salvage. That's the last page of a murder, torn out."

"I know," said Odile gently. "I've been trying to tell you since yesterday. You're the one who wasn't listening to yourself."

Rehm went out. In her cabin she opened the ledger and, beneath *pending*, wrote the only honest entry she had made in nineteen years:

*Client: not a shipping concern. Client: the government that did this. Cargo: the proof. My fee: —*

She left the fee blank. She looked at the blank space for a long time.

Then the lookout's bell rang twice from the masthead, and Vosk's boots hit the deck overhead, and a voice came down the hatch, excited and breathless: small boat off the starboard quarter, running dark, no lights, no flag, cutting straight for them out of the reeds — and Rehm closed the ledger on the blank space and went up to see who was foolish enough to come to *her* in the night.

Through the glass she made out the shape of it: a smuggler's skiff, shallow and fast, and at the tiller a small wiry figure she'd seen once through a checkpoint scope, and standing in the bow with a white cloth tied to a boat hook like a man surrendering to a river —

— Bram Vane, come to trade.
