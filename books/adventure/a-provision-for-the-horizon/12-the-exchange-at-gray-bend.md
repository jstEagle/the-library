## 12. The Exchange at Gray Bend

Odile Sarn had been a hostage for eleven hours, and she had spent nine of them correcting her captor's charts.

It wasn't that she'd planned to. It was simply that when a man spread a hydrographic survey across a gunboat's chart table and it contained an error of four fathoms in the channel off Gray Bend — an error she herself had corrected in a monograph fourteen years ago, thank you very much — something in her chest refused to stay quiet. Hostage or no hostage, wrong soundings were wrong soundings.

"That mark is stale," she said, tapping the offending number with one finger. "The river shifted after the flood year. You'll ground your boat on the near shoal if you trust it."

The Corvane deckhand holding the rifle looked at her the way men look at weather they don't understand. Captain Rehm, standing at the rail with her arms crossed, looked at her differently — with the flat, measuring attention of someone deciding whether a thing was a tool or a liability.

"You're the hydrographer," Rehm said.

"I'm the woman whose tea you interrupted," Odile said. "The hydrographer part is also true."

Rehm had taken her at the fuel dock at Halfway Light, quick and clean, two boats and six men while Odile was bent over her depth gauge checking the morning's readings. No violence. Not even rudeness, really, unless you counted the zip ties, which Odile did. The message had gone to Bram within the hour: *the wreck for the old woman, Gray Bend, second tide.*

*Old woman.* She was fifty-eight. She'd once swum the Serein Narrows in April water. But she supposed that from the vantage of a salvage fleet, anyone who remembered the Vessian treaty being signed qualified as ancient.

They kept her in the wheelhouse because it was warm and because Rehm seemed genuinely curious about her. That was the unsettling part. Ilsa Rehm asked questions the way surveyors ask them — patiently, from several angles, until the shape of the answer emerged whether you wanted it to or not.

"Vane's father," Rehm said, over the terrible coffee her crew brewed. "Elias. You knew him?"

"I trained him," Odile said. "Forty years ago, before he decided maps were beneath him and went off to dig holes in respectable countries."

"And then he came back and dug holes in this one. Secretly."

"People do their best work in secret. It's the only time nobody's telling them how to do it faster."

Rehm smiled without warmth. "You're fond of him."

"I'm fond of accuracy," Odile said. "I'm reserving judgment on Elias Vane, who let his son grow up thinking he didn't exist, and who died alone in a house full of other people's names." She heard her own voice sharpen and made herself stop. Grief was a current; you didn't fight it mid-channel, you waited for slack water. "Ask your next question, Captain."

Rehm asked it. It was about tide tables.

* * *

Bram arrived at Gray Bend an hour before the exchange, alone in Tack's skiff, which was itself a message. *No ambush. No crew. Just me and what you want.*

Odile watched the skiff come down the gray water and felt something complicated move in her chest — relief first, sharp and simple, and then, arriving behind it like a cold layer under warm, a question she didn't want to examine yet.

Because Bram Vane had argued with her about this. Loudly. In the cabin of the barge, the night the ransom note came, he had stood with his fists flat on the chart table and said, "We don't trade the wreck. We don't trade anything. We run for it, we take the tides we have left, and if they take Odile—" and he had stopped there, jaw working, unable to finish the sentence, which was its own kind of answer.

It had been Nen who'd found the way through. Nen, the disgraced archivist, soft-spoken as falling dust, who said: "He doesn't have to trade the wreck. He has to trade a map. Nobody said the map has to be true."

And so Bram had sat up all night with Elias's journal open beside him and drawn, in his father's own drafting style — and here Odile had felt a pang watching him work, seeing the son's hand reproduce the father's habits without ever having been taught them — a map of the Meridian's position that was true in every particular except the one that mattered. The wreck was real. The channel was real. The approach was real. Only the final bearing was wrong, swung twelve degrees east, into a stretch of riverbed where the only treasure was silt and an old barge skeleton that would read beautifully on any sonar.

A forgery built out of truth. Odile had checked it twice and hated how good it was.

Now the skiff grounded on the shingle below the gunboat, and Bram climbed out, and Odile saw that he hadn't slept, and that he was carrying the map case in his left hand the way a man carries a thing he might have to throw.

"Captain Rehm," Bram called up. "You have my hydrographer. I have your map. Let's be quick — the tide won't wait for either of us, and neither will my temper."

"Your temper," Rehm said dryly. "That's the famous Vane inheritance, is it? Come aboard."

* * *

The exchange happened on the gunboat's deck, because Rehm insisted on inspecting the map before releasing anything, and Bram insisted on Odile standing beside him before handing anything over. So they stood in a triangle — Rehm, Bram, and Odile between them, zip ties cut, wrists aching — while Rehm unrolled the forgery on the chart table and studied it.

Odile watched Bram watch Rehm. And what she saw frightened her more than the rifles had.

He was calm. Not the calm of a man who has planned well — she knew that calm, had seen it on young surveyors who'd done their soundings twice. This was flatter. Colder. The calm of a man who had already priced everything in the room, including the people, and found the numbers acceptable.

"Twelve degrees east of the journal bearing," Rehm said, tracing the line. "Your father's notes say otherwise."

"My father's notes are a year old and the river doesn't sit still," Bram said. "Ask her." A nod at Odile. "She's the one who taught us both that."

It was deft. It was even true — the river *didn't* sit still; a shift of twelve degrees over a year was unusual but not impossible in a dying channel. It was exactly the kind of lie that survives inspection because every load-bearing wall in it is genuine.

Rehm looked up. "Hydrographer. Is this chart sound?"

And there it was. The moment. Odile felt it arrive like a change in pressure, the way she always felt weather coming before the instruments confirmed it.

She could say no. She could say *that bearing is false, Captain, I've surveyed that reach myself,* and burn the whole plan, and watch Bram's face do whatever it did next. She owed him nothing of the kind — she owed him, if anything, the opposite; he'd traded for her, hadn't he? He'd come alone, unarmed, into the mouth of a gunboat, for her.

But he'd argued against trading all night. He'd argued until Nen found the loophole. And the question that had been sitting cold under her ribs since the skiff appeared rose up and asked itself plainly: *if Nen hadn't found the forgery trick, what would Bram have traded?*

She thought of the boy Elias had been, forty years ago, brilliant and hungry and so afraid of being ordinary that he'd lied about small things until lying became a habit of hand. She thought of Bram, who had spent five years refusing his father's name and now forged his father's maps without blinking.

"The chart is sound," Odile said. "Within the tolerances that matter."

Rehm held her gaze a moment longer, then rolled the map and tucked it under her arm. "Then we're done. Vane. A professional courtesy: my client checks everything. When your map turns out to be sand and dead barges — and charts like this always turn out — he will assume you played him. Men like my client respond to that assumption poorly. You've bought yourself a tide, maybe two. Spend them well."

"I intend to," Bram said.

"One more courtesy." Rehm's voice dropped, and for a moment the fleet captain was gone and something wearier looked out. "The man who gave the order for your father's house — the search, I mean, not just the search — sits three boats back from mine. If you're going to do what I think you're going to do, do it fast."

She turned and walked away, and that was that. Two deckhands escorted them to the rail, the skiff was cast off, and then Odile and Bram were alone on the gray water with the engine muttering and Gray Bend falling behind them like a bad memory with good coffee.

Neither spoke for a long mile.

* * *

It was Odile who broke first, because silence had never been her strong suit and because the question had been swelling in her chest the whole way downstream and questions, like floods, do damage when you don't give them somewhere to go.

"You'd have traded the wreck," she said. "If Nen hadn't found the forgery. You'd have handed it over."

Bram's hands stayed easy on the tiller. Too easy. "No."

"Bram."

"No, Odile, I wouldn't have." But his voice had the flatness again, the priced-everything flatness, and she'd spent forty years reading rivers and thirty-five reading liars, and both skills said the same thing.

"You argued all night to run," she said gently. "You were going to leave me."

"I was going to come back for you." The tiller creaked under his grip. "After the sixth tide. After the treasury was safe. I told you that."

"You told me that at three in the morning with a man's mother sitting tied to a chair in your head. Plans made at three in the morning are wishes, Bram. Wishes with a schedule."

He almost laughed — she saw it hit him sideways, the corner of his mouth twitching despite everything — and for a moment he was just a tired man steering a skiff, and her heart went out to him so hard it hurt. That was the trouble with Bram Vane. He made you want to believe him, and he believed himself, and those were two different things separated by a channel full of rocks.

"Nen thinks so too," he said quietly. "Doesn't she. That I'd have traded it. That's why nobody's met my eyes since we left the barge."

"Nen thinks you'd have traded it and hated yourself forever. Tack thinks you'd have traded it and slept fine. I think—" She stopped. Looked at the water. The bend was opening ahead, the barge anchored in the lee where the crew would be waiting, and she wanted very much to say something kind and couldn't find one that was also true. "I think you haven't decided yet what kind of man you are, and you're doing it quickly, under pressure, the way most men do. And I think the forgery was the right call. And I think you should know that I know it was the right call *and* that you'd have made the wrong one if it had been harder."

The engine puttered. A heron lifted off the reeds ahead, unhurried, offended by nothing.

"Elias used to say that about me," Bram said finally. "That I'd make the wrong choice whenever the right one cost more than I'd budgeted for." His voice had changed — rougher, younger. "I hated him for it. Now I hear it in my own mouth and I can't tell whose voice it is anymore."

"Welcome to middle age," Odile said. "It's everyone's voice eventually. The trick is choosing which sentences to keep."

The barge grew out of the haze — Tack on the bow, arms crossed, face unreadable; Nen beside him, small and still, and then, as the skiff closed, Nen stepped forward and raised one hand, not quite a wave, more an acknowledgment that Odile existed and was returning, and Odile felt her eyes sting and cursed the dry river air for it.

Tack caught the mooring line Bram threw, hauled the skiff in, and looked at Bram for a long moment.

"Gray Bend's got a Corvane listening post half a mile upstream," Tack said. "You led them straight to our bearing."

"I led them to a bearing," Bram said. "Twelve degrees east of ours. By tomorrow they'll be dredging a dead barge and calling it progress."

Tack considered this. Something in his shoulders loosened by a degree, which from Tack was a parade. "Twelve degrees east," he repeated. "Of lies built on truths." He spat ceremonially over the side. "Your father would've been proud. Or furious. Hard to say with that family."

"He'd have checked my math," Bram said, and there it was again — that flicker, grief wearing anger's coat — and then he turned to Odile and offered his hand to help her over the rail, and his eyes were wet-tired and honest for one unguarded second before the pricing look came back down like a shutter.

"Glad you're not dead," he said.

"Glad you're not entirely your father," she said, and took the hand.

* * *

They gathered in the barge's cramped cabin that evening — Odile wrapped in a blanket Nen produced from nowhere, tea that Tack had somehow made strong enough to strip paint, the chart table cleared for war.

"We bought a tide," Bram said. "Maybe two, if Rehm's client is slow to check the map. Second tide opens day after tomorrow. That's when we go into the hold."

"Not the treasury?" Tack asked.

Bram hesitated. Odile watched the hesitation and understood it a half-second before he spoke.

"The registry first," he said. "Nen's right. The gold keeps. The names don't — paper rots, and every day it sits in that hold is a day closer to losing the only proof those people existed. We pull the registry on the second tide. Treasury after, if we get a third."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Nen had gone very still, her hands folded around her teacup like a woman holding something alive.

"You're saying," Tack said slowly, "we dive past the gold. For paperwork."

"For *people*," Nen said softly. "For forty years of people."

"For a nation somebody paid to erase," Bram said. "And paid my father to help bury. And killed him over." He laid his hands flat on the table, and Odile recognized the gesture from the night of the ransom note — the same fists, the same flatness — but this time it looked less like a man pricing things and more like a man bracing a door against wind. "Rehm gave me a warning. The man who ordered the search of my father's house is three boats back from hers. Which means whoever killed Elias is close enough to touch. And the registry is the thing he's afraid of. So the registry is where we strike."

Odile drank her terrible tea and watched him and felt the doubt settle into a new shape — not *would he trade us*, but something quieter and worse: *what will he become by the end of this?* Because she had seen men find a mission before, and she had seen what missions did to the ones who carried them, and Bram Vane was carrying his father's death and a nation's ghosts in the same pair of arms.

But that was tomorrow's weather. Tonight there was tea, and a rescued old woman, and a tide bought with a beautiful lie.

It was Nen who ended the evening, setting down her cup with a small decisive click. "The second tide," she said. "When we open the hold — Bram, there's something you need to understand about Vessian funerary sealing. The registry hold isn't locked the way the chest was. It's *addressed*. There will be words on the inner door. Words meant for whoever came looking." Her voice caught, steadied. "My grandmother used to say the registry was written to be found by the children. By us. Whatever's waiting in there — it's been waiting for family."

Bram looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the river muttered against the hull, counting down toward the second tide, and somewhere upstream a gunboat's searchlight swept the dark where a forged map was leading armed men to a graveyard of silt.

"Then we'd better be worth the wait," Bram said.
