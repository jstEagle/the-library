## 13. Second Tide: The Hold of Names

Nen had spent eleven years cataloguing other people's losses. She had sorted the confiscated archives of three dissolved provinces into acid-free boxes, alphabetized the estate papers of families who would never learn where their papers went, and once, memorably, been reprimanded for weeping over a drawer of children's school records. The reprimand had been fair. Archivists wept on their own time. That was the whole discipline of it: the past went in the box, and you closed the lid, and you did not climb in after it.

So she had prepared herself, in the professional manner, for the hold of the *Meridian*.

She had prepared herself for gold. Gold was easy. Gold was inert and stupid and made men like Tack whistle. She had prepared herself for plate, for coin, for the crown regalia of a country that no longer appeared on maps — she had even, in a weak moment on the barge, prepared a small speech about how objects of national significance should be surrendered to a lawful successor body, which she suspected would go over about as well as her speech about proper humidity control.

What she had not prepared herself for was a shelf.

* * *

The hold of the *Meridian* opened at the second tide like a throat clearing its voice. Bram's father's journal had been exact about the mechanism — a counterweighted hatch disguised as hull plating, built by Vessian shipwrights who had apparently considered it a matter of professional honor that a treasury should be boring on the outside. The water drained out in a long, groaning rush, and the smell that came up was river-mud and tar and, underneath it, something older: cedar, still holding its oils after forty years, because Vessian carpenters had never believed in doing anything by halves.

Odile went in first with the lamp, because she was the only one who moved through flooded spaces like a woman walking to her own kitchen. Bram went second. Nen went third, and told herself her hands were shaking from the cold.

The gold was there. Of course it was. It lay in the aft third of the hold in strapped chests, and Tack made a sound behind her like a man who had found religion and lost it again in the same breath. But the forward third — the part that had been designed first, you could tell, because the shelving was integral to the hull and the coin chests had clearly been wedged in afterward by someone apologetic — the forward third was a library.

Not a metaphor. Nen's heart did something unprofessional.

Shelves. Sealed lead-lined cases, stacked in ranks, each one stenciled in Vessian script that she could read the way you read your own name in a stranger's handwriting — too fast, too completely, before you could decide how to feel. The stencils said:

*Registry of Persons. Districts One through Nine.*

*Land Rolls of the Vessian Interior.*

*Court of Assizes, Final Decade, Complete.*

*Parish Records, Births, Marriages, Deaths.*

*The Census of the Year of the Two Rivers.*

Every document the treaty had declared had never existed. Every name the treaty had struck from the world. Here, in a hole in a river, in a country that had spent forty years insisting there was nothing to remember, somebody had built a room to remember it in.

"Odile," Nen said, and her voice came out strange, flattened, like a voice heard through a wall. "I need you to confirm that I'm seeing this and not hallucinating, because if I'm hallucinating I would like to be told gently."

"You're seeing it," Odile said. Her lamp hand was not quite steady either. "Nine districts. It's all of it. It's the whole country in a boat."

"It's not the whole country in a boat," Nen said. "It's the whole country *out* of a boat. That's the difference. That's the entire difference." She heard herself getting loud and did not stop. "Do you know what the treaty commission said? I've read the transcripts, I catalogued the transcripts, I *wept* over the transcripts on my own time — they said the records were lost in the disorder. Lost. As though nine million people were a set of keys. And every archivist who asked where they'd gone was told to stop asking, and every archivist who didn't stop asking stopped being an archivist—"

"Breathe," said Bram.

"I am breathing. I'm breathing *professionally*."

Tack, from the doorway of the gold, called out, "Is there a problem in the paper part? Should I be worried about the paper part?"

"The paper part," Nen said, "is the part that matters, and no, you shouldn't be worried, you should be ashamed, but you can be ashamed after we've moved it."

She got her gloves on. She got the first case down. She did all of it with the smooth mechanical competence that eleven years of boxes had built into her hands, and she did not let herself think about what she was doing, because she knew exactly what she was doing. She was going to open a case and find names, and somewhere in the names, in some district, in some decade, there would be—

*Registry of Persons. District Four. Households A through K.*

Her family had been from the river towns. District Four. Her grandmother had said so exactly once, in the tone of a woman setting down something heavy, and never said it again, and when Nen had asked a second time her grandmother had looked at her with such a specific and terrible gentleness that Nen had understood the question itself was a kind of danger, and had not asked a third time in twenty years.

Her family name, before it was shortened to survive, had been Nenadovar. She had been born Nen Nenadovar and had spent her whole adult life being a person whose name was a fragment, a splinter of a word, and had told herself it was a practical name, a working name, a name that fit on a shipping manifest.

She opened the case.

* * *

The pages were rag paper, good stock, inked in the careful clerical hand that Nen recognized because she had been trained by the last people trained in it — a hand that slanted slightly right and never crossed a t twice. The registrar of District Four had been a person who loved their work. You could tell. The entries weren't just names; they were households, trades, the small annotations that a good registrar couldn't help making, the way Nen herself couldn't help noting when a file smelled of smoke or when a signature trembled.

*Household of Maren Nenadovar, ferrywright. Wife, Sela. Daughters, Iva and—*

Nen stopped.

The lamp was in Bram's hand. She was aware of it the way you're aware of weather. He had come to stand beside her without her noticing, which for Bram was practically a declaration of love.

"Nen," he said.

"Iva and Dosa," she finished, out loud, because the entry was not going to read itself and because her grandmother's name — her grandmother was *Dosa*, her grandmother had been Dosa her entire long stubborn life, and here she was, forty years old and two inches tall, in ink, in a case, in a river, in a country that said she had never been. "Iva and Dosa. Ferrywright's daughters. District Four. That's — that's my grandmother. That's my grandmother's name and it was never lost. It was never *lost*. It was in a boat the whole time. Somebody put it in a boat."

"Someone put all of it in a boat," Bram said.

"Who? Who would — how did it get *here*?" She turned and the question turned with her, out at the hold, at the ranks of cases, at the whole impossible act of care that this room represented. Someone had known the treaty was coming. Someone had known the records would be declared lost, or worse, destroyed — and had loaded a ship. Had bribed or forged or simply *done it*, had moved nine districts of human beings onto a river barge and sailed them into hiding, and had kept the secret so well that the secret had outlived them.

"The captain of the *Meridian*," Odile said quietly. She had a case open at the far shelf. "Look at the manifest plate."

They gathered. The plate was bronze, screwed to the forward bulkhead, and it bore the standard Vessian registry seal and a line of text that Nen read twice before her eyes would accept it:

*Deposited under the Provision of Continuance. The record is the nation. Keep the nation.*

"The Provision of Continuance," Nen whispered. "That was a — it was a draft statute. It never passed. It was in the last session before the treaty; I've seen the minutes; it was laughed out of the chamber. *Keep the nation.* They laughed, and somebody in this room didn't."

Bram was looking at the plate with an expression Nen had learned to read on the river: the face of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. "My father knew this ship was here," he said. "He mapped it. He spent years mapping it. He must have known what the hold held."

"Then he knew about the Provision," Odile said. "He knew someone had tried."

"He knew somebody had *succeeded*." Bram's jaw worked. "And he sat on it. He sat on it for twenty years and never told a living soul, and then he died and left me a map like — like a man leaving a lit match in a house he knew was full of what?"

"Of us," Nen said.

The word came out of her before she had decided to say it, and once it was out she did not take it back, because it was true in a way that had nothing to do with blood. She had spent eleven years keeping other people's pasts in boxes, and she had told herself it was work, and it had never once been work. It had been the closest thing to a country she was allowed to have.

Tack had drifted over from the gold, drawn by the gravity of the paper people, and was peering at the registry with the wary respect of a man looking at a weapon he didn't know how to hold. "So all them names," he said. "Everyone the treaty said wasn't nobody."

"Everyone the treaty said had never been anybody," Nen corrected. "Yes."

"And there's, what, thousands?"

"Millions. Nine districts. This is the civil registry — every citizen, every household, every birth and death for the last sixty years before the treaty." She ran her gloved hand an inch above the pages, not touching, the way you'd pass your hand over a sleeping animal. "Tack, do you understand what this is? A person under the treaty can't inherit. Can't claim land. Can't sue. Can't even prove their own parents, because the births were never recorded — except they *were* recorded. They're *here*. This isn't treasure. This is *standing*. Every family in the diaspora, every settlement downriver — this is the document that turns them from ghosts back into people. Legally. In front of any court that will look at it."

"And any court that won't," Odile said.

"Then in front of the ones that will. And in front of the ones that won't, in front of *everybody*, it's still the truth with a seal on it." Nen heard her own voice crack on the last word and let it. Eleven years of professional composure, and she let it crack like a plate, on purpose, in front of witnesses. "I found my grandmother. She's in District Four, household of a ferrywright. She had a sister named Iva. I never knew she had a sister. My grandmother carried that alone her whole life because carrying it out loud was dangerous, and now it's *ink*, it's just ink, it's safe now, it's been safe in a boat for forty years—"

She sat down on a coin chest, which she would have considered a professional atrocity under any other circumstances, and put her face in her hands, and the crew of the unrecorded did the only correct thing, which was to let her.

* * *

After a while — she could not have said how long; the hold kept its own time, tide-time, no-clock time — Bram crouched down in front of her. He had the specific awkwardness of a man who had never once in his life comforted anyone and was now going to attempt it with the same grim thoroughness he brought to dredging.

"We should take inventory," he said. "You should lead it. You're the only one who knows what we're looking at."

"I know what we're looking at," Nen said into her hands. "That's the problem."

"I know." A pause. "My father's in here somewhere too, I think. Not in the registry. In the *why*. He didn't find this ship, Nen. He was looking for it. You don't spend twenty years mapping a wreck you found by accident. Somebody told him. Or he read something. Or—" Bram stopped, and Nen lowered her hands, because she had never heard him stop mid-sentence, not once, not in a cave-in, not with gunboats on the water. "Or he was part of it. The Provision. The keeping. Maybe the Vane name isn't just old Vessian. Maybe it's *load-bearing*."

Nen looked at him — this man who had spent five years refusing his father's name, who had dug under other names on three continents, and who was now sitting in a room full of resurrected people saying *maybe it's load-bearing* like a man testing a floor he'd spent his life avoiding.

"Then we should inventory," she said. "All of it. Every case. Because if your father was part of the keeping, there may be a deposition. A keeper's log. And if there is, it will say who else knew, and who else was *supposed* to know, and—" she stood, and her legs held, which felt like a small miracle she hadn't earned, "—and who killed him for it."

The hold went quiet. Even the river seemed to lower its voice.

"You think Rehm's client knew about this room," Odile said. It wasn't a question.

"I think," Nen said, and the devastation in her chest was doing something she hadn't felt it do in eleven years, which was *hardening*, cooling, setting into a shape she recognized because she'd seen it in Bram every day since the chest came up out of the water, "that nobody hires a salvage fleet with gunboats to recover gold. Gold you can buy anywhere. You hire gunboats for the one thing that can't be replaced. You hire them to make sure a room like this never reaches a shore."

She pulled the District Four case into her arms. It weighed nothing and everything.

"Start the inventory," she said. "Case by case. I want a full manifest by the next tide. And Bram — when we get off this river, we're not taking this to a buyer, are we."

It was not a question either. Bram looked at her, and then at Odile, and then at Tack, who was holding a gold strap like a man holding the last rope on a cliff and visibly doing arithmetic of his own.

"No," Bram said. "We're not."

"Good," said Nen. "Because I've made a list of every archive in the diaspora that could hold this, and the nearest one is a settlement called Karst Hollow, and if half of what I've heard about their elders is true, we're going to need more than a registry to convince them. We're going to need a miracle, a lawyer, and a very fast boat."

"We've got a fast boat," Tack said. "The other two, you're on your own."

Nen hugged the case tighter and, for the first time since she was a girl, felt exactly the right weight in her arms.
