## 14. The Heirs Downriver

Karst Hollow announced itself the way shy people do at parties: by trying very hard not to be noticed.

Tack spotted it first from the pilot's bench, squinting through the morning haze where the river bent around a shoulder of limestone. "There," he said, and pointed with his chin because his hands were busy pretending they weren't wounded. "Smoke. Three chimneys, maybe four. Somebody's baking bread."

"You can smell bread from here?" Odile asked.

"I can smell *intent* to bake bread," Tack said. "It's a smuggler thing. You learn to read a settlement before you read a chart. Chimney smoke means permanence. Bread smoke means they're not expecting trouble. Trouble doesn't get bread."

Bram stood at the rail and looked at the little cluster of rooftops tucked into the fold of the hills like a coin slipped into a pocket, and felt something in his chest he didn't have a name for yet. Forty years ago — thirty-eight, if you counted the way Nen counted, from the signing of the Treaty of Marrow Ford to the day — there had been no Karst Hollow here. There had been Vessia, all of it, and then there hadn't, and the people who had been Vessian had scattered like seed on wind and taken root wherever the ground was indifferent enough to let them.

And now some of that seed had come back to the river's edge, close as they dared, and baked bread.

"They'll know why we've come," Nen said quietly. She had barely slept since the hold of names. Bram had watched her do it — or not do it — through the night, sitting cross-legged beside the registry chest with her hand flat on its lid, the way you'd keep a hand on a sleeping child or a grieving friend. "Word travels downriver faster than boats. They'll have heard about Corvane. About us. They may already know what we're carrying."

"Then this should be simple," Tack said cheerfully. "We knock, we say hello, we hand over a nation, everybody cries, somebody feeds us. I'm banking hard on the feeding part."

Nobody laughed, which was unusual for Tack, who could usually wring a laugh out of a funeral. Bram noticed that too, and filed it away next to the other things he'd been noticing since the knife-fight in the cut lines: how Tack's jokes now came a half-beat late, how Odile checked the horizon twice as often, how Nen touched the chest lid every time she passed it, like a superstition she'd decided to believe in on purpose.

The romance of the thing had died in the dark water three days ago. What remained was heavier and stranger, and none of them had learned yet how to carry it lightly.

* * *

They moored at the old ferry landing, which someone had recently repaired with new pilings and old stubbornness, and walked up the switchback path into the Hollow proper. It was a village built by people who expected to be asked to leave: low stone houses hugging the hillside, roofs weighted with rocks against weather and worse, gardens tucked into crevices where a casual eye wouldn't count them. Everything useful was slightly hidden. Everything beautiful — a carved lintel here, a painted door there, a wind chime made of river glass — was small enough to deny.

A woman met them at the well at the center of the village. She was perhaps seventy, straight-backed as a surveyor's rod, with white hair braided in a style Bram had seen only once before — carved into the bronze stamp of the chest that had started everything.

"You're the ones from the wreck," she said. Not a question.

"We are," Bram said. "I'm Bram Vane. This is Odile Sarn, Tack Ferrow, and Nen—"

"Ilves," the woman said, and turned to Nen, and her face did something complicated. "You have your grandmother's chin. And her terrible habit of walking into places uninvited."

Nen made a sound Bram had never heard from her — half laugh, half something breaking gently. "Aunt Sefa?"

"Everyone's aunt. It's cheaper than being anyone's mother." The woman — Sefa — looked them over one by one, and Bram watched her arrive, item by item, at an understanding of what they carried. Her eyes stopped on the wrapped bundle slung across Tack's shoulders — they'd brought the registry itself, because leaving it on the boat had seemed unthinkable and leaving it with any one of them alone had seemed worse. "So it's true. You found the Meridian."

"We found more than the Meridian," Bram said. "We found the registry. Every name. The whole civil record — everyone the treaty erased. We came to bring it home."

He had rehearsed versions of this moment the whole way downriver. In some of them there were tears. In some, singing — he was embarrassed later to admit he'd imagined singing. In all of them, the elders of Karst Hollow received their own history back into their hands with the gravity it deserved.

Sefa looked at the bundle for a long moment. Then she looked at Bram with an expression he would come to think of, afterward, as the exact color of the word *no*.

"No," she said.

* * *

They sat in her kitchen, because Sefa Ilves was the kind of woman who refused people over bread whether they wanted bread or not, and it would have been rude to refuse, and besides, Tack had been right about the baking. It was dark rye, dense as ballast, and Bram ate his slice slowly while the conversation went nowhere he wanted it to go.

"It's not that we don't want it," Sefa said. She said it to the table, to the bread, to the steam off her tea. "Understand that first. There isn't a soul in this Hollow who wouldn't walk barefoot to the wreck and carry those pages up the hill on their backs. My mother's name is in that book. My brothers. Half the village has someone in it — someone the treaty said never existed, whose house was given away, whose grave was dug up and the stone recut." She set down her cup with great care, the way you handle a thing when you're afraid your hands might otherwise express an opinion. "You're offering us our dead. Don't mistake me for a woman who doesn't want her dead."

"Then what—" Nen started.

"Then think," Sefa said, "about what happens the day we accept it."

She laid it out plainly, the way she apparently laid out everything, and Bram felt each piece of it land like a stone dropped down a well.

Accepting the registry meant acknowledging it. Acknowledging it meant acknowledging the theft — publicly, formally, on the record. And the treaty government, the one that had paid Corvane to bury the Meridian and had, if the paymaster's papers told the truth, arranged Elias Vane's death to keep it buried, could not permit that acknowledgment to exist within reach of its courts. Karst Hollow was forty families on land held under a grazing lease so old nobody had audited it. It was invisible because it had made itself invisible. The registry, sitting in the village archive, would make it visible forever.

"They won't send soldiers," Sefa said. "Soldiers make stories, and stories travel. They'll send surveyors. A boundary dispute. A lease review. Some clerk will discover we've been squatting for forty years, and there will be forms, and hearings in a city four hundred miles away, and lawyers we can't afford, and meanwhile the herd goes unmilked and the roofs go unrepaired, and one winter — one bad winter — the Hollow empties out, quiet as breath. And the registry goes into an evidence vault, and the vault is sealed pending litigation, and the litigation outlives everyone in this room." She finally looked up. "I've watched them erase us once, boy. I'm not going to help them do it again, slower."

The kitchen was quiet. Somewhere outside, a goat expressed opinions.

"But hiding it hasn't kept you safe either," Odile said gently. "Odile—"

"Odile Sarn. Yes. Your monograph on tidal bore dynamics is on my shelf, and I understood perhaps a third of it, which is more than I can say for most books." Sefa almost smiled, then didn't. "Say what you were going to say."

"Hiding it means the theft stays finished," Odile said. "Forever. The treaty wins by default, every year, quietly. The registry in the open — even at risk — is the only version of events where the ending isn't already written."

"And who pays for that open version?" Sefa asked. "Not you. You'll sail away. Not him—" a nod at Bram, "—he's got a famous name to hide behind, even if he hates it. We pay. We always pay. That's what being erased *means*. Everyone else gets to be brave with our lives."

Bram opened his mouth to argue and found, to his considerable irritation, that he couldn't. Because she was right. He had come downriver full of the clean heat of restitution — give it back, restore it, done — and he had never once, until this kitchen, asked the question that should have been first: *back to whom, and at what cost to them?*

Justice, it turned out, had an invoice, and it was addressed to the people who had already paid the most.

* * *

It was Nen who broke the stalemate, though it took her most of the afternoon.

She had gone out after the midday meal — Bram saw her go, up the hill behind the village, alone — and came back at dusk with river mud on her knees and her eyes red-rimmed, and she asked Sefa to gather whoever was willing in the meeting house. Not the elders. Whoever was willing.

Maybe sixty people came, out of a village of perhaps two hundred, and Bram understood that even showing up was a decision some had made against fear and some against family. They sat on benches under a roof of rough-hewn beams, and lamplight moved on faces that held, variously, hope and fury and the careful blankness of people who had learned not to want things.

Nen stood in front of them with the registry open on the table, and she did not give a speech. Bram had half expected one — she was an archivist, words were her trade — but instead she simply read.

Names. She read names. First names, family names, trades, street addresses in cities that no longer officially contained those streets. She read them the way you read a litany, evenly, without performance, and somewhere around the fortieth name an old man in the second row began to weep without any noise at all, just water running down a face that had clearly practiced not doing this in public.

Then Nen turned to a page near the middle and stopped, and Bram saw her hand flatten on the paper.

"Ilves," she read. "Maret. Weaver. And Ilves, Danek, age nine." Her voice caught and held. "My grandfather's sister and her son. Registered residents of Vessia. Persons. On the record. Real." She looked up. "I spent eleven years in exile archives looking for proof my family existed. Eleven years. And the proof was underwater the whole time, forty miles from where I was born."

Silence. Then the old man who had been weeping spoke, in a voice like dry leaves. "Read another one."

So she did. And another. And the room, which had come divided, began — not unanimously, nothing in this life was unanimous — to lean forward.

* * *

The elders deliberated until well past midnight, in the meeting house, with the door closed, and the crew waited outside in the cold like defendants. Tack fell asleep sitting up against a fence post around the second hour and had to be prevented from falling over by increasingly creative means. Odile paced out the dimensions of the building, muttered calculations about load-bearing timber, and declared it structurally sound, as if this were the night's pressing question. Bram stood at the fence and watched the windows glow and thought about his father.

Elias Vane had spent twenty years documenting a drowned country and told no one. Had written testimony naming powerful men and sealed it in bronze. Had died for it, probably, and left the whole dangerous inheritance to a son who'd spent five years crossing borders specifically to avoid inheriting anything. And Bram had been angry about that — was still angry, in a way that had stopped having anywhere to go — but standing in the cold outside a village of people deciding whether to risk everything for the truth, he felt the anger shift its weight. His father hadn't been reckless. He'd been *waiting*. Waiting for someone who could carry it further than he could.

That didn't mean he forgave him. But it meant the anger had company now, and the company was grief, and between the two of them they were teaching Bram something about patience.

Near dawn, the door opened. Sefa stood in it, gray-faced with fatigue, and behind her the elders wore the look of people who had argued themselves hollow and arrived somewhere none of them liked.

"We'll take it," she said. "Conditionally."

Bram waited.

"Not here. Not stored here, not archived here, not *near* here — not until it's safe, and safe means public, and public means witnesses, many witnesses, the kind that can't be disappeared. We'll receive it formally — that matters, legally it matters, Nen explained it and she's right — but it goes back to your boat, and you find a way to make it undeniable before you bring it home. Read it aloud somewhere they can't silence. Get it into hands they can't buy." Her jaw tightened. "We survived forty years by being deniable. We'll stay deniable until the truth is louder than we are. That's the price, and we shouldn't be the ones paying it, and we're paying it anyway, and I want it noted for the record that I know exactly how unjust that is."

"It's noted," Bram said quietly. "By me, at least."

Sefa studied him for a long moment, and something in her expression eased by a degree. "You have his jaw," she said. "Elias Vane. He came through here, you know. Fourteen years ago. Asked questions about the old ferry routes. We didn't know who he was — he gave a false name, badly." Almost a smile. "Vessians can spot a bad false name at fifty paces. It's a national art."

"He was here?"

"Eat something, boy. You look like your father did, and he never ate either." She turned to go inside, then paused. "One more condition. When you make it undeniable — whatever you do, wherever you do it — you do it soon. Because Corvane's gunboats are downstream, the dry season is coming, and that wreck of yours has maybe two tides left in it before the river takes it back. Whatever's still down there when the water drops—" she shrugged, "—stays down there until the spring floods. If it survives the dredging."

The cold that went through Bram then had nothing to do with the hour.

Two tides. He'd been counting six from the beginning; somewhere between the knife-fight and the kitchen and the long night of waiting, he'd lost track of the ledger. He counted backward quickly, grimly, and arrived at the arithmetic he'd been avoiding.

They'd used four.

* * *

They were loading the registry back aboard — Nen wrapping it herself, refusing all help, handling each volume like a relic and also like ordnance — when Tack, who had gone up the path to see about breakfast and possibly reconnaissance, came back down at a jog that pulled at his bandages and didn't care.

"Bram." He was breathing hard and grinning, and the grin had no humor in it at all. "You need to see the bend downstream. Right now."

"What is it?"

"Smoke." Tack pointed south, toward the gray line of the river gorge. "Chimney smoke, camp smoke, and coal smoke — engine smoke. Lots of it. And before you ask: no, I can't smell bread."

He looked at Bram, and the joke landed the way his jokes landed now, a half-beat late and heavy.

"That's not a checkpoint," Tack said. "That's a fleet. And it's between us and the wreck."
