## 15. Third Tide: Cut Lines

Tack had run cargo through checkpoints on three rivers and two seas, and she had a rule about fear: you let it ride in the boat with you, but you don't let it take the tiller. Fear was a passenger. Passengers paid their fare by keeping quiet.

The fare was getting expensive.

She crouched at the stern of the barge, third day of the third tide, watching the water of the drowned channel go from brown to green to that strange glassy teal it turned when the river dropped low enough to let the sky in. The Meridian lay beneath them like a sleeping animal — you couldn't see her yet, not from up here, but you could feel her. The current bent around her hull the way a stream bends around a knee. Tack had spent her whole life reading water, and the water was saying: *something big is down there, and it is not asleep for long.*

"You're doing the face," Nen said, settling beside her with two tin cups of Odile's terrible chicory. Nen did everything quietly, the way archivists do, even walking on a barge. "The one where you look like you're arguing with the river."

"I'm not arguing. I'm losing politely."

Nen smiled into her cup. She'd been smiling more lately. Three days ago she'd found her family's names in the registry — Tack hadn't understood what it meant until she saw Nen's hands shaking over the page, the way you'd shake over a letter from the dead that said *we were here, we counted, we mattered.* Since then Nen had taken to carrying the first volume of the registry against her chest like a child, and nobody teased her about it, and that was the closest thing to tenderness Tack's line of work had ever produced.

"Third tide," Nen said. "Half gone."

"Half gone means half left." Tack drank the chicory and pretended it was coffee and pretended the pretense worked. "I've smuggled worse odds in worse boats. Once I ran a hold full of untaxed cheese past the Harbormaster of Vell with his own customs seal stamped on my manifest."

"You did not."

"Forged his seal myself. He was so proud of his seal, that was the problem. Nobody checks a thing a man loves." She said it lightly, because that was her job on this crew — lightness, ballast, the joke that kept the boat from listing. But her eyes stayed on the water. Because the water had changed again, just slightly, a new wrinkle in the current a hundred yards downstream where no wrinkle should be.

Somebody's line. Somebody's buoy. Somebody breathing through a hose.

The romance of the thing — and Tack would admit, privately, in the locked room of her own head, that there had been a romance to it, the chest and the map and the dead father's secret and the crew of the forgotten — the romance had been dying for two days now, and this was where it finished bleeding out.

She set down her cup. "Nen. Go get Bram. Walk, don't run. Running makes people stupid."

* * *

By the time Bram reached the stern, Tack had counted four of the wrinkles. Corvane divers, working the far side of the hull, cutting toward the hold from the shadowed quarter where the wreck leaned against the old channel wall. They were good. Tack had to give them that. She'd only spotted them because she'd spent twenty years learning to see the river lie.

"Four," she said. "Maybe a fifth. They're coming up from the downstream quarter, using the current to carry their bubbles away from us. Somebody over there did this before."

"Rehm's people," Bram said. He had the look he got when his father entered the conversation without being invited — jaw set, eyes somewhere else. "They've been cutting since the tide turned. Odile figures they reach the hold in—" he glanced at the sun, at the waterline on the channel wall, doing the arithmetic the way Odile had taught him, "—two hours. Maybe less."

"Then we've got two hours to have an argument we should've had three days ago," Tack said. "Because here's my professional assessment, captain. We can't stop four divers with a barge, a retired hydrographer, an archivist, and my considerable charm. We can't outrun them — the Meridian isn't going anywhere and neither are we, not with the hold half-open. And we can't call the law, because the law is currently paying for their air."

Bram looked at the water. Tack watched him do it — watched him actually look, the way he'd learned to somewhere in his five years of running, the way his father must have looked at dirt before he ever dug in it. She'd decided something about Bram Vane on the second day, something she hadn't said aloud: that he was a man who had spent his whole life refusing an inheritance and was only now realizing the inheritance wasn't money. It was a job.

"Then we go down first," Bram said. "We're already in the hold. We hold it."

"With what? Nen's got a letter opener. Odile's got a boat hook and opinions."

"With whatever we've got," Bram said, and there it was, the job, accepted. "Wake Odile. Tack — you've run rivers. You know what's under a wreck. I need you in the water."

Tack looked at the teal glass of the channel, at the sleeping animal underneath, and felt her fare-paying passenger of fear shift its weight.

"Captain," she said, "you have no idea how much I wish you'd asked me to forge a customs seal instead."

* * *

The water at depth was the color of strong tea and old secrets. Tack went down the guide line they'd rigged through the forward hatch with a knife on her belt, a lantern on her wrist, and a heart doing a workmanlike job of hammering. Below her, the hold of the Meridian opened up like a cathedral that had drowned mid-sermon — rows of shelving, the registry crates Nen had wept over, and beyond them, in the flooded dark, the treasury: gold and plate and the long sealed cases of state, all of it furred with forty years of river silt, all of it worth more than every boat Tack had ever stolen, which was a number she was fond of.

The trouble with treasure, she thought, kicking down through the green murk, is that it turns a smuggling job into a war. Nobody ever fought to the death over cheese.

She found Bram at the hold's downstream bulkhead, where the Corvane cutting line had already burned a neat orange seam through the outer plating — she could see the glow of it through the gap, a thin bright thread in the dark, getting longer. Bram had wedged a cargo spar across the breach as a brace, which was about as useful as a polite note pinned to a door.

"That'll slow them," Tack signaled, hands talking in the crude diver's language Odile had improvised. *How long.*

"Minutes," Bram signaled back. His face behind the borrowed mask was grim and steady. "When they come through — don't fight. Fall back to the registry crates. We only need to keep them off the documents."

*And the gold?*

Bram's hands paused. "The gold doesn't matter if the names burn."

Which was the moment, Tack thought later, that the romance finished dying — not with the knife, but here, in the dark, with a man she'd known a week saying *the gold doesn't matter* like he meant it, and meaning it costing him something visible even underwater. Because Tack had spent her life around treasure and she knew what treasure did. Treasure was the reason for the boat. Treasure was the point. And here was this digger's son, this man who'd refused his own name, standing in front of a fortune saying it was ballast.

She was still deciding whether that made him a fool or the only honest man on the river when the bulkhead seam split.

* * *

They came through like eels — three of them, the fourth hanging back at the breach with the cutting torch, its blue flame stuttering in the flooded dark. Corvane kit, Corvane masks, moving with the ugly competence of men who had done this to other wrecks and other crews. The first one came through the gap shoulder-first and Bram hit him with the cargo spar, a swinging shove that sent him tumbling into the shelving in a bloom of silt. The second came low and fast, straight past Bram, straight at the registry crates, and Tack did the math in half a heartbeat — *he's not here for gold either, Rehm's orders are the same as ours, the papers* — and did the thing she'd sworn on two rivers she'd never do again.

She went after him.

Fighting underwater is nothing like the songs. There is no drama in it, no ring, no crowd. There is silt and bad light and your own breath sawing in your ears, and every movement costs you air you cannot replace, and the other man knows all of it too. The Corvane diver was bigger than her and better armed — a short pry bar against her knife — but he was fighting in a cathedral he didn't know, and Tack had been reading wrecks since she was fourteen. She let him come at her through the shelving row, let him commit his weight, and then kicked off a shelf bracket and took him from the side, knife first.

She meant to cut the air hose. That was the plan, the whole plan, the clean plan: cut the hose, he surfaces or he drowns, either way he leaves, no blood, no body, no crime. That was the story she'd have told Nen at supper, the funny version, *you should've seen his face when his air went.*

The river had other plans. He twisted — a good twist, a trained twist — and her blade that should have kissed rubber found the gap at his shoulder instead, and went in, and the water around them went from brown to a slow spreading plum.

The man made a sound through his regulator that Tack would hear for the rest of her life. Not a scream. Worse. A *question.* Like he was asking her why.

Then his partner had her from behind, and the pry bar came down on her shoulder, and the world went white and then green and then very, very quiet, the way deep water is quiet, the way the bottom of everything is quiet.

* * *

She woke on the barge deck with Odile's hands on her, quick and impersonal and gentle all at once, the way river people touch wounds — *I've seen worse, you'll live, hold still.* Her shoulder was a bonfire wrapped in linen. Nen was holding her head. Bram was dripping wet, mask down around his neck, having carried her up the guide line one-armed through forty feet of dark, and his face was the color of the silt.

"The diver," Tack managed. Her voice came out like a hinge. "The one I — did he—"

"He surfaced," Odile said. She didn't soften it. Odile never softened anything; it was why Tack trusted her. "His people pulled him out. He was dead before they got him into the boat. The hose, and the—" she paused, and Tack watched her decide not to lie, "—and the rest."

Tack lay back and looked at the sky, which was doing its dry-season thing, enormous and indifferent and going gold at the edges. Somewhere down there, in the drowned cathedral, a man was dead because she'd aimed for a hose and hit a shoulder. He had a name. He had a boat somewhere with his kit in it, a mug with his initials maybe, a woman or a mother or nobody at all. He'd been paid to be there, same as her.

"First one," she said. "I've never—" She stopped, because the sentence was a lie and they all knew it; she'd cut lines before, she'd left men swimming, she'd once scuttled a patrol skiff with two customs men aboard who'd lived only because the river was feeling generous. But there was a difference, and the difference had a body now, and the body had come out of *her* knife, and the romance — the chest, the map, the dead father's map, the whole beautiful smuggler's ballad of it — was dead on the deck next to the man from Corvane.

"Hey." Nen's hand was cool on her forehead. "Look at me. You were protecting the registry. You were protecting *my family.* I watched you do it."

"That's not the same as it being right."

"No," Nen said, with archivist's precision, "but it's the same as it being *counted.* It goes in the record. Everything goes in the record. That's the whole point of what we're doing. You don't get to pretend it didn't happen, and neither do they." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on *they.* "That's what they've spent forty years doing. Pretending."

Tack laughed, which hurt, which made her laugh more, which made Odile tell her to stop being an idiot with a tenderness that would have fooled a stranger.

Bram crouched down. He was still dripping. "The other two pulled back to their line," he said. "They've got the breach, but we've got the hold, and Odile thinks the tide turns in—" he checked, "—ninety minutes. Tack. You don't have to go back down. Nobody's asking."

"Captain," Tack said, and closed her eyes, and found to her surprise that under the bonfire of her shoulder and the plum-colored memory, something had settled. Not peace. Ballast. "In ninety minutes, I'm going to be right down that line, because if I let a Corvane man die being the last thing that happened in that hold, then they win the story. And the story," she opened her eyes, "is the whole treasure. Isn't that what you said? The gold doesn't matter if the names burn."

Bram looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way men nod at things too heavy to say.

* * *

They buried the hour in work, because that was the only shroud they had. Odile re-rigged the guide lines. Nen moved the registry volumes to the driest shelving and read the inventory aloud in a low voice, like a lullaby, like a vigil. Bram and Tack — Tack one-armed, shoulder strapped, chewing on Odile's willow bark and refusing to be grateful about it — planned the next dive the way you plan anything now: not as an adventure, but as a defense. Stakes. Positions. Who covers the breach. What you do when the torch light comes through the plating again.

It was near the turn of the tide, the light going long and copper on the channel, when the sound came up the river.

Not the gunboats — they knew the gunboats now, the deep diesel knock of them. This was smaller. A skiff, running fast against the current, running *toward* them, which on this river this week meant one of two things: a trap, or a defector.

The skiff came around the bend flying no colors at all, which on the Corvane fleet meant a captain's boat, and at the tiller sat a woman Tack recognized from a hundred yards — broad shoulders, grey-streaked hair, a face like a ledger that had finally found an error it couldn't reconcile.

"Ilsa Rehm," Bram said quietly, and his hand found the spar.

But Rehm wasn't looking at them. She was looking downstream, back the way she'd come, at the gunboat rounding the bend behind her with its running lights already on in the copper dusk. And when she turned to the barge and called across the water, her voice carried the way voices do when a person has stopped caring how they sound:

"Vane! You need to hear what they paid me for. You need to hear it *now*, because that boat behind me isn't here for the gold — and it isn't here for the registry either. It's here to make sure nobody ever testifies about what happened to your father."

Tack looked at Bram. Bram looked at the water, at the wreck sleeping beneath it, at the copper light going down.

"Come aboard, Captain Rehm," he said. "Mind the guide lines. We've buried enough people this tide."
