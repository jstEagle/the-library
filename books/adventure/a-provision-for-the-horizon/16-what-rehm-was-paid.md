## 16. What Rehm Was Paid

The paymaster's name, according to the manifest Tack lifted from a drowned courier skiff two nights before, was Doss Merrow. According to Odile's charts, he would be at Gray Bend on the fourth night of the dry season, paying out wages to Corvane divers in coin that had never seen a mint stamp — because stamped coin could be traced, and everything about this operation was built to leave no fingerprints except the ones it wanted found.

According to Bram Vane, Doss Merrow was going to talk.

They took him between the second and third bend of the oxbow, where the river doubled back on itself so tightly that a boat traveling one bank could watch its own wake pass on the other. Tack ran the barge in dark and engineless, poling through the shallows with the particular silence of a man who had spent twenty years not being where people were looking. Nen waited in the reeds with a lantern shuttered to a coin of light. Bram waited in the mud, which was less dramatic and considerably wetter.

Merrow came ashore at half past midnight in a longboat rowed by two men who clearly wished they were anywhere else. He was smaller than Bram had imagined — a neat, gray man with the posture of someone who had spent his life sitting behind other people's desks, carrying a leather case chained to his wrist like a child holding a parent's hand.

The two rowers they let go. Not gently — Tack relieved them of their boots and their boat, because boots float poorly and men swim worse, but alive, and pointed downstream toward the checkpoint they'd never reach before dawn. It was Merrow Bram walked up behind in the reeds, and it was Merrow's own case chain that went around his wrists when he turned and found a digger's knife resting against the soft place under his jaw.

"You're making an error," Merrow said. His voice didn't shake. That was almost impressive. "Corvane doesn't negotiate for field personnel."

"We're not negotiating," Bram said. "We're having a conversation. The knife is just punctuation."

* * *

They kept him in the hold of the barge, which smelled of bilgewater and old rope and, since the second tide, faintly of paper — the registry's presence had soaked into the wood itself, as if the names were breathing out through the planks. They gave him a blanket, tea that Nen made from river mint and that tasted like punishment, and a stool. Bram sat across from him with the paymaster's own ledger open on his knees.

It was beautiful work, honestly. Bram had spent five years doing contract salvage for mining concerns, and he knew what clean books looked like. Every diver's wage entered twice, once real and once fictional. Every gunboat listed as "survey equipment." Every payment routed through three shell concerns and a chapel fund. A man could read this ledger for a year and learn nothing except that Corvane was very good at existing without being anywhere.

Except that Merrow was tired. That was the thing. He'd been rowed out of his bed at Gray Bend, chained with his own hardware, fed punitive tea, and left alone in a floating box full of dead people's names for six hours, and somewhere in those six hours the neat gray armor had developed a crack. When Bram came down the ladder, Merrow looked up at him with the expression of a man who had been waiting to be asked.

"You're Vane," Merrow said.

Bram stopped on the last rung. "I don't recall introducing myself."

"Rehm described you. 'Tall, angry, digs like he's apologizing to the ground.'" Merrow almost smiled. "She has a gift."

"Where is Rehm?"

"Holding position below the wreck. Waiting." He said it simply, and somehow that was worse than a threat. "Mr. Vane, I'd like to propose something, and I'd like you to hear all of it before you decide how I die."

"That's generous of you. Most men skip straight to the deciding."

"I'm a bookkeeper," Merrow said. "I believe in complete entries."

Bram pulled the stool closer and sat, elbows on knees, close enough that the lantern threw both their shadows huge against the hull. "Then give me a complete entry. Start with who's paying for six gunboats, forty divers, and a salvage contract on a wreck your client claims doesn't exist."

Merrow was quiet for a moment. Out beyond the hull, the river made its small endless arguments with the banks. Somewhere above them, Tack was pacing the deck in the uneven rhythm of a man whose stitches itched, and Nen was reading aloud to herself from the registry in a language only she and the water remembered.

"The client is the Office of Restitution and Settlement," Merrow said finally. "You know it?"

"I know the name. It's on the treaty."

"It administers the treaty. Has for forty years. Every asset of the former Kingdom of Vessia passed through its hands — every account, every deed, every archive. And every year, for forty years, it has certified to the successor governments that no significant Vessian holdings remain unaccounted for." Merrow's chained hands turned over in his lap, palms up, a small helpless gesture. "Your chest is a problem for that certification. Your father's journal is a larger problem. And the registry—" he nodded upward, toward the deck, toward the paper sleeping overhead, "—is not a problem. It is a catastrophe."

"Why?" Bram asked, though part of him already knew, and the knowing sat in his chest like cold iron. "It's just names."

Merrow looked at him with genuine, weary surprise. "Just names. Mr. Vane. Forty years ago, the Office declared that Vessia had no citizens — that the nation dissolved into a legal vacuum, that its people became residents of whatever province drew their border, with no collective claim, no standing, no history that any court was obliged to notice. That declaration is the foundation stone of every settlement, every seizure, every 'provision' signed since. But a civil registry—"

"—is proof they existed," Bram finished. "All of them. Together. As a nation."

"A nation with a treasury," Merrow said. "A nation with heirs. A nation that can walk into any tribunal on this continent and ask one question: *you told us there was no one here to owe. Then who are these?*" He exhaled slowly. "The contract was never salvage, Mr. Vane. Salvage was the cover story we told ourselves. The contract is destruction of records. We are paid to make sure the Meridian stays a rumor."

The lantern hissed. Bram found that his hands had gone still in a way he recognized from bad moments in collapsing shafts — the stillness of a man standing under something that hasn't fallen yet.

"And my father," he said. His voice came out level, which surprised him. "Elias Vane. Where does he fit in your complete entry?"

Merrow didn't answer immediately. He looked down at his chained wrists, and Bram watched a man weigh something — not whether to tell the truth, but how much of it the truth required.

"He was contracted first," Merrow said. "Eleven years ago. Before Rehm, before any of this. The Office hired him quietly — he was the best river surveyor alive, and he had access to waters no Corvane vessel could enter without questions. They wanted him to locate the Meridian and report. Nothing more. Locate and report."

"But he did more than that."

"Elias Vane located the wreck in fourteen months." Something that might have been respect crossed Merrow's face. "And then he lied. For nine years, he reported progress, difficulties, seasonal setbacks. He sent the Office beautifully drawn maps of places the Meridian was not. And all that time he was documenting it himself — the true position, the holds, the contents. Building his own record." Merrow raised his eyes. "He was building a case. You understand? Not a treasure map. A *case*. Testimony, provenance, chain of custody. He intended to hand the whole thing to someone with standing to use it."

"To whom?"

"He never wrote that in anything I saw. But eleven months ago he sent the Office a letter. One page. He told them he had completed his documentation, that copies existed in safe hands, and that if anything happened to him, the record would surface within the year." Merrow's voice had gone very quiet. "He thought the threat would protect him. He thought they'd calculate the cost and leave him be."

The river filled the silence. Bram heard his own breathing, slow and deliberate, the way you breathe when the ground is moving.

"What happened eleven months ago?"

"There was a meeting," Merrow said. "At a lock station upriver. Elias Vane traveled to meet a man he believed was a buyer for his testimony — a middleman named Corvel, who was in fact the Office's chief of settlements. There were witnesses. Two lockkeepers. They reported later that Vane left by boat in the evening in good spirits." Merrow closed his eyes briefly. "His boat was found three days later, thirty miles downstream, holed at the seams. He was never found at all. The Office certified it as accidental sinking. The certification was filed by the same office that had ordered the meeting."

Bram stood up. He didn't remember deciding to stand. The stool went over backward and neither of them looked at it.

"You have documents," he said. "You're a bookkeeper. Complete entries. Tell me you carry the authorization."

"In the case," Merrow said. "Second compartment, under the false bottom. The original instruction regarding E. Vane, dated four days before the meeting. Signed by Undersecretary Halven Corvel." He said it steadily, a man setting down a weight he'd carried too far. "I've read it perhaps two hundred times, Mr. Vane. I want you to know that. I've read it until the words stopped meaning anything, and then read it again until they meant too much. I am a bookkeeper. I keep accounts. And that entry has been sitting in my case for eleven months like a debt I couldn't balance."

Bram knelt and opened the case. The false bottom came away under his thumbnail as easily as a lie. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a single sheet of heavy office paper, a wax seal, a signature in a clerk's careful hand — and beneath the signature, in the margin, in different ink, a note in Merrow's own writing:

*Paid in full, per instruction. No further entry required.*

"No further entry required," Bram read aloud. His voice sounded strange to him, distant, like someone else speaking through the hull. "That's what they wrote. About my father."

"Yes."

"He dug rivers for thirty years. He mapped water nobody else could read. He refused to take his own name off his work even when it cost him every contract in the delta—" Bram stopped. The anger wasn't the hot kind he knew well, the flash-flood anger that crests and passes. This was the other kind, the deep aquifer kind, cold and pressurized and patient. "And they wrote *no further entry required*."

"I know," Merrow said softly.

"You don't." Bram folded the paper along its original creases and put it inside his coat, against his chest, where it felt less like a document than a bone. "But you're about to help me make sure everyone does."

* * *

They brought Merrow up on deck at dawn, because Bram had decided — walking the deck half the night with the paper against his ribs — that the man deserved daylight and a choice rather than a hole and a summary judgment. Tack disagreed loudly. Nen said nothing, which from Nen was a verdict pending. Odile, who had come aboard two nights past with frostbitten fingers and a fury she wore like a coat, simply looked at Merrow for a long time and then said, "He's telling the truth. I've audited liars for thirty years. This one is only tired."

"So what do we do with him?" Tack demanded. He was sitting on the cargo hatch with his arm bound and his knife out, cleaning his nails with it in a way that was either menacing or habitual; with Tack it had never been possible to say. "We can't turn him loose. He'll run to Rehm."

"He won't," Bram said.

Everyone looked at him.

"Because Rehm doesn't know what's in his case," Bram said. "Do you, Merrow? Does Rehm know her client ordered a murder?"

The gray man shook his head slowly. "Captain Rehm believes she is conducting a salvage operation with an aggressive legal mandate. She is many things, most of them expensive, but she reads her contracts. If she had known the true scope — if she had ever seen that instruction — she would have burned the fleet and sailed inland." He paused. "Or I've wanted to believe that for eleven months. At some point the difference stops mattering."

"She should see it," Nen said.

They all turned. She stood by the rail with the registry's reading copy in her arms, morning light coming up gold behind her, and she looked older and younger at once, the way people do when grief and purpose arrive in the same hour.

"Ilsa Rehm took our hydrographer hostage," Nen said. "She threatened us, hunted us, cut toward our dead with torches. And none of that is why she should see this paper. She should see it because right now she thinks she's a professional doing hard work, and professionals can be bought, beaten, or outrun. But a woman who learns she's been rowing for murderers—" Nen's voice caught, steadied. "My family is in this registry. Fourteen names. I have spent six tides learning that the worst thing isn't being erased. It's finding out who held the eraser. Rehm deserves to find out too. Everyone does. That's the entire point of everything we're doing."

Silence on the deck. Even the river seemed to lean in.

"Send him back," Bram said. "Unchained. With a copy of the instruction and a message for Rehm: *read it, and then decide whose side of the water you're standing on.*" He looked at Merrow. "You'll deliver it. Because you're the only messenger she'll believe, and because you're going to spend the rest of your life balancing that entry, and this is the first payment."

Merrow stood. He looked, Bram thought, lighter than a man being released into danger had any right to look — or perhaps exactly as light as such a man should.

"Vane," he said, at the rail, before they lowered him into the recovered longboat. "Your father. In eleven years of reports, he never once missed a deadline. Do you understand what that cost him? Lying in triplicate, on schedule, to the people who owned his name?" Merrow's mouth twisted. "I processed his invoices. He always signed them *Elias Vane, surveyor*. Never consultant. Never contractor. Surveyor. Like he was keeping the title warm for something."

Bram said nothing. He watched the longboat shrink downstream toward the gunboats, carrying his father's death warrant to the woman who'd been aiming her cannons at the wrong crime, and he felt the cold aquifer rage in his chest settle at last into something with a direction — not a flood anymore, but a channel, cut deep, running fast toward one specific sea.

Odile came to stand beside him. She didn't touch his shoulder; she never had, not once in thirty years, and wasn't about to start now. She just stood, and after a while she said, "So. Now we know why."

"Now we know why," Bram agreed.

"And the ring?" she asked quietly. "Nen says the inner chamber opens for it. Says your father's journal all but shouts it, if you read the marginalia the way a Vessian archivist reads. Whatever Elias locked in that chest, boy — he locked it for blood."

Bram reached into his shirt pocket and touched the signet ring he'd taken from the sealed house, the ring he'd dismissed for thirty years as his father's one vanity, the plain band with its worn little crest of a bridge over water. It was warm from his skin. It had been warm from his skin for two days, and he was only now letting himself feel it.

"Fourth tide's tomorrow," he said. "Low water at noon. We open it at the wreck, where he found it — where he *kept* it, all those years, lying in triplicate." He looked downriver, where the gunboats waited, where a captain was about to learn what she'd really been paid for. "Time to see what my father died keeping."

Above them, the sun cleared the bluffs, and the river began, tide by tide, to give up its water.
