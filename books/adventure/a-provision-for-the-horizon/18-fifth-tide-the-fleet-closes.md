## 18. Fifth Tide: The Fleet Closes

Ilsa Rehm had run the Corvane Salvage Fleet for eleven years, and in that time she had learned that the river always told you who was going to win. It told you in the small things: the way a crew's lines were coiled, the angle of a boom, whether the cook's smoke smelled like fear or like breakfast. Fear smelled like burnt grease. Confidence smelled like coffee and onions.

The barge tied up at the Meridian's exposed flank smelled like coffee and onions.

"Damn them," she said, to no one in particular, and to everyone on her gunboat's deck.

"They're cooking breakfast," said her first mate, Doss, who had the gift of stating the obvious with the reverence of scripture.

"They're cooking breakfast *at my wreck*."

"Technically, Captain, it's nobody's wreck. That's rather the whole legal—"

"Doss."

"Shutting up, Captain."

She raised her glass. The morning was the pale, washed-out gray of the dry season's beginning, the river already a hand's width lower than it had been two days ago, the Meridian rising out of the water like something being slowly, reluctantly introduced. Her three gunboats held the downstream channel in a loose crescent — textbook positioning, the kind she'd have praised in any subordinate. The barge was caught. There was no upstream passage that didn't end in the Corvane checkpoint at Gray Bend, and no downstream passage that didn't end in her guns.

And yet they were cooking breakfast.

Through the glass she could see the archivist — the little Vessian woman, Nen — standing at the rail with a mug, gesturing at the sky as though the sky had asked her opinion. The smuggler pilot was laughing at something. Even Odile Sarn, who Rehm had personally held in a hold for two days and who should by rights be furious and diminished, was up and moving about the deck like a woman with an appointment she intended to keep.

And Bram Vane stood at the bow, looking directly at her gunboat, and raised his own mug.

The insolent little dredger *saluted* her.

"He's mocking us," said Doss.

"He's *feeding* his crew," Rehm said, lowering the glass. "There's a difference. Mocking is cheap. Feeding your people before a fight is a statement of intent." She had done the same thing herself, once, in her first year of command, before the delta run, when they'd all been certain they'd die and had eaten eggs anyway. She remembered the eggs. She remembered deciding, somewhere between the eggs and the gunfire, that she would rather be the kind of captain whose crew ate well than the kind whose crews survived on discipline alone.

She wondered, not for the first time that week, when she had stopped being that captain.

* * *

The message came aboard at midmorning, by the client's courier skiff, in the client's usual style: a sealed folio, no name, no signature, only the seal of the Provisional Authority and a single line of instruction.

*The site is to be cleared. The materials are to be destroyed in situ. No documentation is to reach any party. Compliance is total and immediate.*

Destroyed. Not recovered — destroyed. She had known, of course. She had known since the paymaster went missing and came back with a new boat and a new silence, and she had known since the contract's fine print specified *incineration of paper materials* in a clause that had nothing to do with salvage and everything to do with a civil registry that named every citizen a government had spent forty years pretending never existed.

But knowing a thing in the fine print and holding the order in your hand were different animals.

She read it three times. Then she walked to the stern rail, alone, and looked at the water.

Eleven years. Eleven years of running a clean operation in a dirty business — she had told herself that, anyway, and she had mostly believed it. Corvane didn't smuggle. Corvane didn't run guns. Corvane recovered property and paid its crews and its crews' widows, and if some of the property had complicated histories, well, the river was full of complicated histories, and a woman had to eat. She had built the fleet from two leaky barges and a borrowed gunboat into the only name on the water that banks trusted and insurers didn't argue with. She had a pension plan. She had a *reputation*.

And now she had an order to burn the names of a dead nation, and the name at the bottom of that order was the same name that had paid for Elias Vane's death, and she was expected to be the kind of person who read such an order and said *aye*.

"Captain?" Doss, at a respectful distance.

"The client wants the registry burned," she said. She didn't know why she said it aloud. Perhaps because saying it aloud made it a fact in the world, and facts could be argued with, whereas thoughts just festered.

Doss was quiet for a while. He was a slow man with fast eyes, and she had promoted him precisely because he never answered quickly when the answer mattered.

"My mother's from upriver," he said finally. "Kessel's Landing. Before the treaty, that was Vessia. She used to say we were Kesseli. Then one year the maps changed and she said we were from nowhere in particular." He scratched his jaw. "She kept saying Kesseli anyway. Stubborn about it. Died stubborn about it."

Rehm looked at him. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you asked the river a question and I thought you'd want an answer eventually." He shrugged. "Captain — if we burn that registry, we burn her name too. She's in it. Probably. People like her are in it. That's what it *is*."

The river said nothing. The river, Rehm thought, was leaving this one entirely to her.

* * *

She took the launch over alone. That was deliberate. Whatever happened at the barge, she didn't want witnesses with orders, and she didn't want Doss implicated in whatever she was about to do, because she still didn't know what she was about to do, and a captain should never board a hostile deck without at least a plan for her own face.

Bram Vane met her at the gangway with a mug of coffee.

"I didn't say I'd take coffee."

"You didn't say you wouldn't. It's good coffee. Nen's roasting. Apparently the Vessians had opinions about roasting, and apparently she intends to inflict them on all of us."

She took the mug, mostly because refusing it would have required a speech, and the coffee was, infuriatingly, excellent. "You're a very irritating man, Vane."

"I've been told. Usually by people holding my invoice." He leaned on the rail beside her, and for a moment they both watched the Meridian. At low water the wreck's ribs stood out of the river like the carcass of something enormous and patient, and the treasury hold gaped open at its center, and she could see — because he wanted her to see — the crates stacked inside, and the flat iron case of the registry sitting on top of them like a crown on a cushion.

"You got my client's order," he said. Not a question. "You have that look. I've seen it on men who've just been handed something heavy and told it's a gift."

"You have no idea what I've been handed."

"I have a guess. My father was handed the same thing, in a way. He was told to stop looking. He didn't stop. It cost him." Bram turned and looked at her, and up close his eyes were the flat gray of river water in shadow, and there was nothing mocking in them now. "Captain Rehm. I'm going to ask you something, and I want you to answer it as a salvage woman and not as Corvane's captain. When your client says *destroy the materials* — do you know what's in the materials?"

"I know."

"Names. Forty years of names. Every person the treaty unpersoned. Nen found her family in it — her mother, her brothers, their trades, their street. Pages of them. The gold, you can have opinions about. The gold is money. But the registry—" He stopped, and she watched him decide to say the plain thing. "The registry is the proof they existed. Burn it, and the treaty wins twice. Once when it took the country, and once when it took the evidence it took it."

Rehm drank the coffee. It really was very good. She hated him a little for that.

"You're asking me to mutiny," she said.

"I'm asking you to remember what you are when nobody's paying you. The mutiny's your own idea. I'm just standing near it."

* * *

She went back to the gunboat and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for two days.

One tide. The fifth tide would peak at midafternoon and begin to fall around four; the sixth, tomorrow, was the last in which the Meridian's hold would sit above the waterline, and after that the dry season would drop the river below the wreck's ribs and no one — not her, not Bram, not the client's entire army — would reach it this year. The barge, loaded, could make Karst Hollow in one long tide if the pilot was as good as his reputation, which meant the portage from the wreck to the hollow's landing had to begin almost immediately, overland, with crates, in country that had no roads worth the name.

It was insane. It was also, she realized with a sensation like a trapdoor opening, the only plan on the river that she could live inside afterward.

There was a version of her life where she gave the order, the registry burned on the water at dusk, and the client paid, and the fleet survived, and every morning thereafter she woke up as a woman who had burned a book of names, and Doss's mother's name among them, and she understood with total clarity that she would not survive that version. Not die. Worse. She would continue. That was the thing about the kind of corruption that came in on clean paper — it didn't kill you. It just kept you.

"Doss," she said. "Get me the crew on deck. All three boats."

He looked at her for a long moment, and then he went, and he didn't ask, which was its own kind of answer.

She stood on the gunboat's foredeck with her three crews assembled below her — forty-one souls, every one of them hired fair, paid fair, insured fair, and every one of them about to be offered a choice she had no authority to offer.

"Here's where we are," she said. "The client has ordered the destruction of the Vessian registry. Not salvage. Destruction. Burning the record of every citizen the treaty erased — and some of your mothers and grandmothers are in that record." She let that sit. "I'm not going to give that order. What I am going to do is give Captain Vane one tide's head start to move the registry and the treasury overland to Karst Hollow. After that tide, I'm going to report that I arrived to find the site already cleared, that I pursued, that weather and low water prevented recovery. It will cost me the contract. It will probably cost me the fleet. Corvane does not forgive lost contracts, and the client does not forgive anything.

"Any of you who wants no part of this, take the skiff to Gray Bend now, and I will write you the best reference of your life and mean every word of it. No one who leaves today will be punished by me. That's a promise on my name, and my name is the only thing I've got left after today, so it's a good promise."

Nobody moved.

That was the thing that undid her, afterward — not the drama of it, but the ordinariness. Forty-one people standing on decks in the gray morning light, and a long silence, and then the cook from the second gunboat — a heavy, unglamorous man named Pell who had been with her nine years — said, "Captain, if we're burning things, can we burn the client's folio first? It's insulting paper."

And the whole deck laughed, and that was that. That was her mutiny: decided by a cook and a joke.

* * *

She took the launch back to the barge alone, again, because the next part had to be between captains.

Bram met her at the gangway again, and this time he didn't offer coffee, which showed he could learn.

"One tide," Rehm said. "The fifth. It starts falling around four o'clock. You'll have from now until then to get everything off this wreck and onto whatever mule trail passes for a road out here, and then Tack — it is Tack, the pilot, yes — Tack has one falling tide to run your barge down to the hollow's landing, and if he grounds her in the shallows I will deny under oath that I ever met any of you."

Bram stared at her. She had the rare pleasure of watching a man who always had a next sentence find himself without one.

"Why?" he said finally.

"Because my client ordered me to burn a book of names, and one of the names is my first mate's mother, and I find I've run out of ways to pretend the fine print doesn't apply to me." She said it flatly, without decoration, because decorating it would have made it easier and she didn't want it easy. "Also because your father spent ten years doing alone, in the dark, what I was too comfortable to do at all, and I owe him. Also because you make intolerable coffee and I want you gone before I develop a habit."

"That's three reasons."

"I'm a generous woman. It's one of my many flaws."

He put out his hand. She looked at it for a moment — the hand of a dredger, scarred across the palm the way hers was — and then she took it, and they shook, and somewhere in the shake the whole war on the river changed shape, though no treaty would ever record it.

"One condition," Rehm said. "The registry reaches Karst Hollow. Not a vault, not a buyer, not a museum in some capital where it can be 'relocated' in twenty years. The hollow. The heirs. Swear it on your father."

"On my father," Bram said, and she believed him, because he said it the way people say things that cost.

* * *

The loading began within the hour, and Rehm stayed to watch, because a captain should witness her own ruin and take notes.

It was, she had to admit, a beautiful operation. Tack ran the barge's derricks like a man conducting an orchestra that was also trying to rob him; the wounded smuggler worked one-armed with his bandaged shoulder and swore at the winch in three languages, two of which Nen corrected with scholarly precision. Odile Sarn directed the crate order from the hold with a hydrographer's obsession for weight distribution, and when Rehm made the mistake of suggesting a faster sequence, the old woman looked up at her with an expression of such serene contempt that Rehm actually apologized.

By three o'clock the registry was crated, the treasury was lashed, and the barge sat low and heavy and ready. Bram stood on the deck beside Nen, and the archivist was saying something to him that made him laugh — actually laugh, in the last hour before the last tide, with gunboats downstream and ruin upstream — and Rehm, watching from her launch, felt something in her chest that she refused for a full minute to identify, and then identified anyway, because she was not a woman who lied to herself on the important days.

It was the feeling of having thrown away everything she'd built and finding, underneath it, that she was still standing on something.

"Captain." Doss, beside her on the launch, quietly. "The client's courier skiff. Coming upriver. Fast."

She raised her glass. The skiff was flying the Provisional Authority's colors, and it was making directly for the Meridian, and behind it, far downstream, she could see the gray shapes of two more gunboats under the client's flag — not hers, not Corvane's, but the treaty government's own navy, coming to do the job in case she flinched.

One tide. Maybe less.

"Doss," she said, "signal all boats. We're going to have a very crowded afternoon."
