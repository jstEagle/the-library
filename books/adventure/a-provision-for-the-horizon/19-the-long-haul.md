## 19. The Long Haul

The registry weighed less than a sleeping child. The treasury did not.

Bram stood at the tree line above the dying river and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since dawn: eleven miles of ridge country between the barge and Karst Hollow, no road, no track anyone had maintained since the treaty, and four hundred pounds of bronze, lead, and testimony that had to arrive before the sixth tide came and went and the Meridian became a memory again — this time with Corvane's divers picking its bones at their leisure.

"Say the number," Tack said beside him. Her arm was bound to her chest where the diver's knife had found her, and she was gray around the mouth in a way that meant pain being managed by pride alone. "You always do the number out loud eventually. Saves us all the waiting."

"Eleven miles," Bram said. "Four days' easy walking. We have one."

"One and a half," Odile corrected from behind them, folding her charts with the crisp economy of a woman who had spent forty years telling rivers what they were allowed to do. "The tide tables don't negotiate. I've asked."

"You've *asked*?"

"I made conversation with the moon once, in my youth. It went poorly." She tucked the charts into her coat. "One and a half days, if we walk like people who mean it."

Behind them, down the bank, the barge sat low in the brown water with its hold gutted — crates pried open, contents repacked into frames Nen had designed out of salvaged dunnage and stubbornness. The registry ledgers rode in oilcloth inside Bram's own pack, because he had decided, somewhere around the third hour of the night, that if anything happened to him the names would go on his back and nowhere else. The treasury — the actual gold and plate of an erased nation — went into six carrying frames lashed to poles, two bearers each.

Nen crouched over the last frame, checking lashings for the fourth time. She'd been doing that all morning. Not because the knots were wrong. Because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

"My grandmother's candlesticks are in there," she said, without looking up. "I know they're hers. There's a maker's mark under the base — a wren in a circle. Every household in the Vessin quarter had them cast the same year, from the same smith, when the treaty said we couldn't own anything anymore so we owned small things instead." She tightened a strap that didn't need it. "Forty years in the mud, and the wren's still sharp."

"We'll get them there," Bram said.

"Don't promise things about objects," Nen said. "Promise things about people. Objects float." She stood, shouldered her pole end, and looked at him with the flat, level gaze she used when she was about to say something true. "People sink. Walk carefully."

* * *

The country did not want them in it. That was the honest way to put it, and Bram thought in honest terms now; five years of dredging other people's rivers had burned the romance out of him, and what grew back in its place was a kind of attention that noticed everything and trusted nothing.

The ridge country above the Vessa had been farmland once. You could see it in the bones of the place — fence posts rotted to stubs, an orchard gone feral and sour, a wellhead with a carved lintel whose inscription someone had chiseled off but not deeply enough. The treaty hadn't just unpersoned the Vessians. It had unpersoned the *ground*. Maps showed this as waste commons, good for nothing, belonging to no one, which legally meant it belonged to whoever could hold it, which meant it belonged to men with guns, which meant Corvane's last gunboat was somewhere behind them on the river, and its captain would not be limited to water.

They walked in a staggered line through the dead orchards, and the silence had texture. Not empty silence. *Watched* silence.

"There," Odile said quietly, near midday. She nodded at a fold in the hillside where a farmhouse foundation sat in nettles, and on the foundation, fresh-cut, a mark: three horizontal strokes over a wren.

Nen stopped walking. "That's a waymark. That's a *Vessian* waymark." Her voice did something complicated. "Someone cut that recently. The wood's pale."

"The elders sent scouts," Bram said. "After we sent word from Gray Bend."

"After you traded a forged map for my life and half the crew stopped speaking to you, you mean," Odile said. It wasn't cruel. It was just accurate, the way she was accurate about everything, including tides. "And yet here we all are, carrying your treasure up your mountain."

"It's not mine."

"No," she agreed. "It's worse than yours. It's everyone's. That's why it's heavy." She adjusted her pack straps. "For what it's worth — and I know what it's worth, I did the math while Rehm's people were deciding whether to shoot me — I think you'd have traded the wreck for me. I watched your face do it. And then I watched you cheat instead, which is the better instinct. Cheating is a form of care, Bram. Remember that about yourself on the bad nights."

He didn't answer. He walked. But he carried it with him the rest of the day, the way you carry a smooth stone in your pocket without deciding to.

* * *

Tack collapsed at the second mile marker — a leaning stone that might once have been a boundary post and was now just a stone with opinions — and refused, absolutely refused, to be carried.

"I'm fine," she said, from the ground.

"You're gray," Bram said.

"I'm *efficient*. Gray is a color of efficiency." She tried to stand, got halfway, and let him take her weight without ever quite admitting she was doing so. "Knife wound's nothing. It's the fever underneath it that's rude. River water in a cut, you know how it goes."

"I know exactly how it goes. I've pulled three bodies out of rivers that started as cuts."

"Well, aren't you a delight at parties." She leaned on him, breathing carefully. "Listen. If I slow the crew down, leave me at the next shelter with the rifle and the good attitude. I'll catch up."

"You will not catch up. Nobody catches up. This isn't a race we can lose pieces of."

"Bram." She turned her head and looked at him, and beneath the flippancy there was something older and more tired. "I've been cargo before. On boats, in holds, in a magistrate's cart once, whole other story. Being cargo is the worst thing a person can be. Don't make me cargo."

So they didn't. They redistributed instead — Tack's share of the treasury frame split among the others, her rifle given to Odile, who held it like a dead fish and was told by three separate people never to fire it unless the situation improved dramatically. Tack walked. Slower, grayer, but walking, and every time the line paused she waved them on with her good arm and a word that was mostly vowels.

It was Nen who stayed back with her, matching her pace without being asked, talking the whole time — about the wren mark, about the archive she'd been expelled from, about a cat she'd owned at nineteen named after a treaty clause she'd hated. Bram, hauling his frame two switchbacks above, heard fragments of it drifting up the hill, and understood that Nen was doing medicine. The kind that doesn't come in bottles.

* * *

The rain came at dusk, because of course it did, because the country wanted nothing found in it and rain was simply the country's politest method of saying so.

They sheltered in the ruin of a granary — walls standing to shoulder height, roof long gone, replaced by Odile's tarp and a great deal of arguing about drainage. They ate hard bread and river fish Tack insisted on calling "trout" despite its obvious personal history of disappointment. The treasury frames sat in a ring around them like sleeping animals.

Bram took first watch, sitting on a fallen lintel with the registry pack between his boots, listening to the rain work the nettles.

Nen couldn't sleep either. He heard her moving, and then she was beside him on the lintel, wrapped in everything she owned, holding two cups of something hot that tasted like bark and optimism.

"My family were record-keepers too," she said. "Before. Did you know that? The Vane line kept the civil registries — that's in Elias's journal, page sixty-something, he was very thorough about his own pedigree, which I found funny given how thoroughly he wasn't thorough about being your father." She blew on her cup. "My line kept the tax rolls. Less romantic. More honest, arguably. Every nation is built on somebody writing down what somebody else owes."

"Elias was good at writing things down," Bram said. "Just not at saying them."

"Yes. I've read the journal. All of it, twice." She glanced at him sideways. "There's a letter in the back pocket of the field case. Unsent. Addressed to you."

"I know. I haven't read it."

"Why not?"

"Because I've spent five years being angry at him, and anger is load-bearing. You start pulling it out and you don't know what the building does." He turned the cup in his hands. "After Karst Hollow. After the sixth tide. When there's nothing left to carry."

Nen considered this with the seriousness she gave everything, even cats and tax rolls. "My mother used to say grief keeps better than bread but worse than wine," she said. "Meaning: don't store it too long, but don't drink it all at once either." She stood, pressed her empty cup into his hand like a duty, and added, "Page sixty-one. He writes that he named you Bram because it was the only name in the family that had never signed anything. He thought you might want a name with clean paper." A pause. "He was wrong about a lot of things. Not that one, maybe."

She went back to Tack's side. Bram sat with the rain and the registry and the strange new weight in his chest, which was not the pack, and was heavier.

* * *

They walked through the second night, because the tide tables did not negotiate and neither, it turned out, did Odile Sarn.

By the third dawn the country changed. The nettles gave way to terraces — old ones, hand-built, still holding their shape against forty years of neglect, and on the terraces, gardens. Actual gardens. Beans, squash, a row of fruit trees pruned recently enough that the cuts shone white. Smoke rose from a hollow ahead, thin and domestic, and a dog began barking in the flat, businesslike way of a dog that has decided these strangers are a problem for other people to solve.

Karst Hollow.

They came down the last slope in a ragged line — six carriers, one wounded smuggler propped between Nen and Odile, one hydrographer carrying a rifle she had heroically failed to fire, and Bram at the front with the registry on his back, walking like a man whose legs had filed a formal complaint.

The elders met them at the first terrace. Five of them, old and straight-backed, dressed in the muted clothes of people who had learned not to be seen, and the eldest — a woman with a face like a riverbed, all layers and patience — looked at the frames, at the pack, at Bram, and said nothing for a long moment.

Then Nen set down her pole, walked forward, and lifted the corner of the nearest frame's oilcloth. The wren candlestick caught the early light.

The eldest woman made a sound Bram had never heard a person make. It was not a word in any language. It was the sound of a door opening in a house that had been sealed for forty years, and it undid the whole line of elders at once — they moved toward the frames like water finding its level, touching the metal, touching each other, and one of the old men sat down right there in the dirt of the terrace and wept without any noise at all, which was somehow worse and better than noise.

Bram stood apart and let them. Some deliveries you don't narrate.

But the eldest woman — Maren, they called her, Elder Maren — came to him last, when the others had begun the careful, reverent work of unpacking. She looked up at him, and she was small, and her eyes were not.

"You're the Vane boy," she said. "Elias's son."

"Heir," Bram said. "Apparently. I'm still getting used to the word."

"He came here once. Twenty years ago. Asked us to take the registry back, if he could ever bring it home." Her mouth twisted. "We told him no. We were afraid. We're still afraid — the treaty government hasn't forgotten where we live, and now they'll have a gunboat's worth of reasons to remember." She looked past him, down the slope, toward the distant glitter of the river. "You've brought us a gift with teeth, digger."

"I know," Bram said. "That's why I'm not staying for the ceremony."

Odile appeared at his elbow, chart case under her arm, already doing math with her eyes. "Fifth tide turns in nine hours," she said. "The wreck exposes at low water for the last time tomorrow. If Corvane means to strip the hold, they'll do it then — unless there's something at that wreck worth more to them than bronze." She looked at Bram. "You have that look. The one where you decide something terrible and call it a plan."

"The registry's safe," Bram said. "The testimony isn't. Elias's full account — the treaty's architects, the signatures, the payments — it's still in the inner chamber of the chest, and the chest is still on the barge at the wreck site, because we couldn't carry everything and I chose wrong." He rolled his shoulders, which cracked like green wood. "Rehm's gunboat will be there. So will I. Somebody has to read those names out loud where witnesses can hear them, and it can't wait for a court that doesn't exist yet."

Maren studied him for a long moment. Then she turned and shouted something in Vessian — a language Bram had never heard spoken aloud, only seen in ink — and the elders began moving with sudden purpose, and children appeared from the hollow as if summoned, and someone brought out ropes and a mule with a philosophical expression.

"You'll not walk to that river alone," Maren said. "You'll walk with us. If the truth is going to be spoken at that waterline, it will be spoken by Vessian mouths, with Vessian witnesses, and any gunboat that wants to stop it will have to do it in front of the people it erased." Something fierce and old moved across her face. "Let them explain *that* to their employers."

Bram looked at the mule. The mule looked at Bram with deep, mutual misgiving.

"Tack stays," he said. "Fevered, armed, and in charge of the treasury until we're back. Odile — tide tables. Nen—" He stopped. Nen was already packing the registry ledgers into a satchel, her jaw set in a way he recognized now, having seen it in a mirror his whole life without knowing whose face he was copying.

"Nen's coming," Nen said. "Obviously."

The fifth tide was turning. Somewhere downstream, the river was beginning to fall away from the Meridian's ribs for the last time, and Ilsa Rehm's gunboat was falling with it, and eleven miles of hostile country lay between the truth and the water that had buried it.

Bram took the first step, and the Hollow walked with him.
