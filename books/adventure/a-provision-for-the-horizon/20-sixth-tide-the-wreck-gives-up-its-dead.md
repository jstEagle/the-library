## 20. Sixth Tide: The Wreck Gives Up Its Dead

The river began to give the Meridian back at dawn.

Bram stood on the mudflat that had been, six tides ago, four fathoms of brown water, and watched the wreck emerge the way a body emerges from a long illness — rib by rib, spar by spar, the great black spine of her hull rising out of the silt like something exhaling. The dry season had done in five days what forty years had refused to do. The Meridian was coming up for air.

"Last one," Tack said beside him. She had a fresh bandage on her forearm and a rifle slung across her back with the casualness of a woman who had stopped pretending she wasn't going to use it. "Tide after this, she's mud again until next year. And we won't be here to see it."

"No," Bram said. "We won't."

Behind them, strung along the levee road in a ragged line two miles long, came Karst Hollow.

He had never seen anything like it and suspected he never would again. The elders had come down from the hills overnight — old men in funeral coats, old women with their hair bound in white, children carried on hips because no one would leave them behind, not for this. They brought lanterns though it was morning. They brought paper and ink. Nen walked among them carrying the registry's iron case against her chest like an infant, and every few steps someone touched her shoulder, or the case, or simply reached out and let their fingers trail through the air near her as if proximity itself were a kind of prayer.

"They're singing," Odile said quietly. She stood at Bram's left, her survey instruments abandoned in the boat behind them because today there was nothing left to measure. "Do you hear it? That's the Vessian mourning cadence. My grandmother used it for funerals." A pause. "I didn't know I remembered it until just now."

The song was low and wordless and it moved down the levee like weather. Bram felt it in his sternum.

Downriver, half a mile off, the Corvane gunboats sat at anchor in the deep channel — three of them now, black-hulled, their deck guns trained not on the wreck but on the shore. On the people. Rehm's flagship flew no colors this morning. Bram had been watching her through Odile's glass since first light, and he had seen her crew muster twice and stand down twice, and he had seen her standing alone at the rail for a very long time, looking at the line of mourners coming down from the hills, and he did not know what he was looking at.

"You think she'll fire?" Tack asked.

"I think she's spent three days deciding who she works for," Bram said. "And I don't think it's Corvane anymore. I think she just hasn't told Corvane yet."

* * *

They gathered at the wreck's exposed hold as the tide bottomed out.

It was strange, Bram thought, how ordinary it looked. A hole in the world, timber-lined, half-choked with river clay. The treasury chests they'd already raised sat in a row on the flat, roped and sealed, stamped with the bronze sunburst of Vessia. The registry case sat apart from them, on a crate Nen had chosen herself, facing the water.

The elders formed a crescent around it. There were perhaps two hundred of them now, and more still coming down the road, and Bram understood suddenly why the treaty government had wanted the registry drowned rather than destroyed. You could burn a book in secret. But these people had spent forty years being told, politely, officially, in every document that governed their lives, that they had never existed. And here was proof, in government ink, in civil hand, in eleven hundred pages of names — that they had existed, all of them, and someone had known it, and written it down, and hidden it where only a dying river could keep the secret.

Elder Maren Solm — ninety-one years old, hands like tree roots, the woman who had refused the registry in her own kitchen three days ago — stood at the front of the crescent. She looked at the case for a long time.

"When you brought this to us," she said, not loudly, but the mourning-song had stopped and every word carried over the flat, "we said no. We said: to take it is to admit it was taken. To sign our names to restitution is to sign our names to the theft first." She turned. Her eyes found Bram. "Why have you come anyway, digger?"

"Because I read the first page," Bram said.

"And?"

"And it isn't a list." He took a breath. He had rehearsed nothing; he had only read it, once, alone, at midnight, and then sat in the dark for an hour unable to move. "It's a census. It's not what Vessia lost. It's what Vessia *was*. Every name has a trade beside it. Every household. Every child's birth year. It wasn't written to be evidence. It was written because a nation keeps its own count, and nobody asked permission before they erased it." He looked at the elders, at the children on their hips, at Nen standing with her jaw tight and her eyes bright. "You said accepting it means acknowledging the theft. Maybe. But refusing it means letting them finish the job. Right now, the only people who know those names are dead men and a salvage crew. When this tide comes back, so does the river. This is the last low water. If the names go under unspoken, they stay under."

Silence. Somewhere out in the channel, water chuckled against the hull of a gunboat.

Maren Solm looked at him for a long moment, and then something in her face shifted — not softening exactly; more like a door that had been barred for forty years swinging open onto a room someone had kept dusted the whole time.

"My mother's name is in that book," she said. "Third page. Sera Solm, weaver. I was seven when the treaty men came through and told us she had never been born." She stepped forward and laid her root-hard hand flat on the iron case. "Open it."

* * *

Nen opened the case with the blood token.

That had been its own argument, settled at dawn: the ring belonged to the record-keepers' line, and the record-keepers' charge, Elias Vane's journal said in a passage Bram had read a dozen times now, was that *the count be spoken where it can be heard.* So Nen held the case, and Bram wore the ring, and when the inner lock tasted the signet's seal it gave way with a sound like a held breath released.

He lifted out the first page. The paper was forty years old and river-dried and it trembled in his hands, and he let it.

Then he turned to face the river.

Because that was the thing he had decided in the night, lying awake while Tack re-bandaged her arm and Odile recalculated the tide tables for the fourth time: it was not enough to open the case. Documents could be seized. Chests could be impounded. Courts could be bought — had been bought, forty years ago, by the architects of the treaty whose names were written in his father's testimony in a hand that shook with fury. Paper was exactly as strong as the power behind it.

But a voice was different. A voice, spoken aloud, in front of witnesses from every side, could not be unspoken. His father had understood that. It was why Elias Vane had spent twenty years documenting instead of publishing — waiting, Bram finally understood, for a moment when the truth could be spoken into a crowd too large to silence. His father had died waiting. His son would not.

"Corvane!" he shouted across the water. "Captain Rehm! Your client sent you to bury this! Come and hear what you were paid to drown!"

For a long moment, nothing. Then, on the lead gunboat, a launch dropped into the water. It came across the channel fast, riding low with armed men, and beached thirty yards from the crescent of elders, and out of it climbed a man Bram had never seen but knew instantly from the paymaster's confession — Undersecretary Doss of the Treaty Provisional Authority, gray-suited, gray-faced, with the look of a man who had come to supervise a burial and found a congregation instead.

"This is a restricted salvage operation," Doss called. "Under the terms of the River Compact—"

"The River Compact," Bram said, and his voice cracked across the flat like a whip, "was signed by nine men. Eight of them are named in the testimony I am holding, along with the sums each was paid, the accounts it moved through, and the order — signed, dated, countersigned — directing that the passenger manifest and civil registry of the steamship *Meridian* be sealed and the vessel scuttled with all records aboard." He let that land. He watched Doss's face do arithmetic. "Forty years ago your government murdered a shipload of clerks to erase a nation's name. Yesterday your client tried to murder mine. I have the paymaster's sworn statement and I have the man himself, and both of them are going to survive this morning, which is more than can be said for your career."

"You have nothing," Doss said. "A thief's papers. A dead man's fantasies. This matter will be resolved through proper channels—"

"Then let's resolve it properly," Bram said. "In front of everyone. Since everyone's here."

And he began to read.

* * *

"*Sera Solm, weaver, born in the Year of the Double Harvest. Her daughter, Maren.*"

The old woman made a sound that was not quite a word.

"*Ondrek Vail, ferryman. Ilse Vail, his wife, midwife, who attended four hundred births and is recorded in the parish rolls of Karst Hollow under a name the treaty says does not exist.*"

An old man in a funeral coat sank to his knees in the mud, and the people on either side of him held him up.

"*The family Nen. Archivists to the Vessian crown for six generations. Last entry: a daughter, born the winter of the treaty, registered in secret by her grandfather, who wrote beneath her name: 'She will ask who she is. Let this answer her.'*"

Nen did not cry. Bram had expected her to cry. Instead she stood straighter than he had ever seen her, both hands pressed flat over the iron case, and she mouthed along with the words because — he realized — she had memorized them. She had found her own page days ago and had carried it inside her ever since, unsaid, and now it was being said, aloud, into the air, where it could not be taken back.

He read for an hour. Names, trades, births, the small marginal notes of a clerk who had loved his work — *a good humor, this one; mends nets faster than her father* — and the crowd on the levee grew and grew until the road itself was full, and the mourning-song started up again underneath his voice, and somewhere in the middle of it Bram understood that his voice didn't matter anymore. The names were carrying themselves. He was just the bell they rang in.

Doss stood on the mudflat the entire hour. Bram watched him calculate, watch him glance at the gunboats, watch him signal — twice — and watch the gunboats not answer.

* * *

On the deck of the *Corvane Pride*, Ilsa Rehm listened to a dead nation introduce itself, and made her decision.

It was not a hard decision, which surprised her. She had expected it to cost her everything and she had braced for the pain the way she braced for storms, and instead what she felt was relief — the specific, shameful relief of a woman setting down a weight she'd convinced herself was structural.

Twenty-six years in salvage. Twenty-six years of telling herself the law was the law, that possession was nine-tenths and the tenth tenth went to whoever could afford the lawyers, that a woman with no country and no family and no name anyone respected could still build something honest out of other people's wrecks. And every year the honest part got thinner and the other people's part got bigger, until here she was, anchored off a mudflat, under contract to help a government prove that two hundred living human beings had never been born.

Her radio crackled. Doss, for the third time. *"Rehm. Fire on my mark. Clear the shore."*

She picked up the handset. She thought about her first command, a leaky dredge barge, and the old hydrographer who had taught her to read water — not Odile Sarn, another one, dead now, who had told her: *the river always gives back what it's owed, girl. Your job is just to not be standing where it lands.*

"Negative," she said.

*"Rehm—"*

"You heard the names, Undersecretary. All of us heard the names. Two hundred witnesses on that shore, my whole crew on this deck, and every wireless operator within fifty miles listening to me right now, because yes, I left the channel open, and yes, I'm stupid enough to say the rest of this out loud." She looked down the deck of her flagship, at her gun crews, at her first mate, at forty faces that had followed her through twenty years of gray water — and not one of them looked away from her. "Corvane Salvage Fleet stands down effective immediately. Our contract with the Provisional Authority is void. Our guns—" she paused, and felt the strangest smile arrive on her face, "—our guns are now pointed at whoever tries to make this morning unhappen. Starting, if necessary, with you."

The silence on the wire lasted four full seconds.

*"You're finished,"* Doss said. *"You understand that. There will be no fleet, no license, no—"* and then his voice changed, because he had turned, on the mudflat, and seen what she had seen: the second launch leaving the *Pride*, and the third, and Corvane's own boarding parties wading ashore — not to fight, but to witness. Her own people, choosing.

"I know," Rehm said, and cut the wire.

* * *

It was Tack who saw the last of it coming, because Tack always saw the last of things coming.

"Bram." She caught his sleeve mid-sentence, between one name and the next. "Boat. Upstream. Fast, and flying no flag, and packed with men who walk like soldiers."

Bram turned. A gray launch was cutting down the channel at speed, and even at this distance he could see it was not Corvane — the men were wrong, too disciplined, too quiet, rifles held with the economy of professionals. The treaty government's own hand, arriving late, arriving deniable, arriving to do with bullets what documents could no longer do.

"How long?" Odile asked, already reading the current.

"Four minutes," Tack said. "Maybe less."

The elders had not stopped singing. Maren Solm looked at the launch, and then at Bram, and her face was calm in a way that frightened him more than the guns.

"Keep reading," she said.

So he kept reading. His voice did not shake, which later seemed impossible to him. Around him the crew moved — Tack and the Corvane boarding party forming a line on the flat with a coordination no one had ordered, Odile positioning the elders' boats between the launch and the shore, Nen holding the case high above her head so that whatever happened, the names would be visible. And down the channel, slow and deliberate and unmistakable, the guns of the *Corvane Pride* traversed to cover the water between the gray launch and the mudflat.

The launch slowed. Hung in the current. Considered.

Two hundred voices sang. One voice read. Three gunboats held their breath.

And the gray launch, finding itself — for the first time in forty years — outnumbered by the truth, put about and ran.

* * *

They finished the registry as the tide turned.

Bram read the last name — the clerk's own, entered last of all, with the note *recorded by his own hand, and glad of the work* — and closed the book, and the water was already sliding back up the mudflat, licking at the Meridian's ribs, taking her home. But it didn't matter anymore. The names were out. They were in two hundred memories and one open wireless channel and the sworn statements of a paymaster and a captain, and no tide in the world could reach them there.

Maren Solm took the book from his hands and held it against her chest, and the old woman who had refused it in her own kitchen wept without any shame at all.

"Well, digger," she said, when she could speak. "Now we find out whether the law is worth anything."

Down the channel, Ilsa Rehm's launch was coming ashore, and she was carrying a ledger under her arm — the real one, the contracts, the payments, the orders — and she was walking toward the elders with the expression of a woman about to testify against everything she had ever built.

Bram looked at Tack. Tack looked at Odile. Odile, for the first time in six tides, smiled.

"No," Bram said. "Now we find out what we can build instead."
