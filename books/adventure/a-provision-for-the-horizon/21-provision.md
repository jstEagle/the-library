## 21. Provision

The negotiations were held in the granary at Karst Hollow, because it was the largest roofed building in the settlement and because, as Elder Maren put it, "if they're going to take things from us again, let them do it where we store our grain, so they understand what kind of people they're dealing with."

Bram had expected tents. He had expected a river barge fitted out with flags, or a government steamer with a delegation in pressed linen. What arrived instead was a single launch flying no colors at all, carrying three officials who introduced themselves by titles so long that Tack, leaning against the granary door, whispered, "I've smuggled cargo with shorter names than that man's job."

The senior of them was a thin woman named Undersecretary Halven, whose face had the particular grayness of someone who had spent the last nine days reading documents she could not un-read. Bram recognized the look. He'd worn it himself, standing in his father's house with the testimony spread across the floor.

"Mr. Vane," she said, extending a hand.

"Bram," he said, and did not correct her further. It was strange, how easily the name sat now. A year ago — even a month ago — he would have said *just Vane*, or nothing at all, and let people assume what they liked. Now he found he wanted the whole of it, father and all, like a tool you'd refused to carry and then discovered was exactly the right weight.

They sat at trestle tables under the granary's high rafters, between sacks of millet and hanging bundles of dried peppers, and for three days they argued about what justice looked like when it arrived forty years late and slightly water-damaged.

* * *

Nen conducted most of the talking on the Hollow's side, which surprised no one who had watched her work. She had spent the first two days cataloguing the registry — every name copied twice, every page photographed by Odile's camera rig from four angles — and she came to the table each morning with the calm of a person who knew exactly how many souls she was speaking for. Fourteen thousand, six hundred and eleven. She had counted. She counted them aloud once, early on, when an official suggested the registry might be "a matter better handled through archival channels."

She counted until he asked her to stop.

"It's not a matter," Nen said afterward, packing away her ledger with careful hands. "It's a census. You don't file a census. You read it."

The treaty government, to its credit — or its terror; Bram never fully settled which — did not fight the central claim. They couldn't. Elias Vane's testimony named the architects of the Vessian dissolution in their own handwriting, with their own signatures, alongside requisition orders for the treasury that had been signed, stamped, and filed as though theft were simply a form of weather. There is a particular silence that falls over powerful men when they realize the paper trail runs both directions. Bram watched it fall across the table on the second day, when Halven's junior counsel turned a page and went very still.

"The restitution framework," Halven began, on the third morning, "would vest title to the recovered treasury and the civil registry in—"

"In the heirs," Nen said. "In Karst Hollow and the diaspora settlements. In the people named in the registry and their descendants. Not in a trust administered by your ministry. Not in escrow. In them."

"There are questions of capacity—"

"We have capacity." Elder Maren spoke from the far end of the table, where she had sat silently through two days of procedural fencing, shelling sunflower seeds into a bowl with the patience of a woman who had outlasted worse than lawyers. "We buried this nation once already. We can be trusted to carry it."

Halven looked down at her papers. When she looked up, something in her face had shifted — the grayness receding, replaced by something almost like relief. "Then we'll draft it that way," she said. "Provision One: full legal recognition of the registry as the civil record of Vessia, retroactive to the date of dissolution. Provision Two: unconditional transfer of the treasury and all recovered materials to the heirs' council at Karst Hollow. Provision Three—" and here she paused, and Bram saw her choose her words the way a diver chooses a route through wreckage, "—a public inquiry into the circumstances of the treaty's execution, including the death of Elias Vane."

The granary went quiet. Outside, the river ran low and brown over the shoals, doing its slow arithmetic toward the dry season.

"He died before any of this," Bram said finally. "He never saw one name restored."

"No," Halven agreed. "But the record will show he was right. That's not nothing. I've been in this ministry twenty years, Mr. Vane. Being right, on the record, is nearly the only thing that lasts."

* * *

Rehm testified on the fourth day.

She arrived under escort — her own choice, Odile explained; surrendering voluntarily played better than being taken, and Rehm understood optics the way Tack understood currents. She wore her fleet coat without insignia. Corvane's remaining officers had stripped her rank the moment the paymaster's ledgers surfaced, but she wore the coat anyway, and when Bram asked her about it later she shrugged and said, "It's a good coat. The company doesn't get my clothes too."

Her testimony took six hours. She gave the contract numbers, the payment schedules, the standing order regarding the registry — *recover or destroy, client's discretion* — and the name of the man who had signed off on Elias Vane's death. She gave it flat and complete, without ornament, and Bram noticed that she never once looked at him while she did it. He understood why. Pity would have been unbearable, and apology would have been insufficient, and Rehm was constitutionally incapable of offering either. So she offered facts instead, laid out like salvage on a deck, and let the tribunal make of them what it would.

Afterward, in the yard behind the granary, she stood smoking and watching the light go off the water.

"You could have kept the head start quiet," Bram said. "One tide. Nobody would ever have known you gave it to us."

"Nobody would have known I betrayed a paying client, you mean." She exhaled smoke. "I built Corvane's river division out of nothing, Vane. Fifteen years. Boats, crews, contracts. And it turns out the whole edifice was sitting on top of a murdered surveyor and a stolen country, and every coin that paid for my boats passed through that." She tapped ash into the dirt. "You spend long enough hauling things up out of rivers, you learn the difference between salvage and looting. I told myself I knew the difference." She looked at him then, and her eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "Turns out I just hadn't gone deep enough."

"What happens to you now?"

"Testimony for immunity on the diving crew. The rest of it — the fleet, the contracts — that's gone regardless." She shrugged. "I'm told I may face charges in two jurisdictions. My lawyer says I have a sympathetic narrative." Her mouth twisted. "First time in my life anyone's called me sympathetic. I don't recommend it."

"Tack says you run a fair tide," Bram said. "For what it's worth. From a man who's been shot at by your divers."

Rehm considered this. "Tell him his night run past the Gray Bend checkpoint was the best piloting I've seen in twenty years, and if he's ever looking for work after the trials—" She stopped herself, laughed shortly. "No. Bad habit. Everything's a contract if you say it fast enough." She dropped the cigarette and ground it out. "Good luck with your nation, Vane."

"It's not mine."

"No," she agreed. "That's rather the point of you, isn't it?"

* * *

Corvane collapsed the way large dishonest things collapse: slowly, then all at once. First the clients withdrew — the treaty government's salvage authorization revoked within a day of Halven's inquiry announcement, followed by two mining concerns and a shipping consortium who all expressed being *deeply unaware* of the nature of Corvane's river operations, in language so identical that Odile suspected a shared law firm. Then the creditors. Then the receivers.

Tack tracked the whole decline with the avidity of a man watching a rival's boat take on water. "Third quarterlies are out," he announced one evening, arriving at Nen's worktable with a newspaper folded to the financial page. "'Corvane Maritime Group enters voluntary administration.' Voluntary! Like they chose it. Like it wasn't us."

"Us and a dead man's paperwork," Nen said, not looking up from the registry page she was conserving — a water-swollen leaf bearing nine hundred names, which she was drying with sand and patience.

"Mostly the paperwork." Tack set the paper down. "You know what I keep thinking about? That paymaster's ledger. All those entries. *Disbursement, Meridian operation. Disbursement, site security.* And one line item, small, buried in the middle — *contingency, E.V.* Contingency. Like your father was a weather delay."

Bram, mending a net by the stove — a skill he'd learned at fourteen and never lost, hands working while the mind wandered — felt the old anger rise and, for the first time, pass through without catching fire. "He was a line item to them," he said. "He's a provision in a treaty now. Provision Four. I checked."

"*Recognition of the documentary contributions of Elias Vane,*" Nen quoted softly. "Posthumous. Unanimous."

"He'd have hated it," Bram said, and found he was smiling. "All those years signing nothing. Refusing credit on his own surveys. And now it's in an annex."

"He'd have hated it," Nen agreed, "and kept the clipping."

* * *

The reward question arrived on the last day, riding in with the final session, and it arrived the way Bram had known it would: wrapped in respectability, offered with genuine warmth, and designed to make refusal difficult.

The treasury alone — gold coin, plate, the ceremonial regalia of a court that had governed a river valley for six centuries — was valued, in the government's own conservative estimate, at a sum with seven zeroes. Standard practice in such recoveries allotted a finder's percentage. Ten percent was mentioned. Five percent was mentioned more firmly. Elder Maren, to her eternal credit, mentioned neither, and watched Bram with an expression of polite curiosity, as though the answer were his to give and hers merely to witness.

"Mr. Vane," Halven said, "the heirs' council has authorized me to offer—"

"Don't," Bram said.

Halven stopped.

"It's not false modesty, and I'm not going to perform reluctance while you talk me into it." He set down the pen he'd been holding — he'd been taking notes, actually notes, in his father's shorthand, which he was teaching himself from the journals, and which made him feel closer to the old man than any portrait could. "I dug for hire for eleven years. I know what money does to a find. It makes everything after it about keeping the find. I've got a registry to help conserve, an archive to help build, and a father whose reputation needs rebuilding from the ground up. None of that gets easier with ten percent. Most of it gets harder."

"The council anticipated you might say that," Halven said carefully. "There is also the question of standing. Legal recognition. With your evidence and Ms. Rehm's testimony, you'd have a strong claim to appointment as—"

"As what? Warden of the Vessian memory?" Bram shook his head. "No. Thank you. Genuinely." He meant it, which surprised him. There had been a time when standing — papers, legitimacy, a name that opened doors — was the entire hunger. But he'd watched Nen work these past weeks, a disgraced archivist with no nation and no salary, and seen that she had something better than standing: she had purpose, and a table to work at, and people around her who knew what her work cost. Standing was what other people gave you. Purpose was what you dug for yourself.

"I'll tell you what I want," he said. "One line. In the record. Wherever the final register of this restitution gets kept — I want it to read that the recovery of the Meridian materials was witnessed by Bram Vane, son of Elias Vane, digger." He paused. "Not finder. Witness. The finding was six tides of luck and a crew of people the law forgot. The witnessing is the part I'll stand behind for the rest of my life."

Halven wrote it down verbatim. At the far end of the table, Maren cracked a sunflower seed with great satisfaction, as though a particularly stubborn hull had finally given way.

"That's it?" Tack demanded later, on the walk down to the water. "Seven zeroes on the table and you asked for a *sentence*?"

"A sentence in a permanent record," Bram corrected. "Do you know how rare that is? Empires fall, Tack. Ink outlasts them."

"Ink doesn't buy a boat."

"I don't need a boat. I need a chair, a lamp, and a shelf." He grinned. "And apparently a classroom, because Maren's decided I'm teaching survey methods to the Hollow's kids this winter, and arguing with Maren has a survival rate I'm not willing to test."

Tack snorted. "You're going to be terrible at it. You'll have them dredging the millpond inside a week."

"Probably."

"And you'll love it."

Bram didn't answer right away. The river lay low and golden in the evening light, the wreck's ribs just breaking the surface downstream, exposed now permanently — the government had funded a coffer dam, Provision Seven, so that the Meridian could be studied rather than drowned again. People walked out to it at low water, children mostly, touching the timbers of a ship they'd only heard about in forbidden stories.

"Yeah," he said. "I probably will."

* * *

The signing happened at dusk, on the granary floor, with the chest itself open between the parties — the inner chamber empty now except for the velvet impressions where Elias's testimony had lain, and the outer compartments holding the first of the returned regalia, a crown of river-pearl that Nen had identified as the work of the Vessian record-keepers' guild. Her guild. Her family's hands had made it, generations back. She'd held it for a long time the night it came out of the chest, and then put it on a stand and gotten back to work, which Bram thought was the most Vessian thing he'd ever seen.

Halven signed for the government. Maren signed for the heirs' council, and then passed the pen to Nen, who signed as Keeper of the Registry, a title invented that afternoon and likely to outlive everyone in the room. Odile signed as hydrographer of record. Tack signed — after reading every word, twice, and negotiating one comma — as pilot of the recovery, and then, unable to help himself, added *best pilot of the recovery* in the margin, which Halven, to her credit, allowed to stand.

Then Halven held out the pen to Bram, indicating the witnesses' line.

He took it. He looked at the blank space for a moment longer than anyone understood — except perhaps Nen, who had stopped sorting pages to watch, and Odile, who had known him since he was a boy dragging survey chains along riverbanks and pretending not to care whose son he was.

*Bram Vane.*

Two words. His father's name and his own, joined by nothing but ink, needing nothing else. He wrote them the way his father had written his field notes — clear, unhurried, built to last.

"There," Maren said, into the quiet. "Now it's law."

Outside, the sixth tide had come and gone weeks ago, and the river had settled into its dry-season bed, shallow and honest, hiding nothing. Somewhere upstream, past the shoals and the old checkpoints, the country that had tried to erase this place was learning to say its name again — clumsily, in official pronouncements, getting the vowels wrong at first. Nen said the vowels would come right eventually. Names usually did, if enough people insisted on them.

Bram rolled up the register, helped stack the tables, and blew out the lamps. Tomorrow there would be conservation training, and a letter to answer from a university museum wanting to "advise" on the collection (Maren had already drafted a reply consisting of the word *no*, elaborately punctuated), and a winter of teaching ahead, and a certain unsent letter in his father's hand that he had carried unopened from the riverside house to the wreck and back again, waiting for a quiet that had, until now, never quite arrived.

Tonight, walking back along the levee under a sky rinsed clean, he decided tomorrow was soon enough.

The letter could wait one more night. For the first time in his life, Bram Vane wasn't going anywhere — and the horizon, patient as a river, would keep.
