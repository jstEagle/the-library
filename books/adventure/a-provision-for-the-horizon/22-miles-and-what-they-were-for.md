## 22. Miles, and What They Were For

The rain came to Karst Hollow the way Nen said it always had, sideways and apologetic, sliding off the ridge in gray sheets and pooling in the old market squares that the treaty maps still called *unincorporated lowland*. Bram had learned to love it, which surprised him more than anything else about the year. Rain meant the river was fat. Rain meant the wreck of the Meridian sat under six fathoms of honest water instead of drying out like a fish on a slab. Rain meant nobody was digging anywhere, including him, and he was discovering — slowly, the way a man discovers a splinter worked loose — that he did not mind.

"Vane." A knock at the archive door, then Tack's head around it without waiting, because Tack had never once waited for permission in her life. "You're wanted in the reading room. The elders are arguing about the shelving again."

"Again?"

"They've been at it since breakfast. Old Sef wants the registry alphabetized by district. Marta wants it by date of registration. And Yeva says — and I quote — *names don't have an order, that's the whole point.*" Tack leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, grinning. "They asked for you specifically. I think they want a tiebreaker."

"What makes you think I'm a tiebreaker?"

"Because you're the only person in this building who doesn't have family in the registry, so nobody can accuse you of cheating." She said it lightly, the way she said everything now, the knife scar on her forearm catching the lamplight. The wound had healed crooked. She refused to let anyone call it that. "Also because you're the only one who can lift the crates."

Bram set down his pen. He had been drafting a lesson — third-year students, the module on hydrographic surveying, the chapter titled *How to Read a River That Is Trying to Lie to You* — and he was glad of the interruption, because the chapter was going badly. It turned out that knowing a thing and teaching it were two different trades, and he had spent forty years learning the first and none learning the second.

The reading room of the Karst Hollow archive had been a granary once, then a chapel, then nothing at all for thirty years. Now it held the Vessian civil registry in its long cedar cases, the bronze chest open on a plinth at the room's center like a heart that had finally been allowed out of the body, and — as of three months ago — forty-two students, most of them under fifteen, all of them descendants of people the treaty had tried to erase from the record of the living.

The chest stayed open. That had been the elders' first and only unanimous decision. *A closed chest is just a box,* Yeva had said. *An open one is a promise.* Inside, on a bed of faded velvet, lay the first page of the registry — the names, the districts, the dates — and beside it, under glass now, the testimony of Elias Vane, complete, signed, dated in his small fierce hand.

Bram's father's signature. He still caught himself looking at it. Forty years of a man signing nothing — no letters to his son, no name on the salvage permits, no acknowledgment on the title to the riverside house — and then, at the end, this: *Elias Vane, record-keeper of Vessia, witness.* Two words after his name. *Record-keeper. Witness.* Bram had spent a whole night, early on, trying to decide which one had cost him more to write.

"Vane." Sef, ancient and sharp, waving him over to the argument. "You've surveyed half the rivers on the continent. If you were filing the names of a nation, how would you do it?"

Bram looked at the registry, at the three elders glaring at each other with the particular fury of people who have finally been given something worth fighting over. He thought about it honestly, which was the only way anyone did anything in Karst Hollow anymore. Honesty was the local currency. It had been devalued for so long that now everyone hoarded it.

"By district," he said, "but keep the original order in a copy. The order they were written in is testimony too. Somebody stood at a desk and wrote those names one after another, and the order they chose tells you what they thought mattered."

Silence. Then Yeva, slowly, nodded. "The record-keeper's son," she said, "understands the record-keeper."

"I'm a digger," Bram said, on reflex, the old reflex, and then heard how it sounded, and heard something else underneath it, and let it go. "Both. I'm both."

Marta laughed and wrote the decision in the ledger. Outside, the rain kept up its sideways apology, and somewhere down the lane a child was shouting about a boat.

* * *

The letter had been sitting in the bottom of Bram's kit for a year.

He had found it in the house at Gray Bend, the day after the treaty government's negotiators left and the archive was formally — *legally*, a word that still tasted strange in his mouth — transferred to Karst Hollow. It had been in his father's desk, in a drawer that opened easier than the others because no one had ever opened it, wedged under a false bottom that Bram had found by the simple method of being his father's son and knowing that Elias Vane never trusted a drawer to be a drawer.

The envelope said, in his father's handwriting: *For Bram. If he ever comes.*

Not *if he comes home.* The house had never been home to Bram. His father had known that, and had not pretended otherwise, and somehow that was the kindest thing in the whole envelope before Bram even opened it.

He had not opened it. A year, and he had not opened it. He told himself it was because of the archive, the teaching, the endless small work of a nation reassembling its own name. And that was true. But it was also true that he had spent five years moving — three continents, a hundred contracts, a new camp every season — precisely so that there would never be a quiet evening, never a drawer, never a letter. Movement had been his way of not reading what his father had to say. And now he had stopped moving, and the letter had stopped letting him.

It was Odile who called it, the way she called everything, with hydrographic precision. She had come up from the coast for the autumn session — she taught two weeks a year now, *tides and testimony*, the most oversubscribed course in the settlement, because Odile Sarn could make the behavior of water sound like a moral argument — and she found him on the archive steps at dusk, turning the envelope over in his hands like a man checking a coin for counterfeit.

"You've had that since the house," she said. Not a question.

"A year."

"Six tides was enough time to save a nation. A year is more than enough time to open a letter." She sat down beside him on the step, which for Odile was an act of tremendous tenderness, and looked out at the rain. "You're afraid it will be angry."

"He had a right to be angry. I never answered a single letter he sent. I changed my name on the work permits. I —"

"Bram." She said his name the way she said *flood stage*: a fact, not a judgment. "You were a boy who lost his mother and then lost his father to a locked room and a closed mouth. Anger was the only honest thing either of you had. But you're not boys anymore, either of you, and one of you has run out of time to say what he meant." She stood, brushed off her coat, and paused at the door. "The river drops what it's carried when it's ready. Not before. But it does drop it. I've never known it to hold a thing forever."

She went inside. Bram sat on the steps until the lamps came on down the lane, and then he went up to his room above the archive — a small room, a rented room, the longest he had lived anywhere since he was nineteen — and he opened the letter.

* * *

*Bram,*

*If you're reading this, then either you've forgiven me or you've come to rob me, and knowing you it's both, and knowing you, you'll have picked the lock, which I left deliberately easy. Check the third drawer. No — the real third drawer.*

*You'll want to know why I never told you. I've drafted this letter eleven times and every draft starts with the treaty and it shouldn't. It should start with your mother.*

*She died in the spring, and I went to the funeral of a nation that same autumn, and I could not tell the difference anymore between grief and work, so I did what I know, which is to work. I found the registry. I found the testimony. And then I found out what the men who signed the treaty do to people who find things, and I made a decision that I have hated every single day since, which was that you would be safer hating me than mourning me.*

*I was wrong. I want that in writing, since I've spent my life writing things down. I was wrong. Fear is not a provision. I told myself I was leaving you a provision for the horizon — something to run toward when you had to run — but the truth is I was a coward with a survey kit, and I hid behind the work the way I hid behind the name, and the cost was you.*

*You dug for hire across three continents rather than be my son, and Bram — I don't blame you. I'd have done the same. You were better at being me than I was.*

*The ring is in the chest. The map is in the journal. If you're reading this, the fleet came, or the river dropped, or you simply got tired, and any of those is reason enough. The names are yours. The nation is not mine to give and never was — it belongs to the living — but the names, the keeping of them, that trade runs in our blood further back than I ever understood until I sat in the archive at Vessia and read my own great-grandmother's hand in the margin of the old registry. We were record-keepers, Bram. We kept the names when it was dangerous to keep them. I kept them when it was fatal. You'll keep them when it's merely hard, and that's the whole of what I ever wanted for you: that the work be hard, and honest, and yours.*

*Come to the river in the dry season. I'll show you the wreck myself.*

*Your father,*
*Elias Vane, record-keeper, witness*

*P.S. — You were right about the name. A man should sign his own. But you were wrong about why. It was never vanity. I wore it so that if they came for me, they'd know exactly whose son they were dealing with, and think twice about what else was in the line.*

Bram read it three times. The rain kept on. Somewhere below, the chest sat open on its plinth, and the ring — his ring now, though he wore it on a cord rather than a finger, because it was still half a size too large and he had never gotten around to having it sized, and because Nen said a blood token should touch the skin, and the cord did that well enough — the ring sat in the dark of the cedar case with the names.

He did not forgive his father that night. Forgiveness, he was learning, was not a tide that came all at once; it was more like the river itself, dropping what it carried grain by grain, silt by silt, until one morning the channel had changed and you couldn't say when.

But he sat by the window until the lamps went out, holding the letter, and when he finally slept, he slept the way he had slept as a boy at Gray Bend, before the silence, before the miles: deeply, and without one ear listening for the road.

* * *

Spring came to Karst Hollow the way it came everywhere in the valley — all at once, indecently, the hillsides going green overnight as if someone had spilled the paint. The dry season was over. The Meridian sat under her six fathoms, and the elders had voted, after a year of argument that made the shelving dispute look like a friendly chat, to leave her there. *She's a grave and a vault,* Yeva had said, *and the river is her keeper. We don't need the gold. We have the names.* The gold, what there was of it, had gone into the restitution fund — schools, boats, a proper roof for the archive — and the treaty government, exposed and bleeding, had signed the transfer without a fight, because Ilsa Rehm's testimony had taken the fight out of them like a lance out of a wound.

Rehm herself had come through in the winter. Not to visit — Rehm did not visit, she *passed through*, always with somewhere to be — but she had stood in the reading room for a long while, hat in her hands, looking at the open chest, and she had said only, "Your father would have hated this," and then, when Bram raised an eyebrow, "All this openness. He struck me as a man who liked a locked room." And then she had laughed, once, a real one, and left a donation in the box by the door that was large enough that Sef chased her down the lane to return it, and she refused, and it became, for a while, the best story in the settlement.

Tack ran the supply route upriver now — legitimate, mostly, though her definition of *mostly* had not changed — and she came through every few weeks with paper, ink, lamp oil, and news, and always, without fail, a bag of the terrible dried fish that Bram had never acquired a taste for and ate anyway, because refusing Tack's fish was, she had made clear, a betrayal of everything they had been through.

Nen ran the archive. That had never been voted on; it had simply become true, the way water becomes a channel. She had found her family in the registry — her grandmother, her two aunts, the uncle whose name had been the first thing she'd ever learned to write — and on the anniversary of the reading at the river, she had stood before the open chest with the whole settlement behind her and spoken their names aloud, one by one, into the record, and the record had held them. Bram had stood at the back. He had not spoken. His names were in a different register — the small one, the family one, the one written in a locked drawer in a locked house on a river that had finally, this year, been given back its old Vessian name on the new maps. *The Brightwater Reach,* some of the old folks still called it, and Nen had teased him about it for a month. *Lena Brightwater's reach,* she'd say. *Your mother's river, digger. It kept what it was owed after all.*

On a bright morning in the second spring, Bram stood before his third-year students in the reading room and taught them to read a river that lies.

"The water tells you where the channel is," he said, "but only if you ask it in its own language. A smooth surface lies. A boil tells the truth. A standing wave is a rock introducing itself. And a river that drops something at your feet —" he paused, and forty-two young faces looked up at him, and he found, not for the first time, that he had to take a breath before the next part, "— a river that drops something at your feet is telling you it's been carrying it a long time, and it's ready to set it down."

He dismissed them for the midday meal, and they went out shouting into the light, and Bram stayed behind in the empty reading room with the chest and the registry and his father's testimony under its glass, and he took out the letter — he carried it now, folded, in the inside pocket where other men carried a watch — and he read the last line again. *Come to the river in the dry season. I'll show you the wreck myself.*

He couldn't, of course. That was the grief of it, the permanent grief, the kind you don't get over but get *around*, the way you get around a rock in a channel. But he had gone to the river in the dry season. He had gone with a crew of people the law forgot, and he had beaten a fleet by a single tide, and he had stood in the rising water and read the first page of the registry aloud while the whole valley listened, and in every way that mattered — in every way a father could be shown anything — he had.

"I went," he said quietly, to the chest, to the room, to the man who had signed nothing for forty years and everything at the end. "I went, and I signed my name."

Outside, the children were racing paper boats down the flooded lane, and one of them — Sef's granddaughter, fearless, loud — was winning, and the light came through the high windows and lay across the open chest like a hand laid flat on a page.

Bram Vane — digger, teacher, record-keeper's son, witness — closed the letter, put it back over his heart, and went out into the morning to see what the river had brought.
