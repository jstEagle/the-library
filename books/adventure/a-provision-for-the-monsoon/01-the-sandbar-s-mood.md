## 1. The Sandbar's Mood

The sandbar below Tevoro Bend had moved again.

Casper Nakamura saw it from the wheelhouse before either of the twins did, which was the way he preferred it. The river had spent the night rearranging its furniture — a shoal that had sat honest and visible on the starboard side for three weeks now bulged into the channel like a shoulder turned in sleep, and the deep water, the water the *Kestrel* needed, had slid forty yards toward the cut bank where the current chewed at the roots of a fig tree. He read it the way other men read a face: the color of the water where it shallowed, green going to tea going to milk; the little V of ruffle where the current combed over the bar's new shoulder; the drift of a dead branch riding the seam between fast water and slow, showing him the channel's spine the way a fish's fin shows the current.

He brought the wheel over a spoke at a time. No drama. The *Kestrel* was sixty feet of patched iron and stubbornness, and she did not like to be hurried, and neither did the river, and Casper had made twenty years of peace with both.

"Bar's walked," he said, not loudly, because the wheelhouse was small and Bao was in the engine room below and sound traveled down through the deck plates like rumor through a village. "Starboard side's gone shallow. I'm taking the seam on the left of the dead fig."

From the roof of the wheelhouse came the twins' voices, Sefa's and Lio's, pitched in the shorthand they'd spoken since the womb. "Left of the fig." "Fig's got a wasp nest." "Wasps don't swim." "Everything swims in this river eventually."

Casper let it run under him like the engine note. The twins were nineteen, maybe — they'd come aboard in the port of Sarn Halud two years ago with nothing but each other and a deckhand's willingness, and they had never once asked him where he'd learned the river or where he'd been before it, which was a courtesy he repaid by never asking them either. Sefa had the watch now, coiled at the bow rail with a boathook and the particular stillness of someone listening to the water through her boots. Lio was aft, mending a net he was forbidden to fish with, because cargo boats that fished arrived late and cargo boats that arrived late lost contracts, and contracts were the only thing Casper trusted less than people but needed more.

Below, the engine changed its mind about a bearing and Bao's voice rose through the deck, addressed to the engine in the low, patient tone he used for the engine and for nothing else on earth. "No. No, we're not doing that. You did this in March. You know what happened in March." A pause, the clink of a wrench, the note settling. Bao could talk an engine down off a seizure. Casper had watched him do it in the dark, both of them ankle-deep in bilgewater, Bao murmuring to the machine like a man soothing a horse in a thunderstorm. It was the only conversation Casper had ever heard him finish.

The seam took them clean. The *Kestrel* leaned her shoulder into the current, iron plates ticking as the temperature came up, and the dead fig slid by close enough that Lio, without looking up from his net, reached out with the boathook and knocked three wasps out of the air that had come to investigate. The bar's new shoulder hissed past to starboard, pale and wet and indifferent, and then they were through, and the river widened, and the morning opened ahead of them like a door.

Casper allowed himself one breath that was not about steering.

From the galley hatch came the smell that had, over four years, become indistinguishable from the smell of the boat herself: Marisol's cooking. This morning it was rice and smoked fish and something with chilies that she grew in a bucket lashed to the rail, a bucket that had survived two groundings and one near-sinking and that Casper privately considered the toughest crew member aboard. Marisol cooked the way Bao listened to engines — as a form of devotion she'd have denied under oath. She fed them all like it was nothing, like it was just rice, and Casper had learned not to look too long at the way she watched each of them eat, because looking too long at things was how you started needing them, and needing things was how the past found your address.

That was the rule. He'd made it the first year, alone on the river with a boat he'd won in a card game he'd cheated at, and he'd kept it since: *never stay anywhere longer than the water level.* Water rose, water fell; a sandbar was here in June and gone by September; nothing on a river was permanent, so nothing on a river could hold you. He paid in coin, he took cargo, he left. Twenty years of leaving had worn him smooth as a river stone, and if there was a hollow underneath the smoothness he had gotten very good at not dropping anything into it to hear whether it rang.

Tevoro Bend gave way to the long straight below Karu Landing, and Karu Landing was their delivery, and Karu Landing was the problem.

* * *

Karu Landing sat on the west bank where the river made a lazy question mark, a village of forty houses on stilts, drying racks hung with fish like laundry, and a jetty of blackened pilings that had been rebuilt so many times it was less a structure than a sediment of jetties. Casper brought the *Kestrel* in against the ebb with the twins on bow and stern lines, and the whole operation had the wordless economy of a body doing something it had done ten thousand times — Sefa reading his hand signals from forty feet, Lio throwing a line that landed over the bollard on the first cast because Lio could throw a line the way other men threw insults, casually and with perfect aim.

The cargo was salt, lamp oil, a crate of carpenter's nails, and the small ordinary miracles that a river village cannot make for itself. The village came down to the jetty the way villages do, in ones and twos, with the studied casualness of people who have been waiting since dawn.

Elder Tam was waiting too. He stood at the head of the jetty the way he always stood, straight as a piling, a small man gone lean and grey, with hands like knotted rope. He was seventy if he was a day, and he had run Karu Landing's fish trade for forty years, and Casper had been doing business with him for six of those — longer than Casper did business with anyone, which should have been a warning and at the time had felt like nothing at all.

"Captain." Tam's voice was gravel over gravel. "You're late."

"Bar walked at Tevoro Bend." Casper handed over the manifest without looking at it. He knew the manifest. "Salt's dry, oil's full measure, and I brought the nails you wanted."

"The nails I wanted." Tam took the paper, and Casper saw — because he had spent twenty years learning to see the small failures in people's faces, the way a pilot sees the small failures in the water — the flicker that went through the old man's eyes. Something paid. Something held back. Something swallowed.

They unloaded. The twins worked the crates, Marisol came ashore to trade her chilies and gossip with the fisherwomen in the rapid, laughing pidgin of the river villages, and Casper stood on the jetty with Tam and did the arithmetic of the transaction and watched, out of the corner of his eye, the thing he was not supposed to see.

A man from the interior came down to the jetty mid-morning. Not a villager — Casper could read a villager at a hundred yards. This one had city boots, city boots gone wrong in the mud, and a ledger under his arm, and the particular expression of a man who enjoys his work. He spoke to Tam quietly. Tam did not answer. The man wrote something in the ledger, and Tam's hands, those knotted-rope hands, closed slowly at his sides, and Casper understood the shape of it before he understood any of the words.

Debt. The syndicates that owned the fish presses downriver ran a gentle business in the villages — gentle as a garrote. Salt on credit, nets on credit, a bad season, a good season swallowed anyway, interest compounding like floodwater, and at the end of it the press owns your boats and the boats are your living and the living is theirs. He'd seen it a dozen times up and down the Kestrel. He'd never once done anything about it, because doing something about it meant staying, and staying meant roots, and roots were how the past collected debts.

But Tam had taught him the mood of the sandbar below Tevoro Bend, six years ago, on his first run up here, standing beside him at the rail and saying, *that bar lies in the dry season, boy, she shows you deep water where there is none, you must read her mood and not her face.* And every season since, Casper had read that bar by Tam's teaching, and the bar had never once lied to him, and the man who taught him was standing on his own jetty being quietly foreclosed.

The city-boots man went back up the path. Tam turned back to Casper with his face reassembled, and they finished the business, and Casper signed the manifest, and Tam said, "Good salt this year, Captain. Dry. You keep your cargo honest."

"I keep everything honest," Casper said. "It's cheaper."

Tam laughed, and the laugh had a crack in it.

* * *

He did it after dark, and he did it in the way that left no story behind.

The fish press had an agent in Karu Landing, a soft man named Pell who kept the ledger and the strongbox in a room above the drying sheds, and Casper went up the bank after the village lamps were out and knocked on Pell's door with the particular knock of a man with business. Pell knew him by repute — the *Kestrel* ran salt and oil to half the river, and captains who pay cash are always welcome — and Casper bought the debt the way you buy a crate of nails. He'd counted it out that afternoon from the strongbox under his bunk, the boat's emergency fund, the money that was supposed to buy them out of a sinking or a famine or a bribe, and he had told himself while he counted it that this was not charity, it was salvage: Tam was a commercial asset, a pilot's education, an investment in safe channels. He was very good at telling himself things.

"Curious purchase," Pell said, counting it twice, because soft men are careful men. "Most people come to me to borrow."

"I'm not most people."

"No," Pell agreed pleasantly, and looked at Casper a moment too long, the way you look at a face you intend to remember. "No, I don't suppose you are."

Casper walked back down to the jetty in the dark with the receipt folded in his pocket, and the receipt was the whole point — a piece of paper saying the debt of Elder Tam of Karu Landing was paid in full — and he did not deliver it. He meant to leave it under Tam's door, unsigned, unexplained, and let the old man make of it what he would. But the jetty was not empty.

Tam was sitting at the head of it, on the bollard, in the dark, smoking a pipe that had gone out. He'd been sitting there, Casper understood, the way old men sit where their wives used to be, or their boats used to be. He looked up, and in the lamplight spilling from the nearest house Casper saw the old man's face do the thing faces do when they have been braced for a blow and the blow does not come.

"Couldn't sleep," Tam said. "The jetty talks at night. You get so you hear it."

Casper sat down on the bollard beside him, which was not in the plan, and said nothing, which was always his best line.

They sat. The river went on below them, saying the things rivers say, the endless low argument of water and wood. Somewhere out in the dark a fish jumped, and the sound was enormous and then gone.

"My father fished this bend," Tam said at last, to the river, not to Casper. "His father before him. The press has paper on us since before you were born, Captain, but paper isn't water. Paper doesn't know the bend. I told myself that for forty years." He knocked the dead pipe against the piling. "Tonight I was sitting here working out which boats I'd have to sell. The *Little Mercy* first. She was my wife's name, the boat. I was doing sums on my wife."

Casper took the receipt out of his pocket. He'd meant to leave it and go, and instead he heard himself say, in a voice rougher than he intended, "Your debt's paid. All of it. As of this evening."

The silence that followed was a long one, and it was not the comfortable silence of the river. Tam looked at the paper, and did not take it, and looked at Casper instead, and his eyes in the lamplight were wet and furious and old.

"Why," Tam said. It was not gratitude. It was the question of a man who has survived forty years by knowing exactly what everything costs.

"Because you taught me the sandbar," Casper said. "Six years ago. That's the whole of it. A debt's a debt."

"That's not the whole of it."

"It's all of it I've got."

Tam took the paper then. He held it like it might go off. And Casper watched the old man's face try to find the shape of the thing — the gratitude, the suspicion, the pride, all of it fighting for room — and he understood that he had made a mistake, that a kindness is not a coin you can slip under a door, that once you hand a man a piece of yourself the man holds a piece of you, and Tam was looking at him now the way no one had looked at him in twenty years, like a person who had a name and a history and a place at a table.

"Stay the night," Tam said. "The village will feed you. My daughter makes a fish curry that would make you weep, and you'll tell me where you learned the river, because no man reads water like that without somebody having died teaching him."

And there it was. The question under all questions. *Where did you come from. Whose are you. What happened to you.* The past, arriving not in official letterhead but in an old man's kindness, wanting its address.

"Can't," Casper said, and stood, and made his voice into the captain's voice, flat and easy. "Tide turns at four. I run on the tide."

"It's a river, Captain. There's no tide above the falls."

"Then the water level," Casper said, and heard how it sounded, and didn't fix it.

He walked down the jetty. Behind him Tam said his name — not *Captain*, but the name, the one Casper had given Tam the first year, the false one, the one he wore like a coat — and Casper did not turn around. He stepped aboard the *Kestrel*, and the deck rose to meet his foot the way it always did, the only floor in the world that had ever held him steady, and he stood a moment in the dark of the well deck with his hand on the rail, listening to the village settle behind him, the lamps going out one by one, the dog that had barked at him every visit for six years barking at him now for the last time, though he did not know that yet.

* * *

They cast off at three, before any light, which was the way he liked to leave places: as if they had never been there. The twins took the lines in without a word, sensing the mood of the wheelhouse the way they sensed everything. Marisol left coffee on the ledge by the wheel, black and bitter, and did not ask why they were leaving in the dark when the run to the next landing was four hours and the next landing was nowhere. Bao's engine turned over on the second try and settled into its working note, and the *Kestrel* slid off the jetty of Karu Landing stern-first, the way she'd arrived at a hundred jetties, backwards, leaving.

The river took them. The bar at Tevoro Bend, going down, lay where Tam's teaching said it would lie, and Casper threaded the seam in the dark by memory and the sound of the water on the hull, and it was perfect, the run was perfect, the boat was perfect, and none of it touched the place in him where the old man's voice had gotten in — *stay the night, tell me where you learned the river* — the way water gets into a crack and then the winter comes.

He told himself it would pass. Everything passed. That was the whole provision of the river: it rose, it fell, it carried you, it never asked you to be anything but moving.

They made the bend and turned the corner above Karu Landing, and the village was gone behind the fig trees, and Casper set the wheel and looked down the long straight water ahead, forty miles of it to the next landing, and that was when Sefa, at the bow, straightened up from the rail and pointed downriver without turning around.

"Captain," she said. "Boat coming up. Fast. Flying something at the mast."

Casper found the glasses. A government pennant, white on grey, coming up the channel against him at a speed no cargo boat made, riding low in the water like it was carrying something heavy — men, or paper, or trouble. It would pass them within the hour, at this stretch of river, in this light.

He lowered the glasses. The coffee had gone cold on the ledge, and the river ran on ahead of him, wide and brown and patient, carrying everything toward everything else, and Casper Nakamura stood at his wheel in the dark before dawn and felt, for no reason he could name, the way a sandbar must feel when the flood season starts.
