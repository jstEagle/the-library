## 2. Paper from the Capital

The letter came upriver on a government steamer, which was how Casper knew it would be trouble. Nothing official ever traveled light.

He saw it first as smoke, a gray smudge against the morning haze where the channel bent around Heron's Tooth, and he stood at the wheel of the Kestrel with his coffee going cold in one hand and counted the seconds until the shape resolved itself: paddle-wheeler, white hull, the colonial crest painted on its flank like a brand on livestock. It ran deep in the water — too deep for the season, too deep for the sandbars below the bend — and Casper watched it with the detached interest of a man watching someone else's horse step into quicksand.

It made it through. Barely. The pilot clearly had charts, and charts were exactly the problem. Charts were last year's river wearing this year's clothes.

"Government," Sefa said, materializing at his elbow the way the twins did, without sound or warning, her hair still wet from scrubbing the deck. Lio appeared on her other side a breath later, mirror-image and grinning, because Lio grinned at everything that wasn't yet proven to be a disaster.

"I can see that."

"They're slowing down."

"I can see that too."

The steamer put out a tender — a launch, brass fittings, a man standing in the bow in a linen suit the color of old teeth. Even at two hundred meters Casper could read the posture: spine straight, chin level, a man who had never once considered that the river might not cooperate with him. Behind him, a clerk clutched a leather dispatch case like it might hatch.

Casper set down his coffee. "Bao," he called toward the engine hatch. "Keep steam up. We're not stopping."

"You're stopping," Marisol said from the galley doorway, wiping her hands on her apron. She said it mildly, the way she said most things, but there was iron under it. "You can't outrun a conscription order, Casper. I've seen men try. They end up owing more than they started with."

"I don't owe anything."

"No," she agreed, and went back inside, and let the silence finish the sentence for her.

* * *

The administrator's name was Pellham-Wraye, and he introduced it the way a man lays down a winning card. He came aboard the Kestrel with his boots leaving wet prints on Casper's clean deck, looked around at the patched planking and the cargo lashed under oilcloth and the twins coiling rope with the lazy precision of people who could stop being lazy in half a second, and smiled the smile of a man cataloguing assets.

"Captain Nakamura." He pronounced it carefully, correctly, which meant he'd practiced. That was worse than getting it wrong. "I'm told you know this river better than any chart in the survey office."

"The charts are wrong," Casper said. "That's not knowledge. That's just attention."

"And attention is precisely what we require." Pellham-Wraye accepted the crate Marisol offered him — she'd brought tea out unprompted, because Marisol believed even vultures should be fed properly — and did not drink it. "A survey team. Six men, government contract, mapping the upper reaches above Widow's Gorge. They've gone silent. Three weeks now."

"Radios fail in the gorge country. Everyone knows that."

"Their instruments are still transmitting." The administrator set a paper on the crate between them: a telegraph flimsy, coordinates, timestamps. "Position beacons, logging every hour. The machines report. The men do not. No reply to signal, no movement of camp, no tracks reported by the last trading post that sent anyone up to look. The trader turned back at the gorge mouth. Said the water was wrong."

Casper looked at the coordinates and felt something cold settle behind his ribs, because he knew that stretch of river the way a man knows the back of his own hand — better, actually; hands don't change their minds the way water does. Above Widow's Gorge the Kestrel narrowed into granite throat after granite throat, and the sandbars there weren't sand at all but gravel shoals that migrated with every flood, and the channels braided and unbraided like something alive making decisions.

"Send a bigger boat," he said.

"Bigger boats draw more water than yours. And the season turns in—" Pellham-Wraye consulted nothing, because he'd memorized it, "—five weeks. After that, snow closes the high passes and the river drops below navigation. Whatever happened to those six men stays happened until spring." He folded his hands. "Your boat, Captain. Your reputation. You're the only option that isn't an obituary."

"No."

The word landed flat on the deck between them. Sefa and Lio had stopped pretending to work. Bao had come up from the engine hatch and stood wiping his hands on a rag that wasn't dirty, which was what Bao did when he wanted to look busy while listening.

Pellham-Wraye didn't seem surprised. He took a long sip of tea — finally, deliberately, a man demonstrating patience — and then reached into the dispatch case and drew out a folder, buff-colored, tied with red tape, stamped along the edge in faded ink.

Casper's whole body went still. Twenty years of moving had taught his body to go still before his mind caught up, the way animals freeze before they run.

"Do you know what this is?" Pellham-Wraye asked, though he plainly knew the answer. "Provincial archives. Land registry annexes, twenty-two years old. There was a valley," he said, conversationally, terribly, "upstream of the Kestrel headwaters. Terraced fields. A village that flooded — officially attributed to seasonal inundation, though the syndicate's diversion works upstream were, shall we say, a contributing factor. There were claims filed. Compensation claims. One family's claim was particularly persistent." He untied the tape with one finger. "Nakamura. Filed by a Nakamura."

"Put it away."

"A brother, I believe. Younger. The claim was denied on procedural grounds — improper documentation, the usual machinery. But the file was never closed, Captain. Files like this have a way of reopening. An inquiry, say. Into irregularities. Into whether certain parties downstream profited from waters they had no right to divert." Pellham-Wraye smoothed the paper flat on the crate. "Or files like this have a way of staying buried. It depends entirely on the cooperation of interested persons."

The river moved past them, brown and patient. Somewhere below the hull a fish rose and slapped back down. Casper heard all of it from very far away, because part of him was twenty-two years old again, standing in a flooded field that had been his grandfather's terraces, holding a ledger with his brother's handwriting in it — *filed, they'll have to answer, Casper, they'll have to* — and the water coming up over the bunds like it had been invited.

"You thought you'd bought your anonymity with distance," Pellham-Wraye said, softer now, almost kind, which was the cruelest register he owned. "But paper doesn't move, Captain. Paper waits. That's its whole genius. You spent twenty years running downriver and the whole time your name was sitting in a drawer in the capital, gathering dust and interest."

Casper looked at the crew. Bao's face was carefully blank. The twins had gone very quiet, and quiet twins were a bad sign. Marisol stood in the galley doorway with her arms crossed, watching him with an expression he refused to interpret.

"What do you want," he said. Not a question. A surrender shaped like one.

"Passage. Up to the gorge, above it if the water allows, and whatever the survey team left that needs recovering. You'll carry a passenger — a surveyor, coming up from the coast, due at the jetty within the week. Sealed orders, sealed case, none of your concern." Pellham-Wraye retied the red tape with a small, precise knot, and slid the folder back into the case, and Casper understood that the knot was the point: the debt stayed tied either way. "Do this, and the file closes permanently. Refuse, and I reopen it, and the inquiry will find whatever inquiries find. The syndicate has friends in the capital. So do I. We are, in that respect, old acquaintances of yours, though I doubt you've had the pleasure."

* * *

They left him alone with it, mostly. The administrator withdrew to his launch with promises of a written order to follow, and the white steamer wallowed off downriver toward the next bend, drawing a wake that rocked the Kestrel against her mooring lines like a nudge from a large, indifferent animal.

Evening came on gold and then violet. Marisol fed everyone rice and river fish and green mango pickle, and nobody talked about the folder, and everybody was talking about the folder in the way they ate, and worked, and didn't look at him.

It was Bao who found him at the rail after dark, when the first stars were out and the current muttered against the hull.

"My mother used to say," Bao offered, in lieu of greeting, "that the river gives back everything you throw in. Just slower. And colder."

"Your mother sounds wise."

"She sounds like someone who never threw anything in." Bao leaned beside him. "Twenty years, Casper. You never told us why you move. I never asked. That's the deal. Nobody asks anybody anything on this boat, and somehow we all know each other anyway." He paused. "So I'm not asking. I'm just saying — a file in a drawer in the capital. That's a heavy thing to carry alone, and you've been carrying it so long you probably forgot it has weight."

"It's not weight," Casper said. "It's ballast. It keeps me moving."

"That's not how ballast works."

"No," Casper admitted. "It isn't."

Downriver, the government steamer's lights had vanished, but he could feel the wake of it still in the water, spreading, inevitable, touching every shore. He thought about the coordinates on the telegraph flimsy. Instruments still logging. Men gone silent. Something above Widow's Gorge that machines recorded and mouths would not speak of.

He thought about his brother's handwriting, and the water coming over the bunds.

"We take the job," he said to the dark, because saying it to Bao would make it a conversation, and he couldn't afford a conversation. "We take the passenger, we run the gorge, we come back before the snow. Nobody signs anything. Nobody stays anywhere longer than the water level."

Bao straightened. "And the file?"

"When it's done, the file closes. That's the trade." Casper turned from the rail. "One run. Then we're strangers again, all of us, the way we've always been."

Behind him, unheard or unheeded, Marisol stood in the galley light with a dish towel in her hands, and shook her head slowly at the river, at the man, at the word *strangers*, as if it were a spice she'd tasted before and found bitter.

Two days later, at the coast jetty, a woman came down the gangplank carrying a locked instrument case, and Casper knew before she said a word that the past had only just begun unpacking.
