## 3. The Surveyor with the Sealed Case

The jetty at Fort Meridian smelled of tar and wet rope and the particular rot of a colonial town trying to be something it wasn't. Ada Okonkwo stood at its end with her case in one hand — the locked case, the sealed case, the case that had been chained to her wrist through three trains, two ferries, and one night in a customs house she did not care to remember — and watched the riverboat come up against the current.

She had read the file on the *Kestrel* before dawn. Not much of a file. A hull number, a tonnage that was probably a lie, a captain's name spelled three different ways in three different ledgers: Nakamura, Nakamora, once, memorably, Nacamura. Twenty years on the water and the administration still couldn't decide what he was. That, more than anything in the file, told her what kind of man she was about to meet. Bureaucracies could classify anything they understood. What they couldn't classify, they misspelled.

The boat came in slow, which surprised her. She'd expected the arrogance of a fast approach, the showmanship of a reverse-astern landing. Instead the *Kestrel* simply arrived, as if the jetty had been waiting for her all along and the river had merely agreed to allow it. She was smaller than Ada expected, patched in places where other boats would have been scrapped, her paint gone the grey-brown of driftwood, her name repainted so many times it stood up from the hull in ridges like scar tissue.

A boy — no, not a boy, a young man, though he moved with the loose-limbed economy of someone who'd grown up on a moving deck — caught the bow line before the mate threw it, as if he'd already known where it would fall. His twin took the stern. They didn't speak to each other. They didn't need to.

"You the surveyor?" The voice came from the wheelhouse rail, unhurried, carrying over the water without effort. Casper Nakamura looked down at her the way men look down at rivers: assessing depth, reading current, deciding whether to trust what they see.

"I'm the surveyor." She lifted the case slightly, letting him see the lock, the government stamp, the chain. "Ada Okonkwo. I'm told you've been conscripted."

Something moved behind his eyes — not quite a flinch, but the memory of one. "Told correctly," he said. "Come aboard, then."

* * *

The gangplank flexed under her boots and she made herself walk it without hurrying, because she had learned long ago that hesitation on unfamiliar ground was a debt you paid for weeks. The deck was scrubbed pale, the wood worn into a smoothness that spoke of decades of feet. Coils of rope hung in perfect flemishes. Brass fittings gleamed. Everything aboard was either old or mended, and everything mended had been mended well.

A woman appeared out of the deckhouse doorway wiping her hands on an apron, and the smell that came with her — fried plantain, chili, something with lime — hit Ada with such force of homesickness that she nearly staggered. The woman was broad and silver-streaked and looked at Ada the way cooks look at everyone: as a mouth to be fed, and therefore a problem to be solved.

"You'll eat at six," the woman said. It was not an offer.

"Marisol," said Casper, by way of introduction or warning.

"Marisol," Ada repeated, and filed the name away.

Below, in the corridor, she passed the twins — Sefa and Lio, the file said, though the file hadn't said how identical they were, how they moved like two halves of one thought — and an older man in the engine room hatch who looked up at her with an engineer's flat appraisal, cataloguing her weight, her baggage, her probable trouble, and adding it all to some running total he kept in his head. Bao. He wiped his hands slowly and went back to his engine without a word.

She was shown to a cabin barely wider than her bunk, and she set the case on the bunk itself, because there was nowhere else, and sat across from it on the single stool. Through the bulkhead she could hear the boat's sounds sorting themselves into a language: the engine's heartbeat, the river's breath against the hull, footsteps above with their own particular rhythms. Marisol's heavy tread. The twins' doubled lightness. Casper's — she paused, listening — Casper's were almost silent. A man who had learned to walk quietly on his own boat. She wondered what he was listening for.

The case sat on the bunk and said nothing, as cases do. But she found herself talking to it anyway, low, in the dark, the way you talk to a grave.

"Three weeks," she told it. "Then I hand you over and I go home."

She did not believe herself, and the case knew it.

* * *

They cast off at first light, and Ada climbed to the wheelhouse uninvited, because the first rule of surveying was that you never learned a place from your cabin.

Casper glanced at her, said nothing, and returned to the channel. The wheelhouse smelled of engine oil and old tea. Charts lay rolled in a rack by the window, and she saw immediately that half of them weren't charts at all — they were hand-drawn, annotated in pencil, corrected in ink, corrected again in a third hand. Someone had been redrawing this river for twenty years. Someone had been keeping a private cartography of the Kestrel more accurate than anything the Survey Office possessed, and had never sent it downstream.

"That sandbar," she said, pointing ahead to where the water dimpled and smoothed in a way that meant shallow, that always meant shallow, "is going to move after the next flood."

"It already has." He eased the wheel a spoke. "It moves every spring. The chart says it's forty meters east. The chart is wrong."

"Then why carry the chart?"

"Because the chart is what other people believe. It's useful to know what other people believe." He looked at her then, fully, for the first time. "You came upriver on the mail steamer. You read my file. So tell me what other people believe about me, and I'll tell you where the chart's wrong."

It was, she recognized, an opening move. She declined to play it. "I believe you know this river better than any living person. I believe that's why they chose you. And I believe you didn't want the job."

"No," he agreed. "I didn't."

"But you took it."

"The alternative was worse." He turned back to the window. Ahead, the river bent around a bluff forested to the waterline, and the morning mist was lifting off it in ropes. "Your orders. The ones you won't share. How far above the gorge am I taking you?"

Ah, she thought. There it was. "To the last known position of the survey team. Camp Seven."

"And after?"

"After depends on what we find."

"Nothing depends on what we find," Casper said. "That's the thing about finding things. They're already there. All that changes is who knows." He adjusted the wheel a degree, correcting for a current she couldn't even see yet. "I ferry you to Camp Seven. I don't go into the high valley. I don't go past the snow line. And when the monsoon's within four days of landfall, I turn around whether you're aboard or not. Those are my terms."

"Those are generous terms," Ada said, "for a conscripted man."

"I'm a very generous man," he said, without a trace of humor, "when nobody's watching."

* * *

She spent the second day learning the boat, because the first rule of measuring a man was to measure what he'd built around himself.

The *Kestrel* was a contradiction in wood and iron. Every system aboard was maintained past the point of reason — Bao's engine room was cleaner than the Survey Office drafting hall, the twins coiled and stowed with a discipline no cargo boat needed — and yet nothing aboard was named. No photographs on the bulkheads. No letters pinned up. Marisol cooked for five people and remembered exactly how each of them took their tea, and if you asked any member of the crew what the others were to them, you got a shrug, a list of duties, a change of subject.

But watch them at work. Watch Sefa call a depth and Lio echo it back before the sounding pole even finished its arc. Watch Bao answer a change in the engine note that Casper hadn't ordered yet, because he'd heard the wheelhouse bell pattern and knew what it meant. Watch Marisol put a bowl of food in front of Casper at the exact moment his hands started to shake from fatigue he would never admit to, and watch him eat it without acknowledging it had been placed there, and watch her take the empty bowl away with the satisfaction of a woman who had won an argument twenty years running.

They were a family, Ada thought, watching from the rail as the gorge country began to rise around them. A family that had signed a treaty never to use the word. She knew the type. She was, she suspected, looking at a crew of people who had each lost something to names — to the names of owners, of debts, of places they'd been driven from — and had concluded that the safest thing to be was nobody, together.

It should have made her feel superior. It only made her feel seen, which was worse.

* * *

The friction came on the fourth day, at a place Casper called the Devil's Elbow, where the river doubled back on itself around a spur of black rock and the current ran like something fleeing.

"My chart shows a cut-through on the inside of the bend," Ada said. She had spread her own maps on the wheelhouse chart table — the official ones, the ones with the vanished team's route marked in red — and Casper had looked at them exactly once, the way a man looks at a stranger's photograph of his own mother.

"Your chart shows a cut-through that existed in 1889," he said. "The spur's been sloughing since. There's a shoal across the throat now, and it moves. We take the outside. It costs us an hour."

"It costs us three hours, and we don't have three hours." She kept her voice level. This was the argument she'd rehearsed on the mail steamer, against a man she'd imagined as slower and stupider. "The team has been silent for nineteen days. If they're alive—"

"If they're alive, they've been alive for nineteen days without us, and they'll be alive for three hours more. If they're dead, three hours won't insult them." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "Outside of the bend, surveyor. I don't run water I haven't read."

"You've read this water."

"Not today's water." He pointed at the surface with his chin. "Look at it. Tell me what you see."

She looked. She was a good cartographer; she could read water better than most. Ripples, seams, the darker thread of the deep channel, a V of standing waves below the point. "Flood debris coming down," she said. "From the upper reaches. Heavy rain in the headwaters."

"Two days ago. Which means the headwaters got it worse, which means Camp Seven's ground is soft, which means—" he stopped, and she watched him decide not to finish the sentence, and hated him a little for the courtesy.

"Which means we should hurry," she finished for him.

"Which means we should arrive in one piece." He rang the engine telegraph to half-ahead and took the outside of the bend, wide and patient as a man crossing a sickroom, and the lost hour unspooled behind them in brown water while Ada stood at the rail and told herself the anger she felt was grief for people she'd never met.

It was partly true. Nineteen days. She'd shared a mess tent with those people. Halvorsen had shown her his daughter's drawing. Okafor — no relation, they'd joked, badly, twice — had taught her the office card game. And now their kettle might still be warm somewhere above the gorge, and the empire's response was to send one cartographer and one reluctant boatman, because the land those people had been mapping was worth more to the capital than the people themselves.

She knew why she'd really been sent. The orders in her breast pocket said *recover or complete*, but the memorandum beneath them, the one not in her breast pocket, the one she'd burned in the customs house stove rather than let it cross the water — that one had said *ensure the findings do not reach unauthorized parties*. She was not a rescuer. She was a lid.

And Casper Nakamura, she thought, watching him at the wheel with his silent feet and his twenty years of private corrections, was a man who could smell a lid. He hadn't asked about the case once. Not its weight, not its contents, not the chain. He'd looked at it exactly twice, both times the way you look at a box you already know is full of something that bites.

* * *

That evening, moored against the bank below the gorge approaches, she found Marisol on the aft deck shelling beans into a basin, and let herself be handed a stool and a handful of pods without being asked. The twins were on the foredeck running knots against each other in silence, fingers flying, stakes apparently established long ago. Bao's engine ticked as it cooled, a sound like a slow clock.

"Your captain," Ada said, working a pod open, "doesn't talk much."

"He talks plenty," Marisol said. "Just not in words." She tipped her chin toward the wheelhouse, where Casper's silhouette moved behind the glass, checking something, checking it again. "Twenty years I've cooked for that man. He's never once asked me where I came from, and I've never once asked him. That's the arrangement."

"That doesn't strike you as strange? Crewing a boat for twenty years with strangers?"

Marisol's hands didn't stop. "Who said strangers?" she said, and the words landed between them like a dropped tool, and she went back to her beans as if she'd said nothing at all.

Ada looked out at the river. Dusk was coming down purple and enormous, and the first stars were out over the black shoulder of the gorge country, and somewhere up there, above the snow line, a camp sat empty with its instruments running. She thought about the case chained below, holding whatever the capital wanted buried, and about the burned memorandum, and about the word *strangers*, spoken like a shield held up by a woman who knew exactly what it was covering.

"You're all running from something," she said quietly. It wasn't an accusation. It was almost admiration.

"We prefer 'traveling,'" Marisol said, and smiled, and it was the saddest smile Ada had ever been offered with a bowl of beans.

* * *

Later, unable to sleep in the strange rocking dark, she came up to the wheelhouse for air and found Casper alone at the chart table, working by lamplight. He didn't look up. She stood in the doorway a moment, undecided, and then saw what he was doing.

He was drawing. Freehand, in pencil, from memory: the reach they'd run that day, every bar and snag and seam of it, laid down in fine confident lines beside the official chart. Where the two disagreed — and they disagreed everywhere — his lines were newer, sharper, alive. It was the best hydrography she had ever seen done without instruments, and it was going into a drawer instead of an archive.

"That's worth money," she said.

"It's worth nothing," he said. "That's the point of it. Things worth money get taken." He blew graphite dust off the page, rolled it, and slid it into the rack with the others. Dozens of them. Twenty years of a river, drawn and redrawn, owned by no one.

"You could have been chief hydrographer of the whole basin," she said. "You know that."

"I could have been lots of things." He finally looked at her, lamplight catching the tired lines of his face. "What were you, before they gave you a case to carry?"

"A cartographer."

"That's what they called you. What were you?"

The question went through her guard like water through a torn net. She thought of the drafting hall in the capital, of her name on maps that erased villages, of the day she'd understood what her beautiful contour lines were for. "Owned," she said, before she could stop herself.

Something passed across his face — recognition, brief and quickly shuttered. He nodded once, as a man nods to another man across a room neither of them wants to be in.

"Camp Seven tomorrow," he said. "We make it by midday if the water behaves." He turned back to his charts, dismissal and invitation in the same gesture. "Get some sleep, surveyor. Whatever's up there stopped answering nineteen days ago. It'll keep till morning."

She went below. In her cabin the case waited on the bunk, patient as a stone, and she lay down beside it and listened to the river push them upstream through the dark, toward the gorge, toward the warm kettle and the cold instruments and whatever the high valley had been worth hiding.

Near midnight, half-dreaming, she heard footsteps on the deck above — Casper's near-silent ones — stop just outside her door. A pause, long enough to mean something. Then they moved on, quiet as water, and she understood that the captain had come to look at her door the way he looked at sandbars: reading its mood, deciding what lay underneath, and how much it would cost him when it shifted.

She slept badly, one hand resting on the case's cold lock, and dreamed of contour lines running uphill.
