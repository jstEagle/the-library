## 4. Instruments Still Running

The camp announced itself before we saw it — a thread of woodsmoke so thin it might have been memory, rising off the eastern bank where the river bent north toward the gorge country.

"There," Sefa said from the bow, pointing with her chin the way she did everything, hands busy coiling line. "Smoke."

"Smoke means people," Lio said.

"Smoke means *was* people," Sefa said.

I stood at the rail beside Casper and watched that gray filament climb into a sky the color of unpolished tin. Three days above Widow's Gorge now, and the river had changed its character entirely — narrower, faster, braided around gravel bars the color of old bone, flanked by forest so dense and dark it seemed less like trees than like a decision the land had made to keep something secret. The air smelled of cold stone and resin. My instruments, packed in their cases below, had been chattering to themselves all morning: barometric pressure falling in long slow steps, the kind of decline that meant weather gathering somewhere over the high country, waiting.

"You said the last transmission came from this bend," Casper said. He hadn't looked at me when he said it. He rarely did. He navigated by peripheral vision the way other men navigated by charts.

"The last *scheduled* transmission." I had the logbook open on my knee, though I no longer needed it; I'd read the entries so many times they'd worn grooves in me. "Day forty-one of the survey. Barometric readings, water levels, a note about provisioning. Nothing unusual. Then nothing at all. Eleven days of silence."

"And the administration sent you alone to find out why."

"They sent me because I'm the only one who can read what they left behind." I closed the book. "The finding is one thing. The *understanding* is another. You can't recover a survey if you don't know what you're recovering."

Casper said nothing to that, but I'd learned his silences the way I was learning the river's — there were silences that were walls and silences that were doors, and this one was a door left deliberately ajar. He turned the wheel a few degrees and the Kestrel swung toward the eastern bank, her patched hull shouldering through a slack eddy with the weary competence of an animal that knew exactly how tired it was allowed to be.

I had been aboard nine days. Long enough to learn the boat's vocabulary: Bao's engine room speaking in percussion, Marisol's galley in aromatics, the twins in a shorthand of gesture and half-finished sentences that required no completion because each always knew what the other meant. Long enough to notice what I had noticed in the first hour, actually, and had been carefully not saying since: that these five people functioned as a family with the word surgically removed, the way some cultures remove a name from use after a death. They fed each other without being asked. They anticipated each other's weight on the deck. And when Casper gave an order, they obeyed not with the obedience of employees but with something older and more involuntary, the obedience of people who had decided, somewhere in the past, that this man's judgment was the fixed point around which their lives would orbit — and who would have denied the decision under oath.

I recognized it because I had done the same thing once, with a survey team, in a capital office full of instruments and ambition. I had decided that Dr. Halloran's judgment was the fixed point. And then Halloran and six others had walked into this valley and stopped transmitting, and the empire had handed me a sealed case and a ticket on a smuggler's boat and called it a career opportunity.

The bank came up to meet us — shingle and clay, a steep cut through which a game trail descended to a flat shelf of ground perhaps thirty meters above the waterline. And there it was.

The camp.

* * *

It was intact. That was the first thing, the thing that made the hair on my forearms rise beneath my sleeves even before I'd set foot ashore. Intact was the wrong word for what camps become after eleven days untended. Camps are eaten. Rain softens them into the ground, animals redistribute them, wind edits them. This camp had been *preserved*. The tents stood taut, guy-lines still dressed and coiled in the regulation fashion Halloran insisted on. The instrument table under its oilcloth shelter held the theodolite, level, its lens cap on. A crate of provisions sat half-unpacked, its lid propped open at the angle of a man interrupted mid-task.

And the kettle was warm.

Sefa found it first, because Sefa found everything first. She crouched at the fire ring, held her palm above the iron, and looked back at me with an expression I will remember longer than most things I remember.

"This is warm," she said. "Not *was* warm. Is."

Lio was already moving through the camp with the twins' particular methodical grace, lifting oilcloth, checking crates, reading the site the way I read contour lines. "No tracks," he reported. "Not faded tracks. No tracks. The ground here hasn't been walked on since the last rain, and the last rain was—" he glanced at me.

"Nine days ago," I said. "The night of the final transmission."

"Nine days ago," Lio agreed. "And nobody has walked here since. Not them. Not anybody."

"No blood," Sefa added from the far tent. She said it flatly, professionally, and I understood in that moment that the twins had seen sites like this before, or near enough — that Casper's crew carried knowledge in their bodies that their ages argued against. "No signs of struggle. Their boots are here. All seven pairs. Their coats are here."

Seven pairs of boots, ranked neatly outside the tents the way survey teams rank them, soles wiped. Seven men and women who had gone to sleep, or risen, or paused mid-morning — and stepped out of their boots and out of the world without disturbing a single grain of the ground beneath them.

Casper stood at the edge of the camp and did not enter it. I noticed that. He stood with his hands in his pockets and his weight on his heels, a man at the boundary of a property he refused to own, and his eyes went over the site with a speed and thoroughness that told me he'd catalogued every detail in ten seconds and was now simply standing guard against whatever the details implied.

"Well?" he said finally.

"I need the instruments running before I can tell you anything." I moved to the instrument table and lifted the oilcloth. The theodolite sat where it should sit. Beside it, the survey team's field recorder — a brass-cased logging device, the pride of the imperial cartographic corps, a machine that scratched its observations onto a rotating paper drum with the fussy precision of a conscience. Its drum was still turning.

Eleven days of entries, logged automatically, no hand needed. The machine had kept working after the men had gone, faithful as a dog at a graveside.

I sat down on the wet grass, opened the recorder's housing, and began to read.

* * *

The early entries were routine. I say routine knowing what I know now, which is that nothing in this valley is routine, but at the time they read as routine: elevation fixes, solar observations, magnetic declination checks, notes on water flow in the tributary that came down from the high valley to the north. Halloran's hand appeared in the annotation margins — his small, exact script, the script of a man who drew letters the way he drew maps, every stroke load-bearing.

*Day 38. Tributary discharge higher than seasonal model predicts. Source must be glacial, but no glacier shows on the 1889 provisional chart. Requesting permission to extend survey north into the upper valley.*

*Day 39. Permission denied by wire. Reason cited: scope. Halloran's marginal note, in pencil, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper: Scope. They've never cared about scope in eleven years. Something else cares.*

I felt the temperature of the record change at day 40. It's a subtle thing, temperature in a document — you feel it in the length of the sentences, in what gets written down versus what clearly happened and didn't get written down. On day 40 the entries grew shorter. The handwriting in the annotations grew larger, looser, the hand of a man writing quickly or writing frightened.

*Day 40. Took the north trail with Okafor and Lindqvist. The valley opens at approximately 2,900 meters. What is there cannot be put in this log. Will report verbally to the Director.*

*Day 41. Returned. Instruments recalibrated — all readings in the upper valley are correct. That is the problem. They are correct. I have sealed the supplementary charts and lodged them with the case manifest. If anything happens to us, the work speaks for itself.*

*Day 41, later. Someone signaled from the ridge at dusk. Lantern code, three-and-two, which is a salvage pattern, not a survey pattern. We doused our fire early.*

That was the final handwritten entry. After that, the drum turned on alone, recording what machines record: pressure, humidity, temperature, the heartbeat of the weather. And then, at 0317 on the night of the eleventh day past, the automatic trace showed a single anomaly — a vibration signature, brief, rhythmic, unlike anything in the machine's calibration range. I measured it against the drum's time base. It lasted four minutes. It was regular as footsteps.

Or oars.

I sat back from the recorder and found the whole crew gathered behind me. I don't know how long they'd been there. Marisol had brought coffee without anyone asking her to; she pressed a tin cup into my hands and her fingers were steady and her eyes were not.

"Well?" Casper said again.

"Halloran found something in the upper valley," I said. "Something worth hiding. He hid it himself — sealed charts, lodged with a case manifest. Then someone signaled him from the ridgeline using salvage code, and eleven days ago, in the small hours, seven people left this camp without their boots, without tracks, without blood, in the space of a single night." I looked down at the drum, at that four-minute vibration, regular as a heart that wouldn't stop. "Whatever took them came by water. Or by something that moves like water."

Nobody spoke. The river spoke instead, sliding past below us with that sound it makes when it isn't in any hurry because it knows it will arrive regardless.

Bao broke the silence, which surprised me; in nine days I'd heard him say perhaps twenty words, most of them to engines. "Vibration signature," he said, looking at the drum without touching it. "Four minutes. Regular interval." His eyes came up to mine. "You know what else logs like that?"

"No," I said.

"Neither do I," Bao said. "But engines don't. Animals don't. Paddles do." He wiped his hands on his rag, slowly. "Paddles, or poles. A lot of them. In rhythm."

"A boat," Lio said.

"A lot of boats," Sefa said.

"Or one very long boat," Lio said.

Casper had come closer while Bao talked — close enough now to stand inside the camp's boundary, though he crouched rather than stood, keeping his center of gravity low, a man reading the ground itself. He touched the fire ring's stones. He looked at the ranked boots. Then he did something I didn't expect: he walked to the edge of the shelf where the game trail dropped to the river, and he studied the mud at the top of the landing with his head tilted, the way a man studies a page written in a language he almost knows.

"Someone landed here," he said. "After the rain. After your team vanished." He pointed without touching. "See the scour? A hull came in bow-first, heavy, and backed off. Draft marks say a boat drawing better than a meter. Wide beam." He straightened. "They came back. Or others came. They searched this camp — gently, thoroughly — and they left everything, which means they didn't find what they wanted."

"The sealed charts," I said.

"The sealed charts," Casper agreed. "Halloran hid them well enough that professionals couldn't turn them up in a night. So the professionals went away empty." He turned and looked at me, and for the first time since I'd boarded the Kestrel he looked at me directly, fully, and I understood that this was a currency he spent rarely and that he was spending it now because the situation had become expensive. "Which means whoever they work for still wants it. And still doesn't have it. And knows that a cartographer from the capital just came upriver asking questions."

The dread arrived then, not as a thought but as a physical fact, settling into my chest like ballast. Because he was right, and because I had been careless — I had asked questions at three landings on the way up, shown my credentials at two, mentioned Halloran's name aloud within earshot of strangers. I had moved through the river towns like a woman carrying a lantern, announcing myself to everything in the dark.

"How many people knew I was coming?" I asked. "Knew what I was carrying?"

"The administrator," Casper said. "His clerks. The wire operators between here and the capital. Every harbor master whose tea money comes from someone other than the government." He shrugged, and the shrug was terrible in its economy. "On this river, information is cargo. Somebody ships it."

* * *

We went back aboard as the light failed. The valley floor drank the dusk in one swallow and the stars came out hard and close, altitude stars, and the temperature fell the way it does above the gorge country — fast, honest, without negotiation. Marisol produced a stew that tasted of smoke and cumin and something sweeter underneath, some spice she guarded the provenance of like state secrets, and the crew ate in the galley's lamplight while I sat at the chart table in the stern cabin trying to make the day's findings arrange themselves into a shape I could act on.

They wouldn't arrange. That was the trouble. Seven people don't vanish from a warm camp without leaving a mark unless they leave willingly, and people don't leave willingly without their boots. And yet no tracks, no blood, no struggle — the evidence contradicted itself, and evidence that contradicts itself isn't evidence anymore, it's a message. Someone had arranged the camp to say *nothing happened here*, and arranging takes time and confidence, and both of those cost money.

I was still staring at the drum trace when Casper came aft. He didn't announce himself; he simply existed suddenly at the edge of the lamplight, the way he did, a man built out of the same material as shadow.

"You flagged a boat at the second landing," he said. "Three days ago. Green hull, twin masts, riding light."

I remembered it — a sleek salvage craft that had lain at the landing with its crew conspicuously not looking at us. "Yes."

"What was her name?"

"I didn't see a name. Her transom was—"

"Painted over," Casper said. "Fresh paint. I saw her again yesterday, anchored off the confluence, and her crew bought ice and fuel and nothing else, and this morning she was gone." He set something on the chart table between us. A coin. Not a coin — a token, brass, stamped with a stylized wave and a serial number. "One of the twins traded tobacco for it at the confluence. A boy was showing it around, asking whether anyone had seen a steamboat with a patched hull heading north. Asking specifically about her captain."

My mouth had gone dry. "A paid informant."

"A paid spotter. That token is a syndicate scrip — redeemable at any of their factor houses on the lower river. Somebody has bought eyes on this stretch of water, and the eyes are reporting our position, our heading, and our schedule." He picked the token up and turned it in the lamplight, and his face did something complicated and brief — grief's distant cousin, passing through. "Twenty years I've run this river by being worth less than the trouble of noticing. One week hauling government paper, and I'm worth watching."

"Casper." I made myself say it plainly, because he deserved plainness and because I was afraid that if I wrapped it in anything softer I wouldn't be able to say it at all. "If they were willing to take seven surveyors off a mountainside without a trace, they are willing to take us. Whatever Halloran found, it's worth more than seven lives to them. Possibly more than seven plus five."

He was quiet for a moment. Down the deck, forward, I could hear the twins' voices, low and overlapping, and Bao's engine ticking as it cooled, and Marisol humming something tuneless over dishwater — the ordinary music of the Kestrel, which until tonight had sounded to me like safety and now sounded like something that could be taken.

"Here's what I know about rivers," Casper said at last. "You can't outrun what's already downstream of you. You can only decide what you'll be carrying when it reaches you." He set the brass token down on my chart, precisely in the middle, like a marker on a map of trouble. "Get some sleep, surveyor. Tomorrow we go north into the upper valley, and we find what Halloran sealed — before the people who paid for that boy's tobacco do."

He left. I sat with the token and the drum trace and the falling glass of the barometer, and outside the cabin window the stars wheeled over the black shoulder of the range, and somewhere up there in the high valley, seven people's boots stood ranked in the cold beside a kettle that was somehow, impossibly, still warm.

At dawn, Sefa woke the whole boat by shouting from the bow.

There was a lantern burning on the eastern ridge. Three flashes and two. Salvage code.

*Answer received,* it said. *We see you.*
