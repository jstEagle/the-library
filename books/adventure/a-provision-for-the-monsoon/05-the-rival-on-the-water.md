## 5. The Rival on the Water

The river told him first.

Casper had learned to read the Kestrel the way other men read letters — slowly, with his whole attention, trusting nothing to summary. And for two days now the river had been saying the same thing over and over: *you are not alone up here.* A heron lifting off a bend too early. Fishermen on the far bank who didn't fish, their nets dry as old rope. Smoke from a cookfire that moved when they moved, keeping pace a half-day behind like a dog that hadn't decided whether to bite.

He said nothing about it. That was the captain's oldest trick, and he'd been practicing it longer than most captains had been alive: you kept the worry in your own hull and let the crew believe the water was ordinary. Ordinary water made ordinary men. Frightened water made mistakes.

But Bao noticed anyway. Of course she did. She spent her days belowdecks with her ear against the engine like a physician listening to a chest, and engines heard everything that touched the hull.

"Something's following us," she said on the third morning, appearing at the rail beside him with two cups of tea, handing him one without asking. She was a small woman built like a rivet, forearms scarred with old burns, and she never wasted words the way she never wasted fuel. "Screw wash. Astern. Big boat, shallow draft, running quiet."

"How long?"

"Since the gorge mouth." She sipped. "They're good. Better than the last ones."

"The last ones," Casper said, "were smugglers."

"These aren't smugglers." Bao looked out at the brown water sliding past, at the walls of green that leaned in from both banks, dripping with the season's first humidity. "Smugglers run dark. This one's showing us its smoke on purpose. That's not hiding. That's *herding*."

Casper drank his tea and thought about the informant in the downstream village — the man with the syndicate's coin in his pocket and the Kestrel's schedule in his mouth — and felt the old familiar cold settle into his shoulders, the cold of being known. Twenty years of moving light and leaving no ledger behind, and it had taken one sealed case of government paper to make him legible again.

"They want us to know they're there," he said.

"Then they want something else too." Bao set down her cup. "Because knowing isn't enough. Nobody shadows a boat for three days just to be seen. They're pushing us somewhere."

She went back below, and Casper stood at the wheel a long time, watching the river herd them.

* * *

Ada Okonkwo came up to the pilothouse at midday, which was itself a message. In four days aboard she had learned the boat's geography the way she presumably learned all terrain — by instrument and inference — and she knew the pilothouse was Casper's territory, entered only when entering was worth the cost.

"Captain." She set her notebook on the chart table. She was tall and precise, with the kind of stillness that made you aware of your own fidgeting, and her eyes went to the water astern before they came to his face. "We're being followed."

"I know."

"You've known since yesterday."

"Since the gorge mouth."

Her jaw tightened. He watched her decide whether to be angry about it and then decide, visibly, to file the anger away for later use. She was economical that way. It was one of the few things about her he'd come to respect.

"In my line of work," she said, "unreported information is called a survey error."

"In mine it's called Tuesday." He turned back to the wheel. The channel ahead bent hard to starboard around a drowned willow island, the current stacking up against the outside bank in long brown ridges. "You want to tell me who's back there? Because I have a guess, but my guesses are twenty years stale."

Ada was quiet for a moment. Through the open window came the sounds of the afternoon watch — Sefa and Lio forward, coiling lines and arguing about knots in the easy bickering rhythm of twins, Marisol's voice rising from the galley scuttle singing something low and off-key about a woman and a train.

"The team that vanished," Ada said finally. "The six above Widow's Gorge. They weren't just mapping elevation and flow rates. They were doing cadastral work. Boundaries. Title." Her hand rested flat on the notebook, as if holding it down against a wind he couldn't feel. "That work has value to exactly two kinds of people, Captain. The people it would give land back to. And the people it would take it away from. Only one of those kinds can afford a salvage boat."

"Voss," Casper said.

He watched her surprise flicker and die, quickly mastered. "You know the name."

"I know the name the way you know weather coming. You haven't seen it yet, but the birds are all flying wrong." He tapped the chart. "Voss runs salvage out of Port Meridian. Or she did, ten years ago, when I still bothered learning names. She works cheap and deniable, and she doesn't lose boats. If someone in the capital wanted your survey team's work recovered or destroyed without fingerprints, she's who they hire."

"And you're certain it's her?"

"No." He let the word sit. "But whoever's back there is running a forty-foot steel hull with a shallow draft and a muffled exhaust, and there are maybe three boats on this river that fit that description, and two of them are Voss's."

Ada absorbed this. Then, in a different voice — quieter, more careful — she asked the question he'd been waiting four days for her to ask.

"What happens when they stop following and start boarding?"

Casper looked at the river. The drowned willow slid past to port. Somewhere behind them, patient as tide, the smoke kept pace.

"That," he said, "depends on the sandbars."

* * *

He chose the ground himself, because if the river was going to become contested territory, then by God he would pick where the border ran.

There was a reach thirty miles upstream that every pilot on the Kestrel called the Needle's Eye, though no chart named it, because charts were drawn by men who surveyed from the air and the Needle's Eye only revealed itself to boats. The channel narrowed between two great sand spits that migrated every season, fed by a side stream on the north bank, and the deep water threaded through them in an S-curve no wider than the Kestrel twice over. To a stranger it looked like open braided water, wide and inviting, dotted with bars that might be firm or might be quicksand depending on the week and the rain. To Casper it was a room he'd furnished himself. He knew which bar had grown a foot since spring. He knew where the current had cut a new throat through the eastern spit after the last flood, deep enough to swing a boat through if you dared it, shallow enough to tear the bottom off anything that tried it slow.

He brought the Kestrel into the reach at late afternoon, with the sun low and flat on the water and the light doing what low light does to braided channels — erasing the difference between sand and water, turning the whole world to hammered gold.

"Sefa. Lio. Sounding poles fore and aft, and eyes on me, not the water. When I call marks, you call them back." He rang down for half ahead and felt the engine answer, steady as a heartbeat. "Marisol — get anything loose lashed down. Bao—" he hesitated, then said it plainly, because the hour for tricks was passing, "—they'll likely try to force us in the narrows. Whatever they do to us, we may need to do some of it back. Have the engine ready to take abuse."

Bao grinned, which from Bao was roughly equivalent to another woman drawing a knife. "Ready since the gorge mouth, Captain."

Ada took the second seat in the pilothouse without asking and said nothing, which he permitted, because her eyes were good and her silence was better than most men's help.

Behind them, right on schedule, the smoke grew a boat.

She came on fast once the reach opened — a black steel hull with a raked bow and a wheelhouse like a gun turret, no name visible, no flag, running the braided water with the confidence of a boat that had paid someone for soundings. Which meant she'd bought the same informant, or a better one. But no informant could sell what the river had done in the last three weeks. Casper watched her come and felt the strange calm that always arrived in him when the arithmetic got simple: *she doesn't know the ground. I do.*

"She's gaining," Ada said.

"She's supposed to gain. Look at her line." The black boat was cutting straight up the middle of the reach, straight as ambition, straight toward the place where the middle was a lie. "She thinks the channel runs true. Everyone thinks the channel runs true until the river tells them otherwise."

The Kestrel swung left at the first spit, close enough that Sefa's pole found bottom at nine feet and he sang it back hoarsely, and the black boat held her course a quarter mile astern, closing, closing, and Casper understood suddenly what she was doing — not chasing, *compressing*, driving them into the narrows at speed so that they'd have no room to maneuver when she came alongside. It was a salvage man's move. Boarding wasn't done at rest; it was done at speed, hook lines and fenders and overwhelming tonnage, and Voss — if it was Voss — wanted the Kestrel pinned against the western spit with nowhere to go but onto her deck.

"Captain," Ada said quietly, "what is she doing?"

"What I'd do." He turned the wheel a spoke, easing the Kestrel into the S-curve's first bend, feeling the current take the stern and walk it. "Which is why we won't be where she wants us."

The narrows closed in. Green walls rose on either side, the sand spits gleaming wet and treacherous, and the channel bent away to starboard so sharply that the far bank seemed to swing across their bow like a door. Casper read the water the way he'd read it ten thousand times — the wrinkles on the surface that meant sand, the smooth glassy tongue that meant depth, the driftwood riding the seam of the current — and he put the Kestrel exactly where the river wanted her, which was nowhere any chart would ever say.

Then he did the thing he'd been saving.

At the apex of the S, where the new flood-cut throat ran through the eastern spit, he rang down full ahead and swung the Kestrel hard to port, off the honest channel entirely, straight at what looked like solid sand.

He heard Lio yelp forward. He heard Ada's breath catch — a small, involuntary sound, the cartographer's horror at a vessel leaving the mapped world. The spit rushed at them, gold in the low light, and Casper held the wheel steady and trusted the river, trusted the three-week-old cut he'd read on the way downstream a month ago, trusted the glassy tongue of deeper water threading the sand like a vein.

The Kestrel crossed the spit's shoulder in eight feet of water, her hull shivering, sand hissing along her keel like static — and then the cut took her, the current seizing the bow and swinging it through, and they were through, into the narrow throat between the spits, the honest channel abandoned behind them.

And the black boat, committed to her compression, driving hard for the point where the Kestrel had been — met the sand.

It happened almost gently, at first. Her bow lifted. Her screws bit mud and roared. Then her momentum, forty feet of steel at twelve knots, carried her up onto the bar with a grinding crunch that came across the water like a bone breaking, and she slewed sideways, hard aground, listing, her wheelhouse suddenly much taller than it had been and much more foolish.

Casper brought the Kestrel down to dead slow in the throat and let the current hold them, and he looked back at the stranded boat, and he did not smile, because smiling at rivers was how they killed you.

"Well," Bao called up the engine hatch, "that sounded expensive."

* * *

But the river wasn't finished with either of them, and neither, it turned out, was Voss.

The black boat's engine kept turning. That was the first thing. A truly grounded boat went quiet; this one kept churning, working herself, backing and filling, and Casper watched with narrowing eyes as her stern swung slowly, ponderously, free of the softest part of the bar. She'd grounded on the spit's toe, where the sand was fresh and loose, and she was light — he could see it now, no cargo, tanks probably near empty, a boat stripped down for speed and violence. She backed off the bar in a boil of mud, hung there in the gold light like a thing deciding, and then came about.

Not toward the narrows. Toward the Kestrel.

"She's not trying to board," Ada said, and her voice had gone very level, the levelness of someone reading bad instruments. "She's going to ram."

In the throat of the Needle's Eye there was nowhere to go. That was the whole design of the place — that was why he'd chosen it — and he saw now the flaw in his own cleverness, the trap he'd built for himself out of his own mastery. The channel was twice the Kestrel's width. The spits on either side would take a hull and hold it. Ahead, the S-curve bent away blind. Behind, the black boat was gathering way, her bow wave building, and standing at her rail, small in the distance but unmistakable, a figure in a long coat raised one arm — not a weapon, just an acknowledgment, almost courteous, *I see you, I have you* — and Casper understood that courtesy perfectly. It was the courtesy of one professional to another. It was also, he realized with a cold drop in his stomach, a distraction.

"Lio!" he roared through the tube. "Off the bow! Get off the—"

The black boat didn't aim for the Kestrel at all. At the last hundred yards she swung her helm and hit the *spit* — drove her steel bow deliberately up onto the eastern sandbar at the throat's mouth, plowing a wall of water and sand across the channel, and the wave she raised came down the narrows like a moving hill.

The Kestrel took it broadside.

Casper had the wheel over, trying to bow into it, and got maybe half of what he needed. The wave caught the stern and threw it; the boat rolled, sickeningly, everything not lashed going airborne; he heard Marisol's crockery die below in a single comprehensive crash, heard Bao swearing in three languages at her engine, and then the hull struck something — a snag, a stump, the buried spine of the old spit — with a crack he felt in his teeth, a deep wooden groan that ran the length of the keel like a sentence being pronounced.

Then the wave passed, and the river smoothed, and the black boat was backing off the sandbar, unhurt, unhurried, and standing off at a respectful distance in the widening water beyond the reach, where the evening was coming down purple and gold and utterly indifferent.

The figure at her rail was still watching. Then it went inside, and the boat turned downstream, and the smoke of her faded into the dusk as though none of it had happened — as though the river were simply a road she used, and today she'd merely stopped to rearrange the furniture.

* * *

They anchored that night in the lee of the western spit, and Bao found the wound at midnight.

Casper went below with the lamp while the twins pumped the bilge — taking water, not fast, but taking it, a slow insistent sloshing that no captain ever gets used to — and Bao knelt in the reeking dark of the hold with her ear against the frames like a woman listening for a heartbeat, and found it: a crack, two feet long, opened along a seam just above the garboard strake where the snag had kissed them. Not holed. Cracked. The hull had taken the blow and split along its old scars, the patches over patches that Casper had laid down over twenty years of moving, and the river had found the one seam where the past hadn't held.

"There's a village," Bao said. "Two days up. Mid-river. Good timber, good caulkers, if they'll have us."

"They'll have us," Casper said, and heard how it sounded, and didn't correct it.

Above, faintly, through the deck planks, he could hear the others — Marisol already boiling water for the crew despite the wreckage of her galley, the twins arguing in tired voices about whose watch it was, Ada's footsteps pacing the aft deck, restless, thinking. The sounds of a boat full of people who had, without anyone ever saying it, arranged themselves around each other like timbers around a keel.

And two days' run behind them, or ahead, or hidden in some side slough waiting for morning, Captain Voss was doing her own arithmetic. She hadn't sunk them. She hadn't boarded. She'd cracked them, the way you crack a nut to see if it's worth opening — and now she knew the Kestrel was wounded, and slowing, and would have to stop.

Casper climbed the ladder into the night air. The stars over the eastern hills were hard and close, and the river breathed against the hull, patient, enormous, holding all their futures in suspension the way it held the silt.

"We'll need that village," Ada said quietly, materializing at the rail beside him. "Repairs. Time. Days, maybe."

"I know."

"And she knows it too." Ada looked astern, at the empty dark where the smoke had been. "So the question, Captain, is what we do with the time she's given us. Because she hasn't given it to us for free. Nothing on this river is free anymore."

Casper watched the black water slide past the anchor chain, and thought about cracks, and seams, and the things that get in through them.

"No," he said. "It never was."
