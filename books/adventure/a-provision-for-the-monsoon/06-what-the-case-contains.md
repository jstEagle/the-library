## 6. What the Case Contains

The river rose in the night the way a liar raises his voice — not because he has more to say, but because he wants to drown out what you already know.

Ada woke to the sound of it: a low, muscular thrumming beneath the hull that hadn't been there when she'd slept, and above it, Marisol's voice calling down the companionway in three languages at once, none of them calm. She was out of her bunk before her boots were on, the sealed case already in her arms. She had taken to sleeping with one hand on it, the way a mother sleeps with a hand on a child's chest — not to hold it, exactly, but to know it was still breathing.

On deck, the Kestrel's world had turned brown and fast. The river had come up two feet since dusk; she could see it on the pilings of the fish-drying racks they were passing, dark water climbing pale wood like ink soaking up a page. Driftwood ran past in ranks — whole trees with their roots still clutching clots of bank, spinning slowly, patient as things that meant to stay.

"Side channel," Casper was saying at the wheel, his voice stripped down to its working bones. "Bao, I need everything the port engine will forgive me for."

"It forgave me nothing last Tuesday," Bao called back from below, but the engine note climbed anyway, a stubborn ascending argument.

Sefa and Lio were already forward, fending off a half-submerged fence post with boat hooks, the twins moving with that wordless economy Ada had been watching for days now — no calls between them, no gestures, just a shared shifting of weight, as if they were one person distributed across two bodies by some clerical error at birth.

"Surveyor." Casper didn't look at her. He never looked at her when he gave her orders, which she had decided was either courtesy or contempt and had not yet ruled between them. "Get below. Take anything that can't swim with you."

"The case stays with me."

"I said get below—"

"And I'm telling you the case doesn't leave my hands." She heard her own voice go flat and official, the capital accent hardening like frost on a window, and hated it, and couldn't stop it. It was the voice she'd used on junior staff, on porters, on the vanished team's last expedition leader, whose name she still could not say aloud. "You don't know what's in it."

"No," Casper agreed, and put the wheel over hard. The Kestrel leaned, groaned, came around. "That's rather the point of a sealed case."

* * *

They took refuge in an oxbow an hour later — a crescent of dead water behind a willow island where the flood current went by like a freight train without stopping for passengers. Casper backed them into the shallows, dropped both anchors bow and stern, and then stood at the rail for a long time watching the main channel, reading it the way other men read scripture: something in it, some meaning, that Ada couldn't parse and suspected he couldn't have explained either.

Marisol brought coffee that was more sugar than coffee, which Ada understood was medicine, not refreshment. Bao came up dripping from the engine room and reported, in the tone of a man announcing weather, that they'd taken water over the aft hatch and the bilge pump was doing all it could and would soon be asked to do more than it could.

"Then we lighten her," Casper said.

"She's cargo-light already."

"Then we get high." His eyes went to the case in Ada's arms. Not accusing. Just arithmetic. Everything on this boat was weight, and everything was either ballast or burden, and the case sat in some category Ada realized she alone could see.

"Open it," Sefa said.

Everyone looked at her. The twins stood together at the rail, soaked to the shoulders, Lio wringing out his shirt with the grim efficiency of someone who had done this many times. Sefa's eyes were on the case — on the wax seal, cracked now along one edge where Ada had caught it against a stanchion during the worst of it.

"You've been carrying that thing like a coffin," Sefa said. "We've been running rapids and dodging salvage crews and sleeping in our boots for a dead man's errand, and nobody's told us what we're dying for. Open it."

"Sefa." Casper's voice was quiet. It wasn't a rebuke. That was somehow worse.

"He's right that it's not mine to order," Sefa said, chin up, addressing Ada directly now. "But it's hers to answer. We're the ones on the water."

Ada looked around the deck. Marisol with her arms folded, flour dust still ghosting her forearms even in the rain. Bao, expressionless, hands jammed in pockets — but not leaving. Lio beside his sister, silent, which from him was a kind of testimony. And Casper, who had turned away from the rail to face her fully for perhaps the first time since the gorge, rain running off his jaw, waiting. Not demanding. Waiting, the way you wait at a bedside.

She thought about the seal. She thought about the man who had pressed it — Okoro, the team lead, who had sealed the case in the capital and told her, in the flat voice of a man who had seen too much country to believe in it, *if anything happens to us, take it upriver yourself. Don't trust it to anyone who gets paid by the mile.*

She thought about how everyone on this boat got paid by the mile.

And then she thought about the kettle. Warm. On the stove in an abandoned camp four hundred miles upstream, with no tracks leading away from it in any direction. Whatever had happened to Okoro's team, they had not left in haste, and they had not left in fear, and they had not left at all. Someone had wanted the world to think they simply walked off the edge of the map. And the people who wanted that were the same people, she was now nearly certain, who had paid a downstream informant to watch this boat, and who sent Captain Voss after them like a dog after a wound.

These people had bled for her errand already. Lio had a rope burn across his palm from the gorge approach that he never mentioned and never bandaged properly. The hull had a crack in it patched with canvas and pitch and Bao's blasphemy. They deserved — no. That wasn't the word. Deserve was a ledger word, and she was beginning to understand that nothing on the Kestrel was kept in ledgers.

"They found the grants," she said.

The rain filled the silence for a moment.

"Okoro's team wasn't surveying timber quotas," Ada said. "That was the cover story. They were running title searches. Chain-of-title, parcel by parcel, village by village, all the way up the valley system. Forty years ago the land syndicate registered these valleys as *terra nullius* — unclaimed — and the colonial office rubber-stamped it, and every family that had worked this river since before there were maps got reclassified as squatters on their own ground." Her voice was steady. She made it be steady. "Okoro found the original grants. Crown-issued, pre-syndicate, never extinguished. Legally, the villages own everything from the lower terraces to the snow line. Legally, the syndicate has been collecting rent for a generation on land that was never theirs."

Bao had gone very still. Ada noticed that the way you notice a held breath.

"This case is the originals," she said. "Sealed copies, countersigned, with the registry numbers. If they reach a magistrate who isn't bought, they undo forty years of dispossession in a single filing. Every village on this river. Every one."

"And if they don't," Marisol said quietly.

"Then they're paper. Wet paper, eventually. Or ash. Or they end up in a syndicate vault, which is the same thing." Ada shifted the case in her arms. "There are people who would burn the whole valley to keep this from being read aloud. I think some of those people are behind us on the water. I think that's why Okoro is missing. I think—" and here her voice did finally crack, one hairline fracture, quickly plastered, "—I think he sealed this knowing what it would cost, and sent me because I was the only cartographer he trusted and the only one expendable enough to survive the trip."

Nobody spoke. The river went by, enormous and indifferent, hauling the mountains' melt toward the sea.

It was Lio who broke it, in his rare, unhurried way. "So it's not government work."

"It stopped being government work the moment I boarded."

"Then whose work is it?"

"Mine," Ada said. "I suppose it's mine."

Lio nodded slowly, as if she'd answered a different question correctly. Then he looked at his sister, and his sister looked at Marisol, and Marisol looked at Bao, and Ada watched something pass around the deck of the Kestrel that had no words attached to it — a relay of glances, quick and old, the shorthand of people who had been voting on things informally for years.

"We should vote," Sefa said. "Properly. Out loud."

"On what?" Casper asked. His voice was careful. Too careful.

"On whether we carry it," Sefa said. "All the way. Past the gorge, past the pass, into whatever's waiting. Whether this stops being your conscripted run and becomes ours."

Casper's jaw worked. Ada watched him watch the river, and understood suddenly that he was not deciding — he was looking for a way out of having to decide, some sandbar of neutrality he could run aground on. She had met men like him in the survey service. Men who believed that if you never planted anything, nothing could be taken from you. She had once believed the map itself was neutral ground. She knew better now. There was no such thing as a blank space. There was only space someone had paid to keep blank.

"Vote," Casper said finally. "Fine. But I'm not voting."

"You have to vote," Sefa said.

"I abstain."

"You can't abstain, you're the captain—"

"Which is exactly why I abstain." For the first time all day, something moved behind his eyes that wasn't calculation. It looked almost like pain. "If I vote yes, I've chosen for all of you, and I won't do that. If I vote no, I'm ordering you to abandon something worth doing, and I won't do that either. So I abstain, and I'll steer wherever this boat decides to go. That's the only honest thing I've got."

The rain ticked on the deck boards. Somewhere below, the bilge pump changed pitch, laboring.

"For," Marisol said. No hesitation, no explanation. She said it the way she salted a pot — automatically, generously, as if the question had been settled long ago inside her and the rest of them were only catching up.

"For," said Lio.

"For," said Sefa, and then, grinning through the rain, "for, obviously, I called the vote."

They all looked at Bao.

Bao looked at the deck. At his hands. At the brown water sliding past the hull, going somewhere with terrible purpose. When he raised his head, his face was composed in that particular stillness Ada had learned, in the survey camps, to read as the loudest thing in the room.

"For," Bao said.

* * *

They rode out the flood in the oxbow through the afternoon and into the dark. Ada sat in the galley with the case open on the table for the first time since the capital, and the crew gathered around it the way people gather around a fire — not touching, just warming themselves on the fact of it.

Under the oil lamp, the papers were beautiful. She hadn't expected that. She had expected bureaucratic gray, but the original grants were written on rag paper in a clerk's careful copperplate, each parcel described not in coordinates but in relationship — *from the fig tree that leans over the water, downstream to the stone the children dive from.* The empire drew boundaries; the grants drew homes. She found herself reading the descriptions aloud, softly, and Marisol listening with her eyes closed, and once, when Ada reached a grant for a village on the middle terraces with a name Marisol recognized, the cook opened her eyes and said, "Read that one again," and Ada did, twice.

Casper stayed at the doorway. He didn't come in. He stood with his shoulder against the frame, arms crossed, watching the papers the way he'd watched the flooded channel — reading something in them, or refusing to.

Near midnight, when the others had gone to their bunks, Ada began the slow work of repacking: oilcloth, then tin, then the case itself, and a fresh seal from her kit, because the old one was broken and broken seals invited questions she didn't want to answer at checkpoints.

"You could have lied to us," Casper said from the doorway.

She looked up. "Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

Because you would have known, she almost said. Because your crew votes and your cook feeds people and your deckhand twins fend off driftwood like it's a religion, and lies don't survive on a boat this small. But what she said was: "Because you paid off an elder's debt in Kamari village and left before dawn so no one could thank you. I heard about it from the ferryman. A man like that doesn't lie well, and I didn't want to make him try."

Something crossed his face — annoyance, mostly, at being known. She was learning to treasure that look.

"The pass closes in nine days," he said instead. "Maybe eight, if the snows come early. Voss is somewhere behind us with a faster boat and better guns. And now we're carrying the one object on this river that everybody with money wants destroyed." He pushed off from the doorframe. "I want you to understand what you've done tonight. You haven't given us a mission. You've given us a reason to be hunted."

"I know."

"Do you?" He came one step into the galley, and the lamplight found the lines around his eyes, twenty years of squinting at water. "Because there's a difference between being hunted and knowing you're bait. From here to the pass, every person on this boat is worth less to me alive than that case is to them dead. I need to know you've done that arithmetic."

Ada closed the case. Set her palms flat on the warm tin. "I've done it every night since Okoro sealed it," she said. "I did it again just now. The number doesn't change."

He studied her for a long moment, and whatever he was looking for, he seemed — not to find it, exactly, but to stop looking. "Eight days," he said. "Sleep when you can."

He turned to go, and she said, "Captain. You abstained."

"I abstain a lot. It's a hobby."

"Why?"

He paused in the doorway, half in lamplight, half in the dark of the passage, and for a moment she thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "Because the last time I chose for people," he said, "they drowned."

And he was gone, his footsteps fading up the ladder to the wheelhouse, leaving Ada alone with the lamp and the case and the sound of the flood going by in the dark — and with the new knowledge, settling over her colder than the rain, that the man steering them toward the pass was not afraid of Voss, or the syndicate, or the snow.

He was afraid of the vote he'd just cast by refusing to cast it.

Above the oxbow, far upriver, thunder muttered behind the peaks — though whether it was weather or avalanche, at this distance, no instrument she owned could say.
