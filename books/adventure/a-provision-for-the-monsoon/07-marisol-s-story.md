## 7. Marisol's Story

The village of Tern Hollow announced itself by smell before sight — woodsmoke and wet clay and the green rot of reed beds, all of it carried downriver on a wind that had been waiting three days for something to push against. Marisol stood at the Kestrel's rail with her hands flour-white to the wrist and watched the jetty come on, crooked as an old man's spine, and felt the particular ache she always felt when they stopped anywhere longer than a tide.

Casper never stopped long. That was the rule nobody spoke. Water level, that was his measure — stay only as long as the river stayed, and the river never stayed. But the hull had a crack in it now, a long splintering whisper below the waterline where Voss's boat had kissed them too hard on the sandbar, and Bao had said the word *careening* in the flat voice he used for bad news, and Casper had looked at the chart and then at the sky and said, "Tern Hollow," like a man choosing which tooth to have pulled.

So they would stop. Days, maybe. Long enough for roots to try.

Marisol wiped her hands on her apron and went back to her galley, because bread did not care about hull cracks or captains' moods, and bread was the one thing aboard the Kestrel that behaved exactly as it should. You gave it warmth and patience and it gave you back something whole. She had built her life around that exchange, more or less, though she'd learned it late, and the hard way, and in a house that no longer existed except in a syndicate ledger as *Parcel 44, acquired.*

*Acquired.* She still tasted the word sometimes, bitter as scorched fat.

* * *

They warped the Kestrel against the village jetty by midafternoon, Sefa and Lio hauling lines while Bao barked depths from the bow and Casper stood at the wheel doing that thing he did — reading the water like a face, every ripple a sentence, every slack patch a lie. The villagers came down to watch. They always came down to watch. A patched riverboat with a foreign name and a crew that didn't match was better than a festival.

An old woman with a walking stick made from a boat oar took one look at the hull's wound and said, "You'll want my son's slipway. And you'll want to feed him while he works, because he sulks hungry."

"He won't sulk," said a broad-shouldered man behind her, already eyeing the Kestrel's lines with professional grief. "I'll charge double and be cheerful about it."

His name was Dario, and by evening he had the Kestrel hauled out on greased skids above the waterline, hull bare to the air for the first time since Marisol had known her, and there was something almost indecent about seeing the boat that way — planking exposed, seams opened, the old patches showing like scars on a body. Lio kept circling her with his hands in his pockets, not touching, the way you circle a sleeping animal.

"She looks smaller out of the water," he said.

"She looks honest," Bao said, running a thumb along a seam. "Out here you can see everything she's survived."

Marisol brought them all food on the slipway — rice and smoked fish and the last of the pickled mango, eaten off her knees in the lamplight — and watched Casper eat standing up, facing the river, the way he always ate, like a man who expected to be called away mid-bite. Twenty years she'd cooked for this crew and he had never once sat down to a meal with his back to anything but a wall.

She knew why. She'd pieced it together over the years the way you piece together any story told entirely in its gaps. But knowing why didn't make it less lonely to watch.

That night, while the twins argued over a card game whose rules changed every hand and Bao slept sitting up against the wheelhouse door — he'd been doing that since the sandbar, and nobody mentioned it — Marisol found Casper alone at the stern, smoking, watching the village lights gutter along the bank.

"You should sleep," she said.

"I will."

"Liar."

He almost smiled. In the dark it was mostly voice. "Hull'll take two days. Maybe three if the planking's soft."

"I wasn't asking about the hull."

He said nothing to that. He was very good at nothing; twenty years of practice. Marisol leaned on the rail beside him and let the silence sit between them, because she had learned that with Casper you didn't pull stories out, you just left doors open and waited to see if anything walked through.

Nothing walked through. Something else did instead.

"Voss," he said. "Her boat was built in the same yard as ours. Same year. I checked the strakes when we were locked together." He flicked ash into the water. "Somebody's been buying up the river yards. Whole yards. You know what that means?"

"It means somebody wants to own every way off this river," Marisol said quietly.

"It means somebody thinks the river is property." He said it without heat, which was worse than heat. "Been happening for years. Little by little. Land, moorings, ferry rights. Paper eats paper."

Marisol looked at the village lights. At the reed beds where the villagers cut their winter thatch. At the dark shapes of houses that had stood, she happened to know, for four generations on land someone in the capital had recently begun calling *underutilized.*

"Casper," she said. "Do you know what happened to my farm?"

He turned his head. Not much. Just enough.

"You've never asked," she said. "Any of you. Twenty years, and nobody's ever asked me why I cook on a boat for people who won't even say the word family."

"The word costs too much," he said, so low she nearly missed it.

"Yes," she said. "I know. I paid it too."

* * *

She told him there on the stern, because the dark made it easier and because she had decided, somewhere between the slipway and the card game, that the case Ada carried — the sealed case, the paper case, the case that had made Casper's jaw go tight whenever anyone mentioned grants or inheritance — deserved to know what paper had already done to her.

"My father grew coffee," she began. "Above the middle falls, on a terrace slope my grandfather terraced with his own hands. Forty years of carrying stone. You could smell our land from the road in season — roasted green and rain, that smell. I can still make myself smell it if I'm tired enough."

The cigarette burned down unsmoked in Casper's fingers. He let it.

"There was a company — you'd know the name, everyone knows the name, it's on the shipping crates and the deed offices and the little brass plaques they put on things they've bought. They wanted the slope. Not for coffee. For the water rights underneath it. The falls fed three villages, but the paper said the water belonged to whoever held the land above it, and they meant to hold everything above everything."

"They flooded you out?"

"Worse." Marisol heard her own voice go flat and was glad of it; flat was how she'd survived the telling the first hundred times, in her head, in the dark. "They bought the debt. My father owed money — a bad season, then a worse one, the usual slow knife. They bought the debt from the bank and then they were kind to us. That's the thing nobody tells you. They were *kind*. They sent a man with soft shoes and a soft voice who drank coffee with my father every week for a month and talked about restructuring. Restructuring. As if the farm were a chair."

"And then?"

"And then one morning the river came down early." She watched the water move under the lantern light, black and patient. "Not a flood season. Not rain enough for it. But the new dam upstream — theirs, though the papers said a consortium's — needed to test its gates. They tested them in March, without warning, and the water took the lower terraces and half the drying sheds and the road out, and after that the farm was worth nothing and the debt was worth everything, and my father signed. He signed in the office with the brass plaque, and the man with soft shoes poured him a drink, and we walked out with what we could carry."

She paused. This was the part she never told anyone, the part she kept below her own waterline, and she found that tonight, on this boat, on this river, she wanted it said aloud once, in front of one witness, so that it existed outside her skull.

"I went back. Years later. I got work on a survey crew — before Ada, before any of this, just carrying stakes and holding chains — and the crew was sent to map the middle falls for the syndicate's expansion. I walked up to the terrace slope. The coffee trees were still there. Nobody had pulled them out; they'd just let them go wild. And the fruit was coming in. Nobody picking it. All that sweetness falling into the grass and rotting, season after season, because the men who owned it on paper had never once walked up the hill to see it."

Casper had gone very still. It was the stillness of a man listening to his own future being read to him.

"So when I cook," Marisol said, "for this crew, on this boat, for people who are starving in every way except the one I can fix — that's why. Because I know what it is when nobody feeds what they own. I know what it is to be land that the owner never visits. I feed this crew because somebody has to visit what matters before it goes wild and rots." She turned to look at him straight on. "And because you won't say the word, Casper, somebody has to do the work of it. Words are cheap. Bread isn't. I've been doing the word's job for twenty years and I'll keep doing it whether you ever say it or not. But I needed you to know that I know. About paper. About what it takes."

For a long moment Casper said nothing. The river said plenty — slapping the hull, worrying the jetty pilings, going about its ancient business of leaving.

"Parcel forty-four," he said finally.

Marisol's breath caught. "What?"

"That's your parcel. On their ledgers." He was looking at the water, not at her. "I've seen their ledgers. A long time ago. Another life." He ground out the dead cigarette with unnecessary force. "Your farm is listed as Parcel 44, acquired by gate test, March. There's a column next to it. Projected yield."

"I know," Marisol whispered. "I've seen it too. In the office. They showed my father the projected yield to prove the land would be *better used*."

"Yeah." His voice had gone somewhere rough. "Mine's in there too. Different column."

He didn't explain. She didn't ask. Some doors you leave shut even when you're both standing in the hallway.

But before he turned in, he said, "The case Ada's carrying. If it's what I think it is — if it's grants, real ones, countersigned — then it's not just paper to those villages. It's the difference between being visited and being owned." He looked at her then, and in the lantern light his face was older than twenty years of rivers could account for. "Thank you. For telling me."

"You didn't say anything back."

"No," he agreed. "But I listened. That's not nothing."

It wasn't. With Casper, it was practically a speech.

* * *

Marisol rose before dawn, as she always did, to get the dough started while the river was still gray and thinking. She was surprised to find the galley lamp already lit.

Sefa sat at the little table, head in her hands, and beside her lay the compass — the good brass one, her father's, the one with the hairline crack in the crystal that she polished every Sunday and that had been dying by degrees all season, the needle wandering like a drunk looking for home.

"It's done," Sefa said, and her voice cracked on the single syllable. "Bao says the pivot's worn through, says you can't buy that part anymore, says I should get a new one. A modern one. Plastic." She said *plastic* the way other people said a curse. "It was my father's. He steered by it for thirty years."

Marisol put her hand on the girl's shoulder and felt her shaking, and thought: *here it is again. The work of the word.* She was reaching for the kettle, for the universal medicine of sweet tea, when a shadow filled the galley doorway.

Casper. Dressed, awake, tool roll under his arm, which was strange because Casper did not fix things. Casper replaced things. Casper bought new rope rather than splicing old, new chain rather than mending, new everything rather than loving anything enough to repair it. Repair meant history and history meant debt and debt meant staying.

He looked at the compass for a long moment. Then he sat down across from Sefa, unrolled the tools, and picked up the instrument with a delicacy Marisol had never seen him give to anything.

"Pivot's not worn through," he said. "It's clogged. River grit in the bearing oil. Happens to every compass that lives on the water." He glanced up. "Your father never cleaned it?"

Sefa blinked. "Clean it? You can open it?"

"You can open anything," Casper said, "if you accept it might not close again the same." And he set to work.

Marisol stood at her stove and kneaded her dough and watched, out of the corner of her eye, Casper Nakamura disassemble a dead man's compass under a swinging lamp: tweezers and jeweler's screwdrivers and a patience she hadn't known lived in him, flushing the bearing with clean oil, easing the needle free, blowing grit from the jewel bearing while Sefa watched like a girl at a miracle. The dough rose under her hands. The lamp swung. Outside, the river ran on toward the gorge, toward whatever waited above Widow's Gorge, toward the sealed case and the eleven days and the snow line, all of it coming for them whether they were ready or not.

And when it was done — when the needle swung true and settled north like a bird landing — Casper closed the case, screwed it shut, and slid it back across the table without ceremony, the way you hand someone back something that was never really broken, only neglected.

"There," he said gruffly. "Don't tell anyone I can do that."

Sefa hugged the compass to her chest and couldn't speak, and Marisol turned back to her bread so neither of them would see her face.

Because she understood what she'd just seen. Repair was a language, and Casper had just spoken it fluently for the first time in twenty years — and a man who repairs instead of replaces is a man who has decided, whether he knows it yet or not, to stay.

She was still thinking about that, flour to her wrists, when the lookout knocked on the galley frame and said, in the careful voice reserved for bad news:

"Captain. There's a man on the jetty asking for you. Says he's from above the gorge." A pause. "Says the survey team's instruments have stopped logging."
