## 8. The Gorge Portage

My grandmother used to say that a river is a rope the mountains are pulling taut, and somewhere along its length there is always a place where the rope frays. Widow's Gorge was where the Kestrel River frayed.

We heard it before we saw it — a sound like the sky clearing its throat, low and continuous, a vibration that came up through the deck plates and into the soles of my feet and settled somewhere behind my sternum and stayed there for two days. Sefa hears things first; that has always been my job on this boat, the ears while Lio does the seeing, though we're twins and he'd argue the point until the stars burned out. But I heard the gorge first, and I wished I hadn't.

The walls rose out of the morning mist like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down to die a thousand years ago and was still deciding about it. Grey stone streaked black with old water-marks. The river narrowed from a hundred meters of braided channels to a single throat maybe thirty wide, and through that throat the whole Kestrel went from a walking pace to a gallop, white-maned, furious, throwing spray twenty feet up the rock faces.

"No boat goes through that," Lio said, standing beside me at the rail. He said it in the flat voice he uses when he already knows the answer and wants it said aloud by someone else so it becomes real.

"No boat goes through that," I agreed.

"Then how—"

"We don't go through." Casper's voice came from behind us, quiet as always, the way a knife is quiet. He stood at the wheel with one hand resting on the spoke, looking at the gorge the way other men look at an old enemy across a market square — with recognition, without surprise. "We go over."

* * *

Over, it turned out, meant apart.

Casper beached us at a shelf of gravel below the gorge mouth called, according to Ada's charts, Portage Head, and called by everyone who actually worked the river, Marisol told me later, the Widow's Front Step. There were iron rings driven into the rock there, ancient ones, furred with rust and lichen, and a winch house whose roof had fallen in but whose great drum still turned when Bao oiled it, groaning like a man asked to remember his youth.

"The Kestrel comes apart," Casper said, and the crew stood in a half-circle around him on the gravel while the gorge roared behind him like an audience that disapproved. "Hull off the keel. Engine out. Wheelhouse down. Everything light enough to carry goes over the goat track on the north wall. The heavy pieces go up the winch line to the shelf above the first rapid, then we reassemble her in the dead water below the second fall and run the last three miles to the upper landing." He looked at each of us in turn, and I noticed — not for the first time — that he never looked at any of us longer than he looked at the others. Fairness as a kind of distance. "It takes four days if nothing goes wrong."

"And if something goes wrong?" Ada asked. She had the sealed case strapped across her back even here, even now, and she stood with her arms crossed against the spray-chill like a woman refusing to admit the weather existed.

"Then it takes longer," Casper said, "or it doesn't happen at all," and that was all the answer anyone got, because it was the only answer there was.

I will tell you what taking a boat apart feels like, because nobody tells you this: it feels like undressing someone you love who is asleep, and being terrified they'll wake. Bao went into the engine bay with his tools and his lamp and his murmured apologies — he talks to the engine, has talked to it for years, calls it *old woman*, and I have never once heard him call it anything else — and Lio and I stripped the deck, coiling lines, unshipping rails, passing crates up the goat track to the cache point Casper had marked. Marisol cooked double rations without being asked, because that is what Marisol does when the world gets frightening: she makes it smell like garlic and onions until it forgets to be frightening.

And Casper — Casper touched every piece of her. That was the thing I watched, those first hours, the thing I didn't understand yet. He ran his hands along the hull seams as they came free. He checked the scarred patch over the cargo hold where some previous owner had run her onto something hard years before we ever owned her. When the wheelhouse came down on its skids and he stepped inside it one last time before the crane tackle took it, he stood at the wheel for a long moment with both hands on the spokes, and I looked away, because some griefs you're not invited to.

She wasn't *the boat*. I want to say that clearly, because I didn't understand it yet myself. She was the floor under our feet when the river got rough. She was the reason Lio and I had stopped running from the debt collectors in Saliki harbor four years ago — because Casper had taken one look at us, two skinny kids stealing rope off his stern, and said, "You tie a bowline better than my deckhand did. Want breakfast?" She was the smell of Marisol's kitchen and the clank of Bao's hammer and the particular creak of the third stair that meant Casper was coming up to check on the night watch. A boat is wood and iron. This was never going to be just a boat. I knew it the way you know a storm is coming, from the pressure in your teeth, and I didn't know it yet at all, both at once.

* * *

The winch line was the heart of the operation, and everyone knew it, which is why what happened next should have been impossible and wasn't.

Bao rigged it on the second day: steel cable running from the drum in the old winch house up the cliff face to an anchor point above the first rapid, with pulley stations bolted into the iron rings our predecessors had left — men dead fifty, eighty, a hundred years, their rings still holding, their names worn off every plaque. The hull came up the cliff in slings, forty tons of patched teak and iron rising slow and swaying against the grey stone, and every time she swung I held my breath, and every time she settled I let it out, and by the third hull section I had used up all the breath I had saved for anything else.

Voss's salvage boat had been seen twice that day, once far downstream, once as a smudge of smoke beyond the gorge mouth, never close, never gone. Casper doubled the night watch anyway. He didn't say why. He never said why. He arranged the world and let you read the arrangement.

It was on the third day, with the hull sections up and the engine halfway raised, that Lio drew the midday watch at the lower pulley station, and I drew the drum, and between us stretched sixty meters of cliff and cable and the roar of the falls filling every space where a warning might have lived.

I saw it afterward the way you see lightning — everything normal, normal, normal, and then the whole sky rearranged. The engine sling was rising, Bao's pride and joy swinging in its cradle of chain, and Lio was guiding the tail line at the lower station, leaning back into the pull, boots braced on wet rock, and I was watching him because watching Lio work is like watching your own hands from outside, and then —

The cable sang.

Not the working hum it made under load, which I'd been hearing for two days and could have identified blindfolded. A different note. Higher. Wrong. A note like something letting go.

The anchor point above the first rapid — the upper one, the one bolted into rock that Ada herself had tested with a hammer and a geologist's ear — sheared. Not slowly. All at once, the way trust goes. And the whole load, the whole geometry of tension that Bao had spent three days building, transferred down the line to the lower station, to the old iron ring, to the tail line, to Lio.

He didn't scream. That's the part I keep returning to, the part that wakes me up some nights even now, after everything. He didn't scream. The line hauled him off the ledge and into the air and he made one short sound, more surprise than fear, *huh*, like a man reading a bill he can't believe, and then the river had him.

The lower rapids at the gorge mouth aren't the worst water on the Kestrel but they are water with intent. It took him sideways into the throat and folded him under and I was already moving, down the ladder, down the scree, no thought in me at all except the oldest one, the twin-one, the one that predates language: *mine, that one is mine.* I heard Bao shouting behind me. I heard the winch drum freewheeling, screaming, cable whipping loose up the cliff like a live thing dying. None of it mattered. Nothing has ever mattered less.

Lio surfaced once, thirty meters downstream, an arm and a shock of black hair and then gone again, sucked under by the seam where two currents argued. I hit the shallows at the tail of the gravel bar and lost my footing on the round stones and went down and got up and went in deeper, and the cold closed on my chest like a hand.

And then Casper passed me.

I didn't see him arrive. That's the truth and it sounds like a lie and I've decided I don't care. One moment I was alone in the water reaching for a brother who wasn't there, and the next moment Casper Nakamura went by me like an otter, fully clothed, boots and all, swimming not toward where Lio had gone under but toward where he would come up — upstream of the seam, angled across the current, reading the water the way Ada reads her instruments, the way I imagine a man reads a face he has loved a long time. Because that's what it was. I saw it plainly, in the middle of all that terror. He wasn't searching for Lio. He knew where Lio was. The river had told him. The river tells him everything; it has been telling him things for twenty years, and he listens the way the rest of us pray.

Lio came up in the eddy below the seam, exactly where the water folds back on itself, the dead spot every drowning man aims for without knowing it, and Casper's arm was there. He caught him under the chin, turned him, and rode the eddy's slow circle with Lio's head locked against his shoulder, and then he kicked for the shallow tail of the bar, not fighting the current, never fighting it, using it, letting the river hand them over the way you'd hand a package across a counter.

They came ashore ten meters from me. Lio was coughing, alive, furious, alive. Casper knelt in the shallows with the water breaking around his knees, holding the back of Lio's neck the way you hold a child's, saying nothing, breathing hard, the only man on that riverbank making any sound at all besides the gorge.

"You read the seam," Lio gasped, when he could talk. "You — you went straight to it—"

"The river showed me," Casper said. Which was not an explanation. Which was the whole explanation.

* * *

The sabotage we found within the hour, because Ada found it, because Ada finds everything.

The upper anchor bolt hadn't failed. It had been helped. Someone had cut the shank three-quarters through with a hacksaw and filled the cut with wax and rock dust — patient work, hours of it, done sometime in the dark of the last two nights while the watch changed. The wax hid the cut. The load finished the job. Whoever did it wanted the line to fail under full weight, at the worst moment, and wanted it to look like the mountain's fault.

"Voss," Bao said, and spat, which Bao never does, which is how you know what the word cost him.

"Voss's paymaster," Ada corrected quietly. She was crouched at the anchor point with the broken bolt in her gloved fingers, turning it, reading the cut ends like text. "Voss is a captain. This is a syndicate's patience." She looked up at Casper, and something passed between them that I couldn't read — a question asked and not quite answered, a door opened a crack and shut again. I have thought about that look many times since. At the time I filed it away with all the other things about Ada Okonkwo I didn't yet understand.

Casper stood at the base of the cliff with the ruined cable pooled at his feet like a shed skin, and I waited for it. We all waited for it. Every one of us knew the arithmetic he was doing, because we'd all learned it from him: four days become seven, the season shortens, the snows don't care about our schedule, and a man who has survived twenty years on this river has survived by knowing when a venture is dead and cutting it loose. Cut losses. Keep moving. Never stay anywhere longer than the water level. That was the law of Casper Nakamura, carved plainer than any of the survey team's benchmarks.

He looked at the wrecked winch line. He looked at the gorge. He looked, last and longest, at Lio — soaked, shivering, wrapped in Marisol's blankets, glaring up at the cliff as if he intended to fight it personally.

"Rig the spare cable," Casper said. "Double the watch. We finish the portage."

"Casper." My own voice, surprising me. "They'll try again. Next time they won't bother with the rigging. They'll wait for one of us."

"I know," he said.

"So we should—" I gestured at the downstream smoke, at the whole sensible world. "Turn back. Go around. Wait them out. You always say—"

"I know what I always say." He picked up a length of the cut cable and coiled it slowly, methodically, a man giving his hands something to do while the rest of him decided something. "Twenty years I've kept moving because moving means nothing can be taken from you. You can't burn down a man who's never built anything. You can't drown a man who never moors." He looked down at the coil in his hands, and then at the Kestrel's hull sections stacked on the high shelf above us, pale and patient against the grey stone, waiting to be whole again. "But they've already taken something. Today. They tried to take my crew, and they took my sleep, and they took four days I don't have." He handed the coil to Bao. "So we finish. And when Voss comes to see why we're still here, he can ask the river why. The river and I have an understanding."

Nobody laughed, but something loosened in all of us at once, the way a knot loosens, and Marisol went back to her fire and started cooking like a woman preparing a siege, and Bao began walking the cliff with the spare cable over his shoulder, talking to it too, telling it it would do better than its brother had.

* * *

That night, camped under the overhang at Portage Head with the gorge roaring its one long syllable into the dark, I woke to voices and lay still and listened, the way I always listen, because listening is my job and my habit and my sin.

It was Lio and Casper, at the edge of the firelight. Lio had found him the way I find him sometimes, awake at odd hours, keeping the watch nobody assigned him.

"Why did you jump?" Lio was asking. "You could have thrown me a line. Anyone else would have thrown a line."

"A line reaches where the thrower stands," Casper said. "You weren't there."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have." A pause. The fire cracked. Somewhere above us, loose stone ticked down the cliff face like a clock counting something. "When you came aboard, four years ago, you stole forty feet of my stern line. Do you know why I didn't put you off?"

"Because Sefa cried."

"Because you tied the bowline first," Casper said. "You stole from a moving boat, in the dark, and you tied off your prize before you jumped. Any fool can steal. Very few people secure what they've taken." Another pause, longer. "I've spent twenty years securing nothing. Tonight the river reminded me what I actually have. It isn't cargo."

Lio didn't say anything for a while. Then, very quietly, in the voice he only uses with me, the voice without armor: "The boat almost died today."

And Casper said — and this is the sentence I will carry, the sentence that changed the shape of the word in my mouth forever — "No. The *Kestrel* almost died today. The boat is what she is when nobody loves her."

I lay in the dark with my eyes open and felt something settle into place in my chest, permanent as ballast. The Kestrel. Not the boat. The Kestrel, who had carried us, who had been carried, who was lying in pieces on a mountain shelf tonight like a saint dismembered for safekeeping, waiting for her people to make her whole.

Two days later we ran the last three miles to the upper landing with her reassembled, patched, lighter than she'd been in years, and when she took the first clean water below the gorge and the spray came green-gold over the bow, Lio threw both arms around the mast and hugged her, actually hugged her, and nobody laughed at him, and nobody ever called her *the boat* again.

Ada came aft that evening with her charts and her sealed case and stood beside me at the rail for a long time, watching the walls of the gorge fall away behind us into the evening haze.

"Your instruments," I said, nodding at the case. "What are they telling you?"

She was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. Then she unclipped the barometer housing from her belt and held it so I could see the needle, trembling at the bottom of its arc like a bird that knows the season has turned.

"Falling," she said. "Faster than it should. Sefa — the monsoon isn't eleven days out anymore."
