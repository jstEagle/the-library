## 9. Snow Line

The trees gave out the way a voice gives out at the end of a long sentence — not all at once, but thinning, hoarser, until there was nothing left to say and only stone remained.

Ada Okonkwo had watched treelines before. She had drawn them on maps for six years, a neat hatched line with a label in her own careful hand: *limit of forest growth*. It had always been an abstraction, a boundary between one kind of shading and another. Now she stood inside it, boots in gravel the color of old bone, and understood that no map she had ever made had told the truth about this place. The truth was that above a certain altitude the world simply stopped pretending to be gentle.

The Kestrel's crew had made camp in a bowl of granite below the first real snowfields, where a stream ran so cold it burned. The boat itself was lashed and tarped at the head of navigation, two days behind them now, looking from here like something abandoned — a scarred brown hull against grey scree, small as a coffin lid. Ada kept glancing back at it. She couldn't help it. Somewhere between the gorge portage and this morning's climb, the vessel had stopped being *the boat* in her mind too, and become the Kestrel, a name spoken the way you'd speak of a person who'd bled for you.

She set up the theodolite on its tripod while the light was still good, and then she set up the barometer beside it, and then she took out the dead team's logbook — Okafor's logbook, her missing colleague's, recovered from the camp above Widow's Gorge where the kettle had still been warm — and began, for the fourth time since dawn, to do arithmetic she did not want to finish.

The instruments were unambiguous. That was the cruelty of instruments. They had no talent for mercy.

*Landfall: eleven days,* said the pressure series, plotted against the monsoon onset curves she carried in her head from a decade of coastal stations. *Snow closure: nine.* She had run the numbers against three separate indicators — the barometric trend, the cloud formation over the southern range, the behavior of the meltwater streams, which were already running high and dirty with the first flush of seasonal snowmelt. All three agreed. The pass ahead of them would close under new snow in nine days, give or take a day either side depending on how the jet behaved, and the monsoon would reach the lower river eleven days after today, drowning every route back down and sealing the valley in for a season.

Nine days to cross a range that the government's own maps showed as four weeks' travel by the established route.

She heard Casper coming before she saw him. He walked quietly for a big man, but he had a rhythm to him, a deliberate placement of each foot, as though the ground were a deck and he was reading its swell. She'd noticed it the first week aboard: Casper Nakamura did not walk over terrain, he negotiated with it.

"The twins say the stream's rising," he said, stopping at the edge of her work area without entering it. He never entered her work area. She had catalogued that too — the careful distances he kept, as if proximity were a debt he might be asked to repay.

"It is." She didn't look up from the logbook. "Meltwater. It'll peak midday and drop by evening. Normal."

"Nothing about this is normal." He crouched, forearms on his knees, and studied the western horizon where the clouds were building — great white towers with grey undersides, stacked like cargo nobody had secured. "That's not normal."

"No," Ada admitted. "It isn't."

She closed the logbook and stood, and decided — because she had decided somewhere over the last hundred miles that this man deserved the same honesty she demanded from her instruments — to tell him everything at once, the way you pull a tooth.

"Eleven days until monsoon landfall on the coast. Nine until snow closes the pass. The standard route through the range takes twenty-six days at best, and we've used up our margin getting here. If we take the mapped route, we don't make the valley. We don't even make the far side. We winter on the mountain, or we die trying, or we turn around and spend the wet season trapped below the gorge while the syndicate does whatever it likes with the villages." She let the silence sit for exactly as long as it needed to. "Those are the options. I've checked them twice."

Casper looked at the mountains for a long time. Wind came down off the snowfields and moved his hair, which was going grey at the temples in a way that seemed less like age than like weather damage.

"You're sure of your figures."

"I'm sure of my instruments."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," she said. "It's better. Instruments don't hope."

Something moved across his face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live in a man who had dismantled most of his expressions for parts. "Marisol will want to feed everyone before we talk routes. Bad news travels worse on an empty stomach. Her rule, not mine."

"Is any of it yours?"

He considered that with what looked like genuine curiosity. "The river's mine," he said finally. "Everything else is borrowed."

* * *

They gathered around Marisol's fire at dusk, when the cold came down off the ice like something poured. Sefa and Lio sat shoulder to shoulder as always, the twins' shared blanket pulled around them both, passing a tin cup of tea back and forth without ever quite seeming to hand it over. Bao sat slightly apart, as he had since the col — no, Ada corrected herself, since before the col; since the gorge, maybe, or earlier, some private frost she'd mistaken for his usual reserve. His hands were steady on the teacup. Too steady. She filed that away with the other things she was filing away, in the drawer she kept for observations she wasn't ready to open.

Marisol served stew thick enough to stand a spoon in, and flatbread, and dried fruit she'd been hoarding against exactly this kind of evening, and only when everyone was chewing did Casper speak.

"Ada has numbers," he said. "I'll give them to you straight, because you'd get them straight from her anyway, and she tells them better."

So Ada told them. Eleven days. Nine days. Twenty-six days of mapped route. She drew it in the dirt with a stick because she was a cartographer and dirt was her native language: the range, the pass, the valley beyond where the vanished survey team had found — and hidden — the grant papers that could give the river villages back their land. She watched their faces as she talked. Lio went very still, the way young men go still when they realize a story has stopped being a story. Sefa put a protective hand on the compass at her neck, the cracked one Casper had repaired instead of replacing, and Ada thought: *she holds that thing like a promise.*

"So we're short seventeen days," Lio said. "On a mountain. In autumn."

"We're short seventeen days on the *mapped* route," Ada said.

"There's another route?" Sefa asked.

"There's always another route," Casper said quietly. "There's just usually a reason nobody uses it."

And then he did something Ada had never seen him do in seven weeks aboard the Kestrel, something so small that anyone who hadn't been watching him the way she'd been watching him would have missed it entirely.

He hesitated.

Casper Nakamura, who read water like scripture, who had threaded the Kestrel through sandbars no chart acknowledged, who navigated by a private atlas in his head compiled over twenty years of moving — that man looked at the mountain range glowing faintly blue in the last light, and said:

"I don't know this country."

The words fell into the fire-circle like a stone into still water. The twins exchanged a glance. Bao's cup stopped halfway to his mouth.

"I know rivers," Casper went on, and his voice was level, but Ada could hear the effort of the leveling, the cost of it, like a man carrying weight on a bad shoulder. "Every kind. I can look at moving water and tell you what it's doing underneath. Give me a river and a day and I'll find you a channel. But this—" he nodded at the peaks, at the snowfields already going violet with shadow, "—this is rock and ice and weather, and I have never once crossed a range like this. I've gone around them. Around is what I do." He paused. "If I plan this route alone, I'll plan it wrong, and wrong up here doesn't mean aground. Wrong up here means we don't come home."

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved through a notch in the ridge with a sound like a long exhalation.

"So," Casper said, and here his eyes found Ada's, and held them, and what she saw in them was not weakness and not surrender but something rarer and more expensive: a man paying out rope he could not take back. "I need a surveyor. I need someone who reads terrain the way I read water. And I need the twins, because they see things in pairs that I miss alone. And Bao, because anything mechanical between here and the valley will try to kill us and he speaks its language. And Marisol, because a crew that isn't fed is already defeated." He looked around the circle. "For the first time in twenty years, I'm asking instead of deciding. Somebody tell me how we get over that mountain in nine days."

Ada felt the question land in her chest and stay there, warm and heavy, like a coal passed hand to hand.

It was Sefa who answered first. She reached up and touched the compass at her throat — the broken-then-mended compass, the one whose needle wobbled half a degree left of true no matter what, the flaw Casper had chosen to repair rather than erase.

"My grandmother herded goats above the tree line when she was young," Sefa said slowly. "Before the syndicate took the low pastures. She used to talk about tracks the maps never had. Goat tracks. Ways over the top that the herders kept alive by walking them, generation after generation." She looked at Casper. "You're right that you don't know this country. But somebody's grandmother knew it."

Lio snorted. "We're going to navigate a mountain range by grandmother's stories?"

"By grandmother's stories and a surveyor's instruments and a captain who admits what he doesn't know," Sefa said. "That's three more things than most expeditions have."

Bao laughed — a short, surprised sound, quickly smothered, but a laugh, and Ada noted it the way she noted everything now, the way you note the behavior of a river before you trust it. "She's not wrong," he said. "Half the passes on the government charts follow old herd trails anyway. The empire just drew lines around them and claimed the credit."

"Then we find a herder," Casper said. He was already different, Ada thought — not softer, nothing about Casper got softer, but *open*, the way a lock is open, which is to say ready. "Tomorrow we climb to the high pastures and we find whoever still walks these slopes, and we ask. Politely. And we pay well."

"With what?" Marisol asked practically. "We spent the last of the trade goods at the village."

Casper was quiet for a moment. Then: "With salt. We still have the salt."

The whole circle went still. Ada didn't understand at first — and then she did, watching their faces. Salt was money in the high country, yes, but the Kestrel's salt was more than cargo. It was ballast. It was the boat's trim, her balance, part of how she sat in the water. Selling the salt wasn't spending coin. It was giving up a piece of the ship herself.

"You said everything except the river is borrowed," Ada said quietly.

Casper looked at her across the fire. "Salt's just mineral," he said. "People aren't." And then, as if embarrassed by his own plainness, he stood and announced that he'd take first watch, and the meeting was over, the way his meetings always ended — abruptly, decisively, leaving no room for the feelings they'd generated.

* * *

Ada stayed up with her instruments long after the others slept.

She worked by lamplight inside the canvas shelter she shared with Marisol, checking her figures a third time because the first two times hadn't made them kinder. Outside, Casper's silhouette moved along the rim of the camp, patient as a lighthouse. The stars here were ferocious, unwinking, close enough to insult you, and between them and the firelight the cold pressed down like a thumb.

At some point Marisol stirred in her bedroll and said, without opening her eyes, "You're going to ruin your sight working like that."

"I'm going to ruin my sight regardless. It's the latitude."

Marisol laughed softly and rolled over, and then, into the dark: "He meant it, you know. What he said at the fire. About not knowing."

Ada's pen stopped.

"Twenty years I've cooked for that man," Marisol went on, drowsy and unhurried, "and tonight is the first time I've heard him say *I need* out loud. You could have knocked me over with the ladle." A pause. "Whatever you're plotting with those numbers — plot gently. He's further from shore than he looks."

After she slept, Ada sat for a while with the lamp turned low, holding Okafor's logbook, thinking about the last entry in it — the entry where her missing colleague had written, in his cramped field hand, *found what they sent us to bury; hiding it where only the honest will dig* — and thinking about a man on watch outside who had just handed her the one thing he'd spent twenty years making sure no one could take from him: his certainty.

Trust, she wrote in the margin of her notes, in handwriting smaller than her usual, *is just humility with witnesses.*

Then she blew out the lamp, and in the dark she heard, faintly from the ridge above the camp, a sound she would remember for the rest of her life: the thin, ringing clang of a goat bell, far up in the blackness, moving.

Someone was up there. Someone was already walking.
