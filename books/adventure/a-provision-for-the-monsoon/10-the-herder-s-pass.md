## 10. The Herder's Pass

The snow line did not arrive all at once. It came the way debt comes — a little higher each morning, patient, certain of itself. On the first day above the gorge it was a smudge of white on the far peaks, something you could pretend was cloud. By the third morning it had crept down to the scree fields and begun to eat the green, and by the fourth it was close enough that when the wind turned from the north it carried a smell like iron and clean stone, the smell of a door closing.

Casper stood at the Kestrel's rail with his hands in his pockets and watched it come.

They were past the gorge now, past the portage that had nearly taken Lio and very nearly taken the boat herself, and the river here was a different animal entirely — narrow, quick, braided with meltwater, running cold enough to kill a swimming man in ten minutes. The banks had steepened into shoulders of grey rock. There were no more villages. No more smoke rising from cooking fires, no more children running the shoreline to race them through the shallows. Just the water, the stone, and the white creeping down like a tide with all the time in the world.

Behind him he heard Marisol's galley hatch bang open and shut twice, which meant she was making bread, which meant she was worried. Bao's engine note had changed overnight too — a half-tone lower, laboring — because the air up here was thin and the old diesel drank it grudgingly. Even Sefa and Lio were quiet on deck, coiling lines without their usual argument about who coiled better. The mountain had put its hand over the whole crew's mouth.

"Captain." Ada came up beside him, her instrument case slung across her back like a rifle. She'd taken to carrying it everywhere since the gorge, even to sleep. "Barometer's falling faster than I'd like. And my readings from last night—" She stopped herself, recalibrated her voice into something less alarming. "We have nine days before the pass closes. Maybe eight."

"I know."

"You know, but do the others know? Do they know what eight days means at this altitude? That if we're caught above the tree line in snow—"

"They know." He nodded toward the twins, who were pretending very hard not to listen. "Sefa hasn't argued with Lio since yesterday morning. That's how you can tell."

Ada didn't smile. She was learning his humor slowly, the way you learn a river — one sandbar at a time, with occasional groundings. "There's a track," she said instead. "On my aerials. A thread of a path cutting west off the valley, up through that saddle." She pointed to a notch in the ridge ahead, high and pale, where the rock showed through the snow like bone through skin. "Too small for a road. Goat traffic, maybe. If it goes through, it saves us two days of following the river around the shoulder of the range."

"If it goes through."

"If it goes through," she agreed. "And if someone lives up there who knows it."

* * *

Someone lived up there.

They found the first sign an hour later — a cairn, then another, marking a trail that left the riverbank and switchbacked up through dwarf pine so stunted and wind-bent they grew sideways, like men leaning into a gale for their whole lives. Then a bell. A single goat-bell, tinny and thin in the thin air, ringing somewhere above them in the fog.

"You're not thinking of taking the boat off the water," Bao said, appearing at Casper's elbow with grease to his elbows and disbelief all over his face. "Tell me you're not thinking it."

"The river ends ahead." Casper nodded upstream, where the channel broke apart into a fan of gravel shoals too shallow for anything with a keel. "Past that bend it's a creek. We walk from here regardless. The question is whether we walk two days or four."

"So we take the cargo off, haul it up a goat track—"

"We take the salt off," Casper said. "Just the salt."

Bao stared at him. Behind him, Sefa and Lio had gone still, the way crew go still when they sense the captain is about to spend something that isn't money.

"The whole cargo?" Lio said. "That's forty sacks. That's the run. That's what pays for the hull patches and the diesel and Marisol's flour—"

"I know what it is."

"Then—" Sefa started, and stopped, because Casper had already turned away and was shouting for the mooring lines.

* * *

They beached the Kestrel on a shelf of gravel below the trailhead, warped her bow up onto the stones with the winch until she sat as snug as a hen in a nest, and began the climb in the early afternoon, forty sacks of coarse grey salt distributed among seven backs. It was brutal work. The trail was barely a trail — a goat's opinion of a path, worn into the hillside, climbing through fog that thickened as they rose until the world shrank to twenty feet of wet rock in any direction. The salt bit into their shoulders through the sacking. Twice Lio slipped and was caught by Sefa's hand without either twin saying a word about it, which was its own kind of language.

Casper carried his sack at the front and watched the fog and thought about weight.

Salt was the oldest cargo on the river. Older than the colonial administration, older than the syndicate, older than any map Ada would ever draw. Salt for the high villages, who had no sea and needed it for their animals, their cheese, their blood. Every trader he'd ever known haggled over salt like it was scripture — ounce against ounce, day against day, because salt was the one thing every household above the snow line would beg for by winter's end, and a man holding salt in September held a knife at every throat in the range.

He had spent twenty years being that man. Twenty years of knowing exactly what things cost and never paying a copper more than he must, because the arithmetic was the armor. You could survive anything — grief, guilt, the sight of a drowned valley — if you kept the ledger clean. Debts paid, favors owed to no one, roots nowhere. The ledger was how you stayed free.

And yet here he was, hauling forty sacks of it uphill to give away.

He heard Yeva before he saw her: the click of a wooden staff on stone, and a voice like gravel poured slow, calling down through the fog in the mountain dialect. Then an answering call from Sefa, who spoke three words of it, and then laughter — actual laughter, cracked and dry as old rope — and out of the white came a woman leading six goats on a rope woven from hair.

Old Yeva was small and wide and brown as a chestnut, wrapped in layers of felt that made her shapeless as a boulder, her face a network of creases so deep they cast shadows. Her eyes went over the crew one at a time — the twins, Bao, Marisol, Ada with her case — and settled on Casper with the unhurried assessment of someone who had watched weather her whole life and knew how to read what was coming.

"You are going over," she said in trade-tongue, accented but sure. "In eight days, maybe, the pass closes. Nine if the mountain is kind. The mountain is not kind."

"That's why we've come," Casper said. "We need your track. And we'll pay for it."

"Everyone needs my track." She spat, expertly, downwind. "The government men needed my track, two years ago. They gave me paper. Paper burns." One of the goats butted her hip and she shoved it away without looking. "What do you have?"

"Salt."

Her eyes changed. Not greed — he knew greed after twenty years of reading faces, and this wasn't it. This was relief, quickly buried. "How much?"

"All of it."

The word came out of him before the arithmetic could catch it, and he felt the whole crew behind him go rigid. Forty sacks. The run. The winter. He said it again anyway, louder, so there could be no taking it back. "All of it. Forty sacks, delivered to your door, carried up by our own backs. For the track, and for whatever you know about it — where the ice lies, where the fog sits, where the goats won't go."

"Casper." Bao's voice, low and strangled. "A word."

"In a minute."

"A word *now*."

* * *

They stepped aside, ten feet up the trail, though everyone on the mountain could hear every word.

"You're giving away the cargo," Bao said. "You. Casper Nakamura. The man who once charged a widow's orphanage full freight because, and I quote, sentiment is a leak in the hull."

"It was a different time."

"It was *March*."

"Bao." Casper looked back down the trail, at the fog, at the white line creeping down the peaks, and found that the explanation was simpler than he'd expected and harder to say. "When my brother died, there were people upstream who could have warned us. Who knew the syndicate had bought the sluice gates, who knew the flood was coming. They stayed quiet because they were paid, and because staying quiet was cheaper than helping. I've spent twenty years being the man who stays quiet because it's cheaper." He turned back. "I'm tired of cheap."

Bao looked at him for a long moment, and something moved behind his face — something complicated, given everything Bao was carrying that none of them yet knew he was carrying. "The crew doesn't eat on goodwill," he said finally, but the fight had gone out of his voice.

"The crew eats on Marisol's cooking and my reputation, both of which will survive one empty hold." Casper clapped his engineer's shoulder, feeling the tension in it, the wire-tautness he'd been noticing for days and telling himself was just the altitude. "Besides. Winter's long. A herder with forty sacks of salt owes somebody forever. I'd rather be owed by her than by anyone else on this mountain."

* * *

Yeva fed them that evening in a stone hut perched on the shoulder of the world, under a roof of sod and slate, around a fire of dwarf-pine that smoked and spat and smelled resinous and holy. She killed a chicken without ceremony and boiled it with wild garlic, and set out a hard cheese that tasted of the pasture it came from, and the crew ate like people who had forgotten eating could be simple. Outside, the fog lifted briefly at sunset and showed them the pass itself — a notch of sky between two walls of black rock, already furred with white — and then closed again like a book.

It was while Marisol was washing the pot that Yeva sat down next to Casper on the bench by the fire, uninvited, the way old women sit everywhere, and spoke without looking at him.

"You did not haggle," she said.

"No."

"Twenty years I have traded with rivermen. They would sell their mothers' teeth for a discount on salt." She poked the fire. A knot exploded in sparks. "So either you are a fool, or you are buying something other than a track."

"Maybe both."

She snorted. Then she was quiet a while, and the fire talked for both of them, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped into a register meant only for him.

"Last week," she said, "a man climbed up here. From the far side. Downriver clothes, city boots, a face like a closed door. He offered me gold — real gold, not paper — to send any boat crew that asked me for the track back down the way they came. To tell them the pass was already closed. To say nothing, and let the mountain do the rest."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a goat complained at the dark.

"What did you tell him?" Casper asked.

"I told him the mountain decides who crosses, not me." She turned and looked at him then, and her eyes in the firelight were pale and sharp as a hawk's. "But hear me, riverman. He will not stop at me. A man who buys silence once buys it again, somewhere cheaper. There is a col on the far side of the pass — the high saddle, the only way down into the valley you're bound for. Narrow as a doorway. And between here and there is two days of country where a dozen men could hide behind a hundred rocks." She reached over and gripped his wrist, her hand dry and strong as a root. "You paid me honest. So I tell you honest: someone does not want you to reach that valley. Someone is willing to buy the mountain to stop you. Watch your people. Watch them all."

Casper looked across the fire. Marisol was laughing at something Lio had said, wiping her hands on her apron. Sefa was showing Ada a card trick and getting it wrong; Ada was pretending not to notice the error, which was its own kind of kindness. Bao sat slightly apart, as he had sat slightly apart for days, turning his cup in his hands and looking into it as though the answer to something were written at the bottom.

Watch them all, she had said.

"I will," Casper said, and found that the words sat strangely in his mouth — heavier than they should have been, the way a warning always weighs more after you've ignored the last one.

* * *

They slept in the hut's outbuilding among the fodder, packed together for warmth, and Casper lay awake listening to the mountain breathe. Wind in the rocks. The tick of cooling stone. Once, far off, a sound he couldn't name — not a goat, not wind. Something shifting in the dark below the trail, there and then gone, the way doubt is there and then gone, and then not gone at all.

Before dawn he was up, and he did a thing he had never done in twenty years of captaining: he woke Ada quietly and asked her to teach him the route. Not to approve it. To learn it. All of it — the col, the descents, the places Yeva had scratched into wax on a slate by firelight. Because if the mountain was bought, then the map was the only thing on it that couldn't be, and he wanted it in more than one head.

Ada studied him in the grey light, and something in her face softened by a degree — the smallest thaw, the first crack in the ice of a river in spring.

"Someone paid Yeva to turn us back," Casper said simply. "Which means someone knows exactly where we're going. Which means the hard part isn't the pass anymore." He shouldered his pack in the dark and looked up at the notch of paler sky where the track climbed into the clouds. "The hard part is whoever's waiting on the other side."
