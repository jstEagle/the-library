## 11. Voss's Offer

The ridge camp sat above the tree line like a wound that had learned to breathe — three tents guyed against a wind that came off the snowfields with the smell of iron and old ice, and below them the world fell away in folds of cloud so thick it looked solid enough to walk on. Ada had pitched her instrument shelter at the camp's edge, partly because the sightlines were better there, and partly because she had learned in twenty years of survey work that the edge of any camp was where you could hear what people said when they thought no one was listening.

She was recalibrating the barometer when the wind changed.

Not the direction — the *quality* of it. On the Kestrel she had learned to read water by feel, Casper's fingers on the wheel translating current into something almost musical; up here she was learning to read air the same way, and the air coming up the western face of the ridge carried a wrongness in it, a stillness at its center, the way a river goes quiet over a deep hole.

She did not turn around. She finished writing the reading in her log — 612 millibars and falling faster than it should have been for this hour, the monsoon's breath already reaching up to touch the high country eleven days early, or nine days, if you believed the pass would close first — and then she closed the log, squared it under the instrument case's strap, and turned.

Captain Voss stood six paces away, between two boulders, dressed for altitude in a grey coat that made her nearly invisible against the scree. She had come alone. That was either courtesy or calculation, and Ada had met enough people in the empire's service to know it was never courtesy.

"You climb well," Voss said. "For a cartographer."

"For anyone," Ada said. "I've been walking surveys since I was nineteen. You don't get maps by waiting for them to be brought to you."

"No," Voss agreed. "You get them by taking them." She moved closer, boots finding purchase on stone with the economy of someone who had spent her life on unstable ground. "That's what I wanted to talk to you about. Taking."

Ada kept her hands where they were — visible, empty, resting on the case's strap. The case itself sat inside the shelter behind her, locked, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from the little spirit stove she kept running because cold made the instruments lie. Everything about this conversation was going to be about that case, and they both knew it, and so neither of them mentioned it yet. It was like watching two currents meet: the violence happened underneath, where you couldn't see it.

"Your men stopped following us two days ago," Ada said. "Above the gorge portage. I assumed you'd given up."

"I assumed you'd assume that." Voss smiled. She was perhaps fifty, weathered to the color of driftwood, with eyes the pale grey of a river before flood. "I don't give up, Miss Okonkwo. I reposition. There's a difference. Giving up is for people who believe the outcome is out of their hands."

"And yours isn't?"

"Mine is exactly where I put it." Voss nodded toward the shelter. "As is yours. Sealed inheritance. Land grants. The legal future of every village on the lower Kestrel, sitting in a box behind a woman who has no more claim to it than I do."

"I have orders."

"You have paper." Voss said it gently, almost kindly, and that gentleness was the most frightening thing about her. "We all have paper. I have a commission signed by the Colonial Survey Office. You have instructions from an administrator who conscripted your passage because he couldn't find another captain foolish enough to make this run. Neither of those papers means anything up here. What means something is who holds the instruments when the snow comes down."

The wind gusted, flattening the tent canvas, carrying with it a thin hiss of spindrift off the cornice above the col. Somewhere down-slope, faintly, Ada heard the crew — Marisol's laugh, the twins arguing about knots, the small domestic noise of people making a home out of nowhere. She had spent nine days listening to that sound. Nine days of learning how a boat full of strangers had become, without anyone saying the word, a family.

She thought: *he will not let me keep it.* She thought: *that is why he is talking to me and not to Casper.*

"What do you want, Captain?"

"To make you an offer," Voss said, "that I am instructed to make. And then, because I am not the woman my employers think I am, to tell you what I actually think of it."

* * *

They walked as they talked, because Voss clearly didn't want to be seen from the camp, and because walking gave the conversation somewhere to go. They followed the ridge east, along a shelf of broken granite where the wind came in long pulses and the cloud below opened and closed like a slow eye. Once, through a gap, Ada saw the valley they were climbing toward: a green thread of river, terraced fields stitched tiny as embroidery, smoke rising from villages that had no idea their future was being carried up a mountain in a locked box.

"My employers," Voss began, "are not bandits. I want that understood. Bandits want gold. These people want *order*. They want the river mapped, titled, parcelled, and sold, because unmapped land frightens them. A valley that belongs to itself is a valley that can't be borrowed against. Do you understand what I'm telling you? The syndicate doesn't hate these villages. It simply cannot tolerate their existence. Existence without title is, to them, a kind of chaos."

"And the survey team that found the grants?"

"Went into the ground," Voss said, without inflection. "Or out of it. I wasn't consulted on method. I'm a salvage captain, Miss Okonkwo, not a murderer. Though I notice the distinction matters less every year."

Ada said nothing. Below them the cloud closed again, and the world contracted to wind and stone and the pale shape of the woman beside her.

"The offer is this." Voss stopped at a cairn, one of the old herder's markers, its stones furred with lichen the color of rust. "Deliver the case to my people at the lower river. Not destroyed — delivered. They'll honor the contents' existence; they only need them not to arrive. In exchange: the Chief Cartography post. Imperial Survey. Every expedition, every chart, every name that gets written onto the map of this territory for the next thirty years, written in your hand."

The words went into Ada like cold water into a boot — not painful at first, just present, seeping, impossible to ignore.

Every map. Every name.

She had wanted that once. She could admit it now, here, at five thousand meters with her lungs burning, in a way she could never have admitted it in a room. At nineteen, apprenticed to a coastal survey, copying soundings by lamplight, she had understood with total clarity that maps were not records of the world but arguments about it — that whoever drew the coastline decided where the sea ended and the owning began — and she had wanted, with a hunger she'd been taught to call ambition, to be the one holding the pen. To write names. To make a marsh into a harbor by drawing it as one. To take a place that had existed for ten thousand years without her and make it, on paper, hers.

The empire had trained that hunger carefully. It fed it just enough.

"And if I refuse?" she asked.

Voss looked out at the cloud. For a moment her face did something complicated — a flicker of the thing underneath the driftwood, some older weather.

"If you refuse, we take the case, and we take it in whatever way costs least. I'd prefer it cost nothing. My preference has historically carried less weight than my instructions." She turned back. "But here is what I actually think, since I promised. I think my employers are fools. I think they believe a map is a thing you finish, and any mapmaker worth her salt knows a map is a thing you abandon — you draw it, the river moves, and everything you drew is a beautiful lie. I think the grants are real and the villages' claim is real and thirty years from now, if those papers burn, some other surveyor will stand on this ridge and find the same truth in the ground, and the syndicate will have bought a decade and called it forever." She paused. "And I think you are not the woman they sent me to buy. But I was told to make the offer, and I have made it, and now you know exactly what you're worth to them."

"A chief cartographer," Ada said. "That's what I'm worth."

"That's what they're willing to pay. It isn't always the same number." Voss's mouth twisted. "Take a day. Tell no one, or tell everyone — it changes nothing about what happens at the lower river. But know that I asked you first, and know that I meant the part about the fools."

She turned and went down the ridge, grey into grey, and within ten paces the mountain had taken her back, and Ada stood alone at the cairn with the wind pulling at her coat and the offer sitting in her chest like swallowed ice.

* * *

She did not tell anyone. That was the decision she made in the first minute, and it was the wrong one, and she would understand later exactly how wrong — but in the moment it felt like prudence, like containment, like the professional instinct that had kept her alive through nine years of surveys in country that did not want her. An offer made in private should be answered in private. Bringing it to the crew would only light a fire under people who needed sleep; bringing it to Casper —

Bringing it to Casper.

She found him at the cook fire an hour later, mending a winch shackle with a file, working the metal in slow strokes while the others slept. He didn't look up when she sat down across from him. He never did, at first; he let you arrive at your own speed, which she had decided was kindness and now wondered might be surveillance — a man who watched everything by pretending to watch nothing.

"Barometer's falling fast," he said. "Faster than your instruments predicted?"

"Faster than anything predicted." She held her hands to the fire. The heat felt thin at this altitude, warmth as a rumor. "Casper. If someone offered you something you'd wanted all your life — offered it honestly, from someone who had the power to deliver — and taking it meant giving up something you'd only recently realized you had. Would you even be able to hear the offer? Or would you already be gone?"

The filing stopped.

He looked at her then, fully, and she saw the thing she had seen once before, crossing the sandbar shallows at dawn in chapter one of their acquaintance: a man doing arithmetic he hated. Not suspicion — not yet — but the raw material of it, the ore from which suspicion is smelted. Twenty years of moving, Marisol had told her, of leaving before the debts could find him. A man like that does not hear a hypothetical question. A man like that hears a confession wearing a hypothetical's clothes.

"Who made you an offer?" he asked quietly.

And here was the moment. Here was the fork in the trail, invisible, unmarked, the kind of feature she would have noted on any map she drew because unmarked forks are where travelers die. She could tell him. All of it — Voss, the cairn, the chief cartography post, the strange half-honesty of a woman who called her employers fools while carrying out their errands. He would have believed her. She knew that with the certainty of someone who had spent her life reading terrain: he would have believed her, because nine days on a river builds a trust deeper than reason, and because he was, beneath the armor, a man who wanted to believe people.

But the offer was still open. Voss had said *take a day*, and Ada's mind — the surveyor's mind, the mind that kept every option logged, every route drawn in pencil until the last possible moment — had already begun, treacherously, to draft the pencil lines. *If the pass closes early. If the monsoon breaks and the papers can't reach the villages anyway. If delivering them to Voss intact saved more lives than delivering them wet and dead—*

Pencil lines. Just pencil lines. But Casper Nakamura had spent twenty years learning to read the difference between a line drawn in ink and a line drawn in hope, and he read hers now, in the half-second silence, the way he read sandbars.

"You're considering it," he said.

"No." Too fast. "No. I refused. I'm refusing. I will refuse."

"But you waited to tell me." He set down the file. The fire cracked and spat. Above them the stars burned with the hard clarity of altitude, indifferent as ledger entries. "You sat down at my fire and asked me a question about yourself instead of telling me what happened on that ridge, and I had to ask. Ada — I've been bought before. I know what the first hour feels like. The hour where the thing they're offering is still real to you. I'm not angry. I'm asking you to say it plainly, so I know which side of it you're standing on."

"I'm standing on yours." And she meant it — she meant it with her whole chest, with the part of her that had watched him repair Sefa's compass by lamplight and understood finally what tenderness looked like when it had been starved for twenty years. "On the crew's. On the villages'. I refused her, Casper. I'm telling you now."

"Now," he repeated. Not cruelly. Worse than cruelly — accurately. "After the hour was over."

The wind came down off the col and bent the flames sideways, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the distance between the two sides of the fire was maybe four feet and might as well have been the width of the gorge.

"You want to know the truth?" Ada said. "The truth is she offered me every name on every map. Do you understand what that is to someone like me? People like us don't get offered pens, Casper. We get offered handles. And for one hour — one hour — I held the pen in my head and wrote nothing with it, and I came down the ridge and sat at your fire, and I am telling you that nothing was written. That has to be enough. It has to be, because if it isn't, then no one who has ever been tempted can ever be trusted again, and you of all people—"

She stopped. Because his face had closed, not all the way, but the way a door closes when someone decides to stop arguing and start enduring, and she recognized it because she had seen it on survey teams, on ships, in every company of humans under pressure: the arithmetic resuming behind the eyes.

"It's enough," he said, in the voice of a man for whom it was not. "Get some sleep. We move at first light — Yeva's track, before the snow takes it."

He banked the fire and went to check the mooring lines they'd rigged between boulders, though nothing was moored, though there was no water for a hundred miles, because checking lines was what his hands did when his judgment had gone somewhere he didn't want to follow.

Ada lay in her shelter with the case against her ribs and did not sleep. Through the canvas she could hear the camp breathing — Sefa's snore, Lio talking softly in a dream, Marisol turning over — the sound of the family that wouldn't say its name. And she lay there cataloguing, the way she catalogued everything, the exact topography of what had just broken: not trust itself, not entirely, but the seam where trust had been welded to doubt, the seam that would hold, probably, would bear weight, probably — but that she would feel flexing, from now on, every time he looked at her and did the arithmetic.

Below the ridge, far down in the dark, a light moved. Small, steady, descending the western face on a line no goat track followed. Voss, going back to her employers. Or going somewhere else. With Voss, Ada was beginning to understand, those had never been different destinations.

And the light paused — once, briefly — at the base of the ridge, directly below their camp, and held there for a long minute, like a man taking a bearing.

Like a man confirming that the case was sleeping exactly where he thought it was.

Then it went out, and the mountain was dark, and eleven days became ten.
