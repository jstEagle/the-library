## 12. The Cold Camp

The storm came down on them like a lid.

Lio felt it before he saw it — a pressure in his ears, the same way the river announced itself through the hull at night. Above the tree line there was no river to listen to, only rock and sky and the thin whistle of wind finding its voice in the passes, but his body had learned the grammar of weather on the Kestrel's deck and it spoke to him now in the old tongue: *get low, get small, get under something.*

They got under something. It was barely worth the name of cave — a hollow where the mountain had shrugged off a slab of granite centuries ago, leaving an overhang deep enough to shelter six people if six people agreed to become very good friends with cold. Sefa and Lio dragged the tarp from the pack frame while Ada staked it with pitons she'd carried all the way from the gorge, hammering with a rock because they'd left the mallet with the winch gear two valleys back. Marisol got the stove going in the lee of the entrance, shielding the flame with her body the way she shielded everything else, and Old Yeva's goat track fell away below them into a whiteness so total it looked like the world had been erased rather than covered.

"Nobody moves," Casper said. He said it quietly, which was how you knew it wasn't a suggestion. "Storm like this can last a day. Can last three."

Lio pressed his shoulder against Sefa's, the way they'd done since they were small enough that the gesture meant *I'm here* instead of *move over.* His twin was shivering already, jaw tight, refusing to admit it. Lio pulled the spare blanket from his own pack and dropped it over her without looking at her, because looking would have made it a thing, and things between the twins were only ever safe when they went unremarked.

Above the tree line. Lio kept turning the phrase over. All his life the world had ended at the water's edge — rivers, then the sea, then rivers again. He knew the smell of mud in nine dialects and the sound a rope makes ten minutes before it parts. But this — this white silence, this air like broken glass in the lungs, this landscape with no current to read — this was country without a language he spoke. He felt illiterate. He felt, strangely, free.

* * *

By the second hour the stove had warmed the cave's throat enough that breath no longer froze in the air, and by the third hour Marisol had produced food that should not have been possible at four thousand meters in a whiteout — flatbread crisped on the stove top, lentils with the last of the dried chilies, tea strong enough to stand a spoon in. She cooked the way she did everything: as an argument against despair, delivered without raising her voice.

It was Ada who broke the silence first, and Lio watched her do it with the particular care of someone who had studied the crew for weeks and was only now willing to spend what she'd learned.

"When I was eight," she said, to the fire more than to anyone, "my mother sold our house to pay for my father's funeral, and then the buyer's lawyers came back and said the sale hadn't been witnessed properly, and we lost the house anyway and owed the difference." She turned her tin cup slowly. "I became a surveyor because maps are the only documents I've ever seen that couldn't be argued with. Ink fades. Boundaries hold." A pause. "That was a lie I needed for about fifteen years. The syndicate's maps argue just fine."

Nobody laughed. Nobody was supposed to. In the Kestrel's economy, a story like that was payment — tender offered against debts nobody had named aloud, and Lio understood that Ada had just put her coin on the table and was waiting to see if anyone would match it.

Sefa did. Of course Sefa did; his twin had never once let him be the braver one.

"We came aboard at Tarnmouth," she said, nodding at Lio to include him, because even now, even after everything, she did that — folded him into her sentences like he was half of every word. "Harbor town, south coast. Our mother worked the fish markets and our father worked the syndicate's drying sheds, until the sheds burned and the syndicate decided the fire was his fault. They took the boat. Not a fishing boat — the *house*. It floated. Half the families in Tarnmouth lived on floats back then, moored behind the breakwater. Cheaper than land. Land was for people whose papers were in order." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Our papers were never in order. So one morning there was a tug, and men with clipboards, and our house went upriver on a chain with us standing on the dock watching the kitchen window get smaller. I was eleven. Lio was eleven. We ran along the pier until it ended."

"And then?" Ada asked softly.

"And then we stole a dinghy," Lio said, "badly," and the cave laughed — a short, surprised sound, steam in the cold — and he let them, because the truth underneath the joke could stay underneath. *We ran along the pier until it ended.* That was the whole story, really. Everything after was just learning which piers didn't end.

Marisol fed the stove another brick of dung-and-twine fuel and told hers without being asked, the way she gave out food — as though withholding it would be the stranger act. A farm in the middle valley. Good soil, her grandfather's, then her father's, then — on paper, after a loan written in figures no farmer could check — the syndicate's. "They didn't even want the land at first," she said. "They wanted it near other land. Water runs downhill and so does ownership. My mother stayed to fight it in the courts and I left to stop hating her for losing." She shrugged, a small motion inside her many layers. "I cook because it's the one thing I do that can't be taken away once it's given. You can repossess a field. You can't repossess a meal somebody's already eaten."

Bao said nothing. Bao had said nothing for days, and Lio had noticed — noticed the engineer checking the ridgeline too often, the way his hands stayed busy past the point of usefulness, coiling rope that was already coiled. But the storm made a wall around them, and walls make confessions feel safe, and Bao stared into the stove's blue flame and said, "My family is in the eastern provinces," and then stopped, as if the sentence had cost more than he'd budgeted, and Sefa caught Lio's eye across the fire and neither of them asked. Not yet. The mountain would keep. Some debts keep better cold.

* * *

It was Yeva, wrapped in goat wool older than any of them, who aimed the evening at Casper. She did it the way she seemed to do everything — sideways, without appearing to aim at all.

"You," she said. "Captain. Twenty years on this river and no village claims you. In my country we'd say you are running from a debt." She sipped her tea. "Or paying one."

The wind screamed across the cave mouth and the tarp cracked like a sail. Lio held his breath. Everyone held their breath. There were questions a crew does not ask its captain, and everyone had spent years not asking this one, and now a woman they'd known for three days had asked it over lentils.

Casper looked into the fire for a long time. Long enough that Lio thought the silence would simply close over the question like water over a stone, the way it always had.

"My brother's name was Tomas," Casper said.

The name landed in the cave like a dropped tool. Lio had never heard the captain say anyone's name with anything but command in it — *Bao, take the wheel; Sefa, ease the line* — and this was different. This was a name said the way you'd set flowers on water.

"He was older. Four years. When our parents died — fever year, I was fourteen — he took over the boat, our father's boat, a little grain barge called the *Paloma*, and he was good at it. Better than me. I was the one who read charts for pleasure; he was the one who could feel a sandbar move in his sleep." Casper's hands were still around his cup, but Lio saw the knuckles. "This was before your time on the river, Marisol. Before the syndicate bought the upper valley. Back then they wanted the flood. You understand? Everyone thinks the floods are weather. The early ones weren't. They opened the upper sluices in a dry season to drown out the villages that wouldn't sell — drowned the fields so the deeds washed away, because a deed means nothing if the land it describes is underwater, and underwater land gets resurveyed, and resurveyed land gets new owners." He said it evenly, like a man reading cargo manifests. Lio realized his hands were shaking and pressed them flat against his knees.

"Tomas ran relief barges. Grain, seed stock, roofing tin, upriver ahead of the water, warning the villages. The syndicate told him twice to stop. The third time they didn't tell him. They opened the sluices at night with no warning downstream, and he was moored at Ferro Bend with a full hold and a sleeping crew, and the wave came down the narrows at—" Casper stopped. Started again. "The river rose nine feet in twenty minutes. Nine feet. There's no mooring in the world built for that."

The stove ticked. Somewhere above them, snow slid off the overhang and hit the tarp with a soft, enormous weight.

"I was nineteen," Casper said. "I was on the far bank. I watched the *Paloma* come apart in the torchlight and I watched the water take the pieces, and I never found—" His jaw worked. "I never found him. And I understood, standing on that bank, that staying is a fiction. That roots are just the part of you the flood reaches first. So I bought a patched hull and I hired a cook who was angrier than me, and I made a rule: never stay longer than the water level. Because if you don't stay anywhere, nowhere can be taken from you. If you love nothing that holds still—" He shook his head. "Twenty years. It works. I want you all to know it works. Nothing has ever been taken from me again."

He looked up, and his eyes went around the cave — Sefa, Lio, Marisol, Ada, Bao, Yeva — and Lio watched him see them, really see them, maybe for the first time since Tarnmouth, since the gorge, since the beginning.

"Nothing," he said again, quieter, "except the twenty years."

No one moved. The fire popped. And then Marisol did the thing that Lio would remember for the rest of his life, the thing he would tell his own crew someday if he ever had one: she reached over and refilled his cup without a word, the way you feed a fire, the way you provision a boat, and Casper looked at the steaming cup as if it were a document he'd been served, and drank.

* * *

Later, when the others slept in their heap of blankets and wool, Lio lay awake between Sefa's steady breathing and the wind's unsteady one, and thought about piers that end and rivers that don't.

He thought about how the captain had said *nothing has ever been taken from me again* while sitting in a circle with five people who would, any of them, die before letting something be taken from him. How the rule had stopped working somewhere back at the gorge, or maybe at the sandbar standoff, or maybe the first morning Marisol cooked breakfast and Casper ate it sitting down instead of standing at the wheel. Rules don't break all at once. They erode, like banks. Like sandbars shifting their moods.

He thought about Bao, silent in the dark on the far side of the fire, lying with his back to all of them, awake — Lio could tell by the breathing, shallow and deliberate, the breathing of a man rehearsing something.

And he thought about tomorrow. The col was a day's climb, and beyond it the long descent to the valley where the survey team waited, where the case in Ada's keeping would finally mean whatever it was going to mean. The storm would clear by dawn; Yeva had read the sky and pronounced it so, and her forecasts had been right about everything so far except the price of salt.

Sleep took him at last with the image of the *Paloma* in his head — not breaking, the way Casper had to carry it, but whole: a grain barge moored at Ferro Bend, lantern lit, kitchen window glowing, waiting for a brother who had spent twenty years sailing away from every harbor and was finally, without admitting it, sailing home.

At first light the wind had died and the sky was the hard clean blue of a blade, and they broke camp and climbed toward the col in silence, six small figures on a white mountainside —

— and Lio, glancing back once at the cave, saw Bao pause at its mouth and look down the southern slope, the wrong direction, the direction of Voss and the low country, for exactly three seconds too long.
