## 13. Betrayal at the Col

The col announced itself the way grief does — not all at once, but in increments you only recognize afterward. First the wind changed its grain, coming off the snowfields instead of up from the valleys, carrying a smell like iron and old ice. Then the trees gave out entirely, surrendering to scrub, then to lichen, then to nothing but stone and the blue-white glare of altitude. By midmorning they were walking through a country that had never heard of rivers, and Casper felt it like an amputation.

He had lived his whole life within earshot of moving water. Even on the Kestrel's worst nights, even hull-down in a storm with the bilge pump screaming, there had always been the river's voice beneath everything, patient and enormous, telling him where he was. Up here there was only wind, and the crunch of boots on scree, and the small bell of the lead goat Yeva had lent them tinkling ahead like a conscience.

"Two hours to the top," Old Yeva had said at dawn, spitting into the wind with the precision of a woman who had done it ten thousand times. "Then down the far side before the light goes. The pass doesn't forgive campers."

Casper checked the sky anyway. It was the wrong color — too clean, too hard, a blue like fired porcelain. Storm weather, or the memory of storm weather, gathering somewhere behind the peaks. He said nothing. The crew was tired enough without borrowing trouble.

Marisol walked in the middle of the line with the flour barrel slung across her back, refusing help, as she refused everything that looked like pity. Sefa and Lio flanked her, arguing in their twin shorthand about whether a marmot they'd seen yesterday had been fat or merely wet. Ada ranged ahead with her instruments, reading the thin air the way Casper read water. And Bao —

Bao walked last. Bao had been walking last for two days.

Casper noticed things. It was his trade, more than cargo ever was: the mood of a sandbar, the lie in a current's surface, the way a boat sat wrong in the water by half a finger's width. And he had noticed that Bao no longer slept facing the fire. That he'd stopped humming the Cantonese opera fragments he hummed when he worked the engine, the ones Marisol pretended not to love. That he'd asked, casually, three days ago, how many hours' walk it was from the col down to the tree line on the southern side — and then asked again this morning, differently, as if checking an answer against a map.

Casper had told himself it was the altitude. Everyone went quiet up here. The air took something out of you.

He told himself that right up until the col itself, when the wind dropped all at once, the way it does in high places, and in the sudden silence he heard the click of a case latch behind him.

* * *

The col was a saddle of gray stone between two shoulders of black rock, wide enough for twenty men, littered with the cairns of every traveler who had ever crossed and wanted the mountain to know it. Prayer flags Yeva's people had hung years ago hung now in ribbons, bleached colorless, snapping in the thin wind. Below them, the southern side fell away in a long white ramp toward the cloud forest, invisible under its own weather.

They stopped to breathe, because you had to. Casper set down his pack and turned to count heads, the old habit, the captain's rosary: Marisol, Sefa, Lio, Ada, the goats, and —

Bao stood apart, near the largest cairn, with the case in his hands.

Not Ada's instrument case. The other one. The real one, the one that had ridden inside the flour barrel since the ambush at the ridge camp, wrapped in oilcloth, heavier than paper had any right to be. The grants. The inheritance of six villages. The thing Voss had hunted them across a mountain range for, the thing they had defended with rope and river-craft and Lio's nearly drowned lungs.

Bao held it the way a man holds something hot — competently, at arm's length, already apologizing with his body.

"Bao." Casper kept his voice level. In thirty seconds it wouldn't matter what tone he used, but those thirty seconds were his, and he would spend them carefully. "Put it down."

"I'm sorry, Captain." Bao's voice was steady, which was worse than shaking. Bao's hands shook when he was afraid for himself; they were always steady when he was afraid for someone else. "I need you not to follow me. Not yet. I'm going down to the tree line, to the camp Voss's men keep at the bridge. I give them the case, they make a call, and by tomorrow my sister walks out of a building in the capital that I have spent four years trying not to think about."

The crew had gone still around the col's rim. Sefa's hand had found her knife; Lio's hand had found Sefa's wrist, holding it down. Marisol stood with the empty barrel strap still over her shoulder, and her face was doing something terrible and tender, the face of a woman watching a wound reopen that she'd been dressing for years.

"You could have asked," Ada said quietly. She had not moved toward him. Casper noted that — the surveyor measuring distances, always, even now. "We'd have found another way. We were going to find another way."

"There is no other way." For the first time Bao's voice cracked, just at the seam. "You don't understand what they do, Okonkwo. They don't keep her in a cell. Cells end. They keep her in a *job*. A good job, a comfortable room, a salary paid on time, and a door that opens onto a corridor that opens onto another corridor, and somewhere in that building is a piece of paper with her name on it that says she can leave whenever she wants — and everyone she works with tells her daily how lucky she is, and she has started to believe them, because four years is long enough to believe anything." He swallowed. "I get letters. Friendly ones. They let me write back. That's the cruelty of it — they're *kind*, Captain. Kindness with a lock in it."

The wind came back and took the words apart. Casper stood very still and did the arithmetic he was built for, the survival math, the cold ledger he had kept for twenty years of moving water:

One case. Five people. A pass that closed with snow in — he counted the sky again — perhaps seven days now, maybe six. A descent through cloud forest with a man who knew the ground better than any of them. Behind them, a valley full of villagers waiting on papers that were currently in Bao's hands, and ahead, Voss's men at the bridge, armed, expecting exactly this delivery.

Every number said the same thing: cut your losses. Let him go. Turn back, take the crew down the north side, winter in Yeva's valley, come back in spring when the syndicate's interest had cooled and the case could be recovered or replaced or mourned. Twenty years ago — five years ago — last week, even — Casper Nakamura would have already been turning, already calculating the new route, already folding the loss flat and putting it away the way he folded charts.

That was the whole architecture of his life. You moved because staying meant debts, and debts meant names, and names meant the past could find you and collect. You kept the crew fed and paid and unnamed so that losing any of them would hurt less than it would if you admitted what they were.

Bao met his eyes across the col. There was no defiance in it. That was the unbearable part. Bao looked at him the way a man looks at a captain he loves, asking permission to sink.

"The engine," Casper said.

Bao blinked. "What?"

"When we portaged the gorge. The winch line was cut, and Lio went into the water, and you were the one who got the spare line rigged in ninety seconds. Ninety seconds, Bao. I counted, because counting is what I do instead of praying." Casper took one step forward, gravel shifting under his boot. "You are the best engineer I have ever shipped with, and I have shipped with thieves, deserters, a pirate, and one man who claimed to be a bishop. Whatever they have on you, whatever paper they hold — it isn't worth more than that. It isn't worth more than *you*."

"Don't." Bao's jaw tightened. "Don't do the speech, Captain. I've had four years to imagine this moment and in none of them does the speech work."

"It's not a speech." Casper looked at the case, then back at Bao's face. "It's a heading."

"Casper." Marisol's voice, low. Not pleading. Marisol didn't plead. "The villages."

"I know," Casper said. "I know what's in the case, Mari."

"Then—"

"He knows too." Casper hadn't taken his eyes off Bao. "And he's carrying it down to them himself, which means some part of him is hoping we stop him. Because if he truly wanted to hand it over clean, he'd have taken it at night, three days ago, at the cold camp, when we were snowed in and blind. He waited until daylight. He waited until we could see his face." He paused. "You waited, didn't you."

Bao's composure finally broke — not dramatically, just a single fissure, the way ice gives. "My sister," he said, and couldn't finish it.

"Sefa." Casper turned. "Lio."

The twins came forward. Sefa's knife was away now; Lio had a coil of light climbing line over one shoulder, because Lio always had rope, the way Marisol always had salt and Sefa always had an opinion.

"Captain?" Sefa said.

Casper looked south, down the long white ramp, at the weather sitting on the cloud forest like a lid. Then north, at the pass, at the arithmetic. Then at his crew — his unnamed family, his floating debt he'd never once regretted.

Survival math said turn back. Survival math had kept him alive for twenty years and alone for every one of them.

"We go after him," Casper said. "All of us. Now, while his tracks are fresh and the light holds."

"Captain—" Bao started.

"You want to save your sister. Fine. But you're not trading six villages for one hostage, Bao, because that trade doesn't work — you know it doesn't, or you'd sleep at night. The syndicate takes the case and keeps her anyway, because the case is worth more than she is, and men like that don't release collateral once the debt's cleared. They *reinvest* it." He watched that land, watched it land badly, watched it land true. "So here's the new heading. We take the case back. We take *you* back. And then we take your sister — all the way, out of that building, out of their ledgers, the same way we're taking these grants to the valley. Nobody gets left as collateral. Not her. Not you. That's the provision."

"That's not how the world works," Bao whispered.

"No," Casper agreed. "It's how we work. There's a difference. It's taken me twenty years and a mountain range to learn it, and I'll be damned if I let you graduate faster than I did."

Ada had unslung her instrument case and was already doing something to the sextant's housing, her fingers quick, her eyes distant. Casper caught the shape of it and almost smiled despite everything: the surveyor was thinking about bearings, about where Voss's camp sat relative to the bridge, about angles. Good. Angles were weapons too.

"Tracks head southeast along the ramp," Lio reported from the col's edge, crouched over the scree. "Fresh. He was moving fast, Captain — but fast downhill on scree means careless. He's turned an ankle or he will."

"Then we move before he rests." Casper shouldered his pack and felt the whole weight of the decision settle into it, familiar as ballast. "Sefa, point. Lio, rope ready — the ramp will ice where the shadow crosses. Mari, stay center with the goats. Ada, give me a bearing on that bridge camp and every hour of light we have left."

"And me?" Bao stood with the case still in his hands, stranded between headings.

Casper looked at him for a long moment. The wind snapped the dead prayer flags. Somewhere below, under all that cloud, a river was running — he couldn't hear it, but he knew it was there, the way you know your own pulse.

"You walk in front of me," Casper said. "Where I can see your hands. And when we get your sister back, you're going to teach me the name of that opera you hum, because Marisol's been dying to ask you for four years and I'm tired of watching her not ask."

Something moved through Bao's face — not hope yet, maybe, but the ground hope grows in. He shifted the case to one hand and started downslope, and the crew fell in around him without being ordered, the way water finds a channel, the way a family finds its shape the moment it stops pretending it isn't one.

Behind them, the first flakes came down out of the porcelain sky, fine and dry and early, and Casper did not look back at the pass closing. Ahead, far down in the white, a thread of woodsmoke rose from the tree line where Voss's men waited for a delivery that was about to arrive with six escorts.

Casper checked the sky one last time, read its mood the way he read a sandbar's, and did not like the forecast at all.

"Move," he said. "We're racing the snow now — and I intend to win."
