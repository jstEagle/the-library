## 14. Descent into the Cloud Forest

The col was empty when we reached it, and the fog was already coming up the mountain like something with intent.

I saw the case's absence before I saw anything else — that pale rectangle of disturbed scree where Bao had set it down while he checked the rope anchors. Casper crouched over the spot for a long time without touching it. When he finally stood, his face had gone the color of old river ice, and I thought: *he's going to turn us back.* Twenty years of moving had taught him that arithmetic. One man lost, a case lost, a mountain turning hostile — cut your losses, keep the crew alive, live to run another season.

Instead he said, "Which way would you go, if you were him?"

And I understood that something in him had already broken loose from its moorings.

"Down," Lio said. "Voss will be below. He'll want to hand it off fast."

"There's no trail down from here," Marisol said. "Not on this face."

"There's a goat track," Old Yeva said. She'd come as far as the col with us, wrapped in her felt cloak like a stone that had learned to walk. "My father used it to bring sick animals down before the snows. It drops through the cloud forest. It is not a trail for people." She looked at Casper. "It is not a trail for anyone."

Casper looked at the fog eating the ridgeline, then at each of us in turn — Marisol, Lio, me, Ada with her empty hands and her jaw set like a cleat. He didn't look at me longest, but he looked at me last, and I knew why. The twins were the youngest. The twins were the ones he always counted twice.

"Sefa," he said. "You're on point. You read ground better than any of us."

It wasn't praise. It was weight, handed over deliberately, the way you hand someone a load you trust them not to drop. I took it.

* * *

The cloud forest began forty meters below the col, where the pines gave up and the moss took over everything — trunks, branches, stones, the air itself, so thick with moisture it beaded on my eyelashes. Sound went strange in there. Our footsteps vanished into the wet. Somewhere below, water ran, but it could have been ten meters away or a hundred; the fog folded distance like cloth.

We roped together at Casper's order, five of us strung out along thirty meters of line, and I went first because he told me to, feeling for the track with my boots the way Yeva had described it — a shelf of stone no wider than a coffin lid, cut into the slope, disappearing and reappearing under centuries of root growth.

Leeches found us within the hour.

They came off the wet leaves in their hundreds, thin brown threads that stretched and swayed like the forest itself reaching for warm blood. You couldn't fight them; fighting them left their jaws in the wound. You let them feed or you burned them off with salt, and we had salt, but only what Marisol carried in her pockets, and she doled it out grain by grain like a miser.

"Hold still," she murmured, working the crystals along Lio's collar line. "Hold still, little fish."

"I hate this forest," Lio whispered back. "I hate it politely, but I hate it."

"You can hate it when we're out of it."

Ada moved behind me, and every few minutes she'd murmur bearings — *north by northeast, descending, watch the gradient* — reading the fog with her instruments the way I read the ground. Once, when I slipped and dropped half a body-length before the rope caught me, her hand closed on my harness strap and held until I found my feet again. She never mentioned it after. Neither did I. That's how things are done on the Kestrel.

By late afternoon the light had gone the color of drowned things, and the track narrowed to nothing, and then the trees opened and there it was: the river gorge below us, running white and loud, and spanning it —

The bridge.

Yeva's father's bridge. Two great cables of braided hide and hemp, sagging across maybe sixty meters of roaring air, slats rotted to lacework, the whole thing furred with moss like something the forest had grown rather than built. And on the far side, small in the murk, a figure moving downhill fast.

Bao.

"He's crossing," Lio breathed.

"No," Casper said. "Look at him. He's stopped. He's looking at the anchor."

Bao stood at the near end of the bridge — our side, thank whatever gods mind rivers — and he was doing what any engineer does before trusting a structure: testing it. Even now. Even betraying us, he checked his knots. Something about that cracked me open a little, and I hated it.

"We take him on the bridge," Casper said quietly. "Not before, not after. On the bridge he can't run sideways, and if it goes, we all go together."

"And if it goes?" Marisol asked.

Casper didn't answer. He just started down toward the bridgehead, and we followed, and the fog closed behind us like a door.

* * *

I went second, behind Casper, because he put himself on point for the crossing and nobody argued. The cables were slick as eels under my gloves. Each slat gave a little underfoot, a soft wet crunch of rot, and between slats there was nothing but the gorge's white noise rising up through the gaps like breath.

Halfway across, the fog swallowed both banks entirely. We were on a thread over a void, five people and a ghost of a sixth ahead of us, and Bao had turned around.

He stood ten meters out, one hand on the cable, the case strapped across his back, and even through the fog I could see his face. I'd shared a mess table with that face for six years. It wasn't hard. It wasn't cruel. It was the face of a man standing on a bridge between two lives, and both ends were burning.

"Go back, Casper," he called. His voice came flattened by the mist, close and far at once. "Please. Just let me cross."

"You know I can't do that."

"The syndicate has my sister." It came out of him like something torn, not spoken. "Do you understand? Not threatened. *Has.* Since spring. They wrote to me in the capital — photographs, dates. Every month I didn't report, they sent another photograph. She's in a house in Corvane with bars on the windows and a woman who brings her meals, and they told me the case buys her door opening." His voice broke apart. "What was I supposed to do? What would any of you have done?"

Nobody answered, because there was no honest answer except *the same thing*, and everyone on that bridge knew it.

"Bao," Casper said, and his voice did something I'd almost never heard it do — it softened without weakening. "Come back. We'll find another way. There's always another way on a river."

"There isn't!" Bao shouted, and stamped his foot, and the bridge — the rotten, moss-furred, hundred-year-old bridge — answered him.

I felt it before I heard it: a shudder running up through the cable into my hands, a deep structural groan from somewhere below the fog. Then the sound — a crack like a mast going, and the far anchor let go.

Not all at once. That's the thing people don't understand about failures. The far cable tore loose from its stone in stages, fibers parting one by one, and the whole span swung down and sideways into the gorge like a pendulum, and we were on it.

I've been in rapids. I've been in a hull-cracking collision. Nothing prepared me for the sky turning. The world rotated — fog, rock, white water, fog — and the cable under my hands went slack and taut and slack, whipping, and somewhere Lio screamed my name, and my harness bit into my ribs as the rope between us snapped tight and held, held, *held*, Casper's knots, Casper's rigging, the only reason any of us was not already falling.

The bridge's near end held. The far end hung. We ended up stacked against the near anchor point at maybe a thirty-degree angle, the broken span dangling below us into the mist, and Bao —

Bao was gone from the deck.

For one full second I thought he'd fallen, and my stomach dropped through the bottom of the world. Then Lio, hanging lowest on the rope, shouted: "There! On the cable!"

He'd grabbed the intact near cable when the deck went, and now he hung above the abyss with the case dragging at his shoulders, arms shaking, boots finding nothing. The cable hummed with his weight. Below him, sixty meters of fog, and under the fog, the river that eats boats.

Casper didn't hesitate. I watched him make the decision the way you watch a man step off a ledge — no ceremony, just motion. He paid out rope, anchored it around the remaining stanchion, and went down the cable toward Bao hand over hand, calm as if he were walking a dock in flat water.

"Drop the case," Casper said, when he was close enough.

"No—"

"*Drop it.* It's bait anyway. Ada made sure of that much. Drop it and take my hand."

I saw Bao's face do three things at once — disbelief, calculation, and then something worse, which was hope. The case's strap slid from his shoulder. I watched it fall, turning slowly, and vanish into the white below, and then Bao's hand found Casper's, and we hauled them both up through the fog onto the ruined anchor point, five of us and a traitor, soaked, bleeding where the cable had flayed our palms, alive.

Marisol got to Bao first. Not with fists — with a blanket, pulled from her pack, wrapped around his shaking shoulders like he was one of ours, because apparently he still was, because apparently that decision had been made for all of us somewhere on that bridge and nobody had consulted me.

"Why," Bao whispered into the wool. "Why would you—"

"Shut up and drink this," Marisol said, and put the flask in his hands.

* * *

We camped that night on a shelf of rock above the gorge, too spent to descend farther, the fog pressing in around our little fire like a crowd of listeners. Bao sat at the edge of the light, apart, and nobody told him to move closer, and nobody told him to leave. The rope between those two positions was exactly as long as it needed to be.

Ada sat across from him with her knees drawn up, and for a long time she just watched him the way she watches instruments — patiently, waiting for a reading.

"The case that went into the gorge," she said finally. "Tell me what you think Voss gets for it."

"A waterlogged box," Bao said dully.

"A waterlogged box containing papers I redrew myself, three nights ago, in Marisol's galley." Ada's voice was level, almost gentle. "The grant boundaries are shifted four kilometers south. The surveyor's annotations name Voss as the syndicate's contracted agent. If their lawyers open that case believing it's real, Voss hangs for theft of government property, and the syndicate's claim collapses in court." She paused. "The real case never left the boat. It's in the flour barrel. It's been in the flour barrel since the col."

Bao stared at her. Then he laughed — a horrible, cracked sound, halfway to sobbing. "You let me steal it. You *gave* it to me."

"We let the syndicate steal it," Ada said. "There's a difference. Though I'll admit the distinction required you."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you have a sister," Ada said, "and because mercy is only mercy if it arrives before it's owed." She leaned forward, firelight moving on her face. "Here is what I propose. Voss expects delivery. So we deliver. You walk into her camp tomorrow with the case — wet, damaged, authentic-looking — and you tell her you barely escaped us, that we're a day behind you, crippled on the goat track. And while she's opening her prize and reading her own arrest warrant, we come down through the fog behind you, and we take her boat, her radios, and her contract. All of it. Everything the syndicate owns on this river."

"It's insane," Lio said.

"It's bait," Ada said. "The case has been bait since the day I sealed it. The only question was who would swallow."

Everyone looked at Casper. He'd been silent through all of it, working a knot in a scrap of line, the way he does when his hands need to outrun his thoughts. Now he looked up, and he looked at Bao — really looked, the long measuring look I'd seen him give sandbars before trusting them with the whole boat.

"Your sister's name," he said.

"Linh."

"When this is done, we get Linh back. Not the inquiry, not the lawyers — *we*. Whatever it takes, however far we have to run the river. That's the price of my yes." He tossed the knotted scrap into the fire. "That, and you cook for Marisol until she says stop. She's been wanting an assistant for six years."

Bao made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been the other thing. "She'll work me to death."

"Probably," Marisol said comfortably, and passed him the rice.

Later, when the others slept, I took the middle watch and found Casper awake beside the coals, mending the frayed section of our climbing rope with small, exact stitches. I sat down next to him. For a while neither of us said anything, because on the Kestrel silence is also a language, and some things are only sayable in it.

"He chose his sister," I said finally. "Over us."

"He chose the only way he could see," Casper said. "There's a difference. Most of the bad choices on this river are just good ones made in fog." He pulled a stitch tight. "Get some sleep, Sefa. Tomorrow we go down into their camp, and I want your eyes fresh."

Below us, far down through the dissolving fog, I could just make out the salvage boat's riding lights, small and patient on the black water — Voss's camp, waiting for a delivery that was already a trap, for a betrayal that had turned itself inside out somewhere on a rotten bridge in the cloud forest.

Tomorrow we would bring the case to her ourselves.

And the case, all along, had been carrying *us*.
