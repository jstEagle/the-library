## 15. The False Case

Ada had learned forgery the way most people learned grief: suddenly, and from someone who loved her.

Her mother, in the last year before the syndicate took the farm, had sat at the kitchen table with a lamp turned low and a stack of the landlord's own receipts, teaching her daughter how ink aged, how paper remembered water, how a signature was not a name but a set of habits — the little hesitation before the crossbar, the way a tired hand let its descenders trail. *If they take everything else*, her mother had said, pressing the pen into Ada's fingers like a key into a child's palm, *they can never take what you know about their paper.* It had not saved the farm. But it had saved Ada, in ways she was only now beginning to count.

She worked by lamplight in the Kestrel's aft cabin, with the real case open in front of her and Marisol's flour barrel standing sentry at her elbow, its lid weighted with a stone. Outside, the cloud forest dripped. Somewhere below them, down the mountain they had descended two days ago, Voss's crew was camped on the river terrace, close enough that their cookfire smoke rose through the trees at dusk like a gray flag announcing itself.

The plan had been Sefa's, which surprised everyone except Sefa.

*They want the case,* she had said, turning her repaired compass over in her hands — Casper had fixed it in the village, patiently, with tweezers and a drop of oil, and she had not let it out of her sight since. *So give them one. Voss won't open it until she's safe away, will she? She'll want to verify it with her buyer first. So make the papers inside say something she doesn't know they say.*

*Say what?* Lio had asked.

*Say she stole them,* Sefa said simply. *Make every page accuse her.*

It was elegant. It was cruel in the precise way that only kindness under pressure could be cruel. And it required someone who could forge a government surveyor's hand well enough to fool another surveyor — because Voss, Ada now knew, had been a mapmaker once too. The syndicate's mapmaker. Which meant Voss would read documents the way a musician reads a score, hearing the false notes.

Which meant Ada could not afford a single one.

She began with the paper itself. The grant documents were printed on stock from the capital's survey office, heavy and faintly ribbed, and there was no duplicating that — so she used the real thing. Three of the forty-one grants could be sacrificed; the villages' claims were recorded in triplicate logic, each parcel described twice, once in prose and once in the marginal coordinates, and Ada spent a full hour confirming that removing three pages left every remaining claim legally whole. The three she chose covered land the syndicate already held — worthless to the villages, priceless as bait.

Then she boiled tea.

Not to drink. Marisol watched, arms folded, as Ada steeped it black and brushed it across the pages in thin washes, aging them, giving them the yellow-brown patina of documents that had traveled. *My grandmother did that with coffee,* Marisol murmured. *For letters my grandfather wrote home from the war. He'd missed three years of birthdays and wanted the paper to look like it had suffered with him.* She shook her head slowly. *Everyone lies with something. Flour, ink, knots. I always thought it would be guns.*

*Guns are just faster lies,* Ada said, and Marisol laughed — a short, startled sound, as if the laugh had escaped without permission — and went back to her galley, where she was doing her own part of the work.

Because the ambush needed a reason to be believed, and the best reason was hunger. A camp that has been running hard for days eats badly; a camp that stops to bake bread is a camp that feels safe. So Marisol was making bread — real bread, the smell of it rolling downriver through the trees like an invitation — and the flour barrel stood where a flour barrel would stand, and inside it, wrapped in oilcloth beneath twenty pounds of the finest white flour Marisol owned, the true case slept among the grants that would save forty villages.

*You're putting our future in with my baking?* Marisol had asked when they proposed it.

*I trust your barrel more than any strongbox,* Ada had told her, and meant it. No thief on earth searches the food. Hunger makes men honest around provisions; even Voss's people would carry off a flour barrel rather than spill it, if they carried it at all. But the plan was that they wouldn't. The plan was that they'd find the decoy case sitting in plain view, abandoned in the panic of a staged rout, and never look further.

The decoy case itself was the survey team's spare instrument box, which they had recovered from the last camp along with everything else the vanished cartographers had left behind — kettle, logbooks, instruments still faithfully recording a world they no longer occupied. It was brass-cornered, scarred, and bore the same official stencil as the true case. Ada transferred the lock plate herself, filing the hasp so that it would look forced when the moment came. Bao helped her with that, saying almost nothing, his hands steady and his eyes anywhere but hers. She understood why. She let him understand that she understood. Some mercies are just silence, correctly aimed.

Inside the box, she built the lie in layers.

The top layer was honest: survey blanks, coordinate tables copied fair and true, the kind of padding a case accumulates. Beneath that, the three aged grant pages — and here was where her mother's lessons earned their keep. She forged the countersignature of the vanished team's lead surveyor, Aldous Wren, whose hand she knew intimately from four hundred pages of his field notes. Wren signed with a slight forward lean, impatient, the final letter of his surname hooked like a fisherman's catch. She gave him that hook. She dated the endorsements eleven days ago, above the snow line, in weather records confirmed the team had actually endured.

And then, in the margins — small, in the red pencil surveyors use for corrections, the color of authority — she added what Sefa had asked for. Not accusations. Better than accusations. *Provenance.*

*Received into custody of Capt. E. Voss, per arrangement, [date]. To be conveyed to buyer upon verification. V. to hold no copies — see clause 4, syndicate indemnity.*

Clause 4 was invented. That was the beauty of it. Voss would open the case expecting grants and find instead a paper trail — a meticulous, initialed, red-penciled paper trail — establishing that she had taken possession of stolen instruments of title, that she was to be denied copies precisely so that she could be blamed for them later, that the syndicate had prepared her as the fall from the beginning. Every document Voss had ever signed for them, Ada gambled, had contained some version of this clause. People believe the lies that match their experience.

She finished near dawn, blotted the last page, and sat looking at the box with its cargo of beautiful poison. Her hands were steady. Her eyes burned. On the bunk across the cabin, Casper lay awake — she could tell by his breathing, which never quite reached sleep's rhythm — and after a while he spoke into the dark.

*Will she believe it?*

*People believe what flatters their fears,* Ada said. *Voss fears being disposable. I'm handing her proof.*

A long pause. Rain started on the canvas overhead, soft as sifting flour.

*And if she opens it right there in the camp?*

*Then she reads it in front of us, and we watch her face do what faces do.* Ada closed the box. *Casper. You've spent twenty years reading sandbars. I've spent ten reading signatures. It's the same skill. Water and ink both tell you where they've been.*

He was quiet a long time. Then, in the dark, almost too low for her to hear: *My brother kept ledgers. For them. Same syndicate. He thought paper would protect him.* Another pause. *Paper is how they hold you. I never thought of it as how you cut loose.*

*That,* Ada said, *is because you never had a mother who lost a farm.*

* * *

They made the false camp two bends downstream, on a gravel terrace where the river ran shallow and loud — loud enough to cover voices, open enough to be seen from the ridge trail Voss's scouts used. Marisol baked through the morning, and by noon the smell of bread had done its work before a single shot was fired: the scouts would report a camp at rest, cooking, unafraid.

The performance began at midday.

Lio played the sentry who wasn't. He lounged at the terrace's upstream end with a fishing line in the water and his rifle leaning against a stone exactly far enough away to be a story told later — *he never even reached it.* Sefa tended a second fire, banking it badly, the way a tired person banks a fire. Casper sat visible on the Kestrel's deck, mending a net with slow, deliberate strokes, a captain who believed the river itself was armor.

Ada was the treasure. That was the casting no one argued with. She walked the gravel bar with the decoy case in her hand, openly, setting it down beside her while she checked readings on a instrument she didn't need to check — a woman guarding a thing carelessly because she believed herself unfindable. Twice she caught herself glancing upstream and made herself stop. Fear, performed badly, was more convincing than fear performed well. Everyone knew real people tried to hide it.

Voss came at the turn of the afternoon tide, when the light goes long and shadows lie.

They heard the boat before they saw it — her salvage tug running quiet against the current, engine throttled to a murmur, sliding out of the willow shade like a knife coming out of a drawer. Eight men on deck. Voss at the bow, tall and weathered, her coat dark with river spray, her eyes going immediately — Ada noted, admiring despite everything — to the case on the gravel, then to Ada, then to Casper on the deck, cataloguing threats in order of distance.

*Captain Nakamura,* Voss called, and her voice carried the way voices do over water, flattened and intimate at once. *You're a long way from your charted waters.*

*The river doesn't chart itself,* Casper said, not rising. *Can I offer you bread? My cook bakes when she's nervous.*

*I imagine she's been nervous for weeks.* Voss stepped onto the gravel. Two of her men fanned wide, casual as crows, cutting the angles. *I'll be brief. The case, the surveyor, and free passage downstream. In exchange, I don't burn a boat you clearly love, and nobody drowns today.*

*You've tried drowning us before,* Sefa said pleasantly, from her bad fire. *It didn't take.*

*Sefa.* Casper's voice, mild. A rein, not a whip.

Ada stood up slowly, the case in both hands, and made herself do the hardest thing in the whole architecture of the lie: she hugged it to her chest. *These are government instruments,* she said. *Under seal. If you take them, you're not a salvager anymore, Captain Voss. You're a thief of record.*

Something moved behind Voss's eyes — Ada watched for it and found it — not greed, exactly. Recognition. The look of a woman being handed the exact weight she had carried for years and finding it familiar. *Everything on this river is theft of record,* Voss said quietly. *The question is only whose name the record carries.* She extended her hand. *Last time, surveyor. The case, or the boat burns and we take it anyway, and I promise you my men are less gentle with barrels of flour than I am.*

There it was. A flicker, half a heartbeat, in Voss's own face — she knew about the flour. Of course she knew; her scouts had watched the camp all morning; Marisol's baking had been reported in detail. Ada felt the plan tilt beneath her like a deck in a swell, and made the only choice left, the one Sefa had built the whole design around:

She ran.

Not convincingly — convincingly was wrong, too slow, too strategic. She ran the way a real person runs when cornered: badly, upstream along the bar, boots slipping on wet stones, the case clutched hard enough to hurt. She heard Voss shout, heard boots behind her — two men, closing, professional — and then Lio's fishing rod hit the gravel and he came off his stone like a thrown knife, and for three glorious seconds it was a real fight, fists and gravel and the river roaring, until one of Voss's men put Lio down with a short blow and the other wrenched the case from Ada's arms and spun her by the shoulder so hard she went to one knee.

*Enough.* Voss's voice cracked across the bar like a winch cable snapping taut. She stood over Ada, breathing hard, the case under her arm, and looked down with an expression Ada would think about for years: not triumph. Relief. The relief of a long job finally loading onto someone else's shoulders. *Nobody follows us past the bend,* Voss said to Casper, backing toward her boat. *You have my word, and my word is the only clean thing I own.*

*Your word,* Casper said, rising at last, and his voice was terrible and calm, *has a hull like yours. Cracked in three places.*

But he didn't follow. That was the discipline of it, the thing Ada understood only afterward, watching the tug slide back into the willows with its stolen prize: Casper had known — had known from the moment Voss stepped ashore — that the case was bait, and had let his crew be knocked down and robbed anyway, had stood on his own deck and let it happen, because the alternative was a firefight on open gravel with eight rifles and two children aboard. He had traded his pride like a man trading ballast in a storm. She caught his eye across the bar as the willows swallowed the tug, and he looked away first.

* * *

They waited out the afternoon in the false camp, eating Marisol's bread, binding Lio's split lip, listening to the river. At full dark, Sefa climbed the ridge trail to the overlook and came back down at midnight with the news, breathless, grinning through the cold.

*They opened it.* She dropped onto the gravel, laughing silently, shoulders shaking. *Two miles down. We could see the lanterns. They opened the case on the foredeck, passed pages around — and then Voss read the margins. She read them twice. And then she threw a lantern.*

*A lantern?* Marisol asked.

*Into her own chart table. Set her own maps burning. They're still arguing. One of her men went over the side and swam for shore — deserted, right there in the current.* Sefa wiped her eyes. *She believes it. Every word. She thinks they built her to hang.*

Ada exhaled a breath she felt she'd been holding since her mother's kitchen, twenty years and one lifetime ago. Around the fire, the crew was laughing — really laughing, Lio with his fat lip, Marisol with flour still dusting her forearms, Bao smiling for the first time since the col, small and broken and real. Casper said nothing. He was looking at the fire, and then at the flour barrel standing by the galley door, and then — briefly, gravely — at Marisol, who met his eyes and touched the barrel's lid once, lightly, the way you touch a sleeping child.

*Tomorrow,* Casper said at last, *we run the lower gorge. Voss will come for us again — angrier, and alone now, off her employers' leash. That's worse. That's much worse.* He fed a stick to the fire. *But tonight—* and here he paused, and Ada watched him search for words the way he searched for channels, feeling along the bottom of himself for deep water — *tonight, the bread was good.*

Marisol snorted. *The bread was bait, you impossible man.*

*It was both,* Casper said. *Best things usually are.*

Ada turned in early but did not sleep. She lay listening to the rain arrive in earnest now, the monsoon's outriders testing the valley, and she thought about Voss on her burning deck, holding papers that named her guilty, believing at last the worst thing anyone could tell her: that she had never been anything but expendable. Tomorrow that belief would make Voss dangerous beyond calculation. Tonight it had made her human.

And beneath twenty pounds of white flour, sealed in oilcloth, the true grants waited — every signature honest, every margin clean, every page carrying nothing but what it truly said: *this land belongs to these people.* Ada pressed her palm flat against the deck planking, over the hold, over the barrel, a cartographer taking one last fix on the only coordinates that mattered.

Outside, the rain thickened. Downstream, two miles through the dark, a rival captain fed her burning charts to the river, and the current carried the ashes past the Kestrel's mooring like black snow — the old maps going to the sea, and the true ones riding high and dry in a cook's flour, waiting for morning.
