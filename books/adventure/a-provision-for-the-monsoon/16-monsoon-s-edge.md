## 16. Monsoon's Edge

The river told him first.

Casper felt it through the soles of his boots before he heard it — a tremor in the hull that wasn't engine, wasn't current, but the deep-water shudder of a river remembering what it was. He stood at the Kestrel's rail in the grey half-light and watched the lower reach of the valley, where the channel widened toward the lowlands, and saw the water there moving wrong. Not faster. Fuller. The way a vein looks different when the heart behind it has changed its mind.

"Landfall," he said, to no one, because saying it made it a fact instead of a fear.

Bao came up from the engine well wiping his hands on a rag that had been black for years and would be black until it rotted off him. He didn't ask what Casper meant. Bao had been listening to the river longer than most men listened to their wives. "How long?"

"Hours." Casper tested the word in his mouth and found it too generous. "Maybe fewer. The headwater clouds came over the col before dawn — I watched them while you slept. They weren't drifting. They were arriving."

Above them the sky had gone the color of wet slate, low and bruised, and the air carried that smell he knew better than his own name — green rot and hot metal, ozone and drowned earth. The monsoon's smell. Twenty years he'd run ahead of it, up one river and down another, always moored somewhere dry when the first rain hit, always gone before the water could learn the shape of his keel. Now he sat at its edge like a man waiting on a doorstep, and the strangest part, the part he couldn't get comfortable with, was that he wasn't alone on the doorstep. The whole crew was awake. Sefa and Lio were already lashing cargo under Marisol's shouted inventory, and Ada stood at the rail beside him with her instruments cradled against the coming wind, reading the sky the way she read contour lines: patiently, hungrily, as if it owed her an answer.

"Eleven days ago you gave us nine," she said. "The pass closed in eight. We beat both." She said it without triumph. She'd learned, over these weeks, that Casper didn't trust triumph; it arrived wearing the same face as luck. "Now the river does the arithmetic."

"The river always does the arithmetic." He nodded downstream. "The question is who else is doing it."

Because they were not alone on this flooded reach either. He'd seen the smoke at first light — not cooking smoke, which rises lazy and domestic, but boiler smoke, flat and purposeful, hugging the water two bends below where the channel braided around Charcoal Island. And beneath the smoke, when the wind shifted, the sound he'd hoped never to hear again in this lifetime: the heavy slap-slap-slap of an airboat's pusher propeller, that blunt mechanical heartbeat that belonged to no river he loved.

The syndicate's buyer had come by air, and the flood had come to meet him.

* * *

They took the Kestrel up a side channel to watch, because Casper had spent twenty years learning that the safest place on any river is the place nobody thinks is a river. The slough behind Charcoal Island was barely a boat's width in the dry season, choked with reed and deadfall, but the rising water had lifted them over the snags with a handspan to spare, and now they lay in the green shadow with the engine idling at a whisper and the crew pressed along the rail like children at a keyhole.

The buyer's airboat sat anchored off the island's downstream point, and it was everything the syndicate's money could buy and none of its subtlety: white hull, brass rail, a canvas canopy the color of fresh bandage, and standing in the bow a man in a linen suit that had no business above the delta. Even at two hundred meters Casper could read the posture. Hands clasped behind the back. Weight on the heels. A man who had never once needed to know how deep the water was under him, because other men were paid to know it for him.

With him, drawn up on the island's shingle, were Voss's men. Or what was left of them arranged in ranks. Voss himself stood apart, and Casper watched her the way he'd watch a sandbar whose mood had changed overnight — the same shape, the same bearing, but something underneath had shifted, and the next boat to trust the chart would break its back on it.

Ada had the spyglass. She passed it to Casper without a word, and he put it to his eye and found Voss's face.

She was reading a paper. A contract, folded once, held at arm's length in the flat light. Her lips moved — not speaking, checking. Line by line, the way Ada checked a survey entry. And then she lowered the paper, and looked at the man in the linen suit, and Casper saw the exact moment the arithmetic inside her came out wrong.

"She knows," he said quietly.

"Knows what?" Lio whispered.

"That we played her." Casper handed the glass back. "That case she took at the ambush camp — the one her divers pulled out of the flooded cache, the one she's been carrying like a crown — Ada built it to hang her. Doctored grants, forged provenance, a chain of custody that ends with Voss's own signature on a theft. Open it in front of the right magistrate and she doesn't lose the commission. She loses everything."

On the island, Voss did something small and total. She tore the contract in half. Then in half again. Then she dropped the pieces on the shingle, and the man in the linen suit said something — Casper couldn't hear it, but he knew its shape, the bored irritation of capital addressing labor — and Voss turned her back on him and walked to her own boat, and her men, after a heartbeat's confusion, followed her.

Not all of them. Three stayed on the island, and the man in the suit gestured, and one of those three began loading the torn contract pieces into a document case with the care of a man collecting evidence.

"He'll use it," Ada said. She'd taken the glass back and hadn't lowered it. "He doesn't need Voss anymore. He needs a villain, and she just resigned from the role and volunteered for it anyway. He'll open our false case, prove the forgery, and blame her for stealing the real grants — while his own people deliver the true papers to whoever burns land deeds in this province."

"And Voss?"

Ada lowered the spyglass at last. In the green light her face was hard to read, and Casper had learned enough about her to know that when her face was hard to read, the reading mattered most. "Voss is a mapmaker who discovered her map was being used to erase people. I know what that does." She paused. "It can make you careful. Or it can make you a fire."

Downstream, Voss's salvage boat cast off. It didn't turn toward the buyer's airboat. It didn't turn toward the delta. Its bow came around slow and deliberate, pointing upriver, pointing at the gorge, pointing — though there was no way she could know it yet — at a green slough behind Charcoal Island where a patched riverboat held its breath among the reeds.

Casper eased the throttle forward a finger's width and began, without hurry, without wake, to leave.

* * *

They ran the rest of the day with the flood climbing the banks beside them, and by late afternoon the monsoon stopped being a forecast and became a presence. It came the way it always came this far inland — not as a wall but as a decision. The light went bronze and strange. The birds went silent all at once, a silence with edges. And then the first drops fell, fat and warm and far apart, striking the river with little gasps of sound, each one a scout reporting back: *they're here, they're here, they're here.*

Marisol got the deck tarped and the galley sealed and still found time to press bowls of something spiced and steaming into every pair of hands, because that was Marisol, feeding people as if hunger were the only emergency worth acknowledging. Bao ran the engine high and hot, coaxing revolutions out of machinery that had been sunk, shot at, winched over a mountain, and patched with more hope than metal. The twins worked the lines barefoot, calling depths to each other in the twin-language that sounded like birdsong and meant *sandbar, snag, drop-off, safe.*

And Casper stood at the wheel and read the river.

That was the truth of it, the thing he'd never said aloud even in the snow cave, even to Ada with her patient questions: he didn't steer the Kestrel so much as negotiate with the water. Every reach had a mood. The flood had turned the whole lower river into a stranger — bars drowned that had guided him for years, channels reversed, eddies running backward where the main current leaned hard against the cut banks. A chart would have been useless. Half the charts of this river were lies anyway, drawn by men who'd seen it once in October and gone home to write about it. What he had instead was twenty years of accumulated acquaintance, and even that he held loosely now, humbly, the way you hold the hand of someone older than you who might let go at any moment.

"Talk to me," Ada said, coming to stand beside him at the wheel. Rain beaded on her braid. She didn't wipe it away. "Out loud. I want to learn how you see it."

"You don't want to learn how I see it. It's mostly superstition with a vocabulary."

"I've published three papers arguing that all navigation is superstition with a vocabulary. Try me."

So he talked. About the boil line off the point ahead — see how the water wrinkles there, that's the bar underneath shrugging its shoulders, give it room. About the color of the current seam, tea-dark against clay-brown, and how you crossed seams at an angle never square, because the river respected courtesy. About the floating debris — leaves, twigs, a whole uprooted sapling sailing past like a green ship — and how debris was the river's mail, telling you where the main thrust ran. Sefa drifted close under the pretense of coiling a line and stayed, listening. Then Lio. Then, pretending not to, Bao.

Casper kept talking, and understood, somewhere in the middle of explaining why a flooded cutbank should never be trusted at night, that he was doing the thing he had sworn off for twenty years. He was passing it on. Roots, he'd called them once, the way you'd say *debts.* But watching Sefa's eyes track the boil line and find it unassisted, watching her nod like a woman agreeing with an old friend, he felt the word change shape in his mouth. Not roots. Provision. Grain in the storehouse. Water in the cistern before the rains.

The thought lasted exactly as long as it took the lookout to shout.

"Sail! Dead ahead — no, starboard bank — coming fast!"

Voss's salvage boat came around the bend below them like a thrown knife.

She'd stripped her vessel for it — cargo booms dropped, salvage gear jettisoned or lashed, every scrap of weight traded for speed — and the flood gave her the same gift it gave everyone: current, tons of it, free and furious. She had the center of the channel and the sense to keep it, and her bow wave stood up white in the failing light, and riding her stern rail with one hand and something long-barreled in the other was a man Casper recognized from the portage, the one with the burned forearm, the one who'd cut the winch line that nearly took Lio.

"They're not signaling," Sefa said. "No flag, no horn."

"No," Casper said. "She's done signaling."

He did the math the way he always did, cold and fast, the survival math, the math that had kept him alive and alone for two decades: Voss was lighter, lower in the water, running with the flood on a straight reach. The Kestrel was overloaded, drawing heavy, patched twice since the gorge. On open water, in this light, with this current, she would run them down inside ten minutes, and the first shot would go to the wheelhouse because the man with the rifle understood boats too.

The old math said: take the side channel, hide, wait her out. The old math had served him faithfully for twenty years and cost him everything he'd never counted.

But the side channel led nowhere but deeper into the drowned country, and behind them, hours downstream, the man in the linen suit was opening a forged case, and ahead of them the villages waited on papers that were sewn into Marisol's flour barrel, and the monsoon was about to arrive in earnest and turn every channel, every slough, every secret he'd ever kept on this river into something new and unmapped.

There was only one way out of the lower valley before the flood peaked, and it ran straight through the gorge's final rapid — the one the pilots' guild had struck from the books thirty years ago, the one no chart showed, the one Casper had run once, young and stupid and lucky, in water a meter lower than this.

The old math said no.

"Marisol," he called. "Get the flour barrel lashed to the ring bolts. Double-lash it." He put the wheel over, gentle, courteous, and swung the Kestrel's bow toward the gorge. "Everyone else — hold on to something that loves you back."

Behind them, Voss saw the turn and understood it, and her engine note climbed into a scream of pursuit.

Ahead, through the thickening rain, the gorge walls rose black against the last bronze light, and the river between them went white.
