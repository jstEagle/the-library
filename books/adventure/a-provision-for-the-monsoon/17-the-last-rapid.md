## 17. The Last Rapid

The river told him first.

Not the rain — the rain was still an hour out, a bruise-colored wall stacked against the eastern ranges, grumbling to itself like an old man asked to move furniture. Not Voss, either, though the salvage boat's engine was a constant low toothache somewhere behind them, patient as debt. It was the water itself. Casper stood at the wheel with his palm flat against the rail and felt the Kestrel's hull change its argument with the current, the way a horse changes its argument with the ground before an earthquake any man can feel.

The river was rising. Fast.

"Bao!" he called down the engine hatch, and didn't wait for the answer he knew was coming. Bao had been down there since before dawn, coaxing the patched hull along with hands that still wouldn't meet Casper's eyes — that was a debt for later, if there was a later, if the gorge let them live long enough to owe each other anything at all. "Bao, I need everything she has and then some she doesn't."

"She doesn't have some," Bao's voice floated up, cracked and sooty. "But I'll find it."

Casper looked at the water and read it the way he'd been reading it for twenty years, the way other men read letters from home. The Kestrel had run this gorge twice before, in lower water, and both times he'd taken the long channel — the one the colonial charts showed in confident blue ink, the one with the wide, honest entrance and the rock garden waiting at the far end like a joke with a long setup. The charts were ten years old. The river had rewritten them every monsoon since, and this monsoon hadn't even landed yet. It was only leaning on the door.

The sandbars had moods. That was the first thing old Ferro had taught him, back when Casper had been a boy with nothing to his name but a stolen knife and a talent for not being where people looked. A sandbar in low water is a liar — it shows you a channel that isn't there. A sandbar in rising water is a gambler — it moves its money while you watch. A sandbar in falling water is a mourner, and it will grieve you onto its spine before you know you've run aground. But a sandbar with the first flood coming down on it, the first cold snowmelt-and-rainwater shoving the whole river up its throat — that sandbar tells the truth. It has no time for anything else. The flood strips it bare, shows you its bones, and in the bones is the channel.

And there — a half-mile ahead, where the gorge pinched down to its final throat — Casper could see the truth being told. A standing wave where no wave should stand. A boil of brown water curling backward off something submerged, folding over itself like a hand closing. The old channel was dead. The chart was dead. The river had dug a new one overnight, somewhere no surveyor had ever thought to look, and it was showing him the entrance right now, this minute, before the flood rose another hand's width and closed it forever.

"Sefa! Lio! Lines off the cargo — everything loose goes under the tarp and lashed double. Marisol—" He stopped. Marisol was already at the rail, flour-barrel lashed to her hip like a child, her face set in the expression she wore when she'd decided the world would do what she said. The real case was in that barrel. He didn't say it. Nobody said it. Some cargo you don't name, because names are how the river collects.

"We're going to run it," Lio said. Not a question. The twins had grown up on this water, or water like it, and they knew the sound of a captain who had stopped choosing.

"We're going to run the channel that isn't there," Casper said. "The one the river dug last night. Starboard of the drowned bar, where the water folds back on itself. It'll look like I'm steering us into the gorge's teeth, because I am. Trust the boat. Trust me. Don't trust the chart, the chart's a rumor."

"And Voss?" Sefa asked, chin jerking downstream.

Casper turned. The salvage boat had come around the last bend below them, running the same flood, and she was bigger than he remembered — a river tug gone to war, iron-bowed, built to shove dead hulls off bars, and Voss had built a ram onto that bow that made the purpose of it embarrassingly plain. She was gaining. The flood lifted all boats, but it lifted the heavy one faster; she had the deeper channel, the wider beam, the engine that hadn't spent three weeks being patched with village scrap and prayer.

Voss stood at her wheel. Even at this distance Casper could see she wasn't looking at the water. She was looking at the Kestrel, the way a man looks at a debt.

"She's behind us," Casper said. "That's the only good news today."

* * *

The rain arrived the way the monsoon always arrived — not as weather but as a verdict.

One moment the gorge was gray and grumbling; the next, the sky simply opened, all of it at once, a curtain of water so dense it erased the canyon walls, erased the ridgelines, erased the world above the river and left only the river and the boat and the sound, which was not a sound so much as a pressure, a hand laid flat against the whole gorge. The first drops hit the deck like flung gravel. Then there were no drops. There was only rain, everywhere, the river rising to meet the sky and the sky falling to meet the river, and the Kestrel between them like a coin between two palms.

"Ada!" Casper roared, and Ada came up the companionway with her instruments wrapped in oilcloth, her hair already plastered to her skull, and she didn't ask what he wanted because she'd already read it off his face. She'd been reading the sky all morning with her barometer and her instruments, and she'd told him the truth at dawn: landfall by afternoon, flood crest by dark, and the final rapid was passable for perhaps two hours after landfall and not one minute more.

"How high?" she shouted.

"Two hands since the bend!" He pointed at the gauge — a notched stick Marisol had nailed to the rail years ago, a joke that had become an instrument. Ada looked at it, looked at the water folding back over the drowned bar ahead, and did the arithmetic he watched move behind her eyes like fish under ice.

"The channel closes in twenty minutes," she said. "Maybe less. The flood's coming down the gorge faster than it's coming up the river — the crest will hit this throat before it hits us, and when it does—"

"I know what a crest does."

"I know you know." She gripped the rail beside him, and for a moment, in all that roar, there was just the two of them and the water. "Casper. The chart channel — the rock garden—"

"Is a graveyard in flood. The standing wave at the entrance is six feet and climbing. We go where the river dug last night, or we don't go."

"Then go," she said simply, and put her hand over his on the wheel, and took it away again, and that small touch in all that violence was the loudest thing in the gorge.

He brought the wheel over. The Kestrel leaned, groaned, argued — she was overloaded to the gunwales with salt and tools and the village's future in a flour barrel, and she handled like a barge with opinions — but Bao found something in the engine, some reserve that shouldn't have existed, and the old boat came around and pointed her scarred bow at the place where the water folded back on itself.

It looked, from the wheel, like steering into a wall. The standing wave reared off the drowned bar, brown and muscular, and behind it the water poured backward into a trough that no honest channel should have, and beyond that the gorge's final throat narrowed to a gap that looked, in the rain, like a door slammed shut. Every instinct Casper had bought with twenty years of moving screamed at him to bear off, to take the chart channel, to take the wide honest entrance with the graveyard at the end of it.

He held the wheel. He watched the water fold. And he saw it — the seam. The line where the floodwater coming down met the river coming up and slid along the bar's drowned spine, a tongue of smoother water, greener, moving fast, a ribbon of channel no wider than the Kestrel herself, threading between the bar's bones and the gorge wall. It was there and then it wasn't, there and gone, visible only in the instant the rain thinned, and Casper understood that the river was showing it to him the way you show a card to a friend: once, quickly, and only if you're already looking.

"Now," he said. "Bao — everything."

* * *

The Kestrel hit the seam at full throttle, and the world became water.

The standing wave took the bow and threw it — Casper felt the whole boat go weightless, felt the cargo shift below with a sound like furniture in a landslide, felt Sefa and Lio go down the deck on the run to the lines because they'd known, the way crew know, that this was the moment the lashings would try to quit. The bow came down into the trough beyond and the trough was deeper than the boat was long, and for one full second the Kestrel was falling, falling, and Marisol was at the rail with both arms around the flour barrel, shouting something into the rain that was either a prayer or a recipe, and then the hull met the water at the bottom of the trough and the whole boat shuddered like a struck bell and came up running.

They were in the channel. The channel that wasn't there.

It was worse than it had looked. It was a slot of green water between the drowned bar's bones and the gorge wall, and the bones were everywhere — Casper could see them now, black rock shoulders under the surface, sliding past the hull close enough to touch, and the flood was pouring down this slot faster than the Kestrel could motor through it, so that they were less running the rapid than being swallowed by it, aimed, the river holding the whole boat in its throat and deciding, second by second, whether to spit or swallow. Rain hammered the wheel out of his hands' grip; he wrapped the spokes in his forearms and steered by feel, by the tremor that came up through the hull when the channel bent and the water told him which way before his eyes could find it. Left. The hull tremored left. He gave it left. The black shoulder of the bar slid past so close he could have leaned out and laid his palm on it.

"Rock, starboard!" Lio's voice, high and clear, the boy reading the water off the bow the way Casper read it off the wheel — and Casper realized, in some small sealed room at the center of all that chaos, that the boy had learned it. That somewhere in three years of sandbars and dawn departures, Lio had learned to hear what the river said, and was saying it back to him now, call and answer, the old language passed hand to hand in the middle of a flood.

"Ahead!" Sefa, from the stern, watching the water they'd already run. "Behind us — she's at the entrance — she's taking the chart channel—"

Casper didn't turn. He couldn't; the channel was bending and the bend was blind. But he heard it, or imagined he heard it — the deep flat boom of a heavy iron bow meeting rock that had been waiting ten years for something heavy and proud. The sound came through the water, through the hull, through the wheel in his arms, one long grinding note, and then a second note, higher, like something tearing.

The rock garden. The chart's honest channel. The graveyard with the long setup.

He held the seam. The channel narrowed, bent again, and here was the worst of it — a hole in the water ahead, a vortex where two currents fought over the bar's last bone, and the channel went directly through the edge of it, close enough that the whirlpool's shoulder would take the stern if he gave it a hand's width too much room. Below the hole, the water calmed. Below the hole, the gorge opened. Below the hole was the lower river, and the villages, and the valley, and everything they had crossed a mountain range to carry.

"Bao!" he roared down the hatch. "On my mark, cut power to nothing — dead nothing — one breath, no more!"

"Nothing?!"

"Nothing! The hole pulls harder than we push — we let it take the bow, we let it turn us, and we come out the far side pointed downstream. Trust the boat!"

A pause, one heartbeat, down in the engine room where a man who had betrayed them and been forgiven was holding his hands on a dying engine. Then Bao's voice, steady as a level: "Ready."

The hole swelled ahead of them, a black eye in the brown water, turning.

"Mark!"

Bao killed the engine. The Kestrel went silent — truly silent, for the first time in three weeks, just the rain and the water and the creak of a hull holding its shape out of stubbornness — and the vortex took the bow. Casper felt it grip, felt the whole boat begin to pivot, the stern swinging wide, the whirlpool spinning them like a leaf deciding, and he stood at the wheel with his arms wrapped around the spokes and did nothing. Nothing was the hardest thing he had ever done on this river. Nothing was harder than the portage, harder than the col, harder than ordering his crew after a man who'd stolen from them. He did nothing, and the river turned them, and the turn was exactly as far as he had read it would be, and no farther.

"Power," he said.

Bao gave it everything. The engine caught with a sound like a cough and a shout, the screw bit, and the Kestrel came out of the hole's edge the way a fish comes out of a heron's beak — late, sideways, and alive. The stern cleared the vortex's shoulder by less than an arm's length. Casper watched the whirlpool swallow the water where they had just been, close its black eye, and keep turning, patient, keeping whatever came next.

And then the gorge opened.

It opened all at once, the way the rain had opened — the walls fell back, the sky came down in a gray sheet, and the Kestrel ran out of the throat into the wide brown flood of the lower river with the villages somewhere ahead in the rain and the crest still coming down from the mountains behind them. The channel that wasn't there fell away astern. Casper brought the wheel amidships and found that his arms were shaking, and that his face was wet, and that he could no longer have said which of it was rain.

* * *

They came off the rapid one at a time, back to their stations, the way a bell comes off its strike — the ringing continuing long after the hammer has lifted. Sefa and Lio stood in the rain among the lashings, checking knots with hands that moved on their own. Marisol had not let go of the flour barrel. Ada stood at the rail with her instruments forgotten under their oilcloth, looking back up the gorge at the wall of rain where the throat had been.

Then Sefa said, quietly: "Look."

Behind them, out of the rain, the salvage boat came drifting.

She came down the channel they had run — not the chart channel, not anymore. The rock garden had done its work; Casper could see the story of it in the way she sat in the water, down by the bow, listing, her ram torn half away and her hull opened along the starboard side like a gutted fish. Her engine was dead. The flood carried her the way the flood carried everything now — logs, roofs, uprooted trees, the wreckage of things that had believed themselves too heavy to move. And on her deck, small in the rain, figures moved: lowering a boat, or trying to. The flood was taking her toward the shallows above the drowned bar, toward the bones that had opened her, and the water there was full of rock, and the rain was still falling, and the crest was still coming down.

Voss stood at her wheel. Even now, even listing and sinking and beaten by the river she'd spent her life reading, she stood at her wheel, and Casper understood that she would stand there until the water took the wheel too.

The crew had gathered at the rail without anyone calling them. He could feel them beside him — Marisol's flour-barred arms, the twins' soaked shoulders, Ada's silence, Bao come up the hatch into the rain with soot to the elbows. Nobody said turn around. Nobody said leave her. The river below the throat was wide and brown and rising, and somewhere in it were the people who had hunted them across a mountain range, and the Kestrel was patched and overloaded and had just given everything she had.

Casper looked at the water. He looked at his crew. He put the wheel over.

"Bao," he said. "Get me a line. Sefa, Lio — bow fenders, all of them. Marisol—" and here he almost smiled, in the rain, in the flood, at the very edge of everything—"get the galley stove lit. We're going to have wet, angry people on my deck in ten minutes, and I have never once known you to feed fewer than everyone."

The Kestrel came about, and turned upstream into the rain, toward the wreck and the woman at her wheel — toward whatever the river, having finally told the whole truth, meant them to do about it.
