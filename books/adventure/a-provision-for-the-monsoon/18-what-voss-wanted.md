## 18. What Voss Wanted

The rain found us before we found her.

It came down the valley in a grey wall, the monsoon's outriders, and it turned the lower river into something that no longer answered to charts. I stood at the Kestrel's rail with my instruments lashed under oilcloth and watched the water climb the pilings of a drowned jetty — one hand-span in an hour, then two — and understood that every measurement I had ever loved was now provisional. The river had stopped being geography. It was becoming weather.

Casper stood beside me, saying nothing, which was how he said most things. His eyes were on the channel ahead, where the flood had swallowed the sandbars whole and left only their moods behind: a boil here where current met rock, a long smooth lie there where the water deepened without warning. He read them the way Marisol read dough — by feel, by years, by something that could not be written down.

"There," Sefa called from the bow, and pointed.

At first I thought it was driftwood. Then the driftwood groaned.

Voss's boat lay on its side across a shoal that the flood had made and would unmake by morning, wedged like a bone in a throat. Her hull — black, fast, built for salvage and for taking — was stove along the starboard line, planks sprung outward like ribs. Debris ringed her in a slow carousel: a crate of chain, a hatch cover turning end over end, a lifebuoy going around and around as if searching for someone to save.

And on the shoal itself, small against the grey water, a figure in a soaked coat, kneeling, then standing, then raising both arms — not waving, I realized. Surrendering. Holding her hands open to the sky so we could see they were empty.

"She'll have a gun," Lio said. He had come up quietly, the way he did, and his voice was flat with the memory of the gorge, of the winch line, of his brother's face going under brown water. "She tried to drown us."

"She tried to sink the boat," Casper said. It was not a correction so much as a distinction, and I watched him make it deliberately, the way a man tests ice. "Boats can be mended."

"Can people?" Sefa asked, and no one answered her.

The rain thickened. Below us, the figure on the shoal did not move. She stood in the rising water to her knees, arms open, letting the monsoon's first true rain hammer her shoulders, and she waited for our decision the way a condemned woman waits — not hoping, exactly, but refusing to give the river the satisfaction.

Casper looked at the channel. He looked at the sky, doing the arithmetic he never spoke aloud — hours of light, feet of rise, the distance still between us and the valley. Then he looked at me, and what was in his face was not mercy. Mercy is cheap; it costs a moment's warmth. What was in his face was recognition, though I did not yet understand of what.

"Bring her in," he said. "Unarmed or not at all."

* * *

Getting her off the shoal took everything the crew had, and I think that was part of why Casper ordered it — not sentiment, but the plain fact that a drowned enemy tells you nothing, while a rescued one might tell you everything. Sefa and Lio ran a line through the flood on the skiff, cursing in twin harmony, and Bao held the Kestrel's engine at a tension that sang in the deck plates, and Marisol stood at the rail with a blanket under her arm and an expression that dared anyone to comment on it.

When they finally hauled Voss aboard — half-drowned, coughing the river out of herself, her hands still open and empty even as she retched — she looked smaller than she had from the ridge camps, smaller than she had in my nightmares after the col. Captain Voss of the salvage boat *Reprisal*, who had shadowed us for six hundred miles, who had bought informants and burned contracts and hunted us out of pride when her employers' money stopped mattering: she knelt on our wet deck in her ruined coat and shook like a landed fish.

Marisol put the blanket around her shoulders anyway.

"Don't," Voss rasped. "Don't be kind to me. I haven't earned—"

"No one earns anything on this boat," Marisol said. "That's rather the point." And she went below to put the kettle on, because that was Marisol's answer to every catastrophe, and I have since learned it was a better answer than most.

We tied Voss to the mast — Casper's condition, delivered without heat: *unarmed means unarmed, and tied means tied* — and gave her soup, because Marisol insisted that interrogation goes better fed. The rain came down harder. The river rose around us, and Casper took us into the flooded timber at the bank's edge, where the trees broke the current, and dropped anchor in three fathoms of what had been dry land a week ago. We would ride out landfall's first fury there, lashed to living wood, waiting for the water to decide what it wanted to be.

Night came early under the storm. In the lamplight of the main cabin, with the rain drumming the roof like a thousand small fists, Voss finally stopped shaking and started talking.

I expected defiance. I expected bargaining — she had come to me once before, at the ridge camp, with an empire's cartography department folded inside an offer like a knife inside a coat. Instead she sat with her wrists raw from the lines and looked at the case sitting under my arm — the real case, which had spent the last hundred miles wrapped in flour-sack cloth inside Marisol's flour barrel, though Voss didn't know that, wouldn't know it until we chose to tell her — and she began, quietly, to laugh.

"You're carrying it in a *flour barrel*," she said. "The instrument that decides who owns the valley. In a flour barrel."

"It decided better there than it would have in your hold," I said.

"Yes." She laughed again, and the laughter cracked in the middle and became something else. "Yes, it would have. That's the joke, surveyor. That's the whole rotten joke."

"Tell us what you wanted," Casper said. He sat across from her with his arms folded, and his voice had none of the river's music in it — just bedrock. "Not the syndicate's want. Yours."

Voss was quiet for a long time. Outside, lightning stitched the clouds above the range we had crossed, and the thunder came down the valley seconds later, rolling like cargo.

"I wanted to stop being owned," she said.

* * *

She told it plainly, which is the only way such stories can be told.

She had been a mapmaker — a real one, trained, good, the way I am good — and twenty years ago the syndicate had found her the way it finds all of us: through a debt, or a sister, or a village with a paper trail leading back to a loan shark in the capital. Hers had been a debt. Her mother's medical bills, itemized and compounding, sold twice over to men whose names changed but whose handwriting never did. They had bought her instruments, her training, her eye. They had put her in a room with the surveys of a hundred valleys and told her to draw the lines that made dispossession legal.

"You think the grants are unusual," she said to me. "You think your vanished team found something rare. They didn't. Every valley on this river has papers like yours somewhere — buried, misfiled, 'lost' in a fire. My job was finding them first. My job was drawing the map that made the old papers meaningless. Boundary adjustments. Easements. A road here, a reservoir there, and suddenly a village that had held its land for four hundred years was squatting on crown property." She flexed her bound hands. "I drew the Widow's Gorge easement myself. Eleven years ago. Did you know that? The portage you nearly died in — the rapids that took Lio — I put a haulage right-of-way through there on behalf of a mining concern that never mined an ounce. It was a fiction. A line on a map. But lines are load-bearing, surveyor. You know that better than anyone."

I did know it. It is the first thing they teach you and the last thing they admit: that a map is not a picture of power but an engine of it, and whoever holds the pen holds the future. I had spent my career telling myself my instruments were neutral. Sitting in that lamplit cabin, listening to the woman who had drawn the world I'd been measuring, I felt the floor of my profession tilt beneath me.

"Why tell us this?" Lio asked. His arms were crossed the same way Casper's were; he had learned it from watching. "Why should we believe a word?"

"Because I burned the contract," Voss said simply. "The night I found your decoy case. I opened it in my cabin expecting grant papers, and instead I found a forged provenance trail that named *me* as the thief — dated, countersigned, beautiful work, by the way," she added, with a bitter glance at me that I chose to receive as a compliment. "My employers meant to hang their own dog. So I burned the contract, and I came after you alone, out of pride, because pride was the only thing they hadn't bought." She looked down at her wrists. "And pride wrecked me on a sandbar in a flood. So here I am. Owned by nothing, owned by no one, and about to be owned by whatever you decide to do with me."

The rain hammered. The lamp swung gently with the current's pull on the anchor line, and shadows moved around the cabin like the ghosts of all the boats this woman had chased.

"What do you want?" Casper asked again. Softer now. That was the thing about him — he asked twice because the first answer was always armor.

Voss was silent so long I thought she wouldn't answer. Then:

"When I was drawing those maps," she said, "there was one rule. One thing they paid me never to do. Never draw a boundary that *gives* land back. Adjustments, takings, easements — fine. Restitution — never. There are villages on this river, dozens of them, whose grants exist in some archive or other, and in eleven years I was never once allowed to draw a line that returned anything." She raised her head, and her eyes in the lamplight were dry and fierce and terribly tired. "I want to see it done once. That's all. I want to stand in a square somewhere and watch a line on a map give land *back*. I don't expect forgiveness. I don't expect to be crew. I'll row. I'll bail. Put me ashore at the valley if you like and I'll walk out of these mountains when the snows melt and never trouble you again. But let me see it done. Let me see whether it's possible — whether the pen can go the other direction — before I die knowing only what it takes away."

No one spoke. The kettle ticked on the stove. Somewhere above us, the storm leaned on the mountains, and the river rose another finger-width against the hull.

* * *

Casper untied her himself, later, when the others had gone to their bunks and the watch had been set. I stayed — partly because I didn't trust her, mostly because I didn't trust him not to do something reckless and generous and call it seamanship.

He worked the knots loose without looking at her face. "Rules," he said. "You handle no lines forward of the wheelhouse. You carry no blade. You eat last until Sefa says otherwise — she keeps the stores, her word is law. And when we reach the valley, you stand where I can see you."

"And if I run?"

"You won't." He coiled the lines. "Running costs more than staying. You've just spent twenty years learning that the expensive way. Same tuition I paid."

Voss rubbed her wrists and studied him with the appraising look of a woman reading water. "Nakamura. The syndicate has a file on you this thick." She held up two fingers. "I've seen it. Twenty years of sightings, ports, aliases. Do you know what struck me about it? Not the length. The *shape*. Every entry is a departure. There isn't a single arrival in twenty years of records. No address longer than a season. No name given twice." She tilted her head. "A man shaped entirely by leaving. I used to wonder what had happened to him, to make a shape like that."

"You could have read the file's first page," Casper said. "It would have told you."

"Flood year. Nakamura. Brother, deceased." She said it without cruelty, and I saw that this, too, was a kind of accuracy — the same precision she had brought to boundaries, turned at last toward truth. "I know what it is to be owned, captain. I told you that. But I think you know something worse. I think you know what it is to be *unowned* — to have nothing left that anyone can take, because you already gave it all up, and to discover that being free of them and being free of everything look identical from the outside."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from the door. On the river, the rain had settled into its long siege, the monsoon finding its rhythm, hour upon hour, the sound of a season arriving that would remake every shoreline we knew.

"That's enough," Casper said quietly. Not angry. Just finished. He hung the coiled lines on their pegs, each in its place, the small liturgy of a man who kept order where he could. At the door he paused, his back to us, and I watched him decide something — watched it move through his shoulders like current under a calm surface.

"The kettle's still hot," he said. "Marisol will feed you whether you deserve it or not. That's not kindness. It's policy." And he went out into the rain to take the first watch, leaving the two mapmakers alone in the lamplight with the case between us — the real one, heavy with other people's futures, smelling faintly of flour.

Voss looked at me across it. "Your captain," she said, "is going to stop running someday. Before the file gets any thicker. Mark me — I've drawn the maps of men like him. There's always a valley at the end of the chart where the departures run out."

"He'll need a reason," I said.

"He'll need a reason," she agreed, and closed her eyes, and slept — truly slept, for the first time in six hundred miles, in the belly of the boat she had tried to break, while the monsoon came down on the roof of the world.

I sat up with the case in my lap and did not sleep. Through the cabin's small window I watched Casper at the wheelhouse rail, motionless in the rain, keeping his watch over water that rose all night — and I thought about arrivals, and departures, and what the morning would bring when the flood finally showed us the shape of the last miles to the valley.

Because far upstream, through the rain, a light was burning in the high valley where the vanished team had sheltered all winter. We would see it at dawn, if the river let us through. And everything — the grants, the syndicate, the administrator coming upriver behind us with warrants in his pocket — everything would come to a head in a village square before the ink of the monsoon could wash the world clean.

The light burned. The river rose. And the Kestrel, patched and overloaded and crewed by people who had chosen each other, rode her anchor in the flooded timber, pointed home.
