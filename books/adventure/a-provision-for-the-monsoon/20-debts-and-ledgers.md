## 20. Debts and Ledgers

The rain had been falling for two days when the administrator's launch came up the valley river, and it came the way all official things come — too loud, too clean, and certain of its welcome.

Casper heard it before he saw it: the flat slap of an outboard working against current that wanted to argue. He was on the Kestrel's deck under an oilcloth awning, re-caulking a seam he'd already caulked twice, because his hands needed work the way other men's hands needed prayer. The whole valley smelled of wet earth and woodsmoke and the green rot of the monsoon's first full week. Down at the village jetty — the jetty he'd been rebuilding plank by plank since they'd moored here, as if a man could build himself into a place by fixing its edges — Marisol was hauling her cook pots under cover, and Sefa stood at the rail with rain running off her chin, watching the bend where the sound came from.

"Launch," she said, though he'd already told her.

"I know."

"Government."

"They usually are." He set down his caulking iron. "Get Ada."

The launch rounded the bend flying the administration's grey pennant, and standing in its bow with a hooded cape beaded silver with rain was Administrator Corvel himself — the same narrow man with the same narrow voice who had conscripted Casper in a paper-walled office half a world of river ago. Corvel had sent letters then. Now he'd come in person, which meant either the matter had grown very large or very desperate, and Casper had learned long ago that those were usually the same thing.

The launch bumped the new jetty. A soldier in soaked khaki jumped ashore first, rifle slung, boots slipping on Casper's hand-laid planks, and Casper felt an old anger rise in him at the sight — not of the gun, but of the boots on his planks, the ones he'd fitted in the rain for people who would never wear boots like that.

Corvel stepped ashore with more dignity and less grip. He looked around the village square with the expression of a man reading a ledger he hadn't authorized: the drying racks, the grain stores raised on stilts and packed deep, the Kestrel moored bow-and-stern like she'd grown there, the villagers gathering in the rain without hurrying, without scattering. They did not scatter. That was the thing Corvel's eyes kept snagging on. In the capital, people scattered.

"Nakamura." Corvel's voice carried the way official voices do, pitched for a courtroom whether or not one existed. "You're difficult to find. I've been looking for you since the gorge."

"You found me," Casper said. "Congratulations."

"I've found rather more than you." Corvel drew a folded paper from inside his cape, and even sodden by spray it managed to look official, the way a snake manages to look like a stick until it moves. "A warrant. For the arrest of the person or persons responsible for the theft of government survey property, the forgery of colonial land instruments, and the obstruction of a lawful recovery operation." His eyes went past Casper, up the slope, to where Ada Okonkwo stood under the eave of the meeting house with her instrument case at her feet and rain streaming off the roof between them. "I believe you know who I mean."

* * *

They gathered in the meeting house because the rain gave them no choice and the village gave them no better option. Corvel sat at the head of the long table the elders used for disputes about water rights and bride-prices, and he sat there as if the chair had always been his, and Casper watched Old Yeva — who had come down from her pass with three goats and a face like weathered granite — watch Corvel sit there, and knew what she was thinking, because Casper was thinking it too: *men like this have never once been asked to leave a room.*

The warrant lay on the table between them, blotting rain.

"Theft," Ada said. She'd taken the chair directly across from Corvel, and she sat the way she charted rivers — precisely, with nothing wasted. "Whose property?"

"The government's. The survey team's instruments and findings are crown property. Their final report, their maps, their—"

"Their grants," Ada said.

Corvel's mouth thinned. "There are no grants."

"There are." Ada reached into her coat — the soldier's rifle came up halfway and Casper's hand was already moving before he decided to move it, and then everyone remembered themselves and froze — and she drew out not the sealed case but a slim oilskin folder, tied with cord. "You said *forgery of colonial land instruments.* I'd like to know how you knew to use that phrase, Administrator, since the grants in question have never been entered into any colonial registry. No one outside this valley and your syndicate's paymasters could describe them accurately enough to allege forgery."

The rain filled the silence. It came off the thatch in ropes, drummed the packed-earth floor, ran in bright threads through the doorway. Somewhere outside a goat complained, philosophically.

Corvel recovered the way officials recover, by becoming colder. "You are not the one being questioned."

"No," Ada agreed. "But I'm the one holding the evidence." She untied the cord and opened the folder, and turned it, and slid it across the table so the pages faced him. "These are the syndicate's papers. Not mine. Not the survey team's. Yours — or your masters'. Forged grant documents, backdated eleven years, transferring the valley lands to the Meridian Land and Salvage Consortium. We took them off Voss's wreck. And here—" she turned another page, and Casper, standing behind her shoulder, watched Corvel's face do something small and terrible, something a man's face does when a floor he believed solid shows him its underside, "—is the covering letter. Signed. Stamped with the colonial secretariat's own seal. Authorizing the consortium to *recover and suppress* the survey findings, by whatever means necessary, including the disappearance of the survey team itself."

"That letter is a fabrication," Corvel said.

"It is," Ada said pleasantly. "That's the point. It's a fabrication made by your own office. Look at the ink batch. Look at the watermark. Your clerks forged these grants eleven years ago to dispossess these villages, and when the survey team stumbled onto the original titles — the true ones, the ones that return every hectare to the people who never stopped living on them — your office forged again, to bury them. I didn't make any of this. I just read it. Reading is what I'm for."

She laid the last page down. It was the countersigned grant — the real one, witnessed in the square nine days ago while the monsoon broke over them like a held breath finally let out, the ink smudged but legal, the elders' marks pressed beside the surveyor's seal.

"The inquiry will decide what's genuine," Corvel said, but his voice had lost its courtroom pitch. It sounded, suddenly, like a man's voice.

"Yes," Ada said. "It will. That's why I'm giving you everything. Both sets of papers. Let your magistrates compare ink with ink, seal with seal, lie with lie. I only ask one thing." She leaned forward. "When they compare them — make sure the inquiry includes the lower provinces. Make sure it reaches the prison at Karst Hollow."

Casper went still.

He hadn't heard the name spoken aloud since Bao had confessed it at the col, broken open by fog and rope-bridge and mercy: *Karst Hollow,* the administrative prison where the syndicate kept its leverage, where Bao's sister Mei had been held for six years on charges nobody could name, the debt that had bought a good engineer's hands for treason. Casper had ordered the crew after Bao knowing that name was the reason. He had not known, until this moment, that Ada had been carrying it too — folded into her maps, plotted like a coordinate, waiting for exactly this table and exactly this man.

"Karst Hollow," Corvel repeated carefully.

"A woman named Mei Lin Bao," Ada said. "Detained without trial. Six years. Her file will show her detention was ordered under emergency provisions signed by the same office that signed your consortium's letter. When the inquiry opens that file, Administrator, it opens yours."

* * *

They left the next morning — Corvel, the soldier, the warrant left behind on the table like a snake that had forgotten how to strike. Casper watched the launch recede down the flooded river and felt nothing he could name, which was, he was learning, often the shape relief takes when it arrives after too long a wait.

Then the waiting began in earnest, and Casper discovered that waiting was worse than rapids. Rapids at least required your whole attention. Waiting left room for the mind to run its old arithmetic: *what we gained, what it cost, what comes due.* He spent the days on the jetty because the jetty asked nothing of him but planks. He spent the evenings in the meeting house because the elders had begun, without discussion, to include him in their deliberations about grain stores and drainage, and he had begun, without deciding to, to attend.

Ada worked. Of course she worked. She'd spread her charts across the schoolroom floor and was drawing the valley for the villages — not for the empire, not for a sealed case, but at the scale a farmer needs: where the flood reaches, where the wells run sweet, which fields drain and which drown. Villagers came and pointed and corrected her, and she wrote their corrections into the map in their own words, and Casper understood he was watching something being built that would outlast all of them.

On the ninth day the mail boat came upriver — a stubby government steamer, unarmed, carrying a clerk, a sack of letters, and a single passenger.

Bao saw her before anyone. Casper was at the rail of the Kestrel when Bao came up the bank at a dead run, slipping in the mud, and the woman coming down the gangplank dropped her bundle and ran too, and they met in the shallows of the flooded landing and held each other like people pulling each other out of water. Mei Lin Bao was thin and sun-starved and laughing and crying in the same breath, and the clerk stood on the deck holding her release order and looking embarrassed, as clerks do when paperwork produces joy instead of obedience.

"She wouldn't tell me anything," Bao said later, hoarse, at Marisol's fire, his sister asleep against his shoulder under a shared blanket. "Six years I imagined it. What they did to her. Whether she hated me. And the first thing she says is —" his voice cracked and he pushed through it — "*you got thin.*"

Marisol put another ladle of stew in his bowl without asking. Casper sat across the fire and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the stew wasn't already saying.

But that night, alone on the Kestrel's deck with the rain gone soft and the valley breathing around him, Casper took out the old ledger.

He kept it in the chart drawer beneath the false bottom, and he had not opened it since the col. It was a small book, oilcloth-bound, and in it — in his own cramped hand, twenty years of it — were debts. Names and sums. The elder whose granary he'd quietly paid off in chapter one of this life; the harbormaster bribed; the passage-fees; the informant downstream paid to watch his own boat and report nothing. Every entry a payment made to keep the past at arm's length. Every payment a wall-stone. He had built himself out of this book the way a hermit builds out of stones, and he had called the building freedom.

He turned to the page near the front, the oldest entry, the one written in a younger hand, harder, the ink almost brown now. *Corvel, R. — 4,000 — silence re: Nakamura, C. — hold indefinitely.*

His brother's death. The syndicate's flood that took the delta farms and took Wei with them, and the young clerk named Corvel who had processed the water rights and could have testified and had been paid, by Casper, twenty years ago, to keep quiet about what he knew — and who had kept the receipt, and had waited, and had finally cashed it as a conscription. *An old debt Casper thought buried.* That was what the summons had been. Not justice. Leverage. Corvel had owned him for twenty years with a piece of paper, and Casper had let him, because owning yourself was easier than facing what the owning was for.

And now Corvel was gone, hauled downriver by his own office's sins, and the debt —

Casper sat with the ledger on his knees for a long time. The rain ticked on the awning. Moths orbited the lamp.

He had thought, all this way up the river, that the end of it would be him paying. That was the shape his whole life had given the future: someday the bill would come due and he would settle it, buy his freedom outright, and keep moving, free and rootless and alone, which he had confused for the same thing. He had planned to pay Corvel off with the case, or with silence, or with his own surrender if it came to that. He had done the math a hundred times in a hundred anchorages.

But the family had paid it. Ada, with her ink comparisons and her terrible careful reading. Marisol, whose dispossession had given the case its weight. Sefa and Lio, who had winched the Kestrel through Widow's Gorge and stopped calling her *the boat*. Bao, who had betrayed them and been forgiven and whose sister walked free tonight because Ada had aimed the inquiry like an instrument. Even Voss, pulled half-drowned from the shoal, sitting sullen and unarmed in the village guest hut, had contributed her wreck-full of forged papers to the pile that buried Corvel.

Not one coin of it had come from Casper's ledger. The debt that had chained him to the river's length for twenty years had been discharged by other people — by the people he had refused, for twenty years, to call what they were.

He laughed. It came out rough, half a cough, startling a night bird off the roof beam. Twenty years of arithmetic and the sum had been carried by someone else entirely.

He struck a match and held it to the corner of the ledger page — Corvel's page, the oldest one — and watched the flame take the entry, the numbers curling and going black, the ink that had owned him burning down to ash on the wet air. Then he closed the book. He didn't burn the rest. Some debts were worth keeping — the kind you owed forward, to villages and sisters and a woman mapping a valley in the rain. Those he intended to pay slowly, for the rest of a life that suddenly had a location.

From the shore, a lantern bobbed along the bank path toward the jetty. Small, steady, familiar. Ada's walk — he knew it now, the way he knew sandbars, by their particular mood. She stopped at the head of the jetty and raised the light, and he saw her face tilt up toward the Kestrel, questioning.

"Casper," she called through the soft rain. "You'd better come look at this. The clerk brought mail. There's a letter — it's addressed to the crew of the Kestrel." A pause, and he could hear the frown in her voice. "All of us. By name. Someone in the capital knows exactly who we are."

Casper set the ledger down, picked up the lamp, and went to meet her — walking, for the first time in twenty years, toward his name instead of away from it.
