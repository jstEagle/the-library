## 22. A Provision for the Monsoon

The first sign was the smell.

Casper stood on the village jetty at dawn with his hands wrapped around a cup of Marisol's tea, and the wind coming down the valley carried something it hadn't carried in a year — wet stone, wet dust, the green animal breath of rain still hours away but already thinking about them. The monsoon had a smell the way people had faces. You could forget it over a dry season. You could not mistake it once you'd known it.

Behind him, the Kestrel sat moored at her new berth, and he still caught himself looking at her the way a man looks at something he expects to be gone by morning. A year now. She hadn't gone anywhere. Neither had he.

She wore her year well. Bao had recaulked every seam with pitch from the forest pines and replaced the cracked planking they'd hauled out of Widow's Gorge piece by piece, and the patches showed — pale wood against dark, like scars on brown skin — but she sat high and honest on her lines, no longer a boat running from anything. Her name was painted fresh along the bow in Sefa's careful hand: *Kestrel*, and beneath it, smaller, at Lio's insistence, *of the Valley*. Boats didn't have surnames where Casper came from. This one did now.

He drank his tea and watched the light come up the eastern wall of the range, gilding the goat track Old Yeva's people still kept, and let himself do the thing he had spent twenty years refusing to do: take inventory of what stayed.

The granary first, because everything else depended on it. It stood above the flood line on stone pilings Yeva's nephews had helped raise, and it was full — millet and barley and dried river fish and sacks of salt from the last cargo the Kestrel would ever carry as cargo. Enough for two bad seasons. Three if they were careful. Ada had drawn the granary into her map of the valley with a small square symbol and, beside it, in her precise surveyor's script, a word she'd refused to change when the elders suggested a grander one: *provision*.

The grants were held in the meeting house under glass that Bao had fitted himself, behind a lock that Sefa had picked twice just to prove it could be done, and then relocked, sheepish, while the whole village pretended not to watch. The papers were countersigned, sealed, witnessed, copied — one copy sent downriver to the capital with an escort, one copy buried in a copper tube beneath the jetty's third piling, because Ada trusted paper and Casper trusted mud, and between the two of them nothing was going to take this valley's name off its own land again.

And the crew. He turned the word over the way he'd turned it over all year, testing it for rot, finding none.

Bao had his sister back — she'd come upriver in the spring with the inquiry's final letter, thin and quiet and blinking at the mountains like a woman reading a language she'd been told didn't exist — and the two of them had taken over the engine room together, arguing in their mother tongue about compression ratios while the whole jetty listened through the hull. Lio had stopped flinching at fast water. Mostly. Sefa had started keeping a logbook, which meant someone was writing down what happened to them, which was either the end of Casper's old life or the beginning of the new one; he hadn't decided. Marisol had planted a garden. That was the fact that had undone him most thoroughly, if he was honest, more than the signed papers or the moored boat. Marisol, who cooked for a family that wouldn't call itself one, had put seeds in the ground behind the cookhouse in rows so straight you could sight a gun along them, and things were growing there, and she checked them every morning the way other people check the weather.

Roots, he thought. Twenty years of running from them, and here they were, growing in a kitchen garden, and the world had not ended. The past had come to collect, and the family had paid it down together, and what remained was just — this. Morning. Tea. Rain on its way.

The wind shifted. The smell strengthened.

Casper set down his cup and went to find Sefa.

* * *

They took the skiff out while the water was still low and green-gray, before the river swelled with the first of it. Sefa rowed; Casper sat in the stern and made himself do the hardest thing he had ever done on this river, harder than the gorge, harder than the last rapid with Voss's wreck burning behind him.

He said nothing. He let the sandbars speak first.

Sefa knew the drill by now. They'd been doing these runs since the ice came off the upper water — out at dawn, no engine, oars only, reading the channel the slow way. But today was different and they both felt it. Today the monsoon was coming, and the river would change its mind completely, and whatever Sefa learned in the next few hours would have to hold through the season of learning nothing safely.

"Talk to me," Casper said finally, when the first bar showed its back downstream of the meeting-house bend.

Sefa shipped oars and looked. Casper watched his student look — really look, the way he'd been taught, not at the water but at the mood of it. A year ago Sefa had seen a sandbar and thought: obstacle. Now —

"It's lying," Sefa said. "See how the current breaks clean around the upstream nose? Looks like deep water hugging the left. But there's a boil line out there, see it, that seam of rough water — that means the bar runs farther than it shows. There's a tail spit under the surface, reaching for the right bank." A pause. "It wants you to cut the corner early."

"And?"

"And you don't. You stay in the broken water on the left, ugly as it looks, until you're past the boil line. Then you can cross." Sefa frowned at the bar. "It's hungry. Bars are always hungry this time of year. They've been starving since the dry season and the first flood is when they feed — they'll eat a boat that gives them an excuse."

Casper felt something move in his chest that had no convenient name. Pride was too small a word. This was the thing itself, the passing-on, the river going from one pair of hands to another the way it had gone from some drowned pilot to him twenty years ago, on a night he'd never told anyone about except once, in a snow cave, with the storm howling and the truth easier to say in the dark.

"That's it," he said. "That's the whole secret. Everyone thinks the river is water. It isn't. It's appetite. Every bar, every eddy, every reach has a mood, and the moods change with the season, and your job is never to outmuscle any of it. Your job is to know what it wants and decide whether you're willing to give it." He nodded at the bar. "Row us through. Left side. Ugly water."

Sefa rowed them through. The skiff bucked and juddered in the chop and Sefa's face was set and young and absolutely certain, and the current tried twice to shove them onto the hidden spit and twice Sefa read it coming and countered before Casper could open his mouth.

On the far side, in the calm below the bend, Sefa laughed — a short, startled sound, the laugh of someone who has just discovered a muscle they didn't know they had.

"I felt it," Sefa said. "Under the oars. I felt it wanting us."

"You'll feel it every day for the rest of your life," Casper said. "That's how you know it's yours now."

They drifted. Above them the clouds had begun to build along the crest of the range, gray-white towers stacking up with the patience of something that had crossed an ocean to get here. The first gusts came down the valley in cold fingers, touching the water, lifting little cat's-paws of ripple.

"Casper." Sefa wasn't looking at him; Sefa was looking at the sky, and the question came out carefully, the way questions did in this family, each person having learned the others' tender places. "Are you going to teach Lio too? Properly? Because Lio asks me things now. Things I only know because of you. And I keep thinking — " Sefa stopped, started again. "I keep thinking about the logbook. Someone should write down the moods. All of them. Every bar, every season. A chart nobody's ever made, because everyone who knew this water kept it in their head and then kept moving."

Casper looked at the river for a long time.

There had been a version of him — most of him, for most of his life — for whom that sentence would have been a threat. Write it down. Fix it. Name it. Give the past an address. He had built twenty years on the belief that anything written down became a debt, and every debt became a hook, and every hook eventually found your hull.

But he had watched Ada draw the valley map, every sandbar and spring and goat track rendered in her patient ink, and he had watched the villagers gather around it in the meeting house and point and argue and laugh, and he understood now what he'd refused to understand: a map wasn't a chain. It was a gift. The difference was who it belonged to.

"Yes," he said. "Both of you. We'll survey it proper — soundings at every stage of the water, low season and flood. Ada will want to run the instruments. She'll pretend it's for the villages." He smiled, and it didn't hurt. "It is for the villages. That's the point."

Sefa grinned and dug the oars in, and they turned for home ahead of the weather.

* * *

The monsoon made landfall in the late afternoon, and the whole valley came out to meet it.

That was the difference, Casper thought, standing under the meeting-house eave with Marisol and Ada while the first real rain came down — not a curtain yet, but a drumming, generous, warm rain that turned the dust to gold and raised a smell so rich the children ran out into it screaming with joy. Last year the monsoon had been a race, a terror, an arithmetic of days. This year it was a guest. They had grain stored. They had the grants held. They had roofs mended and channels cut and the Kestrel double-moored with lines Bao had spliced himself, and when the river rose — and it would rise, ten meters, twelve, eating the bars it had starved on all summer, feeding at last — it would find nothing here unprepared.

Marisol had cooked for two days without stopping. The meeting house smelled of roasted fish and pepper stew and the sweet fried dough she only made for occasions, and the villagers ate with the crew and the crew ate with the villagers and somewhere in the last year the distinction had quietly dissolved, so that when the old elder — the one whose debt Casper had paid off in another life, another river, a hundred kilometers and a lifetime downstream — rose to speak, he spoke to all of them as one household.

"The papers held," the elder said simply, in the lull between rain-squalls. "A year, and the syndicate's lawyers came twice, and the capital's clerks came once, and every time they opened their cases, our case was older and truer and countersigned. The land is ours. The water is ours." He looked around the room, and his eyes found Casper, and Casper made himself hold the look instead of studying the floor. "And we owe this to people who arrived on a patched boat and could have passed us by. In the old stories, debt is a burden. But there is another kind. The kind you carry gladly. The kind you feed."

Marisol, beside him, made a small sound and pressed her sleeve to her eyes, and Casper put his hand briefly on her shoulder, and she leaned into it, and neither of them said anything, because they didn't need to. That was the discovery of his fifty-third year: that silence shared was not the same as silence kept.

Later, when the rain had settled into its long steady voice and the lamps were lit against the early dark, Ada found him on the jetty.

She came out with an oilskin over her shoulders and her hair plastered flat, and she stood beside him at the rail looking at the Kestrel riding her moorings, deck awash with rainwater, lamp glowing yellow in the wheelhouse window where Bao's sister was cataloguing spare parts by lamplight because that was what she did on nights she couldn't sleep yet, in a place that was still becoming hers.

"She'll float through it," Ada said. "I checked the lines this morning. Twice."

"I know. I checked them three times."

Ada laughed softly. For a while they just listened to the rain on the river, a sound like the whole world whispering at once.

"I got a letter," she said eventually. "From the capital. They're offering me the surveyor-generalship. The whole empire's mapping authority. Every coast, every range, every name on every map, in my hand." She turned the words over like stones. "It's the offer Voss made me on the ridge, except official, and with a pension."

"What did you tell them?"

"I told them the position was filled." She smiled. "I'm mapping the valley. Low water next month, flood stage after that, then the snow line in autumn. Four seasons, minimum. Probably the rest of my life, if the work keeps being this good." She glanced at him sideways, rain dripping off her chin. "You could help. Someone has to show me where the bars lie. Apparently they have moods."

"Who told you that?"

"A very unreliable captain. Never stays anywhere longer than the water level." She bumped her shoulder against his. "I hear he's changed."

Casper looked out at the dark river, rising already, an inch, two inches, the bars beginning their long feeding. He thought about the letter he had never received because no one alive knew where to send it — the brother, the floods, the twenty years of paying off strangers' debts in the dark and leaving before dawn. He had believed, all that time, that stopping was the cost. That staying was what you paid, and moving was what you bought yourself with the payment. He had built his whole life on that ledger, and the ledger had been wrong, backwards, upside down, and it had taken a sealed case and a gorge and a flour barrel and a snow cave and a woman who mapped things for a living to show him the true entry.

Stopping was never the cost.

He watched the rain stitch silver threads across the black water, and the Kestrel sway gently on her lines, and the lamp in her window burn steady against the dark, and he finished the thought the way he'd finish a channel through a bar he'd read a thousand times — with certainty, with relief, with the particular peace of a pilot who has finally brought his boat all the way home:

It was the provision.
