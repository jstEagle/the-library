## 1. The Man Who Stole Nothing

The clerk's office smelled of ink and old paper and the particular desperation of a man who had already rehearsed his story too many times. Petra Oyelaran stood in the doorway a moment longer than courtesy required, letting the heat of the street drain off him, taking inventory the way he always did: two exits, one window barred, a filing cabinet heavy enough to matter, and behind the desk a frontier functionary with sweat at his temples and a ledger open in front of him like a shield.

"You're the tracker." The clerk said it as if confirming a rumor he hoped was false.

"Depends who's asking."

"Corvane. Deputy registrar, Saltmere district." The man gestured at the chair across from his desk with the stiff precision of someone who had never once sat down without permission. "Sit, please."

Petra didn't sit. He'd learned years ago that sitting made you a client, and clients answered questions. He leaned against the doorframe instead, arms crossed, hat pushed back just enough to show a face that had spent nine years learning how to be unremarkable — brown skin gone darker under the sun, a beard trimmed to nothing distinctive, eyes that gave away exactly as much as a closed door.

"The notice said a map was stolen," Corvane said. "From the survey archive. I need it recovered."

"Notice said more than that. Said nothing else was taken."

"Nothing else *was* taken." Corvane's voice climbed half a tone, then caught itself. He smoothed the front of his coat, a nervous habit Petra had catalogued within the first ten seconds. "That's the strange part. That's why I need someone like you. A thief breaks into a locked archive, past a night watchman who swears he saw nothing, and takes one map. One. Leaves the strongbox. Leaves the silver. Leaves everything else in the room worth carrying."

"Maybe he couldn't open the box."

"He could have opened it with a boot heel. The lock was older than I am." Corvane turned the ledger around so Petra could see an entry written in a careful administrative hand: *Survey chart, regional, Kessarine Reach — missing.* "It's not even a valuable map. It's forty years old. Half the rivers on it have moved. Any cartographer in town would sell you a better one for three coppers."

Petra looked at the entry for a long moment. Not at the words about the theft. At the name.

*Kessarine Reach.*

He kept his face still. He was good at that. Nine years of practice at being a man to whom nothing meant anything.

"So you want me to find a thief who stole something worthless," Petra said, "and left the money."

"I want you to find my map."

"Your map." Petra let that sit. "Funny thing about a worthless map, deputy registrar. Nobody steals it by accident. And nobody leaves coin behind unless they're trying to tell you something." He straightened off the doorframe. "What's the pay?"

Corvane named a figure. It was decent. It was also, Petra noted, roughly what a man would offer when he wanted the job done quietly and hadn't thought through what quiet cost.

"And who else knows it's gone?"

"No one. I reported it as a routine loss pending review. If my superiors learn a restricted survey document walked out of a locked room—"

"They'll ask why a restricted document was in a frontier archive at all." Petra watched the man's face do the arithmetic of his own career. "That's the real theft, isn't it? Not the map. Your standing."

Corvane's jaw tightened. For a moment the rehearsed story fell away and something rawer showed underneath — fear, yes, but also a kind of baffled honesty. "I don't know what the map is," he admitted. "I inherited this office eight months ago. The thing was filed under a district code that doesn't exist anymore. I only found out it was missing because the watchman's log had a gap in it and I went looking for the gap."

"A gap."

"One night. No entry. The watchman swears he wrote it up and lost the page." Corvane rubbed his eyes. "Everyone in this building has suddenly become very bad at their jobs, Mr. Oyelaran, and none of them can tell me why."

There it was. His name, spoken plainly, in a government office, on a warrant-warm morning. Petra felt the old itch between his shoulder blades, the one that said *door, now, while the exits are still yours.* But the pay was real, and refusing a deputy registrar without cause was its own kind of signature. Men who refused small jobs got remembered for refusing them.

"Terms," Petra said. "I work alone. I report to you and no one else. And when I find your thief, I decide whether he comes back breathing or in a sack. You get the map either way."

"That's—" Corvane started, and stopped, because arguing with a desert ranger about methods was a conversation that happened entirely on paper after the fact. "Agreed."

"Half now."

The coins crossed the desk. Petra counted them without apology, weighed the purse in his palm, and asked the last question he intended to ask in that room.

"The watchman. The one who saw nothing. How long has he worked here?"

"Eleven years. Why?"

"Because a man doesn't lose a page of his own log after eleven years." Petra settled his hat. "He sold it. Or he was paid to sleep. Either way, your thief didn't break into your archive, deputy. Someone opened the door for him. Find out who benefits from a forty-year-old map walking out of a government building, and you'll know more than my tracking will ever teach you."

He was at the door when Corvane spoke again, quieter now, the rehearsal finally abandoned.

"There's one other thing. The map — the region it covers. There's no record of any survey ever being conducted there. No expedition logs, no requisitions, nothing. It's as if the place was mapped once and then every trace of the mapping was... filed away. Like someone wanted it forgotten."

Petra's hand rested on the door latch. Outside, Saltmere was waking — cart wheels, a water-seller's cry, the ordinary noise of a town that had never heard of Kessarine and would have sworn, if asked, that no such nation had ever existed.

His mother had told him otherwise. Once. In a voice low enough that he'd spent nine years pretending he'd imagined it.

"I'll send word when I have something," he said, and stepped out into the light.

* * *

The trail began badly, which meant it began honestly.

The watchman, a soft-bellied man named Dulk, told Petra everything inside of twenty minutes, most of it unprompted, all of it useless except for one detail: a stranger had bought him dinner twice in the month before the theft. A traveler. Quiet. Interested in old records, old roads, old names.

"Old names," Petra repeated.

"That's what I said." Dulk wiped his palms on his apron. "Asked me did the archive hold anything from before the Reorganization. I told him we don't keep anything from before the Reorganization. Officially." He grinned, pleased with his own discretion, unaware he'd just confessed to selling it. "Then he asked about the Reach. The Kessarine Reach. I told him there's no such place."

"What did he say to that?"

Dulk's grin faded into something almost wistful. "He said, 'That's what worries me.'" 

The traveler had paid in fresh-minted coin, Dulk remembered, and had hands that were ink-stained at the edges — a clerk's hands, or a scribe's, or a scholar's. He'd headed east when he left town. East, where the hardpan started and the wells thinned out and a man without a guide became a story people told later.

Petra rode out the same afternoon.

Three days of hardpan taught him what the client's description had hidden: the thief was good. Not soldier-good, not bandit-careless, but *considered*-good, the kind of tracker's opposite — a man who moved like someone who had studied trackers. He camped late and broke early. He watered at caches Petra himself had stocked in another life and resealed them neatly, leaving each one a measure fuller than he'd found it. He never doubled back, but he slowed at every rise long enough to be seen by anyone patient enough to look.

By the second day Petra understood he wasn't chasing a man. He was being *escorted*. The trail was legible on purpose, a sentence written in boot-prints and cold campsites, and the sentence read: *come slowly, come alone, come ready to believe something strange.*

On the third evening, at the edge of the flood country where the hardpan surrendered to river silt, the trail stopped being careful altogether. It ran straight for the water, stumbling, the stride shortened, drag marks at the left heel. Sickness, or injury, or both. Petra followed it down through the tamarisks as the light went copper, and came out onto a bend of the river that the spring floods had recently rearranged — new banks, new gravel bars, whole trees stood on their heads in the mud.

The thief lay at the waterline, on his back, one arm flung toward the river as if he'd been reaching for something when it took him. Dead some days. Drowned, by the look of him — no wounds, no violence, just a man whose body had finally failed faster than his purpose. His satchel had washed half-buried in the silt nearby, straps torn, contents scattered.

And beside him, half-exposed by the flood that had rearranged the bank, wedged in the gravel like something the river had been holding for a very long time and had finally decided to give back:

A chest. Bronze-bound, iron-hasped, sealed with pitch that had cracked along one seam. Small enough for two men to carry. Heavy enough, by the way it sat in the earth, to have been buried rather than lost.

On its lid, stamped deep into the bronze, was a crest: a bridge over water, beneath a crown with no king's name.

Petra knew it. That was the terrible part. Before he touched it, before he'd even finished crossing the gravel, he knew it — his mother's voice, low, in a kitchen that no longer existed anywhere but in him: *the bridge and the empty crown, little one. If you ever see it, walk away. Walk away and don't let them see you know.*

He stood in the copper light with a dead man at his feet and a drowned nation's seal under his hand, and understood, with the flat certainty of a man reading his own death warrant, that the thief had not stolen a map at all.

He had delivered one. And the delivery address was Petra Oyelaran — because somewhere in a forty-year-old survey chart, in a ledger in a locked archive, in whatever waited inside that pitch-sealed chest, his name was already written.

The river moved against the gravel. Somewhere upstream, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of many horses.
