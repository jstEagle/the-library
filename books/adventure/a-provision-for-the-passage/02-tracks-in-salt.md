## 2. Tracks in Salt

The hardpan began where the clerk's money ended, three days out of the river town, and Petra had learned long ago that the two things usually ran out together.

He made his camp that first night in the lee of a wind-carved shelf, ate cold salt-pork and hard bread, and studied the ground he'd covered since dawn. The thief's trail was there if you knew how to look — a scuffed stone set back in place a half-turn wrong, a boot heel pressed deeper on the left than the right. A man favoring one leg? No. A man carrying weight on one shoulder, shifting it as he walked.

Petra turned a pebble between his fingers and thought about what the clerk had told him. *Nothing else was taken. Just the map.* And the coin left behind, arranged neat as an apology on the desk's corner.

Nobody steals nothing. Everybody steals something — coin, pride, time, a name. A man who takes only a map and leaves payment is either a fool or a man making a point, and fools don't cross hardpan with their weight balanced like a mason laying brick.

He slept with his boots on.

* * *

The second day taught him the shape of the man he was following.

Water caches came first. The frontier kept them the way old houses keep mice — unofficially, everywhere, if you knew the walls. A tin box wired under a rock overhang near the Split Fang formation. A sealed jug buried at the base of a dead acacia, its position marked by a cairn of exactly six stones, no more. Petra checked each one and found them untouched, which told him the thief knew where they were without needing to search. A local, then. Or someone who'd done this route before, recently enough to remember.

Then the misdirection started, and this was what made Petra sit down on a hot stone and laugh, once, without much humor in it.

At midday the trail bent south toward the Ashen Sinks — bad country, soft ground, a place where a man could vanish into cracked mud that swallowed boots to the knee. Any tracker following would assume panic, or ignorance, or both. Petra walked the false trail for a quarter mile, found where it doubled back along a dry wash, and followed *that* instead, because the wash's gravel held prints the way a ledger holds debts: precisely, and against you.

The doubling-back was clean. No hurry in it. The thief had laid the southern trail deliberately, spending maybe forty minutes he could have saved, to put a quarter mile of garbage between himself and anyone reading his sign.

*He wants to be followed,* Petra thought. *But slowly.*

That was the part that sat wrong in his stomach through the afternoon heat. A man running wants distance. A man hiding wants silence. This man wanted a shadow at a respectful interval — close enough to arrive eventually, far enough to arrive alone, strung out and tired and grateful for water caches he'd been allowed to find.

You didn't build a trail like that for yourself. You built it for whoever was coming after you. Which meant the thief knew who that would be, or knew what kind of person the client would hire, or — and here Petra's jaw tightened — knew that the client would hire *him specifically*, and had planned around Petra Oyelaran the way a carpenter plans around the grain of wood.

He drank from his skin, measured what remained, and told himself he was being paranoid. Nine years of paranoia had kept him breathing; it had also made every rock look like a message. He kept walking.

* * *

The third day, the trail got honest, and honesty frightened him more than the tricks had.

The misdirection stopped. The walking straightened out, mile after mile, bearing true northwest toward the river country — toward the flooded bends the seasonal rains had been talking about all spring, the lowlands where water did what it wanted and men built nothing they couldn't afford to lose. The thief had stopped performing. Whatever he'd been keeping his audience for, the theater was over.

Petra found the first real mistake of the journey at noon: a boot print in clay pan beside a seep, deep and unguarded, the print of a man who'd knelt there drinking a long time. Beside it, pressed into the clay edge, the clear outline of a hand — not bracing, not climbing. Resting. A tired hand.

And under a flat stone near the seep, placed where only someone following closely would ever see it, a strip of cloth. White, sun-bleached, torn from something larger. Petra picked it up and turned it over twice before understanding arrived like heatstroke: it was a marker. Not of direction. Of *pace*. The kind of thing a guide leaves for a party moving slower than planned.

A guide.

Petra stood very still in the noon glare while lizards ticked away across the clay and the wind said nothing useful. He thought about the coin left on the desk. He thought about the map — a map of what? The clerk hadn't known, or hadn't said, and Petra hadn't pushed, because pushing meant questions and questions meant names. He thought about a trail engineered to bring one specific tracker, alone and exhausted, to a specific place at a specific pace.

*Someone's instrument,* he thought. *And I'm the other one.*

The suspicion curdled as the sun went down. Because here was the arithmetic he couldn't escape: a thief smart enough to lay false trails across hardpan doesn't need to be caught. A thief who wants to be caught wants something from the catching — a witness, a courier, a body standing in a particular place when something happens. And the only person in this whole dry half of the world with reason to want Petra Oyelaran walking a straight line toward a river nobody visited was whoever had whispered the job to the frontier clerk in the first place.

He built a small fire anyway, because the nights were cold and because a man who stopped lighting fires was already halfway to being prey. He sat with his back to a boulder, rifle across his knees, and watched the coals breathe.

His mother's voice came back to him, the way it did out here where there was nothing else to listen to. *Names are debts, Petra. Every one somebody else gave you is a debt you'll pay.* She'd been talking about christenings and family obligation, mostly. She'd also been talking about warrants, though neither of them had said so yet, that last year.

He banked the fire and slept in shifts, waking at each hour to check the dark, and the dark offered him nothing but stars and the enormous patience of the desert.

* * *

On the morning of the fourth day, the land began to fall toward water, and the trail became a confession.

Green showed first in the washes — tamarisk, cane, the bright rude grass that follows floods. The air thickened. Birds he hadn't seen in days moved overhead in noisy argument, heading the same direction he was, and Petra took a grim comfort in that: even the wildlife was being herded somewhere.

The thief's prints were fresh now. Hours old, then less. At a bend in a dry channel Petra crouched over sign so new the edges of the prints still stood crisp in the damp clay, and felt the peculiar quickening he always hated in himself — the hunter's pleasure, rising up in a man who had sworn off hunting anything but his own survival.

Toward evening he smelled it: water, green rot, wet stone. The river. Not the polite seasonal stream the maps promised but the swollen brown animal the rains had made of it, running high and loud through country it had no business owning.

He came down through the tamarisk with the light failing, rifle up, every sense open, and stepped onto the floodplain where the river had spent the spring rewriting its own borders.

The thief was there.

He lay on his back at the water's edge, half in the shallows, arms flung wide in an attitude that might have been surrender or might have been the last thing he'd managed. Dead. Petra circled wide, came in downwind, checked the man's hands and throat and eyes before he let himself believe any of it. No wounds. No rope marks. Lips faintly blue, skin slipped loose at the jaw. The man had drowned — drowned in six inches of moving water, which meant fever, or heart, or a leg gone cramping in the current, or simply a body that had decided, three hundred miles from anywhere, that it was finished.

In his stiffened fingers, clutched like a sacrament, was a scrap of oilcloth.

Petra pried it loose gently, the way you handle a dead man's things, and unfolded it on his knee.

It wasn't the stolen map. It was a rubbing — charcoal on cloth, taken from metal — of a crest: a tower above crossed keys, ringed by lettering in an alphabet Petra had never seen and yet recognized instantly, the way you recognize a face from a story heard before you could read.

Kessarine.

The word arrived in his mother's voice. *The nation that isn't anywhere anymore, little one. Erased so cleanly the maps apologized for it.* He'd been six. He'd thought she meant a fable, the way she'd meant fables by most of what she said. He had spent nine years making sure no record anywhere connected him to her, and now her bedtime stories were stamped in bronze on a chest sitting ten feet from a drowned man's hand.

Because there was a chest. He'd been so fixed on the body he'd almost missed it, and that failure scared him worse than the corpse did. It sat on the floodplain where the water had lately receded, mud-stained to its lid, bronze-bound, the size of a coffin built for a child. The river had exhumed it — recent scour, fresh silt lines, the earth around it still dark with seepage. The seal on its face matched the rubbing in the dead man's fingers exactly.

Tower and keys. Kessarine.

Petra stood in the failing light with a dead stranger at his feet and his mother's oldest story come true in bronze and mud, and understood, finally and completely, that he had been walked here. Laid trail by laid trail, cache by cache, at exactly the pace a dying man could manage, to arrive alone at this riverbend at roughly this hour. The thief hadn't stolen a map. He'd written an invitation, and then died before the guest of honor could RSVP.

Which left the question Petra's paranoia had been circling for four days, now wearing teeth:

If the thief was someone's instrument — whose? And what, exactly, had he been steered here to find?

He was still holding the rubbing when he heard them: hooves, many, still distant but closing, coming down the river road with the unhurried confidence of people who already knew what waited at the end of it.

Petra looked at the chest. He looked at the dead man. He looked at the darkening water, and made the only decision available to a man who couldn't afford to be found standing next to a national treasure and a corpse.

He picked up the rubbing, stepped backward into the tamarisk, and vanished — four days too late to stop being followed, and exactly on time to watch whoever came next take the bait meant for him.
