## 3. What the River Gave Back

The river announced itself a full hour before Petra saw it.

First the ground changed underfoot — hardpan giving way to cracked clay, then clay to silt that held his bootprints like wet paper. Then the smell: green rot and mineral water, the particular stink of a desert river doing something it wasn't supposed to do. By the time he crested the last low ridge of wind-carved stone, he could hear it — not the polite trickle of a seasonal wash but a broad, muscular sound, water moving with intent.

The riverbend had flooded. That much was obvious from two miles out, from the line of debris strung through the scrub like laundry on a wire: reed mats, a split door, half a coracle, the swollen carcass of a goat with its legs pointing at the sky. The bend's outer bank had failed somewhere upstream, and the river had taken a bite out of the land and then, in the way of rivers, changed its mind and left the wreckage behind.

Petra crouched on the ridge and studied it for a long time before descending.

Three days of tracking had taught him things about the thief that the frontier clerk's description hadn't come close to covering. The clerk had called him *a man, middle-aged, unremarkable, probably alone*. What the trail said was: a man who walked twelve hours a day without complaint, cached water at intervals only someone raised in dry country would think of, and misdirected with the patience of a craftsman. False trails branching off toward a dead well. A second set of boot prints, faked by walking backward in his own track for a quarter mile. A cache buried so precisely at the depth a careless tracker would dig that Petra had nearly missed the real one, tucked twice as deep beneath a stone that had been placed there years ago and never moved.

None of it was flight. Flight is fast and sloppy and gets worse as fear compounds. This was an invitation, written slowly, in a hand the thief wanted read.

*He wants to be followed,* Petra had written in his head on the second night, lying under stars so thick they looked like spilled salt. *But slowly. He wants me to arrive when he wants me to arrive.*

Which meant either arrogance or a plan, and the trail had shown no arrogance anywhere. So: a plan.

Now, looking down at the drowned riverbend, Petra understood that the plan had not survived contact with the water.

* * *

He went down along the ridge's spine, keeping to rock where he could, and reached the debris line as the sun cleared the eastern mesa and turned the floodplain into a sheet of hammered copper. Mud everywhere, already skinning over in the heat, printed with the tracks of birds and one very large lizard and nothing else. No human sign since the water dropped. Whatever had happened here had happened days ago.

He found the camp first, or what remained of it. A hollow behind a stand of tamarisk, fire ring swept clean, bedroll gone — carried off or eaten by the river, impossible to say. But the thief had been meticulous even here. In the ash of the fire ring, weighted under a flat stone, Petra found three coins.

Frontier scrip, worn smooth, worth maybe a week's rations. Deliberately left. Not lost — lost coin doesn't arrange itself face-up in a triangle.

The same signature as the theft itself. The clerk had been very clear, almost proud of the strangeness of it: the map case opened, the map gone, and the strongbox untouched. Coin left on the desk. Enough to cover the map's assessed value, plus ten percent. *Nothing else taken,* the clerk kept saying, as though repetition could make sense of it. *Nothing else taken.*

A thief who pays. A fugitive who leaves a trail. A careful man whose care finally ran out at a flooded river.

Petra pocketed the coins because leaving money in the open desert was how you got someone killed over it later, and told himself he'd settle the account if there was anyone left to settle it with. Then he followed the bank downstream, reading the mud.

The story wrote itself quickly, the way drownings do. Boot prints approaching the water's edge at what must have been dusk — the light angle was preserved in the slant of the prints, weight forward, cautious. Then a gap where the mud had slumped. Then, forty yards on, churned ground where something had been dragged out of the shallows, and beside the drag marks, a set of knee prints, deep, a body kneeling and working at something heavy.

And past that, the chest.

* * *

It sat above the new high-water line, wedged against a fallen tamarisk that had caught it like a net. Bronze-bound cedar, the wood gone black with age, the metal crusted green except where the flood had scoured it bright. It was the size of a grain chest, big enough that one man could not have carried it far, which explained the knee prints and the drag marks and, probably, everything else. The thief had found it exposed by the flood — the river had cut the bank away and given up whatever it had been holding — and had spent his last strength trying to move it.

He'd gotten it maybe thirty feet. Then the river, or his heart, had finished the argument.

He lay six feet from the chest, on his back, arms at his sides, arranged — no. Not arranged. Settled. The water had put him there and left him, facedown first, and someone — no one; the mud showed no other prints — the receding water had rolled him as it withdrew. His mouth was full of gray silt. His eyes were open and had been claimed by ants, and Petra made himself look anyway, because you owed the dead that much: a witness who didn't flinch.

No wounds. No blood in the mud beyond what the river itself leaves. His hands were torn and abraded, the nails split — the hands of a man who had fought a bronze-bound chest across thirty feet of wet clay until his body called the debt due. Drowned, or drowned-adjacent; a heart stopping in cold water after a day of hauling looks exactly like drowning when the water does the final paperwork.

Petra knelt beside him and went through the pockets with the impersonal thoroughness the work required. No papers. No coin. A flint and steel, worn concave from decades of use. A wooden whistle on a leather cord, plain, the kind a shepherd gives a child. And sewn into the lining of his coat, a strip of oilcloth wrapped around something stiff.

He unwrapped it on his knee.

A page. Old paper, ragged at the edges, dense with handwriting in faded brown ink — not ink at all, he realized, but iron-gall gone the color of dried blood. The script was tight, disciplined, the hand of someone trained to record rather than express. He couldn't read the language. But at the bottom of the page, beneath the last line of text, was a name written larger, in a different hand, and under the name a word repeated twice, and Petra felt the back of his neck tighten before he knew why.

He folded the page away inside his shirt. Evidence. Or inheritance. He wouldn't know which for a long time.

Then he stood, and turned, and looked properly at the chest for the first time.

* * *

The seal was stamped into the bronze on the lid's face, pressed deep enough to survive centuries of burial and a flood besides. A circle. Inside the circle, a tree whose roots and branches were the same shape — a tree folded back on itself, crown becoming root, growing downward into its own reflection. Around the rim, characters in that same unreadable script, small and even as stitching.

Petra knew it.

That was the problem. There was no reason on this earth he should know it, and he knew it the way you know a scar — instantly, intimately, with the memory of how it got there attached.

His mother's kitchen. He was seven, maybe eight, the age when children are permitted to ask dangerous questions because no one believes the answers matter. She was grinding grain, and he had asked her about her mother's people, about where she'd learned the lullaby she sometimes sang that none of the neighbor women recognized, and she had stopped grinding. Just stopped, the pestle still in her hand, and looked at the window instead of at him.

*There was a country once,* she said, in the voice she used for stories that weren't stories. *Before the maps. They drew a tree that grew both ways, root into branch, branch into root, because they believed a people who remembered correctly could live in either direction — forward or back — and never be lost.* She laughed, short and without any humor in it. *They were wrong. You can be lost in your own name. Ask me how I know.*

He had asked. She had said, *Someday I'll tell you what happened to Kessarine, and you'll wish I hadn't,* and she had gone back to grinding, and she had never told him, and four years later she was dead, and the question had gone into the ground with her like a seed nobody watered.

Until now. Until a river in the middle of nowhere coughed up a box with her forbidden country's seal on it, guarded by a dead man who paid for what he took and carried pages he couldn't have written in a language Petra couldn't read.

He regretted knowing it immediately and completely. Knowing it meant the chest wasn't salvage. Knowing it meant the thief hadn't stolen a map — he'd *bought* one, overpaid, and walked into the desert to find this, which meant the map was drawn by someone who knew where the river would bite, which meant—

Which meant the theft was a summons. And Petra had just answered it.

* * *

He did the professional things first, because the professional things were load-bearing and if he stopped doing them he'd have to think. He circled the site twice, wide, confirming what the mud already said: no one else had been here since the water dropped. He noted the chest's position against landmarks for the report he would or wouldn't file. He checked the ground between the chest and the old bank line, where the flood had cut deep, and saw the dark seam of disturbed earth where the box had lain buried — shallow, deliberately shallow, buried by hands rather than by time. Someone had hidden this chest to be found. Just not by whoever found it.

The sun climbed. The mud began to steam. Petra sat on the fallen tamarisk, ate a strip of dried meat he didn't taste, and let himself think.

Options. He could walk away. Take the coins, take the page, mark the location loosely in his head, and report to the clerk that the thief was dead and the map unrecoverable — technically true, usefully incomplete. The clerk would pay half, complain, and forget him. That was the smart play. That was nine years of instinct talking: touch nothing that has a history, leave no trace that has a future.

But the page against his sternum had a weight that had nothing to do with paper. And the chest had a lock — a bronze plate mechanism, corroded shut, but intact — and inside it was presumably the reason a careful man had killed himself moving it thirty feet. And somewhere behind all of it was a map that had been purchased honestly by a man who wanted to be followed, and Petra kept circling back to the same ugly little thought:

*Followed by whom?*

Not the clerk. The clerk was a functionary who counted stationery. Not garrison men — their trackers were competent and loud, and this trail had been neither. Someone had paid for a map, and the thief had chosen to leave a trail readable by exactly one kind of person: a desert ranger good enough to read it and desperate enough to take the job. Petra had spent nine years making sure no one fit that description better than he did.

He'd been selected. The thought sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone.

And then, faint but unmistakable, from the direction of the ridge he'd descended that morning: the sound of hooves. Many. Moving at the deliberate trot of people who are not hurrying because they believe they have already arrived.

Petra was moving before the thought finished, dropping the tamarisk between himself and the ridgeline, pulling his travel-stained burnous close, scanning for cover on a floodplain that offered exactly none. The chest was too heavy to hide and too distinctive to bury quickly. The dead man had no cover at all and no need of it.

The hoofbeats crested the ridge and resolved into riders — eight, maybe ten, armed, flying no colors he could see at this distance, spreading out along the rim with the drilled ease of soldiers or soldiers' cousins. And at their center, a figure in traveling clothes too fine for the terrain, raising a hand, and the column halting as one animal.

They'd seen the chest. From the ridge, in this light, the bronze would catch the sun like a signal fire.

Petra pressed himself flat against the tamarisk's trunk and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding for three days. Whoever these people were, they had come fast and come equipped, which meant the summons had more than one addressee. The thief had mailed a letter and the whole world was answering it.

The lead rider started down the slope toward the river, and Petra, with a dead stranger's page against his heart and his mother's forbidden word sitting in his mouth like a coal, prepared to learn whether he'd been summoned as a guide, a suspect, or a witness.

The rider was a woman. And she was riding straight for the chest.
