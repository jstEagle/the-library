## 4. The Magistrate's Daughter

Ilse Varn had learned to lie before she learned to read, and she had learned to read at four.

The trick, her father taught her, was not to change your face. Faces changed; that was how liars were caught. The trick was to change the truth's weight — to hold it so lightly in your mouth that it seemed to cost you nothing, because a truth that cost nothing could be anything at all. *You never deny, Ilse. You never explain. You offer the next thing so quickly that the last thing never lands.*

She was thinking of this as the expedition's six mules picked their way down the last rise toward the riverbend, and she saw, for the first time, the man she had crossed four hundred miles of bad country to find.

He was standing over a grave.

That was her first impression, and it was a good one — she would keep it, turn it over later, use it. A man standing over a grave in the middle of nowhere, hat in his hands, was a man who buried strangers properly. Her father would have called that a weakness. Ilse had begun to suspect it was the opposite. It was a man who had decided what he owed, and to whom, and the ledger of it lived somewhere no magistrate could reach.

"Rider coming up the wash," said Brakk, her captain, reining in beside her mule. He said it the way he said everything, as though the information were mildly disappointing to him personally. "One man. The tracker, if the surveyor's map holds."

"It holds," Ilse said. "It cost enough to hold."

The surveyor's map had cost her father eleven days and a favor she did not want to think about, called in from a clerk in a river town whose name would never appear in any record Ilse controlled. That was the part of the expedition her father had arranged, and the part of it he had not explained, and the part of it — she was honest with herself in the privacy of her own skull, nowhere else — that had kept her awake two of the last three nights.

The chest was salvage. The papers were sound. She had drafted them herself, and she did not draft unsound papers.

So why had he said *unopened*?

She put it away. The mules needed her present, and the man below needed her best.

* * *

Petra Oyelaran — though she did not yet know his name, and would not for another day — watched the column descend with the stillness of a man who had spent nine years being watched for and had developed opinions about it.

Ilse read him as she came closer, cataloguing the way her father had taught her to catalogue: boots resoled twice but well, a ranger's duster gone the color of the hardpan, a rifle slung muzzle-down out of courtesy, hands visible. He did not reach for the rifle when the column fanned out — Brakk's men did it automatically, six riders opening like a hand — and that told her more than anything else about him. He had been surrounded before. He knew the choreography. He was not afraid of it, which meant he was afraid of something else.

"You buried him," she said, before anyone else could speak. It was not a greeting. It was a probe, and she watched it land.

"Seemed rude not to," the man said. His voice was dry as the country. "He'd have done the same for me. Probably."

"You knew him?"

"I knew his tracks for three days. That's a kind of knowing, out here." He glanced at the fresh mound of stones at the riverbank's edge, at the chest beside it, bronze-bound, half-shadowed by a tarp the man had evidently rigged to keep the sun off it. He had tarped it. Ilse filed that away too. "You'll be the salvage party. The papers said a week. You're two days early."

"You've read our papers."

"Your clerk's clerk talked in his sleep at the waystation. Also I read them." He produced the folded documents from inside his coat — her documents, her seals, her father's office — and held them out without handing them over, a negotiation in miniature. "Salvage rights, surveyed route, provisioned return. Signed Varn. There's only one Varn that signature means."

"Magistrate Aldous Varn is my father."

"Then you're the daughter." He said it without flattery or contempt, which was rarer than either. "Ilse Varn. The river town gossips got as far as the name before I left."

"And you didn't give them yours."

"No," he agreed pleasantly.

Brakk shifted in his saddle. Ilse raised two fingers without looking — *stand down* — and dismounted, because she had decided in the last hundred yards how this would go, and it would not go from horseback. You negotiated eye-level or you negotiated from above, and this man was the kind who made above into a liability.

"Ilse Varn," she said, and gave him her hand.

He looked at it for a moment. Then he took it. His grip was brief and dry and entirely unimpressed, and she found, to her private irritation, that she liked him better for it.

* * *

She let him make tea. It was a small surrender that cost nothing and bought the first ten minutes of actual conversation, and while the little spirit stove hissed she walked the bank and looked at what the river had given back.

The flood had done its work with the indifference of all large forces. It had peeled open the bank like a letter, and there the chest had sat for however long — years, decades, she genuinely did not know — until a thief with a stolen map and a talent for misdirection had walked three hundred miles to kneel in front of it. And then the river had taken him. Not the chest. Him.

"Flash flood?" she asked, though she had already read the ground.

"River came up four feet in an hour, three nights back. He was camped too low. By the time he'd have heard it—" The tracker shrugged. "Drowning doesn't leave a mark you'd mistake. I've seen it twice."

"You seem certain it wasn't anything else."

"I'm certain the river doesn't need help." He handed her tea in a tin cup, and she noticed he'd given her the clean one without making a performance of it. "You want to open it."

It was not a question, so she did not treat it as one. "The chest is the property of the Varn salvage charter, ratified by the frontier registry. Its contents are administrative records of a defunct jurisdiction. My father's office has statutory interest in their recovery." She recited it the way she'd drafted it, smooth and weightless, a truth with no cost in her mouth. "There's nothing in it but paper, Mr.—?"

"Petra," he said. Just that. He watched her over the rim of his own cup, and his eyes were the color of the flats at noon, and they did not believe her, and she could not tell what they believed instead.

"Paper," she said. "Ledgers, correspondence, the residue of a nation that stopped existing before my grandfather was born. It has archival value and no more. The registry wants it catalogued and sealed. That's the whole of it."

"That's the whole of it," he repeated, in a tone that agreed with nothing.

*You never deny. You never explain.* She smiled at him, and the smile was her father's smile, and it worked on everyone it had ever been aimed at, and it did not work on him, and something in her — some small, unaccountable thing — sat up straighter at the failure, the way a fencer sits up when she finally meets a blade that answers hers.

"Mr. Petra. I'm going to be direct with you, because I suspect indirection annoys you and because we're short on time. The dry season closes the salt corridor in — what would you say, Brakk?"

"Eighteen days," Brakk said. "Nineteen if the wind's kind. It won't be."

"Eighteen days. After that, the flats seal until next year, and this chest sits on a riverbank for another twelve months, and my charter lapses, and a great deal of money and effort is wasted." She turned back to the tracker. "I need a guide who knows the corridor. The registry's survey lists exactly one name with corridor experience who isn't dead, imprisoned, or a fable. That's you. I'm offering triple your going rate — I know your going rate, I made inquiries — plus a bonus on delivery, plus my father's protection, which in the frontier districts is worth more than the money."

"And in exchange I walk a column of armed men and six mules across the flats, following a chest I found, containing paper of no importance, for a magistrate whose name I only know from warrants." He said it mildly. He said it the way a man reads a menu. "Did I get all of it?"

"You got all of it except the part where you say yes."

He looked at her for a long moment. The river made its small sounds behind him. Somewhere upstream a bird complained about the heat, or the water, or the general state of the world, and Ilse thought, absurdly, of her father's study, the sealed annex in the inventory manifest, the single line in her orders she had read eleven times: *Annex contents to be returned unopened and unexamined. This is not negotiable. — A.V.*

Every other line of her orders came with reasoning attached. Her father reasoned the way other men breathed. This line stood alone, unadorned, like a wall.

"Triple pay," the tracker said. "And I choose the pace, the camps, and the water. And when we reach the river town, you file my fee through the registry clerk, not your father's office."

"The registry clerk is slower."

"The registry clerk doesn't ask questions." He said it lightly, and she heard, underneath the lightness, a man telling her exactly as much of the truth as he wanted her to have, which was that he had a reason, and that the reason was none of her business. She respected it the way she respected good masonry. She also noted it for later.

"Done," she said, and they shook on it, and his hand was steady, and hers was, and both of them were lying about something, and only one of them knew what.

* * *

They struck camp at dusk, because he said the first leg wanted a night start, and Ilse watched her expedition reorganize itself around a stranger in under an hour and understood that she had just hired the most dangerous man in the column, and that everyone except Brakk knew it.

The chest went on the lead mule, tarped and lashed. The grave stayed behind with its stones. Ilse paused beside it on her way to her own bedroll, and found the tracker standing there again, hat in his hands, saying nothing.

"You knew him," she said again. It was not a question this time either.

"I knew what he wanted," he said. "Which out here is the same thing." He settled the hat back on. "Get some sleep, Miss Varn. Tomorrow's the easy day. They're all easy days until one isn't."

She walked back to her tent, and lay down on her cot with her boots on, because she had learned that habit from soldiers and it had saved her exactly once, and she stared at the canvas and did her father's trick in reverse — took the day's truths and weighed them, one by one, to see which ones rang false.

*Administrative records of a defunct jurisdiction.* Her own words, her own draft, and they had rung false in her own mouth, and she knew why. She had read the inventory manifest on the boat upriver. She had read *annex* and she had read *unopened* and she had read *not negotiable*, and her father — who annotated everything, who reasoned compulsively, who had never in her life issued an order he didn't want argued with — had sent this one naked.

And the thief. A thief who stole a map and left the coin behind. The clerk's report had said it twice, in the flat language of clerks: *no monies taken, no goods disturbed, map only.* She had read that report four times on the boat and each time it had sounded less like a crime and more like a message, and she did not yet know to whom it was addressed, but she had a growing, cold suspicion that the addressee was in this tent, and that it was her.

Outside, the tracker's fire burned low and steady. She heard Brakk's men settle into their watch rotation, and the mules shifting, and the river going about its business in the dark, and she thought: *eighteen days.* Eighteen days to cross the flats, eighteen days to deliver a chest full of paper of no importance, eighteen days in the company of a man whose name was not a name, carrying an order from her father that she was beginning to understand she might not be able to obey.

Her father had taught her never to explain. He had never taught her what to do when the person who needed an explanation was her.

She slept, eventually. Badly. In the morning the flats began, white and endless under a sky like a struck bell, and the man called Petra took the lead without waiting to be asked, and the expedition followed him into the salt.
