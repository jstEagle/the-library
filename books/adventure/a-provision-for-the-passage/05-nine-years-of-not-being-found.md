## 5. Nine Years of Not Being Found

He said yes before he understood he was saying it.

That was the trick of it, Petra thought later, standing in the clerk's office with the salvage contract warm in his hand and the ink still wet under his thumb. Refusal was the expensive answer. Refusal meant questions. *Why not? Where are you going? What's your name again?* A man could dodge a hundred questions a year and trip on the hundred-and-first. So when Ilse Varn's factor slid the paper across the desk and asked if he would guide them home through the salt, Petra heard himself say yes in a voice that sounded borrowed.

The factor, a soft man named Drellin with ink-stained cuffs and the eyes of someone who counted other people's money for a living, smiled the way men smile when they believe they've bought something.

"Triple rate," Drellin said. "Paid half in advance. The magistrate's daughter is generous to competence."

"I'm not competent," Petra said. "I'm available. There's a difference."

"There's a difference," Drellin agreed pleasantly, "and I'm paid not to care about it."

Petra signed. Not his real name — never that, not since the fire — but the name he'd worn so long it had grown roots. *Corren Vale.* Nine years old now, that name. Old enough to have calluses of its own.

He walked out into the river town's heat with the advance purse heavy against his ribs and felt nothing like relief. That was the thing nobody told you about running: you didn't get lighter as you went. You got heavier. Every year added weight, and the strange part was that none of it was guilt. Guilt would at least have been simple.

* * *

The garrison at Marrow Ford had burned on the third night of the month of Rains, nine years ago, and Petra Oyelaran had been twenty-two years old and on watch.

He remembered the heat first. Not the flames — the heat, rolling off the records annex like a living thing, pressing his face back even at forty paces. He remembered the way the roof beams sang when they went, a low groaning chord, and how one of the stable boys had come running barefoot and stood beside him with his mouth open, and neither of them had moved to do anything about it for what was probably too long.

He remembered Commander Hale arriving at a gallop, dismounting before the horse had fully stopped, and looking — not at the fire. At him.

"You were on watch," Hale had said. It wasn't an accusation yet. It was arithmetic. Watch, fire, Oyelaran. The sum would come later.

"I was on watch," Petra had agreed, because he was.

And here was the part he had never told anyone, the part that made the warrant both true and false in a way no tribunal had ever been equipped to untangle: he had set the fire. And he had not set the fire.

His mother had died in the spring of that year, slow, in a room that smelled of camphor and boiled willow bark, and near the end she had gripped his wrist with a strength that frightened him and said, *There is a copy of the dispersal order in the garrison annex. Your grandmother's name is in it. Our name. Burn it, Petra. Burn it before they finish what the treaty started.*

She had talked about Kessarine the way other mothers talked about weather — casually, constantly, as though it were simply there. A nation erased from every atlas, she'd say, and he'd thought it was a story, the way children think everything their mothers repeat is either a story or a law. She'd taught him the seal before she'd taught him letters: the coiled river, the three stars above it. *If you ever see this mark,* she said, *you'll know the past has found you.*

So he had gone to the annex on his watch, with a lamp and a plan, meaning to burn one document among ten thousand. And the fire had taken all ten thousand, because fire does not read, and by the time he stood outside watching the roof sing its groaning chord, he genuinely did not know whether he had committed arson or been overtaken by accident. He had carried the lamp in. He had knocked it over reaching for the dispersal order — or he hadn't, or he'd knocked it over on purpose and let the lie of clumsiness grow over the truth like scar tissue. Nine years of telling himself different versions had worn the versions smooth, until what remained was not a memory but a shape where a memory used to be.

Hale had believed the simplest version. Arson, deliberate, treasonous — because why else would a half-blood ranger whose mother came from nowhere, from a country that didn't exist, burn the empire's records?

Petra had run before the inquiry convened. Not because he was guilty. Because he could not prove innocence in a thing he himself could not remember committing, and because staying meant answering questions, and answering questions meant saying his mother's name aloud in front of men who would write it down.

* * *

The cost of stopping, he had learned, was not capture. Capture would almost have been a mercy — a cell, a trial, an ending. No. The cost of stopping was smaller than that and fell on you every day.

It was this: a man who cannot be found cannot be known. He ate alone. He took work through intermediaries who knew him as Corren Vale, tracker, reliable, no history. When a woman in a mining camp south of the flats had looked at him across two seasons of small kindnesses and asked, gently, *Who were you before?*, he had packed his gear at dawn and walked north, and he had hated himself for it with a clean and useless hatred.

Memory compounds. That was the desert's own lesson, written in every dry well. You don't pay the debt of the past all at once; it accrues interest. Every year he ran, the fire grew larger in him, the mother's voice fainter, the boy at Marrow Ford more foreign. He was thirty-one years old and he could no longer recall his mother's face without effort, but he could recall with perfect clarity the exact sound the annex roof had made, and some mornings he woke having dreamed the sound, and lay in the grey light doing the accounting: another year, another charge against an account he could never close.

Refusing Ilse Varn's job would have meant explaining himself. Explaining himself meant choosing which version of the fire to tell, and he did not know anymore which version was true, and a man who lies to himself should not testify.

So: yes. Triple rate. Guide them home. Keep moving, because moving was the only prayer he still knew how to say.

* * *

They made camp that first night at the edge of the floodplain, eleven souls and four wagons and the chest lashed under oilcloth in the second wagon like a coffin riding to burial. Petra took the perimeter walk alone, as was his habit, checking picket lines and water stores, and ended up — not accidentally — at the chest.

Sef was there. The young archivist sat cross-legged beside the oilcloth with a slate on his knees, copying something by lamplight, and he looked up without guilt, which marked him immediately as either honest or very good.

"It's stamped," Sef said. "Under the tarp. I only lifted the corner."

"I know. I found it."

"The seal." Sef hesitated. "Do you know it?"

Petra looked at the corner of exposed bronze, at the coiled river and the three stars pressed into the metal, and felt the old cold move through him, the cold of a story becoming real.

"My mother showed me a mark like that once," he said. "When I was small. She said it belonged to a country that got erased."

"Kessarine," Sef breathed, and then glanced around as though the word might be overheard by the dark itself. "Nobody uses the name. It's not in any atlas in the Varn collection. I've checked."

"Maybe that's the point of erasing something," Petra said. "You check, and you find nothing, and after a while you stop checking."

Sef looked at him for a long moment with those quick archivist's eyes, cataloguing. Petra turned away before the boy could catalogue anything useful. He finished the perimeter, checked the horses twice, and lay down on his bedroll staring up at stars his mother had named for him in a dead language.

Nine years, he thought. Nine years of not being found, and in three days he had touched a stolen map, a drowned thief, and the seal from his mother's stories. The past wasn't chasing him anymore. It had stopped running and started waiting, patient as salt, and it had put itself directly in the path home.

* * *

Near midnight, he heard footsteps and did not reach for his knife, which told him something about how far he'd already slipped. Ilse Varn stood at the edge of the firelight with her arms crossed, watching the chest-wagon rather than the fire.

"You checked the pickets twice," she said. "Drellin says you're thorough. Drellin also says you're evasive, which he seems to consider a professional credential."

"Drellin is observant."

"My father wants the contents inventoried before we cross the salt. Records degrade. He was very specific." She said it smoothly, the way she seemed to say everything, each sentence ironed flat. Then, a fraction off-key: "He was *very* specific."

Petra sat up. In the firelight her face was composed and young and carrying something heavy behind the eyes, the way soldiers look before their first action.

"And you're telling me this because?"

"Because you found the chest. Because you knew the seal." She paused, and he watched her decide to spend a truth like a coin she couldn't afford. "Because my father ordered one part of it returned unopened, and I haven't decided yet whether I'm the kind of person who obeys that order."

The fire cracked. Out on the plain, a jackal gave its one long note and quit.

"Ma'am," Petra said carefully, "I guide people across salt. What's in the box is between you and your father."

"Yes," Ilse Varn said. "That's exactly what worries me."

She left. Petra lay back down and did not sleep, and somewhere in the black hours he admitted the thing he'd been refusing to admit since the riverbend: he had taken this job thinking he was still running. But the warrant, the fire, the nine years of borrowed names — all of it had been leading here, along a trail a dead thief had laid down deliberately, slowly, wanting to be followed, wanting someone to arrive at this chest and this night and this conversation.

The debt had compounded long enough. Tomorrow they marched for the salt corridor, and whatever waited in the sealed annex of that chest, Petra understood with a certainty that felt like falling: it had his family's name in it, and this time he was not going to run from the reading.
