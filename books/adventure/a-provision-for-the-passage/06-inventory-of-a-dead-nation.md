## 6. Inventory of a Dead Nation

The lantern made the tent feel smaller than it was. Ilse had chosen the smallest one on purpose — a clerk's tent, canvas patched at one corner, borrowed from the quartermaster without asking because asking meant explaining, and explaining meant her father's name, and his name was the last thing she wanted spoken aloud tonight.

Outside, the camp settled into its night noises: the picket lines, the guard rotation she'd set herself, the river working at its banks with the patience of something that had already won once and intended to win again. Somewhere out there in the dark, the guide they'd hired was doing whatever desert rangers did instead of sleeping. She'd watched him all evening without meaning to. He moved like a man who had practiced being unremarkable until he'd gotten good at it.

She turned back to the chest.

It sat open on the folding table, bronze bindings green-black with river rot, the wood beneath swollen but sound. Whoever had built it had built it to survive exactly this — decades underground, decades underwater, whatever came. The seal stamped into the lid's center plate she had seen twice now, and both times her stomach had done something complicated and unwelcome. A tower inside a circle of wheat. She knew the shape the way you know a word you've read but never said.

Her father's instructions were folded in her breast pocket, written in his own hand, which he did only for things he never wanted entered into any record:

*Recover the chest intact. Inventory for salvage valuation. The annex returns sealed. Do not open it. Do not let anyone open it. Its contents are not relevant to your commission.*

*Not relevant.* Her father was many things — careful, ambitious, precise as a blade ground by someone who feared dullness more than blood — but he had never once in her twenty-six years used the phrase *not relevant* about anything. He weighed everything. He priced everything. To call a thing irrelevant was to admit fear of it, and Magistrate Aldous Varn did not frighten easily.

So of course she had opened it.

Not yet. Not first. First, the work, because the work would steady her hands and give her an hour to talk herself out of it, or into it, whichever won.

* * *

She began the inventory the way her father had trained her, back when training her had still seemed like an investment rather than a risk. Ink, quill, ledger paper. Columns: item, description, condition, estimated value. She worked by lamplight with the tent flap tied down against the river damp, and for two hours the dead nation of Kessarine passed through her hands one document at a time.

There were more documents than she had expected. That was the first surprise. She had imagined — she admitted this to herself honestly, because dishonesty with herself was the one luxury she refused — she had imagined treasure. Coin, plate, deeds to land that no longer existed on any map she owned. Something a magistrate's office could convert into leverage.

What she found instead was paper. Mountains of it.

Land grants. Water rights. Tax rolls covering three generations. Correspondence between ministries whose names appeared nowhere in the imperial gazetteers — a Bureau of Channels, an Office of Common Stores, something called the Wardenship of the Long Fields. A civilization's entire administrative nervous system, preserved in ink, folded into oilcloth packets labeled in a script she could not read but could recognize as meticulous. Every packet numbered. Every number cross-referenced.

"They weren't conquered," she murmured to the empty tent. "They were *organized*."

That was the second surprise, and it was worse than the first. Empires did not keep records like this. Empires kept records the way a butcher kept ledgers — enough to track the meat, nothing that might indict the knife. But these documents assumed continuity. They assumed the nation reading them would still exist next year, and the year after. There was no crisis in them anywhere. No wartime emergency measures, no final desperate decrees. The last dated document in the top layer was a routine quarterly report on grain storage, signed off with what looked almost like boredom.

A nation does not file its paperwork neatly before it dies. Unless it believed, right up to some specific moment, that it wasn't dying at all.

She wrote *administrative archive, exceptional condition, provenance uncertain* in the value column and then crossed out *uncertain*, because provenance was exactly what she was certain of, and wrote nothing instead.

Under the correspondence, wrapped separately in waxed linen gone stiff with age, she found the ledger.

It was smaller than the rest — a hand-sized book bound in grey leather, the kind of thing a clerk could carry in a coat pocket. The cover bore no title. When she opened it, the first page stopped her breath for a moment, not because of what it said but because of how plainly it said it.

*Register of Dispersal.*

Three words. Then columns. Names — hundreds of names, thousands, the handwriting shifting across years and across different clerks' hands but never losing its terrible neatness. Family names, given names, ages. And a final column headed, in the original hand, with a word she translated twice to be sure: *destination*.

She turned pages. The destinations were towns she knew. River towns, mining camps, frontier garrisons — places within a week's ride of where she sat. Whole families, entered line by line like freight, scattered across the empire's margins so thinly that no two households landed within sight of each other.

She had grown up on the history of the borderlands. She could recite the treaties. The Treaty of Cession, the Border Accords, the Settlement Acts — the legal architecture of how the empire had come to hold everything from the mountains to the salt. Nowhere in any of it was there a nation called Kessarine, and nowhere in any of it was there a word about dispersal. The histories said the region had been *sparsely settled*. The histories said the rivers had been *underutilized*.

The ledger in her hands said otherwise. The ledger said the rivers had been full of people, and the people had been counted, and the counting had been done carefully, and then the people had been sent away.

Her father's voice arrived uninvited, the way it always did when she stood at the edge of something: *Ilse. Read what is useful. File what is not.* He had taught her that at fourteen, over a stack of land dispute petitions, and she had admired it for years — the discipline of it, the refusal to drown in what didn't serve.

She wondered, turning the pages of the dead, when exactly she had stopped admiring it.

* * *

Near midnight, she reached the bottom of the chest.

The annex sat there the way a debt sits at the bottom of a purse — not hidden, exactly, but waiting. It was a separate case, lead-lined, fitted inside the main chest like a heart inside a ribcage. The seal on it was intact. Wax the color of dried blood, pressed with the same tower-and-wheat device, and beneath it a second mark she hadn't seen on the outer lid: a closed hand.

Her father's letter was suddenly very heavy in her pocket. *Do not open it. Do not let anyone open it.*

She sat with her hand flat on the lead casing for a long time. The lantern hissed. Outside, a guard coughed; the river kept talking to itself.

Here was what she knew, laid out with the clarity she prided herself on: her father had known this chest existed before the thief ever stole the map. The map theft had been reported to his office within a day, and his response — funding an expedition, drafting salvage papers, sending his daughter with thirty armed men — had been faster than any bureaucracy she had ever served under. He had known what was in the chest. He had known what was in the annex. And he had wanted the annex returned to him *unopened*, which meant he already knew what it said, which meant the only reason to keep it sealed was that other people must not read it.

Which meant the annex was not treasure. It was evidence.

And she was sitting alone in a canvas tent at the edge of the empire, holding the only copy.

She thought about the guide again — Oyelaran, the ranger, the man who moved like practiced invisibility. She thought about how he had looked at the seal on the chest when they'd arrived at the riverbend. Everyone else had seen salvage. He had seen something else. For half a heartbeat his face had done something complicated and unwelcome, the same thing her own stomach had done, and then it had been gone, smoothed away like writing licked from a slate.

He recognized it too. She was nearly sure of it. And a man who recognized the seal of a nation erased from every atlas was either very old, very well-read, or from somewhere the atlases had also erased.

*Later,* she told herself. *One problem at a time.*

Then she broke the seal on the annex, because she had never in her life been able to leave a later problem alone, and because her father had raised her to believe that information was the only inheritance worth defending, and because — she wrote this in her own head, plainly, refusing to dress it up — she needed to know whether her father was guilty of ambition or of something with a body count.

Inside the lead case: a single bound volume, thinner than the ledger, and a bundle of loose papers tied with wire.

The volume was testimony. She understood that within the first page. It was written in the same meticulous hand as the earliest entries in the dispersal register — a record-keeper's voice, dry and exact, the voice of a man who clearly believed that precision was a form of prayer. He described, in language stripped of all rhetoric, the negotiations that had ended Kessarine. The treaty terms presented to their council. The guarantees. And then, chapter by chapter, the guarantees' dismantling: the grain levies that became famines, the famines that became leverage, the leverage that emptied the towns. He named the imperial officials involved. He quoted their letters. He kept copies.

The manufactured famine was in the fourth chapter. Ilse read the numbers twice, checked them against the tax rolls she'd inventoried hours earlier, and found they matched. The record-keeper had cross-referenced his own testimony against the empire's own paperwork, so that no one could dismiss him as a bitter remnant inventing grievances. He had built his case to survive exactly the skepticism she was bringing to it, and it survived.

On the final page, in a different, shakier hand, a single line had been added long after the main text:

*If this is being read, we failed to stay forgotten. Remember us correctly or not at all.*

She put the volume down. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. Inside, nothing was.

Her father's signature was in the bundle of loose papers. She had known it would be — had known since the phrase *not relevant* — but knowing did nothing against the fact of it. There it was, in the confident downward stroke she'd watched sign a thousand judgments: Aldous Varn, junior magistrate at the time, witnessing a supplementary dispersal order. Witnessing. His name attached to the machinery, early, eager, decades before he'd risen high enough to fund expeditions and write letters in his own hand.

He had started here. Whatever he was now, he had started here, at the bottom of a dead nation, and climbed.

Ilse sat in the lamplight for a long while, doing the arithmetic her father had taught her. If the annex went home sealed, her future was secure — the partnership, the name, the career built on being the magistrate's daughter who got things done. If the annex went anywhere else, she lost all of it, and gained a war with a man who did not lose wars.

There was a third option, and it was the one she took, and she despised herself for how small it was.

From the dispersal register she tore a single leaf — one page, families beginning with the letter O through R — folded it small, and slipped it into her boot, against the skin of her ankle, where the guards' inspections would never look. One page. Not the whole truth. Just enough of it to be held, to be carried, to be *hers* — a down payment on a debt she couldn't yet name.

She repacked the chest exactly as she'd found it. She resealed the annex with wax from her own kit, pressing it flat with her thumb so the forgery would pass a glance if not a study. She blew out the lantern and lay down on her cot in the dark and listened to the river and did not sleep.

One name from the torn page kept surfacing behind her eyes, the way a single face surfaces from a crowd. A family entered in the register forty years ago, dispersed to a garrison town on the eastern frontier, destination column noting a labor attachment.

Oyelaran. Mother and son.

The same surname as the guide who recognized the seal, who watched her too closely, who had taken her triple-rate offer without haggling and without once meeting her eyes longer than courtesy required.

In the morning, she decided, she would say nothing. She would watch him instead. In the morning, she would begin finding out what else the desert had been hiding.

But the night was long, and the page against her ankle felt warm as a coal, and sleep, when it finally took her near dawn, took her straight into a dream of wheat growing up through the floorboards of her father's courtroom.
