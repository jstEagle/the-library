## 7. The Company of Strangers

The expedition left the riverbend an hour after dawn, and by the second hour I had counted every way it could die.

Twenty-three souls, two wagons, eleven mules, and a chest that weighed like a sin. Ilse Varn rode at the front on a gray mare that cost more than the wagons together. Behind her came twelve guards in matched leather, hired clean and paid better — men whose loyalty had a price tag stitched into it. Then the scholars: a cartographer named Doss with ink-stained fingers and eyes that never stopped measuring, two surveyors who spoke to each other in numbers, and at the back, walking beside the mules because he liked them better than people, a young archivist named Sef.

I rode drag. Last position, best view of everything behind us, worst view of everything ahead. It suited me. Nine years of habit doesn't break in a morning.

The first march was short by design. Twelve miles to a rise I called the Split Rock, where the hardpan gave way to gravel and the first real decision point of the route home. Anyone could walk twelve miles. What mattered was how they walked them — who complained, who watched, who drank water they hadn't earned yet.

By noon I knew most of what I needed to know.

The guards were Varn's men, not hers. They took their cues from a sergeant called Brek, a wide man with a soldier's slouch and a mercenary's patience, and when Ilse gave an order they looked at Brek before they moved. She noticed it too. I saw her notice — the small tightening at her jaw, the half-second delay before she repeated herself louder. She was learning, on day one, that her father's money bought her an escort but not obedience. That knowledge would either make her or break her. I hadn't decided which I wanted.

Doss was the other problem.

He found me at the midday halt while I was checking the mules' hooves for salt-crack. He crouched nearby without being asked, unrolled a strip of vellum across his knee, and started drawing the terrain we'd crossed as if we'd crossed it together.

"You cut the trail at the third wash," he said. "Yesterday, coming in. You didn't follow the thief's line straight — you swung north first."

"Ground was softer north."

"Ground was *longer* north." He shaded something with a stub of pencil. "You added four miles to avoid ground you could have crossed. Why?"

"Because the ground you'd have crossed has a lip you can't see until you're on it, and a loaded wagon goes down that lip nose-first."

He looked up. Not offended. Interested, which was worse. "You've taken wagons this way before."

"I've taken things this way."

"What things?"

"Mules," I said, and went back to the hooves.

He didn't leave. That was the thing about Doss — he treated questions like water in a dry country, something you didn't waste and didn't waste anyone else's either. He sat there sketching the rise, the washes, the line of our march, and every few minutes he asked another one. Where I'd learned the route. How long the flats stayed passable after the rains stopped. Whether the maps he'd seen of the region were wrong or simply absent.

"Absent," I said.

"Maps don't stay absent by accident. Somebody keeps them absent."

"Somebody does."

"Was it your somebody?"

I set down the hoof pick and looked at him until he rolled up his vellum and moved off. But I could feel him working at me the rest of the day the way water works at mortar — patient, indifferent to whether I noticed, certain of the outcome.

* * *

Sef was different. Sef found me by accident, or pretended to.

We made camp at the Split Rock with the sun still two hands above the horizon, because I'd told the camp-master that shade was worth more than miles on the first night and someone had actually listened. The guards grumbled about the short day. The guards would grumble about everything; it was how they marked time.

Sef appeared at my fire with two tin cups and a cloth bundle of dried figs, sat down cross-legged without asking, and said, "You put the latrine trench downwind and below the water seep. Nobody told you to."

"Nobody had to."

"The last caravan I traveled with dug theirs upstream of camp. We were sick for six days." He handed me a cup. It held tea, weak and oversteeped, the kind you get when someone boiled the leaves twice to make them go further. "I'm Sef. Archivist. Third-class, technically, but there's no one here to outrank me except the documents."

"Petra."

"I know. You're the guide. You're also the person everyone talks about when you're not in earshot." He said it easily, no edge to it, the way you'd report weather. "Half of them think you're a criminal. Half think you're a ghost. One of the surveyors is convinced you're ex-garrison."

"And you?"

"I think if you were ex-garrison you wouldn't let them keep saying it out loud." He chewed a fig. "So. Ghost, probably."

I should have ended the conversation there. I had a whole architecture for ending conversations — the flat answer, the turned shoulder, the long silence that people eventually filled themselves and then apologized for. I'd built it over nine years and it had never once failed me.

Instead I drank the bad tea and said, "Third-class archivist. What does that mean?"

"It means I carry boxes and catalog what the first-classes find interesting. On this trip it means the chest is mine." His face changed when he said it — not brighter exactly, but lit from somewhere internal. "Twenty-two years old and I'm the custodian of the only Kessarine material in existence. Do you know anything about them?"

"No."

"Everyone says no. That's the strange part. A whole nation and nobody knows anything — not where it stood, not what language it kept, not why it's gone. Erasure that thorough isn't war. War leaves survivors who remember." He wrapped the fig bundle closed again, careful with the cloth. "My grandmother used to say some countries die twice. Once when they fall and once when the last person stops lying about them."

"Cheerful woman."

"She buried three children. She'd earned her opinions." He looked at me across the fire, and there was nothing sharp in his look, nothing probing like Doss — just open, unhurried attention, the kind that made you aware of what you were doing with your own face. "You knew the seal. When we opened the chest at the river. Before anyone said the name, you already knew it."

"I've seen stamps before."

"Not that stamp." He wasn't accusing. He was just refusing, gently, to be lied to, which was somehow more exhausting than an accusation. "That's all right. Everyone here is carrying something they haven't declared. The guards are carrying contracts. Miss Varn is carrying orders she reads twice before she follows. Doss is carrying ambition. I'm carrying a box of dead country." He stood, brushed sand from his knees, and left the remaining figs beside my bedroll. "I just thought you should know I'm not going to ask. People who don't ask are rarer than people who answer."

He walked back toward the wagon where the chest rode under canvas and lock, and I sat there annoyed to discover I'd enjoyed the conversation.

* * *

The fracture showed itself properly on the second morning, over a matter so small it embarrassed everyone involved.

One of the mules had gone lame in the night — a stone bruise, nothing fatal, but enough that it couldn't pull. The question was whether we slowed the column to its pace or shifted its load and pushed on. I said slow. Half a day, maybe less; the animal might come sound on soft going. Brek said push. A lame mule today was a dead mule Thursday, and Thursday we'd want the speed.

Reasonable, both of us. What happened next wasn't.

"We're not losing a day for livestock," Brek said, loud enough for the whole camp. "Miss Varn's schedule—"

"Is my schedule," Ilse said, "and I'll lose half a day whenever my guide tells me to."

Brek turned to her with the particular patience of a man explaining arithmetic to a child. "Your father was clear about the timeline. The flats close. We're paid to deliver that chest home inside the window, and I don't intend to explain to him why we weren't."

"Then you can explain to him that his daughter countermanded you."

"He doesn't take explanations from—" Brek caught himself, but the sentence had already been born, and everyone heard the rest of it die in his mouth: *from women.*

The silence lasted four seconds. I counted them, because counting is what I do instead of feeling.

Ilse didn't raise her voice. That was the first thing that surprised me. She looked at Brek the way you look at a door that has swung open farther than it was meant to, showing you a room you weren't supposed to see, and she said, quietly, "Unload the mule. Walk it. We move when my guide says we move. Is there any part of that arrangement that troubles you, Sergeant?"

"No, miss."

"Good."

It should have ended there. But Doss — because it was always going to be Doss — chose that moment to observe, to no one in particular, "Interesting that the guide's judgment is suddenly the authority. Interesting how quickly that happens with guides." And one of the guards laughed, short and ugly, and another guard told him to shut it, and for a moment the camp had two sides and no one had chosen them on purpose.

I finished cinching the lame mule's load onto a spare and led it out at the front of the column, and the day went on, and nothing else was said. But I'd seen the seam. Every company has one — the place where it will tear when the weight comes on. This one ran between the people Varn paid and the people Varn trusted, with Ilse standing on the fault line trying to be both.

* * *

That evening Sef found my fire again, and this time he brought nothing, which meant he'd decided the tea had done its work and we could skip the courtesies.

"Brek sent a rider note back east tonight," he said. "Fast mule, moonrise, alone."

I'd seen it too. I see everything; it's the entire job. "Supply run."

"For what? We're twelve miles from the river town and fully provisioned." He fed a stick into the flames. "I catalog the correspondence pouch. It's my job. Nobody thinks about the archivist holding the mailbag." He glanced at me. "You want to know what it said?"

"No."

"It said: *Day two. Guide competent beyond expectation. Also possibly known to eastern garrison. Confirming.*" Sef watched my face with that same terrible gentleness. "I'm telling you because if it reaches whoever it reaches, things will happen fast, and you'll want to have decided beforehand what you're doing. People decide badly in fast."

For a long time I listened to the fire eat the stick. Somewhere behind us a guard coughed. The lame mule shifted its weight and sighed.

"Why?" I said finally. "You've known me three days. Whatever I am, turning me in pays better than warning me."

"Does it?" He shrugged. "I don't know what you are. I know what you've done. You cut four miles off a wagon route to save the axles. You put the trench downwind. You voted to walk a lame mule rather than spend it. In my experience the paperwork of a man matters more than his history, and yours is clean." He stood up. "Also, you're the only person in this company who looks at the horizon like he's expecting it to do something, and I'd rather be standing next to that when it does."

He started away, then paused. "The garrison at Easthold. That's where the note goes. Commander Hale's post." He said the name lightly, testing it, and whatever my face did in the dark, he saw it, and he nodded once, slowly, like a man confirming a suspicion he'd been polite enough never to voice.

Then he was gone, and I sat alone with the fire and understood that something had gone wrong with me.

Nine years I'd kept the ledger balanced: trust no one, need no one, owe no one. It was simple arithmetic and it had kept me alive through country that killed the careless. And in three days this company had started writing entries in my book I hadn't authorized. The lame mule's breathing. Sef's crooked smile when he talked about his dead country. Even Ilse's jaw, that small stubborn tightening — I'd caught myself today watching her ride and thinking, *she'll hold,* the way you think about someone who matters, and I'd hated myself for it, quietly, thoroughly, because caring about people is just a list of ways to lose them and I had lost enough.

They were strangers. Twenty-two strangers and a sealed chest, bought and paid for by a magistrate whose signature I had reasons — old, buried, dangerous reasons — to fear. In a week the desert would start sorting them, the way it sorts everyone. Some would prove harder than they looked. Some would prove softer. At least one, statistically, would not go home.

And I lay back on my bedroll and realized, with the flat clarity of a man reading his own warrant, that I had already begun arranging the march in my head around who would survive it. Which guards to put on the flank. Where Sef should walk, out of arrow range of the ridgeline. Which mule Ilse should ride when the footing got bad.

I cared. That was the fracture I hadn't seen — not the one between Brek and Ilse, not the seam in the company. The one in me. Nine years of not being found, and I'd gone and gotten found anyway, by a boy with a mailbag and a woman with her father's orders and a lame mule I'd refused to spend.

I was still lying there hating it when I heard the mule — not the lame one, the picket line, all of them at once, heads up, ears forward, staring south into the dark where there was nothing to see.

Nothing that a man on a fast horse, riding moonless, couldn't cover by morning.

Easthold was three days' hard ride from the river town. The note would arrive tomorrow night.

Which meant whatever Hale intended to do about me, he'd already started doing it — and I had maybe five days to decide whether I was running again, or whether, for the first time in nine years, I'd stand and let the past catch up on ground of my choosing.
