## 8. Water Is a Verdict

The well at Tamsen's Rest was supposed to hold water.

Petra had said so himself, three days ago, standing over his map in the firelight with the confidence of a man who had drunk from that well twice before the war and once after it. He had said it the way he said everything now — measured, unhurried, a voice built for people who needed to believe him. *Tamsen's Rest holds through the dry months. We'll fill every skin there.*

He had been wrong.

They knew it by the smell before they saw it. A well that holds water smells of stone and cold. This one smelled of old rope and something sweeter underneath, the sweetness of things that had died in the dark. Ilse Varn lowered the bucket herself, refusing to let a guard do it, and when it came up the leather hung slack and dripping mud the color of dried blood.

Nobody spoke for a long moment. Twenty-two people stood around a ring of stones in the middle of nowhere, listening to an empty bucket drip.

"Depth?" Ilse asked finally.

"Forty feet," Petra said. "Maybe more."

"Is there water at forty feet?"

"There was."

The cartographer, a narrow man named Quill whose questions Petra had learned to count like coins, opened his mouth. Petra watched him decide against it. That was new. Three days ago Quill would have asked whether the guide had miscalculated or lied. Now he looked at the bucket, then at the horizon, and kept his arithmetic to himself.

The expedition had left the riverbend nine days ago with water for six. That margin had seemed generous. It was the kind of generosity the desert collected interest on.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a rock shelf, and Petra sat up doing sums he didn't like. Two skins per person, refilled where they could — a seep under the shelf gave them maybe three gallons an hour if they were patient, which meant filling through the night. At strict rationing, half a liter a day per head, they had four days of water and five days of walking to the next certain source: the cisterns cut into the ridge country, if the cisterns hadn't silted, if no one else had drawn them down, if the season behaved.

Four ifs between twenty-two people and dying.

He laid it out for Ilse at her fire, plainly, because she paid him to be plain. "Half a liter each. No washing. No cooking beyond what we can spare. The animals get what the seep gives overnight, then nothing until the cisterns."

"And if the cisterns are dry?"

"Then we're having a different conversation."

She nodded. She'd gone thinner since the riverbend; the chest rode in the middle of the column under oilcloth and two guards' eyes, and whatever she expected it to make her, it seemed to be making her tired instead. "You've done this before," she said. "Rationed."

"Once." He did not say more, and she did not ask, and that was the whole architecture of their arrangement: two people agreeing not to open doors.

The trouble started on the second day.

* * *

It was not dramatic. That was the thing Petra would remember afterward — how small it was. A guard named Doss, one of Varn's hired men, a big quiet fellow who'd done nothing worse than complain about the food, was seen at dusk kneeling behind the water cart with a skin already full and a second one filling.

Sef found him. The boy had gone back for a dropped strap and came around the cart's flank and stopped as if he'd walked into glass. Doss looked up. Neither of them moved. Then Doss said, "You didn't see anything," in a voice that wasn't threatening exactly, just tired, just a man stating the weather, and Sef backed away and came straight to Petra, and his hands were shaking.

"He had two skins," Sef said. "Two. Everyone else has one."

Petra closed his eyes. Behind them he saw a well in another desert, another year, another arithmetic, and men who had solved the same problem the way frightened men always solve it — by deciding some lives counted more than others. He had been nineteen. He still carried the sound of it.

"Show me."

Doss didn't run. That was something. When Petra came around the cart, the guard stood up slowly, the full skin in one hand, and set it down on the sand between them like a man laying a card face-up.

"It's for my brother," Doss said. "Back in Corvane. He's sick. I took this contract for his medicine. I wasn't going to drink it myself."

"You'll drink it yourself," Petra said, "the first night it gets bad enough. Or you'll trade it to someone weaker than you for a promise. Or you'll tell yourself a story about your brother until the story is true. Doesn't matter which. Out here it all ends the same way — one man's extra is everyone else's less."

"I'm not asking you to forgive it. I'm telling you why."

"I know why." And he did, and the knowing was the worst part, because the why was human and the crime was still the crime. "Water isn't yours. It isn't mine. It belongs to whoever's still standing when we reach the cisterns. That's the only law out here, and it's older than any magistrate."

Doss's jaw worked. "So what happens?"

"That's not my call alone."

* * *

But it was, and they both knew it. That was the trap inside the job. Ilse commanded the contract; Petra commanded the route; and water belonged to neither of them. Desert law wasn't written anywhere. It lived in the people, and it woke up ugly when it woke at all.

By the time the sun went down, the whole camp knew. Of course it did. Twenty-two people counting skins in the dark, and one skin too many — news like that travels faster than thirst.

Ilse convened them at the main fire. She stood with her hands loose at her sides and her father's seal-ring glinting on her finger, and Petra watched her understand, in real time, that this moment would define her command more than anything the chest ever would.

"Doss took water," she said. "Two days' worth. He says it was for family. I believe him." A murmur ran the circle. She raised a hand. "Believing him changes nothing. If we allow one man two shares, we are choosing who dies of the difference. So we will choose openly, or the desert will choose for us, and the desert cheats."

Quill, of all people, spoke first. "What does the guide say? He's the one who got us to an empty well."

Every head turned. Petra felt the sentence land where it was aimed. He let it sit, then stood.

"The guide says this," he said. "I miscalculated. The well was good eight years ago and it's dust now, and that's on me, and I'll carry it. But the math doesn't care whose fault it is. Half a liter a day gets us to the cisterns with a day to spare. One man taking double takes that day from everybody. So here's the old rule, the one from before maps and magistrates. Hoarding water on a shared crossing is theft from every throat in the company. The thief is bound at night. His share continues — we don't kill a man for being afraid. But the water he took goes back into the common stock, and he walks tied to the cart until we reach the cisterns, and at the cisterns the company votes again on whether he stays bound or walks free."

"And if we don't reach the cisterns?" a guard asked.

"Then none of this matters," Petra said, "and I'll be the first one dead, because I'll have led you here."

Silence. Then Ilse said, "We vote. All in favor of the guide's rule."

Hands came up one by one, slower than Petra liked and faster than he'd feared. Doss watched them come up around the fire, watching his own fate get decided by people whose names he probably didn't know, and his face did something complicated — not rage, not shame, something closer to a man watching weather.

When it was done, Doss held out his wrists.

* * *

They bound him with his own belt straps, sitting against the cart wheel, and Petra marked the cache — the recovered water, sealed in a skin, buried under a cairn of black stones with a notch cut facing the trail. Anyone coming behind would find it. That mattered to him more than he could explain. Justice that leaves nothing behind is just cruelty with better posture.

Sef brought Doss his evening ration personally, knelt, held the cup while the big man drank. Petra watched from a distance and felt something twist in his chest that he refused to name.

"You think this was right?" Sef asked him later, at the edge of the firelight.

"I think it worked."

"That's not the same question."

"No," Petra said. "It isn't." He turned the cup in his hands. "Law is a machine, boy. You feed it a fact, it turns out a verdict, and mostly nobody looks at the gears. Tonight there were no gears. Just twenty-two scared people and a fire and a vote. It worked because we're small enough to see each other's faces. Get big enough — a garrison, a nation — and the same vote becomes a signature on an order, and the order travels where the faces can't follow." He heard himself and stopped. He had said too much, and about things Sef had no business knowing he knew.

Sef was quiet a while. "You talk like a man who's read a lot of orders."

"I talk like a man who's thirsty," Petra said. "Sleep."

* * *

He didn't sleep. He walked the perimeter, as he did every night, and ended up standing near the cart where Doss sat trussed against the wheel, awake, staring at the stars.

"Your brother," Petra said. "In Corvane. What's wrong with him?"

"Fever lungs. Started last winter." Doss shifted against the wheel. "You think I made him up?"

"I think it doesn't matter tonight. Ask me at the cisterns."

Doss almost smiled. "You're a strange one, guide. Varn's daughter pays you triple and trusts you with everything but her name. The men trust you with their water. Who pays you?"

"Nobody," Petra said. "That's the trick of it. Nobody paying me means nobody owns me."

"Everybody's owned by something."

Petra looked north, where the ridge country waited with its cisterns and its ifs, and thought about warrants, and mothers, and fires. "Yeah," he said. "Ask me again in a week."

* * *

They made the seep's last gallons at dawn and walked. The hardpan whitened underfoot until it hurt to look at. By noon the mirages started — silver lakes lying flat against the horizon, promising everything, delivering heat shimmer. By afternoon even the guards stopped pointing at them.

It was during the third watch of that night, with the column sleeping in a shallow basin and Petra keeping the lonely hours, that he saw it: a spark of light on the northern ridge. Gone. There again. Not a star — stars didn't move along ridgelines, and stars didn't travel in threes.

Three lights, spaced like outriders, pacing the column's line of march.

Petra stood very still in the dark and did the only arithmetic that mattered. Nobody camps on that ridge. Nobody travels it at night without knowing the ground. Which meant somebody out there knew this desert — knew it the way he did, or better.

He thought of Hale, and put the thought away like a knife back in its sheath. Too soon. Probably too soon.

Probably.

He woke Sef with a hand on the boy's shoulder and a finger across his own lips, and pointed north, and watched the young archivist's face go through wonder and fear in the space of a breath.

"Get Ilse," Petra whispered. "Quietly. And Sef — wake the others slow. From tonight, nobody drinks alone."
