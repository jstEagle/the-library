## 9. Riders on the Ridge

Sef found the first rider by accident, which was how he found most things worth finding.

He had stopped to re-tie a bootlace at the rear of the column, and while he crouched in the dust with his fingers working the knot, he looked up out of habit — the archivist's habit, the habit of checking what was behind a thing as carefully as what was in front of it — and there was a smudge on the northern ridge that hadn't been there an hour ago.

Not a cloud. Clouds moved. This held its position against the horizon like a splinter under skin.

He finished the lace. He stood slowly, the way you stand when you don't want your body to tell anyone what your eyes have told you, and he walked forward along the line of the column until he reached Petra, who was walking drag as usual, half a mile behind the last cart, watching the ground behind them more than the road ahead.

"Petra."

"Sef." Petra didn't stop walking. He never seemed to stop walking; Sef suspected he slept upright like a horse and dreamed in stride.

"There's dust on the north ridge. It's been holding position for — I don't know. Since the second water stop, maybe."

Something happened to Petra's face. It was very small, and it was over very fast, and if Sef hadn't spent three weeks learning to read this man the way he'd learned to read marginalia — the notes in the margins, the corrections, the things a writer tried to hide from his own hand — he would have missed it entirely. But he didn't miss it. Petra's jaw tightened once, and then went deliberately loose, the way a man loosens a fist so no one can see it was ever closed.

"Show me."

They walked back a hundred paces to where the terrain dipped, and Petra looked at the ridge without looking at it, his gaze sliding across it sideways like a man checking a ledger for a figure he already knows is wrong.

"Cavalry," Petra said. "Four, maybe five. Holding our pace. They've been doing it since morning, I'd guess."

"You knew?"

"I suspected." Petra turned and started walking again, and Sef had to lengthen his stride to keep up. "Dust doesn't lie about numbers. Five riders can shadow a column for days without spending much water. They're not chasing us. Chasing costs water."

"What are they doing, then?"

"Counting," Petra said.

* * *

Sef thought about that word all afternoon. *Counting.*

He was twenty-three years old and he had come on this expedition because the frontier archive had offered him a stipend and a chance to handle documents older than any empire currently claiming the map, and because Ilse Varn had interviewed him personally and asked him one question that wasn't about his credentials: *Can you keep your mouth shut when it matters?* He'd said yes, and mostly he could, and mostly the question had struck him as standard paranoia from a magistrate's daughter traveling with salvage papers into country where salvage rights were settled by whoever had the most guards.

But counting. You counted things you intended to take inventory of later. You counted troops before an engagement, cattle before a sale, prisoners before transport. The word sat in him like a stone in a boot.

At the evening halt, the column formed its rough square around the carts, and Sef took his supper ration — flatbread and a smear of bean paste, the beans already tasting of the leather bag they traveled in — and went to sit where he always sat, near the third cart, near Petra's fire, because Petra's fire was small and disciplined and nobody talked at it, which after a day of the guards' constant betting and complaining felt like silence you could rest inside.

Ilse Varn came to the fire instead of summoning Petra to hers, which Sef noted. She did that sometimes. A magistrate's daughter who summoned people taught everyone her power. A magistrate's daughter who came to their fire taught them something else, something Sef hadn't named yet.

"The ridge," she said, without preamble. "My spotter says riders. Yours says cavalry. Which is it?"

"Garrison pattern," Petra said. "Lances wrapped, riding light. Frontier patrol formation, but they're not patrolling anything. They're pacing us."

"Whose garrison?"

Petra fed a twig to the fire. "Could be any of three posts along this stretch. Hardpan country draws hard men."

It was the smoothest lie Sef had ever heard delivered, and it was smooth precisely because it was built on true materials. There were three posts along this stretch. Garrison patrols did ride wrapped lances. Every fact in the sentence was load-bearing and every one of them was a wall placed in front of a door.

Ilse looked at Petra for a long moment. Sef watched her do it — watched her weigh him, the way she weighed everything, her father's money and her father's orders and her own face, which she kept arranged like a document she didn't want stamped yet.

"If they close," she said, "they'll want to know what we're carrying."

"They'll want to know that regardless," Petra said. "Question is whether we tell them before or after they ask."

"And your advice?"

"Keep the column tight. Double the night watch on the carts. And nobody rides out to parley alone."

"That last one sounds like experience talking."

"It sounds like arithmetic," Petra said. "Parleys are where columns lose people."

Ilse nodded once and left, and Sef sat with his cooling bread and understood two things at once, the way you sometimes understand a whole page from a single line. First: Ilse Varn was frightened, and managing it, and managing it well. Second: Petra Oyelaran knew exactly whose cavalry those were, and he would rather eat the fire than say so.

* * *

The trouble with keeping your mouth shut when it matters, Sef reflected somewhere past midnight during his watch shift, is that you get very good at noticing other people not doing it.

He walked the perimeter slow, boots soft on the hardpan, past the sleeping forms of guards who trusted Varn's coin and a cartographer named Doss who talked in his sleep about contour lines, and he let his mind work the problem the way it wanted to work it, the way it worked everything: as a text.

Here was the text. A thief steals nothing but a map. Leaves coin behind — Sef had heard the story in the river town, everyone had, the detail too strange not to travel. A dead man at a flooded riverbend beside a chest from a nation that isn't on maps anymore. An expedition funded by a magistrate, guided by a ranger who won't say where he learned the salt flats, shadowed by garrison cavalry that the guide pretends not to recognize. And through all of it, a man who walks drag, watches the backtrail, sleeps facing away from fires, and flinches — flinches, one small tightening of the jaw — at the word *garrison*.

Sef had catalogued manuscripts long enough to know what a forgery looked like. Forgeries were almost always too clean. Real documents had scars: water damage, erasures, palimpsests where someone had scraped away an old text to write a new one over it. Petra Oyelaran was a palimpsest. Underneath whatever name he was writing himself as, there was an older text, and somebody had tried to scrape it off, and the scraping had left marks everywhere — in the way he watched ridges, in the way he counted water caches without being asked, in the way he said *parleys are where columns lose people* like a man quoting a bill he'd already paid.

And now riders on the ridge, pacing them, counting them.

Sef stopped at the northeast corner of the perimeter and looked out at the dark. The moon was a fingernail paring, low and thin, and the ridge was a blacker black against the sky, and he couldn't see the riders but he knew they were there the way you know a footnote exists — the little number sitting in the margin of the page, promising that somewhere below, the real story waited.

He thought: *I could ask him.*

That was the temptation, and it was a real one, sharper than he'd expected. Not curiosity exactly — or not only. Three weeks of hard marching does something to a man's allegiances. Petra had taught him to read the hardpan for seep signs, had shown him which plants lied about water and which told the truth, had quietly repositioned Sef's bedroll two nights ago so a flash-flood channel wouldn't be sleeping on top of him, and had never mentioned it, and Sef had only figured it out the next morning from the scoured mud line. That was friendship, or the seed of it, growing in the specific soil of the desert where you learn fast whose judgment keeps you breathing.

And friendship, Sef was discovering, was a kind of loyalty, and loyalty was a kind of debt, and debts made you complicit whether you signed anything or not.

If he asked, Petra would either lie to him — and the lying would change something between them, some load-bearing wall would crack — or he would tell the truth, and the truth would be a weight Sef would then carry, and carrying it would make him a party to whatever Petra was running from. Either way, the easy thing between them ended. He understood that with archival clarity. Some questions, once asked aloud, cannot be re-shelved.

So instead of asking, Sef did what he did best. He started a private index.

In his head, in the careful script he used for provisional catalogues, he began to list what he knew, entry by entry:

*Item:* Guide travels under name Oyelaran. No record of any ranger family of that name in the frontier registries Sef had filed. (He had checked, the first week, out of professional habit. Nothing. A name with no paper behind it, in a country where even beggars had paper.)

*Item:* Guide reads garrison formations at distance. Identifies lance-wrap protocol unprompted.

*Item:* Guide advises against parley with tone of prior loss.

*Item:* Riders hold pace for one full day without closing. Discipline suggests commissioned command, not bounty riders. Bounty riders chase. Commissioned men wait for orders.

*Item:* Guide watches the north ridge more than the road ahead.

He stood there in the cold, adding entries, and the index grew teeth. Because here was the entry he kept circling and couldn't file:

*Item:* Whatever the riders want, they want it from him. Not the chest. Him.

He'd seen Ilse's spotters report, seen her face when she heard the word *cavalry*, and her fear had been administrative — fear for cargo, for schedule, for her father's investment. But Petra's fear had been personal. It had flashed across him like heat lightning, there and gone, illuminating a landscape Sef now badly wanted to map.

* * *

Morning came up red, and with it came proof.

The column broke camp in the grey hour, and by full light the riders had made their first move: they'd closed to within visible range, five of them in a loose skirmish line on the ridge's shoulder, close enough now that Sef could see the sun-flash off a helmet, the unmistakable geometry of a military banner furled tight on a lance. Not bandits. Bandits didn't carry banners; banners were paperwork you could be hanged under.

The column stopped. Word ran down the line, and the guards clustered around Ilse, and Doss the cartographer climbed onto a cart with his spyglass like a man going to watch a play, and Petra stood apart from all of them, at the front, absolutely still, looking north.

Through Doss's glass, passed hand to hand down the line, the picture resolved: five riders, imperial drab, and on the banner — half-unfurled now, deliberate, a message sent at distance — a device Sef recognized from the archive's seal room. A frontier garrison crest. Third Column, he thought. Or Fourth. One of the posts that answered to the provincial command, and above the crest, stitched smaller, a commander's pennon.

One rider detached from the line and began walking his horse down the slope toward them. Unhurried. A man delivering an invitation, not an attack.

Beside Sef, Petra exhaled, very slowly, through his nose, and Sef — standing close enough to see it, closer than anyone else bothered to stand — saw the whole architecture of the man rearrange itself around that breath. Not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who has found the page he's been dreading since the book began.

"Hale," Petra said. Quietly. To himself, or to the desert, or possibly — Sef thought later — to nine years of running, which had just crested a ridge on a horse.

Sef didn't ask who Hale was.

He stood there with the name in his hands like a page torn from a restricted file, and he felt the pull of it, the fierce archival itch to read further, to cross-reference, to demand the full text — and underneath that pull, steadier, something else. Petra had repositioned his bedroll out of the flood channel and never said a word. Petra had taught him which plants lied. Whatever was written on the pages of this man's past, Sef had three weeks of evidence about the character of the hand that wrote it.

So he chose, for now, the archivist's discipline: he catalogued the name, sealed the entry, and shelved it.

*Hale,* he wrote in the index behind his eyes. *Commander. Holds something over Oyelaran. Pending.*

The rider came on. Petra stepped forward to meet him, alone, ignoring Ilse's sharp intake of breath behind him, and Sef watched his friend walk out toward the past with his shoulders squared and his hands loose at his sides, and Sef understood that whatever happened next, the quiet version of their journey — the version where the guide was just a guide — had just been formally withdrawn.

The rider drew rein ten paces out. Even at that distance, Sef could hear the first words clearly, pitched to carry to the whole column, the way a summons is always pitched to carry:

"Petra Oyelaran. Commander Hale requests your surrender — and the chest."

Behind him, Sef heard the guards' murmur ripple outward like a stone dropped in still water, and he heard Ilse Varn say, very softly, to no one: *"Oyelaran?"*

And Sef, keeper of sealed entries, felt his private index burn a hole straight through its shelf.
