## 10. The Annex

The chest had two locks, and Ilse had the key to only one of them.

Her father's instructions had been precise, and precision was the family trade. The bronze-bound chest recovered from the riverbank was to be inventoried in full, catalogued item by item, and transported home under seal. Everything except the annex. The annex — a flat iron case nested in the chest's base, wrapped in oilcloth, stamped with a second seal she did not recognize — was to be returned unopened. *Unopened* had been underlined twice in his letter, and her father did not underline.

She had told herself, for three days, that she would honor it.

Now the camp slept around her. The guards had drawn lots for first watch and lost, and their resentment kept them at the far fire, playing dice with the kind of quiet concentration that meant they didn't want to be spoken to. Sef had gone to his tent with the ledger and a lamp he'd promised to ration. Petra Oyelaran was somewhere beyond the picket line, the way he was every night — not gone, exactly, but arranged at the edge of things like a man who had learned that the middle of any group was the first place trouble looked.

Ilse sat alone at her own fire with the chest open in front of her and the annex case in her lap, and admitted what she had been refusing to admit since the riverbend: she had not come out here for salvage rights. Salvage rights were the paperwork's name for it. What she had come for was the thing her father wanted back without looking at, and a man who wanted something returned unopened was a man who already knew what was inside.

She turned the case over. The second seal was wax over lead, pressed with a device like a broken tower — a tower with its top third sheared away, the stump jagged. She had seen it once before, years ago, in her father's study, on the spine of a ledger he kept in a drawer he locked. She had been fourteen and had asked what it was, and he had said, *an old administrative mark, nothing current,* and had changed the subject with the practiced ease of a man who changed subjects for a living.

Nothing current. Nothing, in fact, at all — because Kessarine was not current anywhere. It was not on maps. It was not in treaties. It was a word that appeared in her father's correspondence only in the passive voice: *the Kessarine matter, the former territories, the eastern disposition.* A nation that had become a filing category.

She worked a knife under the wax.

The lead underneath resisted, then didn't. The seal cracked along the tower's broken edge, which felt less like breaking it than completing it, and she told herself that too was nothing — a figure of speech, a coincidence of metal. The hasp gave. The oilcloth came away in dry, whispering folds.

Inside were documents. Not treasure, not the trade concessions her father's letters had danced around, not the mineral surveys the expedition's backers murmured about over brandy. Documents. Testimonies, most of them, written in a careful administrative hand on paper that had been good paper once and was now the color of old teeth. She lifted the first one out and read it by firelight, and the firelight seemed to get thinner as she read.

It was a deposition. A woman named — the name was smudged, water had found it even in the chest — a woman of Kessarine, testifying to a tribunal that had never convened, describing the winter the grain shipments stopped. Not failed. *Stopped.* The granaries of the Kessarine valley districts had been full in the autumn, she wrote, and by midwinter they were empty, and the imperial administrators who had managed the transfer of the harvest under the terms of the Integration Accord — she used the treaty's full name, the way people use a name when they want it to be answerable for something — had signed the manifests and sealed the storehouses and shipped the grain east, out of the territory, while the people whose harvest it was stood outside the walls and were told the stores were quarantined for reasons of public health.

There was a famine that winter. Ilse knew this the way she knew the capital's weather records — as a fact, a line in a ledger of years, a bad winter in a province that had stopped being a province shortly after. Her tutors had called it regrettable. Her father's generation had called it necessary consolidation. The woman's deposition called it what the manifests called it, and gave dates, and gave numbers, and gave the name of the administrator who countersigned the shipment orders.

Ilse read the name three times before she could make her eyes move past it.

She knew the name. Not the man — he had died before she was born, or retired, or been promoted somewhere the histories didn't follow. But the name was on buildings. It was on the facade of the Hall of Records. It was the name of a scholarship her father had once suggested she compete for, when she was deciding what to become.

She set the deposition down. Her hands were steady, which surprised her, because nothing else was.

* * *

There were eleven documents in the annex. She read all of them, because stopping seemed worse than continuing, the way a fever is worse for breaking.

The second was a surveyor's report on the Kessarine river boundaries, with marginalia showing how the surveyed line had been moved — not by error, but by amendment — so that the treaty's water rights fell to the imperial side of a border that had been redrawn to make the treaty's terms look generous. The third was a petition from the Kessarine merchant guilds, polite to the point of desperation, requesting arbitration under the Accord's own provisions, stamped *received* and then stamped again, months later, *jurisdiction declined.* The fourth was a military memorandum assessing the valley districts' capacity for resistance and concluding, in the flat language of staff officers, that dispersal of the population was preferable to garrisoning it, being cheaper and final.

The fifth through the ninth were more testimony. Farmers, a schoolteacher, a priest of a rite Ilse had never heard of, a woman who had been a girl during the removal years and described the trains — not cattle cars, the empire had learned better by then, they were passenger cars with the windows painted over — and the destinations, which were scattered. Deliberately scattered. A family to this town, a family to that one, no two communities deposited within a hundred miles of each other, so that the language would thin out in a generation and the graves would have no one left to tend them in two.

The tenth document was the order itself.

It was not a dramatic document. That was the thing Ilse would remember afterward, in the years when she remembered it at all — that the instrument dismantling a nation fit on two pages and was mostly boilerplate. *By authority of the Office of the Magistrate for Eastern Administration, pursuant to the Integration Accord and subsequent enabling statutes.* Clauses about resettlement stipends. Clauses about property transfer. A schedule. And at the bottom, in the signature block, the name of the office that had issued it — her father's office, the same office, the same chain of delegation, the same seal that sat in the lacquered box on his desk and came down on the documents he sent her into the desert to recover.

His office had signed it. Not his hand — he had been a junior clerk then, or not yet born, she genuinely did not know, and the not-knowing was its own kind of nausea — but his office. The office he now held. The office whose authority he exercised, whose precedents he enforced, whose secrets he had sent an armed expedition into the salt flats to retrieve and bury.

*Return unopened.* She had thought it was about leverage. She had assumed, in the way of daughters who love their fathers, that the secrecy was strategy — that the annex contained something valuable, something to be traded or suppressed for advantage, something that would explain the expense of this expedition and the quality of its guards. She had built a whole architecture of reasonable motives and lived inside it for weeks.

It was not leverage. It was evidence. Her father had not sent her to recover a prize. He had sent her to recover a crime.

The eleventh document was the shortest. A single page, a covering memorandum, unsigned, in the same careful hand as the depositions — the hand of whoever had assembled the annex, the hand of a clerk who had known exactly what he was filing and where it would need to be found someday. It read, in its entirety:

*Filed in the expectation of a tribunal that has not yet been convened. The signatories believe the record will be destroyed. The record is not destroyed. Whoever holds this: the names are the nation. Keep the names.*

Ilse sat by the dead-down fire for a long time.

* * *

She should have resealed it. That was the thought that finally got her moving — not conscience, not yet, but procedure, the reflex of a woman raised by an administrator. A broken seal was a reportable event. A broken seal discovered by the guards, by Sef, by Oyelaran, would be a catastrophe she could not manage, and she was, whatever else, a manager. She gathered the documents with hands that wanted to shake and did not, wrapped them in the oilcloth, and was reaching for the wax to forge the seal closed —

— and stopped.

The ledger. The chest's main compartment held the ledger Sef had been reading every night, the ledger of names, and she had catalogued it without reading it because it was cataloguing, and cataloguing was safe. But the memorandum said *the names are the nation,* and the annex said the nation had been taken apart name by name, family by family, dispersed on a schedule, and the ledger was a list of names, and she had not let herself ask the obvious question, which was: names of what?

She opened it to the page Sef had been copying.

It was a register of the removal. Households, in the order they had been processed. Origin district, destination, date of transport, and a column headed *status* that the clerk had kept filling in, year after year, in different inks — *arrived, arrived, deceased in transit, arrived, unlocated, deceased, arrived* — as though he had spent his whole life tracking where the pieces of his country had been scattered, waiting for someone to come and read it.

She turned the pages slowly. The fire had burned to a red core and the light was bad, and she was leaning close to the page when she saw the surname.

Oyelaran. Household of four. Origin: the river districts, the valley, the drowned green country that was now a line on no map. Destination: a frontier garrison town she had never heard of, a place called — she mouthed it — a place that meant nothing except that it was where the empire had put one Kessarine family and walked away. Status, in the clerk's latest hand: *unlocated.*

She sat back. The night made its small sounds — wind on the tent canvas, a guard clearing his throat, the desert doing its enormous quiet work of not caring — and Ilse Varn, who had crossed the salt flats on her father's money with her father's papers and her father's plan, understood that she was carrying something that could not be put down.

The guide. The warrant-runner. The man who read tracks like scripture and stood at the edge of every fire. Oyelaran.

It could be a coincidence. She was her father's daughter; she knew how to weigh a coincidence. The name was not so rare. The ledger held hundreds of households. A ranger on the frontier could have been born to any of them, or none.

But she had watched him look at the seal on the chest. She had been standing right there at the riverbend, watching his face instead of the water, because she had already learned that the guide was the most informative thing in any landscape. And she had seen it — a flicker, gone in a blink, a man recognizing something he had been hoping not to find. She had filed it then as *he knows the stories, the frontier is full of old stories.* She filed it now where it belonged.

He was from the ledger. He was one of the names. He was walking her home through the desert that had eaten his family's country, for her father's pay, and he did not know — or he knew, and was saying nothing, which was worse, or better, she could not decide.

The wax was in her hand. She looked at the broken seal, at the tower sheared of its top, and understood that she was standing at the exact place her father had spent his career making sure no one stood: between the record and the reading of it.

She did not reforge the seal. She wrapped the annex in the oilcloth, and she took one page — the covering memorandum, the eleventh document, the one with no signature and no number and nothing to tie it to the case — and she folded it small, and she put it inside her coat, against her ribs, where a person keeps the things that have stopped being property.

Then she closed the chest, locked the lock she had a key for, banked the fire, and went to her tent, and lay awake listening to the wind, doing the arithmetic she could not stop doing.

Her father had signed the orders — not the old ones, the new ones, the ones that mattered now: the recovery order, the sealed-annex order, the *return unopened* order. He had funded this expedition from accounts she was beginning to understand were not salvage accounts. Every ration the company had eaten was bought with the proceeds of a crime, and she was the instrument of its recovery, and the man guiding them home was its victim, and the truth was in her coat, against her ribs, warming to her body heat like a living thing.

In the morning, Sef would want to compare his transcriptions against the ledger's later pages. In the morning, Oyelaran would stand at the edge of the fire and watch her the way he watched everything, missing nothing, and she would have to decide which of them was lying to the other, and about what, and whether the difference mattered anymore.

She slept anyway. It was the last easy night of the journey, though she didn't know it.

What she knew, falling asleep, was the memorandum's last line, repeating itself under her heartbeat like a second pulse: *the names are the nation.* And that at dawn she would look across the fire at a man named Oyelaran and see, for the first time, not a guide she had hired but a name she was keeping — and that he was going to look back at her and know, the way people who have lost countries always know, exactly what she had done in the night.
