## 12. Sef Reads the Page

Sef found the page on the ninth night out from the riverbend, and he found it the way he found most things: by accident, while looking for something else.

He was hunting a pencil. His good one had rolled under the supply cart during the evening halt, and the camp was settling into its usual architecture of small noises — the guards' dice game at the fire, the mules shifting in their pickets, Ilse Varn's voice giving quiet instructions to her quartermaster near the lead wagon. Sef crawled under the cart with a lantern shielded behind his body, because light discipline was one of the few rules Petra Oyelaran had bothered to teach him directly.

*Light draws eyes,* Petra had said. *Eyes draw questions. Questions are expensive out here.*

It was good advice. It was also, Sef would think later, advice from a man who knew exactly how expensive questions could be.

The pencil wasn't there. But wedged in the gap between the cart's axle box and the frame — pressed flat, deliberate, hidden rather than lost — was a folded sheet of heavy paper, the kind used for records, not letters. Sef should have left it. He told himself that afterward, many times: *you should have left it.* But his fingers were already unfolding it, and the lantern was already tilted to give him light, and there are moments when curiosity moves faster than conscience, like water finding a crack before the stone even knows it's wet.

The page was an inventory fragment. A list of holdings seized under some authority Sef didn't recognize — *by provision of the Passage Compact* — dated in a calendar system that didn't match any he'd studied. Grain stores. A printing house. Three wells. And at the bottom, in a different hand than the rest of the document, a line of annotation so small it might have been an afterthought:

*Family registers transferred to Annex. Do not disperse.*

The handwriting stopped him. Not the words — though the words were strange enough — but the hand itself. The letters leaned forward slightly, as if hurrying. The descenders on the g's were long and hooked. He'd seen that hand before. He'd spent three evenings copying entries out of the chest's ledger for Ilse Varn's catalog, and he'd complained to himself about the ledger's cramped script the whole time, and now here it was again, on a loose page hidden under a cart, in a camp where exactly one person had access to the chest's contents outside of supervised inventory hours.

Ilse Varn had taken this page. Ilse Varn, who lectured the guards about chain of custody, who made Sef initial every entry he copied, who spoke of the archive like a priestess speaking of relics — she had stolen a page from her own salvage.

Sef folded the paper along its original creases and put it back exactly where he'd found it, and then lay awake in his bedroll until the moon set, doing arithmetic.

* * *

The arithmetic went like this: if Ilse had taken one page, she'd read more than one page. If she'd read more than one page, she knew what the chest actually contained, which meant her smooth public summaries — *administrative records, land grants, nothing of strategic value* — were performances. And if she was performing for her own expedition, then whatever she knew was worth performing for, which meant it was dangerous, which meant Sef needed to know it too, because Sef had signed onto this expedition for the same reason most young archivists signed onto anything: to touch the unrepeatable. A nation erased from every atlas. You did not walk away from that. You got closer.

But there was a second column in the arithmetic, and it was less comfortable.

Over nine days of marching, Sef had done what he always did with primary sources: he'd memorized them. The ledger's structure lived in his head now — the columns of names, the columns of destinations, the columns marked with a word that recurred so often it had become almost meaningless through repetition: *dispersed*. Four hundred and eleven entries copied or skimmed. Names, towns of origin, assigned settlements across three imperial provinces. He hadn't thought much about the names themselves. They were data. That was the training. Data doesn't bleed.

Except that afternoon, filling the water skins at the seep Petra had located by reading a line of greener grass from half a mile off, Sef had watched Petra work — the sure, unhurried economy of him, the way he touched the ground like a man greeting an old acquaintance — and a name had surfaced in Sef's memory with terrible clarity.

*Oyelaran.*

Entry sixty-two. Town of origin: Kessarine proper, river district. Destination: a settlement in the eastern provinces whose name Sef had copied without thinking, because it was just a name. Family members listed: four. Mother, father, two sons. One son's given name had been recorded only as an initial.

Petra. It wasn't a common name. It wasn't a Kessarine name either, as far as Sef could tell from four hundred other entries — but names travel, names get changed, names get worn down like coins passed through too many hands. And Petra Oyelaran had appeared at the chest before anyone else, alone, with a dead thief, and had never once — not once in nine days — asked what was in it.

Sef had noticed that absence early and filed it as discretion. Now it looked like something else. Now it looked like a man not asking because he already knew, the way you don't ask whether a wound is deep when you can see the bone.

* * *

He waited two days for the right moment, because confronting someone wrongly is worse than confronting them late. On the second day the column crossed a low ridge of black rock that cut the flats like a scar, and Petra called an early halt in its shadow — shade, windbreak, defensible sightlines, all three reasons given in his flat ranger's voice. The guards grumbled happily. Ilse approved it with a nod. And Sef saw his chance, because Petra always took the first watch of the evening alone, walking the perimeter, and he always walked it slow.

Sef fell into step beside him at the ridge's northern end, where the rock dropped away into a pan of salt so white it hurt to look at in the last light.

"You knew the thief," Sef said.

Petra didn't break stride. "I tracked him. That was the job."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," Petra said. "It isn't." He walked another twenty paces, boots crunching on salt-crusted stone, and Sef matched him, heart going hard against his ribs. Then Petra stopped at the edge of the drop and looked out at the white distance, and his profile in the fading light was unreadable as the horizon.

"Ask your question," he said. "You've been carrying it for two days. It's making you walk badly."

"I copied the ledger," Sef said. "All of it. I have a good memory — that's why Varn hired me, that's why you tolerate me talking at dinner. Entry sixty-two." He made himself say it plainly, no flourish, the way you'd present evidence. "Oyelaran. Kessarine river district. Dispersed east, under the Passage Compact. Four family members. Two sons."

The silence that followed was long enough that Sef heard a guard laugh at the far fire, heard the mules stamping, heard his own pulse. Petra didn't turn. When he finally spoke, his voice was level in a way that Sef understood now was effort, not ease.

"The page under the cart. Did you read it?"

So Ilse had known he'd find it — or Petra had. Or they'd both underestimated him equally. "Yes."

"Then you know the compact is real. You know the dispersals weren't migration. They were sentence." Petra turned then, and his eyes in the dusk were steady and very tired. "Say the rest of it. You came all this way; don't stop at the door."

"Your name is in the record of a country that doesn't exist," Sef said, "and you've spent nine days pretending you don't care what's in that chest, and I have been telling myself you're just a professional, and I don't believe it anymore." He ran out of prepared words and stood there breathing salt air. "Why didn't you tell me? I'm not Varn. I'm not Hale's people. I copied those names with my own hands and I would have — I don't know. Been careful with yours."

Something moved through Petra's face then, quick as the shadow of a bird — and Sef realized, with a strange lurch, that it was relief. Not the relief of a secret discovered, but the relief of a weight set down by someone else's hands.

"Do you know what it's like," Petra said quietly, "to be legible? To have your whole life written down somewhere by people who meant it as a sentence?" He looked back out at the salt. "For nine years I've been a shape. No history, no origin, no family — a tracker who came from nowhere, because nowhere was safer than anywhere. And then a clerk hires me to chase a map, and the map leads to a chest, and the chest has my mother's handwriting in it." He said it flatly, but his hand, hanging at his side, had closed into a fist. "She was a record-keeper's assistant. Did your entry say that? No. Entries don't say what people were. Just where they were sent."

"She hid the registers," Sef said slowly, assembling it. "In the annex. *Do not disperse.* She wanted them kept together."

"She wanted them kept *findable*." Petra's voice roughened. "There's a difference. A thing destroyed is gone. A thing hidden waits. She used to tell me a story when I was small — about a seal, a crest, a city the maps forgot — and I thought it was a story. I spent my whole life thinking it was a story." He laughed once, without any humor in it. "And then I knelt in the mud at a flooded riverbend and saw that seal stamped in bronze, and I knew it, Sef. First glance. I regretted knowing it before my heart finished its next beat."

Sef looked down at the salt pan, at all that blinding blankness, and understood suddenly why Petra had taught him light discipline, route planning, the reading of grass — why he'd tolerated a chattering young archivist trailing him across the hardest country in the world. Not patience. Recognition. Petra had seen a boy who loved records, and records were the thing that had eaten his family, and he'd taught him anyway, because —

"Because someone should know how to read them properly," Sef said aloud. "That's why you've been teaching me."

"Someone should know how to read them *carefully*," Petra corrected. "There's a difference there too."

They stood together at the edge of the white as full dark came down. Below them the salt held the last grey light like a bowl holds water. Somewhere south, if the scouts' reports were right, riders were shadowing the column — garrison cavalry, though nobody had said the commander's name aloud within Sef's hearing. Nobody had to. He'd seen Petra's face change at the ford, two days ago, watching the distant dust. He'd thought it was fear. Now he thought it was something older and heavier than fear: the face of a man seeing paperwork arrive.

"Ilse took the annex page," Sef said. "Her father ordered the sealed section returned unopened. She opened it. She's carrying it against him."

"I know."

"You *know*?"

"I inventory people the way you inventory documents," Petra said. "She started sleeping with her satchel buckled. She stopped meeting her quartermaster's eyes. Guilt has a stride length, same as anything else." He glanced sideways at Sef, and for the first time since Sef had met him there was something almost warm in it, dry as everything else about him but warm underneath. "You confronted me instead of blackmailing her, and you did it privately, and you gave me the chance to lie first. That's three better choices than most people make. So I'll give you one truth free of charge, archivist, and you can decide what it costs."

Sef waited.

"There are pages in that annex naming every dispersed family," Petra said. "Four hundred and eleven of them. Mine is one entry. There are four hundred and ten others, and every one of those families has descendants living right now in towns they were marched to, calling themselves by names that were given to them like prison numbers." He turned back toward the fires, toward the column, toward the road home that ran through six days of salt that would kill them all if he misjudged it by a single night. "Whatever happens with Varn and her father and the man holding my warrant — those pages get out. Not to a court. Courts can be bought; I've read the receipts. To the towns. To the people. A name you learn yourself is worth ten that get read to you."

"And the chest?" Sef asked. "Varn's orders are to bring it home intact. Her father's orders, anyway."

Petra was quiet for three steps. Then: "The desert teaches you one law above all the others, Sef. Water, blood, paper — it's all the same rule. What's hoarded kills. What's shared keeps people alive." He didn't say more than that. He didn't need to. Sef had learned to read him the way he'd learned to read the ledger: the meaning was in what was withheld.

At the fire, the guards were dealing cards by lantern light, and Ilse Varn stood apart at the edge of the glow, her satchel strap crossed over her chest like a bandolier, watching the dark southern horizon where the riders were. She looked, Sef thought, like a woman carrying something fragile through a crowd — and he understood that he was now one of perhaps three people alive who knew what it was.

Petra clapped him once on the shoulder, brief and hard, a gesture Sef had never received from him before.

"Sleep," Petra said. "Tomorrow we talk routes. Six days of salt ahead, and I'm going to need your memory more than you know — because if we're carrying four hundred and eleven families across those flats, we're not losing a single one of them twice."

He walked toward the fires, and Sef stood a moment longer at the edge of the ridge, looking south, where — very faint, at the limit of sight, like writing about to surface through water — there were lights. More of them than there had been yesterday.
