## 13. The Salt Corridor

Petra had been dreading this conversation for nine days, which was nine days longer than he usually allowed himself to dread anything.

He stood at the edge of the hardpan where it began its slow surrender to salt — a white crust over black mud that would hold a man's weight in the cold hours and swallow him whole by noon. Beyond it, the flats ran flat and blinding to a horizon that didn't so much end as dissolve. Somewhere out there, forty miles of nothing, was the corridor: a seam of firmer ground winding between the brine sinks like a vein through meat. He'd crossed it twice as a younger man, once going east with a survey crew whose names he'd let himself forget, once coming west alone with nothing but a water skin and a reason.

Nobody else alive knew the way. He'd made sure of that, back when making sure of things was the only skill he practiced.

Behind him, the expedition's camp sprawled across the last reliable ground — six wagons, twenty-three souls, the chest lashed under oilcloth on the lead cart like a coffin riding to a funeral nobody wanted to name. Ilse Varn stood at the camp's edge, arms folded, watching him watch the flats. She'd been doing that more lately. Watching him. He hadn't decided yet whether that was good or very bad.

"You've been standing there since before dawn," she said.

"Checking my memory against the ground."

"And?"

"The ground hasn't changed." He turned. "I have some bad news about the route home."

Her jaw tightened in that way he'd learned meant she was filing something rather than feeling it. "Say it."

"There are two ways back to the river towns. The northern track adds eleven days and crosses the Kessel escarpment, which is garrison territory now — Hale will have riders on it if he doesn't already. And there's the corridor." He nodded at the white. "Straight through the flats. Four days, maybe five, if everything goes right."

"But."

"But the corridor closes." He crouched and pressed two fingers into the crust at his feet. It gave, soft as old bread. "This is what happens when the heat comes up from under. By midday the surface turns to soup with a lid on it. Right now we can walk it at night and rest on the rises during the day. In six days — seven at the outside — the dry season seals it. Not slowly. All at once. One morning you step out and the flats simply stop being land."

Ilse looked past him at all that whiteness, and for a moment he saw her do the arithmetic he'd already done: eleven days north with cavalry behind them, or four nights across ground that would kill anyone who took a wrong turn.

"You know the way," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I know *a* way. There isn't another one."

"How do you navigate it? At night? There's no—"

"There are markers." He straightened, brushed the salt from his fingers. "Old ones. Stakes driven before either of us was born, when people still crossed here for reasons worth dying over. Most have fallen. Some have drifted. You find them by the way the wind scours around them, and by the color of the crust where the ground underneath runs firm, and by a hundred small things I'm not going to list for you because you don't need to know them and I don't want you thinking you do."

She studied him. "So we're trusting you completely."

"Yes."

"And if you die out there?"

"Then everyone should lie down next to me and save themselves the thirst."

Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile, but the place where one would go if she were a different woman. "You could have led with that. Instead you let me stand here while you stared at mud."

"I wanted to be sure before I said it out loud." He met her eyes. "Ilse. Once we're on the corridor, there's no turning back. Not because of pride. Because the entry ground won't hold a wagon's weight coming the other direction after three days of sun. We go forward or we don't go."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then: "How much water does the crossing take?"

"Four days' ration per person, plus a reserve. We'll need to cut down at the ford before we enter — fill everything, drink heavy, dump anything that isn't water, weapons, or the chest."

"The guards won't like dumping gear."

"The guards will like drowning in salt less. Tell them however you want. But it happens tonight, and we march at moonrise."

* * *

The argument came anyway, because arguments always come, and Petra had learned that the ones you win quickly are the ones that come back later wearing knives.

It happened at the fire, which was less a fire than a suggestion of one — a handful of scrub burning low and blue in a pit dug below the windline. Corvin, the senior guard, a broad man with a face like a slammed door, did the talking while the others watched their boots.

"Four days on rations," Corvin said. "Half measures. And you're telling me to leave the spare axle, the tool chest, half our grain."

"I'm telling you to leave anything that isn't going to keep someone breathing."

"And if a wagon breaks?"

"It won't."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one the desert gives." Petra kept his voice level. He'd learned long ago that authority wasn't volume; it was the refusal to blink first. "Corridor rules are simple. Everyone carries. Everyone drinks on schedule, not on thirst. Nobody wanders off the line, even ten paces, even to piss. If the ground sounds hollow under you, you freeze and call out. That's all of it. Break any of those and you don't just kill yourself — you pull the rest of us in after you, because somebody will try to save you."

"And who put you in charge?"

"Nobody. That's the point." He let the silence sit. "Out there there's no magistrate, no warrant, no company charter. Just the flats and whoever's still standing when they're done with us. You can follow me or you can take the northern track alone. I'll draw you a map."

Corvin's eyes narrowed. "You'd split the column."

"I'd give any man his choice. That's more freedom than most places offer." He glanced around the fire. "But understand what choosing alone means. Eleven extra days. Escarpment passes that Hale's outriders can cover with thirty men. Water holes that may or may not still be there. I'm not selling the corridor because it's easy. I'm selling it because it's the only road where nobody's waiting for us."

A young guard near the back — Tam, the one who'd taken the arrow graze at the ford and hadn't complained once — spoke without raising his hand. "Hale's still following us?"

"He was two days back. On the corridor he can't follow. Cavalry can't cross the sinks, and he knows it. That's why he'll try to stop us before moonrise, and why we're leaving before moonrise."

That landed. Petra watched it move through them — the shift from *this guide is asking too much* to *this guide knows something we don't*, which was the only currency he'd ever really carried.

Corvin held out a while longer, mostly on principle. Then Sef, sitting cross-legged at the fire's edge with his ledger open on his knees, said quietly, "The thief crossed these flats. East to west. Alone. In high summer."

Every head turned.

"His journal says so," Sef went on, unbothered. "Page nine. He writes about the stakes — the markers — and how his grandfather described them to him. He calls the corridor *the provision*. As in, a provision made for the passage." He closed the book. "If a man with no water sense and no escort could read his grandfather's notes well enough to make it across, then a guide who's actually walked it can get twenty-three of us home."

Petra felt something twist under his ribs. He'd read that journal too, huddled over it by lamplight while the others slept, and he'd missed — or refused — the word. *Provision.* The dead man hadn't just been following a map. He'd been following a promise somebody had made, generations ago, that the way home would stay open.

And now here was Petra, the only living soul who knew the way, being asked to keep that promise for strangers.

"All right," Corvin said at last, heavily, like a man setting down weight he intended to pick back up later. "We dump the gear. But I'm carrying the axe."

"Carry two," Petra said. "You're the biggest one here."

Someone laughed — Tam, probably — and the tension broke the way ice breaks, all at once and along a clean line. Petra stepped back from the fire and let them argue about pack weights, because arguing about pack weights was a healthy thing for frightened people to do, and walked to the edge of the light.

Ilse was there, of course. She was always there, at the edges, doing her quiet accounting.

"They'll follow you now," she said.

"For four days."

"And after?"

"After, they'll follow me until there's somewhere else to follow." He shrugged. "That's how it works. You're useful until you're not, and then you're a story."

"That's a bleak way to see it."

"It's an accurate way to see it." He looked at her. "You've kept me on this expedition past every point where you needed me. I've been wondering why."

She didn't answer right away. Out on the flats, the last light was draining off the salt, and the world was turning the color of old bone.

"Because everyone else in my life is paid to agree with me," she said finally. "You're paid to disagree, and you do it for free. It's the closest thing to honesty I can buy." She turned away before he could decide whether that was a confession or a deflection. "Moonrise, Oyelaran. Don't be late for your own miracle."

* * *

They entered the corridor two hours after dark, in single file, wagon wheels wrapped in hide to muffle the sound — though sound hardly mattered out here; the flats swallowed noise the way they swallowed everything else. The moon was three-quarters full and generous, laying a silver road across the white, and Petra walked at the head of the column with a staff in one hand and his whole attention spread out ahead of him like a net.

This was the part no one could help him with. This was his alone.

He read the ground the way other men read letters. A faint darkening in the crust where subsurface moisture firmed the mud: safe. A patch of pure white, powdery, rising in little feathered curls at the edges: brine sink, death, go wide. A low ridge of crusted rise barely taller than a boot: the bones of an old levee, planted by hands two centuries gone, running true northwest for as far as the moon could show. He steered by these and by the stakes — most fallen, one still standing, a black finger of petrified wood leaning east like a man listening — and every hundred paces he checked his bearing against a star he'd trusted since he was twelve years old and had never once told anyone about.

Behind him the column moved in silence. Twenty-two sets of feet on firming ground. He counted them by sound without meaning to; counting had become a habit of survival so deep it had worn grooves in him. Once, early in the second hour, a wheel found soft crust and sank to the hub, and the whole line froze while six men lifted the wagon out by main strength, nobody speaking above a whisper, and Petra stood with his heart hammering and his face showing nothing, because his face showing nothing was also part of the job.

Sef fell into step beside him during the third hour's rest, on one of the firm rises where they'd halt through the worst of the midday heat.

"You haven't slept," Sef said.

"I'll sleep when we're across."

"You said that at the ford."

"And I was right. We got across."

Sef sat down beside him, close enough to share warmth, far enough to be polite about it. For a while he said nothing, just watched the moonlight lie on the salt like frost on a field. Then: "Can I ask you something you won't answer?"

"That's your favorite kind of question."

"When this is over. When we're home and the chest is whatever it's going to be. What happens to you?" Sef kept his eyes forward. "I've been thinking about it. Every route we've taken, you've chosen the one with no witnesses and no records. You navigate at night. You know the desert better than the maps do, and the maps clearly don't know it at all. You're not hiding *from* the desert, Petra. You're hiding in it."

Petra was quiet long enough that the wind filled the space between them, hissing faintly across the crust.

"My grandmother used to say," Sef went on, "that a person can run so long the running becomes the house they live in. And that the saddest thing she ever saw was a man tearing down his own house because he couldn't remember why he'd built it."

"Your grandmother sounds like she charged by the proverb."

"She did. Family rates." Sef smiled, small and crooked, and didn't push. He just got up, brushed the salt from his knees, and said, "Fourth hour. Corvin wants to know if the line holds together or if we rest longer."

"We move," Petra said. "The ground's best in the next two hours. After that it starts getting soft in the low stretches, and I want the sinks behind us before they wake up."

Sef nodded and went back down the line, and Petra sat alone on the rise with the whole white world breathing around him, and admitted the thing he'd been not-admitting since the riverbend.

He didn't want to disappear anymore.

Nine years he'd built his life out of absence — no name spoken aloud, no debt unpaid, no trail warm behind him. And it had worked, the way a locked room works: perfectly, and only as long as you never wanted out. But somewhere between the flooded bank and this moonlit nothing, the arithmetic had changed. These people — the archivist with his dead grandmother's proverbs, the guard with his axe, the magistrate's daughter buying honesty at retail — these people were depending on him to know the way, and knowing the way had started to feel less like a burden and more like a purpose, and purpose was the one thing he'd sworn off, because purpose left tracks.

For the first time in nine years, moving forward and staying hidden pointed the same direction. He intended to walk that line until it forked.

He rose, tested the crust with his staff — firm, singing faintly under the iron-shod tip, the good sound — and raised one arm. The column stirred, gathered itself, and followed him down onto the salt, twenty-two shadows strung out behind one, silent as a procession in a dream.

They made the far rim of the sink country an hour before dawn, and Petra allowed himself one breath of something almost like relief.

Then he looked south, where the horizon had been clear all night, and saw the light.

Not dawn. Dawn was hours off and came gray from the east. This was a smudge in the southwest, low and bruise-colored, and it was moving, and he had seen it twice before in his life and both times it had rewritten the landscape underneath it.

"Sef," he called softly. "Wake Varn. Wake everyone."

"What is it?"

Petra watched the smudge grow teeth. "Storm," he said. "Salt storm. Off the flats behind us — it's eating the corridor we just crossed." He turned to face the column, and his voice, when it came, was flatter and harder than he'd ever let it be in front of them. "In a few hours there is going to be no sky, no ground, and no moon. Whatever I tell you to do when it hits, you do it instantly and you do it exactly, because in a whiteout the only thing that survives is the thing that stays tied to the rope."

Above them, the stars were already starting to go out, one by one, from the south.
