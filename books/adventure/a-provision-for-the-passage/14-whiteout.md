## 14. Whiteout

The sky went wrong an hour before the wind arrived.

Ilse noticed it first as a color — the horizon over the flats turning the pale, bruised yellow of old paper, as if the whole world were a document left too long in the sun. Petra had stopped mid-stride at the head of the column and stood with his head cocked, listening to nothing she could hear.

"Salt storm," he said. "Coming out of the northwest."

"How long do we have?"

"Less than you'd want." He turned to the column behind them, twenty-two souls and four carts, and his voice changed — flattened, stripped of everything conversational. "Rope up. Everyone ties to the person ahead. Carts get lashed together or they'll be in another province by nightfall. If we scatter, we die scattered."

The expedition had learned, in nine days of marching, to move when Petra's voice took that shape. Ilse watched the guards fumble for their lines, watched Sef knot a rope around a terrified cartographer's waist with quick, sure hands, and felt the particular helplessness of command discovering its own limits. She could buy men, paper them with authority, promise them triple pay and imperial protection. She could not make the air hold still.

"We need shelter," she said. "There's nothing out here."

"There's one thing." Petra pointed north-northeast, at a low white swell on the horizon that might have been a dune if dunes grew in salt. "Old lake bed. The water used to cut banks before it died. There'll be an undercut somewhere along the rim — deep enough to crouch in, shallow enough not to flood when the salt comes down. Maybe half a mile."

"You've sheltered there before?"

"I've crossed this country eleven times." He said it without pride, the way a man reports the weather. "I know where its bones are."

They made the half-mile in twenty minutes that felt like two hours. The wind reached them while they were still walking — not a gust but a pressure, a hand laid flat against the entire column, shoving. Then the light failed all at once, noon collapsing into a brown twilight, and the storm came down on them like the end of something.

Ilse got her first mouthful of salt before she found the bank. It tasted of the sea and of blood, fine as flour, working instantly into her eyes, her teeth, the seams of her gloves. Someone was shouting orders — Petra, close beside her, his hand hard on her shoulder strap, hauling her down and forward into a hollow beneath the dead shoreline. The undercut was barely three feet high. They crawled into it, and the world became a roar.

* * *

They crouched in the dark for what the pocket-watch in her coat told her was four hours, though her body insisted on a full day. The storm did not howl so much as grind — a vast, patient abrasion, the desert sanding itself down grain by grain. Salt hissed past the lip of the overhang in sheets. When Ilse put her hand outside, the wind tried to take the skin off her knuckles.

They were alone in the hollow. That was the arithmetic of survival: the undercut would hold perhaps six people comfortably, and Petra had filled it with the weakest — Sef, the cartographer Voss, two guards with bad lungs, and then, when the last rope nearly tore free of a guard's grip, he had gone back into the whiteout himself and brought the man in bodily. Ilse had ended up wedged at the far end of the hollow with Petra because there was nowhere else, and because he had put everyone else ahead of himself without appearing to think about it at all.

Now the others slept in shifts, drugged by exhaustion, backs pressed together for warmth. And Ilse sat with her knees drawn up, watching the shape of the guide who was not simply a guide, and turned over in her mind the thing she had carried for six days against her ribs.

She had read half the annex.

Not all of it. She had meant to open it once, at night, alone — just to know what her father wanted kept from even her, just to hold the fact of her own disobedience like a stone in her pocket — and then she had not been able to stop. Testimony pages, sworn statements, a census fragment. Enough to understand that Kessarine had not fallen. It had been *dismantled* — its treaties voided by fraud, its granaries bought and burned, its people dispersed under an order bearing a signature she had known since childhood, since the documents her father signed at his desk while she sat on the floor doing sums.

She had repacked the annex exactly as she'd found it. She had kept one page. And she had told no one, which made her, she understood, a liar of exactly the kind she had spent her life despising in her father's ministry: the kind whose lie is silence.

The storm gave her cover. That was the ugly truth of why she spoke now — the roar made confession cheap, made it deniable, made it almost private. Almost.

"Petra."

He didn't startle. He had been dozing sitting up, arms on knees, and now his eyes opened in the dark and found her. "Storm's got hours yet."

"I know. I want to talk while it can't hear us."

He said nothing. Waiting. He was very good at waiting; she had noticed it the first day, the way he let silences do work other men needed words for.

"The chest," she said. "You've been asking yourself what's in it. You have the right to ask. I'm going to tell you part of the answer, and I'm going to watch your face while I do, because I need to know something about you."

"That's fair," he said. "I'll want the same trade."

"Then we're negotiating." She pulled her coat tighter. The cold in the hollow was the strange cold of the flats, dry and total. "My father sent me for that chest with specific instructions. Bring it home sealed. Open nothing. He said it was survey records — old boundary claims, worthless except as precedent. He lied to me. I opened it anyway, six nights ago, while the camp slept."

Petra's breathing didn't change. But something in the set of his shoulders did — a fractional settling, a man bracing a load he'd already estimated.

"What did you find?" he asked.

"Testimony. Sworn statements, dated across thirty years. A census ledger. And a sealed annex my father ordered returned unopened — which is the only reason I believe the rest of it, because a man doesn't seal a lie." She heard her own voice go flat and ministerial and hated it, and pushed through anyway. "Kessarine wasn't conquered, Petra. It was dissolved. Deliberately. Its treaties were voided on forged grounds, its food supply was engineered into famine, and its population was dispersed under a legal order so the nation would stop existing on paper before it stopped existing anywhere else. My father's office signed the dispersal order."

The storm ground on. Somewhere behind them in the dark, Sef murmured in his sleep.

"That's half," Petra said quietly. "You said half."

"Half of what I know. Half of what I'm willing to say out loud." She turned to face him fully. In the brown darkness she could see only the outline of him, which made it easier. "Here is what I need to know. When I tell the rest — when I decide what to *do* with the rest — are you a man who sells what he learns? Because I can pay you more than anyone else will. Or are you a man who keeps things? Because I've watched you keep things for nine days. You keep them carefully. Like a man who knows what secrets cost."

A long pause. The wind shrieked past the lip of the overhang, and grit pattered down onto them like slow rain.

"You're testing me," he said.

"I'm hiring the rest of you," she said. "The part nobody's paid for yet."

Another pause. Then, low, almost amused: "All right. Fair trade, like you said." He shifted, and she heard him settle his back against the salt-hardened bank. "You want to know what I keep. Here's one thing I keep: there's a warrant with my name on it. Garrison warrant. Arson. Nine years old. I burned a garrison records house to the ground, and I ran, and I've been running since. Your Commander Hale — the cavalry shadowing us — held that warrant. He's not following the chest. He's following me."

Ilse went very still.

Of all the confessions she had imagined extracting from this man — smuggling, desertion, a dead partner, stolen pay — arson in service of what, she had never guessed. And Hale. She knew Hale's name from her father's correspondence; he was a reliable instrument, a man who executed warrants cleanly. Her father had *sent* him. Which meant her father knew Petra Oyelaran was on this expedition, or had learned it fast, and had decided the guide was worth recovering alongside the chest.

Or instead of the chest.

"Why did you burn it?" she asked. It was the only question that mattered, and they both knew it. A man who burns buildings is a criminal. A man who burns certain buildings is something else.

Petra was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. When he did, his voice had gone somewhere she hadn't heard it go before — down, careful, like a man stepping onto ice.

"My mother was Kessarine," he said. "Dispersed line. She married out, changed her name, never talked about it except once — a story, when I was small, about a seal on a bronze box, and she made me swear I'd forget it. I forgot it for thirty years. Then the river gave that chest back, and I saw the seal, and I remembered." A breath. "The garrison kept local copies of dispersal records. I don't know what I intended the night I walked toward the records house with a lamp. I know what I did. I set the fire. Whether I meant to, whether my mother asked me to on her deathbed — those are questions I've been answering differently every year for nine years. The fire happened. The warrant is real. Everything else is weather."

*Two liars*, Ilse thought. *Trading partial truths.* Because she had given him testimony without the page in her pocket, and he had given her the fire without the deathbed, and both of them knew the shape of what was withheld, and neither of them flinched from it. And yet —

And yet she found, examining herself in the grinding dark, that she trusted him *more*. Not because the truths were complete, but because they were expensive. He had paid for his silence with nine years. She had paid for hers with six days of a stone against her ribs. Liars who pay that much are not liars anymore; they are custodians.

"There's a name in the ledger," she said. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "In the census of dispersed families. I looked for it before I knew why I was looking. Oyelaran."

The silence between them stretched so long that the storm seemed to fill it entirely, roaring, erasing.

"I know," Petra said finally.

"You — " She stopped. Started again. "How could you possibly know?"

"Because Sef showed me a page. Three nights ago. Your page, Ilse. The one you took from the annex." His tone held no accusation, which was somehow worse. "He found it in my pack. I let him believe he'd found it by accident. He matched the handwriting to the ledger entries. My family is listed. Dispersed east, two generations back. So whatever you're carrying against your ribs, you should know the boy has already read it, and I have already survived it, and neither of us told you — which makes three liars in this hollow, and I count that as a company."

Ilse laughed. It escaped her before she could govern it — a short, cracked sound, swallowed immediately by the wind. Three liars. An expedition built on salvage papers and triple pay, and underneath it, three people keeping versions of the same truth warm between them like coals.

"The annex," she said. "There's more than testimony. There's enough to indict an office, maybe a ministry. If it reaches the right tribunal — a civilian one, frontier jurisdiction, outside the imperial courts my father owns —"

"Then whoever carries it becomes the most important person alive," Petra said, "and the most hunted. I know how that works. I've been the hunted thing for nine years. It's survivable. Barely. But you should choose it with your eyes open, because once you speak it, you can't unspeak it. The truth is like the fire. It happened. Everything after is weather."

The storm began, very slowly, to change its note — the grinding pitch dropping, the pressure easing off their chests. Hours yet, Petra had said, but the desert was already deciding to forgive them.

Ilse reached into her coat, and her fingers found the folded page, and for a moment she almost drew it out and handed it to him there in the dark — finished, surrendered, done with custody. Then she thought of her father's desk, and the signature moving across paper like a spider, and she closed her hand around the page again.

"Not yet," she said. "When we're off the flats. When there's a tribunal to hear it and witnesses who can't be bought. I'll give you everything then — the annex, the page, all of it. I'm telling you now so you can hold me to it."

"I will," Petra said. "Hold you to it, I mean. That's a provision I'll enforce."

Above the lip of the overhang, the brown light was thinning toward something almost gray. From deeper in the hollow came the sound of Sef waking — a sharp intake of breath, the rustle of a boy checking that his satchel was still against his body. And then, cutting through the dying wind from the direction they had come, faint but unmistakable:

A whistle. Three notes, repeated. Cavalry signal.

Petra was on his feet before Ilse had finished drawing breath, head tilted, listening, and when he spoke his voice had gone flat and cold as the flats themselves.

"They're not waiting for the ford anymore. They've found us in the open."
