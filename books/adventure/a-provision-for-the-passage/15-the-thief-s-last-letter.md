## 15. The Thief's Last Letter

They buried the thief where the river had found him, under a cairn of stones that would be gone by the next flood. Sef had helped carry the rocks. It had felt like the only honest work anyone had done in weeks.

The dead man's effects went into a canvas satchel that Ilse ordered sealed and catalogued, because Ilse ordered everything sealed and catalogued now — she had changed since the annex, or maybe since before it, and nobody said so out loud. The satchel rode in the second cart between the water barrels and the spare axle, and for six days Sef told himself he wasn't thinking about it.

He was an archivist. He was, in fact, the only archivist on this expedition, which meant that by every professional logic he owned, those effects were his jurisdiction. A dead man's papers were records. Records were his.

On the seventh night, camped in the lee of a basalt ridge with the wind combing grit off its shoulders, he opened the satchel.

* * *

Petra was on first watch. That mattered. Sef had learned to read the man's silences the way you learn to read weather — Petra's watch meant the camp breathed easier, meant no one checked the perimeter twice, meant Sef could sit by the low fire with a leather folder open on his knees and not have to explain himself.

The effects were pitiful. That was the first thing. He'd expected tools, maybe — lockpicks, forged seals, the kit of a professional. Instead: a water skin patched three times. A folding knife worn down to a sliver of steel. A wooden comb with two teeth missing. And paper.

Four sheets, folded small, carried so long the creases had gone soft as cloth. One was the map — or what remained of it, the route from the garrison archive to the riverbend, annotated in a cramped hand with water sources Sef now recognized from having drunk at them. Three were letters. Two were unfinished drafts, abandoned mid-sentence, crossed out and begun again. The third was finished. It was addressed to no one, which Sef understood immediately was the point. It was addressed to whoever came.

He read it once by firelight. Then he moved closer to the coals and read it again, slowly, the way he'd been trained to read anything that might matter later — because the mind lies about what it saw the first time, and documents deserve better than a single witness.

*To whoever is reading this,*

*You followed me. I'm sorry for making you walk so far, but I couldn't make it easy, because easy things get turned back. If you are reading this then you got past the caches, which means you were serious, which means someone finally took the bait.*

*I need you to understand that I stole nothing.*

*My grandfather was Tamas Reval, last record-keeper of Kessarine. When they dissolved the nation — and it was dissolved, look for the word "dissolved" in your imperial archives and see how gently it sits there — he was given a choice. Sign the transfer of the civic records to the magistrate's office in Varnhold, or be dispersed with the third resettlement cohort. He signed. Everyone signed. Then he spent forty years telling my mother that signing was the worst thing a keeper can do, worse than burning, because burning at least leaves ash and a story about fire.*

*Before he died he gave me one thing: the location of the chest. Not the chest itself — he didn't have it, it had already gone into the river country with the last courier, and he'd spent his whole life not knowing whether it survived. But he knew where it would have been taken, and he knew that someday the river would give it back, because rivers always do, rivers are the only honest archivists left.*

*I am sixty-one years old. I have waited thirty years for the flood season to run high enough. Last spring it did. I went to the garrison, I took the map, I left the coin on the desk because I did not want them hunting a thief, I wanted them hunting a map, and maps get followed. I walked slow. I cached water where a smart tracker would find it and bad water where a stupid one would drink. I wanted to be followed slowly enough that whoever came would arrive after the flood, when the chest stood exposed on the bank like a debt the river was calling in.*

*I did not plan to drown. I want that recorded too. The bank gave way. I had the chest rope in my hand and the mud took my feet and the current took the rest. If you're reading this, you pulled me out and buried me, and I thank you, though I'd have thanked you more for opening the chest first and burying me after.*

*Inside the chest is everything my grandfather kept. Names. Orders. The signatures of the men who dismantled nine hundred years of a people with ink instead of armies, because ink doesn't cost anything and armies do. My grandfather believed records are a provision for passage — that a nation isn't land, it's the memory of itself, and as long as the memory exists somewhere, the nation can cross any distance, even the distance between being and having been.*

*He also believed someone would come. He was right. It just took him dying, and me dying, and thirty years of high water.*

*Whoever you are: don't let them seal it again. Don't let them catalogue it into a drawer. Read it out loud. Records only live in mouths.*

*Tamas Reval's grandson,
Aldric Reval*

Sef sat very still.

The fire had burned down to a red eye in its ring of stones. Somewhere behind him a guard coughed in her sleep. He became aware that his hands were shaking, and he looked at them with a kind of clinical detachment, as if they belonged to someone else — some other archivist, in some other life, who hadn't just discovered that the entire journey he'd been on had been designed before he was born into it.

The crime was a summons. The theft was an invitation written in the only language the empire's offices would bother to answer: property. Aldric Reval had known that no petition would work, no testimony, no old man standing in a magistrate's hall saying *my grandfather kept the records of a country you erased.* They'd have fed him to the same paperwork that ate Kessarine. But a stolen map? A map was an asset. Assets got pursued. Pursuit got boots on the riverbank, and boots opened chests.

He had made himself into bait and died holding the line anyway.

* * *

"You've been sitting there a long time."

Sef flinched hard enough to nearly knock the folder into the coals. Petra dropped onto the rock beside him, unhurried, rifle across his knees, and glanced at the open pages without reaching for them. He'd had six days to snoop and never had. Sef understood now that this wasn't indifference. It was discipline. The man treated other people's secrets like unmarked wells.

"I opened the effects," Sef said. "I know I should have asked Ilse—"

"She'd have said wait until Varnhold." Petra's voice was flat. "She'd have been wrong."

"There's a letter. From the thief." Sef turned the page toward the firelight, toward Petra, offering it. "His name was Aldric Reval. His grandfather was Kessarine's last record-keeper."

Something moved through Petra's face — quick, deep, gone. Sef had seen it before, at the riverbend, when the chest's seal came out of the mud. Recognition, and regret at recognizing.

"Read it," Petra said.

So Sef read it aloud, quietly, while the camp slept and the wind worked at the ridge. He watched Petra listen. The ranger didn't interrupt, didn't ask questions, but at certain lines his jaw tightened — at *dissolved*, at *dispersed with the third resettlement cohort*, at *ink doesn't cost anything*. When Sef reached the end and the signature, Petra was silent for a long moment.

"Reval," he said finally. "That name was in the ledger too. The dispersal lists." He looked at the fire. "Whole families. Pages of them."

"You've read the ledger?"

"I've read enough." Which was Petra for *yes, and I'm not ready to tell you the rest*, and Sef had learned to accept that currency. They traded in partial truths, the two of them. It was, strangely, an honest arrangement.

"He drowned," Sef said. "It says so. The bank gave way. He wasn't murdered — everyone assumed—" He stopped. Everyone had assumed, including Sef, including, he suspected, Ilse, who had looked at the body with the particular wariness of a woman calculating who else might come looking for what the dead man carried. "He was sixty-one years old and he'd been walking toward this for thirty years, and the river killed him fifty meters short of finishing it."

"Rivers don't care about finishing things," Petra said. "That's why he trusted one with the chest. No agenda. Just return."

Sef heard something in that — a private frequency, a meaning folded inside the plain words — and filed it away without pressing. He was getting good at that. Out here you learned that pressing people was like pressing on hardpan: it held, until the day it split and swallowed you.

"He wanted to be followed," Sef said. "Slowly. The caches, the misdirection — Petra, I tracked parts of that trail myself, before we joined you. The false cache at the dry fork. The backtrack at the scarp. I thought he was evading pursuit. He was *curating* it. He was choosing his readers."

"A summons," Petra said.

"Yes." Sef looked down at the letter. "And we answered it. All of us. Every person on this expedition — Ilse with her salvage papers, the guards with their pay, me with my catalogues, you with your—" he gestured vaguely at all of Petra, "—your way of knowing exactly where water is. We're not a recovery operation. We're a reply."

Petra was quiet. Then, in a voice lower than the wind: "Thirty years. He waited thirty years for someone to take the bait, and the someone turned out to be a magistrate's daughter hauling a claim of ownership and a commander with cavalry at her back." He shook his head slowly. "If I'd written that letter, I'd have specified. *Send an archivist. Send no one with a signature to protect.*"

"Maybe he did specify," Sef said. "Maybe the drafts say it. There are two others — unfinished. He kept starting over." He smoothed the soft-creased pages. "That's the part that gets me. Not the plan. The revisions. He wrote this letter more than once. He cared how it sounded. A man planning his own death as a message, and he still revised."

"That's a record-keeper's grandson for you." Something almost like a smile touched Petra's mouth, and didn't stay. "Drafts are grief with the emotion taken out. You revise until you can hold the thing without bleeding on it."

"Do you speak from experience?"

"I speak from knowing people who write things down." Petra rose, shouldered the rifle. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we hit the salt corridor approach, and after that nobody reads anything by firelight — there won't be fire."

He walked the perimeter line, a dark shape against darker stone, and Sef watched him go and felt the letter rearrange everything it touched.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat with the drafts instead, because an archivist's grief is done with gloves on, working.

The first draft was angrier. *They will call me a thief,* it began, and then crossed out *thief* and wrote above it, in letters pressed so hard the paper nearly tore, *I am a courier. I am delivering thirty years late.* The draft broke off after a paragraph about his grandfather's hands — how they'd shaken signing the transfer, how the shake wasn't fear but fury, fury with nowhere legal to go.

The second draft was shorter and colder, all logistics: dates, distances, the flood-year calculations, instructions for verifying the chest's contents against a manifest sewn into the lining of the lid. At the bottom, alone, a single sentence that had survived every revision:

*If the chest reaches an office instead of an open air, I have failed, and so have you.*

Sef copied that sentence into his field notebook, word for word, and then sat looking at his own handwriting and understanding that he had just become part of the chain of custody. That was what an archivist was, really — not a keeper of drawers but a link in a passage. Aldric Reval's grandfather had passed the location to Aldric; Aldric had passed the chest to the river; the river had passed it to the bank; the bank to Petra; Petra to the expedition. And now the letter had passed to Sef, who was carrying it toward whatever came next, and the next link after him was not a drawer. He would make sure of that if it was the last professional act of his life.

Near dawn, Ilse emerged from her tent, hair braided tight, eyes already moving over the camp the way her father's clerks must have moved over ledgers. She saw Sef by the dead fire. She saw the folder.

Her step slowed. For a moment — Sef would remember this precisely — she looked less like a magistrate's daughter than like a woman standing at a door she both needed and dreaded to open.

"You went through the effects," she said. Not a question.

"He was an archivist's problem," Sef said. "I'm the archivist."

"And?"

Sef thought about the annex, about the page she'd pocketed and the secret she was carrying, about the economy of partial truths that held this expedition together like stitching holds a seam. He thought about Aldric Reval revising his death until he could hold it.

"He left a letter," Sef said. "For whoever came. He wanted to be found. The theft was a summons — he stole the map so someone would follow it to the chest." He held her gaze. "And he left one instruction that survived every draft. If the chest ends up sealed in an office, he failed. So did we."

Ilse stood very still. The eastern sky was going grey behind her, and the salt flats beyond the ridge were beginning to show themselves — six days of white silence, the only road home, closing a little more with every hour of sun.

"My father's orders were explicit," she said at last, quietly. "Return the contents unopened."

"I know."

"Then one of us is about to become a criminal, Sef. Again." Her mouth twisted around the word. "Or maybe we've been criminals since the riverbend, and the only question left is whose crime we're committing — hers, his, or ours."

Behind them, from the direction of the flats, Petra's voice cut the morning: "Pack up. Storm light on the horizon. We march for the corridor today, or we don't march home at all."

Ilse closed her eyes for half a breath. When she opened them, she held out her hand, palm up, and Sef placed the letter in it — all three drafts, the whole revised grief of Aldric Reval — and watched the magistrate's daughter fold a dead man's summons into her coat, over her heart, next to the page she'd already stolen.

"Read it aloud when the time comes," Sef said. "Records only live in mouths."

She nodded once, and turned toward the white horizon, and the expedition began to break camp around them like a wound closing — except that nothing, Sef understood now, was closing. Everything they carried was opening, one sealed thing at a time, and somewhere out on the salt, riders were waiting to decide what it would cost.
