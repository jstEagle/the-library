## 18. Fire on the Flats

The canyon mouth was a throat of red stone, and Hale filled it.

I counted them from the ridge while Ilse argued behind me — forty riders, maybe fifty, drawn up in two ranks with the morning sun at their backs so that every lance point threw a needle of light. Behind them, supply wagons. Behind those, a second column still coming down from the pass. Hale hadn't ridden out to negotiate. He'd ridden out to end it.

"He's asking for parley," Sef said beside me, glass to his eye. "One man under flag."

"Me," I said.

"You don't have to go."

I took the glass from him. Found the flag, white rag on a spear, and the man beneath it sitting his horse like he owned the ground the horse stood on. Commander Dain Hale. Nine years I had built my life around not seeing that silhouette up close, and here it was, close as a hand on my shoulder.

"I know," I said, and went down.

* * *

He met me at the canyon floor, between the lines, close enough that I could see the gray in his beard now and the same campaign medal he'd worn the day the garrison burned. He looked older. He looked tired. He looked like a man who had carried a piece of paper for nine years and finally gotten tired of carrying it.

"Oyelaran." He said my name like a key turning in a lock. "You look well. Running agrees with you."

"Hale."

"I'll be brief, because you were never a man for ceremony." He gestured back toward our camp, where the chest sat under guard among the wagons, bronze-bound and stamped with a seal that half these soldiers would not recognize and all of their paymasters did. "Amnesty. Full pardon, countersigned by the magistrate himself. The fire, the warrant, the nine years — gone. You walk away clean. In exchange: the chest, the ledger, everything inside it, delivered intact to Varn's office. And you ride east and never come back, which I understand is your natural direction anyway."

"And the others?"

"The expedition goes free. Every one of them. Even the magistrate's daughter — her father will smooth whatever feathers get ruffled." He smiled without warmth. "Everyone walks, Oyelaran. Except you're walking free instead of walking ahead of a rope. That's the offer. It doesn't improve."

Behind him, forty horses shifted. Ahead of me, behind me, the people who had voted in the dark over a hoarded waterskin, who had marched a salt corridor on my word alone, who had refused — yesterday, at the ford, when it cost them a day and Maro's arm — to give me up. I thought about how easy this was supposed to be. Take the paper. Walk away. Nine years of wanting exactly this, handed to me on a cavalryman's glove.

"You've read what's in the chest," I said.

His face didn't move. That was the answer.

"I've read orders," he said. "For twenty-six years I've read orders, and the ones I didn't care for, I read again until I did. What's in that chest is paper, Oyelaran. Old paper. Paper doesn't feed garrisons or hold borders. Paper burns." Something flickered behind his eyes then, quick as a lizard. "Which reminds me. You'd know."

"That's why you want it burned yourself."

"That's why I want it *delivered*," he said, too fast, too smooth, and we both heard the truth underneath it. He wanted it out of the world. His signature was in the annex — I'd seen Ilse's face when she told me, seen her hands shake around a page she'd stolen from her own father's inventory. Hale's name was in there too. Countersigning dispersals. Countersigning a famine.

"No," I said.

Hale exhaled through his nose. "No?"

"No." And the strange thing, the thing I would carry for the rest of my life, was how quiet it came out. Not defiance. Just a door closing. "Nine years ago I ran because running was all I had. I'm not running anymore. And I'm not trading everyone in that ledger for a piece of paper with your name on it."

"Then you've killed them all," Hale said, almost gently. "Because if you won't give it up, I take it. And people die taking things from soldiers."

"I know," I said. "So do I."

I turned and walked back toward my own line, and I did not run, though every part of me that remembered how to survive screamed at me to.

* * *

It happened faster than I could have planned, which is the only reason it worked.

Hale gave us an hour. An hour to surrender the chest, he said, before he took it and apologized to no one. Ilse spent the hour writing — she wrote three copies of something, sealed them, and pressed one into Sef's hands with instructions I didn't hear. Maro, his arm bound in a sling of cartographer's linen, checked the guards' weapons twice. And I sat with the chest open in the shade of a wagon and looked at what we were about to lose.

The records of Kessarine. A nation no atlas remembered, dismantled so thoroughly that its own descendants had forgotten they were descended from anything. Testimony. Treaties signed in fraud. A famine ordered into being because a dispersed people are a governed people, and a starving one is a quiet one. And the ledger — the great ledger, the one the thief had died beside, the one his grandfather had kept — names. Hundreds of names. Families, towns of origin, destinations after the dispersal. Where they were sent. Where their children might still be.

My mother's name was in it. Mine. Written in a record-keeper's careful hand thirty years before I was born, because someone, somewhere, believed that a people erased should at least be *counted*.

Sef crouched beside me. "Whatever you're thinking," he said, "think it fast."

"I'm thinking about fires."

"You set one once."

"One that mattered." I looked at him. Nineteen years old, ink-stained fingers, the only person on this march who knew the whole shape of me and hadn't flinched. "Sef. If I burn this, it's gone. All of it. There's no tribunal that can subpoena ash."

"There's no tribunal that can seize ash either." He said it simply, the way he said everything, and then: "But the names don't have to burn. Names aren't evidence against anyone. They're just — people."

Just people. My mother used to say it differently. *A name is a provision*, she told me, the night she made me promise, coughing blood into a rag in a room that smelled of lamp oil. *A name carried far enough is a passage home.* She made me swear to destroy the garrison's copy of the dispersal order, and I did, and I ran nine years for it, and I had spent every one of those years believing the fire was the worst thing I ever did.

Sitting there with the ledger open on my knees, I understood that it was the best thing. I just hadn't finished it yet.

I tore the pages free.

* * *

Hale's hour ended with hoofbeats.

They came down the canyon in a wedge, lances leveled, and our line — eleven of us, plus a magistrate's daughter and a boy archivist — braced behind the wagons, and I heard Ilse shout something legal-sounding that the wind took apart, and then the world was noise.

I had already moved. While they formed up I had dragged the chest clear of the camp, out past the picket line to the flat scoured ground where nothing would catch but what I chose. I had the lamp oil from Maro's survey kit, the good stuff, the kind that burns wet. I had flint and steel in my hand because I had always had them, because for nine years fire had been the thing I was.

The first rider reached me before the oil was poured. A sergeant, young, visor down, shouting words I didn't bother to parse. I held up the lantern of my open palm — the ledger, pages fanned, and above it the flask tipped and streaming.

"Come closer," I said, "and I'll show you what your commander is afraid of."

He reined up. Horses hate fire more than men do; it's the oldest treaty there is. Behind him the wedge was breaking against our wagon line, and I heard Maro roar, heard steel, heard someone scream — but the sergeant in front of me wasn't looking at the fight. He was looking at the seal on the chest. Kessarine's seal. And I watched something move across his face that I recognized, because I'd felt it at a riverbank three weeks ago: the vertigo of a story your mother told you turning out to be true.

"My grandmother," he said, and stopped, and started again. "She used to sing songs nobody knew the words to."

"Her name might be in here," I said. "Or her mother's. Or the town she came from, the one that isn't on any map because it was taken off the map on purpose." I struck the flint. The oil caught along the chest's rim with a soft whump, and heat climbed my forearms, and I fed it pages — the treaties, the fraudulent signatures, the annex with its orders and counter-orders, the whole rotten architecture of a murder committed by paperwork. "Not hers, though. Hers I'm keeping."

He didn't stop me. Whatever else happened that day, that sergeant didn't stop me, and when his commander's voice cracked across the canyon — *take it, TAKE IT* — he turned his horse aside as if he hadn't heard.

The rest tried. Of course they did. Hale came himself, blade out, done with flags and offers, and he might have reached me except that our line broke formation to meet him — Maro swinging one-armed, the defected guards holding the gap, Ilse standing on a wagon bed reading aloud, actually reading, the dispersal order in her father's voice pitched to carry over the fighting, so that Hale's own soldiers had to choose between their ears and their orders. And Sef — Sef was beside me the whole time, tearing pages free ahead of my hands, sorting, saving, feeding the fire only what deserved to die.

The ledger went last. I saved the family pages first — every name, every town, every destination, stacked in Sef's satchel until the seams strained — and then I held the binding itself over the flames and let it go. It fought me. Old leather, old glue, a book that had survived a nation's death and a river's flood and a thief's long walk, and it did not want to burn. I held it down until it did.

Hale got within ten feet of me. Close enough that I saw the medal again, close enough to smell the horse. Then a thrown stone took his riderless attention off me for half a second — Maro, I'd learn later, grinning through split lips — and the moment passed, and by the time he fought clear the chest was a black geometry of slag and the ledger was light and smoke going up into a sky that had never once, in all the years I'd crossed it, asked anyone's permission.

He stood there in the heat shimmer and looked at the burning and then at me, and there was no triumph in his face and no rage either. Just arithmetic. Forty soldiers, half of them wavering, a testimony already spoken aloud that couldn't be unsaid, and nothing left to seize.

"You've destroyed the proof," he said.

"No," I said. "I destroyed the *possession*. The proof is standing in front of you wearing your uniform." I nodded past him, at the sergeant, at the others, at the faces turned toward Ilse's voice. "You can't arrest a story that forty men have already heard. Ask them."

He asked them, in his way. He looked down his own line, and his own line looked back, and not one horse moved forward.

* * *

They withdrew at dusk. Not routed — Hale was too good for that — but withdrawn, in order, with the particular silence of an army that has stopped believing its own orders. Two more of his guards stayed behind when he went. They said nothing. They just watered their horses at our spring and took up positions on our perimeter as if they'd always been there, and after a while it was true.

We buried the slag. It seemed right. Sef said a few words in a language neither of us spoke fluently, and Ilse stood with her arms crossed against the cold, and Maro, bandaged and drunk on pain tincture, sang something obscene until Sef laughed and ruined the solemnity, which was probably the point.

Then I opened the satchel and counted what we had saved. Four hundred and twelve names. Family pages, whole and legible, smelling of smoke. I spread them on a blanket by firelight and the whole camp gathered around without being asked, and I watched soldiers and archivists and a magistrate's daughter lean over the names of strangers looking for their own grandmothers' songs.

The sergeant — Corvan, his name was — found his. Third page. He touched it the way you touch a wound to confirm it's real.

"What happens to these?" Ilse asked quietly, beside me.

"They travel," I said. "Every name goes to the town the ledger says it was sent to. To whoever's living there now who doesn't know why their grandmother sang songs nobody knew the words to." I folded the pages back into the satchel. "A provision for the passage. Somebody wrote that once. I'm starting to think they meant this."

"And you? Hale's warrant didn't burn. Only the chest did."

I looked at the fire, down to embers now, and felt something in my chest that took me a moment to name because I hadn't felt it in nine years. Stillness. The warrant was still out there. Hale was still out there. But the thing the warrant had been chasing — the boy who ran from a fire — had stayed behind in the ashes with the ledger, and what was left was a man holding four hundred and twelve names and knowing, for the first time in his life, exactly where he was going.

"In the morning," I said, "we start for the river town. I have a story to tell a magistrate."

Sef looked up from the pages. "What story?"

"Mine. All of it." I smiled, and it felt strange on my face, like a door opening in a wall I'd bricked over years ago. "Starting with the fire."
