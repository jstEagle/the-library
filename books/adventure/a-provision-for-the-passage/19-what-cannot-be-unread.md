## 19. What Cannot Be Unread

Sef found the ledger's ashes still warm.

They lay in a black drift against the canyon wall, curled sheets and fragments that the wind was already sorting into nothing. He crouched over them without touching, because touching felt like a kind of theft, and there had been enough of that on this journey to last him a lifetime. The fire had been hot enough to crack the bronze binding of the chest. It sat open now like a mouth that had finished speaking.

Around him, the canyon smelled of smoke and blood and wet salt.

"Archivist." One of Varn's guards — Doss, the older one with the gray in his beard — stood a few paces off with his rifle slung and his hands empty, which from Doss meant something had gone either very well or very badly. "Captain wants a count. You able to walk?"

"I'm able to count."

"Then do both."

Sef did both. Eleven of theirs standing, two down — one dead, Brannic, who had hoarded water at the dry well and been bound for it and then, when Hale came, cut loose and handed a rifle anyway, because Ilse Varn did not waste people. The other wounded was a young guard named Toma with a cavalry saber gash along his ribs that Petra had packed with salt-soaked linen while the man bit down on a strap and wept without shame. Sef counted the enemy by what they'd left behind: four horses riderless, three bodies covered with saddle blankets by their own retreating comrades, and a scatter of dropped kit that told him the withdrawal had not been orderly. Hale's column had come apart the way a rope comes apart — strand by strand, from the middle outward, once the load shifted.

The load had been a woman's voice reading aloud.

Sef kept arriving back at that. He had spent the whole march cataloguing things — water caches, rock cairns, the thief's effects, the handwriting in the ledger that matched the page Ilse had stolen and he had read by firelight while Petra slept — and he could not find a category for what Ilse had done at the canyon mouth. She had stood between two armed companies with her father's signature in her hand and read it out in a voice pitched to carry, and she had not stopped when Hale's men started shouting, and she had not stopped when the first shot went wide, and she had not stopped until every soldier on that ridge had heard the words *dispersal order* and *manufactured famine* and *by authority of Magistrate Corvin Varn* spoken in daylight by a witness no one could silence.

Hale had retreated rather than massacre them. Sef understood now that this had not been mercy. A massacre requires witnesses to be worth fewer than the trouble they cause, and after Ilse finished reading, every one of Hale's own soldiers became a witness. You cannot shoot testimony that has already taken root inside your own ranks. The commander had looked at his men looking at each other, and Sef had watched the arithmetic fail behind his eyes, and then the order came: fall back, mount up, leave the chest, leave the dead, leave it all.

Leave it all. As if any of it could be left.

"Sef." Petra's voice, low, behind him. Sef turned. Petra looked like a man who had been wrung out and hung to dry — dust in the lines of his face, a fresh burn blister across the heel of one hand where the ledger had caught his sleeve as it caught. His eyes went to the ash drift, then away. "You shouldn't kneel in that."

"It's evidence."

"It's weather now." Petra held out a hand and pulled him up. For a moment they stood close, and Sef saw something in the ranger's face he had never seen there in all the weeks of hardpan and rationing and night marches: not relief exactly. Relief implies an ending. This was more like the look of a man standing in a room whose walls have just come down, blinking at how much sky there is.

"They'll come back," Sef said. "Hale."

"No."

"You're certain?"

"He can't." Petra said it simply, the way he said true things about the desert. "He came with a warrant and a company. He's leaving with neither. Half those troopers heard your magistrate's daughter indict her own father. By tomorrow half the rest will have heard it secondhand, and by the time Hale reaches a garrison telegraph, the story will have outrun him. You can't arrest a story, Sef. Believe me. I've spent nine years trying to outrun one."

* * *

They buried Brannic at noon, in the canyon's shade line where the ground was soft enough to dig. Ilse spoke over him — not scripture, none of them knew scripture that fit, but the names of the places he'd told her he came from, which Sef wrote down afterward in his field book because that was the only liturgy he trusted. Then Toma, feverish, propped against a pack, asked who would tell his mother if the wound turned, and Doss said he would, and the way Doss said it made Sef look at him twice.

It was Doss who came to him an hour later, while the others struck camp.

"You're the one writes everything down," Doss said. Not a question. Everyone knew. Sef had learned early that being the expedition's memory made you harmless and dangerous at once, and he had decided to live with it.

"I write what I see."

"Then see this." Doss glanced back toward the horses, where a younger guard — Kel, broad-shouldered, barely twenty — was checking girths with the exaggerated care of a man avoiding conversation. "Kel and me signed with Varn's purse because the purse was good and the work was escort. Nobody said anything about cavalry. Nobody said anything about—" He stopped, jaw working. "I served eleven years on the frontier before this. I know what a lawful order looks like. What Hale tried today wasn't one. And what the magistrate signed—" He couldn't finish it. He didn't need to. Everyone in the canyon had heard Ilse read the annex aloud; the words had done their work already, working in people like water works in salt.

"What are you asking me?" Sef said.

"Nothing. Telling you. When we get to the river town, Kel and me aren't going back to Varn's pay. We'll see the lady and the ranger and you safe to the tribunal steps, and then we're done, and if anyone asks, you can write that down too." Doss's voice was flat, but his hands weren't steady. "A man ought to be on record somewhere. Even a soldier. Especially a soldier."

Sef took out his field book and wrote it while Doss watched, and something in the older man's shoulders came down an inch, as though the sentence were a weight that needed a second bearer.

That was the thing Sef kept learning on this journey, the lesson under all the other lessons: people didn't only want the truth spoken. They wanted it *kept*. Spoken truth evaporated; kept truth compounded. He had thought, when he signed on as expedition archivist, that he was hired to inventory a dead nation. He understood now that he was hired to keep the living ones honest.

* * *

They marched at dusk, out of the canyon and onto the long gravel fan that led toward the river country. Petra set the pace, and it was slower than any pace he'd ever set — slower than the salt corridor, slower than the rationed days around the failed well — because Toma could ride but not sit long, and because no one argued about it. That was new too. Before the canyon, every decision on this expedition had been a negotiation between fear and money. Now the money was worthless — Ilse had said so herself, standing over the burned chest, telling her guards their wages died with her inheritance — and the fear had changed shape, and what remained holding the column together was something Sef didn't have a word for yet. He suspected Petra did, and suspected equally that Petra would never say it.

He walked beside the ranger through the first dark miles. The stars came out hard and clean. Behind them the canyon was a black seam in the world, and somewhere south of it Hale's broken company was limping toward a garrison that would no longer be what it had been.

"The thief," Sef said, after a while. "The letter. I've been thinking about it."

"Don't."

"He stole a map so someone would find the chest. He died getting it to the river. Everything since — Hale, the warrant, the annex, the fire — it all comes out of one man's decision to make himself a summons." Sef kept his eyes forward. "I keep wondering if he knew. If he guessed it would cost—"

"He knew." Petra's voice was quiet. "You don't forge a trail that careful unless you've priced what it costs. He wanted it found more than he wanted to live, and he arranged the finding so it couldn't be quietly buried. That's not madness. That's the most disciplined thing I've ever seen a man do." A pause, the length of several strides. "Don't make him a tragedy, Sef. Make him a record. That's what he was. Last of the record-keepers' line. Put him in your book with his name spelled right."

Sef did, that night, by the coals, while the camp slept in the exhausted way of people who have survived something and not yet decided what it means. He wrote the thief's name — Aldous Ren, recovered at last from the letter's signature — and beneath it the lineage the letter claimed, and beneath that the single sentence Sef believed would matter longest of anything he'd written on this journey:

*He committed a crime so that a crime could be read.*

* * *

On the third morning, the river appeared.

Not the flooded brown torrent of the riverbend where the chest had lain — the dry season was taking its toll everywhere — but a silver-braided ribbon in a green corridor, real water, drinkable water, and the sight of it moved through the column like a current of its own. Toma laughed from his saddle, high and cracked with fever. Kel whooped, actually whooped, and Doss cuffed him and then grinned. Ilse stood at the head of the column with her hat off and her hair loose and did not say anything at all, and Sef, watching her, understood that she was saying goodbye to something — the name Varn, maybe, or the version of herself who had ridden out with salvage papers and a smooth lie about the chest's contents.

Petra drank last, as he always did, after everyone else had drunk twice.

They made camp in the riverine trees, and it was there, in the green light, that Sef finally opened his field book to the page he had been avoiding — the copied entry from the ledger, the one naming Oyelaran among the dispersed families of Kessarine, mother and son, settlement unknown. He had matched the handwriting weeks ago. He had confronted Petra privately, and Petra had told him about the fire, the dying request, the nine years. But Sef had never written the connection down in full, in sequence, the way an archivist must, because writing it down made it permanent, and some part of him had been protecting Petra from permanence.

There was no point anymore. Permanence had arrived. It had arrived in a woman's voice at a canyon mouth, in front of forty soldiers, and it could not be repossessed.

He wrote the entry. Family: Oyelaran. Status: dispersed under order signed C.V. Disposition: one survivor, present, testifying.

When he looked up, Petra was sitting across the coals, watching him write, and he didn't look afraid. He looked like a man setting down a pack he'd carried so long he'd forgotten its weight.

"How does it read?" Petra asked.

"Like the beginning of something," Sef said. "The tribunal sits in the river town. Ilse says the civilian magistrates there answer to no imperial office — that's why her father hates them. She'll file tomorrow. And Petra—" he hesitated, then didn't stop himself, because stopping himself was the old habit and the old habit was what had gotten everyone nearly killed, "—she says they can void a warrant. If the testimony's clean. If the man giving it isn't running when he gives it."

Petra was quiet a long time. Downriver, someone was singing — Kel, badly, a marching song with the words worn off it. The river ran on, indifferent and generous.

"Nine years," Petra said at last, "I thought staying hidden was the same as staying alive." He turned his burned hand over, examining the blister as if it were a map. "Turns out I had it backwards."

Sef wrote that down too.
