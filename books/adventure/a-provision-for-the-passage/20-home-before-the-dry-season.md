## 20. Home Before the Dry Season

The river town announced itself by smell before it appeared at all: woodsmoke, tanneries, the green rot of irrigated fields after nine days of salt and stone. Ilse stood at the head of the column on the last ridge and felt something in her chest loosen that she had not known was clenched.

Below, the town sat along the river's bend like a thing that had grown there rather than been built — flat-roofed houses the color of the clay they stood on, date palms in ragged lines, a market square already stirring with morning traffic. Smoke rose thin and straight from a hundred cooking fires. The wells, she knew from Petra's briefing two nights ago, were running low. The dry season was three weeks early and the whole frontier knew it. But there was water here, and law of a kind, and roofs.

Behind her, the column came up the switchbacks in the gray light. Forty-one souls when they'd started. Thirty-six now — two dead in the ford crossing, three who had turned back with Hale's retreating cavalry rather than face what waited in town, and one, the guard Doss, still limping from the wound he'd taken standing between Petra and Hale's drawn sword. Sef walked near the rear with the mule carrying the copied pages, one hand resting on the satchel the way a man rests his hand on a sleeping child. He hadn't let it out of his reach since the flats.

Petra stood a few paces down the slope, apart as always, watching the town with an expression Ilse had learned to read over these weeks. It wasn't relief. It was the look of a man measuring a distance he would have to cross.

"You'll want to go to the tribunal first," he said, without turning. "Before word spreads. Before anyone who owes your father money finds you."

"I know."

"And you'll want to do it alone." Now he turned. His face was burned dark by the salt country, and there was a new line between his brows that hadn't been there at the riverbend. "Not because we wouldn't stand with you. Because if we're standing next to you when you file, they'll call it coercion. A magistrate's daughter, half-starved in the desert, surrounded by armed men she paid. They'll say the testimony was bought."

Ilse looked at him for a long moment. Nine weeks ago she would have bristled at being told how to manage her own declaration. She had crossed a desert since then. She had read her father's signature on an order that starved a nation. She had watched a man burn history to keep it free.

"You've thought about this," she said.

"I've had nine years to think about tribunals." Something moved behind his eyes — not quite a smile. "They're slower than you hope and faster than you fear. Go this morning. File before the heat."

* * *

The tribunal occupied a former granary at the edge of the market square, and it was exactly what its reputation promised: no imperial seal above the door, no portrait of the Magistrate-Regent on the wall, just a scarred wooden bench behind which sat three arbiters — a retired canal engineer, a woman who had once been a caravan judge in the southern passes, and an old man whose only credential, so far as anyone knew, was that both sides of every border dispute for forty years had trusted him. Frontier justice. It operated outside the imperial courts because it had to; out here, the empire's writ ran as far as its garrisons, and no farther than the water.

Ilse dressed for it carefully. Not in her traveling clothes — stained, sun-cracked, smelling of salt — but not in the fine wool she'd left home in either. She wore the plain linen shirt Sef's spare kit had yielded and her boots, brushed clean of dust, and she carried the annex documents wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like something living.

She had rehearsed this speech for six days. On the march, at night, lying awake while the sentries walked their rounds, she had built it sentence by sentence: the formal recitation of salvage, the chain of custody, the presentation of evidence. Her father's voice lived in those sentences. She could hear him dictating them — measured, precise, each clause load-bearing. *The record will show. The tribunal will note. In accordance with.*

In the antechamber, waiting for the morning docket to clear, she tore the speech apart in her head and put it back together differently.

When the clerk called her name — *Varn, Ilse, daughter of Magistrate Edran Varn, claiming under salvage writ* — she walked to the bench and set the oilcloth bundle down and did not open it yet.

"Arbiters," she said. "I'm going to say some things that will be easier for you to dismiss if I say them the way I was taught to say them. So I'm going to say them badly instead."

The old man in the middle almost smiled. The engineer didn't move.

"Nine weeks ago I led an expedition to recover a chest salvaged from the Kessarine floodplain. My father funded the expedition. My father gave me orders about what to do with what we found. I followed some of those orders. I broke the most important one." She unwrapped the oilcloth. "This is the sealed annex from the chest. It contains testimony, records, and a dispersal order bearing my father's signature and his office's seal. It documents that the nation of Kessarine was not lost. It was dismantled. Deliberately. By treaty fraud, by manufactured famine, and by an order signed eleven years ago authorizing the forced dispersal of its surviving population to settlements where they could be watched and worked."

She laid the pages out on the bench's front rail, one by one, face-up, where the room could see them. Her hands were steady. She noticed that the way you notice rain stopping.

"I am filing this record with this tribunal because the imperial courts will bury it. My father's office has jurisdiction over every court that would hear it. And I am filing it knowing what it costs me, because I want the cost on the record too." She drew a breath. "I renounce my inheritance. All of it. My name's protection, my father's estate, my claim to any office or preferment that flows from his. I am no longer Ilse Varn, daughter of the Magistrate. As of this filing I am just a witness with documents, and I ask this tribunal to treat me exactly as it would treat any other witness with documents."

Silence. Somewhere outside, a donkey complained about its load. The clerk's pen had stopped moving.

It was the caravan judge who spoke first, a dry voice like sand over stone. "You understand, girl, that once this is filed, it cannot be unfiled. Your father's advocates will come. They will offer you things. When the offers fail, they will take things. You'll have no name to hide behind."

"I spent twenty-four years hiding behind it," Ilse said. "It never once kept me warm."

The old man in the middle leaned forward and read the top page for a long time. Then he read the signature at the bottom of it. Then he looked up at her, and his eyes were tired and kind and utterly without mercy.

"Signature's authentic?" he asked.

"It's my father's hand. I've known it since I was six years old. I learned my letters copying it."

He grunted. "Clerk. Enter the filing. Full record, all pages, witnessed copies to all three arbiters and one sealed copy held against tampering." He looked at Ilse again. "And enter the renunciation. You're sure?"

"No," Ilse said honestly. "But I'm certain. There's a difference. Certainty is what you have after you've done the thing you were afraid of."

That got the smile out of the old man, briefly, like a fish surfacing. "Filed," he said. "Next matter."

* * *

It took less than an hour for the news to reach the wrong ears.

Ilse was at the well in the market square, filling skins for the column, when she saw the rider come in off the north road at a canter — a courier in imperial gray, dust-plumed, riding straight for the garrison waystation at the town's far end. She watched him go and did the arithmetic without wanting to. North road meant the relay forts. Relay forts meant her father would have word within four days, and riders within eight.

She finished filling the skins. Her hands did the work while her mind stood very still, the way it had learned to in the whiteout, when the world went blind and the only sane response was to hold position and breathe.

Sef found her there. He'd developed a habit of finding her lately — appearing at her elbow with water or bread or a question about cataloguing, always casual, never accidental. She suspected he'd learned it from watching Petra.

"They'll come for the copies," he said quietly.

"Yes."

"The arbiters' copies are safe. Tribunal records are sacrosanct even to the empire — Hale said so himself, at the canyon, when he was trying to buy us." Sef shifted the satchel on his shoulder. "But the sealed copy. Held against tampering. Someone has to petition to open it, and petitions get lost, and clerks get promoted, and—"

"I know how my father works, Sef. I was raised inside the machine."

"Then you know the sealed copy is the weak point."

She looked at him. Nineteen years old, maybe twenty, with ink-stained fingers and a spine that had hardened somewhere on the salt flats without either of them noticing. "What are you proposing?"

"Not proposing. Reporting." He glanced around — the well yard, the women drawing water, the idlers under the awnings — and lowered his voice further. "Last night, while you were at the tribunal offices confirming the filing, I made copies. Not transcripts. True copies, page for page, in my own hand, verified against the originals twice. There are now four sets. One is with me. One is with Petra. One went into the mail packet south this morning with a drover who owed me a favor and doesn't know what he carried. And one—" he tapped the satchel, "—is going wherever the family names go. To the descendant towns. That was always the plan."

Ilse stared at him. "You copied classified annex material without telling me."

"You told me on the flats that trust means assuming the other person is doing the right thing even when you can't see them doing it." He had the grace to look slightly embarrassed. "I assumed."

A laugh came out of her — short, cracked, real. It was the first time she'd laughed since the filing, and it hurt her ribs in a way that felt like healing. "Your favor-owing drover. Does he ride fast?"

"He rides like the devil's behind him, which by tomorrow he'll believe."

* * *

They gathered at dusk in the courtyard of the caravanserai where the expedition had taken rooms — what remained of it, the thirty-six, minus the three who'd gone north with Hale's men. Petra sat on the mounting block with his forearms on his knees, looking older than his years and lighter than he had any right to. Word of the filing had spread through the column by midday, carried in the particular silence that soldiers use to discuss their employers' families. No one had quit. Ilse had checked three times, expecting desertions, and found none, and didn't know what to do with the finding.

Doss, the wounded guard, had it out first. "My pay came from Varn money," he said bluntly. "Does that stop?"

"I have nothing," Ilse said. "So yes. It stops today."

Doss chewed on that. Around the courtyard, faces turned toward her in the lamplight — guards, teamsters, the cartographer Marrec with his ruined maps and his endless questions, the cook, the farrier. People she had bought and people she had bled beside, and she could no longer always tell which category a face belonged to, which she supposed was the point.

"Then we're owed back pay through today," Doss said, "and after that we're volunteers." He looked around the courtyard. "I volunteered for the march home. I'll volunteer for whatever comes next, if someone tells me what it is."

All eyes went to Petra.

He stood slowly, and Ilse watched him take the weight of thirty-five gazes the way she'd seen him take the weight of the salt corridor — without flinching, without performing, simply accepting that the weight existed.

"There's a list," he said. "Pages from the ledger. Names of families dispersed out of Kessarine — where they were sent, what settlements, what happened after. Some of those towns still exist. Some of the descendants are still there, working land their grandparents were marched to, not knowing why." He paused. "The names need delivering. Quietly, town by town, to people who'll know what to do with them. It's slow work. Unpaid work. Dangerous work, if the Magistrate's office decides to care."

Marrec raised a hand like a student. "And the man who does this work — does he have a name? Or does he spend another nine years being nobody?"

The question landed hard. Ilse saw Petra's jaw tighten, saw him glance at her — one glance, quick, asking something she couldn't parse — and then he looked back at the courtyard.

"That's the next chapter," he said. "Ask me tomorrow."

Tomorrow. Ilse turned the word over. Tomorrow Petra would walk to the civilian magistrate's office and surrender — she'd known since the flats, since he told her in the dark with the storm screaming, that this was what he intended, and she had argued with him until the argument ran out of air. Tomorrow he would trade his anonymity for a courtroom and the truth about the garrison fire, and the tribunal would either void the warrant or it wouldn't, and either way the running would be over.

Tonight, though, there was still tonight. The lamps burned low. Someone — the cook — produced a bottle of date spirit that had survived the whole journey unopened, and it went around the courtyard in a silence that was not quite celebration and not quite mourning, the specific silence of people who had crossed something together and come out the other side changed in ways they hadn't inventoried yet.

Sef sat down beside Ilse on the step. For a while neither spoke. Across the yard, Petra was showing Doss how to rewrap the wound properly, patient, hands sure.

"You filed under your own name," Sef said at last. "No more Varn. What will you be called?"

Ilse considered it seriously, the way she'd considered the wording of the filing. Names, she had learned, were provisions — you carried them or you starved.

"Ilse," she said. "Just Ilse, until I've earned the rest."

Sef nodded as if this were a perfectly normal answer, which, among the people in this courtyard, it probably was.

From the north road, faint but unmistakable in the evening stillness, came the sound of hoofbeats — many horses, moving fast, riding through the dark toward the garrison waystation. The column heard it too. The bottle stopped going around. Petra rose to his feet without hurry, and Ilse saw him count the hoofbeats the way he counted everything, and saw him arrive at the same number she had.

Eight days, she'd figured. Her father's riders.

They'd made it in five.

Petra looked across the courtyard at her, and in the lamplight his expression was calm and final, a man setting down a pack he'd carried so long he'd forgotten its weight.

"Well," he said. "No time like tomorrow."
