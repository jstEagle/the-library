## 21. The Warrant, Answered

The tribunal met in the river town's granary, because the courthouse had burned two winters ago and nobody had rebuilt it. That seemed right to Petra. Most of the law he'd known in his life had been conducted in buildings that smelled of grain and dust and the ghosts of things stored away and forgotten.

He had shaved. That was the first thing Ilse noticed when he came down from the room above the ferry house, and she said nothing about it, but her eyes went to his jaw and then away, and he understood that she understood. A man shaves before a hanging or a wedding or a hearing. There was no fourth occasion that required it.

"You could still walk," she said. They stood in the street outside the granary, and the morning was cool the way desert mornings are cool — a borrowed coolness, on loan until the sun finished its climb. "The tribunal sits at midday. The ferry runs at noon. You could be on the far bank before anyone knows you were serious."

"I was never serious about running," Petra said. "I was serious about not being caught. It's a different thing."

"It isn't."

"It is," he said. "Running is a direction. Not being caught is a discipline. I've been practicing the discipline for nine years, and I'm tired of it, and the direction doesn't matter anymore because there's nowhere left to go that the name Oyelaran hasn't already arrived." He looked at the granary, at the two guards at the door who were not garrison men, who wore the plain sashes of the civilian tribunal, and felt something in his chest loosen that he had not realized was knotted. "Also, I promised you testimony. I keep my promises. It's the only currency I've never counterfeited."

Ilse studied him for a long moment. She had lost weight on the salt flats, and gained something else in its place — a hardness around the eyes that hadn't been there when she'd offered him triple pay on a riverbank. She had filed her father's signature with the tribunal the evening they arrived. Petra had not been present for it, but the whole town knew by morning, the way towns know things. Magistrate Varn's daughter, disowning her inheritance in front of three clerks and a bailiff. It was the kind of story that traveled faster than cavalry.

"They'll ask you about the fire," she said. "Not about the chest, not about Hale. About the fire. That's the charge on the warrant. Arson of a garrison records office. Destruction of official documents. They'll want the whole of it, Petra. Everything you did and everything you didn't do."

"I know."

"Do you?" She turned to face him fully. "Because I've watched you give half-truths the way other men give directions — generously, and without ever arriving anywhere. In there, half a truth is a whole lie. The tribunal operates on sworn testimony. If you shade it, if you keep even one corner dark because nine years of habit tells you to, they'll void the hearing and hand you to the imperial courts, and Hale will be there before the ink dries."

"I know," Petra said again, and this time it came out quieter. "My mother made me memorize the dispersal order. All of it. Every clause, every signature. She made me recite it back to her for three nights while she was dying, and when I had it perfect she said, *Now burn the paper, because paper can be stolen, but a son who carries it can only be killed.*" He looked at his own hands, the hands that had done the burning. "I've spent nine years being careful with the truth the way a man is careful with a wound. In there, I'm going to take the bandage off. All of it. You have my word."

Ilse nodded slowly. Then, unexpectedly, she put out her hand, and he took it, and they stood in the street like two people concluding a bargain that had actually been struck months ago on a flooded riverbank, when she'd lied to him about what the chest contained and he'd let her, because he was lying too.

"I'll be inside," she said. "First row. If you look for a face to anchor on, use mine."

"I don't need an anchor."

"No," she agreed. "But it's there anyway."

* * *

The granary had been arranged with a strange, austere formality: three magistrates at a trestle table on a raised grain platform, a bailiff with a ledger, a clerk with a second ledger, and perhaps sixty townspeople standing along the walls because the river town had decided, collectively, that this hearing was not to be missed. Petra recognized faces from the caravan — the two guards who had defected at the canyon stood together near the door, and Sef was there, and the cartographer, who had brought paper and was already sketching the room, because the cartographer sketched everything.

The magistrates were three: an old woman with a weaver's forearms and a magistrate's chain worn over a work apron, a middle-aged man with the sunburn of a man who farmed his own land, and a third, younger, who kept glancing at the door as if expecting Hale's cavalry to come through it. Petra noted that glance and filed it. Fear in a magistrate was a variable, and he had survived nine years by accounting for variables.

"Bailiff," said the old woman. "Read the warrant."

The bailiff read it. Petra listened to his own name pronounced in a voice of official boredom — *Petra Oyelaran, sometime ranger of the Ninth Garrison district, wanted on charges of arson, destruction of imperial records, and flight from garrison justice, warrant issued under the hand of Commander Edran Hale* — and found that it sounded like someone else's life being read aloud. Nine years of that name being a thing to hide, and now it was just words in a granary, with grain dust drifting in the light from the high windows.

"Petra Oyelaran," said the old magistrate. "You have surrendered voluntarily to this tribunal rather than to the garrison. Do you understand the distinction?"

"I do."

"State it, so the record shows."

"The garrison court answers to Commander Hale, who is named in testimony this tribunal has already heard as a party to the crimes I'm accused of burning evidence of. A civilian tribunal answers to no commander. I'm not here to be judged by the man I'm testifying against. I'm here to be judged by people who grow their own food."

A ripple went along the walls. The old magistrate did not smile, but something around her mouth suggested she had wanted to.

"Then begin," she said. "The fire. From the beginning. And Oyelaran — the beginning is further back than you think. We have read the pages your companions carried out of the salt. We have read the annex. We know what Kessarine was and what was done to it. What we do not know is you. Start there."

So he started there.

He told them about his mother. He had not planned to begin with her — he had planned to begin with the garrison, the records office, the dry season, the tactical details, the clean architecture of a crime — but the beginning turned out to be further back than he'd thought, exactly as she'd said, and the beginning was a woman in a house outside a garrison town, teaching a boy to read from a book with no cover, because the book with a cover had been taken by the assessors.

"My mother was Kessarine," he said. "Not descended from. *Was.* She was nine years old when the dispersal came. Her family was on the third page of the ledger — the pages I kept, the pages your clerk now holds. Her father's name, her mother's name, her own name, all entered in a hand that the annex identifies as a treaty clerk's. They were counted, catalogued, and moved. The famine that moved them wasn't an act of the desert. It was manufactured. It's in the annex, in her father's office's own hand — Magistrate Varn's office, that is." He did not look at Ilse. He had promised himself he wouldn't, and he didn't, and it cost him. "She grew up in a resettlement town where the language was discouraged and the records were held by the people who'd done the dispersing, and she married a ranger, and she had a son, and she taught that son the old names because a language survives as long as one person refuses to stop saying it."

"Your father," said the middle magistrate. "He was garrison."

"Ninth Garrison. Supply. He died when I was eleven. He never knew what my mother carried. She was careful. She'd had a lifetime of practice at being careful."

"And the fire."

"The fire was nine years ago. I was twenty. I was a ranger in my third year, attached to the Ninth, and I was good at it — tracking, water-reading, the desert work. And my mother was dying. Slowly, and then not slowly. And in the last month she called me to her bed and told me what she'd never told me whole: the dispersal order, the treaty fraud, the famine. She made me recite it back until I had it perfect. And then she told me about the records office."

Petra paused. The clerk's pen scratched. Somewhere in the walls, grain shifted, settling, the sound of a building holding its weight.

"The Ninth Garrison kept a local copy of the dispersal order. Regional annex, filed with the supply records, because the dispersal had been administered as a supply matter — grain quotas, movement permits. My mother knew it was there because my father had once complained about the filing, years ago, not knowing what he was complaining about. And she said to me: *When I'm gone, they will come and clean the files. There's a review scheduled — I've heard the assessors talk. When they clean the files, the last copy in this district burns with the rest, and then no one can ever prove what was done to us.* And she said, *You will burn it first. Not to hide it. To make them keep it. A record that exists in one place can be destroyed. A record whose destruction is witnessed becomes a question, and questions get asked for a hundred years.*"

"Did you understand her?" asked the old magistrate. "Truly. At twenty."

"No," Petra said. "I understood that my mother was dying and had asked me for the only thing she'd ever asked me for. I went into the records office on a rest night, with lamp oil, and I burned the regional annex. Just that cabinet. I was careful — no one was hurt, no other records touched. And then I ran, because the garrison does not care about a dead Kessarine woman's reasons, and a warrant doesn't have a clause for *my mother asked me to.* I ran, and I told myself for nine years that I didn't know why I was running, that it was just the discipline of not being caught. But I knew. I was running because if I stopped, I would have to decide whether what I'd done was a crime or an answer, and I was afraid of the answer."

The room was very still. The younger magistrate had stopped glancing at the door.

"Commander Hale held that warrant," the old magistrate said. "Did he know why the fire was set?"

"He suspected. I think he always suspected. He was supply command — the annex was his filing. He never pursued me with anything like real effort for nine years. A man who wants to catch a fugitive catches him. Hale wanted me *out there* — unnamed, unproven, a rumor. Because as long as I was a fugitive, I was a madman who burned a cabinet, and the dispersal order was just paper that had, regrettably, been lost. If I ever stood in a room like this one and told you why, I became a witness." He looked at the three magistrates, and then, finally, at Ilse, in the first row, exactly where she'd said she'd be. "So I'm standing here. I set the fire. I set it at my mother's request, to destroy the last local copy of a document that recorded a crime — and to make its destruction a question no one could bury. I have carried the order's full text in my memory for nine years. I will dictate it to your clerk, every clause, every signature, and the clerk can match it against the annex pages Ilse Varn carried out of the salt. If one word differs, call me a liar and give me to Hale."

* * *

They broke at midday, and Petra sat on the granary steps with bread he couldn't taste, and Sef came and sat beside him without asking, which was Sef's particular genius.

"The clerk's been matching for two hours," Sef said. "He's on the fourth page. He keeps making a sound. Like this." Sef made a small, involuntary noise, the sound of a man finding a thing exactly where a thing should be. "You really have it all? In your head?"

"My mother made sure."

"She sounds like she was terrifying."

"She was," Petra said, and found he was smiling. "She was the most terrifying person I've ever known, and I once watched Ilse Varn stare down Commander Hale across a river ford."

Sef was quiet a moment. "Whatever they decide," he said, "I want you to know the caravan's already decided. We talked about it last night. All of us. If they hand you to Hale, we're going to testify — all of it, the well, the rationing, the ford, everything. The whole record of what you did for us."

"You don't owe me that."

"No," Sef agreed. "We don't owe you anything. That's what makes it worth giving." He stood, brushed off his trousers, and paused. "The clerk's on page five. Your mother's family is on page five. He just found it."

* * *

The tribunal reconvened at the fourth bell. The old magistrate stood, and the granary went quiet in a way that sixty people can be quiet, which is to say not entirely, but enough.

"Petra Oyelaran. This tribunal finds as follows. The testimony given is corroborated in full by the annex pages recovered from the Kessarine chest, by the ledger pages in this clerk's keeping, and by the sworn statements of eleven members of the Varn expedition. The regional annex you burned was, by every reading of the treaty law this tribunal recognizes, an instrument of fraud. Its destruction was not the destruction of a lawful record but of a false one. The tribunal further finds that the warrant issued under Commander Hale's hand was issued to suppress, not to pursue, and is therefore void of lawful standing in this jurisdiction."

She looked at him across the grain dust and the drifting light.

"The warrant is answered. You are free to go — or free to stay, which is the greater freedom, and the harder one. Nine years is a long time to practice a discipline, Oyelaran. This tribunal's only counsel is that you practice a new one."

Petra stood. His legs felt strange, unweighted, as if he'd been carrying water for nine years and someone had finally taken the jar. He walked out of the granary into the late afternoon, and the whole town seemed to be in the street, and he did not know a single one of their names, and for the first time in nine years that felt like a beginning instead of a sentence.

Sef found him there, moving fast, with a piece of paper in his hand and an expression Petra had learned on the salt flats to distrust.

"There's a woman at the ferry house," Sef said. "Came in on the morning boat. She's asking for the man who burned the chest on the flats — and she's carrying a ledger page that isn't one of ours."
