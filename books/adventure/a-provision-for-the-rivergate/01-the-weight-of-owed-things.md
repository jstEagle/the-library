## 1. The Weight of Owed Things

The coins came to forty-one coppers and a half-silver worn so thin you could read a newspaper through it.

Emrys Vasquez counted them twice on the floorboard by the window because counting them once felt like surrender and three times felt like prayer, and he had given up on prayer around the same time he'd given up on his mother. The flophouse room was four paces wide and smelled of river rot and lamp oil, and through the shutter slats he could see the harbor working itself into a frenzy below — cranes swinging, gulls screaming over fish guts, the whole waterfront of Port Sallow getting fat on the last dry-season traffic before the monsoon shut the upper river like a fist.

Forty-one coppers and a ghost of silver.

He stacked them in columns the way his father used to stack survey markers: neat, deliberate, meaningless. Aldous Vasquez had been a man who believed order imposed on small things would somehow propagate upward into large ones. It hadn't. The debt had eaten him the way water eats limestone — slowly, invisibly, until one morning the whole structure simply wasn't there anymore, just a hole in the world shaped like a man, and a son standing at the edge of it.

Three hundred and forty drakes. That was the number. Emrys knew it the way he knew his own heartbeat, not because he'd ever seen the ledger but because Corvo's people made sure he carried it everywhere, stitched into him like a second spine.

The knock came at the hour when the light through the shutters went from gold to bruise.

He didn't answer it. He never answered it. He sat very still on the edge of the cot with his hands flat on his knees and let whoever it was knock again, harder, and then let the door open anyway, because it always opened. The lock had been broken since before he took the room. He suspected the landlord kept it that way for exactly this purpose.

Brannick filled the doorway the way weather fills a valley.

He was a big man gone soft in the way of men who no longer needed their muscles because everyone remembered them anyway — a former dockworker's frame draped in a good coat that cost more than everything Emrys owned including the boots he stood up in. His face was broad and almost friendly, which was the worst thing about it. Cruelty you could brace against. Kindness from a collector made your stomach drop through the floor.

"Vasquez." Brannick said the name like he was tasting it for spoilage. "You're a hard man to find. No, wait." He smiled. "That's not true. You're the easiest man in Port Sallow to find. You're always right here."

"I move around," Emrys said.

"You move between this room and the chandlery where you haul rope for nine coppers a day, and the pawnshop on Tanner's Row where you keep trying to sell me back my own patience." Brannick stepped inside without being asked, which was also traditional, and looked around the room with the mild professional interest of a man appraising collateral. His gaze snagged on the coin columns. "Counting?"

"Budgeting."

"For what? A funeral?" He laughed at his own joke, a short bark with nothing behind it, and then the humor drained out of his face the way it always did, like water finding a crack. "Corvo's asking after you. Third time this month. You know what that means."

"It means he wants interest I don't have."

"It means," Brannick said, settling onto the one chair as if it had been built for him, which it hadn't been built for anyone, "that the old man is being patient, and patience in our line of work is like a fuse. It burns quiet. It burns slow. But it doesn't burn backward." He laced his fingers over his stomach. "Your father understood that, near the end. Aldous was a clever man. Clever men are the worst debtors, did you know that? Poor men pay what they can. Clever men pay what they must, and they spend everything else calculating how much that is. Your father calculated for eleven years. In the end he owed us three hundred and forty drakes and we owned his boat, his tools, his name on the guild rolls, and his son's future labor. That's what cleverness buys."

Emrys kept his voice level. "I've paid down sixty drakes in two years."

"And spent more than that keeping yourself fed, clothed, and dry, which is why the number hasn't moved, which is why you're still here, which is why I'm here." Brannick unfolded himself and stood, and the room seemed to exhale. At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and looked back with something that in a better man might have been pity. "There's work coming through the harbor. Big contract money, upstream money. Men who can pilot, who can haul, who can keep their mouths shut. If a certain indebted party were to take such a contract and route his wages through Corvo's office, well." He shrugged, a mountain deciding to be casual. "Numbers move. Fuses get longer. Think about it, Vasquez. The river's going up soon. So is the price of owing."

Then he was gone, and his footsteps went down the stairs heavy as pilings being driven, and Emrys stood in the middle of his four-pace room and hated him with a hatred so clean and so useless it was almost restful.

* * *

He went out because staying in meant counting coins again.

Port Sallow at dusk was a city pretending to be richer than it was. Lanterns bloomed along the quay like an infection of light, and the taverns threw open their shutters and poured out fiddle music and the smell of fried eel, and everywhere there were men spending money they hadn't earned yet on things they didn't need, and Emrys walked through all of it with his collar up and his hands in his pockets, touching the coins there like talismans. Forty-one coppers. He bought a bowl of barley soup from the widow Osei's cart because she gave bigger portions to men who didn't haggle, and he ate it standing at the rail overlooking the black water, watching the lanterns of anchored ships double themselves in the current.

Upstream money, Brannick had said.

Everyone on the waterfront was talking about it, actually. For two weeks now the talk had run along the docks like oil along water: some expedition, some rich backer, buying out cargo space and pilots and every longboat that could float, sending tons of supplies upriver toward the dead country above the falls where nobody went except smugglers and fools. The Rivergate, the talk called it. Old ruins in the karst country. There were stories about the place the way there were stories about every place nobody came back from — drowned temples, cursed gates, cities that the river had swallowed and kept.

Emrys didn't care about ruins. What he cared about was the shape of the sentence *route his wages through Corvo's office*, because it was the first sentence anyone had spoken to him in two years that contained a door instead of a wall.

He was still turning it over when the courier found him.

She was a girl, fourteen maybe, barefoot, with the lean wary look of a child raised on the docks, and she materialized beside him at the rail the way dock children did, silent until they wanted something. She held out a sealed tube of oilcloth.

"You're Vasquez. E. Vasquez."

"Who sent this?"

"The chandlery office. Man said you'd be at the rail. Man said you're always at the rail." She rattled the tube impatiently. "Signature's a copper. Take it or I stand here all night."

He paid her the copper and broke the seal with his thumbnail.

Inside was a contract, printed on real paper, the kind of paper that had weight to it, that crackled when you unfolded it like something alive. He read it by the light of the nearest lantern pole, and the words went into him cold, one at a time, each one landing like a stone dropped down a well:

*The undersigned agrees to ferry contracted supplies from Port Sallow to the expedition camp at coordinates per attached chart, upper reaches beyond the falls, arrival no later than final safe passage prior to monsoon onset. Payment: one hundred twenty drakes, half on loading, half on verified delivery.*

One hundred and twenty drakes. More than a third of the debt. More money than he had held in his life outside of other people's hands.

His eyes went to the signature block, to the line identifying the contracting party, and there the world tilted sideways and quietly rearranged itself.

*Expedition principal: Dr. Ilsa Vasquez, Institute of Antiquities.*

The river sounds fell away. The lanterns, the music, the gulls — all of it receded as if someone had stuffed wool in his ears. He read the name again. And again. As if repetition might change it, the way he'd counted the coins, the way he'd counted everything his whole life, hoping arithmetic could perform miracles.

Ilsa Vasquez.

Twelve years. Twelve years since she'd stood in the doorway of their house with a field pack over one shoulder and his father's brass compass in her hand — his father's compass, the one thing of Aldous's she'd ever wanted — and told him she was going upstream to a dig and that he'd understand when he was older. He'd been fifteen. He was still waiting to be older. She'd sent letters for the first year, then postcards, then nothing, and meanwhile his father had sickened and borrowed and sunk, and died owing three hundred and forty drakes to a man named Corvo whom Emrys had never seen and imagined nightly, and somewhere far upriver his mother had dug holes in the earth and found beautiful dead things and apparently never once thought of the living one she'd left behind.

And now her name was on a contract offering him one hundred and twenty drakes to carry her supplies.

"She knows," he said aloud, to no one. The courier girl was already gone. "Or she doesn't know. One of those."

Because that was the question, wasn't it. Either the great Dr. Vasquez had put her estranged son's name on a hiring list — had known, had chosen, had reached twelve years downriver with one thin paper hand — or some clerk had matched a licensed boatman to a job and neither woman nor son had any say in it, and the universe was simply laughing at him with its usual efficiency.

He read the rest of the contract standing at the rail while the soup went cold in his stomach. Supplies to load at dawn, crates sealed, contents not the carrier's concern. Route per chart. Deadline printed in red ink at the bottom like a warning: *final safe passage, 11 days.* And at the very end, the clause that made him go still:

*Payment to be disbursed at the discretion of the carrier to any accredited financial office.*

Any accredited office. Including, say, one on Corvo's street.

Brannick had said there was big contract money moving upstream. Brannick had practically drawn him a map. Which meant either coincidence — and Emrys had stopped believing in coincidence around the same time he'd stopped believing in his mother — or Corvo's fingers were already on this, already wrapped around it, and taking the contract meant rowing straight into whatever the old man wanted rowed into.

He should refuse. Every instinct that had kept him alive and free-ish for two years said refuse: stay small, stay visible only to Brannick, keep paying sixty drakes a decade until the debt outlived him. That was the smart play. That was the play his father would have calculated.

But his father's calculations had left a hole in the world shaped like a man.

Emrys folded the contract along its creases, carefully, and put it inside his shirt against his chest, and stood at the rail a long time watching the black water slide past beneath the doubled lanterns, going somewhere, going anywhere, going the way water goes, which is always downhill and always eventually to sea.

One hundred and twenty drakes. Half the distance out of the hole. And at the top of the river, at the end of it, a camp full of strangers working for a woman whose face he could barely remember except in fragments — ink-stained fingers, the smell of coffee and dust, a laugh he'd have sworn he'd forgotten until this moment, when it surfaced whole and unbearable, like a body coming up out of floodwater.

She'd abandoned him. He knew that. He'd built his entire adult self on knowing that, the way you build a house on rock.

So why did his hands shake?

Below him the tide turned; he felt it rather than saw it, the harbor's slow breath changing direction, the flotsam at the pilings reversing course. Eleven days to the falls. Forty-one coppers in his pocket and a debt with his father's name on it and his mother's name waiting at the far end of the river like a lit fuse of her own.

Emrys Vasquez pushed off the rail, turned his back on the water, and went to find out how much a leaking longboat cost to rent.

It was, he would think later, the last decision he ever made as a free man — though not, despite everything, the worst one.
