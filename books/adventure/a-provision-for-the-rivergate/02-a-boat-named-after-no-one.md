## 2. A Boat Named After No One

The longboat sat at the end of the pier like something the river had already tried to eat once and given up on.

Emrys stood at the top of the rotting steps and looked at it for a long time before he went down. Dawn was coming up gray behind the fish market, and the smell of the harbor — diesel, brine, and the sweet rot of whatever had died in the shallows overnight — rose around him like a tide. The boat was maybe nine meters stem to stern, flat-bottomed, shallow-drafted, the kind of hull built for rivers that changed their minds. It had been painted blue once. Now the paint came off in scales when he touched it, and underneath was wood gone silver-gray with age, checked and split along the gunwales in places where the sun had gotten at it for years without mercy.

There was no name on the transom. Just a bare patch of wood where a name had been burned or painted away, and above it, stenciled in fresh white letters small enough to miss: *Property of C. Holdings.*

Corvo's mark. Even the boat belonged to the debt.

"You're not going to find anything better," said the man from Corvo's office — Dario, the one with the soft voice and the ledger eyes who'd watched Emrys count coins two nights ago with the patience of someone counting someone else's money. He stood up on the pier, hands in his pockets, careful not to come down the steps. "And you're not going to find anything else. That's the point."

"The point," Emrys said, "is that I'm supposed to take this thing four hundred kilometers upstream through the narrows during dry season, carrying cargo worth more than my life, in a hull that leaks if you look at it wrong."

"It doesn't leak if you look at it right." Dario smiled. He had good teeth and no warmth behind them. "That's why we picked you. Your file says you can fix things."

*Your file.* Emrys filed that away too, in the same drawer as everything else Corvo's people seemed to know about him — his father's debts, his mother's name, the two years he'd spent rebuilding outboard engines in a shop in Port Meridian before the shop folded and the debts didn't. They knew him the way a butcher knows a carcass: by weight, by cut, by what each part could be sold for.

He climbed down the steps anyway.

The boat rocked under his boots when he stepped aboard, a lazy, drunken roll that told him the bilge had water in it. He knelt and pulled the floorboards — three of them came up easy, two fought him, and under all of them the bilge lay dark and still, holding perhaps four centimeters of green-black water and a smell like old coins. He found the seep within ten minutes: a seam near the bow, port side, where the caulking had dried and shrunk until there was a gap you could have fed a pencil through. Another along the starboard chine, smaller. A third he only found by pouring water into the bilge and watching where it ran.

Three leaks. Not fatal ones. Not yet.

"Engine?" he called up.

"Yamaha forty. Two years old, serviced last month." Dario crouched at the top of the steps now, arms on his knees, watching with the mild interest of a man observing weather. "Fuel's in the drums aft. Spare prop, spare shear pins, tools in the box under the console."

Emrys lifted the engine cowling and looked. The Yamaha was real enough, clean enough, its spark plugs new. Someone had spent money here — just enough money, in exactly the places where failure would kill the cargo and not the plan. He closed the cowling and said nothing, but he added it to the ledger he was keeping behind his eyes. *They want me to arrive. They don't much care what shape I arrive in.*

"I'll need pitch," he said. "Oakum if you've got it. A sheet of marine ply, some epoxy, canvas, and a bag of roofing nails."

Dario raised an eyebrow. "The contract covers fuel and provisions."

"The contract covers a boat that floats. This isn't one. Yet." Emrys stood, wiped his hands on his trousers, and made himself look up at the man without blinking. "You can send me upriver in this, and in about six days the river will send it back to you in pieces, along with your crates and your reputation. Or you can spend forty dollars on pitch and let me make it seaworthy. Your call. But make it today, because I'm leaving tomorrow whether the boat's ready or not."

For a moment Dario just looked at him, and Emrys understood that this, too, was part of the test — maybe the whole test. Corvo hadn't hired a boatman. Corvo had bought a man who owed him, and was checking whether the man would sink quietly or fight the water.

"Chandlery on Rope Street," Dario said finally. "Tell them to bill C. Holdings. Don't get creative with the invoice."

He left. Emrys stood alone on the leaking boat and felt something move in his chest that he refused, for the moment, to name. It wasn't hope. Hope was a luxury item and he'd stopped buying luxuries years ago. It was closer to the feeling of picking up a tool that fit his hand.

* * *

The chandlery on Rope Street smelled of tar and turpentine, and its owner, a woman named Bess with forearms like dock rope, took one look at the invoice and one look at Emrys and said, "C. Holdings. You're the poor bastard taking the supply run up to the Rivergate dig."

Word traveled fast in a harbor town. Faster than honesty.

"You know the expedition?" Emrys asked, signing.

"Everyone knows the expedition. Half the town's been feeding off it for a year — fuel, food, guides, laundry for thirty people." She stacked his supplies on the counter: a brick of raw pitch, a coil of oakum, epoxy hardener and resin, a folded sheet of ply, a canvas tarp stiff with age. "Then three weeks ago it all stops. Boats come back down empty. No mail out. The radio man at the mission says nobody answers on their band anymore." She shrugged, a big rolling motion. "Dig's finished, probably. Or they found what they wanted and flew out rich. Either way, none of my business."

"And now they're sending supplies up?"

"Now they're sending *you* up." Bess looked at him over the counter, and her eyes were shrewd and not unkind. "Which is different. Supplies go by schedule. Men go by somebody's decision." She wrapped the pitch in newspaper. "You want advice, boatman?"

"Advice is free. Everything else on that invoice isn't."

"Don't trust the dry season dates on your contract. The rains came early two years running now. Old men blame the valley, say the river's angry about something. Young men blame climate. Me, I blame contracts — they always promise more weather than the sky agreed to." She pushed the bundle across. "Patch your hull below the waterline first. And whatever's in those crates you're hauling —"

"I'm not allowed to open them."

"No," Bess said. "I imagine you're not. That's usually how it goes with things worth more than the man carrying them."

* * *

He worked through the afternoon and into the dark, alone, because he wanted it that way.

There had been a time — he was nineteen, maybe twenty, still speaking to his mother then, still living in the long shadow of her reputation — when there had always been someone to help. Graduate students who wanted favor with Dr. Ilsa Vasquez, local laborers hired by the university, a whole ecosystem of useful hands that orbited her work and would happily hold a plank steady for an hour if it meant being seen beside her. Emrys had grown up believing that competence was a team sport, that every hard job came with assistants attached.

Then she'd left. Not died — left, which was worse, because death at least had the decency to be final. She'd taken the compass, his father's brass compass with the dented lid, the one thing in the house that had belonged to the man whose debts were now eating his son alive, and she'd walked out of his life with it in her pocket, and twelve years of silence had followed. In those twelve years Emrys had learned the other kind of competence: the kind that assumed no one was coming. The kind that measured a man not by what he knew but by what he could do with his own two hands when the hands were all he had.

So he worked alone, and he worked well, and he knew it was pride even while he fed it.

First the seams. He raked out the old caulking with a bent screwdriver, driving the blade deep into the gaps between planks, pulling out oakum gone gray and brittle as ash. Then he twisted new oakum into tight cords and drove them home with the caulking iron and a mallet borrowed from Bess, working the fibers into the seams until they swelled and bit. The rhythm of it took him back somewhere he didn't expect — a dig site in the hill country, fourteen years old, kneeling in a trench while his mother showed him how to clear soil from pottery shards with a bamboo pick. *Slow is smooth,* she used to say, in the voice she saved for things she actually cared about. *Fast is broken.* He drove the iron into the seam and did not think about her. He thought about the seam.

By full dark the pitch was melting in a pot over a Coleman stove lashed to the pier rail, and he painted it hot into every seam he'd caulked, working it deep with a putty knife, the smell of it thick and resinous and honest. The starboard chine got a scarf patch: he cut away the rotten wood with a keyhole saw, shaped a piece of ply to fit, bedded it in epoxy, and nailed it home with roofing nails clinched flat. It wasn't pretty. It would hold. Below the waterline, where it mattered, the boat went from a colander to a vessel in six hours of work that left his hands cracked and reeking of tar.

Twice, men drifted down the pier to watch — a deckhand off a freighter, an old fisherman mending net — and twice they offered help, and twice Emrys declined it with a flatness that surprised even him. *"I've got it."* The words came out of him like a reflex, like a scar tissue reflex, and afterward he sat back on his heels in the lamplight and wondered briefly at the anger in his own voice. These men meant nothing by it. But help was a currency and he was done owing anyone anything, even a held plank.

The old fisherman took the refusal without offense. He squatted at a respectful distance, net across his knees, and watched Emrys paint pitch for a while.

"Good seam work," the old man said at last. "Who taught you?"

"A person," Emrys said.

The fisherman nodded as if that answered everything, which, Emrys supposed, it did.

* * *

The crates arrived at midnight, delivered by a truck with no lights and two men who did not speak.

There were eleven of them, each stenciled with the C. Holdings mark and a manifest code, each sealed with steel banding and a lead seal stamped with a number. They were heavier than supplies should be — Emrys hefted the first one and revised his loading plan immediately, because whatever was inside was dense, packed, and non-negotiable. Food drums and fuel went aft; the crates went amidships, low and centered, strapped to the frame rings with webbing he doubled over twice.

One crate rode differently than the others. It was smaller, maybe sixty centimeters square, and when he shifted it, something inside slid with a heavy, deliberate weight — not loose parts, not instruments rattling in foam. Something solid and singular, moving as one mass, like a stone in a box. He set it down and looked at it longer than he should have.

The manifest taped to its top read: *RG-09. FRAGILE. DO NOT OPEN. DO NOT X-RAY.*

*RG.* Rivergate. He peeled the corner of the tape and found beneath it a second layer of tape, and beneath that a hair-thin wire seal that would snap if the tape moved another millimeter. Whoever had packed this wanted to know — instantly and unmistakably — if anyone had tried.

"You're curious," said a voice behind him.

Dario stood at the head of the pier. Emrys hadn't heard him come down, which bothered him more than the man's presence. The night was warm and the river breathed against the pilings, and Dario stood in it with his hands in his pockets like a man visiting his own property, which, Emrys reminded himself, he was.

"I'm a courier," Emrys said. "Curiosity costs extra."

"It cost your father plenty." Dario came down the steps and walked the length of the boat, reading it the way Emrys had read it that morning — the fresh pitch gleaming black in the lamplight, the ply patch, the doubled webbing. He stopped at RG-09 and rested one hand on it lightly, almost fondly. "Eleven crates go up. Eleven crates come back signed for. You open nothing, you photograph nothing, you describe nothing to anyone — including whoever's left at that camp. Especially including her."

"My mother's name doesn't need protecting from me."

"Everything needs protecting from everyone. That's the whole business model." Dario turned, and in the dark his face was mostly shadow and one lit edge. "You did good work tonight. The boat will float now. I told them it would." He started up the steps, then paused. "One more thing. The dry season window on your contract closes in twenty-six days. If you're not back downstream by then, the debt doesn't die, Emrys. It compounds. Daily. With interest calculated by people who enjoy math."

He left. The truck left. The pier settled back into its own sounds — water, rope, the far music of the bars on the front street.

Emrys stood in the lamplight over the crate marked RG-09 and let himself feel the curiosity fully for exactly ten seconds, because denying it entirely would only make it stronger. Something from the Rivergate dig. Something dense and singular and precious enough to seal with wire. Something Corvo's organization was sending *upstream* to a camp that had gone silent three weeks ago — not asking questions about the silence, not sending armed men, just shipping boxes through a stranger who owed them.

Unless the silence was the reason. Unless the boxes weren't supplies at all but payment, or bait, or a question asked in cargo form: *are you still there, Doctor Vasquez? Did you find it?*

He cut the thought off, strapped the crate down, and killed the lamp.

* * *

He slept aboard, in the space under the console, wrapped in the canvas tarp that still smelled of tar and turpentine. The river talked to him all night — slapping the hull, worrying the mooring lines, saying things in its flat wet voice that he couldn't quite translate. Twice he woke sure the boat was sinking, and twice he found the bilge dry, his patches holding, and lay back down with his heart hammering and something that was almost satisfaction cooling slowly in his chest.

Before dawn he loaded the last of it: fuel drums, food, his own kit — a change of clothes, a machete, his father's old river chart, annotated in a dead man's handwriting that Emrys knew better than his own. The contract radio went under the console, its antenna lashed to a pole at the stern. He tested it once. Static, and beneath the static, faintly, the long sigh of the upper valley coming down the airwaves like breath.

At first light he cast off. The engine caught on the second pull, clean and strong, and the patched hull came off the pilings without a groan, riding low but level, the eleven crates lashed amidships like a heart in its chest.

No name on the transom. He'd decided that suited it. A boat named after no one, carrying cargo for no one to see, piloted by a man who owed everyone — heading upriver toward the one person in the world who had taught him that slow is smooth and fast is broken, and who had broken every promise she'd ever made him anyway.

The current took the bow. Somewhere ahead, past the first bend, the river narrowed, and the map in his father's hand marked the beginning of the rapids with a single word in the margin, underlined twice:

*Careful.*
