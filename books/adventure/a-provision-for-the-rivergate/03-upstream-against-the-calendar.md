## 3. Upstream Against the Calendar

The river did not care that he owed money.

That was the first thing Emrys learned about it, and the last honest thing it told him for three days. It ran brown and muscular past the stern of his leaking longboat, indifferent as a creditor, and he loved it immediately in the way a man loves anything that cannot be bribed.

He had been on the water since dawn, and dawn here came early and without ceremony — the jungle simply stopped being night all at once, like a lamp switched by some bored hand. Mist lay on the water in ropes. Somewhere upstream a bird screamed twice and quit. He worked the outboard at half throttle because the manual said anything more would shake the transom loose, and the manual was the only authority left in his life that he trusted.

The contract rode in a waterproof sleeve inside his shirt, against his chest where Corvo's enforcer had put it. *Delivery of goods to Rivergate Expedition, Camp Two, above the Karst Narrows. Completion prior to onset of seasonal rains.* And beneath that, in smaller type, the date: **14th day of the dry season's end.** Eleven days from now. The paper smelled faintly of machine oil, because everything Corvo touched did.

Emrys had read the contract eleven times in the flophouse before signing, looking for the trap. He'd found two. The first was the deadline — miss it and the debt didn't just stand, it *compounded*, the words said so with lawyerly relish. The second was subtler: the delivery point was listed as Camp Two, but the coordinates given were forty kilometers beyond where any legitimate survey would stage supplies. Whoever wrote this contract knew the upper river better than the map did.

He'd signed anyway. That was the arithmetic of being broke: you signed, and then you told yourself you'd read more carefully next time, knowing there wouldn't be a next time until there was, and you'd sign again.

* * *

By midmorning the mist burned off and the river showed its teeth.

The guidebook — a water-stained pilot's handbook he'd bought off a one-eyed trader at the dock for more than it was worth — called this stretch the Ladder. Six rapids in eight kilometers, each one rated in a notation system the book never bothered to explain. Emrys took them the way he took most things: too fast to think about properly, hands white on the tiller, reading the water ahead the way his mother had taught him to read stratigraphy when he was nine years old and still worth her time.

*Look for what's underneath,* she used to say, kneeling in an excavation trench with her trowel, dirt to her elbows. *The surface lies. The surface always lies. The layer beneath tells you what actually happened.*

The river lied constantly. A smooth green tongue of current would hide a submerged shelf that could tear the bottom out of a boat. A line of white water that looked like death might be the safest channel through, churning over gravel too shallow to drown in. You couldn't trust your eyes. You had to read the layers — the way debris collected, the way the current bent around something invisible, the V of calm water pointing downstream through chaos like a signature on a document.

He ran the first rapid badly, shipping a quarter tank of water over the bow and spending ten frantic minutes bailing with a dented cooking pot. The second he ran worse, clipping a rock he'd seen too late, hearing the sickening grind along the keel and then, blessedly, feeling the boat slide free. The third he walked — tied a rope to the bow, hauled her empty through the shallows along the bank while the current tried to take the rope and him together, and stood afterward in the shallows breathing hard, soaked to the waist, absurdly pleased with himself.

That was the part that unsettled him, later, when he let himself think about it. He was *pleased*. Three days out from the worst dock in the province, hauling crates for a smuggler upriver to the mother who had left him standing in a schoolyard twelve years ago with a suitcase and a lie about coming back for him — and the river had made him happy.

Freedom, he decided, bailing the pot dry for the fourth time, was mostly a matter of not having time to notice it.

* * *

He made camp that first night on a gravel bar shaped like a crescent moon, tying the boat with two lines because one felt optimistic. The crates sat amidships under a tarp, seven of them, stenciled with a mark he'd studied until it lost meaning: a stylized gate or archway, struck through with a horizontal bar. He wasn't allowed to open them. The contract said so in language that made Corvo's other threats look gentle.

He opened one anyway.

Not far — just lifted a corner of the lid while the fire was small and the night was loud enough to cover the sound of nails. Inside: packing straw, and beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth, instruments. Brass fittings. Something that looked like a surveyor's transit, its lens cap still on. Precision equipment, expedition-grade, the kind of gear that cost more than his father had ever earned in a year of honest digging.

His father. There was a name for the hole in every ledger Emrys had ever kept. Old Vasquez had died owing Corvo's organization money — how much, no one would say exactly, only that it was *enough*, and that sons inherited debts in this country the same way they inherited eye color. Emrys had spent four years paying it down in increments: running cargo, carrying messages, standing in doorways looking intimidating for men who found his thinness amusing. Four years of watching the number refuse to die.

And now the final installment was sitting in a boat, floating upriver toward Dr. Ilsa Vasquez, who had taken nothing from her husband's estate except a brass compass and her own absence.

He closed the crate. Nailed it shut. Sat back on his heels in the dark and listened to the river go by, enormous and patient, and thought: *she doesn't know I'm coming.*

Corvo's people had arranged the contract. Which meant either they hadn't told her who was delivering her supplies — or they had, and she'd said yes, and he didn't know which possibility he dreaded more.

* * *

The second day the valley narrowed and the current quickened, and the river began to feel less like escape and more like a corridor. Walls of limestone rose on either side, gray-green with moss, dripping. The light came down in blades. He passed a fishing weir abandoned so long ago that trees grew through its stakes, and a shrine on the bank where someone had wedged hundreds of small stones into the cracks of a boulder, each one placed by a hand, each one a wish or a thanks or a bribe to whatever lived in the water.

He almost stopped. Something about the shrine snagged at him — the patience of it, the accumulation. People had been coming here for generations to ask the river for things. He wondered if any of them had ever gotten one.

Instead he ate cold rice in the boat and pushed on, because the calendar in his shirt said eleven days and the river said otherwise. The handbook's map showed the Karst Narrows beginning another thirty kilometers up, and beyond them, marked in faded ink with a confidence the mapmaker probably hadn't earned, the words: **RIVERGATE RUINS — SURVEY IN PROGRESS.**

*In progress.* As if the ruins were a task, something you could finish. His mother had talked about the Rivergate for years before she left — he remembered that much, remembered dinner-table lectures about a civilization that had built gates in a river instead of walls around cities, that had treated water as a neighbor rather than an enemy. He'd been a child. He'd thought it was boring. He'd give a great deal now to be bored at that table again, even for five minutes, even with the fight that always came after.

* * *

On the third afternoon he found the flag.

He'd run the fifth rapid clean — finally, finally reading the water right, threading the needle on the first try, and he'd allowed himself thirty seconds of open-mouthed grin at the sky before the current took the grin away and gave him work again. Below the rapid the river widened into a long slow pool, and driftwood collected along the left bank in a windrow, and it was there, tangled in a fallen tree, that he saw the color.

Blue. Not river-blue, not sky-blue. An artificial blue, the blue of dyed canvas, and it was wrong the way a wrong note is wrong — everything else in the windrow was brown and gray and bone-white, and this was *blue*.

He backed water, edged over, and leaned out to haul it free.

It was a flag, or what remained of one. Torn nearly in half, the fabric rotted soft at the edges, crusted with dried silt. But the emblem survived: a white archway on the field of blue, and beneath it, stitched in thread gone gray, letters he could still make out if he held it against the sun.

**RIVERGATE EXPEDITION.**

Emrys sat very still in the drifting boat.

Here was the problem, and his mind laid it out with the cold clarity it saved for disasters: the mapped campsite — the coordinates on his contract, the destination printed in official ink — lay forty kilometers upstream, above the Karst Narrows. This pool was well below the narrows. Well below. Rivers carried things downstream, yes; a flag could tear loose anywhere and travel. But flags were tied down. Flags were flown at camps, on masts, over tents, and they stayed put unless something tore them loose, and if something had torn this one loose *upstream*, it had traveled through six kilometers of rapids he'd personally run, through water that shredded driftwood, and come out the other side with its stitching intact enough to read.

Or it had come from somewhere else entirely. Somewhere between here and the narrows. Somewhere off the map.

He turned the fabric over. The torn edge was old — weathered, bleached, weeks at least, maybe months. This wasn't fresh wreckage. Whatever had happened, had happened a long time ago, and nobody had come looking, or nobody had looked here.

The radio sat in its bracket by the tiller, the contract radio, guaranteed contact with the expedition office downriver. He'd raised static on it every night since the dock. He keyed it now anyway, out of ritual more than hope, and got static, and set it down, and looked upstream.

The valley went on narrowing ahead. The limestone walls drew closer, and the light between them had already gone the color of late evening though the sun was hours from setting. Somewhere up there, past the narrows, a camp was supposed to be waiting for these crates. A camp with his mother's name on its paperwork.

A camp whose flag had ended up here, torn, months ago, in a place where no camp should have been.

Emrys folded the flag once, twice, and put it inside his shirt next to the contract, where the important papers lived. Then he opened the throttle wider than the manual advised, because the transom could shake itself loose tomorrow, and the deadline was in ten days, and the river — patient, enormous, indifferent — carried him upstream into the narrowing dark.
