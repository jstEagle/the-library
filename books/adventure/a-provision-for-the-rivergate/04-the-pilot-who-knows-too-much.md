## 4. The Pilot Who Knows Too Much

Sefa smelled the stranger before she saw him — diesel and river mud and the particular sourness of a man who'd been sleeping in his own worry for three days straight. She was on the veranda of Kessu's trading post with a cup of black tea going cold in her hands, watching the water, because the water always told her things first.

It told her this: a longboat coming up from the delta, riding too low in the stern, patched hull, one man aboard who handled the tiller like he'd learned it from a book instead of a father. The boat was wrong for these reaches. The man was wronger.

She set down the tea and went inside to count her money.

* * *

Kessu's post sat where the Maren River split around a shelf of black rock, a sprawl of tin roofs and drying nets and crates stacked three deep under canvas that had once been white. Everything came through here eventually — salt, fuel, ammunition, lies. Sefa had been living off the lies for two years now, trading small runs of cargo for gossip, keeping her boat fueled and her ears open, waiting for exactly the kind of boat that was now nosing toward the dock below.

She watched him tie off. He did it competently, which surprised her; most men who tied knots like that didn't pilot like amateurs. He was lean, mid-twenties maybe, dark-haired, with the kind of face that had been handsome before it decided to stop trying. He wore a canvas jacket too heavy for the season and carried himself like someone braced for a blow that kept not arriving.

He climbed the steps to the veranda, saw her, and gave the flat nod of a man who wanted to be left alone.

"Water's rising," Sefa said anyway.

"I noticed."

"Then you noticed it's early." She let that sit. "Monsoon's come up the valley six weeks ahead of the almanac. You're heading upstream into that."

"I'm heading to the Rivergate," he said. "Contract work."

There it was. She kept her face still, but something behind her ribs turned over, slow and cold, like a stone shifting at the bottom of a clear pool. *Rivergate.* Two years of listening in dockside bars and ferry offices and the back room of Kessu's own store, two years of following the money upstream like a blood trail, and now it walked up her stairs wearing a dead man's debt on its shoulders.

"Rivergate's closed," she said.

"So I've heard."

"From whom?"

"The contract says dry season access. The contract doesn't say anything about closures."

"The contract," Sefa said, "was written by people who think rivers are roads." She stood, unhurried, and looked him over properly now, close up. Tired eyes. Hands nicked from patching work done alone. A fresh rope burn across one palm. And underneath all of it, the thing she'd learned to read in smugglers and soldiers and men like Corvo's people: fear, worn smooth by repetition, the way stones wear smooth in a current. He wasn't afraid of the river. He was afraid of what was chasing him up it.

"Sefa," she said, offering nothing else.

"Emrys."

"Just Emrys?"

"Just Emrys."

"Liar," she said pleasantly, and went to find out what he was carrying.

* * *

The crates were stacked on the dock under a tarp while he negotiated fuel with Kessu, and Sefa walked past them the way you walk past anything you're not interested in — which is to say, slowly, with your eyes doing all the work.

Six crates, stenciled with shipping codes she half recognized. Medical supplies, the manifest taped to the top one said. Radio batteries. Dry goods. But manifests were poetry, and poetry lied. What mattered was burned into the corner of each crate, small enough to miss if you weren't looking for it, large enough to be unmistakable if you were:

A coiled serpent around a vertical line. Stamped in black ink, crisp as a signature.

Vantoro Consolidated.

Sefa stopped walking. The dock boards seemed to tilt under her feet, or maybe that was just the old vertigo, the one that came whenever she thought about the village above the Karst Reach — the name it had, the church bell, the terraces her grandmother had planted, all of it sitting now under forty meters of brown water behind a dam that Vantoro's engineers had sworn would never fail, and hadn't failed, exactly. It had been *opened*. On a night in the wet season when every gauge said the reservoir was full, when the floodgates had come up like teeth and the valley below had learned what the word *provision* meant when it appeared in an engineering report. *Provisional release volume.* Her mother had used the same word a week later, drunk, staring at the water line on the church wall. *They called it a provision, Sefa. Like it was set aside for us. Like we were in the will.*

Two years. She'd traced the money through shell companies and holding firms and a dig financing operation, and every thread ended at the same coiled serpent. Vantoro Consolidated had funded the Rivergate expedition. Vantoro Consolidated had paid for whatever Dr. Ilsa Vasquez was digging out of the limestone country above the falls.

And here was a boy ferrying their crates upstream, calling himself Just Emrys.

She looked at him through the fly-specked window of Kessu's store — haggling over fuel drums, gesturing at the price board like the numbers were negotiable, which they were not — and she felt the familiar machinery start turning inside her, the gears she'd oiled every night for two years. He knew nothing. That much was obvious. Nobody who knew what that mark meant would carry it upriver grinning about contract terms. He was a mule. A hired hand. Maybe worse — maybe Corvo's bait, though he didn't have the look. Corvo's people had a smell. This one smelled only of diesel and bad sleep.

But the crates were Vantoro's, and they were going where she'd spent two years trying to go, and the river between here and there was closed to everyone except the people willing to break it open.

Sefa finished her circuit of the dock, lit a cigarette she didn't want, and started planning how to make him need her.

* * *

He came out of the store looking like a man who'd lost a fight with an abacus. "Your friend charges like the fuel comes from heaven."

"Kessu charges what the river lets him. Everything upstream costs double since the closure." She flicked ash off the veranda rail. "You heard that part, then. The closure."

"I heard rumors at the last landing. Some official notice about seasonal hazards."

"Notices." She laughed, short and hard. "You know who posts notices? Governments. You know who actually closes the upper Maren?" She pointed south, downstream, toward the delta and everything that came out of it. "Money. There are gunboats on the middle reach, boy. Not navy boats. Corporate security flying a flag nobody's ever seen before, stopping traffic, turning back pilots, paying off the right officials so the paperwork says it's all perfectly legal. Three weeks now. My cousin tried to run a cargo of palm oil past the second checkpoint and they impounded his boat and held him four days in a floating office that smelled like cigarettes and fear. No charges. No receipt. Just — gone, and then back, and a warning not to come again."

Emrys had gone very still. "Why? What's up there worth that?"

"You tell me. You're the one carrying their boxes."

Something moved behind his eyes — quick, controlled, but not fast enough. She'd spent years reading men who lied for a living; this one lied badly and knew it, which made him either honest or desperate, and usually both.

"It's supplies," he said. "For an archaeological dig. Food, batteries, medical kit. I don't ask what they're digging."

"No," Sefa agreed. "You just take the money."

"I need the money."

"Everyone needs the money. That's how they get us all carrying their serpents up the river." She watched him flinch at *serpents* — a small flinch, quickly buried, but real. So he *had* looked at the mark. He'd seen it and chosen not to understand it. That was its own kind of information.

"The point," she said, tapping ash into the rain barrel, "is that you can't get there. Your contract has a deadline printed on it, I can see it eating you alive, and the direct route is shut. Even if you slip past the first checkpoint, the middle reach is running high and hostile, and there's a stretch of gorge past the confluence that turns into a meat grinder when the water comes up early. Which it has. Six weeks early, in case you missed my opening remarks."

"I didn't miss them."

"But you're going anyway." It wasn't a question. She could see it in him, the same thing she'd seen in the mirror for two years — that particular stubbornness that isn't courage, that's just a wound with momentum. "Of course you are. People like us always go anyway. It's why we're poor."

His jaw tightened. "If you're finished—"

"There's another way." She let the words drop like coins on a table. He stopped moving entirely. "An old channel. The Karst Reach. Before the river was dammed and the maps were redrawn, the Maren ran through the limestone country — a maze of channels through the caves and sinkholes, narrow, fast, unmarked, and completely invisible to anyone sitting in a gunboat on the main stem. It comes out above the falls, two days' hard running from the Rivergate plateau. My grandfather piloted it. My mother piloted it. I've run it eleven times, and I'm the only person alive who's run it more than twice."

"And you'd take me through it."

"For a price."

"How much?"

"Money's the cheap part." She ground out the cigarette and looked at him straight, letting him see everything she chose to show him, which was less than half of what she knew. "I want passage. My boat goes with yours, I pilot both through the Reach, and you don't ask me why. When we come out above the falls, if I decide to keep going to the Rivergate, that's my business. If I turn back, that's mine too. You get your crates delivered, I get where I'm going, and neither of us asks questions we'll regret hearing answers to."

"That's not a price. That's a mystery tax."

"That's the market rate." She smiled without warmth. "Take it or sit here watching the monsoon eat your deadline."

He studied her the way he'd probably studied his contract — looking for the clause that would bite him. Good. Let him look. He wouldn't find this one until it bit.

"Why help me at all?" he asked finally. "What do you get out of the Rivergate?"

"None of your business."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting today." She stood and stretched, casual as a cat, and watched him weigh it. She could see the arithmetic running behind his eyes: the deadline, the checkpoints, the storm season, the debt he clearly owed somebody with better collections agents than banks employed. She could see the moment the arithmetic tipped.

"One condition of mine," he said. "The crates stay sealed. Whatever's in them, whoever they belong to — they reach the camp intact and unopened. That's the job. If you've got some other plan for them—"

"I don't make plans for other people's cargo." *Yet,* she did not say.

"And if I say no?"

"Then enjoy Kessu's hospitality. The rooms are cheap and the roof leaks in interesting patterns."

He almost smiled. It transformed his face for half a second — made him look young, made him look like someone who might once have been easy to love — and then it was gone, banked away like a fire someone had regulations about.

"Okay," he said. "Partner. Temporarily."

"Temporarily," Sefa agreed, and shook his hand, and felt the rope burn ridges against her palm, and thought: *you have no idea what you've just tied yourself to.*

Neither, she admitted privately, did she. That was the honest terror of it. Two years of tracking Vantoro's shadow from the outside, and now she was walking into the middle of it on a leaking longboat beside a boy carrying their serpent-marked crates, and the only thing she knew for certain was that the mark on those boxes and the drowned village in her dreams were the same story, and stories like that always ended at the source.

* * *

They loaded through the late afternoon, her boat — the *Marrow*, narrow and black-hulled and faster than anything her size on the river — lashed alongside his unnamed longboat. He worked in silence, competent and solitary, refusing her help with the fuel drums in a way that wasn't rudeness so much as armor. She recognized that too. She'd worn the same armor once, back when needing people still felt like dying.

At dusk she went aboard the *Marrow* to check her charts, and found him standing at the rail of his boat, looking upstream, where the river went dark between the walls of the gorge and the first far thunder of the coming storm muttered somewhere beyond the hills.

"Hey," she said. He turned. "One free answer, since we're partners. Ask me anything."

He considered it. She watched him decide not to ask about the shortcut, or the checkpoints, or her price. Instead he said, quietly: "That mark on the crates. The serpent. You recognized it. What is it?"

So he *had* seen it. Sefa looked at him for a long moment, the river slapping the hulls beneath them, the storm counting itself closer in the dark.

"A company," she said. "Out of the delta. They build dams." She stepped back onto her own deck. "Sleep well, partner. We run at first light, and the Reach doesn't forgive anyone who's tired."

She went below before he could ask the next question, the obvious one — *what do dams have to do with you?* — and sat in the dark of her cabin with her grandmother's river chart spread across her knees, tracing the old channel through the limestone by lamplight, while above her head the first fat drops of the early monsoon began ticking against the deck like a clock winding up.

Six weeks early. Everything was early this year. The storm, the water, the reckoning.

Somewhere upstream, in the dark above the falls, whatever Vantoro had paid to dig out of the earth was waiting. And tomorrow she would carry its supplies to it herself, on the backs of her grandfather's drowned channels, straight into the throat of the thing she'd sworn to burn down.

The rain thickened. Sefa rolled the chart, blew out the lamp, and listened to the river rise.
