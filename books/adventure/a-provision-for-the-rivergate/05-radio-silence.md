## 5. Radio Silence

The radio came in a wooden case with brass latches, the kind of thing built to outlive its owner. Emrys found it in the third crate from the bow, packed in straw alongside a spare antenna coil and a card of instructions printed in four languages, three of which he couldn't read. The fourth said, in clipped bureaucratic English: *Report position nightly at 1900 hours. Failure to report constitutes breach of contract.*

He set it on the thwart and looked at it for a long time.

Nineteen hundred hours. Somewhere a clerk in a dry office had decided that his voice, once a day, would travel up a wire of static to prove he was still alive, still working, still worth the money advanced against his father's name. The same clerk had probably never seen the river. Probably thought of it as a line on a map, blue and obedient.

Emrys cracked the latches anyway. The set inside was heavy, olive-drab, its dials worn bright at the edges by hands that had used it hard and often. Someone had cared about this machine once. He found the battery compartment, found the batteries, found that they were fresh — someone had cared recently, too.

He wired the antenna along the ridge pole of the little canvas shelter he'd rigged over the stern, using fishing line and a bent nail, and when the sun went down behind the western bluffs he turned the crank until the set hummed, and pressed the transmit key, and said the words printed on his contract.

*Supply boat inbound. Position: below the second gorge. Request confirmation of camp status. Over.*

Static answered.

Not the clean static of an empty channel — he knew that sound, the soft hiss of a river of nothing — but a rougher thing, a grinding, granular static, like gravel poured down a tin roof. It filled the cabin of the night, rolled against the canvas, and said nothing at all.

He tried again at intervals until midnight. Then he wrapped the set in its oilcloth, wedged it under the thwart, and lay back to watch the stars wheel over the gorge and tell himself that radio was a temperamental science, that mountains ate signals, that the monsoon clouds building in the south scattered transmissions like shotguns scatter birds.

He almost believed it.

* * *

The third night, he raised a voice that wasn't static.

It came through in fragments, a man's voice, clipped and hurried, talking to someone Emrys couldn't hear — or couldn't distinguish, because the voice kept sliding under the static like a fish under a boat.

*"...repeat, the upper reaches are — do not —"* A burst of noise swallowed whole sentences. *"— contract is void above the — acknowledge, acknowledge —"*

Emrys keyed the transmit. "This is the supply boat. This is Vasquez. I read you. Say again your last. Over."

The static ground on. The voice was gone, or had never been meant for him, or had been meant for him precisely and had said exactly what its speaker wanted: enough to frighten, not enough to inform.

He sat with the handset in his hand for a long time after that, listening to the river work against the hull. The contract said *report nightly*. It didn't say what to do if someone reported *back*.

He thought about the torn expedition flag he'd found snagged on the rocks two days below the mapped campsite — the Vasquez expedition colors, sun-bleached and ripped, snagged far below where any surveyor had said the camp should be. He'd told himself it was flood debris. Rivers carry everything downstream eventually; that was the first law of the water and the last law of excuses.

He keyed the transmit one more time. "Camp Rivergate, this is supply boat Vasquez. If anyone is reading, I am continuing upstream. I have your stores. Over."

Nothing. The gravel-pour static, patient as erosion.

He slept badly and dreamed of his mother's hands — the only thing he remembered clearly about her now, twelve years on, the detail that had survived every other erosion: long fingers, ink-stained at the second knuckle, turning the pages of a survey folio while he sat on the floor of a house that was about to stop being his. She had left with a canvas bag and his father's brass compass and a sentence he'd spent twelve years turning over like a stone looking for what lived under it: *This is bigger than us, Emrys.*

He'd been nine. He hadn't known then that *bigger than us* was the shape every abandonment takes when it wants to feel like a virtue.

* * *

By the fifth day he'd developed rituals, and he hated himself for it, and he kept them anyway.

He checked the antenna line at dawn, walking it hand over hand from the set to the ridge pole, feeling for frays. He cleaned the contacts with the edge of his knife. He tested the batteries against the little meter built into the case lid and logged the readings in the margin of his contract, because the contract had margins and the margins might as well hold something true.

At 1900 hours he called the camp. At 1930 he called again. At 2000 he tried the emergency band, then the general band, then the emergency band again, sweeping the dial like a man searching a dark room for a lamp he'd left somewhere and couldn't quite remember.

Static. Static. Static.

On the sixth night, at the edge of exhaustion, he caught himself whispering into the handset — not the contract language, not *position* and *over*, but her name. *Ilsa. Ilsa Vasquez. If you're there.* The whisper of a nine-year-old in an empty house, and he wrenched the handset away from his mouth as if it had bitten him, and keyed off, and sat in the dark with his jaw clenched until the cramp in it made his eyes water.

The river didn't care. The river went on doing what rivers do, which is everything, always, without asking.

He was past the second gorge now, past the last trading post, past the last of Sefa's warnings — the pilot's voice still in his head from that dockside conversation, *the upper reaches are closed, and the river closes them, not any government*, and the look she'd given his crates, the half-second her eyes had rested on the financier's mark stamped in blue wax on the strapping. She'd said nothing about it. That silence had been louder than the radio.

He was past the last safe anchorage, too. He knew it the way you know a floor is rotten — by the feel of it. The map in his contract showed a marked mooring at the mouth of the third gorge, a cut in the eastern bank where the current slackened and a boat could lie quiet through the night. Below it, the annotations stopped. Above it, the mapmaker had simply given up: blank river, blank country, a single notation in faded ink — *unreliable in high water*.

The water was high. He could read that without instruments. The river had come up a hand's width in two days, and the color had changed, from the green of deep pools to a tea-brown carrying silt and whole trees. Debris moved past him in the current — branches, a fence post, once a drowned goat, bloated and turning slowly, its legs rigid as a chair. The monsoon was early. Everyone had told him the monsoon was early.

He tied up at the last anchorage at dusk on the seventh day, and cooked rice, and did not eat it, and at 1900 he called the camp, and static answered, and he called again, and static answered, and he sat with the handset against his ear for an hour, letting the gravel-pour noise fill his skull, until he understood — not decided, understood, the way you understand a current by being in it — that the static itself was the message.

Someone was jamming the band.

He didn't know how he knew. He was no radio man. But there was a quality to the interference that felt deliberate, a flatness, a wall built across the frequency, and walls are built by people, and people build them to hide something or to keep something in. The expedition had gone quiet not because it was gone but because someone wanted it quiet.

And the crates in his hold — the ones he wasn't allowed to open, the ones with the blue wax mark — had been addressed to a camp that no longer answered, on a river that was closing, in a season that had started killing early.

He looked at the mooring line in his hands. He looked at the dark mouth of the third gorge, where the water went through the bluffs in a single brown throat, and the sound of it came up the river like distant artillery.

Every warning he'd been given stood in a row behind him like witnesses at a trial: Sefa at the trading post, the voice on the band, the mapmaker's faded ink, the drowned goat going by, the contract's own deadline printed in red — *all upstream passages void after the onset of the wet season*.

He cast off the mooring line anyway.

The boat took the current before he was fully ready for it to take the current, and the last anchorage slid away behind him, and the gorge swallowed the last of the light, and he was alone on the water with forty crates of someone else's secrets and a radio that spoke only in static and a name he couldn't stop saying in the dark.

At 1900 hours the next evening, wedged in an eddy below the gorge with his hands still shaking, he keyed the transmit one final time.

"Camp Rivergate, this is Vasquez. I'm coming. Over."

And the static — for the first time in seven nights — broke.

Just for a second. Just a fracture in the wall, a sliver of signal, and through it, faint as a voice through stone, a woman's voice, dry and clipped and unmistakably alive, saying words he couldn't make out before the jamming closed over it like water over a dropped coin.

He sat bolt upright in the dark, heart slamming, and swept the dial, and swept it again, and got nothing but the wall, but it didn't matter, it didn't matter at all, because he knew that voice the way you know a voice you've spent twelve years failing to forget.

His mother was alive, and she was transmitting, and something upstream wanted the whole world deaf to it.

The wind came down the gorge then, hard from the south, smelling of iron and drowned earth, and the first fat drops of the early monsoon hit the canvas over his head like fingers drumming on a coffin lid.
