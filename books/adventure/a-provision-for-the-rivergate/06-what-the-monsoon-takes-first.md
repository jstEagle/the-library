## 6. What the Monsoon Takes First

The first sign was the birds.

Emrys had been running the river for six days by then, long enough to learn its moods the way you learn a stranger's face — slowly, and mostly wrong. He'd learned that the water went glassy and green in the late afternoon before it went dark, that the fish rose at dusk in silver splashes like coins thrown from a submerged hand, that the kingfishers worked the shallows at first light with a blue stutter of wings. He had begun, foolishly, to feel competent.

On the sixth evening the kingfishers were gone. So were the herons, the swifts, everything with wings. The air lay on the river like a wet cloth, and the forest on both banks had gone silent in a way that wasn't peace — it was the silence of animals making themselves small.

He remembered the contract's fine print then. *Contractor acknowledges seasonal weather variability. Dry season transit window closes without exception.* He'd read it as bureaucratic padding when he signed. Now he did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding: the window was supposed to hold for another nine days.

The storm was not going to wait nine days.

* * *

He made camp badly, though he didn't know it yet — in a bend where the river widened and slowed, behind a gravel spit that looked like shelter and would turn out to be a trap. He got the tarp rigged over the crates, ate cold rice out of the tin, checked the radio out of habit more than hope. Static. Six nights of static now, and he'd stopped speaking into it, stopped even saying *Rivergate, Rivergate, do you copy* to the white noise, because hearing his own voice come back at him thin and unanswered had started to feel like a kind of madness.

The first drops came fat and warm, spattering the tarp like thrown stones. Then the sky simply opened, the way a door opens, and the world behind it was all noise.

He'd grown up in a city that rained. He thought he knew rain. This was not rain; this was the river deciding to fall upward for a while. It hit the canopy above him and kept coming through it anyway, a second rain beneath the first, and within minutes the ground around his fire was a shining black mirror and the fire itself was a memory.

Then the river stood up.

There was no other way to describe what he saw in the lightning flashes. One moment the water lapped the gravel spit a boat-length away; the next it was climbing, a brown wall of it sliding over the spit, over the place where his fire had been, shoving driftwood and whole uprooted trees ahead of it like a crowd shoving through a gate. The river's voice changed too — from its constant low murmur to something with edges in it, something that growled.

Emrys moved without deciding to move. That was the thing he would remember afterward: there was no thought in it at all, just his hands untying the bowline and his boots slipping in mud and the boat grinding against the bank as the current took an interest in it. He threw the crates aboard — two of them, three, the fourth one already floating — and shoved off into the flood because staying meant being pinned against the trees or swept under them, and the open channel, insane as it seemed, was the safer bet.

The river disagreed about the word *safer.*

* * *

What happened over the next four hours happened in pieces, each one lit by lightning and separated by stretches of pure black noise.

A piece: the boat surfing sideways down a channel that no longer existed, past a tree standing chest-deep where the bank had been, its leaves still green and streaming.

A piece: the bailer — a cut-down kerosene tin he'd bought at the trading post without knowing why, some instinct he'd be grateful for later — in his hands, scooping, throwing, scooping, throwing, while the rain filled the hull faster than he could empty it.

A piece: the crate marked with the financier's seal — the one Sefa had looked at too long and said nothing about — shifting, sliding, wedging against the gunwale, and Emrys wedging himself against *it*, because if the cargo shifted wrong in water this violent the boat would roll and rolling meant swimming and swimming meant dying, plainly, without drama.

A piece: the tiller tearing loose. Or maybe he let go of it. He never knew which. The boat spun, the river picked it up like a hand picking up a stick, and for a long time — ten seconds, a minute, an hour — Emrys Vasquez was not steering anything. He was cargo.

Somewhere in the black between two lightning strikes, the crate with the seal went over the side. He heard it go — a scrape, a hollow knock against the hull, then nothing, swallowed. Half his pay was in that crate. More than half his credibility, when Corvo's people counted what arrived at the end of this run. He reached after it, actually leaned out into the dark over moving water, and caught himself with his other hand on the thwart, fingers screaming, and hauled himself back in, and hated himself for both things: for reaching, and for stopping.

Then the leak announced itself.

It had been there since day one — a weep in a seam near the keel that he'd patched with pitch and pride, alone, refusing the yard man's help because asking for help felt like admitting the boat was bigger than he was. The patch held for six days of polite water. The flood peeled it back like a lid coming off a jar.

Water came in through the seam in a bright rope under the lightning, and Emrys understood, with a clarity that felt almost calm, that the night had stopped being about the cargo. It was about the bailer now. It was about whether his arms lasted longer than the river's patience.

So he bailed.

* * *

Bailing is not dramatic work. That's what no one tells you. It is the opposite of dramatic. It is a tin can and a rhythm and the slow discovery of exactly how many muscles you have between your shoulder and your wrist, each one taking its turn to fail. Scoop, throw. Scoop, throw. The rhythm became a prayer without words in it, because words required breath and breath required pausing and pausing meant the water gained.

The rain eased sometime after midnight — not stopped, but eased, the sky spending itself out — and the river dropped its voice half a register, and Emrys found that he had survived the worst of it without any skill whatsoever. That was the lesson the night pressed on him, over and over, like a thumb on a bruise: competence meant nothing here. He had read the water for six days. He had patched the hull with his own hands. He had anchored properly, eaten properly, kept the schedule, done everything right, and the river had taken half his cargo anyway and nearly taken him, the way a landslide takes a house — not out of malice, not out of care, just because it was moving and the house was in the way.

His father used to say luck was a currency like any other. Emrys had spent twelve years believing the opposite — that you earned your way clear of trouble or you drowned in it, and either outcome was yours. Tonight the river had taught him the third option, the one he'd built his whole stubborn life to avoid: sometimes you survive because the water happens to miss you, and there is nothing in your hands worth calling credit.

He didn't trust luck. He'd watched it spend itself on other people his whole childhood and skip his house entirely.

Scoop, throw. Scoop, throw. His arms were two strangers attached to him. The leak seeped but no longer gushed — the flood had found its level, and so had the boat, riding low and ugly but riding. Dawn was a gray rumor in the east, then a fact.

* * *

He grounded the boat on a mudbank as the light came up, in a stretch of flooded forest where the trunks stood in brown water and the current ran gentle through them, and he took inventory the way his mother had taught him to take inventory of a dig site — methodically, writing nothing down, letting the loss arrange itself into a shape he could carry.

Gone: the sealed crate. Gone: the food crate, which had been lashed on the same side and gone the same way. Gone: the spare tarp, the tool roll, the coil of good line. Remaining: fuel, enough. The radio, dry inside its oilcloth. One crate of expedition supplies, unopened, still lashed down because it had been heavy enough to argue with the flood and win. A bag of rice gone soggy but edible. The bailer. Himself.

Half. By weight, by value, by every measure Corvo's people would use at the far end of this river — half.

He sat in the bilge water with his back against the thwart and laughed once, a short ugly sound that startled a bird out of the flooded branches. Twelve years of telling himself he needed no one, that self-reliance was armor, that his mother had left because she was weak and he would never be weak — and one night of weather had stripped it all down to a man with a tin can, alive by accident, floating on a boat named after no one.

The radio crackled when he touched it. Just static. But he held it to his ear a long time anyway, listening to the white noise the way you listen to a shell for the sea, and for the first time he noticed something in it — or thought he did. Underneath the static, faint as a pulse, a rhythmic clicking. Three clicks. Pause. Three clicks.

He told himself it was interference from the storm cells still stacked upriver. He told himself it was a loose connection in the set. He told himself a lot of things, sitting in the rising sun with his hands shaking around a kerosene tin, and none of the things held.

Because the clicking had a pattern, and patterns were made by people, and the only people who should be anywhere on this frequency were forty miles upstream at a camp that hadn't answered in six nights.

Three clicks. Pause. Three clicks.

Emrys looked upriver, where the water ran high and brown and fast toward the gorge country, toward the closed reaches Sefa had warned him about, toward the last safe anchorage he had already passed against every warning printed and spoken. The storm had cost him half his cargo. It had not cost him his reason for coming.

He started bailing again. There was still water in the boat, and a long way left to go.
