## 7. Strangers in a Leaking Hull

*Emrys*

The storm didn't end. It just changed its mind about how to kill him.

By dawn the rain had thinned from a solid wall to a gray curtain, and Emrys Vasquez sat ankle-deep in his own boat, bailing with a cracked cooking pot because it was all he had left. The river had swallowed half his cargo in the night — two crates of dried rations gone over the gunwale when the wave hit, a third he'd cut loose himself when it started dragging the stern under. The crates he wasn't allowed to open bobbed away downstream like coffins, and he'd watched them go with something close to relief.

The longboat — *A Boat Named After No One*, as he'd come to think of her, because naming her would mean admitting she mattered — rode low and sullen in the brown water. His patch job on the hull was holding. Barely. Every time he stopped bailing to listen, he could hear the river seeping back in through some seam he hadn't found yet, patient as a debt collector.

He'd been adrift for six hours. The current had taken him off the main channel somewhere in the dark, and now the banks were wrong — too steep, too green, the trees hanging low enough to comb his hair if he stood up. This was shortcut country. Sefa's country, if the woman at the trading post had been telling the truth about where the map ended.

"Closed," she'd said. "Upper reaches are closed this time of year. You want to die fast or slow?"

He'd refused her price then. He was beginning to reconsider what a man's pride was worth against a flooded gorge.

The engine had died at midnight, drowned. He'd gotten the cover off and the water out by lamplight, but the thing wouldn't turn over, and every pull of the cord cost him balance he couldn't spare. So he drifted, and bailed, and listened to the river talk to itself in the voice of something enormous turning over in its sleep.

That was when he heard the other engine.

It came up behind him through the rain — not Corvo's gunboat growl but something lighter, higher, a long-tail motor worked hard and worked well. He turned, pot still in hand, water sloshing around his shins, and saw a slim river skiff cutting the current at an angle no sane pilot would choose, riding the seams between the flood waves like a needle through cloth.

Sefa stood at the tiller. Of course she did. She wore the rain like it owed her money.

She brought the skiff alongside with a precision that made his own handling look like drunk fumbling, one hand out to catch his gunwale before the hulls kissed. Her eyes went past him — to the bilge, to the dead engine, to the three surviving crates lashed down and the empty space where five had been.

"You're sinking," she said.

"I'm bailing."

"You're bailing a hole." She said it without cruelty, the way you'd tell a man his leg was broken. "Where's your pump?"

"Went overboard with everything else."

She looked at him a long moment. Rain ran off the brim of her cap and fell between them into the shared water. He knew what she was calculating; he could see it move behind her eyes like fish under ice. Every hour she spent near him was an hour closer to whatever she wanted upstream, and every hour he stayed afloat alone was an hour closer to the bottom of the gorge.

"Your engine's flooded," she said. "You'll get maybe two pulls before the cord strips. And even if she turns over, you'll be running blind into the gorge with a hull that drinks faster than you do." She jerked her chin at the water rising past his ankles. "That's not a question of skill. That's arithmetic."

"And your price?"

Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Same as before. Only now I collect it either way, because either you come with me or you feed the river, and the river doesn't pay debts. It eats them."

He should have hated her for it. He did hate her for it, a little — the way you hate anyone who catches you falling. But his hands were shaking from the night, and the pot had a crack in it, and the seam he hadn't found was singing its quiet song under the floorboards.

"What's the work?" he asked.

"You bail. I steer. You don't touch my tiller, I don't touch your pride." She stepped across onto his gunwale without asking, light as a bird landing, and looked down into his bilge like a doctor reading a chart. "Tie your line to my stern. When we hit the gorge, you cut loose and run your own boat or you ride mine — your choice, made fast, no arguing in the rapids. Arguing in rapids is how people drown."

"That's two prices."

"That's one price with terms." She was already moving to his engine, pulling the plugs, tipping water out with brisk, unceremonious hands. "Dry these. We've got an hour of slack water before the gorge fills with the morning runoff, and I intend to be through it while it's merely trying to kill us instead of committed to it."

Emrys stood in his flooding boat and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like grief, that he was going to say yes. Not because he trusted her. Because the alternative was arithmetic too, and hers was better than his.

"Tow me," he said.

* * *

*Sefa*

She'd been tracking his wake since yesterday, which was a sentence she never intended to say aloud.

Not him specifically — any fool running supplies upriver in the first week of an early monsoon was worth watching, the way you watch a drunk walking a ledge. But when she'd seen the crates at the trading post, seen the financier's mark burned into the slats — the coiled serpent over the crossed keys, Hollis & Vane Consolidated, the mark that had been stamped on the dredge permits that killed her village — she'd known exactly whose errand this boy was running, even if he didn't know himself.

So she'd followed. Told herself it was reconnaissance. Told herself a lot of things, standing in the rain all night with her engine idling, listening to a stranger bail his boat with a cooking pot and refuse to die.

He was stubborn. That was the first thing she'd learned about Emrys Vasquez, and she learned it the way you learn anything true: by being annoyed by it. Most men in his position — alone, swamped, half his gear gone — would have abandoned the boat and swum for the bank hours ago. He'd stayed. Patched, bailed, cursed at his engine in two languages, and stayed. There was a word for that kind of stupid and the word was *pride*, and Sefa recognized it the way you recognize your own face in a bad mirror.

Now she had him on a towline, and she resented how much easier the morning felt with him there.

Not easier — safer. That was the honest word, and honesty was a discipline she practiced the way other people practiced religion: rarely, and with guilt. With his weight on the line behind her, she could take lines through the flood channels she'd never risk solo, use his drag as a brake in the boils, split the risk of debris strikes between two hulls instead of gambling everything on one. Two boats were a system. A system had redundancy. Redundancy was the only prayer the river ever answered.

And she resented that too, because it meant she needed him, and needing people was how her family had ended up standing on a levee watching the water come for their houses with permits in its pocket.

"Slack water ends in forty minutes," she called back. "Check your patch."

He checked it. She heard him working — the wet slap of pitch, the scrape of the knife. He worked quietly, efficiently, without asking her to watch or admire, and she caught herself cataloguing that too. Hands that had done manual work before this trip. Calluses in the wrong places for a dockhand. A man who'd learned boats somewhere, badly, recently, and learned everything else somewhere else, long ago, and kept both lessons locked behind his teeth.

"Who taught you to read water?" she called, mostly to fill the silence, mostly because silence let her think, and thinking led to places she didn't want to go before breakfast.

"My mother," he said. Then, after a pause long enough to have a whole biography inside it: "Fieldwork. She dragged me along summers. I hated it."

"She taught you well enough to stay alive this long."

"She taught me well enough to know when I'm in over my head." A bitter note. "I'm just choosing to ignore it."

Sefa laughed — one short bark, surprised out of her. She hadn't laughed in days. She decided to hold it against him.

The gorge announced itself before they saw it: a change in the river's voice, the mutter becoming a roar, and a fine cold spray riding the air ahead of the mist. The banks pinched together. The water, already high with monsoon melt, began to move with the purposeful speed of something that had found its throat.

"Listen," she said. "The channel runs left of center until the first bend, then hard right along the cliff shadow. There's a boil below the second drop that will try to spin you. If it catches your bow, don't fight it — give it your stern and paddle out the far side. And when I shout *cut*, you cut the line, because after that point I can't be dragging your weight and mine."

"And if I don't cut?"

"Then we both find out what the bottom of the gorge looks like, and I'll haunt you specifically."

Behind her, in the grey light, she saw him check his knife in its sheath. Twice. A man making a promise to himself.

They went in.

* * *

*Emrys*

The gorge ate the sky.

One moment they were on a river; the next they were inside the river, walls of black limestone rising on either side so close he could have touched them with an oar, water compressed between them into something that had stopped pretending to be liquid and become pure intention. The roar came from everywhere. Spray filled the air like weather. Ahead, Sefa's skiff threaded the chaos with a certainty that looked less like navigation than prophecy.

He steered in her wake and understood immediately why she'd demanded obedience. There was no room here for two opinions. The channel she'd described appeared exactly where she'd said — left of center, a ribbon of smoother water no wider than his boat — and he took it because the alternative was the white wall beside it, and the white wall was not a metaphor.

The first drop hit like a staircase collapsing. His stomach arrived late. Water came aboard in tons, and he bailed without deciding to, hands moving on their own, the cracked pot flashing in the grey. Behind the fear, a cold clear thought surfaced: *she was right, this is arithmetic.* Two hulls, one wake, no arguments. It was working. They were working.

He resented that with his whole chest.

Then the second drop came, and the cliff shadow, and the boil.

He saw it before it saw him — a place where the river seemed to hesitate, a smooth round dimple the size of a courtyard, rotating slowly, like an eye opening. Sefa skirted it clean. He was half a boat-length off her line when the edge caught his bow and the world began to turn.

The gorge rotated with him. Walls, spray, the white wall, Sefa's skiff swinging across his vision — once, twice. His oar was gone. The water had his stern now, exactly as she'd said it would, and every muscle in his body screamed to fight the spin, to dig, to force the boat straight.

*CUT,* she screamed, and the word arrived through the roar like a hand on his collar.

He cut.

The towline parted and his boat swung free, tail-first, riding the boil's rotation out toward the calm seam beyond it — her voice in his head steering him now, *give it your stern, paddle out the far side*, and he found the spare paddle lashed under the gunwale and dug with everything the night hadn't taken, and the rotation loosened, and loosened, and spat him out into slack green water below the falls like something the river had chewed and decided against swallowing.

Sefa's skiff slid alongside thirty seconds later. She looked him over — the empty oarlock, the heaving bilge, the way his hands wouldn't quite stop moving — and whatever she saw, she filed away without comment.

"Not bad," she said.

"Don't," he said.

"I'm just observing—"

"*Don't.*"

She held up both hands, palms out, and for the first time since the trading post, something in her face was not armor. It lasted perhaps two seconds. Then she nodded downstream, where the gorge opened its jaws and let the river out into a wide, drowned valley, and the mist lifted, and the rain began again, softer, almost apologetic.

"Half a day to the fork," she said. "Rivergate camp is up the eastern branch. Assuming," she added, and the armor came back down over her eyes like a visor, "there's a camp to reach."

* * *

*Emrys*

They moored at midafternoon on a gravel shelf beneath an overhang, taking the break neither of them would admit to needing. Sefa built a fire from driftwood with the economy of someone who'd done it ten thousand times, and heated water, and produced tea from somewhere inside her coat without explanation, and Emrys drank it because refusing would have been one more argument and he was fresh out.

His boat sat on the gravel, patched again, bailed dry for the first time in two days. Together — and neither of them acknowledged the word *together*, they simply performed its actions — they'd found the leak: a seam above the third strake, sprung where the debris strike in the night had done damage he'd misread. She'd held the plank while he drove the caulking home. Their hands had nearly touched twice. Both times, both of them had found something urgent to look at elsewhere.

"The crates," she said, without preamble, staring at the fire. "The ones you wouldn't open. What's in them?"

"Supplies. Contract says sealed till delivery."

"To whom?"

"Dr. Ilsa Vasquez. Rivergate expedition." He said the name flatly, drained of everything, the way you'd recite a sentence you'd been forced to memorize. "She's my mother."

Sefa turned and looked at him fully for the first time since the gorge. The firelight moved on her face. He braced for the questions — *your mother? twelve years? why?* — the ones everyone asked, the ones with knives in them.

Instead she said: "Hollis & Vane is financing her dig."

It wasn't a question either. But it landed like one, because of how she said it — the way you'd name a disease.

"You know them," he said.

"I know the mark." She fed a stick to the fire. Her voice had gone somewhere careful, the way water goes still before it drops. "Coiled serpent, crossed keys. They financed a dredging project upstream, six years ago. Flood control, they called it. My village was in the way of the reservoir." She said it evenly, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "There were hearings. There were permits. There was nothing."

The rain ticked on the overhang. Downstream, the river ran high and brown and indifferent, carrying everything toward the sea.

"So when I saw their mark on your crates," Sefa went on, "I followed you. Not to rob you. To see where their money goes. What it digs up." She looked at him across the fire, and her eyes were the exact color of the gorge water, and just as deep, and just as unwilling to be fought. "Whatever Dr. Vasquez found at Rivergate, Hollis & Vane paid for it. And men like that don't pay for knowledge, Emrys. They pay for leverage."

He thought of the contract folded in his breast pocket, soft with sweat now. The dry-season deadline. The sealed crates. The radio frequency he'd raised every night to static.

"They're also holding my father's debt," he said. "Or their man Corvo is. Ferry the supplies, and it's paid."

Sefa was quiet for a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — lower, stripped of its blade.

"Corvo doesn't collect debts," she said. "Corvo collects *people*. If he let you carry his crates upriver to a dig site he could have reached with his own boats, then he didn't need a ferryman." She poked the fire, and sparks climbed into the rain and died. "He needed someone the expedition would open the door for. Someone with a familiar name."

The words settled over the fire like ash. Emrys looked at his hands — the rope burns, the caulking cuts, the hands that had patched and bailed and paddled and survived — and felt the shape of the last two weeks rearrange itself around him, the way a landscape rearranges when the fog lifts and shows you which direction the cliff actually was.

"We should reach the camp by tomorrow evening," he said finally. "If the radio's still dead—"

"It's not dead." Sefa said it quietly, and the certainty in it was worse than dread. "Dead radios are random. That one's been silent on schedule. Somebody upriver doesn't want to be found — or somebody downriver wants to make sure nobody checks why."

She kicked dirt over the fire, and the overhang went blue with dusk, and the river talked on in the dark like something that knew the ending and wasn't telling.

"Sleep," she said. "Tomorrow we find out which."
