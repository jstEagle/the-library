## 8. The Ransacked Gate

They came around the last bend at first light, and the river gave them the camp the way a hand opens to show what it has been holding.

Emrys saw it before Sefa cut the engine — or before she would have, if the engine had been running. She'd let them drift the final half-mile, paddle silent through water gone glassy and brown with silt, so that the camp assembled itself out of the morning mist piece by piece: the ridgepole of a mess tent jutting at a broken angle like a snapped wing, the black scar of a fire pit drowned in last night's rain, the pale rectangles on the ground where equipment crates had stood and no longer stood. Above it all, cut into the cliff face itself, rose the Rivergate — a wall of dressed stone older than any nation on the map, carved with channels and sluice-mouths that had once swallowed a river whole. It looked less like a ruin than like a machine that had stopped.

Nobody was moving down there.

"Cut the current," Emrys said quietly, though there was nothing to cut. His voice came out wrong, flattened, as if the words had traveled too far to arrive with any weight behind them.

Sefa shipped her paddle and let the boat swing. "I see it."

"Maybe they're—"

"I see it," she said again, gentler this time, which from Sefa was worse than shouting.

He made himself look properly. Not at the shapes he'd been inventing since the radio went dead — Ilsa at a field desk, Ilsa arguing with someone off-camera, Ilsa alive because the alternative was unthinkable — but at what was actually there. Six tents, four of them collapsed. A surveyor's tripod lying in the mud with one leg snapped. Rope ends trailing from a stripped cache rack, cut clean rather than untied. And everywhere the particular disorder of a place taken apart by people who knew what they wanted and didn't care about the rest: papers scattered and soaked, a typewriter case open and empty, boots — just boots — floating in a flooded storage pit.

No bodies. He held onto that. Whatever else the camp was saying, it was not saying dead.

Sefa brought them alongside a shelf of rock below the camp terrace and held the painter while he stepped off. His legs were unsteady — three days of monsoon bail-work and a night adrift had left him feeling borrowed — but the ground held, and then his feet were carrying him up the slope faster than sense allowed, and Sefa's voice came after him saying something about waiting, about looking first, and he didn't listen because for twelve years he had not listened when it mattered and he was done starting now.

The camp smelled of wet canvas and diesel and something else underneath, faint and sweetish — rotting food, maybe, or flowers crushed into the mud. Rain had gotten into everything. The nearest tent had been slashed from ridge to floor, one long deliberate stroke, the kind of cut you make to prove you can. Inside, bedrolls were heaped and soaked, a mosquito net hung in shreds, and someone's reading glasses lay folded on an upturned crate, intact, patient, absurd. He picked them up without meaning to. They were heavy-framed, prescription, the kind of glasses a man wore for close work. There was no blood anywhere. That was the thing his hands kept checking for, touching tent fabric, crate edges, the mud — and finding none.

"Emrys." Sefa had come up behind him. She moved through the wreckage the way she moved through rapids — reading it, not fighting it. "Look at the cuts."

"What?"

"The tents. The rope." She crouched by the cache rack. "All one direction. All the same height." She drew her hand across her own throat, level. "Tall men, working fast, working calm. This wasn't bandits. Bandits burn what they can't carry and leave in a hurry. Whoever did this took their time loading a boat."

"A boat," Emrys repeated. "There's no boat here."

"No. There isn't." She stood, scanning the waterline, and he watched her do the arithmetic he couldn't make himself do: how many people, how much cargo, which direction, how long ago. The mud along the bank was churned and healing over already, rain-blurred, but she read it anyway, frowning. "Two days. Maybe three. Before the big storm."

Before the storm. Before the night he'd spent bailing for his life half a mile from help he didn't know existed. Something turned over in his chest, cold and slow.

* * *

They worked the camp together through the morning, and the work kept him sane the way bailing had kept him sane — hands busy, mind leashed. Sefa catalogued what was missing; Emrys catalogued what was left, because the difference between the two lists was a message, if only he could read it.

Gone: every instrument worth stealing. The theodolite, the sample cases, the photographic plates, the expedition's second radio — its guts torn out and carried off, its casing left smashed against a stone. Gone: all the finds, whatever they had been. Empty crates marked in faded grease pencil with catalog numbers sat stacked neatly by the landing, ready for transport, never loaded. Gone: eleven people, or however many the dig had employed. Eleven bedrolls, and every one of them accounted for by absence.

Left: the glasses. Left: a child's tin whistle, of all things, lying in the mud near the fire pit — some pilot's gift, some joke, meaningless. Left: the typewriter, empty. Left: paper. That was the strangest part. Papers everywhere, hundreds of them, field notes and correspondence and carbon copies, all of it soaked, all of it worthless to thieves — except that whoever had ransacked the camp had gone through it first. Emrys could tell because the pages weren't scattered randomly. They were sorted. Piles. Some burned in the fire pit — he found the charred edge-cases, diagrams by their ghost-lines, tables of numbers. What remained was what hadn't mattered enough to burn or carry.

And left: the grave.

Sefa found it. She called his name once, quietly, and he came down the slope behind the mess tent to where the ground rose toward the cliff, and there it was.

It was fresh. That was the first thing — horribly, unmistakably fresh. The earth was dark and settled wrong, mounded with the particular neatness of ground dug by people who knew how to dig, tamped at the edges, ringed with stones pried from the riverbed. No marker post. No name. Just the mound, and at its head, driven into the soil upright, a compass.

Brass. Old. The lid open, the needle gone wild beneath cracked glass, pointing at nothing, at everything, swinging slowly with some tremor in the earth or in his own hands.

Emrys knew it the way you know your own signature.

His father had bought it in a port city before Emrys was born, back when Tomás Vasquez still believed he'd be a ship's officer instead of a debtor. It had lived on the shelf above their stove. It had been the one beautiful thing in a house full of pawn tickets, and his father had taught him to hold it flat, wait for the needle to settle, and *trust the quiet*. When his father died owing money to a smuggler, the compass had been the one thing Emrys had asked to keep — and it had already been gone. Sold? Taken? He'd assumed Corvo's collectors had stripped the house. He'd grieved it, stupidly, more than some of the furniture of his father's memory.

Now he understood. Ilsa had taken it. Twelve years ago, walking out, she had walked out with his father's compass in her pocket, and she had carried it across the world to this river, and now it stood planted in a grave with her name written nowhere and everywhere.

The ground tilted. He heard Sefa say his name again and it arrived from very far away.

"She's dead." His own voice, sounding like someone else's. "This is — they buried her here. She's under this."

"Or it's what they want us to think is under this," Sefa said.

He rounded on her, and the grief in him came out as fury, hot and grateful for somewhere to go. "You don't know her. You don't know anything. You've been following this financier's money like a bloodhound and now you want everyone to be a liar because liars are easier than—"

"Than graves?" she said. "Yes. Easier than graves." She didn't flinch, didn't soften, just stood there in the rain starting to fall again, letting him spend it. "Dig it up."

"What?"

"You're an archaeologist's son. You read sites. So read this one." She pointed at the mound with her chin. "If she's in there, you need to know. If she's not in there, you need to know that worse. Either way the answer is under the stones, and standing here bleeding is not going to move them."

* * *

He dug alone. She offered; he refused; she didn't push, just withdrew to the camp's edge and kept watch on the river, giving him the privacy of the work, which was its own kind of mercy.

The stones came away easily — set, not packed. That was the first wrong note. Grave-robbing robbers who bury a woman do not dress the mound with river stones fitted like masonry. He set them aside in a row, ordered, because order was all he had. Then the spade — someone's spade, left leaning against the cliff, another thing too useful to steal — and the earth came up in dark slices, and he counted the depth the way his mother had taught him to count stratigraphy when he was small enough to be carried on dig sites, before he was old enough to be left behind.

*One spit. Two.* Roots, worms, recent rain. *Three.* The soil changed color, richer, turned within the season. *Four.*

The spade struck stone.

Not a coffin. Not bone. Stones — packed deliberately, layer over layer, filling the grave to its floor like ballast, like a lie built from the bottom up. He knelt in the hole he'd dug and put his bare hands into the gap between two of them and felt nothing but cold rock and colder certainty, and the sound he made was not quite a laugh and not quite anything else.

Empty. The grave was empty. She had never been in it.

He sat back on his heels in the pit and the rain came down harder and he let it, because the relief hit him first — *she's alive, she's alive* — and then, right behind it, riding its shoulders, came the thing he'd spent twelve years refusing to learn: *she did this.* She had staged her own death, planted his father's compass like a signature on a forged letter, and walked away from him a second time. Walked away from everyone. Let the world believe her dead rather than tell anyone — anyone — where she was going or why.

She had even used the compass. She had known exactly what it would do to him if he ever came looking. Or she had known it would mean nothing to him at all, because she had never once, in twelve years, checked whether her son remembered the shelf above the stove.

He climbed out of the grave he had dug for his mother's grief and stood in the rain with the compass in his fist, needle swinging, pointing at nothing, pointing inland.

"Emrys." Sefa was beside him. He didn't remember her crossing the distance. She was looking at the open pit, at the stones, and her face had gone carefully blank in the way he was learning meant she'd seen something that frightened her. "Whoever dug this knew burial customs. Knew how to make ground look settled. That's not soldiers. That's an archaeologist's work."

"It's her work," he said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. Steady the way deep water is steady. "She faked it. She's alive, and she planned this down to the last stone, and she told no one. Not her funders. Not her team — those people are gone, Sefa, eleven people evacuated or taken or scattered, and she let them carry her grave out of here." He opened his fist and looked at the compass. The needle had finally stilled. It pointed northeast, up-slope, toward the falls he could hear muttering beyond the trees. "Except maybe she told one person. Maybe she told herself."

Sefa followed the needle's line with her eyes. "Footprints," she said. "There. Under the overhang, past the grave — the rain hasn't touched them."

He saw them then. A trail of prints leading away from the camp into the treeline, inland, toward the sound of falling water — and, he noticed as he knelt beside them, deliberately confused, overlapped, doubled-back-on-themselves by someone who understood exactly how a tracker reads ground. Someone who had wanted any follower to hesitate. To doubt. To stand in a fake grave in the rain and wonder whether the trail was real or one more layer of the lie.

His mother had taught him that trick too. On a dig, when he was nine. *Never leave honest tracks, Emrys. Honest tracks get people killed.*

He closed his hand around the compass until the brass bit his palm.

"She wants to be followed," he said. "By exactly one person. Anyone else, and the trail is supposed to scare them off." He looked at Sefa, and the old self-reliance rose in him like a wall — the instinct to say *I'll go alone*, to shoulder it, to owe nothing — and died somewhere in his throat, because he owed her already, more than coin, and because the trail led into flooded karst country in monsoon season and he knew, they both knew, what that meant.

"Sefa," he said. "How do you feel about caves?"

She looked at the treeline, at the falls, at the grave with its ballast of stones, and something moved behind her eyes — the ledger she kept, the village upstream, the financier's mark on his crates, all of it converging on this one muddy trailhead.

"I feel," she said, "like whoever faked this death is going to answer for a great many things. Get your pack."

Behind them, the river kept sliding past the ruined gate, brown and patient, carrying everything it knew toward the sea.
