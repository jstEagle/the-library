## 9. Sefa's Ledger

The fire was small, and Sefa built it small on purpose. A ring of river stones, dry driftwood from the high bank, flames no taller than her hand. Anything bigger would be a signal, and she had spent the last three years learning what signals cost.

Emrys sat across from her with his knees drawn up, the contract radio between them like a dead animal neither wanted to touch. He'd tried it again at dusk — *Rivergate camp, Rivergate camp, this is supply run Vasquez, do you copy* — and the static had answered the way it always answered, patient and empty, a sound like the inside of a shell.

Now he watched her instead of the radio. She could feel it, that particular attention of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"You've been circling it for two days," she said. "Ask."

"Ask what?"

"Whatever you've been chewing since I came aboard." She fed a stick to the fire. "You look at my boat, then at your crates, then at me. You think I'm slow because I'm polite about it."

He didn't deny it. She gave him credit for that much. Most men filled silence with noise; Emrys Vasquez let it sit there like a stone in a net, weighing everything down.

"The crates," he said finally. "When we loaded at the trading post, you looked at the stenciling on the third one. The anchor-and-key mark. You knew it."

"I know it."

"And you didn't say anything."

"No."

"Why?"

Sefa turned her hands over in the firelight. They were pilot's hands, rope-burned and salt-cracked, and she had learned long ago that people read hands before they read faces. Let him see the work. Let him understand where the answers had come from — not from cleverness, but from years of it.

"Because once I say his name out loud," she said, "you're going to have to decide whether you're rowing with me or away from me. And I wanted one more night of not knowing."

* * *

She told it plainly, the way you tell a thing you've told yourself so many times it has worn smooth, like a pebble carried in a pocket.

"My family's village is called Tavolo. Was. Upstream, past the second gorge, where the valley narrows and the rice terraces climb the walls like stairs for giants. My grandfather piloted cargo through those gorges. My mother kept the ferry ledger — every crossing, every toll, written in her own hand going back sixty years. When I was nineteen I had a boat of my own and a route and a name people trusted." She paused. "You know what a name is worth on a river."

"It's worth everything," Emrys said quietly. "It's the only collateral that can't be stolen."

"That's exactly what they took."

She pulled a folded oilcloth packet from inside her jacket — the same jacket, he now realized, she had never taken off, even sleeping. She unwrapped it with the care of someone handling survey equipment. Inside was a photograph gone soft at the corners, and beneath it, a sheaf of papers.

The photograph showed a village on a bend of river: stilt houses, drying racks hung with fish, terraces climbing gold-green behind them. In the foreground, a woman stood at the rail of a flat-bottomed ferry, one hand raised, caught mid-wave. Young. Laughing at whoever held the camera.

"My mother," Sefa said. "Six months before."

She laid the papers out one by one, turning each so the firelight caught it. Survey documents. Water-flow tables. A map of the upper valley with contour lines drawn dense as fingerprints around Tavolo's bend.

"Four years ago, a company filed for water rights above the gorge. Halcyon Consolidated Development. Their man came through twice — smooth coat, smooth voice, talking about hydroelectric power, about progress for the whole valley. My mother asked him one question at a public meeting. She asked where the water would go when the gates closed." Sefa's voice didn't change at all, which was how he knew. "He smiled and said the engineers had made a provision for every village downstream."

*A provision.* Emrys went very still.

"They closed the gates in March. Not gradually — all at once, the way you do when you don't want anyone moving their belongings out ahead of schedule. The river dropped four meters in six hours. The fish died in the pools. The terraces cracked. And then, eleven days later, they opened them for a 'test release' without warning anyone downstream." Her finger stopped on the flow table. "A wall of water. Two meters over flood stage, in the dry season, with no rain anywhere in the watershed. Forty-one people. My mother was on the ferry when it hit."

The fire popped. Downstream somewhere, water worried at the rocks, indifferent.

"There was an inquiry. Halcyon produced engineering reports showing the release was within safety parameters. Produced signatures — compensation agreements signed by village elders who couldn't read what they were signing. The inquiry closed in five weeks. Everyone involved got promoted." She gathered the papers back into their careful stack. "Except one junior surveyor, who drank himself into the river two years later and left a box of originals in a locker at the Kessarin trading post, addressed to anyone who would use them. I've been using them ever since."

"Halcyon," Emrys said. "That's the financier. That's who funded my mother's expedition."

"Now you see why I didn't say anything." She looked at him across the fire, and her eyes were steady and hard and tired. "I found the anchor-and-key stencil on expedition crates two seasons ago, being hauled by Corvo's runners. I've been following this dig upriver since before you ever counted coins in a flophouse. Every crate, every permit, every supply run — I've tracked them. And then a leaking longboat comes upstream carrying Halcyon-marked supplies, piloted by a man named Vasquez, bound for a dig led by Dr. Ilsa Vasquez." She let that sit. "Your mother's expedition is Halcyon's money, Emrys. Whatever she's digging up at Rivergate, they intend to own it. And Corvo moves their freight."

* * *

He was quiet for a long time. Sefa let him be quiet. She knew what it was to rebuild the shape of your life around new information — the way a boat takes on ballast, lower and heavier, but steadier for it.

"My father owed Corvo money," he said at last. "That's the debt. That's what I inherited. I thought it was just money." He laughed, short and without humor. "Corvo handed me this contract himself. Ferry supplies to Rivergate. I thought I was lucky. I thought the river was giving me a way out."

"Or he was giving you a way in."

"Don't." He rubbed his face. "Don't say that yet. Let me get there slowly."

But she had seen him do the arithmetic already, back on her boat, when the gunboat's wake had come down the channel and he'd gone white in a way that had nothing to do with fear of drowning. She let him arrive at it at his own speed. People believed conclusions they reached themselves. People resented conclusions handed to them.

Instead she did something she had been deciding whether to do since the gorge: she took the last paper from the oilcloth — the bottom one, the one she'd shown no one in four years — and passed it across the fire.

It was a letter. Halcyon letterhead. Dated three years back. It referenced, in the bloodless language of such things, "the Rivergate site acquisition timeline" and "the principal investigator's cooperation regarding subsurface access." It was signed by a name Emrys clearly recognized, because his hand shook when he held it.

"This is why I came aboard your boat instead of just following it," Sefa said. "Because I need something I can't get alone. The caves behind the falls — the old surveyor's maps show chambers under the Rivergate ruins, deep ones, older than anything Halcyon knows about. If your mother found what I think she found, it's proof. Engineering records. Flood tables. Something that shows the whole valley's water was managed, deliberately, for centuries — and shows what happens when modern men play at the same game without understanding it. Evidence that would reopen the inquiry. Evidence that would drown Halcyon the way they drowned Tavolo."

"And you need me because—"

"Because I can't read a dig. I can pilot any water in this valley, but I don't know stratigraphy from sediment, and I can't tell a looted chamber from a sealed one. Your mother taught you. I watched you read that ransacked camp like a ledger." She leaned forward. "And because if there's a way into the caves, there's a way out, and I want a Vasquez standing next to me when we find it. Your name opens doors on this river mine can't anymore. Mine keeps us alive on the water. That's the trade."

Emrys turned the letter over in his hands. "And if I say no? If I take my crates and keep running supplies for Corvo and pretend I never heard any of this?"

Sefa shrugged, and it cost her more than she let show. "Then tomorrow I go upstream alone, and I wish you calm water. I don't threaten people, Emrys. Threats are for men who've already lost. But I'll tell you what I told myself the day I buried my mother: the river gives back what you put into it. Everything else is interest."

He looked at the letter a long moment. Then, instead of handing it back, he set it down on the flat stone between them, deliberately, and left it there.

"Keep it," he said. "You've carried it four years. It should be near the front of the line when we find what we find." He met her eyes. "I'm in. But I have conditions too. We do this clean — no burning villages of our own making, no sinking boats with people aboard. Whatever we take from Halcyon, we take in daylight, with witnesses. I've spent enough of my life paying other people's debts in the dark."

"Daylight," Sefa agreed, and something in her chest unclenched that had been clenched since March four years ago. "Witnesses. Clean." She almost smiled. "You realize that's the hardest possible way to fight a company like that."

"I'm beginning to suspect everything worth doing is."

* * *

They doused the fire early and took turns at watch, and it was during her watch, in the blue hour before dawn, that Sefa heard it.

Not from downstream. From upstream.

An engine — low, throttled down to almost nothing, the sound a gunboat makes when it doesn't want to be heard. It came around the far bend where the channel narrowed, running dark, no lights, and slid past their anchorage on the opposite shore close enough that she could see the silhouette of the man at the bow rail, scanning the banks with glasses.

She put her hand over Emrys's mouth and woke him without a word, and they lay flat among the rocks and watched Corvo's boat crawl upstream toward Rivergate, toward the falls, toward whatever waited in the dark above them.

"He knows," Emrys breathed against her ear, when the engine sound had faded to nothing. "Somehow he already knows about the caves."

Sefa stared upriver into the black, and her mother's face looked back at her from four years ago, mid-wave, laughing, and she felt the old cold settle into her bones — but colder now, because this time she wasn't following the current.

She was racing it.

"Then we stop being behind him," she said. "First light. We run the gorge before he's through the ruins."
