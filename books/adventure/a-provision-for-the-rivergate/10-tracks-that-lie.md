## 10. Tracks That Lie

The grave was wrong.

Emrys had known it from the moment he'd seen it — had felt the wrongness in his teeth before his mind caught up, the way a man feels a stair that isn't there. But knowing and proving were different animals, and he made himself wait until morning, until the light came slanting through the slashed canopy and gave him what light gives: evidence.

He crouched at the edge of the mound with Sefa standing behind him, arms crossed, saying nothing. She'd let him sleep two hours on the boat, then woken him with a hand on his shoulder and one word: *Camp.* Now she watched him work the way people watch a card sharp, half-suspicious, half-impressed.

The mound was maybe four feet long, built of river stones stacked over a frame of cut saplings. Someone had planted a marker at its head — a flat slab of limestone, and lashed to it with wire, face-up so no one could miss it, his mother's compass.

His father's compass.

Brass case gone green at the seams, the glass cracked in a spiderweb from some fall twelve years ago that Ilsa had never explained and never apologized for. Emrys had been nine when she'd walked out of their rented house in the capital with two duffel bags and that compass riding her breast pocket like a second heart. He remembered because he'd tried to grab it off her coat as she passed, a child's stupid last grab at something, anything, and she'd peeled his fingers away gently, one by one, and said, *I'll need it where I'm going.*

She hadn't said goodbye. She'd said *need*.

Now it lay here, in the mud of a ransacked camp, on a grave that was too neat.

"Say what you're thinking," Sefa said.

"I'm not thinking yet. I'm looking." He pulled his sleeve over his hand and lifted the compass free of the wire. The needle swung, settled, pointed north-northeast with a lazy confidence that had nothing to do with truth. The hinge was stiff with river silt. "This wasn't buried. It was wired on top. You don't mark a grave with the dead woman's compass unless you want the grave found."

"Maybe whoever killed her wanted credit."

"Maybe." He set the compass down on his knee and turned to the mound itself. "Or maybe there's nothing under here but a message."

Sefa's eyes narrowed. "You think she's alive."

"I think this camp is a sentence someone wrote very carefully, and I want to read all of it before I decide what it says."

He started at the edge of the mound and worked outward, the way his mother had taught him — not that she'd taught him much else, but she'd taught him this, one summer when he was eleven and visiting her at a coastal dig, the only summer she'd ever invited him anywhere. *Read the ground backward,* she'd said, kneeling in the trench with her trowel held like a pen. *The last thing that happened is closest to the surface. Start there and dig toward the beginning.* It was the only lesson of hers he'd kept, and he'd kept it out of spite as much as love, learning it better than she ever knew, better than she'd ever bothered to check.

The topsoil over the mound had been turned within days. He could tell by the moisture gradient — the exposed earth was still dark and damp, not yet sun-bleached, not yet colonized by the thin green film that crept over disturbed ground in this climate inside a week. Recent. Days, not weeks.

He lifted the first stone.

Underneath: more stones. He lifted those. Underneath those: still more stones, packed tight, filling the shallow pit to its brim. No shroud. No body. No bones. Not even the honest emptiness of a robbed grave — no drag marks leading away, no scatter of displaced fill, no secondary pit where a thief might have reburied what they took. Just stones, laid by hands that had taken their time, in a pit dug to exactly the depth a grave should be.

"They never put anyone in it," he said quietly.

Sefa crouched beside him now, close enough that he could smell woodsmoke and engine oil on her. She picked up one of the deeper stones, weighed it, frowned. "These came from the streambed. Fifty meters east." She glanced up at him. "Whoever built this carried rocks fifty meters. In the rain. That's effort."

"That's intention." His voice came out flatter than he felt. Inside him, something was moving — cold and bright and dangerous, a fish turning in deep water. Hope. He didn't trust it. Hope was the thing his mother's leaving had taught him to handle like a blade without a grip. "You don't carry rocks fifty meters for a corpse you actually have. You do it for an audience."

"Corvo's men?"

"Corvo's men don't stage graves. Corvo's men leave bodies where they drop and burn the tents after." He'd seen enough of Corvo's methods secondhand — through the enforcer's casual anecdotes, delivered over card games in dockside rooms, stories meant as warnings. "This is—" He stopped. Started again. "This is careful. This is *archaeological*. Whoever did this understood stratigraphy well enough to fake disturbance layers. They raked the surface to look trampled, they cut the tents from the outside so the slashes would read as violence, they—"

He stood abruptly, because staying crouched next to the empty grave while hope climbed his throat was unbearable, and walked the camp instead.

It looked destroyed. That was the first impression, and it was a good forgery. Tents collapsed with their poles snapped, crates split and gutted, a field table overturned with instruments scattered across the mud. But Emrys walked it slowly, reading backward, surface down, and the sentence began to unravel.

The tent canvas was cut, yes — but cut in long, clean strokes, all of them angled the same way, left shoulder to right hip, as if one person had walked the row doing them in sequence. Real raiders slash in panic or fury; the cuts go every direction, shallow and deep, ragged where the blade catches and drags. These were surgical. And the tents' contents — sleeping rolls, cook pots, clothing — hadn't been scattered. They'd been *arranged* to look scattered. A mess is random. This mess had rhythm. Three paces between each cluster, always on the left side of the central path, always visible to someone entering from the landing beach.

Someone had designed this wreckage for a specific viewer arriving from a specific direction.

Him. Or anyone coming upriver. But the contract had named him. The supplies were his to ferry. Anyone reading the situation would know who the river would eventually deliver to this place.

"The equipment," Sefa called from across the camp. She'd gone to the stripped storage area under the big tarp. "Look at what's missing."

He joined her. She was pointing at the bare ground where heavy gear had sat — the pale rectangles of undisturbed earth marking where crates and cases had rested long enough to kill the grass.

"What's missing is everything worth money," she said. "Radios, outboard motors, the good tools, the medical kit. What's still here—" she nudged a broken crate with her boot, revealing a jumble of ceramic sherds, a bundle of drawing paper, a camera with its lens cracked, "—is everything worth *knowledge*. They took the diesel generators and left the finds. Raiders don't do that. Raiders are illiterate. They take the gold and smash the pottery."

"Unless the raiders weren't raiders."

"Unless," Sefa agreed, and there was something new in her voice — not warmth, exactly, but a recalibration. She was looking at him differently. "Your mother taught you to read dirt?"

"My mother taught me to read dirt and then left before the lesson was finished." He heard the bitterness come out of him raw and unfiltered and didn't bother to leash it. Twelve years of leashing it, and here, on her staged grave, it finally slipped. "Everything I know about her, I learned by studying what she abandoned. So forgive me if I'm not ready to celebrate."

Sefa said nothing to that. She just watched him with those flat river-pilot eyes that measured current and depth and gave back nothing until they were ready.

* * *

He spent the middle of the day on the perimeter, and that was where the real story lived.

The camp sat on a low terrace above the river bend, backed by rising ground that climbed toward the grey shoulders of the karst country — limestone hills riddled with caves, fanged with sinkholes, wrapped in vine forest so dense it looked solid from ten meters away. The expedition's mapped footprint ended at the terrace. Everything inland was blank space on his charts, marked only with the surveyor's polite fiction of *relief features approximate.*

The footprints started behind the kitchen tent.

They were almost invisible — that was the point of them, and also their failure, because almost invisible is a kind of visibility too. Someone had swept the ground behind the camp with a frond branch, erasing tracks, and then walked out through the sweep in bare feet or wrapped feet, pressing lightly, and then — this was the part that stopped Emrys's breath — had doubled back along their own line twice, overlapping their prints, so that even if a tracker found the trail it would read as three people walking single file, not one.

But the sweeper had worked in the rain, and rain doesn't cooperate with liars. At the base of the slope, where groundwater seeped and the soil stayed plastic, the sweeping had smeared rather than erased, and beneath the smear the substrate held its memory. Emrys knelt over it for a long time, rain dripping off his chin, teasing the layers apart with a splinter of tent pole.

One set of prints, size small — a woman's boot, narrow heel, worn on the outer edge the way boots wear on a person who stands with her weight rolled outboard, the way his mother had stood at every dig site of his childhood, hip cocked, weight on the outside of her feet, forever leaning slightly away from whatever she was looking at. As if even her skeleton kept a reservation.

Two sets of larger prints accompanying her. Companions, or captors. Their stride was relaxed. Nobody walks with a relaxed stride while holding a prisoner at gunpoint — not uphill, not through mud, not for the length of trail these prints covered before the canopy closed and the ground went to root mat and gave nothing more.

Three people had walked into the karst country. Willingly. In order.

And the sweeping, the doubling-back, the whole elaborate choreography of erasure — that had been done *before* they left, which meant it wasn't done to hide their departure from pursuers. It was done to make sure that whoever came later — whoever the river brought — would find the false grave, read the false violence, and stop looking.

A message written in negative space: *Dead. Buried. Grieve and go home.*

Emrys sat back on his heels in the wet ferns and laughed once, a short ugly sound with nothing in it but recognition.

She'd done it to him before. Smaller scale, same grammar. When he was fourteen, a letter had arrived — the only letter in twelve years — postmarked from a city she'd never been to, telling him she was fine, telling him not to look for her, written in a hand that trembled in exactly the places a forged hand trembles when the forger wants it to. He'd spent a year believing she was in danger somewhere, needing him, unable to reach him. He'd saved money to search for her. He'd told no one why. And years later, working passage on a freighter, he'd met a man who'd served on a dig with her in that same season, in a completely different province, and learned she'd been not just fine but *flourishing*, publishing, celebrated, and that she'd mentioned — casually, the man said, casually — that she had a son she didn't see.

She wrote lies the way other people wrote letters. She staged absences the way other people staged photographs. And now she had staged her own death with the same meticulous care she'd given to every excavation of her career, and she had used — God, the cruelty of it was almost elegant — *his father's compass* as the signature, because she knew, she had to know, that the one object on earth guaranteed to make Emrys Vasquez stop and kneel and believe was that battered brass circle that had pointed both their lives into the ground.

"Cold hope," he said aloud, testing the words. "That's what this is."

Sefa appeared at his shoulder. She'd followed his circuit of the camp without being asked, matching his pace, learning his method. "You've got a theory."

"I've got a wound wearing a theory's clothes." He pointed at the seep, at the ghost prints beneath the smear. "Three people went inland. On purpose. After staging all of this." He gestured back at the camp, the pretty ruin of it. "My mother may be alive. My mother may have done all of this herself. Faked her death, emptied her own camp, buried a pile of stream stones under her dead husband's compass, and walked into those hills knowing full well that the river would eventually bring her son to kneel at her lie."

"You sound angry."

"I am angry." He got to his feet. Mud clung to his knees. "I'm angry the way I've been angry since I was nine years old, except now the anger has a shape and the shape has footprints, and I can follow it." He looked at the treeline — the wall of green climbing into the grey karst, going up and up into country where the maps gave out and the limestone swallowed rivers whole. "That's the difference. For twelve years the anger went nowhere. Now it goes *that way*."

Sefa studied him for a long moment. Then she looked at the hills too, and he saw her expression change — saw the private arithmetic running behind her eyes, the ledger she kept, the village upstream and the financier's mark and whatever debt of her own she was carrying. Whatever she was calculating, the sum made her jaw set.

"There's a storm system sitting on the upper valley," she said. "You can smell it. We have maybe two days of decent weather, then the monsoon arrives early or my name isn't worth the paint on my boat. Going inland means getting trapped up there past any rescue window." She turned back to him. "Going back downriver means Corvo finds us eventually, because Corvo is downstream of everything."

"So we're choosing between the flood and the shark."

"We're choosing whether your mother is worth drowning for." Sefa said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "Because that's the actual price. Not the walk. The walk is easy. It's the *after* — the water rising, the exits closing, the months cut off if we commit. That's the price."

Emrys looked at the false grave. At the compass lying in the mud where he'd set it down, needle steady, pointing its patient pointless north. His father had bought it in a port city before Emrys was born, before the debts, before the drinking, before the heart that gave out in a flophouse not so different from the one where Corvo's enforcer had counted coins across a table at him. Two parents, then. One who'd drowned in place, and one who'd faked drowning to get away. And here was their son, standing in the rain between the two truths, discovering that the only inheritance either of them had left him was the ability to read a lie and love the liar anyway.

"She knew I'd come," he said. "She staged it for me specifically. Which means part of her wanted me to find the grave and grieve and leave." He paused. "And part of her — the part that left the trail sloppy enough to read, the part that chose a compass I couldn't help recognizing — part of her wanted me to see through it."

"How do you know?"

"Because my mother doesn't make mistakes at this scale." He picked up the compass, wiped the mud from its cracked face, and closed his fist around it. "She makes choices that look like mistakes to anyone who doesn't know her. And apparently I still know her. Apparently that's the one thing twelve years couldn't take."

Thunder moved in the west, a long low rolling that he felt through his boots before he heard it. The light over the karst had gone the color of old pewter. Somewhere up in those hills, behind the falls, in country the maps refused to describe, a woman who was legally, officially, convincingly dead was waiting out the weather — or working, because Ilsa Vasquez did not wait, Ilsa Vasquez *dug* — and she had no idea yet whether her performance had succeeded.

Emrys turned to Sefa. Rain was starting now, fat drops ticking on the broad leaves, and the trail inland was already beginning to soften at its edges.

"Get the boat hidden," he said. "Take what supplies we can carry. We're going up."

Sefa didn't argue. She just looked once more at the sky, at the storm stacking itself against the horizon like a debt coming due, and said, "If we're not back to the river in four days, the monsoon decides for us."

"Then we're back in four days."

She snorted — the first sound resembling humor he'd heard from her — and headed down toward the water. Emrys stood a moment longer at the treeline, compass in his fist, the false grave at his back, the true trail dissolving softly ahead.

Then he stepped off the terrace and into the karst country, following his mother's footprints into the rain.
