## 11. Corvo's Wake

Sefa heard the engine before she saw anything, and she had been hearing engines on this river since before she could swim.

It came up the gorge the way bad news always did — low at first, a vibration in the water more than the air, felt through the soles of her boots where they pressed against the hull of the patched longboat. She was crouched over the bilge, bailing the last of the morning's seepage with a dented tin pan, and she stopped mid-scoop. Held still. Listened.

Emrys was up on the bank, hauling their remaining crates into the treeline one at a time, stubborn as a mule about doing everything himself. He hadn't heard it. Of course he hadn't. The man could read a campsite like a priest read scripture, but his ears were still city ears, dockside ears, ears tuned to coin and conversation and nothing that mattered out here.

"Sefa?" he called, not looking up. "You want to argue about the anchor line again, or—"

"Get down."

He looked up then. She didn't repeat herself. She pointed downstream, toward the bend below the ruins, where the river narrowed between walls of black stone and the current ran fast and green and deep.

The sound grew. A diesel throb, heavy and unhurried, the kind of engine that didn't care about rapids because it had been built to ignore them. Nothing that size had any business being above the second cataract. Nothing that size had any business being on this river at all, not in the last week of the dry season, when every honest boat sat hauled out on the mud waiting for the rains to end.

"Gunboat," Sefa said quietly.

Emrys came off the bank like a man waking from a dream, dropping the crate he'd been carrying, and for one stupid half-second he stood there staring downstream as if the sound might explain itself. Then Sefa grabbed his sleeve and hauled him down behind the fallen fig tree where they'd cached the supplies, and the two of them went flat against the damp earth while the engine note climbed the gorge toward them.

* * *

The boat came around the bend at half-throttle, and Sefa felt her stomach tighten into a knot she recognized from years ago, from another river, another boat, another time men with guns came upstream wearing somebody else's money.

She'd seen gunboats before. This wasn't one of the government patrol craft, those flat-bottomed, badly maintained things crewed by bored conscripts who mostly wanted to be somewhere else. This was private. Low-slung, dark-hulled, a bow-mounted machine gun under a canvas shroud, twin diesels breathing through snorkels so it could run flooded channels. Fresh paint, matte black, no name on the stern. No registration numbers. A boat that officially did not exist, carrying men who officially were not here.

Corvo's mark was nowhere on it, which was how Sefa knew exactly whose it was. Men like Corvo never put their names on things. They put their names on other people.

There were eight of them visible on deck. Hard-bodied, quiet, moving with the economy of men who had done this in other rivers and other countries and been paid well for it. Not smugglers — smugglers slouched. These men stood. Two of them carried survey instruments. That detail hit Sefa harder than the machine gun did. You brought surveyors when you intended to stay. You brought them when you already knew what you were looking for and only needed its coordinates confirmed.

The gunboat slowed as it passed the ransacked camp on the far bank. Sefa watched through the screen of ferns as a man at the rail raised binoculars and swept the ruined tents, the stripped equipment racks, the fresh grave with its marker. He studied the grave a long time. Then he said something to another man, and the boat swung toward the near bank — toward their side, toward the treeline twenty meters above Sefa's head where she could smell her own sweat and the rot of the fig leaves and Emrys breathing too fast beside her.

"Don't move," she breathed.

"I'm not moving."

"You're breathing like a man being chased."

"I am a man being chased."

"Not yet," she whispered. "Breathe like a man who isn't. Yet."

The gunboat grounded its bow gently on the mud bank below them, and six men came ashore.

* * *

They worked the ruin site the way Sefa imagined surgeons worked a body — methodically, without hurry, without mercy. Two men took the high ground above the camp and set up a watching position with a radio antenna. Four moved through the tents, photographing everything, turning over slashed canvas, kneeling at the false grave and lifting away the stones with gloved hands.

Sefa watched Emrys watch them. His face had gone the color of old paper. She understood why. Those were his mother's tents they were pulling apart. His mother's grave they were opening — even if both of them now knew there was no body in it, the sight of strangers' hands on that ground did something to a person that reason couldn't touch.

"They know it's empty," Emrys murmured. "Look. They're not surprised."

He was right. The man kneeling at the grave lifted out the last stone, looked into the shallow pit, and made a small gesture — not disappointment, not shock. Confirmation. He'd expected exactly what he found. He spoke into a handheld radio, briefly, and turned away from the grave without a second glance.

"They came looking for a hole in the ground," Sefa said slowly, working it through. "Not a woman. Not a camp. They wanted to confirm she wasn't in it."

"So they know she's alive."

"They knew before we did." Sefa's jaw tightened. "Look at the survey gear. Look at the way they're searching — not the camp, the *ground*. They're reading the terrain. They're looking for where she went."

One of the searchers found something — a scrap of fabric, maybe, or a marker — near the treeline where the obscured footprints began, and the whole operation pivoted toward it with a smoothness that made Sefa's skin crawl. Within minutes they had a line strung and men moving inland in a sweep pattern, and the man with the binoculars was back at the rail, speaking into the radio in a voice too low to carry across the water.

And then the radio on the gunboat crackled loud enough to reach them — a burst of static, then a voice, flat and accented and horribly familiar, because Sefa had spent three days listening to Emrys talk about it.

*"...the son is already there. I told you he would be."*

Emrys went rigid beside her. She felt it through the ground, through her shoulder pressed against his.

"That's him," Emrys whispered. "That's Corvo."

*"Search the east bank first. He'll have come by water, and he'll have hidden the boat. Find the boat and you find him. And Voss—"*

A pause. Static.

*"Do not harm him. He is worth more to me walking than buried."*

* * *

They lay in the ferns for two hours while the searchers combed the east bank. Twice a man passed within ten meters of their hiding place, close enough that Sefa could see the stitching on his pack straps, and twice she pressed her hand flat against Emrys's chest and held him still until the footsteps faded. The longboat was hidden well — dragged up a side channel and covered with cut brush thirty meters downstream — but well was a relative word, and every minute the searchers stayed on their bank was a minute the brush cover could fail them.

It was during the second hour, lying in the wet fern-shade with his heart hammering against her palm, that Emrys started to shake. Not fear, Sefa realized. Something worse. Understanding.

"It was never the money," he said, so quietly she almost missed it under the river-noise.

"What?"

"The debt. My father's debt." His voice had gone strange, hollowed out. "Three years Corvo's man has been collecting on it. Three years of reminders, of interest, of keeping me just solvent enough to keep owing. And then suddenly — suddenly — a contract appears. Good pay. Supplies upriver. To an expedition led by Vasquez." He laughed, and there was nothing funny in it. "My mother's name. He used my mother's name. He knew I'd come. He *built* the job so I'd come."

Sefa said nothing. There was nothing to say that he hadn't just said better himself.

"I thought I was paying a debt," Emrys went on, and now the shaking was anger, clean and hot. "I thought I was ferrying crates. I was the compass needle. He pointed me at the ruins and let the river do the rest, because he knew — he *knew* — that if anyone could find where Ilsa Vasquez had gone, it was her own blood. I led them here. Every rapid I ran, every night I pushed past the safe anchorage, every mile — I was doing his work for him and calling it escape."

"Yes," Sefa said.

He turned his head and stared at her. "You knew?"

"I suspected. From the trading post. Your crates had the financier's handling marks — the same marks I've tracked up and down this river for six years. But suspicion isn't proof, and you weren't ready to hear it, and frankly—" she exhaled slowly, "—I needed you to get me here. So yes. I used you too. We can be angry about that later, if we live."

Something moved across his face — hurt, maybe, or respect, or the particular exhaustion of a man discovering that everyone he'd ever trusted had been steering him somewhere. But he nodded. That was the thing Sefa was learning about Emrys Vasquez: he took a wound, he bled privately, and then he stood back up.

"He said don't harm me," Emrys said. "Worth more walking than buried. What does that mean? What am I worth to him?"

"Leverage," Sefa said. "Think. If Ilsa faked her death to hide something from her financier, what's the one thing she can't hide from?"

Emrys closed his eyes. When he opened them again, they were wet and furious. "Her son."

"Her son. Alive, findable, and standing in her way. He doesn't need you to lead him anywhere anymore. He needs you as bait. Or a hostage. Or a knife held to her throat." Sefa shrugged, deliberately casual, because the alternative was letting him see how afraid she was. "Congratulations. You've graduated from debtor to currency."

* * *

Toward late afternoon the searchers gave up on the east bank. Sefa watched them regroup at the landing, watched the survey team confer with the man called Voss, watched the sweep line collapse inward like a closing hand. They hadn't found the longboat. They hadn't found the trail inland — or if they'd found traces of it, the coming storm-light and the karst country beyond had swallowed the sign.

But they weren't leaving. That was the part that sat cold in Sefa's chest as the light failed. The gunboat backed off the mud bank and anchored mid-channel, running lights dark, and the watching post on the high ground stayed manned. They were settling in. Corvo hadn't come all this way to look around. He was going to sit on the Rivergate like a spider on a web and wait for whatever moved — the mother, the son, the records, all of it.

Which meant the river behind them was closed. The river ahead was monsoon. And somewhere inland, past the falls, in caves she didn't want to imagine, a woman who had faked her own death was presumably still breathing, unaware that the net had just dropped over the entire valley.

"We can't go back down," Emrys said. It wasn't a question. He'd reached the same arithmetic she had.

"No."

"And we can't stay here."

"No."

He was quiet a moment, looking inland, toward the black limestone shoulders rising behind the falls, where the obscured footprints vanished into country that ate surveyors and swallowed rain. "Then there's only one direction left," he said. "And it's the one she wanted us to find anyway."

Sefa followed his gaze. The falls thundered in the dusk, white water falling into green dark, and somewhere behind that curtain of noise was a door that a dead civilization had built and a living woman had locked.

"She left the trail for someone to follow," Sefa said slowly. "Obscured, but not erased. Deliberate. She wanted whoever came after her to be able to find it — but only someone who could *read* it."

"Someone like an archaeologist's son."

"Someone like that."

Below them, the black gunboat lay silent on the darkening water, and the watching post's antenna caught the last light like a splinter of bone. Sefa settled back into the ferns, checked the blade at her belt out of habit, and made the decision the way she made all her decisions — quickly, completely, and without telling him yet.

"In the morning," she said, "we go into the karst."

Emrys nodded once. Above the falls, the first thunder of the true monsoon rolled inland, distant and patient, counting down the days they had left.
