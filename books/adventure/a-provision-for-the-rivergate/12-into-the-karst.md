## 12. Into the Karst

They left the boat at first light, hidden beneath a screen of cut ferns and a tarpaulin Sefa had stolen from the watching post's own stores, which she considered a kind of justice. Emrys carried the survey kit, the contract radio, and the coil of rope. Sefa carried everything else, including the machete and, apparently, all of the good humor she owned, which was to say none.

The trail began where the footprints ended — at a seam in the limestone where the ground simply opened its mouth and swallowed the forest. One moment they were walking under dripping canopy; the next, the trees thinned to skeletal casuarinas rooted in gray rock, and the world turned porous. Holes gaped everywhere. Some were the size of a fist; others could have taken a truck. The rain that fell here didn't run off. It went down, into the dark plumbing of the plateau, and somewhere below it became the second river.

"Watch your feet," Sefa said. "The karst doesn't forgive weight."

"Neither do you."

"I'm practicing for old age." She moved ahead, testing each step with the machete's tip before committing her boot, reading the stone the way she read water — by feel, by sound, by the small betrayals of echo. Emrys followed her line exactly. He'd learned enough in three days aboard her boat to know that her confidence wasn't bravado. It was arithmetic. She counted risks the way he counted coins, and she never spent more than she had.

They climbed through a drowned forest where every tree stood knee-deep in green water, their roots making black lace on the surface. Fish moved between the trunks, silver flickers in the gloom, as if the river had come inland to reclaim territory it remembered owning. The air smelled of wet stone and something older underneath — mineral, cold, like the breath from a cellar.

Emrys kept his eyes on the ground because the ground was speaking, if you knew how to listen. His mother had taught him that before she'd taught him anything else. *Archaeology is just listening to dirt,* she used to say, kneeling in some trench with her hands full of somebody's ten-thousand-year-old breakfast. He'd been eight. He'd thought it was boring. He'd thought everything about her work was boring except the part where she came home.

He crouched at a mud seam between two slabs of limestone and saw what the rain hadn't erased: a boot print, shallow, half-filled with water. Beside it, pressed into the clay at the edge, the faint crescent of a walking staff or probe.

"Here," he said.

Sefa backtracked, looked, said nothing. But she adjusted their heading two degrees east, following his find instead of her instinct, and that was its own kind of compliment.

* * *

By midday the flooded country gave way to a maze of pinnacles — blades and needles of gray stone rising from pools of opaque green, some taller than a man, leaning together like conspirators. The trail threaded through them along ledges no wider than a coffin. Below, the water lay still as glass and twice as deep-looking. Above, the sky had gone the color of pewter, and the monsoon's advance guard came through in ragged sheets, soaking them, passing, returning.

It was in one of these pauses between rains that Emrys found the first marker.

He almost missed it. It looked like nothing — a stack of four flat stones balanced on a pinnacle's shoulder, weathered, moss-eaten. But the stones were wrong for the site. They were river cobbles, rounded, imported, and they were stacked with the deliberate precision of someone who stacked things for a living. Survey cairns. He'd grown up around them. His mother built them at every dig, little stone sentences saying *I was here, look this way.*

"Sefa."

She came back along the ledge. He pointed. She studied the cairn, then the terrain beyond it, then the cairn again, and he watched her do the same arithmetic she always did — but this time the numbers troubled her.

"That's not random," she said. "That's a bearing."

"From where?"

"From wherever the last one is." She turned a slow circle, scanning the pinnacles. "There. See it? On the tall one, north side, the pale patch."

He saw it. Another stack, fifty meters on, half-hidden behind a curtain of hanging vine. And beyond it, when they reached it and climbed the vantage it marked, another. A chain of them, strung across the drowned labyrinth like beads on an invisible wire, each one placed so that from the previous marker you could see only the next — never the whole line, never the destination. Someone had walked this route wanting it invisible from anywhere except the path itself.

Someone careful. Someone patient. Someone who thought in sightlines and cover stories.

His mother.

The anger arrived the way it always did — not as heat but as a kind of pressure behind the eyes, a tightening in the jaw. Twelve years. Twelve years of postmarks from three countries and a lawyer who handled the money and a phone number that changed every time he learned it. And now he was following her through the wilderness by reading her handwriting in stone, because she had built a trail she'd meant for no one to follow, and he was following it anyway, and some traitorous part of him was *proud* of how well it was made.

"You knew," he said. It came out harder than he intended. "In the camp. The grave, the compass — you suspected before I did."

"I suspected," Sefa said. "You're the one who proved it. Stones don't rot, Emrys. Whoever buried that grave wanted it found eventually — by the right person, at the right time. That's not grief. That's a message with a delay on it." She wiped rain from her face. "Question is who the message was for."

"It was for me." He heard the bitterness in his own voice and couldn't stop it. "The compass was my father's. She took it when she left. She knew I'd know it. She knew I'd dig." He laughed, short and ugly. "She couldn't send a letter. Twelve years, and she sends me a fake grave."

Sefa looked at him for a long moment. Rain dripped off her chin.

"Maybe a letter could be ignored," she said. "A grave can't."

* * *

They crossed the worst of the karst in the afternoon, wading a channel where the water ran chest-deep and fast between walls of slick stone, Sefa upstream breaking the current, Emrys gripping the rope she'd rigged, both of them breathing hard when they reached the far side and lay gasping on sun-warmed rock like landed fish. The rain had settled in now, steady, businesslike. The monsoon proper was close. You could feel it coming the way you feel a train through the rails before you hear it.

The markers continued. There were eleven of them by the time the light began to fail, and the twelfth was different.

It stood on a shelf of rock above a churning side-channel, and it wasn't stones. It was a length of steel rebar driven into a drilled hole, wrapped at the top with faded red tape, and beside it, wedged under a flat slab against the weather, a brass plate no bigger than a playing card. Emrys pried it out with his knife.

Stamped characters. Coordinates. And below them, in English, in a hand he would have known anywhere — the same slanted, impatient script that had filled the margins of his childhood textbooks:

**GATE 3 — 400 M — DO NOT APPROACH IN FLOOD**

Below that, smaller, almost an afterthought, as if she'd known exactly who might someday kneel on this exact stone:

**E.V. — IF YOU ARE READING THIS, TURN BACK OR COMMIT. THERE IS NO MIDDLE WAY.**

Emrys sat down hard on the wet rock.

The plate trembled slightly in his fingers and he realized it was his hands shaking. Twelve years of silence, and here it was at last — her voice, stamped in brass, addressed to him across the whole width of her stubbornness. *Turn back or commit.* No apology. No explanation. Not even, he noticed, and this stung worse than the rest, any indication that she'd wondered whether he was alive.

"There is no middle way," Sefa read over his shoulder. "Charming woman."

"She's not charming. She's—" He stopped. What was she? Obsessive. Brilliant. Capable of burying a discovery worth killing for under a false grave and walking away from it without telling a soul, without telling *him*, because telling him would have made him a target, or because telling him would have made him matter, and mattering was the one thing she'd never been able to afford. Both answers were true. Neither helped.

"She found something," he said instead. "Something big enough to stage her own death over. Big enough that Corvo funded a dig for years without knowing what he was paying for — or knowing exactly, and letting her run until the moment came to collect." He looked up at the wall of white noise ahead of them, where the falls thundered somewhere behind the rain. "And she left a trail only I could read, which means some part of her always planned for this day. Which means some part of her has been planning since before she disappeared. Which means—"

"Which means you're doing the thing where you talk instead of walk," Sefa said. She held out her hand and pulled him up. "Four hundred meters. Flood's coming. Choose."

He chose.

* * *

The cave mouth was exactly where the coordinates promised, behind the falls — of course it was behind the falls, his mother had never met a dramatic entrance she didn't love — reachable by a ledge that traversed the cliff face through the outer spray of the cascade. They went along it in the failing light, soaked to the skin before the first ten meters, deafened, half-blinded, the whole cliff shuddering under the impact of tons of falling water. Once, the ledge narrowed to less than a boot's width, and Emrys crossed it with his back to the abyss and his fingertips in a crack in the rock, thinking with strange clarity about the contract radio in his pack, dead these six days, and about Corvo's gunboat lying downstream like a promise, and about the fact that there was no version of tomorrow in which he turned around.

Then the ledge turned a corner, the roar fell away to a distant drumming, and there it was.

The entrance was a black archway in the limestone, twenty feet high, its rim hung with pale roots that had found their way down through forty feet of solid rock. The air flowing out of it was cool and constant, carrying the smell of deep water and wet calcite and, unmistakably, unmistakably, woodsmoke — old woodsmoke, banked and damped, the smell of a fire tended carefully and often.

Someone lived here.

Sefa unslung the machete. Emrys took out the flashlight he'd salvaged from the ransacked camp, its battery nearly spent, and swept the beam into the arch.

Just inside, sheltered from all weather, the floor had been worked. Flagstones laid. A low wall built up to waist height. And set into the wall, spaced with surveyor's precision, a row of drilled sockets holding iron pins — and tied to the pins, knotted in a sequence he recognized from a hundred childhood evenings watching her hands move, a line of cord colored with dye: red, red, blue, white, red.

Khipu-style tally cords. His mother's field code. He'd taught himself to read it at age nine purely so he could eavesdrop on her notes, and she'd caught him, and instead of being angry she'd spent a whole winter teaching him the rest.

Red, red, blue, white, red.

*Two days gone. Return expected. Path open.*

She was expecting someone. She had left the door open and the message lit, and the message said she'd be back.

Emrys stood in the mouth of the cave with the water roaring behind him and twelve years of silence in front of him, and felt the awe and the anger braid themselves together so tightly he couldn't tell which was holding up which. She had found something under this mountain worth faking death for. She had engineered a trail of stones and brass to lead him here — to lead *someone* here — and then she had walked into the dark and left the lamp burning.

"She's close," he said.

Sefa looked past him into the black, listening the way she listened to rivers. From somewhere deep inside the mountain, faint through all that stone, came a sound that was neither water nor wind.

Metal. Rhythmic. Patient.

Someone was digging.
