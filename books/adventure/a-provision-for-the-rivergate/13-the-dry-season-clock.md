## 13. The Dry Season Clock

Sefa had learned to read weather the way other people read letters — slowly, with her lips moving, checking every line twice. Her grandmother had taught her on the delta, before the delta was a memory and a drowned name: *the sky lies first in the west, child. The east only tells you what already happened.*

So when she woke at first light in the shadow of the falls and looked west, she did not like what the sky said.

The clouds had come down overnight. Not the high white towers of the dry season, all show and no delivery, but a low gray ceiling pressed against the karst like a lid on a pot. The air tasted of iron and wet stone. Somewhere above the falls, thunder spoke in a language she'd spent half her life pretending not to fear.

She sat up in the longboat's stern, where she'd taken the second watch, and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for two days.

* * *

Emrys slept badly. She could tell by the way he lay — not relaxed but collapsed, one arm flung over his eyes as if even in sleep he was shielding himself from something. He'd talked in the night, once, just a word or two. She hadn't caught it. She'd decided not to try.

There was a version of this story where she left him here. She'd run it through her mind more than once since they'd tied up below the cairn: take the boat, run the shortcut alone, beat Corvo's gunboat back downriver, sell what she knew to whoever paid best for evidence against Halvard Voss. It would have been cleaner. Safer. Hers.

But the man asleep under the tarp had patched a hull with his own hands rather than let a stranger touch it, and then he'd let *her* aboard without asking what she wanted from him. That was either stupidity or trust, and Sefa had learned that the difference between them usually didn't matter until it was too late.

Besides. The village upstream had a name, and she was the last person alive who remembered how it sounded spoken aloud.

She climbed out onto the rocks and started making tea.

* * *

He woke to the smell of it — bitter river-leaf, boiled twice because the good stuff was at the bottom of the flooded supply crate they hadn't recovered. He emerged from under the tarp looking like a man who'd been arguing with someone all night and lost.

"The sky," Sefa said, without greeting.

"I saw."

"You didn't see. You looked at it. There's a difference." She handed him the tin cup. "Sit down. I need you to listen, and you're bad at that before tea."

He sat. He was getting better at listening; she'd give him that much. Twelve days on the water together and he no longer filled every silence like a man afraid it might fill itself with worse things.

"Here's the clock," she said. "I've been counting since the storm hit us. A monsoon season doesn't arrive, Emrys. It *turns*. Like a door. And the door is turning now." She drew lines in the dirt between them with a stick. "This is today. We're here, below the falls, at the mouth of the cave country. Behind us—" a slash downstream "—is the gorge we ran together, which is suicide going down at anything above half flood, and it's at three-quarters now and climbing. Ahead of us—" another mark, inland "—is the karst. Caves, sinkholes, drowned channels. In monsoon, the whole plateau becomes a sponge that drains wherever it wants. No pilot alive runs those waters in the wet season. Not me. Not anyone."

"So we go back now, ahead of it."

"Back through the gorge? Maybe. If we leave within the day. Maybe." She let that hang. "And back means downriver, and downriver means Corvo's gunboat sitting somewhere in the middle reaches like a spider that's already eaten everything else in its web. He knows where we are, Emrys. He's known since the camp. He's not chasing us. He's *herding* us. He wants us flushed out of these caves with the records in hand, tired and cornered, so he can take them off our bodies."

Emrys stared into his cup. "And if we stay?"

"Then we stay past any rescue window. Past the contract deadline. Past the dry season." She ticked it off on her fingers. "Months. Cut off. No radio — yours is dead and there's nothing on this side of the plateau to raise anyway. Whatever food we can carry in, plus whatever the caves and the river give us, which will be less than you think. When the water drops — if it drops on schedule, next dry season — we come out into a valley that Corvo has had a year to make his own."

"That's not a plan. That's a tomb with better scenery."

"It's a choice," Sefa said. "Which is more than most people get."

* * *

They argued. Of course they argued. They were both too proud to do anything else, and pride, Sefa had found, was just grief wearing armor.

He wanted to go forward immediately — find Ilsa, find the records, get out ahead of the water. She made him stand at the falls' edge and watch the river for an hour while she timed the rise against marks she'd cut in the stone. Six inches since dawn. The falls themselves had thickened overnight, gone from white ropes to a single brown wall.

"That's your exit closing," she said. "Every hour you spend underground, the door narrows. Go in today and you're committed. Fully. No changing your mind at the first fork because your mother's trail goes cold."

"And you?" His voice was careful. "You could still run. You've got the skills. You told me yourself nobody knows the shortcut like you."

It was a test, and she knew it, and she hated that he'd learned to set tests.

"I could," she agreed.

"Why aren't you?"

Sefa looked at the water. The honest answer was ugly and simple and she'd been circling it for days like a bird over a kill. So she said it plainly, because her grandmother had also taught her that a debt spoken aloud weighs less than one carried silent.

"Because I came up this river for Voss's throat, and somewhere between the gorge and the grave with your father's compass in it, that stopped being enough." She turned to face him. "Your mother faked her death to keep something out of the hands of the man who drowned my home. Do you understand what that means? She looked at the same ledger I've been carrying and chose to burn her own name to balance it. I have spent nine years wanting Voss dead. I never once imagined anyone had *paid* for it already — paid in the only coin that matters." She shook her head. "If I run now, I save myself and lose the only chance I'll ever have to finish what she started. Revenge gets me a corpse. This might get me the truth. Corpses don't testify."

Emrys was quiet a long moment. "That's not revenge anymore."

"No," Sefa said. "It isn't. I noticed that too. I'm deciding whether to be angry about it."

"Are you?"

A laugh escaped her, short and surprised. "Ask me when we're out of the caves."

* * *

They spent the morning preparing, and preparation, Sefa thought, was its own kind of vow. You didn't say *I'm staying* with words; you said it with rope coiled and dried fish wrapped in oilcloth, with torches cut and waxed, with the longboat stripped to essentials and hauled above the flood line and lashed to trees that had survived a hundred monsoons and looked insulted by this one.

Emrys worked beside her without being asked, and that was new. On the river he'd been competent but solitary, doing his tasks in his own silence. Now he handed her things before she reached for them. Now he asked questions about knots instead of pretending he already knew.

"What happens to the boat?" he asked, tying off the last lashing.

"She waits. Boats are patient. It's people who drown." Sefa patted the gunwale — the patched hull he'd refused help fixing, back at the beginning, in another life twelve days deep. "We'll want her when the water drops. Assuming we're alive to want anything."

"Assuming."

"You keep saying that word like it's a plan."

He almost smiled. Almost. "My mother used to say something like that. About archaeology. *Certainty is for people who haven't dug deep enough.*"

"Smart woman."

"She's a liar," Emrys said, but there was no heat in it, and Sefa heard what lived underneath the words — the same thing that had been living there since the false grave: hope, furious and unwelcome, refusing to die.

* * *

They ate at midday, and after eating Sefa laid out the map of the karst she'd drawn from memory — her own survey, years old, made during the seasons she'd spent hunting Voss's operations along the upper river. Limestone country didn't forgive imprecision, so she'd been precise.

"We go in behind the falls," she said, tapping the entrance the cairn had pointed toward. "The markers your mother left lead deeper. But understand what 'deeper' means down here. These caves drain the whole plateau. When the monsoon peaks, some of these passages will run like rivers. Others will siphon — pull air and water *down*, fast enough to drag a grown man. My grandmother called the karst *the country that breathes*. You feel it breathing, you move toward the exhale and away from the inhale, and you never, ever argue with it."

"And if we get separated?"

"We don't." She said it flatly. "In the karst, separation is death with extra steps. We tie together at every junction. We count each other out loud. You will hate it. Do it anyway."

He nodded, and she watched him absorb it — really absorb it, the way he'd absorbed the storm, the way he'd absorbed the ransacked camp. He was learning to be afraid properly, which was to say: afraid and functional. Most men broke one way or the other. He bent.

"One more thing," Sefa said, and hesitated, because the next part cost her. "Whatever we find down there — whatever your mother found — I need you to remember that I came for Voss. Not for treasure. Not for history. If it comes down to choosing between the records and getting out alive, you choose out. Both of us choose out. Paper burns, people don't un-drown."

"You sound like you've thought about this."

"I've thought about nothing else for nine years." She rolled the map and tied it. "Revenge is a fire, Emrys. It keeps you warm right up until it eats the house. I'd like to get out of this with the house."

* * *

Late afternoon, the light went strange — greenish, submarine, the color the sky turned in the last hours before the season truly broke. They stood at the cave mouth behind the falls, packs on, torches lit, the rope between them already knotted at their belts. The roar of the water filled the world. Spray drifted through the entrance like breath.

Sefa paused with one hand on the wet stone.

"Once we go in," she said, shouting over the falls, "there's no halfway. The water decides when we come out. Not us. Not Corvo. Not the calendar."

Emrys looked back the way they'd come — down at the brown river, the hidden boat, the whole drowning valley — and she watched him weigh it, honestly weigh it, the way he'd weighed every bad choice since the dockside flophouse. Then he looked at her.

"Together, then," he said. "Counting out loud."

She nodded, turned, and led him into the breathing dark.

Behind them, the first true rain of the monsoon began to fall on the plateau, sealing the door.

Three hundred meters inside the mountain, by torchlight, they found the first of Ilsa Vasquez's markers — and scratched across it, fresh, in charcoal that had not yet dried:

*Turn back. He's already inside.*
