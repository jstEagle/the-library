## 14. Chamber of the Second River

The cairn of survey markers led them behind the falls, and behind the falls the world ended.

Emrys felt it first as a change in the air — a cool exhalation rising out of the limestone, smelling of wet stone and something older, mineral and mineral-dark. Then the light changed. The roar of the falls, which had been a wall of sound for two days of climbing, suddenly came from everywhere at once, bouncing off rock he couldn't see, until Sefa's hand on his shoulder stopped him dead.

"Wait." Her voice was close to his ear, barely audible over the water. "There's a shelf. Feel with your feet before you trust your eyes."

He did as she said, and she was right — the floor dropped away to his left in a lip of slick stone, and beyond it there was nothing but the sound of falling water and a blackness so complete it seemed to press against his open eyes like a thumb. He clicked on the headlamp Ilsa had left cached with the cairn, wrapped in oilcloth with a spare battery and a note in her handwriting: *Two days' rations past this point. Trust the markers, not the water.*

*Trust the markers, not the water.* Twelve years without a word from her, and here she was giving him instructions again, precise and impersonal, as if he were still a boy she could direct from across a dig site.

They went in single file, Sefa behind him because she refused to go first into a passage she hadn't chosen, and the falls fell away behind them by degrees until the sound became a heartbeat rather than a flood. The passage narrowed, then opened, then narrowed again, the ceiling dipping low enough that they crawled through one stretch on their bellies through cold mud that smelled of bat guano and time. Emrys counted the survey markers — small brass discs glued into the rock, each stamped with the expedition's serial code — and told himself the count meant his mother had come this way alive, recently enough that the glue hadn't dried to dust.

It was thin comfort. It was also the only kind he had.

* * *

An hour in, or three — time went strange down there, stretched and pooled like water finding its level — the passage opened without warning, and Emrys stopped breathing.

The headlamp beam reached maybe forty meters, and in those forty meters he saw a gallery carved by hands, not water alone. Pillars. Rows of them, square-cut, running off into the dark on both sides, each one incised with lines of script that caught the lamplight like fish scales. Between the pillars, the floor was paved in fitted stone so precisely joined that the seams vanished under a skin of calcite, and the whole surface sloped gently downward, toward —

Toward the sound. A vast, deep, patient sound beneath everything, below even the memory of the falls. Water moving in the dark the way blood moves in a body: unseen, essential, everywhere.

"Sefa," he said. His voice came out wrong, small. "Are you seeing this?"

She came up beside him, and he watched her face in the spill of his own lamp — watched the pilot who navigated rapids by reading foam patterns take in the pillars, the script, the slope of the ancient road descending into blackness. Her jaw worked once.

"My grandmother used to tell a story," she said finally. "That before the maps, before the companies, the river had two mouths. One you could see. One you couldn't." She looked at him. "I thought it was a story."

They followed the road down.

* * *

The chamber, when they reached it, could not be seen all at once. That was the thing Emrys kept failing to explain to himself, turning his head slowly, sweeping the lamp, trying to take it in the way you took in a landscape — and failing, because no landscape behaved like this.

The cavern was enormous. His beam died against a ceiling he never found. And filling the floor of it, running bank to bank in the darkness like a river lying on its back, was water — a broad, slow, black current moving with a confidence no surface river ever had, unhurried, unbothered by season or storm. Along its near edge ran a quay of dressed stone, and moored at the quay, half-sunk and furred with white calcite, lay boats. Dozens of them. Long, shallow-hulled craft, some intact, some collapsed into their own skeletons, a fleet parked at a dock for a voyage that had ended before any nation now on the map had learned to write.

"They built a harbor," Emrys said. "Underground. A harbor underground." He heard his own voice crack on the last word and didn't care.

Sefa crouched at the quay's edge and dipped her hand in the current. "It's warm," she said, startled. "Feel it. Warm as bathwater. There's a source down there somewhere — a spring, a thermal vent, something feeding it." She straightened, wiping her hand on her trousers, and looked up at the invisible ceiling. "This is where the river goes when the valley floods. This is where it comes from when the valley dries." She turned to him, and in the lamplight her expression was one he'd never seen on her: not sharp, not guarded, but genuinely shaken. "Emrys. Your mother didn't find ruins. She found the machinery."

"The machinery?"

"Of the valley. Of the whole river." She gestured at the quay, the fleet, the dark water. "You don't build a harbor under a mountain unless ships are coming and going on schedule. You don't cut a road down to a river that doesn't need roads unless people are moving cargo on schedule. Everything here is scheduled. Engineered." She said the word carefully, tasting it. "My village drowned in six hours. Six hours, after three hundred years of living with that river and learning its moods. I always thought it was bad luck. A freak year." Her voice flattened. "It wasn't luck. Something upstream opened."

Emrys felt the cold come into him then, ahead of understanding, the way a storm announces itself in the joints before the sky changes. "Let's find out what," he said.

* * *

They found the expedition's camp an hour later, on a natural ledge above the quay where the air moved and the stone was dry. Two tents. Crates stacked and labeled in Ilsa's blocky capitals. A folding table covered in drawings, photographs, and artifacts laid out in rows with the care of a surgeon laying out instruments.

And a lantern, still burning. Turned low, but burning. Someone had been here within hours.

"She's close," Emrys said, and hated how much hope leaked into his voice, how nakedly it betrayed him.

Sefa said nothing. She'd gone to the table.

The artifacts were arranged in sequence, and Ilsa had annotated each one on a card in her small, ruthless handwriting. Emrys picked up the nearest: a disc of greenish bronze, palm-sized, pierced at the center, its face engraved with concentric rings of the same scale-like script he'd seen on the pillars. The card read:

*Valve seal, ceremonial replica. Functional originals recovered from gatehouse complex, north wall. Alloy analysis pending. Note the wear pattern on the central spindle — this object was turned, repeatedly, by hand.*

Turned. Like a key.

The next artifact was a fragment of relief carving, plaster-jacketed for transport, showing a procession of figures operating what could only be described as a machine: a great horizontal drum studded with spokes, figures at the spokes, water pouring from channels above and below. Ilsa's card:

*Flood-gate winding mechanism, west portal. Compare with sluice-gate reliefs from the lower valley sites (see photos 44–61). The Rivergate civilization did not merely survive the flood cycle. They administered it.*

Administered it. Emrys set the fragment down with exaggerated care, because his hands had begun to shake.

He walked the length of the table. Gate seals. Calibration weights marked in graduated units. A clay tablet — actual clay, soft-edged with age — bearing a grid of symbols that Ilsa had partially deciphered on the facing card: numbers, columns of numbers, and beside them a repeated glyph she'd translated, tentatively, as *opening*. Dates. A schedule of openings.

A calendar of floods.

"They decided," he said aloud. He wasn't talking to Sefa anymore; he wasn't sure he was talking at all. "When the valley flooded and when it didn't. Which villages got water and which got scoured. They sat in this mountain and turned the river on and off." He looked at the tablet, at the neat columns of numbers, at the tidy little glyph for *opening*, and twelve years of his mother's letters came back to him — the ones she'd written before she stopped writing, full of a language he'd never learned to read, a passion he'd never been invited into. She had always known. Even then, teaching him to skip stones on the delta, she must have wondered what hand moved the water.

"It's beautiful," Sefa said quietly. She was standing over the relief of the winding mechanism, and her voice had a strange doubled quality, wonder and grief wound together so tightly he couldn't tell which was holding which up. "It's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen, and it killed my village twice. Once three thousand years ago, and once last spring." She touched the carved drum of spokes, lightly, the way you'd touch a scar. "Whoever holds this holds the river. That's what this is. Not knowledge. Leverage."

"That's why she hid," Emrys said. The cold in his joints had traveled inward now, settled behind his sternum. "Not the artifacts. Anyone would want artifacts. This—" he swept his hand across the tablets, the seals, the photographs of the gatehouse complex "—this is a weapon with a three-thousand-year safety catch. You find the functional gates. You learn the mechanism. And suddenly you don't have to buy water rights or negotiate with downstream provinces or answer to anyone about dam failures. You just turn the key." He thought of Corvo's enforcer counting coins onto the flophouse table, of the contract with its dry-season deadline, of crates he wasn't allowed to open, and the shape of it finally assembled itself in his chest, whole and terrible. "Corvo didn't fund a dig. He funded a search. And my mother found it first."

"And faked her death to keep it from him," Sefa said. "Which means every man he sent upriver is looking for a corpse that's standing in this room somewhere, waiting to see if we're worth trusting." She glanced at the burning lantern. "Or watching us right now."

The words dropped into the chamber's silence and sank without a ripple. Emrys turned a slow circle, lamp raised, sweeping the black vault above the quay, the ranks of sunken boats, the blind doorways opening off the far walls — and understood, with a lurch, that in a space this size a dozen people could stand within earshot and never be seen. That the darkness here wasn't empty. It was occupied. It had been occupied for three thousand years, and it was very good at keeping things.

Somewhere in that blackness, water moved against stone with the patience of a thing that had outlasted everyone who ever tried to own it.

And somewhere in it too — close, closer than the dark, close enough that the lantern still burned — his mother was deciding whether to step into the light.

Emrys reached out and turned the expedition radio's dial to send, then stopped, his finger on the switch. If she was listening, a call sign might bring her in. Or it might bring in whoever else had a receiver.

Behind him, in the direction of the quay, a pebble ticked against stone.

Someone had moved.
