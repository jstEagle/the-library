## 16. Three Truths, One Exit

The chamber swallowed their lamplight the way the river swallowed rain.

Emrys stood at the edge of the flooded gallery, boots braced on slick stone, and watched his mother's torch play across the walls. Carvings climbed out of the rock in tiers — rivers rendered as serpents, gates rendered as mouths, and everywhere numbers, dense columns of them, chiseled with a precision that made his hands ache to look at. Twelve years he had spent learning to read strata and sediment, and none of it had prepared him for this: a civilization that had not merely lived beside its river but *administered* it.

"You knew," he said. His voice came back flat off the wet stone. "Before you ever took Corvo's money, you knew what was down here."

Ilsa didn't turn. The torchlight carved her profile out of the dark — older than he remembered, thinner, the same stubborn set to her jaw that he saw in mirrors and hated.

"I suspected," she said. "There's a difference."

"There always is, with you."

Sefa shifted behind him. Emrys heard her hand move toward the machete at her belt and then away from it, deliberately, like someone setting down a glass they very much wanted to throw.

"Then say the difference," Sefa said. "Out loud. All of it. Because I've been on this river three days with two people who keep deciding what I'm allowed to know, and my patience drowned somewhere back in the gorge."

Ilsa finally turned. She looked at Sefa for a long moment — really looked, the way she used to look at artifacts before touching them, cataloguing, weighing.

"You're from Sel Ampat," she said.

It wasn't a question, and Emrys saw it land on Sefa like a blow. The pilot's face went still in that particular way he'd learned meant fury being held under pressure, water behind a gate.

"My mother was," Sefa said. "I was born on the water between there and the delta. Why does a woman who fakes her own death know my village?"

"Because I read the hydrological surveys." Ilsa's voice was steady, but Emrys caught the hairline fracture in it. "Corvo's company filed them eight years ago. Upstream flow modeling for the whole valley. Your village sat in one of the modeled flood zones."

"Modeled." Sefa laughed once, and it was an ugly sound. "The monsoon of 'nineteen wasn't a model. It was forty-one houses and a ferry landing and eleven people, including my uncle, gone in one night. They called it an act of God. There were men in Corvo's colors taking measurements *afterward*, and I spent six years telling myself they were counting the dead."

The silence that followed had weight. Water dripped somewhere deep in the cave system, patient as a clock.

"Sefa," Emrys started.

"No." She held up a hand without looking at him. "Let her finish. She's going to finish, because we're past the point where anyone in this cave gets to ration the truth."

* * *

**SEFA**

Sefa had grown up learning to read water the way other children learned letters — current lines, debris trails, the color changes that marked where a river ran shallow over its own lies. It had made her confident. It had made her stupid, too, in the way confidence always did, because she had believed the river was the only thing upstream worth fearing.

Now she stood in a dead civilization's throat and understood that the river had been *managed*. That the flood that took her family's village might have been scheduled.

"Say it plainly," she said to Ilsa Vasquez. "You think the 'nineteen flood was opened on purpose."

"I think the sluice works are real." Ilsa swept her torch across the gallery again, and this time Sefa forced herself to see what the woman was showing her: not just carvings but *machinery* — channels cut into the living rock, sockets where stone gates had once seated, a graded spillway descending into darkness toward the sound of moving water. "And I think whoever built this understood the valley's flood cycle better than any engineer alive today. If you could open and close these gates—" she touched a channel edge, reverent and horrified at once, "—you wouldn't need a dam. You wouldn't need anything visible at all. You could drown a valley and call it weather."

"And Corvo?"

Ilsa's jaw tightened. "Corvo funded this dig because of a letter. A colonial-era survey fragment, sold through a dealer in the capital, describing 'gates beneath the falls.' He didn't fund archaeology. He funded acquisition. My contract gave him first claim on all engineering records recovered from the site." She paused, and Sefa watched her choose honesty over comfort. "I signed it anyway. I told myself I'd find the site first and control what left it. That I could manage him the way I managed everyone else."

"Did you manage him?" Emrys asked quietly. "Or did he manage you? Because from where I stand, Doctor, every road in this story leads through your decisions. You signed his contract. You staged your death. You let me—" his voice caught, and Sefa heard the twelve years inside it, "—you let the whole world believe you were dead rather than tell anyone what you'd found."

"I let them believe it because the last person who tried to report findings like these upstream died in a boating accident," Ilsa said. "Because Corvo's enforcers were already in the delta asking about me by name. And yes—" she met her son's eyes, and did not flinch, which Sefa grudgingly respected, "—because I have always chosen the work. You're allowed to hate that. I'm done pretending otherwise."

"That's not an apology."

"It's not meant to be. Apologies are for things you regret. I regret leaving you. I don't regret keeping this out of his hands." She gestured at the carvings, the channels, the sleeping machinery of a people three thousand years drowned. "This isn't treasure, Emrys. This is a weapon with a river's trigger. And there are four governments and a dozen companies who would pay anything to hold it."

Sefa listened, and felt something in her chest rearranging itself — grief and rage settling into a harder shape. Her village hadn't been taken by weather or by accident. It had been a *test*, if Ilsa was right. A dry run conducted on people who couldn't fight back, using gates older than any nation, mapped by men who filed paperwork afterward.

She thought of her uncle's ferry pole, floating. She thought of the measurement crews in Corvo's colors.

"The records," she said. "Where are they?"

* * *

**EMRYS**

Ilsa led them deeper, along a ledge above the inner channel, to a low chamber where the expedition's remaining crates sat stacked against the wall — maybe a third of what had been shipped upriver, the rest lost or seized when the camp fell. She knelt and worked loose a false bottom with a knife blade.

Inside lay oilcloth bundles, notebooks, and a set of brass-jointed drawing tubes. Survey drawings. Gate schematics. Flow calculations in a dead civilization's notation, half-translated in Ilsa's cramped handwriting in the margins.

"This is everything," she said. "The full mechanism. Where the gates seat, how the counterweights work, what the valley does if you open the upper series during monsoon." She wrapped her arms around the bundle like it was a child. "It dies with me or it goes to a university archive under seal. Those are the only two endings I'll accept."

Emrys looked at the oilcloth and thought about a dockside flophouse, coins counted by lamplight, a man named Brannick saying *your father's debt doesn't die, boy, it waits.* He thought about how Corvo had never once asked him for money.

"He sent me upriver," Emrys said. "The contract, the boat, the supplies. I thought I was paying a debt. I was the compass needle. He needed someone with your name attached to lead him past the checkpoints." He laughed, short and bitter. "He probably paid for the flag I tore coming through the rapids."

"He did," Ilsa said simply. "His gunboat has been anchored below the falls since before the storm. I've heard the generators at night. He was waiting for me to surface or for you to arrive — whichever came first. You were both bait and delivery."

Sefa had gone very still beside him. When Emrys glanced at her, her face had the look he'd first seen at the trading post — that sharp, calculating stillness, a woman doing arithmetic with lives.

"Then here's what I don't understand," Sefa said slowly. "If Corvo's been sitting below the falls all this time, why hasn't he come in after you? The caves have one entrance we found. Men with guns don't wait on politeness."

Ilsa's expression changed — something flickering behind her eyes that Emrys recognized with a cold drop in his stomach, because he'd seen it on dig sites before. It was the look of a person who has just remembered a detail too late.

"The lower chambers," Ilsa said. "When I sealed myself in, I engaged the secondary gate. The old mechanism. It floods the approach passage at high water." She looked toward the distant sound of moving water, and Emrys watched her do the same arithmetic Sefa had been doing. "The monsoon peaked two days ago."

"So the entrance is underwater," Emrys said.

"For now. The system drains on a cycle — the builders designed it that way, a tide inside the mountain. In—" she closed her eyes, calculating against some internal calendar of the valley's rhythms, "—a day, perhaps less, the passage will be passable again. And Corvo will know it too. His engineers will have sounded the water levels every morning since he arrived."

Sefa exhaled slowly. "So we have a day inside a mountain with a man outside it who wants what you're holding, and no exit except the one he's watching."

"Not quite." Ilsa stood, and there was something new in her voice — not hope exactly, but its skeleton. "The builders didn't put all their gates facing downstream. There's an upper series. Outflow channels that vent into the karst above the falls. The survey markers you followed — they weren't pointing to the entrance. They were pointing to the *back door*."

"You never mentioned a back door," Emrys said.

"You never asked what I'd found. You asked if I was alive." She shouldered the bundle of records. "We have until the water drops. After that, every hour is his."

They started back along the ledge, single file, torchlight jumping on wet stone. And Emrys walked at the rear, watching the two women ahead of him — his mother with her stolen inheritance, Sefa with her drowned village — and understood that the truth had not freed anyone. It had only shown them the size of the cage.

Behind them, far down in the mountain, the hidden river kept its ancient schedule, draining the drowned passage one measured inch at a time.

Like a lock counting down.
