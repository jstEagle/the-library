## 17. The Bargain Below

The light came first.

It slid along the far wall of the second gallery like a blade finding the seam of a locked door — a thin, trembling line of white where there had only been blackness and the sound of water. Emrys froze with his hand on the crate of records, and for a moment he thought it was a trick of his own headlamp, some reflection off the wet limestone. Then the line widened. Voices came through it, distorted by stone into something almost gentle.

"They're in," Sefa said. She had gone very still by the entrance to the inner passage, her machete half-drawn, her whole body turned toward the light the way an animal turns toward a hunter's scent. "That's not a torch party. That's a cutting crew."

"How long?"

"Minutes. Maybe less." She looked back at him, and in the lamplight her face was stripped of everything sharp she usually wore. What was left underneath was younger than he'd ever seen it. "The lower galleries will flood when they open that wall. The pressure behind the falls — Ilsa said—"

"I know what she said." He did know. He'd spent two days listening to his mother explain it, watching her hands move over survey maps drawn by people four thousand years dead: *the river is held above us, children. Everything down here is borrowed from it.* "But they don't know that. Corvo's men think they're opening a storeroom."

"Then we run for the upper chambers while they're still cutting."

"And go where?" His voice came out flatter than he intended. "The gorge is flooded. The boat's hidden under three meters of brown water. Every exit on this side of the valley has one of Corvo's men standing in front of it." He heard himself listing the facts the way his father used to list debts at the kitchen table — each one laid down like a card, face up, no bluff left in him. "There's nowhere to run, Sefa. There never was. That was the whole design."

She didn't argue. That was the worst part. Sefa, who argued with rivers, who argued with monsoons, who had argued with him across six days of flooded karst until he'd started to enjoy it — she stood there in the dark and said nothing, because he was right.

From deeper in the cave system, from the chamber where the second river ran black and slow beneath its ceiling of flowstone, came the sound of his mother's footsteps. Measured. Unhurried. Coming toward them.

* * *

Ilsa Vasquez arrived carrying the records as if they were a sleeping child.

Emrys had watched her handle them this way for two days now — the oilcloth packets, the brass cylinders, the notebooks in her own cramped hand — and every time, something in her posture changed. The woman who had faced him across twelve years of silence, who had faked her own death with a compass and a pile of stones, became careful. Tender, even. It made him want to break something.

"We heard the cutting," she said. No greeting. There had been no greetings between them since the chamber; there had only been information, passed back and forth like supplies over a gap.

"You heard it too," Emrys said. "So you know what happens next. They come through, they take these—" he touched the oilcloth, and his mother flinched, actually flinched, "—and they take us. And then Corvo owns the flood cycles of an entire valley, and everyone upstream learns what happened to Sefa's village wasn't an accident. It was a trial run."

"It was a test of the eastern sluices," Ilsa said quietly. "Using living villages as gauges." Her eyes went to Sefa, and there was something in them Emrys couldn't read — grief, or guilt, or the archaeologist's terrible habit of looking at catastrophe and seeing data. "I've told you both. I didn't know the extent of it until—"

"You knew enough to fake your death," Sefa said. Her voice was level. That was how Emrys had learned to recognize her fury: it got quieter, not louder. "You knew enough to let your own son walk into this valley carrying Corvo's crates."

"I didn't know he was coming."

"No," Emrys said. "You never do. You never know anything about me until I'm standing in front of you, and then it's too late for it to matter."

The words landed harder than he'd meant them to. He saw them hit — saw the small collapse around his mother's mouth — and felt nothing like satisfaction. Only the old exhaustion, the one he'd carried out of his father's house at nineteen, the one that smelled like coin dust and cheap rum.

Above them, muffled through forty meters of limestone, came a new sound. Not cutting anymore. Cracking. A deep, structural groan, like a ship changing its mind about sinking.

"The wall won't hold much longer," Ilsa said. "When it goes, the lower galleries fill in under a minute. We need to be moving toward the high passage, now, all three of us—"

"No."

Both women turned. Emrys realized the word had come out of him with a certainty he hadn't known he possessed. It felt strange in his chest, this certainty. It felt like something he'd been carrying unopened for years and had finally, without meaning to, torn the paper off.

He set his hand flat on the crate of records.

"Corvo doesn't want you," he said to his mother. "Not really. You're a problem, but you're not the prize. And Sefa—" he looked at her, and made himself hold it, made himself say the rest of it looking at her, "—Sefa's evidence matters, but Corvo doesn't know how much yet. What he wants is the find. The engineering records. The thing that lets whoever holds it open and close the river like a tap." He swallowed. His mouth was dry as old bone. "And there's exactly one person in this cave Corvo trusts to bring those records out intact."

"Emrys." His mother's voice had changed — the scientist dropping away, something rawer underneath. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"I'm thinking about my father." The words surprised him with their steadiness. "Twelve years ago he signed papers he didn't read, borrowed money he couldn't pay, and died owing a man named Corvo more than our house was worth. And I spent twelve years carrying that debt like it was mine. Because nobody told me it wasn't. Because shame is inheritable — you taught me that too, Mom, just not on purpose." He laughed, and hated the sound of it, and kept going anyway. "Corvo never wanted money from me. He wanted a guide. Someone with your name, someone desperate enough to carry his crates upriver and too stupid to ask why the contract paid triple rate. I led him here. Every rapid, every shortcut, every night I sat on that radio screaming static into the dark — I was doing his work for him. So the debt stops being inherited today. Today I pay it myself. With me."

"Absolutely not." Sefa stepped forward, and her hand found his sleeve — gripped it, hard, the way she'd gripped the tiller in the gorge when the water tried to take them sideways. "You are not walking out of this cave and handing yourself to that man. I have buried enough people who thought sacrifice was a plan. My brother thought that. My father thought that. The river keeps the ones who offer themselves up, Emrys. It always does."

"Then the river and I finally agree on something." He covered her hand with his own. Her knuckles were scarred from rope and engine work; his were soft from counting other people's coins. Between them, he thought, they almost made one complete pair of hands. "Listen to me. Both of you. If the three of us run, they hunt us through flooded karst with guns and lights, and they catch us — maybe not today, maybe tomorrow, but they catch us, and then Corvo has all three of us and the records and no reason to leave anyone breathing. But if I walk out holding these—" he lifted the top packet of oilcloth, letting it catch the lamplight, "—and tell him I cut a deal to save my own skin, he'll believe it. Because it's exactly what he paid me to be. The son who sells whatever's left of the family name."

"That's not who you are," Ilsa said.

"You don't know who I am." He said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "That's the whole point, Mom. That's been the whole point for twelve years. But I'm about to find out."

* * *

They argued for another four minutes. He counted them against the cracking of the wall.

Sefa argued with her body as much as her voice — pacing, turning, once driving her fist into the limestone hard enough to split skin, and Emrys understood that this was her version of weeping and loved her for it and did not let it change his answer. Ilsa argued like an archaeologist: stratified, patient, peeling back his logic layer by layer looking for the flaw. She found several. He acknowledged them all. None of them changed the arithmetic.

In the end it was the water that decided it.

The groan overhead deepened into a sustained roar, and somewhere in the dark below them, the second river answered — a rising note, a swelling, the sound of an ancient machine remembering its purpose. Dust sifted from the ceiling. Their lamps swung shadows in wild arcs across walls carved with figures Emrys had spent two days trying to read: processions of small carved people, climbing toward high ground, generation after generation, the same procession repeated like a prayer.

"They built all this to survive the water," he said quietly. "Every chamber, every sluice, every record. They weren't hiding from the floods. They were negotiating with them." He looked at his mother. "You taught me that. Whatever else you didn't teach me — you taught me that. Things worth keeping survive by knowing when to give ground."

"Don't quote my own field notes at me while you volunteer for a firing squad," Ilsa said, and her voice cracked clean through the middle of the sentence, and for one unguarded moment she was not Dr. Vasquez of the expedition reports, she was just a woman in a cave with her son, and her hands were shaking.

He hugged her.

It was brief and graceless and twelve years late, and neither of them pretended it fixed anything. She smelled like cave dust and lamp oil and, underneath it, faintly, impossibly, like the cedar chest in the hallway of a house he'd grown up in. When he pulled back, she pressed something into his palm — the compass. His father's compass, the one from the false grave, the one she'd carried out of their old life and carried into this one.

"So you can't say I never gave you anything," she whispered.

"Sefa." He turned to her last, because he'd saved her for last, because he was a coward about exactly one thing and he knew precisely which thing it was. "The boat. The patched hull, the honest trade runs, the—" He stopped. Started again. "Downriver. When this is done. Take her downriver, both of you, and don't look back at the gorge, and if you ever find yourself at a dock where a man named Vasquez owes somebody money, keep walking."

"You come tell me that yourself." Her eyes were bright and furious and wet. "You come walk up to that dock yourself, Emrys Vasquez, or I swear on every drowned thing in this valley I will find you and drag you out of whatever grave you're pretending to lie in, and I will make your afterlife so difficult—"

"Sefa."

"—that death will seem like the merciful option, do you hear me—"

"*Sefa.*" He took her face in both hands. Her jaw was set like bedrock. "Six days ago I didn't know you. Now I can't imagine the river without you in it. That's either the cruelest joke this valley has ever played or the truest thing I've ever owned. Either way—" his voice failed him, and he pushed through it, "—either way, it's yours. Keep it. Keep it safe. That's the provision. That's the whole provision."

Above them, the wall gave.

Not all at once — a section of it, high on the left, a slab the size of a door tearing free with a sound like the world's largest bone breaking. White water flashed in the gap. Torchlight poured through after it, and voices, and the unmistakable metallic clatter of men who had been expecting a treasure room and were instead watching a river decide to move in.

"Now," Emrys said. "Go. High passage. Don't stop for anything, don't wait for anything, and whatever you hear behind you—" he slung the records onto his shoulder and picked up the compass, "—remember it's a bargain. It's just a bargain."

Sefa kissed him once, hard, like sealing a letter. Then she took Ilsa's arm and pulled her into the dark of the high passage, and Emrys stood alone in the flickering torchlight with a dead civilization's secrets on his shoulder and his heart hammering out a rhythm he finally recognized.

Not fear. For the first time in twelve years — not fear.

Footsteps. Many of them, splashing. Torches resolving into faces, faces resolving into rifles, and at the center of it, wading through ankle-deep water with his coat held clear of the flood like a man who had never once gotten anything on him that he didn't choose to wear — Corvo.

"Well," the smuggler said, smiling, spreading his arms to take in the drowning chamber, the fleeing darkness, the whole ruined magnificence of it. "The prodigal son. And I see you brought the family silver."

Emrys shifted the records on his shoulder and felt the compass cold against his palm, pointing — for the first time in his life — exactly where he chose.

"I brought a counteroffer," he said. "And you're going to want to hear it before this water gets any higher."

Behind Corvo, unnoticed by every man in the room, the ancient sluice gate at the far end of the chamber began — silently, slowly, with four thousand years of patience — to turn.
