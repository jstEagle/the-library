## 18. Water Remembers

The water came for us before Corvo's men did.

I heard it first — a sound like the mountain clearing its throat, a deep subterranean groan that traveled up through the limestone and into my boots, into my teeth, into the hollow of my chest where fear had been living for days. Ilsa stood at the base of the great sluice wheel, her hands still on the spokes, her face lit amber by the torches we'd wedged into iron brackets older than any nation I could name.

"Emrys," she said. "Get them to the high gallery. Now."

The wheel turned with a resistance that seemed impossible — stone grinding against stone, a mechanism that hadn't moved in perhaps four hundred years, and it moved anyway, because the people who built this place had engineered their machinery to outlast their civilization, and they had succeeded. Water answered somewhere below us. Not a trickle. A roar.

Sefa grabbed my arm. "What did she do?"

"What your village taught you to fear," I said, and hated myself for it even as I said it. "Come on."

We ran. The chamber of the second river stretched away behind us — that impossible vaulted dark where the underground waterway ran black and patient, where the artifacts lay in their niches like sleeping sentinels, where Ilsa had spent months alone with the dead. Behind us now, the sound changed pitch. The river was rising. The Rivergate civilization had not merely lived beside the flood cycles of the valley; they had *written* them, opening and closing gates in the mountain's bones the way a farmer opens and closes irrigation channels, and Ilsa Vasquez had just opened every gate at once.

* * *

Three hours earlier, we had been cornered.

Corvo's men came into the caves the way water comes into a sinking hull — through every crack at once, patient, total. We heard them before we saw them: boot heels on wet stone, the clatter of gear, a voice echoing orders in a flat professional cadence I recognized from dockside flophouses and debt collections. Six men, maybe eight. Carbines. Floodlights that turned the ancient carvings into stuttering shadows.

Ilsa had killed her own torch an hour before, when the first echo reached the inner chambers, and the three of us crouched behind a fallen pillar near the sluice mechanism while the lights swept the great chamber beyond.

"They'll take the records," Ilsa whispered. Her voice was steady in the way that cost her something; I knew that steadiness now, recognized it as the same controlled calm she'd used on me when I was nine and feverish, the calm that meant she was more frightened than I was. "Everything. The survey maps, the hydraulic schematics, the translation folios. Corvo didn't fund twelve years of work to study it. He funded it to own it."

"Then we run," Sefa breathed. "There's a back way through the karst—"

"There isn't. Not anymore. They've covered the entrance." Ilsa's hand found mine in the dark. It was the first time she had touched me since the reunion, since I'd stood in the torchlight of the inner gallery and watched her walk out of four hundred years of silence, alive, having buried a grave full of river stones under her compass and walked away from her own death the way she'd once walked away from me. Her hand was cold and cracked and familiar. "But they don't know what this place can do. And I do."

I understood then what she meant, and my stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

"You'll drown us."

"I'll drown *them*," she said. "The lower chambers flood first. We have the high gallery, the dry passage behind the falls. The engineers who built this left themselves exits everywhere — they were afraid of their own machine too. That's how good it is." She paused. "It will also destroy everything below the flood line. Twelve years of excavation. Artifacts that no one has seen since the last priest of the Rivergate set them down."

"And you're willing to do that." It wasn't a question. It was an accusation, and it was also, I realized with a strange vertigo, the closest thing to understanding I'd felt since I found her. This was the woman who had left me. This was the woman who had faked her death rather than surrender her work to men with guns. Both true. Both the same woman.

"I spent three months deciding whether to tell anyone what I found," Ilsa said quietly. "I decided no. That decision cost me my reputation, my expedition, and—" her grip tightened on my hand, just slightly, "—it cost me the chance to write to you. Again. But if these records leave this mountain, someone upstream will build new gates. New flood cycles. Tested on villages like Sefa's. I would burn it all twice over."

Sefa made a sound low in her throat — not agreement, not refusal. Something older than either.

Above us, boots on stone. A flashlight beam crawled across the far wall, catching the carved procession of figures — priests, engineers, kings — marching forever toward a door that no longer existed.

"It has to be me at the wheel," Ilsa said. "The counterweight sequence is unforgiving. If it's done wrong, the gates jam half-open and we all die in here politely." She looked at me, and in the faint reflected glow from the enemy's lights I saw her face clearly: lined, sunburned, older than my memory of her but unmistakably, infuriatingly herself. "Emrys. The records. The satchel in the survey tent alcove — the real one, not the decoy. If the water takes it, twelve years die for nothing. If Corvo takes it, worse than nothing. You have to get it and get up. Can you do that?"

Twelve years ago she had asked me a different version of the same question — *can you be brave while I'm gone* — and I had answered by hating her for two decades. I thought about Corvo's enforcer counting coins onto the flophouse table. I thought about the contract with my mother's name printed on it, the trap I'd walked into willingly, the debt that had never been about money.

"Yeah," I said. "I can do that."

* * *

So: the wheel. The groan of stone. The roar beneath us.

Ilsa held the sluice open with her whole body, arms locked, feet braced against the base plate, and the sound of the mountain filling with water was the most terrible and beautiful thing I have ever heard — a cathedral organ played by the dead, every pipe at once. Sefa hauled me toward the high gallery stairs cut into the eastern wall, narrow steps worn concave by centuries of feet, and I went, because going was the plan, because the plan was all we had.

"The satchel!" Ilsa shouted over the rising thunder.

I broke away from Sefa's grip and ran the other way.

The alcove was thirty meters across the chamber floor, past the fallen pillar, past the niches of artifacts gleaming wet in the sweep of the intruders' lights. The water was already coming in — ankle-deep, then shin-deep, pouring from channels in the walls that had waited four hundred years for exactly this command, moving with the confidence of engineering rather than the chaos of accident. The Rivergate people had designed their own drowning, and the drowning was *orderly*. Gates opened in sequence. Channels filled in order. Somewhere a dead engineer's arithmetic was still being obeyed, perfectly, long after his name had rotted out of memory.

A flashlight beam caught me mid-chamber.

"There! Stop!"

I did not stop. I have never been less inclined to stop in my life. I heard the crack of a carbine shot and the ricochet went singing off the vaulted ceiling somewhere above me, and then I was at the alcove, tearing aside the false paneling Ilsa had described, and there it was: the oilskin satchel, heavy as a sleeping child, full of maps and folios and the accumulated proof of what the Rivergate civilization had been — not a city beside a river, but a *hand on the river's throat*, the first hydrological empire, the reason the valley flooded when it flooded, the knowledge that in the wrong hands was a weapon aimed at every village downstream of every dam not yet built.

I slung it across my chest. The water hit my thighs.

They were shouting behind me now, three voices tangled together, and another shot sparked white off the stone to my left. Ahead, the stair to the high gallery was a black vertical slot, and Sefa was in it, reaching down, screaming my name in a voice I'd only heard her use once before — on the gorge, in the storm, when the river tried to keep me.

The water took my knees. Then my waist. It wasn't gentle and it wasn't cruel; it was simply *inevitable*, the way the monsoon was inevitable, the way debts were inevitable, the way mothers left and rivers rose and dead civilizations kept their promises. I fought it step by step, the satchel dragging at my neck, and Sefa's hand closed around my wrist — the same wrist, I realized dimly, that she'd grabbed on the boat, and I made a note to tell her someday that she had a habit of saving my life by the same handful of bones.

She pulled. I climbed. Below us the chamber became a lake, then a churning throat, and the flashlights of Corvo's men swung wildly across a surface that was swallowing their footing, their equipment, their certainty. Someone was screaming about the exit. Someone else was screaming about the water level. Ilsa's voice cut through all of it, clear as a bell across water:

"The gates close in eleven minutes! I'd run if I were you!"

And then she laughed. My mother laughed — wild, unburdened, a sound I had not heard since I was small enough to be carried, and I understood that whatever else this night cost, she had already paid it and considered it cheap.

* * *

The high gallery was a natural fissure the builders had widened and floored, running upward behind the falls, and it gave us back our breath in stages — first the water receding below, then the air cooling, then the sound of the flood fading to a vast muffled heartbeat through the stone. We climbed by touch and by the chemical stick of two light-sticks Ilsa produced from her jacket, green light painting our faces like drowned things.

Halfway up, we stopped on a landing wide enough for three, and Ilsa sat down heavily against the wall, and for a moment none of us spoke. The satchel sat between my knees. Water ran out of my clothes and pooled around my boots. Somewhere far below, the mountain finished its work.

"You could have died down there," Sefa said finally. To me, but looking at Ilsa. "Both of you. For paper."

"For proof," Ilsa said.

"For *leverage*," Sefa said, and her voice had an edge I'd learned to respect. "You know what I want it for. My village is under forty meters of reservoir because someone tested old floodgates against it. Those records prove the gates were tested. Prove *when*, and *by whose order*. I'm not letting them burn, and I'm not letting them drown, and I am certainly not letting them go back to Corvo."

"No one said anything about burning," Ilsa said.

"Not yet," I said.

They both looked at me. Green light, wet stone, the smell of ancient water and gun smoke. I pulled the satchel onto my lap and put my hand flat on the oilskin, and felt through the fabric the weight of everything inside — my mother's twelve years, Sefa's drowned village, Corvo's empire, my father's debt, all of it pressed together into one bundle that every person in this mountain wanted and no two of them wanted for the same thing.

"We're not out yet," I said. "Corvo's men are between us and the entrance, the falls are running monsoon-heavy, and the man himself is sitting on the river with a gunboat and a grudge. So we climb, and we stay alive until morning. Then we decide what these papers are worth, and to whom, and what it costs." I looked at my mother. "All three of us decide. That's the provision. Nobody decides alone anymore. Not even you."

For a long moment Ilsa Vasquez said nothing. Then she nodded, once, the smallest concession I had ever seen her make, and it was worth more than any apology she might have rehearsed.

We climbed.

Behind and below us, the water settled into its new channels, remembering its old work, and the mountain held its breath around the three of us like a fist slowly learning to open.
