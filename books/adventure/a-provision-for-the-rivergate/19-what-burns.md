## 19. What Burns

The ledge was barely wide enough for three people and a fire that hadn't been lit yet.

Emrys stood at its lip with the satchel against his chest and looked down the length of the gorge, four hundred feet of green water churning white where it poured through the falls below them, and felt the whole river holding its breath. The monsoon had broken in the night — not ended, never ended this time of year, but eased into a steady grey drizzle that softened every edge and made the world feel like it had been left out in the rain too long.

Behind him, Ilsa crouched at the back wall of the overhang, packing the last of their gear with hands that shook only when she thought no one was watching. Sefa stood at the narrow throat of rock that connected the ledge to the cliff path, her machete drawn, watching the way they'd come.

"They'll be twenty minutes behind us," she said. "Maybe less. The lower chambers will have drained by now — Corvo's divers will have found the sluice tunnel."

"Then we have twenty minutes," Emrys said.

He set the satchel down on the flattest stone he could find and knelt beside it, and for a long moment he didn't open it. The leather was dark with river water, swollen at the seams, the brass buckle furred with green. Inside were the engineering records of a civilization dead two thousand years — the flood-cycles of an entire valley, the gates and channels and sluice-works by which the Rivergate people had taught the river to obey them. His mother's life's work. The thing men had already killed for. The thing his father's debt had been built to deliver into other hands.

*You're the courier now,* Corvo's enforcer had told him in a flophouse that smelled of mildew and old smoke, weeks and a lifetime ago. *Same as your father was.*

His father had carried things for Corvo too. Ledgers, mostly. Then people. Then, at the end, himself — walking into the river one night with everything he owed still unpaid, leaving a son to inherit the arithmetic. Emrys had spent twelve years believing the debt was money. He knew better now. It had always been a route. A boat, a contract, crates he wasn't allowed to open, a mother's name printed on the manifest like bait on a hook. He had rowed himself straight upriver into the trap and called it escape.

"You don't have to do it this way," Ilsa said quietly.

He didn't turn around. "Yes, I do."

"There are copies in my head. In Sefa's notes. In—"

"In nothing." He heard his own voice come out flat and hard, a stranger's voice. "That's the point, Mother. If it exists anywhere, it can be bought. If it can be bought, someone upstream will buy it, and they won't build sluice-gates to save villages. They'll build them to drown villages. You said so yourself. You faked your own death rather than hand it over. Don't ask me to be more careful about it than you were."

She was silent. The rain ticked softly against the overhang above them, running off the lip in a thin bright curtain between the ledge and the drop.

Sefa spoke without turning from the path. "Whatever you're going to do, do it soon. I can hear the boats."

* * *

They had come up out of the caves in the grey hour before dawn, swimming the flooded lower gallery with the records sealed in oilcloth, while the water Ilsa had unleashed thundered through the chambers behind them and Corvo's men shouted in the dark like men drowning in a house they'd thought was empty. The old civilization had done the work for them — two thousand years of hydraulic genius, triggered by a woman who had spent thirty years learning how to pull its levers. The river remembered. That was the whole discovery, Emrys thought. Not gold, not tombs. Memory. Water remembers what was done to it, and gives it back.

They'd climbed the goat-path behind the falls as the light came up sickly and grey, Sefa first with the machete, then Ilsa, then Emrys with the satchel strapped across his chest like a child he refused to put down. Twice the path had crumbled under his boots and dropped him to his elbows over open air, the falls roaring past close enough to wet his face, and both times the thought that had pulled him up wasn't fear of dying. It was the simple, furious clarity of what he meant to do before he did.

Now he opened the buckle.

The oilcloth came away wet and heavy. Inside, wrapped in layers of waxed linen, were the records: sheets of hammered copper etched with the survey diagrams, clay tablets fired hard as brick, Ilsa's own notebooks — years of them, her small precise handwriting filling every margin, every page a map of the second river beneath the valley. He lifted the top notebook and saw her sketch of the main sluice-chamber, annotated in three colors of ink, and beneath it, in the corner, a tiny drawing she must have made absent-mindedly: a boy sitting on a dock, reading, his back to the water.

He closed his eyes.

* * *

Corvo's gunboat came around the bend below the falls at half past nine, black smoke standing up out of its funnel in the windless air, and Emrys watched it from the ledge as if watching a play he already knew the ending of.

It anchored mid-channel with the confidence of something that owned the water it sat on. A second boat followed, smaller, and a third, and armed men spilled onto the shingle beaches on both sides of the gorge, spreading out along the base of the falls, looking up. Through the drizzle Emrys could see the figure at the gunboat's rail — broad, unhurried, hands clasped behind his back, a man dressed for a funeral in a country where nobody buried anyone. Corvo didn't look up right away. He let his men search first. He was a patient man; patience was the whole architecture of him. He had waited years for this site. He had waited months for a debtor foolish enough to lead him to it.

"He knows you're up there," Sefa said. She had come to stand at the lip beside him, machete sheathed now, arms crossed. "Why else anchor in the open? He wants you to see him."

"I know."

"He thinks you're going to bargain. That's why he hasn't sent men up the cliff yet. He thinks you'll dangle it and negotiate — your debt, your mother's life, passage downriver. That's what he'd do in your place."

"I'm not in his place."

"No," Sefa said, and there was something in her voice then, low and dry, almost approval. "You're not."

Ilsa came forward slowly and stood on Emrys's other side. Below them, across the roar of the falls, Corvo raised his head at last and found them — three small figures on a shelf of stone — and Emrys saw him smile. He couldn't have seen the smile at that distance. He saw it anyway. Some smiles carry.

Corvo lifted one hand, palm open. An offer. Come down. Bring it. We'll settle accounts like reasonable people.

Emrys picked up the flint.

* * *

They had no kindling worth the name — everything on the ledge was soaked — but Sefa had kept a tin of pitch-pine splinters dry inside her jacket since the trading post, three hundred miles ago, because she was the kind of person who kept things dry inside her jacket, and Emrys had loved her a little for it since the day he'd noticed. He stacked the splinters now with hands that were steadier than he deserved. Ilsa poured the last of the lamp oil over them without being asked.

"What are you doing?" Sefa asked sharply. "Not the notebooks. Those are hers. Thirty years—"

"All of it," Emrys said. "That's what burning means."

He looked at his mother. She was watching the copper sheets where they lay in the rain-wet light, and her face was the face he remembered from the worst morning of his childhood — the morning she'd stood in the doorway with her pack already on and told him the dig needed her and he'd understand when he was older. Except that now, twelve years later, he could read what he hadn't been able to read then: that she wasn't looking at her life's work the way a scholar looks at a text. She was looking at it the way a woman looks at a fire she has decided to walk into.

"Do it," Ilsa said.

"I need you to say what it is you're agreeing to."

Her eyes came up to his. Rain ran off the overhang between them and the world beyond, a curtain of falling silver, and behind it the gunboat sat on the water with its patient smoke.

"You're asking me to burn thirty years," she said. "Every gate-chart, every flood-table, every tablet. The knowledge of how to command this river and every river like it. Knowledge that built a civilization and knowledge that, in the wrong hands—" she stopped. Started again, plainer. "In my hands it nearly got Sefa's village drowned twice over. Once by the ancients' gates, once by Corvo's greed. There is no safe owner for this, Emrys. I've known it since the day I understood what the tablets said. Faking my death was just the coward's version of what you're about to do."

"It's not cowardice to want to live."

"No," she said. "But it isn't courage either. This is."

Emrys struck the flint.

* * *

The pitch caught on the third stroke, and the fire went up quick and hungry, and he fed it the copper first.

The sheets resisted — hammered metal doesn't want to burn — but the waxed linen wrappings went up like torches and the heat came off the flames in waves that pushed the rain back, and when the copper finally began to discolor, to soften at the edges, to blister and slump in the fire like faces melting, Emrys fed the tablets in one at a time. Fired clay does not burn. But it cracks. He learned that within the first minute: the sharp reports as each tablet split in the heat, shards jumping off the stones, the etched diagrams bursting apart along lines no archaeologist would ever reassemble. Each crack sounded like a small gunshot. Each one, he thought, is a floodgate that will never be rebuilt.

Then the notebooks.

He saved those for last, and he did it himself, refusing Ilsa's offered hand when she reached toward the pile. Page by page. Her handwriting curling and going brown and lifting off the paper in flakes of ash that the updraft off the falls carried out over the gorge, over the water, gone. He watched the sketch of the sluice-chamber go. He watched the flood-tables go, the numbers that had measured the heartbeat of a dead river. He watched the little drawing of the boy on the dock go last of all, the paper browning around it, the ink fading, and he held that page a moment longer than the others — not to save it, but to look at it once more, the boy with his back to the water, waiting for a mother who had chosen the work — and then he laid it gently on the coals and watched it become nothing.

Something in his chest unclenched that he hadn't known was clenched. It hurt worse than the clenching had.

Below, the searching parties had stopped searching. Men stood on the shingle staring up. On the gunboat's deck, Corvo had gone very still, and even at four hundred feet Emrys could see the stillness of him, the absolute motionlessness of a man watching his fortune ascend as smoke.

Sefa laughed — one short, hard bark of a sound, half disbelief. "He's not even angry. Look at him. He's calculating."

"Let him calculate," Emrys said. His voice came out rough, scraped raw by smoke. "There's nothing left to count."

* * *

When the fire burned down to a bed of orange coals and twisted copper, Emrys took the last thing from the satchel: a single sheet of oilcloth, and inside it, wrapped in waxed linen, the one document he had added to the collection himself, three nights ago, by lamplight in the cave while the others slept.

Sefa's evidence. The financier's letters, the survey orders, the test-schedule for the old floodgates with her village's name written in the margin as a datum — *inundation trial, upper valley settlement.* Everything she had tracked for years. Everything that would bring Corvo's empire down not with a shot but with a subpoena, carried on currents slower than rivers and just as certain.

He had copied it all out in his own hand, twice, and burned the originals in the campfire that same night. One copy was sewn into the lining of Sefa's jacket, because she was the kind of person who kept things dry inside her jacket. The other lay in his hands now.

He weighed it. Considered, for exactly as long as it takes a man to weigh a thing he has already decided, dropping it into the fire too. Clean hands. Nothing left that anyone could want. Nothing left that anyone could kill for.

Then he stepped to the lip of the ledge, held the packet high so the man on the gunboat could see it — see it clearly, recognize it, understand — and hurled it out over the gorge, far from the fire, far from the falls, out over the trees on the far side where the cliff-path ran down toward the delta and the courts and the newspapers and the slow gray machinery of justice.

Corvo's men would find it, if they found it at all, after everyone in the valley had already read it.

On the water, for the first time, Corvo moved. Emrys watched him lean over the rail and shout an order, watched the smaller boats cast off in haste, watched the great patient machine of the man's patience finally, visibly, break — and felt nothing at all like triumph. It felt like setting down a weight he had carried so long he'd forgotten it was there, and discovering that underneath the weight there was simply a man, tired, smoking slightly, standing in the rain on a ledge above a waterfall with his mother and the woman he loved, and no debt in the world.

"The fire's dying," Ilsa said softly. "We should move. The cliff-path forks below the falls — his men will take the river road."

Emrys nodded. He looked back once at the coals, at the warped copper and the cracked clay and the fine grey ash already washing away in the drizzle, all that knowledge returned to the elements it had been dug out of, and he found that the grief was real but it was clean — grief without shame in it, for the first time in his life.

"Mother," he said, as she turned to go. "The boy on the dock. Did you ever draw anything else?"

She paused at the mouth of the cliff-path, rain beading in her grey hair, and something moved through her face that was older than apology and quieter than forgiveness.

"I drew a man," she said, "last week, in the dark, by lamplight. Standing in rising water, holding a door open so two other people could get through." She met his eyes. "I've been drawing you my whole life, Emrys. I just kept the pictures in the wrong notebook."

She went ahead onto the path. Sefa squeezed his shoulder once, hard, wordless, and followed.

Emrys stood alone on the ledge a moment longer, above the falls, above the fire's last smoke, above the gunboat now turning in frantic circles on the water. Downriver, somewhere past the bends, the monsoon was breaking over the whole valley at once — and the river was rising, and the way home was open, and for the first time in twelve years he had absolutely nothing to deliver.

He shouldered the empty satchel and started down.
