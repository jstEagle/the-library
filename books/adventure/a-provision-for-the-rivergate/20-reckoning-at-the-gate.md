## 20. Reckoning at the Gate

The fire had gone out hours ago, but Ilsa Vasquez still sat facing the blackened circle where the records had burned, as if the ash might rearrange itself into something she could read.

She had spent thirty years learning to read ruins. She could take a collapsed wall and reconstruct the house that had leaned against it; she could take a scatter of potsherds and tell you which hands had set them down, and whether those hands had been hurrying. She had never learned to read her son. That, she thought now, was the one excavation she had abandoned too early, and the one site that had gone on shifting beneath her the whole time.

The ledge above the falls was still wet from their escape. Their clothes hung on lines she had strung between two leaning monoliths of the old gatehouse — the Rivergate itself, the great carved throat where the valley's first engineers had taught a river to wait. Below her, the falls ran swollen and brown, and beyond them the gorge opened toward the lowlands, where Corvo's gunboat sat at anchor like a splinter in the world's skin. It had not moved since the burning. It would not move for a while yet. She had watched men stand on its deck all afternoon, small and aimless, waiting for orders that would never come, because the thing they had been sent to take no longer existed.

She had done that. Or her son had, with her sluice-gate and her flood and his match. She was no longer certain where her work ended and his began, and she suspected that uncertainty was the truest thing she had felt in twelve years.

Footsteps on wet stone. She knew the rhythm before she turned — Emrys had walked like his father, heel-first, a man perpetually braced for the ground to betray him. He came out of the cave mouth carrying two enamel mugs, and he did not offer her one so much as set it down on the stone beside her and sit, a full arm's length away, facing the falls the way she was.

They watched the water for a long time.

"You should sleep," he said finally.

"So should you."

"I will." He turned the mug in his hands without drinking. "I wanted to say — before Sefa wakes up, before anything else happens — that I'm glad you're alive. I need you to know that part came first. Before everything else I'm feeling."

Ilsa nodded slowly. "I know," she said. "I watched your face when I came out of the chamber. You looked like a man who'd found a body and a miracle in the same room and couldn't decide which one to bury."

A short, startled laugh escaped him — the first she had heard from him since he was a boy, and it hurt her more than the shouting would have. Then his face closed again, and she understood that the laugh had been a door opening onto a long corridor she would now have to walk down.

"Twelve years," he said. "I want to understand it. Not forgive it — I'm not there, and I don't know that I'm ever going to be there. But understand it. You owe me that much, and you've never been a woman who doesn't pay what she owes."

"No," she said quietly. "That was your father's disease. I only inherited it by marriage."

"Then pay it."

* * *

She made herself look at the falls while she spoke, because looking at him would have been a kindness to herself, and she did not trust herself to take it.

"When I left, I told you the work came first. I said it to your face, in that kitchen, with your bags already packed for your aunt's, and I have replayed that sentence every day for twelve years, and I have never once found a version of it that was true. The work did not come first. Fear came first." She turned the mug in her own hands now, mirroring him without meaning to. "I had seen the survey data. The waterway, the sluice architecture, the flood-cycle engineering — the whole valley, governed for four hundred years by people who understood water better than any government upstream understands it now. And I had seen who was paying for the dig. Corvo's money came through three shell foundations and a university endowment, and it took me two years to trace it, and when I did, I understood that I had spent my career digging a weapon out of the ground for a man who would use it. Control the floodgates, and you control every harvest, every town, every price, every life in this watershed. Not a discovery, Emrys. A lever. And I had handed him the handle."

"So you disappeared."

"So I disappeared." She said it flatly, refusing to soften it. "I faked the grave. I took the compass — your father's compass — because I needed something the searchers would believe I'd never abandon, and because I was a coward about it. I told myself it was tradecraft. It was a message to you. I knew you'd come looking eventually. I knew you'd recognize it. And I told myself that if you recognized it, you'd understand —" She stopped. Set the mug down. "That was the lie I lived in. That leaving you a riddle was the same as leaving you a reason."

"It wasn't," Emrys said. There was no heat in it, which was worse. "I got the riddle when I was nineteen. I spent twelve years solving it wrong. I thought you'd found something you loved more than me. I built my whole —" he gestured at himself, at the dockside-thinned face, the rope-scarred hands, "— I built all of this on that. Every bad choice had the same foundation. You taught me that people who say they love you will still choose the work. So I stopped expecting anyone to choose me, and I made choices that didn't require it."

Ilsa felt the words go into her like water into a crack in limestone — slow, silent, widening something that would not close again.

"I can't take that back," she said. "I want to be very clear, because I've watched you my whole life confuse my explanations for my excuses. There is no version of this where I say *I had to*. I chose. I chose the valley over the boy, and I chose the riddle over the goodbye, and I chose twelve years of silence over twelve years of letters that might have been intercepted. Some of those choices were defensible. None of them were free. You paid the freight on all of them, and you didn't agree to the shipment."

He was silent for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough.

"The grave. Did you dig it yourself?"

"I did. Two nights. I've never dug anything slower in my life." She almost smiled. "I put your father's compass on the stones and I sat there until dawn, and I thought, *he will hate me for this, and at least hating me will bring him here.* I needed you angry enough to travel and curious enough to read the ground. It's the only kind of letter I've ever known how to write."

"It nearly killed me. The river nearly killed me. Corvo nearly killed me — and not by accident, Mother. He gave me that contract because he wanted a bloodhound with your name on its collar. My debt was the leash. Every mile I rowed upstream, I was rowing for him."

"I know." She turned to face him fully now, and made herself hold his eyes. "I watched your boat come through the gorge in the storm. Sefa's boat behind yours. I was in the gatehouse with the long glass, and I saw Corvo's launch two miles behind you, and I understood exactly what I had done — that I had used my own disappearance as bait knowing what kind of fish it would draw, and that the bait was my son. If you had drowned in that gorge, it would have been my hand on the sluice-gate of your life, same as it was your grandfather's hand on your father's debts. I come from a long line of people who move water and call it fate."

"That's the first honest sentence I've ever heard you say about our family."

"It only took me sixty-one years."

* * *

The light changed. Down in the gorge, the monsoon clouds that had hung for days finally began to tear, and a blade of late sun came through and struck the falls, and the whole ravine filled with a moving, broken gold. Sefa had gone down to the boat an hour ago to check the hull and had not come back — deliberately, Ilsa suspected. The pilot had a genius for absence, for making room without making a ceremony of it.

Emrys stood, walked to the edge of the ledge, and looked down at the water. "I keep trying to find the thing I'm supposed to feel," he said. "You're alive. That's the miracle. And you're the reason my life went the shape it went. Both of those are true at the same time, and nobody warned me they could both be true at once."

"That's because it's the hardest thing there is," Ilsa said. "Harder than the dig. Harder than the caves. Holding two true things that can't be reconciled, and not letting either one lie to you." She rose and stood beside him, not touching him. "I'm not going to ask you to forgive me. Forgiveness that comes fast is just amnesia with better manners, and I don't want your amnesia. I want the thing that's harder. I want you to know me — all of it, the cowardice and the calculation and the one night I sat by a fake grave and wept because I'd finally built a monument to exactly the mother I was. And then I want you to decide, with all of that on the table, what you can carry and what you have to put down. That's the only reconciliation worth anything. The incomplete kind."

Emrys looked at her for a long time. The sun was in his eyes and she couldn't read them, and she realized she was afraid — truly afraid, more than in the flood, more than when Corvo's men had breached the lower chamber — of what a man she loved might decide about her when he finally had all the facts.

"You know what I keep coming back to?" he said. "The compass. You left it on the grave. Dad's compass. And all through this — the caves, the flood, the burning — I kept thinking, when this is over, I'm taking that compass back. It's mine. It's the last thing of his." He reached into his jacket and drew it out, the brass case dark with river water and cave damp, the needle trembling in its bowl. "But it doesn't point anywhere useful, does it. It never did. He carried it his whole life and it never pointed him toward paying what he owed. It just pointed. North, or whatever north it liked that day."

"And you?"

"I'm going to keep it." He closed his hand around it. "Not because it's his. Because it's yours too now — you carried it twelve years, and it kept you alive down here. It's the one thing in our family that's been lost and found twice." He looked at her then, and his eyes were wet and level. "I don't forgive you yet. Maybe not for a long time. But I'm not leaving the way you left. Whatever happens downriver — Corvo, the delta, all of it — you'll know where I am. You'll get letters. Real ones, with real words in them, no riddles. That's what I can give you today. It's not the whole thing. It's a provision. Enough to live on until the rest gets built."

Ilsa Vasquez had stood in the chamber of the second river and felt the awe of a dead civilization's genius, and it had not undone her. This undid her. She turned away, briefly, toward the falls, and when she turned back her face was wet and she did not wipe it.

"A provision," she repeated. "That's what the old engineers called it — you know that? The inscription in the lower chamber. *We leave this gate as a provision for those who come after.* They knew they wouldn't finish the work. They built enough for the next people to start from."

"Then I guess we come after," Emrys said.

Down in the gorge, a sound reached them — the gunboat's engine turning over, deep and grudging, the sound of a decision being made. They both looked. The vessel was coming about, its wake unspooling white behind it, pointing not upstream toward the falls but downriver, toward the delta, toward the ports where Sefa's copied ledgers were even now moving through hands that Corvo could not drown and could not burn.

"He's running," Ilsa said.

"No," Emrys said, and there was something in his voice she hadn't heard before — something that sounded, improbably, like his father on the good days, like a man who had finally stopped bracing. "He's retreating. There's a difference. Running is what you do when you're afraid. Retreating is what you do when the war's already lost, and you're just deciding which losses to carry." He slipped the compass into his pocket and picked up the two mugs. "Come on. Sefa will want to be on the water by first light, and she'll want us both rowing. The monsoon's breaking, and the river's going down — and everything Corvo built is going down with it."
