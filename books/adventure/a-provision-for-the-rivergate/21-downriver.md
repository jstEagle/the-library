## 21. Downriver

The monsoon broke on the ninth morning the way a fever breaks — all at once, leaving everything soaked and shaking and unsure it had survived.

Sefa felt it before she saw it. She was on watch at the mouth of the cave ledge, wrapped in the wool blanket Ilsa had produced from some impossible cache of expedition gear, when the rain simply stopped. Not thinned. Not eased. Stopped, like a hand lifted off the back of her neck. The silence that followed was so complete she could hear the river breathing — that low, muscular sound of water moving enormous weight through a narrow throat.

Above the falls, the clouds tore open along the eastern ridge, and for the first time in nine days the sun came down into the gorge like something spilled.

She stood there a long time, letting it hit her face, and thought about her grandmother's saying: *the river does not forgive, but it forgets faster than people do.* Her grandmother had been talking about grief. Sefa had spent eleven years learning that the river also forgot guilt, and debt, and the names of drowned villages. It was people who carried those things downstream, year after year, like silt in the blood.

Behind her, inside the overhang where they'd sheltered since the flood, Emrys was feeding a small fire with driftwood, and Ilsa Vasquez sat against the stone wall with the charred remains of her life's work still under her fingernails.

"Smoke's going to draw eyes," Sefa said, without turning around.

"There's no one left to draw," Emrys said. "Corvo pulled his men out yesterday. I watched the last boat go downriver at dawn."

"I watched them too." She finally turned. "I counted six boats. Corvo's gunboat wasn't one of them."

"He'll be waiting downstream," Ilsa said quietly. She hadn't said much since the burning. She sat like a woman whose house had burned down around her while she stood in the yard, watching, unable to decide whether she'd set the fire or the fire had simply found her. "He won't accept this. Not yet."

"No," Sefa agreed. "But he doesn't have us anymore either."

* * *

They left the ledge two hours later, riding a river that had swollen into something barely recognizable as the waterway Sefa had piloted since she was fourteen years old.

The longboat — patched twice, bailed hourly, named after no one — rode the brown torrent like a leaf with opinions. Sefa had the stern. Emrys had the bow pole, though poling was a joke in water this deep; mostly he used it to fend off the deadfall that came spinning down from the flooded tributaries, whole trees traveling end over end like things drowning. Ilsa sat amidships with the last of the survey notes wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, the way other women might hold an infant.

The river had changed its mind about everything. Channels that had been knee-deep in the dry season were now ten feet of fast water. Islands had vanished. A sandbar Sefa could have navigated blindfolded was somewhere beneath four meters of current, and the cutbank below the old trading post had eaten half the shoreline, taking three stilt-houses and the post's flagpole with it.

She read the water the way she always did — by the wrinkles on its skin, the V of a submerged snag, the smooth glassy tongue that meant deep channel, the boiling chaos that meant something huge and hidden just under the surface — but she read it humbly now. Nine days in a cave would do that. Nine days listening to the gorge roar like a thing being strangled.

"Hold!" Emrys shouted, and shoved the bow away from a floating mass of fencing — actual fence posts, wired together, torn out of some farm upstream that probably didn't exist anymore. They watched it tumble past in the wake.

"Whole valley's coming apart," he said.

"Whole valley's coming home," Sefa corrected. "This is what it does. We just usually aren't here to see it."

Ilsa spoke without lifting her head. "The Rivergate civilization saw it every year. That's the whole point of what they built. They didn't fight the flood. They *scheduled* it." There was something raw in her voice, something that kept catching on itself. "Eleven sluice gates. A calendar made of water. And we burned the only complete record of how it worked."

"We burned the only copy *Corvo* could get," Sefa said. "That's not the same thing."

"It isn't?"

"No." Sefa adjusted the sweep oar, easing them past a submerged temple roof — she recognized the carved naga from the dry-season maps, now a reef. "Your civilization built those gates and then the knowledge got lost anyway. Empires fall, paper burns, rivers move. What survives is what people carry in their heads." She nodded at Ilsa. "You've got it in your head. All of it. You told me so, the night in the cave, when you couldn't sleep."

Ilsa looked at her then, really looked, and Sefa held the gaze. She wasn't trying to comfort the woman. Comfort wasn't in her nature and wouldn't have been believed anyway. She was stating a fact, the way she'd state the depth of a channel: you are not empty, whatever you think you burned.

Emrys, at the bow, said nothing. But his shoulders came down half an inch, and Sefa had learned enough about him in the past weeks to know that was his version of agreement.

* * *

They ran the lower gorges on the second day, and it was the finest piloting of Sefa's life, though no one would ever know it and she would never tell anyone.

The river through the Karst country was a white-brown chaos, thirty feet above its normal bed, and the shortcut — her secret passage, the one she'd sold to Emrys for a price back when they were strangers — was now less a channel than a rumor. She took it anyway, because the main gorge had a logjam across it the size of a village, and because pride is a fuel that burns even when everything else is wet.

For six hours she steered by memory and instinct, calling depths to Emrys, who relayed them forward or back with a precision that surprised her every time. He'd been a dock rat and a debtor and a man running from his own name, but put him on moving water and he became someone else — someone who listened, someone who trusted the person holding the oar. She had not expected to trust him back. Trust had been the first casualty of her war with the financier, years ago, and she'd assumed it was gone for good, like her village's church bell, which the company had melted down for scrap.

But here they were, the three of them, alive in a boat that should have died nine times, and trust turned out to be like the river: it didn't forgive, but it could refill.

At dusk they shot the last rapid and came out onto the long middle reach, where the valley opened and the water spread and slowed, and the sunset laid a road of copper across it all the way to the horizon.

Sefa laughed. It startled her — a short, rusty sound, like an engine turning over after years in a shed.

"What?" Emrys asked.

"Nothing. My grandmother used to say the river gives you exactly one perfect evening per journey. Usually at the end. Usually right before it tries to kill you again."

"Cheerful woman."

"Drowned woman," Sefa said, and let the silence sit, because some silences were load-bearing.

* * *

The delta announced itself by smell before sight — mud and rot and woodsmoke and diesel, the great mingled breath of the lower river where a hundred channels braided apart and forgot they'd ever been one water.

They came down the main stem on the fourth morning, and Sefa saw the smoke before she saw the town: columns of it rising from the waterfront district, too many, too black, in a windless dawn.

"Fire?" Emrys asked, coming forward.

"No." She narrowed her eyes. "Fires burn *up*. Those are burning *down*. Someone's burning records."

They tied up at the fish market quay, well away from the commercial docks, and walked into a town that was holding its breath between two heartbeats. Sefa moved through the crowd with her hood up and her ears open, and what she heard, piece by piece, added up to something she had not dared to imagine in eleven years of hunting.

The customs house had been sealed by the provincial magistrate — *sealed*, not bought, an event without precedent in living memory. Two of Corvo's warehouse foremen had been arrested at the rail depot trying to leave with strongboxes. The harbor master had resigned by letter and fled by night, and his replacement had immediately impounded three of Corvo's barges pending investigation. And everywhere, in the market stalls and the tea houses and the queue outside the magistrate's office, people were talking about a ledger.

"A ledger," Emrys repeated, when Sefa reported back. He looked at her. "*Your* ledger."

"My mother's ledger, originally. Mine after." She shrugged, uncomfortable under his stare. "Shipping manifests. Bribes. Dates and amounts and the names of officials on the take. And the floodgate tests — the schedules, the engineers' reports, the correspondence authorizing the release upstream." She had copied it three times over the years and hidden the copies in three places, and had never once believed any of them would matter. Evidence, she'd learned young, was a weapon that only fired if someone was willing to stand in front of it and pull. For eleven years no one had been willing. The magistrate had been Corvo's. The press had been Corvo's. The police had been Corvo's.

"So what changed?"

"The flood changed." She said it slowly, realizing it as she said it. "The monsoon broke the levee at Kessarin. Forty villages underwater. And when the company's own engineers came to assess the damage, their report — the real one, the internal one — leaked. Because the numbers didn't lie. The levee failed at stresses far below what it should have survived, and the only explanation was that the floodgates upstream had been opened during peak flow. Deliberately. As a test." She smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "Corvo tested my village's death on a larger scale, and this time the evidence floated downstream where everyone could read it."

Ilsa had gone very still. "The old gates. The ones your family's village sat beneath."

"The same engineering principle," Sefa said. "He wanted to prove the Rivergate method could control the whole valley's water. Control it, and sell it. Who gets flooded and who stays dry — that's power, Dr. Vasquez. You knew that. It's why you faked your own grave."

"Yes," Ilsa said. "It's why."

"And now everyone knows. The magistrate can't bury it, because forty villages are angry and the water hasn't even receded yet. Corvo's money can't buy that many funerals." Sefa looked out at the smoking waterfront, at the black ash drifting down over the fish market like dirty snow. "Justice arrives sideways. That's the only way it ever arrives. Carried on the current, months late, hitting whatever happens to be in its path."

Emrys was quiet for a moment. "My debt," he said. "If Corvo falls—"

"Your debt dies with his ability to collect it," Sefa said. "Debts are only as real as the muscle behind them. You taught me that, actually. Or your father's contract did."

Something moved across his face — relief and loss tangled together, the strange grief of a burden lifting that had also been holding you upright. She recognized it. She'd worn it herself, briefly, the day she'd realized her revenge might actually happen and discovered she didn't know who she was without it.

* * *

They heard the gunboat before they saw it — the deep diesel knock echoing up the braided channels, unmistakable.

Sefa had the boat pushed off and hidden in a channel of dead reeds within four minutes, and she was proud of that too, later. They crouched among the stalks and watched Corvo's vessel come down the main stem, riding high and fast, too fast for a river full of debris. Armed men lined the rails. On the upper deck, alone, stood a heavyset figure in a rain-dark coat, motionless, facing downriver.

Corvo. In the flesh, for the first time. Sefa studied him with the dispassion of a pilot reading a hazard: rock that looks small until you hit it.

He did not look at the reeds. He did not look at anything. He stood like a man watching his own reflection in the water and not liking the view, and the gunboat thundered past their hiding place close enough that its wake rocked them against the mud, and then it was gone down the southern channel, toward the sea, toward wherever men like him went when the architecture of their power started shedding pieces.

"He knows," Ilsa murmured. "About the ledger. About the magistrate."

"He knows," Sefa agreed. "And he's going to fight. Men like that don't drown quietly. But he's fighting the river now, and the river—" she paused, and found she was smiling for real, "—the river forgets nothing forever either. It just holds things a long time and lets them go all at once."

* * *

That evening, in a room above a noodle shop that smelled of chili oil and river damp, the three of them ate together for the first time since the caves, and Sefa understood that something had quietly finished.

Not healed. Finished — the way a voyage finishes, with loose ends and salt damage and a crew that will never quite be strangers again but needn't be anything more than what they are.

Ilsa talked. That was the change. Since the burning she had been a closed door, and tonight, over cheap bowls and cheaper beer, she opened — haltingly, without apology, which Sefa respected — about the dig, the discovery, the years of decisions that had led her to stage her own death with her husband's compass on a false grave. She talked about the engineering of the second river the way she must have talked to students once, before ambition calcified into secrecy, and her eyes lit in a way that made her look twenty years younger and entirely untrustworthy, and entirely worth trusting anyway.

Emrys listened. He didn't forgive her — Sefa could see the difference, could see the wound still open under his careful neutrality — but he stayed in the room, and staying was its own kind of answer.

And Sefa sat with her back to the wall, as always, and drank her beer, and felt the strangest thing: lightness. Eleven years of tracking, copying, hiding, hating — and the hate hadn't even been the thing that did it. The flood had done it. The dead civilization's own genius, misused, had testified against its thief. Justice arrived sideways, carried on the current, and all she had done was keep rowing toward it.

Her grandmother would have laughed at her. Then poured her another drink.

Outside, the rain began again — soft now, ordinary, the delta's nightly drizzle instead of the sky falling. Down at the quay, the river ran high and brown and endless, carrying silt and branches and the ashes of somebody's burned ledgers out toward the sea, where all rivers deliver their accounts eventually.

Sefa raised her bottle an inch toward the window, toward the water.

"To sideways," she said.

Emrys glanced over. "To what?"

"You'll understand tomorrow." She set the bottle down. "Because tomorrow we're going to talk about what comes next. You, me, that patched-up leak bucket of yours. There's honest freight to run now that the company's barges are impounded, and I know every channel from the delta to the Karst, and you—" she pointed at him with the neck of the bottle, "—owe me a boat. You've been borrowing mine for weeks."

"That was *my* boat originally."

"It was Corvo's boat, which makes it stolen goods, which makes it mine by salvage rights. Ask any pilot." She leaned back, and let the grin come, the first easy one in eleven years. "Besides. The river gave us our one perfect evening already. Which means it's done killing us for this trip."

From the corner, Ilsa laughed — a short, surprised sound, as if the laugh had escaped custody — and outside, the current ran on through the dark, patient and impartial, carrying everything downstream: the ash, the evidence, the names of the drowned, and the three survivors of the Rivergate, into whatever the delta decided to make of them next.
