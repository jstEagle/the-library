## 22. A Provision for the Rivergate

The boat came out of the water on a Tuesday, though Emrys only knew it was Tuesday because Sefa told him so, and she only knew because the customs man at the delta yard kept a calendar pinned above his scale and read the dates aloud to no one in particular. Three weeks since the flood had broken. Three weeks since the fire on the ledge, since Corvo's gunboat had turned downstream with its holds empty and its captain's face like a man who had watched his fortune curl into ash and blow away over the gorge.

The longboat sat on trestles now, patched hull exposed to the sun, and Emrys stood beneath it with a scraper in one hand and tar in the other, working the seams the way he'd learned to work them alone, weeks ago, when refusing help had been the only thing he owned.

"You missed a spot," Sefa said, from her position of maximum comfort on an upturned crate, peeling an orange with a knife that had seen honest work.

"I didn't."

"You did. Port side, third plank from the stern. The same spot you missed the first time, which means it isn't missing, it's deliberate. You're sentimental about your flaws."

He scraped the spot anyway. It needed scraping. He'd known it needed scraping when he walked past it that morning, and he'd walked past it anyway because some part of him still believed that if he left a flaw visible, the river would forgive him for the ones he couldn't see. That was the kind of thinking three weeks of dry land encouraged. On the water you fixed what was broken or you drowned, and there was no room for philosophy between those two options.

The yard smelled of creosote and drying nets and the sweet rot of the delta's edge, where the river gave up its silt and became a hundred brown fingers reaching for the sea. Egrets stalked the mudflats beyond the fence. Somewhere behind the sheds, a radio played a station that faded in and out with the wind, half a love song, half static — the same voice the contract radio had given him every night upstream, except now the static sounded like weather instead of warning.

"Customs cleared us," Sefa said. She flicked a segment of orange peel into the sawdust. "Trade license under both names. I put yours first."

"Why?"

"Alphabetical." She smiled around another segment. "Also because when they ask who owns the boat, I want to watch your face."

"It's your boat. You found her."

"I found her leaking. You made her float. In my family we have a saying about that, but it doesn't translate, so you'll just have to take my word that it's generous."

Her family. She said it more easily now than she had a month ago, when the word had been a wound she carried wrapped in ledger paper — the village upstream, the drowned fields, the financier's name at the bottom of every document she'd spent six years collecting. That name belonged to Corvo's network too, and Corvo's network was coming apart the way rotten pilings come apart: slowly, then all at once, then slowly again while men in clean suits pretended they'd never touched the water. Sefa's evidence had gone to a magistrate in the provincial capital, and from there into newspapers, and from there into the mouths of officials who discovered, with impressive speed, that they had always found the man distasteful.

Justice arrived sideways. Emrys had learned that much. It came on the current, imperfect, late, carrying less than you'd hoped and more than you'd expected, and you took it or you didn't, but either way the river moved on.

* * *

Ilsa came to the yard on Thursday, riding the mail launch down from the ruins, wearing field clothes that had been mended so many times they were more thread than cloth, and carrying a crate of notebooks under one arm like a woman carrying bread.

She looked older than twelve years should have made her. Or perhaps she looked exactly as old as twelve years should have made her, and Emrys had simply spent those years imagining her frozen at the age she'd been when she walked out — young enough to be blamed, old enough to know better. Now she was sixty-one and gray at the temples and she squinted in the delta light like someone relearning how to be looked at.

"Openly," she said, setting the crate on the trestle beside the boat. "That's the word I keep using. Openly. It feels strange in my mouth. Like a coin from a country that no longer exists."

"How's the site?"

"Drowned, mostly. The lower chambers flooded when the sluices opened and stayed open. The upper galleries are intact. Enough to document. Enough to prove the Rivergate people engineered their own floods — controlled them, timed them, fed two valleys with one river." She said it without triumph. A month ago, saying it would have cost her everything; she'd have said it in a whisper, in a cave, to no one. Now she said it on a public dock to her son and a river pilot and anyone within earshot, and the saying of it was the point. "There's a university team coming up next month. Real archaeologists. Slow ones. They'll take ten years to do what I did in five, and the valley will be better for it."

"And you'll stay up there."

"For as long as it takes." She looked at the boat — at the scraped planks, the fresh tar, the new name Sefa had painted on the bow in letters that managed to be both careful and irreverent. *Provision*. "You named her after the dig."

"After the contract," Emrys said. "It was the only honest word on the page."

Sefa snorted. Ilsa almost smiled, caught herself, and let the almost stand, which was its own kind of honesty. They had not yet learned how to be easy with each other. Perhaps they never would. But on the ledge above the falls, after the records burned and Corvo's silhouette withdrew across the gorge, Ilsa had said, *I don't ask you to forgive me*, and Emrys had said, *I know*, and somehow that exchange had held more weight than any apology could have. Forgiveness, when it finally came — arriving in pieces, in shared work, in the willingness to sit across a table — forgave nothing and acknowledged everything, and that was the only version either of them trusted.

"I brought something," Ilsa said.

"You brought forty kilos of notebooks."

"Besides." She reached into her coat and set it on the trestle between them, small and brass-bright in the sun. His father's compass. The one she'd taken when she left. The one that had marked the false grave at the ransacked camp, planted there like a message meant for exactly one reader — *look closer, look inland, look for me* — and which Emrys had dug out of stones that were pretending to be bones, and carried ever since through karst and flood and fire.

"I can't take it back," Ilsa said. "What it meant when I used it. What it cost you to find it there." Her hands were steady; her voice was not quite. "But it was never mine to bury. And it shouldn't stay with me. It points wherever it's pointed. That was always the trouble with your father — he thought it pointed somewhere. It just points."

Emrys picked it up. The brass was warm from her coat. The needle swung, settled, swung again, indifferent to the delta, to the yard, to the whole geography of everything they'd survived. He thought about the boy he'd been at seventeen, watching his mother's boat disappear downriver with this compass in her pocket, and how he'd spent twelve years believing the direction she'd chosen was away from him, when in truth she'd been running toward something she thought would devour the world unless she stood in front of it. She'd been wrong about how to do it. She'd been right about the world.

"Thank you," he said, and found that he meant all of it: the compass, the return, the terrible years, the fact of her standing here in mended clothes with drowned discoveries and a clear conscience she hadn't earned but had, at least, paid for.

Ilsa nodded once, the way she nodded over a find that confirmed a theory — not celebration, just the quiet click of something true settling into place.

* * *

They launched on a morning thick with mist, the kind the delta made when the river exhaled overnight and the sea breathed back in. The Provision sat low and honest in the water, loaded with rice and salt and lamp oil and bolts of dyed cloth — cargo that would be declared, taxed, sold, argued over, and delivered, none of it sealed, none of it secret, none of it worth a single bullet anywhere along four hundred kilometers of brown water.

Emrys took the tiller. Sefa took the pole and worked the shallows off the dock with the economy of someone who had been born knowing where a river kept its bones. Ilsa stood on the dock with her crate of notebooks and did not wave, because waving was not in either of their vocabularies; she raised one hand to shoulder height, flat, like a surveyor marking a benchmark, and held it there until the mist took her.

"She'll write," Sefa said.

"She'll document."

"Same thing, for her." Sefa shipped the pole and dropped onto the thwart beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, which was, by the current state of negotiations between them, roughly equivalent to a declaration. "First run is salt to the middle villages, cloth to the landing at Kesh, then back heavy with dried fish. Nothing about it will be interesting."

"Nothing about it will be interesting," Emrys agreed, and heard in his own voice something he hadn't heard there in years — maybe ever. Not relief exactly. Relief implied a threat recently ended. This was quieter. This was the sound a man made when he stopped bracing.

They ran the delta channels as the mist burned off, past stilt houses and net racks and children diving off pilings who shouted at the boat and were answered by Sefa in a dialect Emrys was beginning to pick apart, syllable by syllable, the way he'd once picked apart strata. The river was high with the monsoon's leavings but falling, day by day, revealing sandbars and drowned fences and the tops of submerged trees like the fingers of a hand letting go. By the dry season the channels would shift entirely. The charts would be wrong. Everyone would buy new ones, or hire pilots who carried the real maps in their feet.

At midday they tied up at a fueling stage and ate rice and smoked fish in the shade, and Emrys took out the compass and set it on the boards between them and watched the needle hunt.

"North," Sefa said. "Mostly. There's iron in the engine block, it pulls a few degrees east."

"There's iron in everything."

"Mm." She leaned against him, deliberately, the way she did everything. "So where does it point? For you. Genuinely. If a man could choose."

He turned the compass in his hand. The needle swung through its small arcs and refused to commit, trembling between north and the pull of the engine, pointing nowhere and therefore everywhere, and for the first time in his life Emrys Vasquez found that he wasn't looking for a reading. His father had died owing money. His mother had lived owing truth. He himself had come up this river owing Corvo, and had paid that debt in the only currency that had ever actually been on the table — a fire on a ledge, a life's work turned to smoke, a choice made with his eyes open. The ledger was blank. Blank pages frightened most people. He found he liked the weight of one.

"Downstream today," he said. "Upstream tomorrow. Wherever the cargo goes."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer the boat understands."

Sefa laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, rare enough that he catalogued it — and reached over and closed the compass lid with a snap, and the needle's indecision disappeared into brass and darkness, patient, waiting, asking nothing.

They cast off into the afternoon current. Behind them the delta opened its green arms; ahead the river ran wide and brown and certain, carrying its silt to the sea the way it had carried everything else — the dead civilization, the living lies, the burned records, the two of them — without judgment, without memory it admitted to, keeping only what it kept.

Emrys steered. The Provision answered her helm like something newly born and already sure of the water. And if, far upriver, at the mouth of a cave behind the falls, the old sluice gates stood open forever, feeding the valleys on a schedule set by people ten centuries dead — well. The river remembered. That was enough. It didn't need him to remember too.

He left the compass in his coat pocket, lid shut, and let the current decide.
