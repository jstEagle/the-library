## 1. The Mood of the Water

The fog on the lower Río Cobre comes up off the water like a lie told slowly, and if you're not careful you start believing it — that the river is empty, that nobody's waiting for you, that the world ends politely at the edge of your own bow wake.

I'd been on the Marisol since three in the morning, running dark with only the pilot lamp lit, because Port Salinas sits at the bottom of the river like a stone in a boot, and I wanted to be well past it before the stone woke up.

"Sandbar," I said to no one, because there was no one to say it to, and swung the wheel two points to starboard. The Marisol leaned into it like an old woman changing her mind mid-sentence, and we slid past the bar by a boat-length and a half. I knew it was there the way you know a bruise is there — not because you can see it but because you've run into it before, more than once, and never learned anything except where it lives.

Nine years on this river. Nine years of dawn fogs and midnight shallows and cargo manifests written by men who thought "miscellaneous" was a unit of measurement. You'd think a woman would get tired of it. You'd think she'd want walls, a garden, a chair that faced something other than whatever direction the current happened to be going.

You'd think wrong.

The truth is I love the river the way you love a person who never asks you questions. It doesn't want to know why I left Port Salinas. It doesn't send letters. It doesn't say *Juniper, mija, when are you coming home* in a voice that makes home sound like a wound instead of a place. The river just asks me to read it, every single morning, fresh, like it's testing whether I've gotten lazy or stupid or dead overnight. And every morning I pass the test, and that's the closest thing to being loved that I've been able to afford.

The fog thickened around the bend below the old customs house. I throttled back until the engine dropped from its working growl to something like a cat's purr, and I listened. That's the trick nobody teaches you about the Cobre: your eyes are nearly useless before sunup, so you steer with your ears. Water moving over a gravel shoal sounds like applause from a very small, very bored audience. Water sliding off a cut bank sounds like silk tearing. And water backing up behind a snag sounds like nothing at all, which is the most dangerous sound on the river, silence being the noise dead things make.

There. Off the port side, faint as a whisper through a wall — gravel hiss. The winter floods had moved the shoal again. Last season it hugged the north bank; now it had crept out into the channel like a drunk inching toward the door. I eased the wheel over and felt the current take the stern and swing us, and for one long breath the Marisol and I were dancing with something neither of us could see, and then the hiss fell away astern and we were through.

"Still got it," I told the fog.

The fog did not disagree. The fog was too polite.

* * *

Port Salinas announced itself the way it always did: first the smell — woodsmoke and fish guts and the particular sweetness of cane rum going bad in the sun — then the church bell tolling six, then the dogs, who consider it their civic duty to bark at anything that moves, including other dogs, including themselves.

I'd planned to slip past on the far channel. I had no business in Salinas. My business, such as it was, lay forty miles upstream at the mouth of the Cañada Verde, where a man named Farrow had promised actual coin — coin, not promises, which on this river put him in rare company — for carrying supplies up to some survey expedition camped above the Gorge of Whistling Stones. Cartographers, he'd said. Mapmakers. Harmless as priests.

But the fog had other ideas. It sat down on Salinas like a hen on eggs, thick and warm and determined, and the far channel was narrow enough that threading it blind would be less piloting and more prayer, and I've found prayer works better as ballast than as navigation. So I did what any sensible captain does when the river takes away her options.

I went into the port anyway, slow, quiet, and mean about it.

The Marisol is sixty feet of pre-war riverboat with a paddlewheel that claps like it's applauding itself and a hull patched in eleven places, each patch representing either a lesson learned or a lesson refused. I brought her alongside the fuel dock rather than the main quay, because the fuel dock's keeper, old Beto, owes me favors the way other people owe money, and because the main quay is where debt collectors stand.

And there he was anyway. Of course there he was.

He was leaning against a bollard with the studied casualness of a man who has been leaning against that bollard since four in the morning, wearing a gray suit that gray suits the climate about as well as wool suits a fish. He had a leather folder under one arm and a smile already loaded, and when our eyes met across fifty feet of fog-thinned air, he tipped his hat like we were old friends at a funeral.

I considered, briefly, backing out and taking my chances with the far channel after all.

Too late. He pushed off the bollard and strolled down the dock with the unhurried gait of a man whose salary doesn't depend on hurrying.

"Captain Villalobos." His voice carried beautifully. Everything about him carried beautifully; I suspected he practiced in front of a mirror. "What a coincidence."

"Is it?" I kept coiling the mooring line, keeping my hands busy and my face bored. "Because from here it looks like a man standing on a dock at dawn holding paperwork. Coincidences don't usually come with folders."

"Señor Ochoa sends his regards." He set the folder on my gunwale without asking, the way a cat sets a dead bird on your doorstep. "He wonders if you've given further thought to the matter of your brother's account."

"My brother's account," I said, "is my brother's account."

"It is. Which is precisely the problem. A debt of that size, accruing interest for nine years—" he made a small sad sound, a connoisseur admiring a vintage, "—it becomes less a number and more a weather system. It affects everything around it. Property. Vessels." His gaze slid up the Marisol's rail with the delicacy of a hand checking pockets. "Even boats, Captain. Even beautiful old boats."

I finished the coil, hung it on its peg, and turned to look at him properly. He was younger than his suit, thirty maybe, with soft hands and the kind of patience that gets paid hourly. Behind him, Beto had materialized at the fuel pump, pretending to check a gauge that hadn't worked since the war, listening with both ears.

"What's your name?"

"Iparraguirre."

"That's a lot of name for what you do."

Something flickered behind his politeness — annoyance, maybe, or respect, hard to tell at this hour. "Captain, I'm not the enemy. Señor Ochoa isn't even the enemy. Interest is the enemy. Time is the enemy. All I'm offering is arithmetic." He tapped the folder. "A payment plan. A modest one. Or—" and here came the smile again, freshly oiled, "—you could continue as you have been, running upriver every time you see a gray suit, and in five years this conversation happens again, except the folder will be thicker and the man holding it will be less pleasant. They rotate us, you know. I'm the nice one."

"The nice one," I repeated.

"I've been told."

I picked up the folder. It was heavy the way all bad news is heavy, dense with clauses and carbon copies and a signature line waiting at the bottom like an open trap. I didn't open it. Opening it would be a negotiation, and negotiations require standing still, and standing still is the one thing I've trained myself out of for nine years running.

"Here's my counteroffer," I said. "I take on a cargo today, upstream. I get paid in money, which I understand is the traditional medium. And when I have enough of it, I'll come back down this river and settle my brother's account in full, in cash, and you can retire on the commission."

Iparraguirre sighed like a schoolteacher. "And when will that be?"

"When the river pays me what it owes."

"The river," he said gently, "doesn't owe anybody anything. That's rather the point of rivers."

For a moment — just a moment — I almost liked him.

"Beto!" I called. "Two hundred liters, and put it on the slate next to the time he fixed my injector pump for free."

Beto spat expertly over the side. "Slate's full, Captain."

"Then start a second slate."

Iparraguirre watched this transaction with the expression of a man doing sums he didn't enjoy. Then he straightened his hat. "The offer stands, Captain Villalobos. Señor Ochoa is not an unreasonable man. But he is a patient one, and patience upstream is cheaper than patience down here." He stepped back from the gunwale. "Enjoy the fog."

He walked away up the dock, unhurried, folderless — I looked down and realized he'd left it. Of course he'd left it. The folder was the message; carrying it back would just be clutter.

I picked it up, weighed it once in my hand, and pitched it into the river. It floated for a surprisingly long time, turning slowly in the eddy behind the wheel, gray paper on gray water, before the current took it somewhere downstream where debts go to drown.

Beto shuffled over while I paid for the fuel. "That's the third one this year," he said. "They're getting younger."

"They're getting cheaper. That last one at least pretended to read the contract before quoting it."

"You could settle it, you know." Beto kept his eyes on the meter, which was the kindest way he knew how to say things. "One good season. Take the right cargo—"

"The right cargo costs more than it pays. It always does. That's how they build the trap — they leave the cheese sitting right in the middle of it and call it opportunity."

"Mm." Beto finished, wiped his hands on a rag older than both of us, and looked at me with those river-flat eyes that miss nothing. "You know what I notice about you, Juniper? Every year your boat gets a little better and your plans get a little shorter. Most people it's the other way around."

"Most people aren't me."

"No," he agreed. "Thank God for that."

* * *

Farrow's agent met me at the Cañada Verde mouth with crates stacked on a cart and a manifest that smelled of fresh ink and fresh lies. I counted boxes while he counted my patience, and we both finished around the same time.

"Survey supplies," he said. "Instruments, provisions, spare parts. The expedition's above the gorge. Thorne's the name — Elias Thorne. Big expedition. Well financed."

"Well financed" is a phrase I trust about as far as I can throw a piano, but the advance was real, in my palm, in bills I could feel, and feeling is believing. I signed where he pointed.

It wasn't until the deckhands — meaning me, meaning my own two arms and a hand-crank winch that predates the concept of mercy — had hauled the last crate aboard that I noticed the stenciling. Under the shipping marks, half-scrubbed but legible in the dawn light that had finally burned through the fog:

**C.M.C. — CONSOLIDADA MINERA DEL COBRE — SURVEY DIVISION**

Mining survey. Not mapping.

I stood there with my hand flat on the crate, feeling the grain of the wood, thinking about a man named Thorne who hired boats through intermediaries and paid in real money for cargo labeled with somebody else's name. Thinking about the Gorge of Whistling Stones, which I had run exactly twice in nine years and both times with my heart in my teeth and my will updated.

Thinking, mostly, about how the river had seemed so friendly this morning, and how it always does that right before it asks something of you.

Behind me, deep in the shadow between the galley bulkhead and the stack of crates, something shifted. Small. Quick. The sound a body makes when it's been holding very still for a very long time and has just lost the argument with its own bladder or its own nerves.

I didn't turn around. I finished lashing the last crate, checked the lashings twice, and walked aft to the wheel with the unhurried stride of a captain who has noticed absolutely nothing at all.

Let them think they're hidden. Stowaways always travel better believing their own story.

I rang up half ahead, and the Marisol clapped her wheel against the current and turned her nose upriver, toward the gorge, toward the mountains just beginning to show their white teeth above the morning haze.

Whatever was hiding in my cargo hold had picked the right boat. Running away from something was, after all, the one cargo I specialized in carrying.
