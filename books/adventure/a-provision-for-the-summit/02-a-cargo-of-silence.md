## 2. A Cargo of Silence

The man who hired me in Port Salinas had a handshake like a wet rope and eyes that never quite landed on anything you'd want them to land on — your eyes, your hands, the cash box bolted under the Marisol's wheel. He said his name was Ferrer, which I doubted, and that the cargo was survey equipment, which I doubted harder.

"Forty crates," he said, "upriver to the Gorge basecamp. Paid half now, half on delivery."

"Paid in coin," I said, "not promises. I've been paid in promises before. They don't burn well."

He laughed like I'd made a joke instead of a financial policy, and counted out silver on the barrel head by the gangplank while his men loaded my deck with crates that were heavier than survey equipment has any right to be. I've hauled dredging gear. I know what forty pounds of ambition looks like strapped to a pallet. These crates came down the dock on the shoulders of two men each, and the dock boards groaned under them like they were confessing something.

I didn't say anything. That's the first rule of river work: you can wonder all you want as long as your mouth stays shut and your boat stays moving. The second rule is that wondering is free, so use it.

* * *

We cast off before noon with the fog burned off and the Cobre running green-gold under a sky the color of an old shirt. Forty crates lashed amidships under canvas, one mule of a current ahead of us, and me at the wheel doing what I do best, which is pretending the river and I are old friends when really we're old adversaries who've agreed to a long truce.

The lower Río Cobre in the dry season is a liar's river. It looks slow and generous, wide as a plaza, and then it puts a sandbar exactly where last week there was deep water and dares you to argue. You read it by the water's mood — the little dimples where current stacks over a shoal, the smooth glassy tongue where it runs fast and deep, the way birds stand on a bar like they're holding a meeting about you. Nine years of this. My hands knew the wheel the way a drunk knows a bottle: intimately, and with consequences.

Tomás appeared sometime after the second bend.

I didn't see him arrive because he didn't arrive; he was simply *there*, the way damp gets into a hold. One moment I was alone with the wheel and my thoughts, and the next there was a boy sitting cross-legged on top of crate seventeen, eating a hard biscuit from my galley tin with the concentration of a scholar translating scripture.

He was maybe eleven. Small for it. Dark-haired, river-tan, with the kind of stillness you almost never see in children — children are usually weather, all motion and noise, but this one was a held breath. His clothes were good once. His shoes had been repaired by someone who loved him and then again by someone who didn't.

"That's my biscuit," I said.

He looked at me. He did not stop chewing. There was a whole negotiation in that look, and I lost it, which was becoming a theme.

"You stowed aboard at Salinas," I said. "Under the canvas."

He swallowed. Nodded, once, like a man confirming charges he considered beneath rebuttal.

"Forty crates," I said, "and you make forty-one. I'm not running a ferry for strays."

He said nothing. He just kept looking at me with those dark, level eyes, and I recognized the look, is the thing. I'd spent nine years practicing it in every mirror between here and the delta. It's the look of someone who has already calculated the cost of being put ashore and decided they'll pay it if they have to, but they'd rather not, and they will not beg, because begging costs more than walking.

I know that arithmetic. I invented it.

"Eat the biscuit," I said, turning back to the wheel. "Touch my charts and I'll feed you to the catfish."

* * *

By mid-afternoon I'd learned three things about Tomás. First, he could work — I set him to bailing the bilge as punishment and he bailed like he was being paid by the bucket, silent and thorough, no complaint, no questions. Second, he didn't talk. Not couldn't — I caught him mouthing words to himself once, checking them like coins — but wouldn't, as though speech were a door he'd decided to keep shut until he knew what was on the other side of it. Third, he watched the crates.

Not idly. When he thought I wasn't looking, he'd drift along the lashings and lay his palm flat against the canvas over crate twelve, or twenty-three, the way you'd check whether a sleeping animal was breathing. And once — only once — I saw him press his ear against the wood.

That evening, anchored in the lee of a gravel point with the fire going and beans on, I opened crate six. Officially to inventory the manifest. Unofficially because unease is a debt too, and I pay mine early.

Ferrer's manifest said *survey instruments, cartographic supplies*. What came out of the straw packing was a theodolite, true enough, fine brass and glass — but under it, wrapped in oilcloth, a dozen core-drill bits, carbide-tipped, and a case of blasting caps packed with the casual optimism of a man who has never met a mistake.

Cartographers don't carry blasting caps. Mapmakers don't need to take the mountain apart to draw it.

I sat on my stern deck with the drill bit in my hand, turning it in the last light, and let the unease finish hardening into something with edges. A mining consortium had dressed itself up as a mapping expedition and shipped its teeth upstream under my deck. Whatever Elias Thorne and his people were doing above the Gorge of Whistling Stones, somebody wanted holes punched in the earth to go with the pretty lines on paper. And somebody had paid me — paid *me*, Juniper Villalobos, whose name was starting to travel downriver attached to words like *reliable* and *discreet* — to haul the teeth without asking whose mouth they belonged to.

I should have turned around. Let me be clear about that. Any sensible captain would have run the Marisol back to Salinas, dumped the crates on Ferrer's wet-rope handshake, and eaten the loss. Sensible captains die poor and old in their beds, though, and I had a debt-shaped hole in my life that coin could fill, and half the payment was already in my strongbox, and the other half waited at basecamp like bait waits on a hook.

Besides, I told myself, hauling isn't approving. The river doesn't ask what's in the hull. It only asks if you can float it.

The river and I have always been alike that way. It's how we justify ourselves.

* * *

Second day upriver, the valley narrowed and the current got opinions. I worked the wheel through the morning rapids with sweat running down my spine and Tomás standing at the bow rail — I hadn't ordered him there, he'd just installed himself, and pointed out rocks with small economical gestures, left hand for port, right for starboard, flat palm for *slow down, woman, there's a ledge*.

He was good. That was the unsettling part. Better than good — he read water like I did, by mood, by rumor, by the flick of a current's tail. Twice he called hazards I hadn't seen yet, and both times he was right, and both times he pretended afterward that he hadn't done anything, retreating into his silence like a hermit crab into a borrowed shell.

"Where'd you learn that?" I asked him over the engine's clatter, after we shot the Narrows with a hand's width to spare on either side.

A shrug. Then, grudgingly, the first full sentence he'd given me: "My father ran barges. Above the Gorge."

Above the Gorge. Where the expedition had gone quiet. Where I was taking forty crates of disguised dynamite ambitions. I filed that away in the drawer where I keep things I'm not ready to think about, and the drawer stuck, the way it does.

That night, anchored below the last settlement before the wild water — a flyspeck of a place called Piedra Alta, all dogs and drying nets — I checked the crates again. More carefully this time, by lamplight, with Tomás watching from the shadows of the deckhouse like a small disapproving ghost.

Crate twelve was different. Lighter than its neighbors, and the packing inside was wrong — straw over empty space, straw laid *over* something, not around it. I dug down and my fingers found canvas, and under the canvas, curled up with his knees to his chin and his eyes open and fixed on mine, was a boy.

Not Tomás. Tomás was standing behind me. This was a second boy.

For one stupid heartbeat I thought I was seeing double — some trick of lamplight, some exhaustion mirage. But no. Two boys. Nearly identical, in fact, close enough in face to be brothers, except this one was thinner, and his wrists had marks on them, rope marks, healing ugly, and when I pulled the canvas back he flinched so hard he cracked his head on the crate lid, and Tomás was suddenly there, past me, kneeling, saying his name — his *only* word in two days, and it came out cracked down the middle:

"Luz—"

Except it wasn't Luz, because Luz, whatever else she was, was apparently a girl, and this was a boy, and Tomás caught himself on the name like a man grabbing a rail that isn't there. The boy in the crate stared up at me with the same level dark eyes Tomás had, and I understood, all at once, the way you understand a sandbar — too late, all at once, with the keel already kissing it.

These weren't stowaways. Stowaways hide in boats. These two had been hidden in *cargo*, in a crate bound for a mining survey dressed as a mapping expedition, and the rope marks said they hadn't climbed in willingly, and the silence Tomás wore wasn't temperament. It was a survival strategy. It was what you learn when talking has been dangerous for longer than you can remember.

"It's all right," I heard myself say, in a voice I barely recognized — low, unhurried, the voice I used on spooked animals and spooked passengers, the voice I hadn't needed since the flood year. "Nobody's going to hurt you. You're on my boat now, and on my boat, nobody gets hurt. That's the law. I'm the law. It's a very short legal code."

The boy in the crate didn't smile. But the flinch went out of him by degrees, like frost leaving a window, and Tomás knelt there with his hand on the boy's shoulder, and the two of them looked at each other in some private language older than words, and I stood in the lamplight with a handful of straw and felt the last of my comfortable ignorance come apart in my hands.

Because here's what I knew now, assembled from drill bits and rope marks and a mispronounced name: someone had put children in a crate and shipped them upriver to a basecamp that wasn't a basecamp. Someone with money, and manifests, and a man named Ferrer with a wet-rope handshake. And I was the boat. I was the *route*. Every mile I steamed tomorrow carried them closer to whatever waited at the end of their shipping label.

I thought about turning around again. Believe me, I thought about it thoroughly, the way you worry a loose tooth.

But the current runs one way, the payment waited upstream, and — I'll be honest, since honesty is the one luxury I've always allowed myself — there was a girl named Luz somewhere behind all this, a name spoken like a broken bone, and I have never in my life been able to walk past a broken bone. It's a character flaw. It's the reason I owe money in four towns.

"All right," I said to the boys, and to the river, and to whoever was keeping score. "Here's how it goes. Tomorrow we deliver the crates, because breaking contract mid-river gets us all noticed in ways we can't afford. After that, we find out who signed for children, and then—" I looked at Tomás, at his brother, at the lamplight trembling on the black water sliding past the hull. "Then we decide who we're stealing them back from."

Tomás looked up at me, and for the first time since Port Salinas, the held breath went out of him. He nodded — once, like a man closing a deal.

And far upstream, faint on the night wind off the Gorge of Whistling Stones, I swear the stones themselves began to whistle.
