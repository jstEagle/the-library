## 3. Radio Weather

Elias Thorne had the kind of smile that made you check your pockets.

I'd seen it before on men who paid in futures — the riverboat brokers in Salinas, the cargo agents who promised "next season's rates," my own brother, God rest his optimism. Thorne deployed it now across a folding table set up on the gravel bar they called basecamp, with a canvas awning flapping overhead and a radio mast leaning against a pine like a drunk against a lamppost.

"Captain Villalobos." He said my name like he'd practiced it, which meant someone had told him to. "The Marisol. I've heard good things."

"You've heard I show up," I said. "That's most of what people hear about me."

He laughed, warm and easy, and gestured at the crates stacked on his table like he was dealing cards. Payment, he said. Supplies for the return run. And then, when I didn't move, he produced an envelope that turned out to contain not money but a letter of credit from the Cordillera Mining and Survey Consortium, redeemable, it said, *upon completion of contracted services*.

"I don't take paper," I said. "Paper burns. Paper gets lost. Paper turns out to be worth exactly what the man who wrote it wants it to be worth, which is usually nothing."

"It's good at any bank in Port Salinas."

"Port Salinas is four days downriver and full of people who'd like a word with me. You'll forgive me if I don't plan my finances around a trip there."

Thorne's smile held. It was a well-built smile, load-bearing. Behind him, two of his men were hauling crates off my deck — the ones I'd carried up from Salinas — and stacking them beside a cook tent. One of them moved wrong for a laborer. Too careful with his hands. Too aware of where everyone stood. I filed him away under *trouble*, which is a crowded drawer on my boat.

"The consortium is good for it," Thorne said. "I'll stake my reputation."

"I don't know your reputation."

"That's easily fixed. Stay for supper. Meet the team. Mara Quill can tell you about the orchids she's found — she talks about them the way other people talk about their children. And I'll have your coin by week's end, when the supply launch comes up from San Vega."

The supply launch. Which meant another boat coming up this stretch of river, which meant more chances for word of the Marisol to travel back down it. Every extra day I sat here was a day my name got passed around campfires like a bad coin.

But the alternative was running the Gorge of Whistling Stones alone, in fog season, with a cargo hold that still smelled faintly of whatever those mining crates had been packed in. And I hadn't decided yet whether I was going to do that.

So I said yes to supper. Not because I trusted him. Because a smart runner learns everything she can about the people paying her, and because supper was free, and because — though I'd have denied it under oath — I was tired of eating alone.

* * *

The expedition ate well, I'll give them that. There was a cook named Beto who made a trout stew with enough chili in it to strip paint, and there were eleven of us crammed under the big awning while the fog came off the water in ropes and the first stars showed through the gaps.

Mara Quill sat across from me, a small woman with dirt permanently worked into her knuckles and a notebook open beside her plate even though she wasn't writing in it. She talked about orchids the way Thorne had promised — a tiny yellow thing growing out of a cliff face above the gorge, found nowhere else in the world, apparently, and she'd nearly fallen into the river getting a specimen.

"Worth it?" I asked.

She looked at me like I'd asked if breathing was worth it. Then her expression did something complicated, and she said, "Ask me again next week," and went back to her stew.

I let that go. I've learned that everyone upriver is carrying something, and pressing on the bruise before the stew is finished is bad manners.

Tomás sat at the edge of the group, near the fire, where a boy could stay warm without being noticed. He'd eaten three bowls and spoken zero words. When Beto offered a fourth, Tomás shook his head but kept his eyes on the pot like it might be taken away. I knew that arithmetic. Eat now, because you don't know when the next bowl comes. I'd done that math myself for a couple of years after I left home, and I hated seeing it on a kid's face more than I wanted to admit.

Thorne held court at the head of the table, telling a story about a survey in the southern ranges, and everyone laughed where they were supposed to. He was good at this. That was the thing I kept circling back to. He was genuinely good — warm, attentive, remembered that Beto's daughter was sick and asked after her, remembered that the young surveyor with the sunburned neck had a birthday coming. A man built to lead expeditions.

And yet.

It was later, when the plates were cleared and most of the crew had drifted toward their tents, that I heard the argument. I'd gone back to the Marisol to check my lines — habit, or excuse — and the short path ran past the radio tent, where the canvas glowed lantern-yellow and the voices inside came through clear as church bells.

"—the ridge is not the objective, Elias. The valley is. It has always been the valley." A new voice. Dry, clipped, the accent of somebody educated somewhere expensive. "The mapping work is cover and you know it. What's under the ridge is the whole reason the consortium funded this."

"And what's under the ridge stays under the ridge until I have a complete survey," Thorne said. His warmth was gone. What was left was flat and hard, and honestly it suited him better. "You want me to announce a find we haven't confirmed, in territory where every village between here and the pass will hear about it inside a month? You want the priests involved? The provincial government?"

"I want results. The investors want results."

"The investors can look at the maps I've already sent. The route through the highlands is real. The gorge crossing is real. Everything else waits for confirmation."

A pause. When the dry voice came back, it had dropped lower, and I had to step closer to the canvas to catch it, close enough that I could see the shadow of a man moving behind the lantern.

"There are people downstream asking questions already. About the cargo manifests. About why a mapping survey needs blasting permits."

Blasting permits.

I stood very still. Somewhere out on the water, a night bird called, that low hooting note the locals say means rain in three days.

"Blasting permits are standard for trail clearing," Thorne said.

"At forty sites? Along one valley?"

Another pause, longer. When Thorne spoke again, his voice had changed register entirely — quiet, reasonable, the voice of a man explaining something obvious to a child. "Nothing happens to the valley. Nothing is going to happen to anyone. But if the consortium decides the water rights are worth developing, that decision gets made in an office in the capital, not around a campfire. My job is to give them accurate information. What they do with it—"

"Is your problem too, Elias. You drew the maps."

"I draw maps," Thorne said. "I don't decide who drowns."

The words hung there. Even through canvas, even secondhand, they landed like a dropped anchor. The dry voice said something else, quieter, and Thorne answered, and the argument wound down into the murmur of men agreeing to disagree, which is how men agree to do nothing.

I walked back to my boat along the dark path with my heart going harder than the walk justified.

*Drown.* It was probably nothing. Men in business argued in hyperbole; I'd heard worse from debt collectors. Maybe it was irrigation. Maybe it was a reservoir. Maybe *drown* was just the word that came to hand at the end of a long day.

But I thought about the crates labeled for a mining survey on a mapping expedition. I thought about blasting permits at forty sites. I thought about the way Mara Quill had said *ask me again next week* like next week was a country she wasn't sure she'd still be living in.

Back aboard, Tomás was awake, sitting cross-legged on the cargo hatch in the moonlight, watching the water. Of course he was. Kids who stow away don't sleep easy in new places.

"Shouldn't you be in a hammock?" I said.

He shrugged. I sat down next to him, leaving the polite gap between us that strangers keep, and for a while we just watched the river slide past, silver-black, carrying its secrets south.

"You heard them arguing," he said finally. Not a question. Kid had ears like the night bird.

"Men argue. It's mostly weather."

"It's not weather." He picked at a splinter on the hatch board. "The porter, Ríos, told the cook the consortium bought up land papers in the valley villages last year. Cheap. Nobody would sell until the price suddenly got good." He glanced at me, quick and sideways, checking my face the way kids check whether the adults have noticed the same monster under the bed. "You ever see what a village looks like after the company men come through?"

"No," I said.

"Me neither," Tomás said. "That's what scares me. Nobody's seen it yet."

He went to his hammock after that, and I stayed on the hatch a long time, doing sums I didn't like. Cargo labels that lied. Blasting permits. Land bought cheap ahead of some announcement nobody had made yet. And a leader who said *I don't decide who drowns* in the tone of a man who had already decided it wasn't his problem.

Nine years I'd kept my boat small and my name quiet and my debts moving, and the deal had been simple: I carry cargo, I mind my own business, the river leaves me alone. That deal had never once been honored, but I kept renewing it anyway, the way you keep feeding a stray that bites you.

Tomorrow, I told myself, I'd deliver whatever needed delivering and point the Marisol downstream and let the ridge keep its secrets. Tomorrow.

The river, as usual, had other plans.

* * *

It came at half past two in the morning, the hour when the fog lies thickest on the water and honest people are asleep and liars like me are lying awake counting the sounds of the night.

I was in my bunk in the wheelhouse cabin when the radio woke up.

Not the scheduled traffic — the basecamp operator kept civilized hours, and the consortium's relay station down in Salinas signed off at ten. This was raw, unfiltered, a voice coming straight off the mountains with nothing between it and my ear but atmosphere and trouble.

The Marisol's set was an old valve rig I'd bought off a wrecked patrol launch and nursed back to health myself, and it had a habit of catching frequencies it wasn't tuned to, especially in fog, when the air itself seemed to conduct. So when the crackle started, I lay there telling myself it was drift, static, the mountain talking to itself.

Then a voice came through.

"—anyone — this is Quill party, upstream camp two, does anyone copy—"

I was out of the bunk before I finished deciding to be.

The voice was young, a man's, pitched high with the particular strain of someone holding panic in one hand and a microphone in the other. Behind him: wind, a lot of wind, the whistling moan of air through stone that gave the Gorge its name, and under it something rhythmic and wrong, a knocking, or water hitting wood in a way water shouldn't.

"Camp two, calling any station. We have — break — the tender is — break —" Static swallowed him whole. I spun the tuning dial with my thumb, fine adjustments, sweat on my fingers despite the cold. The voice swam back. "—three injured, one badly, we're on the north bank above the narrows, repeat, north bank — oh God, the rope bridge is —"

Gone. Just carrier wave, the empty hiss of a frequency with no one on it.

Then, faintly, impossibly, the voice again — but different now, farther, as if the man had stepped away from the set or the set had been knocked sideways:

"—if anyone copies, tell Thorne the case — tell him we kept the —"

Static. Clean, total, final. I sat with my hand on the dial for ten minutes, twenty, waiting for the voice to come back the way you wait at a bedside. It didn't. At some point the fog pressed against the wheelhouse windows and the river whispered against the hull and the radio settled into its ordinary sleep, and I realized my hands were shaking.

*Tell Thorne.*

Well. That was simple, wasn't it? Wake Elias Thorne, report the distress call, let the expedition handle its own dead and dying. That was the contract. That was minding my own business. I could be back under my blanket in fifteen minutes with my conscience officially clean and my tomorrow unchanged.

Instead I sat in the dark and did the geography. Camp two, upstream. North bank above the narrows. The narrows were the mouth of the Gorge of Whistling Stones — thirty kilometers of white water that every pilot on the Cobre swore at and none of them ran, not in fog season, not with a boat, not sober. The overland trail around the gorge took four days on foot, six with gear, and the first twenty-four hours of it climbed through country where a man with three injured companions couldn't walk, let alone be carried.

Which meant that if anyone was going to reach that voice before the storm the night bird had promised — before the cold finished what the river started — it was going to be me. Me and a boat that had never met water she wouldn't argue with.

I thought about the crates. I thought about *who drowns*. I thought about a boy asleep in my hold who'd asked what a village looks like after the company men come through, and how neither of us knew, and how I was starting to be afraid we'd find out.

Dread arrived ahead of the storm, the way it always does, settling into my chest like fog into a valley, filling every low place.

I lit the wheelhouse lamp. I checked the fuel gauge, the bilge, the chart case. On the shelf above the helm, my father's old brass compass caught the lamplight, and I had a sudden, unwelcome memory of him saying that the river gives you exactly as much warning as you deserve.

Upstream, thirty kilometers through the worst water on the Cobre, someone had kept *the case*. Someone was hurt. Someone was waiting.

I put my hand on the throttle and, for the first time in nine years, pointed the Marisol at the thing I'd been running from the shape of all my life.

The Gorge of Whistling Stones waited upriver in the dark, singing its one long note.
