## 4. What the River Keeps

The last sane person to run the Gorge of Whistling Stones was a man named Aurelio Pacheco, and the river keeps his boat. Parts of it, anyway. A section of railing surfaces every spring down at the lower bends, polished smooth as a hymnal, and the ferrymen of San Vega have a saying about it: the Cobre gives back only what it's done chewing.

I had said no to the Gorge exactly four times in my life. Once to a smuggler with a pistol, once to a honeymooning couple who thought rapids were romantic, twice to my own reflection. This morning I said yes to all the wrong reasons at once, which is how the best decisions on the Río Cobre tend to get made.

The distress call had come in fragments the night before — *Marisol, if anyone, above the Gorge, we are* — and then static, and then a voice I didn't recognize saying something in a language I half knew, and then nothing. Elias Thorne had gone upriver with eleven people and a radio he treated like a toy, and now the radio was treating him back.

I stood at the wheel with the fog burning off the water in long gray ribbons and told myself I was going up for the salvage rights. Wrecks mean cargo, cargo means payment, payment means the Marisol eats through winter. That was the story I told myself. I have told myself worse and believed them harder.

Tomás appeared at my elbow the way he did, without footstep or warning, like a cat who'd decided I was furniture he approved of. He'd been aboard three days now, fed once, questioned twice, and still I hadn't thrown him off at Port Salinas, which told me something about myself I wasn't ready to hear.

"The gorge," he said. Not a question. He'd heard me talking to the engine, which is where I do my honest thinking.

"You know a better way up?"

"No." He looked at the water the way other boys looked at girls, or at knives. "But the water will tell you where it isn't."

"That's very poetic for a stowaway."

"I wasn't stowing. I was traveling in the cargo because it was going where I was going." He said it without a flicker, and I almost laughed, because it was the same speech I'd given my mother nine years ago, word for word, and it had sounded just as true and just as false.

"Fine," I said. "Spotter's post. Bow rail. You see white water that looks wrong, you shout. You see a rock that looks friendly, you shout twice. You fall in, I'm not stopping, because stopping in the Gorge is how you become part of the scenery."

He nodded like I'd handed him a commission in the navy.

* * *

The Gorge of Whistling Stones announces itself a mile early, the way bad weather and bad relatives do. First the walls close in — black granite going up and up until the sky is a rumor — and then the sound starts. The river forces itself through a hundred slots and hollows in the rock, and each one plays a different note, low and mournful, and the whole thing together sounds like a choir rehearsing in a cathedral they intend to drown you in.

I had run the lower third once, years ago, with a lighter boat and a heavier heart, and I'd come out the far side shaking so hard I'd had to tie myself to the wheel. The Marisol was built for freight, not for folly. But she was mine, and I knew her the way you know a stubborn animal — every shudder, every lie, every place she'd hold if I asked.

"Engine's warm," I told Tomás. "That's the good news. The bad news is everything else."

I took us in.

The first hour was the kind of piloting that doesn't leave room for thinking. The channel braided and unbraided like a rope coming apart, and I read it the only way it can be read — by the color of the water, by the shape of the foam, by the way the current combed the surface into lines that pointed at the rocks it was about to kill you on. Green water, go. White water, maybe. Brown water, pray.

And the boy — the boy was *good*.

I don't mean he was brave. Plenty of boys are brave; it's cheap as salt. I mean he read the river. He'd stand at the bow rail with his whole body leaning into the current like a divining rod, and he'd call the channel before I could see it. "Left of the white, hard left, the right side is lying." And he'd be right. The right side *was* lying — there was a submerged shelf there that would have opened the Marisol's hull like a tin of sardines.

"Where does a boy learn that?" I shouted over the choir.

"My grandfather fished the Cobre above the gorge." His voice carried back to me thin and clear. "He said the gorge was the river's mouth, and you don't argue with a mouth that big."

"Your grandfather sounds smarter than me."

"He is."

I laughed, and the laugh came out strange, half terror, and the Gorge swallowed it and turned it into another note in the song.

We came around a bend called the Elbow — I knew the name from the ferry pilots' talk, everything in the Gorge has a name, all of it named the way you name a dangerous dog, so you can curse it specifically — and the channel narrowed to less than twice my beam. The walls went vertical. The water went green-black and fast, and the whistling got so high and close it felt like it was happening inside my skull.

And then Tomás screamed.

Not a warning. A name. "There — there, in the water — *persona* —"

I saw it a half second later. A shape in the shallows below the Elbow, where the river spread and slowed over a gravel fan — an overturned tender, expedition-orange, wedged against a boulder like a beetle pinned to a card. And beside it, moving, a hand.

Everything in me that had been piloting stopped, and everything that hadn't started screaming *you cannot stop in the Gorge, you cannot, there is no stopping here* — and both of those things were true, and neither of them mattered.

I did the arithmetic in about a second and a half, and the arithmetic was this: the gravel fan was shallow, the current there was slow, and the Marisol drew two feet empty. I could put her nose into the fan, hold her against the current with the engine at half, and it would be the most dangerous thing I'd ever done, which was a competitive field.

"Hold the rail," I told Tomás. "Both hands. If we hit, you swim for the tender."

"Can you swim?" he asked, which was either concern or a very practical question, and with Tomás it was always both.

"Badly," I said, and drove us at the gravel.

The Marisol grounded with a grinding shudder that I felt in my teeth, and I brought the engine up just enough to hold us, and the whole boat trembled like a horse being asked to stand in fire. I threw a line to the shape in the water.

It was a woman. She was half in and half out of the river, and she had one arm locked through the tender's handhold, and in her other arm she was clutching an oilskin case to her chest the way you'd hold a child, or a heart you'd been given to carry. Her leg was wrong — bent where legs don't bend — and her lips were gray, and she had been in that water long enough that I understood, with a cold sliding feeling, that the hand I'd seen moving had probably been moving for hours.

"Take the case," she said, before I even had the line to her. Her voice was cracked down to the wood. "Take it, don't let it —"

"I'm taking *you*," I said. "The case can swim."

"It can't." And there was something in her face then that stopped me — not fear for herself. She'd left fear for herself somewhere back upriver, in whatever had put her in the water. "Please."

I got the line around her. Tomás hauled — the boy was stronger than he had any right to be, and I made a note to ask about that later, in the long list of things I'd ask about later — and between us we got her over the rail and onto my deck, and the oilskin case came with her, still locked in her arm, and I swear the case came first.

I backed the Marisol off the gravel with my heart in my throat, and the river took us again, and the Gorge sang us through the last two miles like it was bored with us now, like we weren't worth a proper killing.

* * *

She was the expedition's botanist. I got that much out of her in the first ten minutes, wrapped in my one good blanket with the engine room's residual heat doing what it could, while I ran us out of the Gorge's shadow and into the wide gray afternoon of the upper valley.

Mara Quill. She gave the name like she was checking it against a list to see if it still applied. Mid-thirties maybe, the kind of face that goes thin and precise rather than soft, hands that were ruined in the way hands get ruined by a lifetime of fieldwork — split nails, old cuts healed crooked. Her leg I splinted with a boat hook handle and the rest of my first-aid kit, and she took the splinting without a sound, which told me more than screaming would have.

The tender had gone over at the falls above the Elbow, she said, two days ago. Two days. She'd held to the boat and then to the boulder and then to the boat again, and the river had taken everyone else — she said it flatly, the way you report weather — and the case had washed up against her in the first hour, and she understood, she said, that it had been given to her.

"Given by who?"

"By the river." She looked at me with eyes that had gone somewhere past exhaustion into a clear cold country on the other side of it. "Captain, who sent you? Was it Thorne? Is he —"

"Thorne went silent two days ago. I got a fragment of a distress call. That's all I know."

She closed her eyes. "That was me. The call was me. I had the radio out of the tender's kit, and I got maybe twenty seconds before the water took it." Her voice did something then, the first crack in the flatness. "I called for help, and the help — the people who come for that case, they aren't help. Captain, you need to turn this boat around."

"Can't. The Gorge doesn't sell return tickets."

"Then get off the river."

"And go where, exactly?" I said it sharper than I meant to, because my hands were still shaking at the wheel and I hate it when people are right before I've had time to be right myself.

She didn't answer. Tomás answered for her, from the bow, where he'd been sitting with the oilskin case in his lap like it was a sleeping animal he'd been assigned to guard.

"The mining men burned San Rafael," he said quietly. "The village above the falls. That's where the tender was going. That's where I was going."

Mara's head came up. "You're from San Rafael?"

"My grandmother is. Was." The boy's jaw worked. "The men in the black boat came at night. My sister and I ran different directions. I went downriver. She —" He stopped. He looked at the case. "The map in there. My grandfather used to talk about the old route over the mountains. He said it was ours. He said they'd want it."

I stood at the wheel with the current pulling us up the valley and did the thing I'm worst at, which is adding numbers I've been avoiding. A mining consortium that finances a mapping expedition and labels its crates wrong. An expedition that goes silent the same week a village burns. A woman in the water who'd rather drown than lose a leather case. A boy who travels in cargo holds toward fires. And me, with a debt collector in Port Salinas who knew my brother's name, and a black-hulled launch I hadn't seen yet but could feel coming up the river behind us like a change in pressure.

Every instinct I had, the instincts that had kept me alive and alone and solvent-ish for nine years, said: put her ashore at the next landing, put the boy ashore, put the case ashore, and be gone by dark. The Marisol was a freight boat. Freight doesn't take sides. Freight doesn't bleed.

But I looked at Mara Quill, who had held a stranger's case in a killing river for two days because someone had to. And I looked at Tomás, who had called the channel for me all morning like he'd been born at my bow rail. And I thought about the hand moving in the water, and how the river keeps what it keeps, and how for nine years I had been keeping myself — carefully, coldly, like cargo — and what that had actually bought me.

"San Vega is six hours up," I said. "There's a doctor there who owes me money, which is the best kind of doctor. We'll get that leg seen to. Then we'll talk about routes over mountains, and about sisters, and about who exactly is coming down this river behind us. Because someone is, and I'd rather meet them with a plan than with my pants down."

Mara looked at me for a long moment. "Why?" she said. "You don't know me. You don't know him. You could be at Port Salinas by morning."

"I could," I agreed. The fog of the morning was long gone and the peaks ahead had come out, white and enormous, and the river ran toward them silver and certain. "But I ran this gorge today for a distress call I only half heard, and I've been asking myself why ever since. Best answer I've got is that nine years ago the river asked me for something and I didn't give it, and it's been holding the note ever since." I turned the wheel a point to starboard, toward the deep channel, toward San Vega, toward whatever came next. "So. You're passengers now. Both of you. And whatever's in that case —"

"It's a map," Tomás said.

"— whatever it is," I said, "it stays dry, it stays aboard, and it stays *ours*. All of ours. That's the fare. That's the whole fare."

Mara Quill closed her eyes and, for the first time since I'd pulled her out of the water, slept.

And far behind us, back down the long silver reach of the Cobre, so far off it was more a feeling than a fact, I heard — or thought I heard, or my gut heard for me — the low throaty note of an engine that wasn't mine, running upstream against the current, and running hard.
