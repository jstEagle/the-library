## 5. The Oilskin Case

Pain is an excellent editor. It cuts everything down to the essential sentence.

Mine, for the first two days aboard the Marisol, was: *don't die on this woman's boat.* Not because I feared death particularly — a botanist spends her career making peace with the fact that everything blooms once and then composts — but because dying would mean Juniper Villalobos would have to explain my corpse to someone, and she had the look of a woman who charged extra for explanations.

So I lay in the narrow bunk beneath the wheelhouse, leg splinted with what I suspected was a boat hook lashed to a cutting board, and I listened to the river talk to itself through the hull, and I catalogued my failures in alphabetical order.

*Arrogance* — believing eight people could map a gorge that had swallowed better expeditions than ours.

*Bribery* — accepting consortium money in the first place, telling myself the science justified the source.

*Cowardice* — sending that radio call upstream instead of downriver, because Thorne's last order had been *contact the survey office*, and even with him dead or drowned or worse, I'd obeyed it like a good employee.

The fever came and went. When it came, the cabin filled with whistling — the sound of the Gorge of Whistling Stones, which I could still hear inside my skull, wind threading through stone flutes older than any language I knew. When it went, I heard the real sounds instead: the diesel heartbeat of the engine, Juniper's boots overhead, and the boy, Tomás, moving around the deck with the particular silence of a child who has learned that being noticed is dangerous.

On the third morning, Juniper came down the ladder carrying a tin mug of something that smelled like it had been boiled twice out of spite.

"You're awake," she said. "Good. You owe me a story."

"I owe you a great deal more than that."

"Start with the story." She set the mug on the shelf bolted beside my bunk and sat on the crate opposite, forearms on her knees, in the posture of a woman prepared to wait out a siege. "I pulled you out of the water clutching a case like it was your own child. I ran the Whistling Stones for you — which, by the way, every sane captain on this river calls suicide with scenery. And now there's a boy on my boat who won't say where he's from, and my fuel gauge reads lower than my patience. So. The story."

I looked at the oilskin case where it sat wedged behind my pack, and I thought about how many ways there are to tell a truth badly.

"My name is Mara Quill," I said. "Botanist. Seconded to the Thorne expedition as field surveyor. We were financed by the Cordillera Consolidated Mining and Water Company."

"Which makes you—"

"Employed by them. Not loyal to them. There's a difference." I took the mug. The drink was terrible. I drank it anyway, because my body had opinions about fluids and none of them were negotiable. "Though I'll grant you the difference didn't matter much when the checks cleared."

Juniper waited. She was good at waiting. I'd learn that about her later — that her silences weren't empty, they were loaded, like a spring trap you walked into voluntarily.

"Thorne told everyone we were mapping," I said. "Cartographic survey, upper Río Cobre, headwaters above the Gorge. That was true as far as it went. What he didn't tell the crew — what only he and I knew by the end — was who wanted the maps and why."

"Consolidated wants minerals."

"Consolidated has all the minerals it can dig for twenty years. What they want now is water." I shifted against the splint and paid for it, and kept talking through the pain because if I stopped I might not start again. "There's a basin above the gorge. Wide, high, ringed by peaks that hold snow ten months of the year. Three rivers braid together there before they drop into the Cobre. Do you know what's in that basin?"

"Villages," Juniper said quietly. "I trade upriver sometimes. San Rafael de las Nieves. Alto Miraflores. The third one—" she frowned, "—Q'antu Pampa. Little places. Terraces, sheep, a chapel apiece."

"Three villages," I said. "Four hundred years old, some of them. And sitting at the bottom of the finest natural reservoir in the Cordillera del Viento."

The engine thumped below us. Somewhere on deck, Tomás's footsteps paused, then resumed, closer to the hatch.

"A dam," Juniper said. It wasn't a question. River people do arithmetic fast.

"A dam. Forty meters of concrete at the narrows below Q'antu Pampa. The survey wasn't mapping the route for science, Captain. It was mapping the flood line. Every contour we drew was a calculation of how deep the water would stand over those villages. Thorne called it the Provision Project. He said it like it was a gift. Power for the mines, irrigation for the lowlands, progress marching uphill." My hands had started shaking, so I wrapped them around the mug. "He never once said the word *drown*. He said *inundation zone*. As if the water would be polite about it."

Juniper was silent a long moment. "And the case?"

"The case is why I'm alive and the others mostly aren't." I closed my eyes. The whistling started up faintly in the back of my skull, and I let it, because the sound had become inseparable from what I had to confess. "Two weeks ago we found the pass."

* * *

Here is what I told her, and I will tell it now the way I should have told it then, straight through, without the professional hedging I hid behind for nine years of academic writing:

Above the gorge, where the maps went blank and the muleteers refused to go, there is a road.

Not a trail. A road. Cut into the cliff faces, walled with fitted stone, switching back and forth across the shoulders of the mountain in long deliberate grades — engineering, not wandering. It runs from the high basin down through the Cordillera in a way no modern route does, dropping through country too steep for the porter's roads, crossing the watershed at a pass so sheltered that snow barely holds on it. Someone built it to move a whole people across the mountains quickly and invisibly.

We found the first carvings on the fourth day. Symbols cut into the way-stones — a spiral, a bird with spread wings, three lines descending like water. Thorne photographed them and got very quiet, which for Thorne meant he was calculating.

By the sixth day we understood. The road was an escape route. Built centuries ago by the ancestors of exactly those villages Consolidated intended to flood — built by people fleeing some earlier empire that also wanted their valley, their water, their labor. They'd carved their story into the stones as they went: *we passed this way, we survived, remember the road.* The spiral for the journey inward. The bird for the crossing. The three lines for the rivers left behind.

"They mapped their own rescue," I said to Juniper. "In stone. Four hundred years ago. And we stood there with our theodolites measuring how deep the water would get over their descendants."

"And the map in the case?"

"Thorne compiled it. The full route — every grade, every water source, every shelter cave, every symbol and its meaning. Cross-referenced with our survey of the basin." I looked at the oilskin case. "It's the only complete copy. Thorne sent duplicate pages downriver with the supply tender two days before—" I stopped. Started again. "Before the end. But the tender went into the gorge and didn't come out, and whether those pages are at the bottom of the Cobre or in a consortium safe, I don't know. This is the master. His own hand. He sealed it in oilskin the night before the accident and gave it to me, and he said, *if anything happens to me, this goes to the survey office, nowhere else.*"

"And you don't intend to obey him."

It wasn't a question either. Juniper Villalobos read intentions the way I read leaf-veins — quickly, and without much sympathy for the specimen.

"No," I said. "I don't."

* * *

She let me rest after that. Or perhaps she simply went away to think, which for her amounted to the same thing. I slept, and dreamed of whistling stones, and woke in the late afternoon to find Tomás sitting on the deck planking outside my open hatch, cross-legged, watching me with the grave attention of a small owl.

"You were on the tender," he said.

It wasn't an accusation. It was a fact, offered the way children offer facts — as a door left open for me to walk through or not.

"Yes," I said. "I was."

"It sank."

"It sank."

"In the gorge." His eyes didn't leave mine. "My sister was on a boat that went into the gorge. Luz. She's twelve." A pause, and then, with the terrible precision of a child who has rehearsed a sentence until it comes out smooth: "Did you see anyone in the water?"

I had seen things in the water. I would carry them until I composted, like everything else. But not his sister — I searched the memory honestly, and not his sister, and I told him so, and watched him decide whether to believe me. He decided, mostly, to believe me. Children are generous that way, far more generous than they should have to be.

"Captain says you know things," he said finally. "About upstream. About why men burned—" He shut his mouth on whatever came next, and I saw, briefly, the shape of the thing underneath: smoke, a village, a sister taken. I filed it away the way I filed everything, guiltily, promising myself I'd deal with it later, knowing later was a country I might never reach.

"I know some things," I said. "Come in out of the wind and I'll tell you one."

* * *

That evening, with Tomás sitting close enough to warm my good side and Juniper leaning in the hatchway pretending she wasn't listening while listening with her whole spine, I opened the oilskin case for the first time since the gorge.

Inside: the map, folded into its oilcloth sleeves. Thorne's field notebooks. And a folder of correspondence that I had stolen from Thorne's own strongbox two nights before the disaster, because a botanist spends her life noticing what doesn't fit, and certain letters hadn't fit.

I laid it out on the bunk between us. Survey schedules. A memorandum on "resettlement logistics" — forty-one words of bureaucratic prose that added up to four hundred families given ninety days and a rail voucher. A letter from the provincial governor thanking Consolidated for its "civic partnership." And the crown of the collection: a signed authorization for advance survey crews to secure the basin "against obstruction," dated eleven days before we ever arrived.

"Secure against obstruction," Juniper read aloud. Her voice was flat in the way water is flat just before it goes over the edge. "That's a nice way to say burn a village."

"That's exactly what it said." I turned the memorandum toward Tomás, because he deserved to see it, because I had decided somewhere in the last hour that the worst thing I could do to this child was protect him from paper. "San Rafael de las Nieves. Alto Miraflores. Q'antu Pampa. If the dam is built, these stop existing. If the testimony of these villagers ever reaches a court, the project dies in committee. Which is why the advance crews came early. Which is why—"

I stopped. Because here was the part I had been circling for three days, the rock in the channel I kept almost striking.

"Say it," Juniper said.

"The distress call." I made myself look at her. "The night the tender went down, I transmitted. From basecamp, on the expedition band. Standard procedure — Thorne's standing order, contact the survey office if communications lapse. But the survey office frequency routes through Consolidated's relay station at Port Salinas, and I knew that, and I sent it anyway, because I was hurt and frightened and following orders like a good employee." My hands were shaking again. I let them. "Whoever answered didn't identify themselves. They asked for position, casualty numbers, and whether the documentation was intact. *Documentation*, Captain. Not survivors. Documentation. And then they asked me to hold the case at basecamp for pickup."

"And you ran."

"And I ran. Took the case and the letters and went downriver on foot along the gorge rim, which is how I broke my leg, which is how you found me." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Do you understand? Whoever's coming upstream — whoever burned that depot, whoever's hunting this boat — they're coming because of me. I lit the signal fire. I told them the map exists. Every mile of danger on this river right now, I ordered up like supplies."

Tomás put his hand on my arm. Small, warm, absolutely steady.

"You carried it out," he said. "That's the part that counts."

From the hatchway, Juniper made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "The boy's a better philosopher than either of us. Listen to him." She pushed off the frame and came to stand over the spread of papers, and I watched her face do something complicated — watch the temptation arrive and be examined and, for now, set aside. "Triple pay," she said, half to herself. "That's what a case like this is worth to the right buyer. Enough to buy a boat free and clear. Enough to disappear properly."

"Captain—"

"I'm thinking out loud, botanist. It's a bad habit. Thinking out loud is how honest people get caught." She gathered the letters, squared their edges with a precision that told me she'd already memorized them, and handed them back. "Here's what I know. There's a launch with a black hull that's been holding our wake since yesterday, staying just polite enough to deny. There's a boy on my deck whose sister is somewhere upstream in the hands of men who write memos about resettlement. And there's a woman in my bunk holding the only map of a road that four hundred years of ancestors carved in stone specifically so that people like us could run from people like *them*." She looked at me, and her eyes in the lamplight were the exact color of the river in flood season — brown going gold, depth hiding current. "I haven't decided anything. But I'll tell you what I'm not doing. I'm not delivering you to a survey office."

"Then where?"

"Upstream," said Juniper Villalobos, and the word landed in the little cabin like a dropped anchor. "Toward the trouble. Because trouble's where your witnesses are, and witnesses are worth more than paper — though don't tell the paper I said that." She was already turning for the ladder. "Rest your leg, Quill. You're going to need to walk on it soon, and I promise you the walking will be uphill."

She climbed out into the dusk. Tomás lingered, gathering the fallen corner of the map with careful fingers, tracing the three carved lines that meant *the rivers left behind.*

"She's going to help us," he said. Not a question. Children and their facts.

"She hasn't decided," I said.

"She decided," said Tomás, and went out after her, light on his feet, toward the bow — where, if I'd known then what I would learn by morning, the first smoke from the leeward shore was already rising thin and gray against the last of the light.
