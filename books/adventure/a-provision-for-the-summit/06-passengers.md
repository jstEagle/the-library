## 6. Passengers

I had a plan for the problem sleeping in my hold. The plan was simple, the kind of plan I like best: at Puerto Ceniza I would carry Mara Quill and her oilskin case to the dock, set them both down among the fish crates, and steam away before anyone could make eye contact with me. It was a good plan. It had the elegance of all my best cowardice.

The river, as usual, had other paperwork to file.

* * *

Puerto Ceniza announced itself by smell before sight — woodsmoke and wet ash and something sweeter underneath, like sugar left too long on a hot stove. I came around the last bend with the current doing half my work and saw the smoke hanging over the town in a brown-gray shawl, too much of it, too low, and none of it coming from chimneys.

Tomás was already on deck. He'd been there since before dawn, sitting cross-legged against the wheelhouse door like a cat who'd decided this boat was his now and was simply waiting for me to accept the arrangement. He looked at the smoke, then at me, and said nothing, which from that boy was a paragraph.

"That's not our fire," I said, mostly to myself.

He looked at me the way children look at adults who say obvious things wrong.

Mara came up on her own feet, which surprised me. Her shoulder was bound tight under a borrowed shirt of mine, and she was gray around the mouth, but she stood at the rail and read the smoke the way I read water — professionally, unhappily.

"Leeward side," she said. "That's the leeward side of the valley."

"And?"

"The villages are on the leeward side." She turned to me, and there was something in her face I didn't like, because it wasn't fear. Fear I can work with. This was arithmetic. "Juniper. The villages are exactly where the consortium wanted them gone from."

I told myself it could be a cooking fire. A big one. A festival. I have a talent for telling myself things; nine years of practice will do that.

* * *

Puerto Ceniza wasn't really a port — more a shelf of town the river had agreed to tolerate. A stone quay, a cantina, a church with a bell that rang flat, forty roofs and change. When I brought the Marisol in slow, the whole population seemed to be standing on the quay, and they weren't fishing, and they weren't trading, and they were looking at my boat the way drowning people look at driftwood.

An old man waded out to the second step of my boarding ladder before I'd finished tying off. His hands were black to the wrist. Behind him, a woman held two children by their collars the way you hold sacks of grain you're afraid to put down.

"Captain." The old man's voice was scraped raw. "You're going downriver."

"I'm going wherever I'm going," I said, which is the closest thing to a motto I've got.

"Take us." He said it simply, no begging in it yet, just the statement, as if passage were a fact of nature he was reporting. "Our village burned three nights ago. Not an accident. Men came with papers and lamps and left with neither. They said the valley would be emptied one way or another, better we chose the way without fire next time."

Behind me I heard Mara's breath catch. Tomás had gone very still, the way he does, folding himself smaller.

"How many of you?" I asked, hating myself for asking, because asking meant I was negotiating, and negotiating meant I'd already lost.

"Thirty-one," the old man said. "Some hurt. None dying, if we eat soon."

Thirty-one. My boat slept eight comfortably, twelve if they were friendly, twenty if they were desperate and thin. Thirty-one was not a number. Thirty-one was a different kind of vessel entirely.

"I'm carrying cargo," I said. "And a passenger. And I'm expected—"

"You're not expected anywhere," the woman with the children said. She had a burn wrapped along one forearm, dressed in what looked like strips of a church banner. "Nobody is expected anywhere anymore. That's what burning a village means, captain. It means nobody is expected anywhere."

I want it recorded that I opened my mouth to refuse them. I want it recorded clearly, because everything after happened so fast that history might get the impression I volunteered.

* * *

Here is how it actually went: I said no. I said it firmly, with the full authority of a woman who owns her own boat and her own bad decisions. And then the old man's granddaughter — maybe ten years old, hair singed to the scalp on one side — walked past me up the gangplank as if she hadn't heard, sat down on my cargo hatch, and began quietly to cry. Not loudly. Quietly, the way children cry when they've learned crying doesn't bring anything.

And Tomás — quiet, watchful, stowaway Tomás — crossed the deck and sat down beside her and did nothing at all except be there, which is apparently a thing children know how to do that adults forget.

I stood on my own deck, in my own wheelhouse shadow, and felt my refusal die of embarrassment.

"We'll need the crates moved aft," I heard myself say. "All of them. And anyone with a fever rides up front where I can see them. And—" I pointed at the old man "—nobody touches the engine. Nobody. Last boy who touched my engine lost the privilege of fingers."

It took four hours to load thirty-one people onto a riverboat built for eight. They came aboard carrying almost nothing, which is its own particular horror — a whole village's worth of lives reduced to what hands could grab from a fire. A sewing machine. A tin of photographs. One man carried a door, of all things, the actual door of his house, and when I asked him why, he looked at me like I was the mad one and said, "It was my father's."

By late afternoon the Marisol sat low in the water like a dog that's eaten something it shouldn't. I had refugees stacked on the cargo hatch, packed into the hold between crates of survey equipment I was increasingly certain I should throw overboard, and draped along every inch of deck rail. Mara had appointed herself medical officer by virtue of being the only person aboard with a botanist's steady hands and a wound of her own to prove she understood the curriculum. She moved through them with Tomás trailing her carrying her kit, and I watched the boy — watched him hand her bandages before she asked, watch him learn the names of the injured faster than I learn the names of paying passengers.

"He's good at this," Mara said, appearing at my elbow in the wheelhouse while I pretended to study the channel ahead.

"He's good at hiding," I said. "This is adjacent."

"It's not adjacent." She leaned on the doorframe, favoring her bad shoulder. "You've noticed it too. How he watches you pilot. How he knew the sandbar before you called it yesterday."

"I noticed a child who should be asleep."

"Juniper." She used my name deliberately, the way you set a cup down hard enough to ring. "That boy ran away from something toward something. And whatever he's running toward, I think it looks a lot like this boat."

I didn't answer, because the channel required my attention, and also because she was right, and I've never once enjoyed being right.

* * *

We fed them that night off my own stores, because there was nothing else. Rice and beans and the smoked fish I'd been saving, stretched with river water and optimism. I cooked in shifts while Mara supervised the wounded and the old man — his name was Aurelio, and he'd been the village's schoolteacher, which explained the unnerving way he organized the others — kept a ledger of who ate, who needed what, whose cough was getting worse.

I found Tomás last, after most of them had settled. He was sitting at the stern rail with a bowl he hadn't touched, watching the dark water slide past, and Mara was beside him. I only caught the end of it.

"—your sister," Mara was saying softly. "Luz. You said that was her name."

"She's eleven." He said it to the river, not to us. "She likes the blue flowers that grow on the rocks above the rapids. She picks them even though they stain her fingers for days. She thinks I don't notice."

"Mara," I said carefully, "why are you asking about his sister?"

"Because he asked me first," Mara said, "whether I had family. And I told him no. And then he said—" she glanced at the boy, and something passed between them, some treaty signed in the language of children who've lost things "—he said that kin isn't only blood. That his grandmother used to say the villages up here made kin out of whoever survived alongside you. And then he said I was kin now. Because I survived alongside him."

Tomás stared at the water with tremendous dignity, ears going red.

"Well," I said, because someone had to ruin the moment and it has always been my gift. "Kin or not, he still can't touch the engine."

Mara laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, and I saw Tomás's mouth twitch despite himself — and I turned back to my wheel and my lamp and my channel, and pretended I hadn't just felt something in my chest that had no business on a working boat.

* * *

I took the middle watch alone, as always. The Marisol crept downriver under a half-moon, riding heavy, her wake muffled by thirty-one sleepers' worth of extra weight. Aurelio's people had bedded down in piles, the way animals do in cold weather, and the deck breathed with them.

I stood at the wheel and did the accounting I do when I can't sleep. Fuel: six days. Food: less, now. Money: promises from a dead expedition. Cargo: evidence, probably, of something criminal. Passengers: thirty-two, counting the stowaway, counting the botanist, counting — I supposed I had to count myself somewhere in that figure, though I'd spent nine years keeping myself off every manifest I could.

Downriver lay Port Salinas and my brother's debt and every reason I'd ever left. Upstream lay a gorge, a consortium, and men who burned villages to clear the paperwork. Ahead of me, technically, lay nothing at all, because I still hadn't decided where I was going, and I told myself that indecision was freedom, the way I'd been telling myself that for nine years.

A small weight arrived at my side. Tomás, awake when he should be asleep, holding two cups of something hot that Mara must have brewed, because I certainly hadn't.

"You'll want to know," he said, in the tone of a harbinger, "that the man with the door has been talking to Aurelio. About the map."

My stomach dropped through the hull. "What map? There's no map."

"Aurelio says the old route over the mountains — the one the consortium wants buried — his grandmother walked it when she was a girl. He says the symbols on it match carvings in the church at Ceniza. Carvings that burned." Tomás handed me a cup. "He says if the map is destroyed, the route dies with it. Unless people remember it. He says memory is a kind of paper that doesn't burn."

I looked down at the boy — this scrap of a stowaway who'd boarded my boat as cargo and was now delivering intelligence briefings with my own coffee — and I thought about the oilskin case locked in my cabin, and about Renard, whom I hadn't met yet but could feel coming upriver toward me like weather, and about thirty-one people sleeping on my deck because I hadn't been fast enough to say no.

"Go to sleep, Tomás," I said.

"Yes, captain," he said, in a voice that made it clear he considered us partners, and padded off into the dark.

Ahead, far down the black ribbon of the river, a light appeared. Then another. Two lights, traveling together, moving against the current, moving fast.

Black-hulled, I'd have bet the boat on it.
