## 7. Consortium Men

The black-hulled launch appeared an hour after dawn, the way bad news always does — politely, at a distance, giving you time to pretend you hadn't noticed.

I'd been running the Marisol through the long bend below the Devil's Elbow, one hand on the wheel, one hand on the throttle, watching the water go from green to brown to that deep tea color the river takes on when it's been chewing on the high country. Tomás was up in the bow with the lead line, and Mara was below decks, asleep in my bunk because it was the only bunk, her fever finally down to something a body could survive. The oilskin case sat in the engine compartment, wrapped in a spare tarp, wedged between the spare propeller and a crate of spare everything, because I'd learned years ago that the best hiding place on a riverboat is the place that smells like grease and despair.

"Captain," Tomás called. Not *Juniper*, not *ma'am*. Captain. The boy had decided I was a captain the way other children decide a dog is a wolf, and I hadn't yet had the heart to disillusion him. "Boat. Behind us. It's been there since the elbow."

I looked back without looking back, the way you do — a glance at the wake, a glance at the sky, a glance at nothing in particular. The launch sat a quarter mile astern, riding low and mean, its hull painted the black of a beetle's back, its windows smoked. It wasn't fishing. Nothing that clean was fishing.

"Keep sounding," I said. "Call out the depths like you're being paid for it."

"Am I not being paid for it?"

"Paid in experience. It's the currency of the river. Spend it wisely."

He grinned and threw the lead again, and I brought my attention back to the water and the boat behind us and the arithmetic of the situation, which was simple and ugly: a survey launch, black-hulled, following a riverboat carrying the only copy of a map that a mining consortium wanted buried. I didn't need a radio to do that math. I could do it with my hands tied and my eyes shut, which, given how the week was going, might soon be the case.

* * *

They hailed me at noon, off the mouth of the Quebrada Honda, where the current slackens and a captain with any sense would already be tied up for the night. I had no sense left, apparently, so I kept running, and the launch kept pace, patient as a debt.

The voice that came over the water was pleasant. That was the first thing I noticed about it, before I even saw the man. Pleasant, unhurried, with the faint polish of someone who'd learned to speak nicely in a place where nobody else did.

"*Marisol*, Marisol, this is the *Concordia*. Captain Renard. Might I come alongside? I have a business proposition, and I'm told you're the only captain on this river worth making one to."

Flattery, delivered over a megaphone at a hundred meters. I picked up my own horn and answered with the honesty the river deserved.

"Whatever you're selling, I can't afford it."

"On the contrary," Renard said, "I'm buying. And you, Captain Villalobos, can afford a great deal more than you think."

My name. He had my name, and he said it like a man laying a card face-up on a table, not threatening, just showing you he held the rest of the deck.

I ran another hour. He let me. That was the second thing I noticed about Renard: he never pushed when patience would do the work for him. The launch fell back to half a mile, then a quarter, matching my speed with the lazy confidence of something that knew it was faster, stronger, and better fueled than anything I commanded. The Marisol is a good boat. She is not a match for consortium money, and consortium money was what that black hull was made of, every rivet of it.

By late afternoon I'd run out of river worth running. The bend below the Quebrada Honda ends in shallows that would grind my keel to a confession, and night on this stretch means fog off the cold water, and fog means running blind with a feverish woman below and a boy on the bow who deserved better than to die proving my stubbornness. I found a gravel bar behind a screen of alders, backed the Marisol in, and killed the engine.

The silence after was enormous. Then the launch came around the bend, slow and quiet, and put its fenders out, and I understood that I had not, in any meaningful sense, chosen to stop. I had simply arrived where it had already decided I would.

* * *

Renard came aboard alone, which I'll grant him was either courtesy or theater, and possibly both. He was maybe fifty, silver at the temples, dressed in the kind of field clothes that cost more than my boat's entire electrical system and had never once been rained on. He carried a leather folder and a bottle of something amber, and he stepped onto my deck the way a man steps into a church he owns.

"Captain Villalobos. A pleasure."

"It's a small deck," I said. "Make the pleasure quick."

He smiled like I'd said something charming. "You ran the Gorge of Whistling Stones." He said it conversationally, the way you'd remark on the weather. "Three days ago. Against the season, against the warnings, against every scrap of sense. And you came out the other side with a survivor of the Thorne expedition and a sealed case that my employers are extremely interested in."

"Your employers should hire better spotters. Their people went over the falls."

Something moved behind his eyes — not guilt, I don't think he had any, but accounting. Men like Renard don't feel guilt; they file it. "An unfortunate accident. Rivers are dangerous. Which is precisely why my employers prefer to conduct their business with professionals." He set the folder on my chart table, opened it, and turned it around. "Triple your standard rate. For the case, delivered to us at San Vega, contents unexamined by you, unopened, undamaged. Plus a bonus I think you'll find adequate."

The number on the page was not adequate. It was obscene. It was nine years of debt payments and engine overhauls and cheap fuel and cheaper coffee, all of it erased, with enough left over to replate the Marisol's hull in gold, if I'd wanted to be vulgar about it. Which, looking at that number, I briefly did.

"I don't have your case," I said.

"Captain." He said it gently, the way you'd correct a child about Santa Claus. "I watched your deckhand carry a tarp-wrapped bundle below decks at the Gorge. I've read the Thorne expedition's supply manifests, its radio logs, and the insurance claim filed by its parent company. I know what was in the tender when it went over. I know what your survivor was holding when your crew pulled her out of the water." He uncapped the bottle and poured two glasses, uninvited, and pushed one toward me. "I'm not here to insult your intelligence. I'm here because you're the only person on this river who's demonstrated both the capability and the lack of sentiment to make a sensible decision."

I looked at the whiskey. It was very good whiskey. That was the whole problem with Renard in miniature — everything about him was very good, and all of it was bait.

"And if I say no?"

"Then no harm done." He spread his hands, all reason. "We part as friends. I go upstream, you go wherever it is you go. Though—" and here he did the thing I'd known was coming since the black hull first appeared, the thing he'd been carrying in that pleasant voice like a blade in a lined coat — "I do wonder about Port Salinas."

The name landed in my chest like an anchor dropping.

"Nice town," he went on, examining his glass. "My employers have interests there. Fisheries, transport, a certain amount of — call it municipal goodwill. Debts, Captain, are a funny thing. They sit quietly for years, and then someone with resources comes along and buys them, and suddenly the man who owed the money finds his interest rates renegotiated, his payment terms accelerated, his collateral — a family home, I believe, on Calle de las Flores, held in the name of one Rafael Villalobos — suddenly very much in play." He looked up at me, and his eyes were kind the way a trap is kind. "It would be a shame if your refusal to help me cost your brother his house. I'd hate for that to weigh on you."

I will say this for myself: I didn't hit him. It was a near thing — my hand actually started the journey before my brain caught up and turned it into picking up the whiskey glass instead, which I drank in one swallow, partly for the courage and partly so my hands would have something to do that wasn't his throat.

The whiskey was excellent. I hated it.

"You've done your homework," I said.

"It's what I'm paid for. That, and making sure everyone ends up better off than they started." He refilled my glass without asking, which I chose to interpret as a challenge. "Think about it, Captain. The case is paper. Paper is nothing. The villages upstream — and yes, I know about the villages — will be compensated. Relocated. The consortium builds its dam, the valley floods, and in twenty years it's a lake with a pretty name and everyone's forgotten there was ever anything else. That's not villainy. That's progress. You can't stop progress, but you can *profit* from it, and unlike everyone else in this story, I'm offering you the chance."

"And the boy?"

Renard's pleasantness flickered — the first true thing I'd seen on his face. "What boy?"

"The one who was hiding in my cargo. The one your people have presumably been looking for since the basecamp went quiet."

"I have no interest in the boy." Too fast, too flat. "My mandate concerns the survey materials. That's all."

"Then you won't mind if he stays aboard."

"Captain, I mind nothing. I observe, I offer, I conclude." He closed the folder and slid it across the table toward me, leaving it there, the number face-up, patient as the launch in the current. "Keep the offer. Sleep on it. I'll be at San Vega by Thursday; the *Concordia* will be anchored off the fuel dock. If the case is in my hands by Friday noon, the number is yours and your brother's troubles are a memory. If not—" He stood, buttoned his jacket, and smiled the smile of a man who had already won and was simply waiting for the paperwork. "If not, I'll assume you've made your choice, and I'll make mine. No hard feelings either way. That's the difference between us, Captain. I never take anything personally."

"Must be nice," I said. "Taking everything else, though. That seems to be going well for you."

He laughed — a real laugh, damn him, warm and genuine, and I understood suddenly that Renard was the most dangerous kind of man, the kind who genuinely liked people, all of them, the way a fisherman likes fish. He stepped to the rail, paused, and looked back at me with something that in a better man might have been sympathy.

"For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much — I read the file on the flood. Nine years ago. The one at—" he named the town, my town, the one I'd left my heart and my family buried in, and I felt the deck tilt under me though the river was flat as glass. "You were twenty-two. You made the call to open the sluice gates early. Saved the lower quarter, lost the fleet." He shook his head slowly. "The river's been punishing you ever since, hasn't it? Running, running, running. And here I am, offering to buy you out of it. Whatever you decide, Captain — decide it for yourself. Not for the ghost."

He went down the ladder and across to his launch, and the black hull turned in the dusk and headed upstream, running lights winking, and I stood at the rail of my own boat with a folder full of temptation in one hand and a glass of excellent whiskey in the other, hating him more for the kindness than the threat, because the threat I could fight, and the kindness went straight through every defense I'd built in nine years like water through a rotted hull.

* * *

Tomás found me there an hour later. I'd put the folder in the chart drawer, on top of the tide tables, and poured the rest of Renard's whiskey over the side, which felt childish and necessary, and then I'd sat down at the wheel because sitting at the wheel is what I do instead of thinking.

"He offered you money," Tomás said. Not a question. He'd been on the alder bank with the lead line, thirty meters away; sound carries on water.

"He offered me a lot of money."

"For the case."

"For the case."

He sat down on the deck beside my chair, knees up, arms around them, a small dark shape against the last of the light. Downriver, the first stars were coming out over the water, and somewhere upstream, three villages were sleeping in a valley that men in offices had already decided was a future lake bed.

"Are you going to take it?" he asked.

And there it was, the question, in a child's voice with no politeness wrapped around it at all. I thought about the number. I thought about Rafael's house on Calle de las Flores, and my mother's garden, and the debt I'd been paying off from a thousand miles away because paying it off from a hundred miles away hadn't been far enough. I thought about Mara, feverish in my bunk, who'd sent a distress call that had brought me running and brought them hunting. I thought about a boy who'd hidden in a cargo hold to find a sister, and who was now sitting on my deck in the dark, trusting a woman who nine years ago had made one wrong call and drowned a fleet and her own name with it.

"I don't know," I said, because lying to him seemed worse than the truth.

He nodded slowly, accepting this, the way children accept things — completely, and with more grace than adults manage. "Mara's fever came back," he said. "She's asking for you. She says—" he hesitated, and in the dark I heard him choose the words carefully, "—she says you should know what's in the case before you decide. All of it. She says it's not what Renard thinks it is."

I looked down at him. "What is it, then?"

"I don't know," Tomás said. "But I saw her face when she opened it, that first night, before you came. And Captain—" he looked up at me, and his eyes were older than the rest of him by about forty years, "—nobody looks like that over paper. Whatever's in there, Renard's number is wrong. It's either worth nothing, or it's worth everything, and either way he can't afford it."

Below decks, faintly, Mara's voice called my name, frayed with fever and something else — urgency, maybe, or fear, or the particular tone of a woman who has been carrying a secret long enough and has decided, tonight, to set it down.

I put the wheel over to nothing in particular, left the folder sleeping in the drawer with the tide tables, and went below to hear it.
