## 8. Nine Years Downriver

I have never been a woman who dreams in color. My dreams come in water — gray-green, silt-heavy, the exact shade of the Río Cobre the morning it took everything I had left to lose. So when sleep finally dragged me under that night, somewhere below the Gorge of Whistling Stones with an injured botanist sleeping off her fever and a stowaway boy curled against the boiler like a cat that had decided the Marisol was his now, of course the river came for me. It always does.

Nine years is a long time to outrun a memory. You'd think I'd have gotten better at it.

* * *

Port Salinas, nine years ago, smelled like wet adobe and woodsmoke and my mother's bread, which she baked every Sunday whether we could afford the flour or not, because my mother believed that ritual was a form of prayer and prayer was cheaper than flour. I was twenty-two. I knew everything. This is the universal condition of twenty-two-year-olds, but I carried it worse than most, because I also had my grandfather's river charts and my grandfather's certainty, and between those two things I figured the Río Cobre held no surprises for me.

My grandfather, old Aurelio Villalobos, had run boats on that river for fifty years. He used to say the Cobre was a woman he'd loved so long she'd stopped surprising him, and he said it the way men say things they believe will keep them safe. He taught me to read sandbars by the way the current wrinkled over them, to smell rain three valleys away, to know that when the water went quiet — truly quiet, holding its breath — you got your cargo off and you got yourself up the bank, because a quiet river is a river gathering its argument.

The spring of that year, the rains came early and hard. Everyone in the valley said so. The muleteers coming down from San Vega said the high passes were white into June. The old women at the landing said the river was "full of itself." Even the radio man at Port Salinas read out flood warnings for the upper basin in his bored government voice, the voice of a man paid by the announcement and not by the outcome.

And I stood on the deck of the Marisol — half the size she is now, patched, secondhand, my grandfather's pride and my inheritance-in-progress — and I made a calculation.

Here is what I calculated: that the warning was for the upper basin, and we were two days' run below the confluence. That the cargo was mine to deliver, and the contract was mine to honor, and the money was mine to need. That my grandfather's charts showed the high-water channel clear past the Salinas bend. That I was Juniper Villalobos, and the river and I had an understanding.

You can see the shape of it from here. Pride dressed up as competence. Greed wearing duty's coat. Every bad decision I've ever made has had excellent posture.

* * *

What people don't understand about floods — what I didn't understand, standing there with my hand on the wheel feeling like the smartest creature on the water — is that a flood isn't a wave. It's not dramatic, not at first. It's bookkeeping. The river keeps accounts all through the wet season, and when the ledger fills, it calls in its debts all at once, downstream, where nobody who caused them is watching.

The storm hit the upper valley in the night. By dawn the tributaries were running brown and thick as batter. By noon, the gauge post at the confluence — a pole some forgotten Villalobos had driven into the bank three generations back, marked with notches for every flood year — was underwater to the last notch and then underwater past the last notch, into territory the pole had never been asked to measure.

I should have tied off at the bend. There was a backwater there, deep and sheltered, a place my grandfather had named *El Refugio* on his charts because that's what it was. Every boat on the river knew it. Every boat on the river was in it, that afternoon, except mine.

But the Salinas landing had a crane, and the crane cost money by the hour, and if I waited out the flood I'd miss my delivery window, and if I missed my window I'd forfeit the contract, and if I forfeited the contract — well. You see how the arithmetic goes. It always comes out justifying whatever you already wanted to do.

So I ran the Marisol downriver through rising water, alone at the wheel because I'd sent my brother Mateo ashore at the bend with the last of the light cargo. He didn't want to go. He stood on the landing with his arms crossed, seventeen years old and stubborn as our whole line put together, and he said, "Junie, the water's wrong."

"The water's fine," I told him. "Get the truck loaded. I'll be there by dark."

He went. That's the part that lives in me like a splinter too deep to draw — he went, because he trusted me, because I was the one who read rivers, because I had never once been wrong about the water in front of him.

I was wrong about the water.

* * *

A debris jam let go somewhere above the confluence around four in the afternoon. I learned this later, reconstructed it later, from witnesses and wreckage and the long terrible arithmetic of hindsight. A raft of logs and torn-up bank, backed up behind a bend for hours, finally shoved loose by the weight behind it, and it came down the Cobre like a moving island, thirty feet wide, grinding everything in its path.

I heard it before I saw it. That's the thing nobody tells you. A flood doesn't arrive quietly. It arrives like a train made of trees.

I had maybe ninety seconds. Ninety seconds to make a choice that I have spent nine years re-choosing, every night, in gray-green water. Ahead of me was the Salinas landing, half a mile down, where Mateo stood with a truck full of cargo and a dozen neighbors helping him load. Behind me was open bank — scrub and rock, no dock, no people, nothing but the Marisol and me.

If I ran for the landing, I might beat the debris there. Might. And if I did, I'd be putting a wall of floating timber into a crowded dock at flood speed. If I turned for the bank, I'd wreck my boat — my grandfather's boat, our livelihood, the thing that fed us — and lose the cargo, and lose the season, and probably still lose the boat entirely, because the Marisol wasn't built to take that kind of beating broadside.

I did the arithmetic. I want to be very clear about that, because for nine years I let myself believe I'd panicked, and panic would have been a mercy. I didn't panic. I stood at the wheel with the rain hammering the canopy and I added it up, and the sum I got was: the boat survives if I run for the landing, and the boat is worth more than the risk.

The sum was wrong. The sum treated people as a variable I could round down.

I turned the wheel toward home.

* * *

I never reached the landing. The debris caught me at the mouth of the bend, and after that I was cargo myself — a woman clinging to a wheel while the river played dice with forty tons of wood and iron. I remember fragments. The mast going. The hull hitting something that rang like a bell. Water over the deck, fast and black, tearing the hatch covers off like lids off a tin. And then — this is the part I've never told anyone, not Mateo, not anyone — a moment, just a moment, when the Marisol swung broadside in the current and I looked downriver and saw the landing, and saw the people scattering off it, and understood with a clarity I would give anything to have been wrong about that the thing I'd steered toward was the worst place on the river for them to be.

They lived. All of them. Mateo grabbed the two Reyes children and got them up the bluff path with seconds to spare; Doña Inés broke her hip on the stairs and cursed me for it for a decade, which I earned. Nobody died that day on the landing.

But the Marisol broke apart on the shoal below the bend, and the cargo — fertilizer and fuel drums bound for the co-op — went into the flood, and the fuel drums went into the flood, and the flood carried them into the lower fields, and the lower fields were planted, and the oil slicked the irrigation channels for two villages' worth of cropland. Nobody died. But that winter, two villages ate charity rice, and the co-op folded, and the debts that the co-op couldn't pay went looking for someone to live inside.

They found my family. Because the cargo was ours, contracted in our name, insured by a policy whose fine print my grandfather's eyes had been too tired to read and my eyes had been too proud to bother with. *Acts of God,* the insurance company called the flood. *Negligence,* their lawyers called my routing decision, and the worst of it — the part that kept me awake for years — was that they were right. Not legally right, even. Just true. I had chosen the boat over the bank. I had done the arithmetic with people rounded down.

* * *

Funerals are strange things when nobody died. Ours was for the boat, more or less — my grandfather walked the shoal where her bones lay scattered and picked up pieces of her for a week, saying nothing, until the day he found the nameboard with MARISOL half worn away, and sat down on the shingle and didn't get up for a long time. He was eighty-one. He never piloted again. He gave me his charts, though. Rolled them up and put them in my hands and said, "You'll need these more than I will," and I have never known whether it was a blessing or a sentence.

Mateo wouldn't look at me. That was the flood underneath the flood. For months he spoke to me only through our mother, and our mother passed his silences along wrapped in excuses — *he's tired, he's young, he doesn't mean it* — until the day he did mean it, and said it plain, standing in the doorway of the house while I packed.

"You did the sums," he said. "You always do the sums. And we were never in them."

I have thought about that sentence every day for nine years. Some days it's a knife. Most days it's a compass.

Because here's what I understood eventually, though it took me most of a decade and a great deal of river to get there: I didn't leave Port Salinas to escape the debt. The debt was real — the consortium that bought up the co-op's paper bought ours with it, and they collected faithfully, and they're collecting still, through men like the one who waits for me in ports with contracts and smiles — but debt is just money, and money is the least of what I owed. I left because I couldn't stand to be looked at by people who had seen me choose wrong. I left because staying meant being the woman who did the sums, forever, and I hadn't yet learned that the only way out of that woman was through her.

So I ran. I worked decks from here to the coast and back, saved, borrowed, begged, and in five years I had enough — barely, ugly, with interest — to buy a broken steamboat off a salvage yard and rebuild her plank by plank. And when it came time to name her, I stood with the brush in my hand for an hour, and then I painted MARISOL across the bow in my grandfather's letters, because penance needs a name you can't hide from.

Every run since has been the same bargain. Keep moving, keep working, keep the payments flowing upstream to men who never lost a night's sleep over anything, and tell yourself it's freedom. Tell yourself the river is yours because you chose it. Nine years of telling myself that, and do you know how you can tell the difference between freedom and penance?

Freedom sleeps well.

* * *

I woke in the gray hour before dawn with my hands aching from gripping a wheel that wasn't there, and lay in my bunk listening to the Marisol breathe around me — the tick of cooling iron, the mutter of the bank current, Tomás's slow child-sleeping breath from the engine room where he'd insisted on bedding down, and Mara Quill's feverish murmur from the settee I'd given up for her.

Penance, I thought. Nine years of penance, and I'd mistaken it for a life.

But lying there in the dark, something else surfaced alongside the old shame, something new, and it troubled me more than the dream did. Because the arithmetic I'd done at that wheel nine years ago — the cold, clean, self-serving arithmetic that rounded people down — I'd felt its shadow again lately. On the night Renard's launch first showed up, I'd lain awake doing sums: what the case was worth, what my debt was, what my freedom would cost the people sleeping ten feet from me. And I'd hated the sums, but I'd done them. The habit doesn't die just because you feed it different numbers.

Mara coughed in her sleep. Tomás sighed and rolled against the boiler.

And upstream, above the Gorge of Whistling Stones, the river went on keeping its accounts — and I had a bad, familiar feeling, the feeling of a gauge post slipping under its last notch, that sooner or later this voyage was going to ask me to do the sums again.

This time, I promised the dark, I'd put everybody in them.

Then I got up, lit the boiler, and took the Marisol into the fog — because whatever the river was gathering its argument about, we were running straight into it.
