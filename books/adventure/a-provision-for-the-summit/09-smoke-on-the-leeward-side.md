## 9. Smoke on the Leeward Side

I want it known that I did not cry when the depot burned.

There were witnesses. The mule boy saw me standing on the Marisol's rail with my fists in my pockets like a man reading a weather report, and I will deny to my grave that my eyes were doing anything but squinting against the smoke. Squinting is a thing boys do. Crying is a thing I had done exactly once since stowing away aboard this boat, and that was at night, into a coil of rope, where nobody could prove anything.

The smoke came up over the leeward bank of the river around midmorning, a black column leaning east like a drunk uncle, and Captain Villalobos saw it before any of us because she sees everything before everyone. She was at the wheel with her boots planted wide and her jaw doing the thing it does when she's counting trouble.

"That's Herrera's yard," she said.

"Who's Herrera?" Mara asked from under the awning, where she'd been lying very still and pretending her shoulder didn't hurt. She was good at pretending. Not as good as she thought.

"Fuels the whole upper river. Diesel, kerosene, lamp oil." Juniper turned the wheel a spoke and the Marisol leaned into the current like she was trying to see around the bend herself. "If that's burning—"

It was burning. We rounded the last bluff and there was the depot, three long sheds and a dock, all of it wearing a crown of orange flame, and black water underneath where the runoff oil made rainbow skins that caught fire too, little blue tongues dancing on the surface like the river itself had been set alight. Boats sat along the far bank in a helpless row — a fuel barge, two cargo launches, a fishing skiff — every one of them stranded, because you can't run an engine on wishes.

And tied up at the smoking dock, black hull shining like a beetle, was Renard's launch.

"There," I said. My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "That's them."

"I see it, Tomás."

"They're just *sitting* there."

"Of course they're sitting there." Juniper's voice was flat as a coin. "You don't burn a man's livelihood and then run. You stay so he understands who did it. That's not destruction, that's a message."

Mara pushed herself up on her good arm. "What's the message?"

Juniper watched the launch for a long moment. A man stood on its deck in a pale suit, and even across the water I could feel him looking at us, unhurried, like we were a parcel he'd already signed for.

"*Turn inland,*" Juniper said. "*There's nowhere else.*"

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being eleven: adults think you are furniture that talks. They plan things right in front of you, assuming the words slide off like rain off tin, and then they get surprised — actually offended — when it turns out you heard everything and understood most of it and have opinions about the rest.

So when Juniper killed the engine and let us drift with the current, well back of the burning dock, and held a council on the aft deck, I sat on the coil of rope — my rope, the one I'd cried into, which made it mine twice over — and listened.

"The portage road starts at San Vega," Juniper said. "Two days' walk if the mules are honest, four if they aren't. From there we climb."

"Climb *where?*" Mara asked.

"Up. Toward the pass. Toward wherever your map says your villages are safe." She rubbed her face with both hands, and for a second she looked older than anyone I'd ever seen, older than the mountains themselves. "I'm a river captain, Mara. I don't know the first thing about mountains except that they kill people who arrive cocky."

"You know more than that," Mara said. "You know the river's closed."

They all looked downstream, the way you look at a door that's just been locked. Because that was the message, wasn't it. With Herrera's yard burned, there was no fuel above Port Salinas, no fuel below it either until the consortium's own barges came up — and those barges would come asking questions with Renard standing behind them. Every boat on the Río Cobre was now a piece of driftwood. Except ours, because our tanks were still better than half full, and half full only meant we could choose where to be stranded.

"He did this because of us," Mara said quietly. "Because I sent that distress call. Because he knows what we're carrying."

"He did this because he's a man who burns things when buying them gets expensive," Juniper said. "Don't flatter yourself into his motives. It makes people important who should be small."

But her eyes went to the oilskin case lashed under the wheel box, and I saw her hand twitch toward it and stop, like touching it cost something.

"We could give it to him," Mara said.

"No."

"Mara—" Juniper started.

"No, I mean it. Listen to me." Mara sat up straighter, and sweat stood out on her forehead, and I noticed — because I notice things; it's my whole job aboard this boat, noticing things, since apparently nobody hired me to do anything else — that her wound dressing needed changing and she knew it and wasn't saying so. "He'll follow us up that mountain regardless. He has mules and men and money. What we have is a botanist with a hole in her, a captain who hates land, and—" she glanced at me, and I stared back with the blank dignity of furniture — "a boy."

"A boy with excellent hearing," I said.

Everyone ignored me, which was fine. Being underestimated is a weapon. I'd read that somewhere, or decided it, which amounts to the same thing.

"We keep moving," Juniper said finally. "San Vega by tomorrow night. And Tomás—" and here she looked at me directly, which almost never happened and always felt like being caught, "you stay close. You hear me? Close means *close*, not 'within shouting distance while I investigate something interesting.'"

"I never investigate things."

"You investigated the hold. You investigated my cabin. You investigated the fish trap at the last anchorage and got us two catfish and a leech."

"The catfish were worth the leech," I said, "and I stand by that."

Something happened to her mouth then, a twitch at the corner that on another person would have been a smile. On Juniper Villalobos it was more like a smile thinking about applying for a permit.

* * *

We ran the boat hard through the afternoon, hugging the shadowed bank, putting miles between us and the black hull. I took the bow spotter's position because it was mine now — nobody gave it to me, I just kept being right about rocks, and eventually being right becomes a job title whether anyone signs the paperwork or not.

The fever found Mara sometime after sunset.

I found her first, actually, which matters to me and I'm writing it down so it stays mattered. I'd brought her the beans Juniper cooked — plain beans, no salt, because salt costs money and Juniper counts money the way misers count teeth — and Mara didn't take the bowl. Her eyes were open but pointed at some middle distance where I wasn't, and her skin had gone the color of the inside of a shell.

"Mara?"

"Hot," she said. "Isn't it hot?"

It was not hot. The night off the Cordillera foothills had a knife-edge chill coming down off the snowline, the kind that makes you grateful for blankets and suspicious of your own breath.

I put the back of my hand on her forehead, the way my mother used to, back when there was a mother and a back-of-hand. Mara's skin was a stove top. She flinched and murmured something in a language I didn't know, maybe two languages, and her hand came up and gripped my wrist with a strength that frightened me more than the heat did, because weak people shouldn't be able to grip like that. It meant the body was spending everything it had.

"Juniper!" I shouted, and my voice cracked in the middle like a dropped plate, and I hated that, but I shouted again anyway. "JUNIPER!"

* * *

They worked on her through the night, Juniper and the lantern and the medical kit that had more ambition than contents. I stayed. Nobody told me to leave and nobody told me to stay, so I stayed, sitting against the cabin wall with my knees up, watching.

The wound in Mara's shoulder had gone angry. Red spread out from the stitches in a map of its own, hot-edged, and when Juniper cleaned it Mara cried out words that were mostly apologies, which was somehow worse than screaming would have been. Sorry, sorry, sorry, like the infection was her fault, like fever was a debt she'd taken on.

"It's tracking," Juniper muttered. "Needs proper medicine. Penicillin or the herb tea Ilsa-type remedies, something stronger than what I've got, which is iodine and optimism."

"Do you have optimism?" I asked.

"I have iodine."

In the lantern light, with her sleeves rolled and her hands steady, Juniper Villalobos looked like a different woman than the one who counted coins and refused moorings and kept everyone at oar's length. She looked like someone who had done this before, or something like this, somewhere, for someone. I filed that away. Filing things away was becoming a habit. My head was getting crowded.

Toward dawn the fever broke a little — not broke, Juniper said, just *stepped back to reload* — and Mara slept, really slept, and Juniper sat back on her heels and looked ten years older than she had at sunset.

"You should sleep," I told her.

"So should you."

"I slept. Hours ago. You weren't watching."

One eyebrow went up. "Is that so."

"Somebody has to watch. You're busy keeping her alive and steering the boat and hating Renard, and Mara's busy being sick, so somebody has to watch." I said it lightly, the way you toss a stone across water, but it landed heavy and we both heard it splash.

Juniper looked at me for a long time. In the grey light before sunrise her face did several things in sequence, none of which she approved of, and settled on something tired and almost gentle.

"Tomás," she said. "You're eleven."

"I'm aware."

"Children aren't supposed to keep watch over adults. That's backwards. That's the whole design of the world running in reverse."

"Well," I said, and here is the truest thing I said that whole week, maybe the truest thing I'd said in a year, since the night I climbed into a crate of survey equipment and pulled the lid over myself: "the world ran backwards on me already. Somebody took Luz. Everybody else is busy. So it's me."

She didn't argue. I respected her for that. Adults who argue with true things are the worst kind, and there were enough of those upstream in a pale suit.

Instead she reached over and — this is the part I almost don't write down, because written down it becomes real and permanent — she put her hand on top of my head, briefly, rough palm, like blessing a deckhand, and said, "Then watch well. Wake me if her breathing goes shallow. Shallow means wet sounds underneath. You'll know it when you hear it."

"You're giving me a medical job."

"I'm giving you a true job. There's a difference and you know it."

There was a difference. I'd been collecting fake jobs my whole life — *go fetch, go sit, go be quiet* — and this one had weight to it, actual weight, the kind you feel in your arms. I took it the way you take a live coal: carefully, and with both hands.

* * *

San Vega showed up out of the morning haze like a town that had been waiting for us without enthusiasm: a straggle of adobe houses, a mule corral, a church with a bell tower leaning like it had opinions about verticality. Above it all the Cordillera del Viento stood up white and enormous, so tall it made the sky look near enough to touch, and cold poured off those peaks into the valley like water off a table edge.

Juniper tied up at San Vega's rickety pier, and the news was waiting for us before our lines were even fast. Word travels faster than boats; it travels faster than fire, probably. The depot burning had reached the village overnight, and with it, embroidered in the telling, the name of the black-hulled launch and the name of the boat that had been seen turning away from the flames.

An old man selling oranges looked at the Marisol, then at me, and spat with great precision into the water.

"Consortium's men were here yesterday," he said to Juniper, conversationally, the way you discuss weather. "Bought mules. All the good ones. Paid triple."

Juniper went very still. "How many men?"

"Eight. Maybe nine. A soft-spoken one in charge, pale suit, gloves." The orange seller shrugged. "Headed up the porter's road toward the pass."

Which meant Renard wasn't chasing us anymore. He didn't need to chase. He was ahead of us, on the only road that existed, having bought the only mules worth riding, and every hour we spent finding animals and supplies was an hour he spent climbing.

Juniper stood on her deck looking up at that impossible white wall of mountain, and I watched her do arithmetic with her face — fuel, food, mules, fever, days — and lose the sum twice.

"We walk," she said finally. "We strip the engine, take the irreplaceable parts as trade goods, and we walk."

"The Marisol?" I asked. My voice did the cracking thing again. I'd lived on rivers my whole short life, and even I knew what leaving a boat felt like, and I'd only been aboard six weeks.

"She'll wait," Juniper said. But her hand was flat against the wheel, the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping animal's neck, and I noticed, because noticing is my job, that she said *she'll wait* the way people say things they need to believe.

* * *

While the adults argued with muleteers — an activity involving much shouting, spitting, and arithmetic, all conducted at maximum volume — I sat with Mara in the shade of the awning and did my watching.

Her fever had climbed again with the morning. She talked in her sleep, mostly names, mostly places: *Quill station, the ridge transect, Elias, Elias, you promised the specimens would go to the university, you promised.* Once she said my name, clear as a bell, and I nearly fell off my crate.

"Tomás," she'd said. "Watch the transect line."

"I'm watching," I told her, though she was asleep. "I watch everything. It's my job. It's a true job."

A muleteer's daughter, maybe eight years old, stopped to stare at me from the pier. Kids always stare at me; I've decided it's because I look like someone with responsibilities.

"Is she your mother?" the girl asked, nodding at Mara.

"No."

"Your sister?"

"No."

"Then what is she?"

I opened my mouth to say *nothing, she's nothing, she's a botanist I met when I was hiding in a crate* and discovered the sentence wouldn't come out, because it had become a lie somewhere between my chest and my tongue without asking my permission.

"I don't know yet," I said.

The girl considered this with the gravity of a supreme court. "My grandmother says you get to pick some people," she offered. "Not family. The other kind. She says picking them is the important part."

Then she left, because eight-year-olds are busy, and I sat there holding that sentence like it was heavier than the oilskin case.

Because here's what I'd figured out, lying in the dark of the hold those first nights, listening to the river slap the hull: everybody on this boat was running from something, and running people don't look back, and people who don't look back can't see the ones following them. Juniper ran from a town and a flood and a brother. Mara ran from a promise she'd broken. I ran from a silence where Luz used to be.

But somewhere between the Gorge and the burning depot and this dusty pier, without anybody voting on it, we had stopped running in different directions. We were running the same way now. Together. Which meant somebody had to watch the rear, and the rear was where all the danger lived, and the adults were all wounded or grieving or doing arithmetic they couldn't win.

So it would be me. Fine. I was small and quick and invisible and I remembered everything, and nobody — not Renard, not his glove-wearing soft-spoken men, not the mountain itself — had ever once looked at a scrawny boy on a rope coil and thought *that one's dangerous.*

That was going to be their mistake. I decided it right there, sitting in the shade with a fevered woman breathing too fast beside me and white peaks watching from the north like patient gods: I would protect these people. Not because they asked. Because they were mine now, the picked kind, the important kind, and I had already lost one person to men in pale suits, and I would sooner grow gills and live in the river than lose another.

"Tomás," Mara murmured, surfacing halfway from her fever. Her eyes found me, unfocused but finding me anyway. "Why are you smiling?"

"I'm not smiling."

"You are. It looks strange on you. Like sunshine on the north side of a mountain."

"Sleep," I said, and tucked the blanket higher around her shoulders, and when she sank back down I let the smile stay, because there was nobody watching except the mountains, and mountains keep secrets.

Behind us, downriver, the smoke from Herrera's yard had finally thinned to a smudge on the horizon. Ahead of us, the porter's road climbed out of San Vega in a series of switchbacks, vanishing into cloud, and somewhere up that road went nine men, good mules, and a soft-spoken man in gloves who thought he'd already won.

I touched the rope coil next to me — my rope, my post, my watch — and waited for the adults to finish shouting about mule prices.

We had a mountain to climb.
