## 10. The Porter's Road

I want it noted, for the record I am apparently keeping inside my own skull these days, that I did not want to leave the river.

The river and I had an arrangement. It carried me; I studied it; neither of us asked the other to be anything we weren't. A river doesn't ask where you're from. A river doesn't notice that you flinched when the radio said *Quill, M., next of kin unlisted*. A river just moves, and you move with it, and as long as you're moving you are a botanist with a wound, not a woman with a history.

San Vega ended all that. San Vega was a town with a plaza and a church and a woman selling oranges who looked at me exactly the way my mother used to look at me when I came in muddy, which is to say with a love so conditional it might as well have been arithmetic. Fever does that. Fever turns every stranger into family, and family into a tribunal.

"You're burning up," Juniper said, for the fourth time that morning.

"I'm *acclimating*," I said, for the fourth time, and ruined it entirely by sitting down on a bollard that turned out not to be a bollard but a pile of rope, and staying down a while.

The Marisol sat on the bank above the town like a whale someone had decided to teach to fly. Juniper had her stripped to the bones — plating off, engine out, everything that could be unbolted unbolted and crated. The engine was the heart of it: a Cordero marine block, hand-machined, the last of its line. Juniper talked about it the way other people talk about a deceased parent. "There are maybe four of these left on the river," she told the muleteers, as though they'd asked. "You can't buy one. You can only inherit one, or steal one, and I've done neither, so we carry it like a saint's finger."

The muleteers were three brothers named Fuentes and a silent nephew who was either seventeen or forty-five depending on the light. The eldest, Bardo Fuentes, chewed on Juniper's price for an hour and finally agreed to take us up the Porter's Road into the Cordillera del Viento for the engine's carburetor and a promise. He looked at the carburetor the way a man looks at a pearl he suspects is cursed.

"Nobody carries machinery up that road," he said. "The road carries *people*. That's why it's called what it's called."

"Then we'll be an exception."

"Exceptions freeze on the mountain, señora."

"Captain," said Juniper, and Bardo shrugged the shrug of a man who has buried enough clients to stop correcting them.

* * *

Here is what fever does, in case you've been lucky. It doesn't take you somewhere else. It takes *here* and folds it, so that the plaza in San Vega is also the courtyard in Valdemoro where I grew up, and the muleteer's nephew is my brother Tomás-the-elder, who is dead, and the smell of the mules is the smell of the herbarium, which is the smell of safety, except the herbarium is burning now, in my head, because everything I have ever loved has a way of catching fire in the last chapter.

I lost most of a day that way. When I came back, I was in a bed — an actual bed, in the back room of a house that smelled of maize and lamp oil — and Tomás the younger was sitting beside me with a wet cloth, wringing it out with the concentration of a surgeon.

"You were talking," he said.

"About what?"

"A fern. You were apologizing to it."

"I stand by that. I left a *Cyathea* specimen in a press at basecamp. It had been waiting eleven years to be described. Now it's under a reservoir."

He wrung the cloth. He had gotten good at wringing the cloth. Somewhere in the last week he had appointed himself my nurse and no one had voted on it, and I had the distinct impression that in Tomás's internal government, no votes were required.

"Juniper's arguing with the Fuentes about the route," he said. "Ilsa's name came up. They got quiet."

"Ilsa Berríos," I said. The name arrived in my mouth with its own weather. "Mara — I mean, I read her monograph. *Routes of the High Cordillera*. She walked every pass on this range before I was born. If she's alive—"

"She's alive. Bardo says she lives above the tree line and comes down twice a year to insult people."

"Then we insult her gently and ask for her help."

Tomás almost smiled. He was learning to do it, the way you learn a language late in life — correctly but without the accent. Then his face did the other thing, the thing it did whenever the world went quiet, and I knew he was thinking of Luz. I put my hand over his where it held the cloth.

"We'll get her back," I said. I had no right to say it. I said it anyway. Fever makes you generous with other people's hope.

* * *

We left San Vega on the third morning, and I will tell you honestly that the first hour of the Porter's Road was the hardest walking I have done in my life, and I once spent six weeks cataloguing a cloud forest where the trail was mostly a rumor.

It wasn't the altitude. It was the sound. Or rather the absence of it. Nine days on the Marisol and my ears had learned the river's grammar — the slap of water against the hull, the engine's low argument with itself, Juniper's bare feet on the deck boards at four in the morning because the woman apparently didn't believe in shoes or sleep. Take that away and the world went mute as a held breath. Just gravel under boots. Just mule bells. Just my own pulse, which the fever had tuned to a drum I couldn't stop hearing.

A river keeps you anonymous. A mountain does the opposite. On the river you are a boat; on a mountain you are a body, and every body is somebody's. Every switchback I climbed, I could feel myself being reassembled into Mara Quill of Valdemoro, daughter of, sister of, the girl who left. The altitude was stripping my cover story off me in layers, like a mule shedding its winter coat.

"Talk to me," Juniper said, falling into step beside me on the second day. She had a way of appearing at exactly the moment I was worst. I suspect she counted my breaths from a distance and had a threshold.

"I'm fine."

"You're gray."

"I'm *acclimating*."

"You said that in San Vega and then apologized to a fern for forty minutes."

"That fern deserved it."

She grinned, and then, because Juniper Villalobos cannot stand a quiet more than about ninety seconds, she said: "What's a *Cyathea*?"

And that was the trap, and I walked into it with my eyes open, because the alternative was silence and silence on that road meant the tribunal in my head. So I talked. I told her about tree ferns that predate the mountains they grow on, spores that sail on wind older than countries. I told her about the valley we'd left — the three villages, Río Arriba and Dos Aguas and little Corazón del Monte, whose names would exist only in land registries if the consortium got its dam. I told her about the survey, and what we'd actually found under the ridge, and how Elias Thorne had gone from a man I'd have followed into a swamp to a man who looked at me across a map table and said, *the villages are a rounding error, Mara* — and I had said nothing. That was the part I told last, and quietest. That I had said nothing, and then I had sent the distress call that brought Renard's people down on us, and both silences had cost somebody.

Juniper listened the way she piloted — reading the current under the words, not the words.

"You know what I did," she said, when I ran out. "Nine years ago. You've heard it from Tomás, probably."

"He said there was a flood."

"There was a flood. There was a levee I said was sound. There was a town that believed me because I was good with water, the way you were good with plants." She picked up a stone and threw it off the trail, into the drop, and we both listened to it not land for a long time. "So we're a boat of liars, you and I. Good. Liars make the best witnesses. We know exactly how the trick is done."

I laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough put me on my knees in the gravel, and the fever took me again.

* * *

The fever, when it took me, took me *up*. That's the strange mercy of it. The road went on climbing whether I walked it or not, because in the fever I was always walking — through the courtyard in Valdemoro, through the flooded street in Juniper's town which was somehow also mine, through the herbarium with its burning cabinets, and always, at the end of every dream-corridor, a door with water under it.

In the lucid hours I learned what the others did while I slept.

Tomás carried me. That was the fact I had to sit with, in the cold blue hour before dawn when the fever loosened its grip and gave me back my arithmetic. A boy of maybe twelve had been carrying me, slung across his shoulders like a pack, up a mountain, because he had decided I was his. He had claimed me the way you claim a seat on a crowded boat — quietly, without asking, with an elbow. I asked him once, in one of those hours, why.

"You looked at the crates," he said. "Back at the loading dock. Everybody else looked at the money or the river. You looked at the crates, and you knew something was wrong, and you didn't say anything to anybody." He wrung the cloth. He was always wringing the cloth. "That's how I knew you'd keep me."

"Tomás, I didn't even see you."

"You saw the *crates*." As if that settled it. As if noticing was the whole of love, and maybe he was right.

Juniper, in my lucid hours, was a study in a woman arguing with a machine. She had the engine's cylinders laid out on a canvas sheet at camp like organs on a surgeon's table, and every evening she cleaned them, and every morning she repacked them, and every day she looked at the road ahead and the mules' backs and did the terrible arithmetic of weight. Bardo Fuentes had told her on the second day: *the engine or the food, señora. Not both.* And she had said, *Captain,* the way she did, and Bardo had said, *the mountain doesn't care what you call yourself,* and for once she hadn't had an answer.

On the fifth day she sold the crankshaft.

I want to be clear about what that meant, because I didn't understand it myself until Tomás explained it to me, with the flat gravity of a child delivering bad news. The crankshaft was the one part that could not be replaced, remade, or improvised. Without it the Marisol was a very expensive piece of driftwood. Juniper traded it to a mule-train master coming down from the high country for grain, a spare mule, and — this was the part that mattered — information. The train master had come down the western approach. He had seen a black-hulled launch's crew at the trailhead, outfitted for altitude, moving slow but moving. Renard hadn't stopped at the river. Renard was climbing.

I watched Juniper hand over the crankshaft. She held it a moment longer than a sale requires — the way you hold a door handle before leaving a house you know you won't see again. Then she passed it across, took the grain, and said, "Pleasure doing business," in a voice so level that Bardo Fuentes looked at her with something close to reverence.

That night I was lucid enough to sit up by the fire, and she sat next to me, and for a while we both watched the sparks go up.

"You gave up the boat," I said.

"I gave up a *part*," she said. "The boat's the plating and the name and the woman who steers her. I'm still here." But her hand had gone, without her noticing, to the empty place on her belt where the crankshaft's key had hung, and I didn't say anything, because I recognized the gesture. It was the way I touched the pocket where my field notebook used to be, before the tender went over, before the river took my eleven years of *Cyathea* and my pressed ferns and my careful, anonymous life.

We were both of us carrying our own disassembled hearts up that mountain, trading them off piece by piece for altitude.

* * *

The tree line went on the sixth day, and the world turned to bone and lichen, and the cold stopped being weather and became a resident. My fever broke that afternoon — properly broke, all at once, like a knot coming loose — and I woke from a nap against a grain sack to find that the mountains had been waiting for me specifically. They stood all around in the last gold light, white-shouldered, patient, enormous, and I understood with a clarity that had nothing to do with fever that I had never in my life been anywhere this honest. The river had lied for me. The mountain would not. Up here you were exactly as strong, exactly as frightened, exactly as loved as you were, and there was no current to blame it on.

"Better?" Juniper asked.

"Worse," I said. "Clearer. Same thing up here."

"Welcome to my last nine years."

Bardo called a halt early, at a shelf of stone with a spring, and while we made camp the silent nephew — whose name, it emerged, was Anselmo, and whose silence, it further emerged, was policy rather than nature — came and stood in front of me holding out a strip of dried meat, which was, I gathered, a speech.

"Your boy," Bardo said, jerking his chin at Tomás, who was out on the rocks teaching Luz's name to the wind again — I'd heard him; we all had; he said it like a coordinate, like a fix on a star he was steering by. "He spotted the condors an hour before we smelled the smoke. He's got eyes. Where'd a river boy get eyes like that?"

"Where'd a mountain man get a nephew who talks?" Juniper said, and Bardo laughed, and the matter was dropped, but the look he gave Tomás stayed with me. It was the look of a man recalculating. We were not, in his ledger, cargo anymore. We were becoming passengers, and passengers, on the Porter's Road, were people you might have to make decisions about.

I slept that night without the tribunal. In the morning, Anselmo shook me awake before first light, and put a finger to his lips, and pointed east.

The sky over the pass was the wrong color. Not the rose of dawn — a bruised, iron rose, and above it, where the sky should have been empty, a plume of cloud that was not cloud, standing up off the far slope like a dark flag.

Bardo stood beside me with his hands on his hips, chewing on something that wasn't there.

"Smoke," he said. "Leeward side. That's the fuel depot at the halfway house, or it's the halfway house." He spat. "Either way, señora — *capitana* — the road ahead of us just got shorter, and the men behind us just got closer, and I need to know which one of those you plan to be when we meet them."

Juniper was already awake. She was already dressed. She was looking at the smoke the way she read water — not at the surface, but at what was moving underneath it.

"Both," she said.
