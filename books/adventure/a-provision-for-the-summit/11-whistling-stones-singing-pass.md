## 11. Whistling Stones, Singing Pass

I have been guiding people into the Cordillera del Viento for fifty-one years, and in that time I have learned three things that keep a body alive up there. One: the mountain does not hate you, but it does not know you exist, and you should take neither personally. Two: never trust a client who calls the trail "a hike." Three: when a stranger comes to your door at dusk with fevered friends and a look like a debt unpaid, feed them first and ask questions second, because questions go down easier on a full stomach.

So when the knock came at my door in San Vega — a thin knock, the kind a person makes when they are ashamed to be asking — I was already ladling out the stew.

There were six of them on my step. A riverwoman with salt in her manner and river-mud to her knees, holding herself like someone who had been steering against current so long she'd forgotten what still water felt like. A botanist, white around the mouth with fever, leaning on a boy. A boy holding a girl's hand — his sister, I would learn, though at first I took them for twins, they had the same stubborn set to their chins. A muleteer's son I knew by sight. And an old man, one of the villagers from up the valley, with the particular silence of a man who has been made a spokesman against his will and is still deciding whether to resent it.

"You're Berríos," the riverwoman said. Not a question. "They said if anyone can get us over the pass before snow, it's you."

"They say a lot of things in San Vega," I said. "Half of them about me, and most of those lies. Come in before the stew learns manners worse than yours."

I am an old woman and I have earned the right to be rude to people I am about to save. It is one of the few perquisites of the position, and I exercise it fully.

* * *

The fevered one — Quill, the riverwoman called her, a botanist, which explained the satchel of pressed plants she would not let anyone touch even half-conscious — I put to bed by my stove with willow bark and llama tallow poultices on the wound in her side. A bullet graze, or near enough. I have seen enough of both to know the difference, and this one was near enough to make me look at the rest of them with new attention.

The boy would not leave her side. He sat on the floor with his back against the bed frame like a small dog that has decided a thing is his to guard, and when I brought the bowl he thanked me in the careful voice of a child who learned early that adults can be weather — sometimes kind, sometimes not, never predictable.

"You're not her kin," I said. It was not an accusation. I have never had much patience for the idea that blood is the only ledger that matters.

"No," he said. And then, after a silence in which he seemed to weigh something heavier than a boy his size should carry: "But I'm going to be."

Well. I have heard worse life plans. Mine, at his age, had been to marry the blacksmith's son and never leave the valley, and look how that turned out — the blacksmith's son dead in a mine collapse and me fifty years a widow of a man I never actually married. Children make their plans with the materials at hand. His materials were a fevered stranger and a riverboat. It was as good a foundation as any I'd seen.

The riverwoman — Villalobos, Juniper Villalobos, and yes, I knew the name, San Vega is not so far from Port Salinas that names don't travel, and nine years is not so long that a scandal stops being told — the riverwoman stood at my window with her arms crossed, watching the street, watching the ridgeline, watching everything, the way a person watches when they have learned that trouble arrives from every direction at once.

"You're being followed," I said.

"You noticed that too, huh."

"A black-hulled launch came up the river two days ago asking after a survey party. The men wore good boots. Good boots on the river means money, and money on this river lately means the consortium." I ladled more stew into her bowl without asking. "They also asked after a woman named Villalobos with a debt in Port Salinas. I mention it only so you know the whole village knows, and the whole village has decided, on the evidence, that we don't like the men in good boots any better than we like the consortium, so your business is your business. But I thought you should know that we know."

She looked at me for a long moment. I have a face that frightens people, all ridges and weather like the mountain it belongs to, and most people look away first. She didn't. I decided I could work with her.

"They're half a day behind us," she said. "Maybe less. My fuel depot burned. My boat's in pieces on mule-back. And the pass closes with the first snow, which the sky tells me is early this year."

"The sky tells me the same thing." I nodded at the light through the window, that particular bruised yellow that means the Cordillera is clearing its throat. "So. You want the Porter's Road."

"The Porter's Road, yes."

"No," I said, and enjoyed the way her jaw tightened. "The Porter's Road is a goat track that delivers you to a col the snow seals a week before it seals the high passes. If your men in good boots are half a day behind, they'll find your mules dead on the switchbacks and follow your tracks straight to you. The Porter's Road is where people go to die politely. You want something older."

* * *

Here is where I must confess something, because this account will be read — I make sure of it; I have buried copies in three places and taught the words to two granddaughters not my own, because a story that lives in one skull dies with it — and the confession is this: I had already seen the map.

The boy's sister, Luz, had it. She'd kept it inside her jacket, against her skin, through whatever those men in good boots had done to her before the rescue — and I will not write what I saw on her wrists, because this is a guide's account, not a court's, and the courts will have their turn. When she finally slept, in my bed, in my house, under my roof where nothing in good boots could reach her, the oilskin corner of it showed at her collar, and I am an old woman and curious as a magpie, and I looked.

One glance. That was all it took.

I knew that route the way you know your mother's handwriting.

* * *

Let me tell you about the Whistling Stones, since the riverwoman named her boat's run after the gorge and probably thinks the name is hers.

Three hundred years ago — more; the priests' records are useless and the stones don't count — there was an empire in these valleys. Not the Inca, not the Spanish, something older that the lowland historians have never bothered to name because it never built anything they could put in a museum. What it built was dams. Terraces first, then weirs, then great walls of fitted stone across the river valleys, to flood the high meadows and make reservoirs for their cities below. And when a village refused to be flooded — and they always refused, because a village is not a field, you cannot simply move it like a crop — the empire sent men in good boots.

The villages that survived did not survive by fighting. Fighting was what the empire wanted; dead villages make no testimony. They survived by running. Up. Into the Cordillera del Viento, along a route that no army could follow because no army could believe anyone would take it. Over the singing pass, where the wind comes through a notch in the ridge and plays the standing stones like a flute — my grandmother said the stones were set there on purpose, tuned, so that the ancestors could hear their descendants coming and know they were not alone. Over the glacier shoulder, down through the ruins under the ice where the first fugitives wintered and starved and some of them survived, which is the whole of our history in one sentence. And down the far side to the eastern valleys, where they made new villages and swore an oath I can recite in the old tongue, though I will not write it here, because oaths written down become contracts, and contracts are what men in good boots understand.

My grandmother's grandmother came over that pass. Carried, they say, in a basket on her mother's back, the whole village's seed corn in one basket and the whole village's memory in the other. Every family in the high valleys has a version of that story. We do not tell it to outsiders. We have had three hundred years of practice not telling it to outsiders, and we are very good at it.

And then a fevered botanist's satchel spilled on my floor, and a stolen map showed its corner, and there it was. The route. Drawn from the survey plates, corrected, annotated in a careful hand — Quill's hand, I'd learn — with the old waymarks marked in symbols I had seen carved into stone since I was small enough to ride my father's shoulders. The escape route of my ancestors, mapped at last, by the very expedition financed by the men who wanted to drown the valleys it was made to save.

I sat down on my own stool and laughed until the boy looked alarmed, and then I stopped laughing, because the boy's face did something to me.

"You know it," he said. "You know where it goes."

"I know where it goes," I said. "That is not the same as knowing whether I should tell you."

* * *

I did not tell them that night. I am not a woman who decides quickly; the mountain taught me that a fast decision and a wrong decision are usually the same decision wearing different boots. I fed them, I dosed the botanist, I sent the muleteer's son home to his father with instructions, and I sat by my stove with a cup of chicha and let the past sit with me a while, the way you let an old dog lean against your leg.

Because here is the thing they don't put in the histories, lowland or otherwise. The route is not just a path. It is a grave, and a church, and a promise. Every waymark on it is a headstone for someone who didn't make the far side. The notch where the stones sing — my people left offerings there for three hundred years, and the stones are not natural, and they are not decoration, and a cartographer's line drawn through them is a needle through the flesh of everything my family is. Taking outsiders over that route is not guiding. It is opening the door of the ancestors' house to strangers, and praying the strangers wipe their feet.

And here is the other thing, the one that tipped me. Three hundred years ago, men in good boots came to drown the valleys. My ancestors ran from them over that pass. And now men in good boots were coming again — the same boots, I'd swear it, the same money, the same empire with a new letterhead — and this time there was a boatload of refugees with a map, running up the mountain with the old route in their hands, asking an old woman to lead them.

History does not repeat. But it rhymes. My grandmother used to say that, and I thought it was just something old women said to make the waiting bearable. Then I looked at the map, and I heard the rhyme clear as the singing stones on a windy day.

The question was not whether the route was sacred. It was. The question was whether the sacred thing is a thing you keep locked, or a thing you use for exactly the purpose it was made for.

It was made for this. That was the answer, and I knew it the way you know a trail under your feet in the dark. The route was not made to be a museum. It was made so that people fleeing men in good boots could live. Three hundred years it had waited, and here they were on my doorstep: a riverwoman running from a debt, a boy running toward a sister he'd already found, a botanist carrying the only testimony that could sink the dam, a village spokesman with his people's whole future in his careful silence. Fugitives. The route's favorite kind of people.

* * *

In the morning I woke them before the light, because the light was going to be bad and I wanted to be above the first switchbacks before it.

They assembled in my kitchen looking like what they were — six people and no plan — except the riverwoman, who looked like a plan with a person attached, and the boy, who had already saddled two of the mules without being asked. I put the stew pot on the table and the oilskin map beside it, and I watched every face when I unrolled it.

The botanist came awake like a struck match. "Where did you—"

"Your sister sleeps like the dead and keeps secrets like a living one," I said. "I looked. I'm not sorry. Now listen, because I will only say this once, and then we climb."

I put my finger on the notch in the ridge line, west of the Porter's Road, where the cartographers had drawn nothing at all because they believed nothing was there.

"The Porter's Road is a trap and you know it. This is the way my people went, three hundred years ago, when the last empire tried to drown their villages. The men financing your survey are the same men with better stationery. This route was cut by fugitives, for fugitives, and it has been waiting for you since before the Spanish came." I looked around the table, at each of them, because some things must be said to faces. "It is sacred ground. Every stone on it is a grave or a marker or an offering, and I will not have it treated as scenery. You walk it as guests. You take nothing, you leave nothing, and at the singing pass you will be quiet, because the stones are tuned and my ancestors put them there to hear their children coming, and by every right I can reckon, that now includes you."

Silence. The good kind — the kind where people are not deciding whether to obey, but absorbing that they have been included in something larger than their trouble.

It was the boy who broke it, because it is always the boy.

"The carvings," he said. "On the map. The symbols. They're the same ones in the ruins, aren't they? Under the ice."

I looked at him a long time. "How do you know about the ruins?"

"I don't," he said. "I dreamed them. Before we found the map. A village under ice, with marks on the walls." He shrugged, embarrassed, the way children are embarrassed by the true things they say. "I thought I made it up."

The botanist's breath caught. The riverwoman's hand found the boy's shoulder and stayed there.

"Well," I said, because a guide learns to keep her voice level when the mountain does something it has no business doing. "Then perhaps the route has been expecting you longer than I have. Eat your stew. We leave in an hour."

* * *

We were on the trail by the time the sun cleared the ridge, seven mules, six walkers, one old woman who had told herself she was too old for this and had been lying to herself for a decade. The Porter's Road fell away below us to the east, and I did not point it out, and no one asked.

By midmorning we could see the valley behind us, and I saw the riverwoman stop and turn and shade her eyes and go very still. I stopped beside her. I have learned to recognize the posture of a person counting dust on a road.

"How fast can you walk, Berríos?" she said.

"Faster than men in good boots," I said. "Boots like that are made for floors, not switchbacks. But not faster than weather." I nodded at the ridge to the north, where a plume of cloud had begun to stream off the summit like smoke off a signal fire. The Cordillera was clearing its throat again, and this time it meant to sing. "That snow will come early, and it will come hard. If we make the notch before it, the pass will hide us. If we don't—"

I didn't finish. Guides don't finish sentences like that; it's bad for morale and worse for the truth.

She looked at the plume a moment longer. Then she turned and put her hand briefly, hard, on the boy's shoulder, and raised her voice the way river people do, pitched to carry over water.

"Pick up the pace, everyone! The mountain's singing, and I'd rather not be here for the second verse!"

The boy grinned. The mules, sensing either weather or dinner, quickened. And I took the lead up the old way, past the first waymark — my grandmother's grandmother's stone, worn smooth by three hundred years of fleeing hands — and I put my palm flat on it as I passed, the way I have done since I was a girl, and I said the words I do not write down.

*Walk fast, children. The stones are listening, and this time, so am I.*
