## 12. Snowline

The first snow came at dawn on the fourth day above San Vega, and it came the way bad news always comes — quietly, while everyone was pretending to sleep.

I knew before I opened my eyes. The quality of the silence had changed. On the river I could read a sandbar through six feet of fog by the sound the current made against it, and up here on the Porter's Road I'd learned to read the mountain the same way: by what it stopped saying. All night the wind had been muttering through the scree below our camp like an old woman complaining about her knees. At dawn it went quiet, and that quiet had weight to it, the way a held breath has weight.

I crawled out of the lean-to we'd rigged against a granite shelf and stood up into falling white.

Not much of it. Fat, lazy flakes, drifting down without conviction, melting where they touched the mules' backs. But they didn't melt where they touched me, and they didn't melt where they landed on the stones, and after nine years of reading water I can tell you that the difference between snow that melts and snow that doesn't is the difference between weather and event.

"Juniper." Mara's voice from inside the lean-to, rough with fever. "Is it—"

"It's nothing," I said. "A dusting. It'll burn off by noon."

It was a lie and she knew it was a lie and I knew she knew, which in my experience is how most kindnesses between adults are conducted.

* * *

We made camp that night in the lee of a ridge the muleteers called La Espalda del Diablo — the Devil's Spine — because apparently when you spend your life hauling cargo up a mountain you develop a sense of humor about it or you die. The lead muleteer, a broad silent man named Cusi who communicated mostly through eyebrow, looked at the sky, then at me, and raised one brow so high it nearly left his face entirely.

"Snow early," he said, which was three more words than he usually spent.

"How early?"

"This is not early snow." He pointed east, toward the Cordillera proper, where the peaks had gone from gray to white in a single day, the snowline dropping like a curtain coming down on a play nobody wanted to end. "This is *the* snow. Early for it, yes. But this is it."

Behind us, down the valley we'd climbed out of, the world was still brown and gold. Ahead of us it was already winter. We were standing on the seam.

Tomás appeared at my elbow the way he did — no footsteps, just suddenly present, like a cat deciding you're furniture now. He was fourteen and weighed maybe ninety pounds and had appointed himself, somewhere back on the river, the unofficial guardian of every adult in the party, which would have been funny if it weren't so obviously going to get him killed someday.

"The pass," he said. "How long?"

"Three days if the weather holds. Four if it doesn't."

"And Renard?"

I looked back down the trail. From the Spine you could see a long way behind you, switchbacks stitched down the mountainside like clumsy sutures, and beyond them the valley floor, and beyond that the dark thread of the river where the Marisol sat disassembled under canvas in San Vega, her engine parts riding on mule-back ahead of us because I refused to let anything happen to her heart even if the rest of her had to wait.

Half a day back, maybe less now, there was smoke. Not campfire smoke. Cook-fire smoke, big and organized, the kind of smoke men make when someone's paying for the fuel.

"Half a day," I said. "Maybe half a night."

Tomás nodded, absorbing it, filing it away in whatever ledger he kept in that head of his. Then he went back to Mara, who was propped against a pack with her wounded side wrapped and her fever-bright eyes fixed on the snowline as if she could hold it up there by staring at it.

* * *

Here is the thing about being the one everybody looks to: eventually you start looking at yourself, and you don't like the arithmetic you find.

That night, while the others slept — Cusi's men wrapped in their ponchos like a row of boulders, Tomás curled against Mara's good side, old Ilsa Berríos sitting her watch with the patience of a woman who had been watching mountains longer than I'd been alive — I sat up and did the math.

The math went like this. One botanist with a fever climbing toward blood poisoning. One boy built out of stubbornness and not enough food. One old guide whose knees were older than the republic. Three muleteers who were being paid to haul cargo, not to die in somebody else's war. A map in an oilskin case worth, depending on who you asked, either three villages' futures or a mining consortium's entire investment in this valley. And behind us, closing at the speed of money, a man named Renard with twenty armed men, warm tents, fresh mules, and a contract with my name pre-written on the debt line.

And here is the other thing about the math. There was a version of it that worked.

I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the one cargo I've never managed to jettison, no matter how light I needed the boat. There was a version of the arithmetic that came out clean. I walk down to Renard's parley — he'd signaled it that afternoon, actually, a mirror flash from the switchbacks below, two long flashes and one short, the consortium's little courtesy, *we know you see us* — and I hand over the case, and I sign whatever paper makes my brother's debt evaporate, and maybe I even negotiate Mara's safety into it, and Tomás's, because Renard was polite that way, polite the way a knife is smooth. And then the rest of them walk on over the pass free and clear, unburdened, unmurdered, alive. The map burns or gets locked in a vault in the capital, and the dam gets built or doesn't, and the villages drown or don't, but *my people live*, and isn't that the thing? Isn't that the whole job of a captain? You keep your crew breathing. Everything else is philosophy.

I sat with that math for a long time, turning it over like a stone I'd found in the shallows, looking for the crack in it.

Mara shifted in her sleep and made a small sound, and Tomás, even asleep, even fever-adjacent himself from worry, put his arm across her like a drawbridge gate.

There was the crack.

Because here's what the clean version of the math required me to believe: that Renard would keep his word. That a man who burned a fuel depot to strand us, who took prisoners including a child, whose entire profession was making inconvenient truths disappear into reservoirs, would honor a piece of paper once the paper's leverage was gone. I'd met his kind on the river my whole adult life. They smile like bankers and they keep promises like rivers keep banks — right up until the flood season changes.

And here's the other thing the clean version required: that the truth could be surrendered along with the paper. That three villages' testimony, carried in the heads of frightened farmers and one feverish botanist, would weigh anything at all against a consortium's lawyers once the only complete map — the physical proof of the route, the survey data, the evidence of what was found under the ridge — sat in Renard's strongbox. Mara had said it herself, back on the Marisol, trying to talk me into giving it up: *the truth doesn't need paper.* She believed it. She needed to believe it; her guilt over that distress call was eating her alive and surrendering the case was the only penance on offer. But I'd watched men drown the truth before. It doesn't take much water.

So the math wasn't clean. The math was: trade a maybe-dead truth for a definitely-alive family, and then spend the rest of my life knowing which one I'd chosen when the price was posted.

Or: keep the truth, and gamble every life aboard on my ability to outrun twenty men across a mountain that had started closing its doors.

I hate myself for how long I sat there weighing those. I want you to understand that. Whatever else I am — runner, debtor, the woman Port Salinas still spits at the mention of — I am not the person who does that math quickly. I sat in the cold doing sums with people's lives until the fire burned down to red eyes in the ash, and both answers looked reasonable, and that's the most frightening thing I learned that night: that betrayal, done carefully, done *lovingly*, can look exactly like protection.

"You are making the face," Ilsa said.

I hadn't heard her cross the camp. Old women move like that. Like rivers.

"What face?"

"The face of a woman negotiating with herself." She sat down beside me without asking, wrapped her blanket around both of us with the efficiency of someone who has shared warmth with strangers in worse places. "My mother used to say: the mountain does not care what you decide. Only what you do."

"That's very wise."

"It is not wisdom. It is just true, and truth is cheap. Wisdom costs extra." She looked at me sideways. In the firelight her face was all ridges and shadow, a topographic map of a life. "You are thinking of giving it to him."

I didn't insult her by denying it. "If it keeps them alive—"

"If. There is the word that buys everything and pays nothing." She pulled her blanket tighter. "I have walked this road since before you were born, captain. I will tell you what my grandmother told me, and she walked it when the empire's men came the first time. They offered our people a bargain too. Land for peace. Maps for mercy." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and entirely grief. "My grandmother's village is under Lake Verdana now. You fish there. The trout are excellent. Nobody remembers why."

The wind picked up again, and the snow came harder, hissing against the rocks.

"Ilsa. If I give him the map and he kills them anyway—"

"Then you gave away the only thing that mattered and got nothing. Yes." She shrugged. "And if you keep it and he catches you, same result, except you will have died with your hands full. So do not choose based on outcomes. Outcomes belong to the mountain. Choose based on—" she tapped my chest, once, with a finger like a knuckle of driftwood, "—this. What does this say?"

What it said was: *you already know.* What it said was: you've been running for nine years from the night you chose wrong, and you are not going to spend ten more running from a second one. What it said was: the Marisol is named for a dead woman who took you in when your own family wouldn't, and boats don't ask their passengers to pay fare in other people's blood.

But I didn't say any of that. I'm a wry woman, not an honest one, not out loud.

"I'll sleep on it," I said.

Ilsa snorted, which from her was a benediction, and went back to her watch.

* * *

Morning came white and merciless.

Overnight the dusting had become a declaration. Six inches on the trail, more on the ledges, and the snowline had dropped another thousand feet during the night so that when I looked back down the mountain — and I looked, God help me, first thing, the way you check a wound — the smoke of Renard's camp was closer. Half a day, maybe less. His mirrors flashed twice in the early light. Two long, one short. *We know you see us.*

Cusi looked at the trail, looked at the sky, and spoke his longest sentence of the expedition: "We go now, fast, or we do not go."

We went.

The day became a blur of white and strain. The mules plowed; we broke trail behind them; Mara rode the strongest animal with Tomás walking beside her, one hand on her knee the whole way, steering her upright like a tiller. Twice she slipped into that fever-country where the eyes look at you and don't quite arrive, and twice Tomás talked her back with a low stream of words I couldn't hear and didn't need to.

At midday we crossed a shoulder of rock where the wind had stripped the snow bare, and Ilsa stopped so abruptly I nearly walked into her.

She was looking at something carved into the stone. Faint, worn, older than the road itself: a spiral, and beneath it a line of symbols I'd last seen inked on oilskin in Mara's cabin — the route-markers of the ancestors, the escape-signs of people who had run this same mountain from men with contracts.

"They marked the way," Ilsa said softly. "For whoever came after. For us." She glanced back down-valley, at the smoke, at the mirrors. "They knew someone would come after."

We moved on, faster, and the spiral stayed behind us in the snow like a hand letting go.

By late afternoon the clouds had come down to meet us and the world shrank to thirty feet of gray-white in every direction, and it was in that shrunk world, blind and stumbling, that Tomás — out front, because of course he was out front, the boy had appointed himself point and none of us had the heart or the authority to relieve him — stopped dead and said:

"Juniper. Lights."

Below us, down through a gap in the cloud, maybe a mile as the condor flew and three as the switchback crawls: lanterns. Dozens of them, strung out along the lower trail, moving. Renard had stopped waiting for courtesy. He was climbing through the dusk with his whole company, and he was gaining.

Half a day had become four hours.

I stood in the falling snow with the oilskin case strapped across my back like a second spine, and I felt the two halves of myself pull in opposite directions with a force I could hear, almost, like ice groaning on a hull. One half said: *go down, meet him, end it, save them.* The other half said: *run, climb, faster, the pass, the window, tomorrow's dawn.*

Ilsa appeared beside me, following my gaze down to the lights.

"He will reach the bridge by morning," she said. "The rope bridge at Quebrada Honda. There is no other crossing. If we are past it when he arrives—" she drew her thumb across her throat, matter-of-fact as a woman discussing supper.

"And if we're not?"

"Then," she said, "we learn what you decided tonight."

The lights below kept climbing. I turned uphill into the snow, and the whole party turned with me, and above us, unseen but utterly certain, the pass waited with its door already beginning to close.
