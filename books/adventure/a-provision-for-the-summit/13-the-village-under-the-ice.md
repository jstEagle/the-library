## 13. The Village Under the Ice

The storm found us an hour after we lost the mules, which felt like the mountain's idea of a fair trade — take the animals, spare the people, charge full price for the shelter.

I want to say I was brave about it. I wasn't. When the wind came off the glacier it made a sound like the whole Cordillera del Viento clearing its throat to spit us off, and I did what any sensible boy does in that situation: I got very small behind Juniper Villalobos and pretended her coat was a wall.

"Tomás." She had to shout over the wind. "Your eyes are better than mine. Find me something."

"Something like what?"

"Like anything! An overhang, a cleft, a cave — I'll even take a really committed boulder!"

That's the thing about Juniper nobody who's only met her on the river would guess. On the *Marisol* she's all economy and silence, a woman who steers with two fingers and talks like words cost money. Put her on a mountainside in a whiteout and she turns into somebody else — loud, quick, almost cheerful, like danger is a language she's finally fluent in.

I squinted into the flying white. Snow doesn't blur your vision so much as erase it, wipe the world clean and then forget to write anything back. But I'd spent nine days learning to read this landscape the way I used to read water, and water taught me one lesson above all others: nothing is flat where it looks flat. There's always a seam.

"There," I said, pointing left of a rock spine that looked like a broken tooth. "The snow's falling wrong. It's disappearing."

Juniper looked where I pointed, saw nothing, and trusted me anyway. That still catches in my chest when I think about it too long. She trusted me anyway.

We stumbled toward the seam, and the seam turned out to be a gap between the glacier's shoulder and the cliff wall, and the gap opened into —

Well. That's the part I keep starting over.

* * *

It was a street.

Not a crack, not a den, not a hole for animals. A street, maybe four meters wide, running back under the ice shelf, walled on both sides by stone houses with their roofs fallen in, their doorways dark, their windows empty of everything except blue glacier light. The whole village sat in the pocket between ice and rock, preserved like a moth in amber, and the cold down there was different from the cold outside — older, patient, the kind of cold that has already won and knows it.

"Madre de Dios," Mara whispered behind me. She was leaning on Luz's shoulder, fever-bright but walking, because Mara Quill walked through fevers the way other people walked through rain — annoyed, but committed. "This is it. This is the route. This is one of the *settlements*."

"You knew about this?" Juniper asked.

"I knew there were legends. High valleys, hidden villages, people who vanished rather than kneel." She turned slowly, taking in the dead street. "Legends don't usually come with architecture."

Ilsa Berríos said nothing at first. The old guide stood in the middle of the street with her hood back — she'd doffed it before we were even fully out of the wind, which struck me as either reverence or madness — and looked up at the houses the way you look at faces in a crowd, searching for one you know.

"My grandmother sang about this place," she said finally. Her voice went strange in the enclosed air, flattened and close. "A song for putting children to sleep. *Bajo el hielo duerme la gente* — under the ice sleeps the people. I thought it was a song about winter." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "I have been singing a map to children my whole life and calling it a lullaby."

Nobody answered that. What do you say? We were six people standing in a town that had been waiting centuries for someone to walk down its street, and every one of us was thinking the same thing, which is that we hadn't discovered anything. We'd been *expected*. The people who built these walls had cut this road through the high passes precisely so that people like us — running, hunted, carrying things worth killing for — could come this way. We weren't explorers. We were guests three hundred years late.

Juniper broke the spell, because that's what captains do. "Shelter first. Ghosts second. Tomás, Luz — check those two buildings for roof fall, see if one's sound enough for a fire. Ilsa, how much wood do we have?"

"Enough to be sorry about later."

"Then we're rich. Move."

* * *

Here is what I know about being a stowaway, since apparently I'm the resident expert: you learn to make yourself useful fast, or you learn to get put off at the next port. So while the adults argued about firewood and fever management, Luz and I went house to house like a pair of burglars with permission.

The buildings were stone, fitted without mortar, tight as a drystone wall my father once built back home — back in the valley, back before. Inside, most had collapsed ceilings furred with ice, but the third one we tried still held half a roof, and its floor was dry, and along one wall ran a bench of solid stone worn smooth in the middle, the way a chair gets worn smooth.

"Somebody sat here," Luz said. She said it quietly, the way she says most things now. Since the rescue she talks maybe a tenth as much as she used to, and I've stopped trying to fill the silence because I understand it. Some silences aren't empty. They're full.

"A lot of somebodies," I said. "For a long time."

She ran her hand over the bench. "Do you think they were happy here?"

What kind of question is that? A ten-year-old's question, maybe, except Luz is fourteen and I'm twelve and neither of us asks questions like kids anymore, not since Renard's men took her and I spent five weeks learning exactly how large and how empty the world could be.

"I think," I said, "they were safe here. And I think if you asked them, they'd tell you that was close enough."

Luz nodded like I'd passed a test, and we went to tell Juniper we'd found our room.

* * *

The fire was small — Ilsa rationing wood like a woman counting out her own heartbeats — but it made the stone room glow amber and threw our shadows huge against the walls, six giants huddled around a match flame. Mara lay wrapped in every blanket we had left, shivering and sweating by turns, and Juniper fed her melted snow in sips while pretending not to be terrified. I've watched Juniper Villalobos run the Gorge of Whistling Stones without blinking. She is not good at watching people she loves be sick. It's the one water she can't read.

It was Ilsa who noticed the carvings.

She'd gone out alone — old women get to do that, apparently, they just announce it and leave — and came back twenty minutes later moving faster than I'd seen her move on the whole climb, her breath ragged, her eyes lit like lamps.

"Come," she said. "Bring the light. Bring the girl's drawing case. Come *now*."

Mara couldn't come, so Mara sent me with the oilskin case, and I'll admit my hands shook carrying it. Not from cold. From the feeling that the case had gotten heavier since the pass, heavier every day, like it knew what it was and resented being carried like luggage.

Ilsa led us to the largest building on the street, the one at the far end where the glacier wall curved overhead like a wave frozen mid-break. Its inner face — the whole wall of ancient blue ice — was carved.

Not the ice. The stone beneath it, and the ice had melted and refrozen over the centuries until the carvings showed through like bones under skin. Rows of figures. People walking, in single file, carrying bundles and leading llamas, a procession marching across the entire face of the wall. Above them, symbols. Spirals. A stepped pattern like a staircase. A zigzag line that could have been lightning or a river or a road.

And below them, repeated over and over, a symbol I knew so well my stomach dropped through the floor:

A circle, quartered by two lines, with a dot at the center.

"It's the mark," I heard myself say. My voice bounced around the dead hall. "From the map. It's — that's the same mark. It's everywhere on the map. At every junction, every water source, every pass. We thought it meant *survey point*. Elias Thorne's crew thought it meant survey point."

"And what does it mean?" Juniper asked softly.

Ilsa reached out and touched the circle, her fingers leaving prints in the frost. "On my grandmother's side, we called it *el ojo del camino*. The eye of the road. It means *here you will be seen and sheltered*. It means *kin live past this point*." She turned to look at us, and in the lantern light her face was wet, though whether from meltwater or something else I couldn't say. "It is not a surveyor's mark, child. It is a welcome sign. Your map is not a mining document. It is a hospitality network. Three hundred years old. Built by people running from exactly the sort of men who are running after you."

* * *

I didn't sleep that night. None of us really did, but I was the only one who stayed sitting up, cross-legged by the banked fire, looking at the shadows on the wall and doing arithmetic I didn't want to do.

Because here's the thing. Here's the thing I haven't told anyone, not even Luz, especially not Luz.

When Renard's men burned our village — not ours, the one we'd fled to, the second one, after the first one drowned — I hid in a culvert with Luz's hand over my mouth and listened to a man explain to another man why burning homes was efficient. And the word he kept using was *clearance*. Clear the valley. Clear the population. Make the numbers work.

And Ilay there in the dark thinking: I have heard this before. Not the words. The *shape* of it. Because Mara had shown us the consortium's plans, the dam schematics, the maps with three villages marked in red ink labeled RELOCATION ZONES, and the shape of that was the same shape. Men upstream deciding that people downstream are a rounding error.

Three hundred years, and the same men building the same walls, drowning the same valleys. Only now they call it development instead of empire, and they have lawyers instead of soldiers, mostly.

Except.

Except the people in the carvings hadn't just run. They'd built a road. They'd marked it with welcome signs. They'd carried each other over the spine of the world and settled in the high places and *kept going*, and their descendants were Ilsa Berríos, and the villagers whose spokesman was in Renard's camp right now, and — I realized this slowly, the way you realize you're standing in warm water — maybe me. Maybe Luz. Our valley was one of the three. Our grandmother's grandmother's grandmother might have walked this exact street, might have sat on that worn-smooth bench, might have sung the lullaby Ilsa sings.

I had spent five weeks believing I belonged nowhere. Stowaways don't belong anywhere; that's practically the job description. And here was a whole civilization under the ice telling me otherwise. Telling me that belonging isn't blood, isn't a house, isn't even a boat. Belonging is a mark on a wall that says *you will be seen and sheltered*, kept alive by strangers who owe you nothing, waiting three centuries for you to need it.

I cried a little. I'm writing this down because Juniper says the truth is glue, and I figure if I only write the parts where I'm brave, the record will lie, and lying is what started all of this anyway.

* * *

Near dawn, Mara's fever broke. I know because Luz woke everyone shouting it, and because Mara herself confirmed it by immediately complaining about the coffee, which was how we knew she'd survive.

But before dawn, while the fire was lowest and the glacier light was turning from black to gray, Juniper came and sat beside me. She didn't say anything for a long time. That's a riverboat habit, I think. On the *Marisol*, silence is a conversation.

"The carvings," she said eventually. "You understood them before Ilsa explained."

"They matched the map."

"That's not what I mean." She poked the coals. Sparks rose and died. "You looked at that wall like you were reading a letter addressed to you. I've seen you look at water that way. Never at anything on land."

I shrugged, which is the universal language of boys avoiding questions.

"Tomás." She waited until I looked at her. In the dimness her face was all angles and patience. "Whatever you're figuring out about yourself — whoever you decide you are after this — I want you to know the *Marisol* has a berth that stays yours. Whatever happens with the map, with Renard, with all of it. You and Luz both. That's not charity. That's crew."

My throat did something embarrassing. "Even if I stowed away illegally?"

"*Especially* then. Best hiring decision I never made."

She squeezed my shoulder once, hard, the way she does instead of hugging, and went to check on Mara, and I sat there in the oldest room I'd ever stood in, warmer than I had any right to be.

* * *

We left the village mid-morning, when the storm blew itself out and the sky cleared to that brutal high-altitude blue. Ilsa poured a measure of our precious fuel on the stones of the great hall and spoke words in her grandmother's language, asking the sleeping people's pardon for the smoke and thanks for the roof. Then we shouldered our packs — lighter now, hungrier now, the summit two days away — and climbed out of the village under the ice into the sun.

We'd made perhaps an hour's progress along the moraine when Ilsa stopped so abruptly that Luz walked into her back.

Below us, where the Porter's Road dropped toward the river gorge, a rope bridge swayed across a chasm, white with frost. And on the near side of it, arranged with the casual precision of men who wanted to be seen, stood a line of figures in black. Six of them. Seven. Behind them, pack animals, and a tent already pitched, and a cookfire sending up a thread of smoke that said, more clearly than any shout: *we can wait longer than you can.*

At the front of the line, lifting one hand in a gesture that was almost friendly, almost a wave, stood Renard.

"Well," Juniper said, in the voice she used for sandbars and other hazards. "There goes the scenic route."
