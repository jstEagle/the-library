## 14. Renard's Parley

I have been shot exactly once in my life, and I can report that the interesting part is not the bullet. The bullet is over quickly, a hot argument you lose before you understand it was happening. The interesting part is everything after — the fever, the weakness, the way your own body becomes a country with a border you can no longer cross freely.

By the time we reached the rope bridge at Quebrada Honda, I had been that country for six days. Fever in the mornings, lucidity by afternoon, and always the wound in my side pulling like a stitch someone had sewn too tight and then forgotten about. Ilsa Berríos had packed it with a moss she called *soldier's friend*, which told me everything I needed to know about how often soldiers had needed friends on this road. Tomás carried my pack without being asked. Juniper pretended not to notice him doing it, which was her way of noticing everything.

The bridge itself was nothing much to look at — two cables of braided hide strung across a gorge so deep the river at its bottom was less a sound than a rumor, planks laid crosswise with the cheerful irregularity of something built by people who trusted their feet more than their carpentry. It swayed in the wind off the glacier above. Beyond it, the trail climbed toward the pass, and the pass, according to Ilsa, would close within days. Perhaps hours. The sky had been the color of dirty wool since dawn, and the air had that pressed, listening quality of weather deciding something.

We were halfway across when I heard the mules stop behind us.

You learn to hear it — the particular silence of animals who have decided that whatever is coming down the trail is not their problem. I turned, one hand on the cable, and looked back through the thin trees, and there they were: four men on foot and Renard himself on a gray horse, riding at the easy pace of a man who has never once in his life been late for anything worth being early to.

"Señora Quill," he called, pleasantly, as if we'd met at a market stall. "You're looking unwell."

"I'm looking better than you deserve," I said, which cost me more breath than it should have, and which was also, I reflected, not actually true. He looked extremely well. Black coat brushed clean of snow, boots polished against the mud of half a mountain, that narrow face arranged in an expression of patient reasonableness. Men like Renard do the damage first and the reasonable face second. The face is the receipt.

Juniper had already turned the mules sideways across the trail, blocking it, and put herself between us and them with the unhurried economy of a woman who had done arithmetic about distances and liked none of the answers. Tomás stood at her shoulder. Luz stood at his. Ilsa had drifted, without any apparent decision, to the edge of the trees where the terrain went steep and broken — where a woman who had walked these mountains for sixty years could go places four men with rifles could not follow. She caught my eye and touched two fingers to her temple. *I see them. Keep talking.*

"Captain Villalobos." Renard dismounted, handed his reins to one of his men, and strolled forward until he stood perhaps ten meters from Juniper, the gorge wind pulling at his coat. "I've come to make you an offer. A final one — I dislike repeating myself, it's undignified."

"You came all this way to be dignified at me," Juniper said. "That's touching."

"The map," he said. "The case. Everything in it. In exchange, you walk away. All of you. Safe passage down whichever side of the mountain you prefer, my word on it, and—" here he paused, delicately, the way a surgeon pauses before showing you the instrument, "—your debts cleared. All of them. Port Salinas included."

I watched that land on Juniper like a stone dropped into still water. She didn't move. But I had spent nine days on her boat by then, and I had learned to read her the way you read water before trusting it with your hull — the surface calm, the currents underneath doing all the real work. Her brother's debt. Nine years of it, or so the story had come out in the snow cave, in pieces, the way true stories do. Renard hadn't found out about it. He'd *bought* it. Somewhere in Port Salinas a clerk had sold a ledger page, and now here it was, weaponized, offered back to her at the price of three villages.

"That's generous," Juniper said.

"It's business."

"It's what you say when you're losing." She shifted her weight. "Your fuel depot burned because your own men are sloppy. Your horses are spent. You've got half a day on us and a storm coming that's going to close that pass whether either of us likes it or not. You need this map today, Renard, or your consortium learns you chased a riverboat captain and a botanist around a mountain and came home with nothing."

Something flickered behind his pleasantness — quick, cold, gone. "Then we understand each other. Today."

Here is what I want to explain, because afterward — after the bridge, after everything — people asked me why I said what I said next, and some of them never forgave me for it, and I have stopped expecting them to.

I said it because I had read the map. Not glanced at it, the way the others had. Read it. Every contour line, every annotation in Thorne's cramped hand, every symbol copied from the carvings under the ice. I knew what was in that oilskin case better than anyone alive except perhaps Elias Thorne, wherever he was, if he was. And I knew something else, something I had known since the night I sent that distress call from the wreck of the tender and heard it answered by silence instead of rescue: paper is a promise made to strangers.

A map in a courtroom is evidence. A map in a courier's satchel is cargo. And cargo can be lost, stolen, bought, burned, or buried — and the consortium had demonstrated, with a burned village and a drowned valley waiting in their blueprints, exactly how they handled things they wanted buried.

But testimony. Testimony lives in people. In me, who had surveyed the route. In Ilsa, whose grandmother's grandmother had crossed it. In Luz and Tomás and the eleven villagers we'd pulled from the smoke at Río Seco, who could stand in a court and say *we lived there, and here is what they planned.* They couldn't burn us all. They could try — God knows they were trying — but every day we stayed alive and moving, the truth got heavier and harder to carry away from us.

So when Renard turned to me — and he did turn to me, deliberately, because he'd done his homework and knew the scientist would be the reasonable one — I gave him the answer he'd traveled all this way to buy.

"Mara," he said, warmly. "You're a woman of science. You understand documentation. That map represents years of work — yours, Thorne's. Is it worth dying for? Is it worth *them* dying for?" He gestured at the children, at Juniper, at the mules standing hipshot in the wind. "Give me the case. Keep your copies, your notes, your memories. Write your paper. Win your prize. No one needs to fall off this bridge."

And I looked at Juniper, and I said: "Take it."

The word came out of me smaller than I meant it to. Fever does that; it shrinks your voice to fit whatever strength is left. But I said it again, louder. "Take the offer, Juniper. Give him the map."

"Mara—"

"Listen to me." I pushed off the cable and took a step toward her, and the whole bridge swayed, and I didn't care. "I sent that distress call. I brought this down on us — on Tomás, on Luz, on everyone at Río Seco who opened their door to us. Every mile of this road has been paid for out of other people's blood, and I am *tired*, Juniper. I'm tired and I'm feverish and I have watched Ilsa carry me on her back up scree slopes and watched a twelve-year-old stand watch with a rifle taller than he is, all for a piece of oiled canvas." My side was screaming. I kept going. "The map is not the truth. The map is a *drawing* of the truth. I have the truth in here—" I hit my own chest, harder than I intended, and winced. "So does Ilsa. So do the villagers. We don't need the paper. We never needed the paper. Take his deal. Take your brother's debt off your back. Nine years, Juniper. Buy yourself back."

The wind moved through the trees. Somewhere below us, the buried river muttered.

Juniper looked at me for a long moment. And I want to be honest, because honesty is the only thing I have left to offer anyone: in that moment I hated her a little. Because I saw her wanting it. I saw the nine years move behind her eyes like water under ice, and I saw her calculate — fast, instinctive, the same arithmetic she did reading sandbars — what her freedom would cost, and I saw that the number frightened her less than it should have.

"See?" Renard said softly. "The doctor understands."

"She's not the captain," Juniper said.

"No," I said. "She isn't. But you'd throw away the only family that's ever chosen you to settle a debt to people who never loved you—"

"That's enough."

"—and I get to say that, because I'm the one who bled for it—"

"*Enough.*" It wasn't shouted. It was worse than shouted. It was quiet, and final, and it came from Tomás.

He had stepped forward, between me and Juniper, small and straight-backed, and he was looking at me with an expression I had seen once before — on the Marisol, the night he admitted why he'd stowed away, the night the stories were traded like food in the snow cave. It was the expression of a child who has decided the adults are his to protect, and who has just discovered, painfully, that they cannot even protect themselves from each other.

"Luz is down that mountain," he said. "In his camp. Right now. With his men." He pointed past Renard, downtrail, toward the smoke we'd seen at dawn. "You want to give him the map while my sister is tied up in his camp? You think he lets *anyone* walk away safe once he's holding the only thing he wants?" His voice cracked and he hated it and kept going anyway. "He doesn't burn villages because he keeps promises. He burns villages because promises are cheaper than fires."

Nobody spoke. Renard's smile had thinned to something with edges.

"And you," Tomás said to me, and his voice gentled in a way that hurt worse than the rest of it. "You taught me the contour lines. On the boat. You said maps were how the dead talk to the living. You said the old route was proof that people ran from men like him *before*, and won." He swallowed. "Don't give him the dead's words. Please."

I stood on that swaying bridge with my wound burning and my argument in ruins, and I understood that I had made a mistake — not in my reasoning, which was sound, but in my assumption. I had thought the map was a burden we carried for the villages' sake. I had not understood that somewhere between the ice cave and this bridge, it had become something else. Proof. A thread connecting all of us to people who had stood on this exact stone, fleeing exactly these men, and crossed anyway.

It was Ilsa who broke the silence, her voice drifting down from the rocks, dry as the moss she dressed wounds with.

"In my grandmother's time," she said, "the empire's men came to the high villages and asked for the passes to be shown on paper. Paper for peace, they said. The villages gave them paper." A pause, the wind filling it. "The paper was accurate. The peace was not. There is a reason the true route was carved in stone where only the desperate could read it."

Renard sighed, and the performance of patience fell away from him all at once, like a coat dropped, and what was underneath was simply tired, cold, dangerous man. "Captain. Last time. The case, or I take it, and I take casualties doing it, and my men are fresh and yours are—" his gaze flicked over us, cataloguing: my wound, Luz's bandaged wrist, the children, the mules, "—not."

Juniper looked at me. Really looked, the way she looked at water she was about to commit the boat to.

"You're right about the paper," she said quietly. "It's just a drawing. And you're right that I'd burn it myself before I let him dam that valley." She turned back to Renard. "But you made one mistake, offering to erase my debts."

Renard raised an eyebrow. "Enlighten me."

"You reminded me what I owe." She set her hand flat on the oilskin case strapped to the lead mule, and smiled, and it was not a friendly smile; it was the smile of a woman reading a sandbar that is about to eat somebody's boat. "And I pay what I owe. Ask anyone in Port Salinas."

Renard exhaled through his nose. Then he stepped back, unhurried, and nodded to his men, and four rifles came off four shoulders with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat.

"A pity," he said. "I genuinely did hope to avoid the paperwork of a massacre."

Behind him, downtrail, through the thinning trees, I saw what he could not, because his back was to it: a thread of woodsmoke rising from the hollow where his camp lay — and figures moving around it, and among the small dark shapes of prisoners, one that stood straighter than the rest, one that turned its face up toward the bridge, toward us, as if it had heard its brother's voice carried on the wind.

Tomás saw it too. I felt him see it — felt it the way you feel lightning decide where to strike.

"Oh," he breathed. "*Luz.*"

The first rifle came up.
