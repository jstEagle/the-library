## 15. What Juniper Owes

Renard's pen was a good pen. That was the first thing I noticed, and I want it on the record that I noticed it, because a man who carries a fountain pen across the Cordillera del Viento in the first week of snow is a man who intends to do paperwork at altitude, and paperwork at altitude is never for your benefit.

It was brass-capped, heavy in the hand when he passed it to me across the flat of his map case, and the nib caught the last grey light coming off the glacier like it had been polished for exactly this moment. Everything about Renard was polished for exactly this moment. His coat was oiled wool, his boots were city boots with mountain cleats bolted on as an afterthought, and his smile had the warmth of a bank lobby.

"Take your time," he said, which is what men say when they have already calculated how much time you'll take.

I looked at the contract. I'd like to tell you I read every line like a careful woman, but the truth is my hands were shaking — cold, I told myself, just cold — and the paper kept trying to leave with the wind, so Mara held one corner and Tomás stood at my shoulder breathing through his mouth the way he did when he was scared and refusing to admit it. Luz was somewhere behind us with the villagers' spokesman, old Benicio Araya, whose wrists still bore the rope burns from three days in Renard's camp. The bridge creaked under all of us, ropes groaning their one long note, and below it the river we'd left behind nine days ago went on doing what rivers do, which is not care.

"Article One," Renard said, helpfully. "Full remission of all debts outstanding against the Villalobos name, including but not limited to the obligations of one Mateo Villalobos, Port Salinas."

My brother's name in a consortium lawyer's handwriting. I felt it like a hook going in under the breastbone.

"Article Two," he continued, "conveyance of all cartographic materials recovered from the Thorne expedition, in exchange for which the Consortium undertakes no further action against yourself or your passengers, and further provides safe conduct—"

"Safe conduct," I said. "Down the mountain. Past your launch. Through the valley you're planning to flood."

"The valley," Renard said mildly, "is a matter of engineering."

* * *

Let me back up an hour, because the hour matters.

We came down off the moraine at dusk with nothing — the mules gone over the bridge fight, most of the food gone with them, Ilsa gone with everything else — and found Renard waiting at the near anchorage like a landlord at the end of the month. Six men behind him with rifles slung casual, the way you sling a rifle when you want everyone to notice you're not raising it yet. We were eleven, counting Luz and Benicio, half of us limping, one of us fourteen, one of us running a fever she swore was broken and wasn't.

He could have taken the case by force. Everyone knew it. He knew I knew it. But Renard didn't want the case by force, because a case taken by force can be described by witnesses as stolen, and a case signed over is a gift. He wanted paper. He'd crossed a mountain range for paper.

So we sat on rocks at either end of a rope bridge while the light died, and he laid out terms the way a man deals cards he's already counted.

"Captain Villalobos." He used the title like a courtesy and a leash at once. "You've cost me two weeks, four mules, and a fuel depot. And yet I'm prepared to be generous, because I admire competence, and because — frankly — I'd rather buy a thing than dig it out of an icefield."

"And if I say no?"

"Then tomorrow my men walk up that trail, and the day after that they walk up the rest of it, and somewhere in between we stop being polite." He said it without heat, which was the worst part. "But you won't say no. I've read your file, Captain. Nine years of debt. A brother in Port Salinas who took the fall for money you borrowed. You've spent nearly a decade paying for a night of bad weather. I'm offering to erase it. All of it. One signature."

There it was. Not the map — me. He'd done the arithmetic every debt collector since Salinas had done, and arrived at the same sum: Juniper Villalobos can be bought, because Juniper Villalobos has been trying to buy herself back her whole life.

And God help me, standing there with the wind coming off the ice and Mara's blood probably still frozen into the planks of my boat two hundred miles downriver, I wanted to sign. I want to be honest about that, because the whole story is worthless if I make myself better than I was. I looked at that pen and I saw Mateo's face when the flood money ran out. I saw our mother's kitchen with the furniture repossessed around it. I saw nine years of dawn fogs and sandbars and sleeping alone on a boat named after a girl who drowned, and I thought: one signature and none of it ever happened. One signature and I could go home clean.

Renard watched me want it. He was very good at watching. It was, I suspect, most of his job.

"There's a copy for your records," he said, sliding the second sheet toward Tomás, who didn't take it. "Standard form. My legal people drafted it in San Vega before we ever climbed."

That was the sentence that saved everybody, though neither of us knew it yet. *Drafted in San Vega.* Before the expedition went silent. Before the distress call. Before any of us knew there was anything to bargain over.

Because I'd spent enough years around ports and papers to know one thing about documents: the date tells the truth even when the man doesn't.

* * *

"Read it to me," I said. "All of it. Out loud. My eyes are bad in this light."

A small lie. My eyes were fine. But I wanted his voice on every clause, because a man reading his own trap aloud will always stumble where the teeth are.

Renard sighed the sigh of a patient man and began again from Article One. Remission of debts. Conveyance of materials. No further action against yourself or your passengers —

"Passengers," I said. "Define passengers."

"Persons aboard your vessel or in your party as of this date."

"Including them?" I nodded at Benicio and the two villagers who'd come down with us, and at Luz, and at the other three refugees we'd gathered along the porter's road like burrs on a sock.

"Your party, yes."

"Not the village. Not the sixty-odd people sitting in San Vega right now waiting to give testimony about soldiers burning their homes."

The pause was small. If you hadn't spent nine years reading men across a negotiating table you'd have missed it entirely. "The agreement concerns your party."

"Mm." I let him move on.

Article Four was the killer, and he read it in the same level voice as the rest: *In consideration of the above, the undersigned agrees to refrain from making, publishing, or supporting any claim, statement, or testimony, whether oral or written, concerning the operations, plans, or personnel of the Consortium, in any forum, for a period of ten years.*

Ten years. The dam would be built in three.

"There it is," I said.

"I don't follow."

"You don't, or you're hoping I don't? Because here's what I've got, Renard, and correct me if my arithmetic's wrong, since yours is so famously good." I stood up, and my knees cracked like the ice on the moraine, and I didn't care. "You're not buying a map. Maps are cheap. Your own surveyors drew half of one already. What you're buying is silence. Because the only thing standing between your dam and three drowned villages is testimony — Benicio's testimony, the villagers', Mara's field notes, mine. Paper evidence. Sworn statements. And this document doesn't just shut my mouth. It makes everything I've seen worthless, because anything I testify to becomes tainted — obtained under a settlement, contradicted by my own signature. You wouldn't even need to win in court. You'd just need to stand up and wave this page. Every witness who touched this contract is a witness your lawyers get to chew apart."

"That's a creative interpretation—"

"It's the plain one. You drafted it in San Vega before the expedition even went missing. Which means you knew what Thorne found before Thorne found it, and this whole climb wasn't a rescue, it was a recall." I picked up the pen. It was warm from my hand, damn it, warm like it belonged there. "Here's my counteroffer."

"Captain—"

"I keep the map. You keep your paper. My debts stay right where they've been for nine years, because they're mine, and I've been carrying them longer than you've carried that coat, and it turns out I can carry them a while longer." I looked at him then, really looked, past the polish, and saw what was underneath, which was just a man doing sums, and the sums had stopped working. "You offered to erase what I owe. But you never asked what I owe *for*. And it isn't a bank."

* * *

Here is the thing nobody tells you about absolution: it comes in two kinds, and only one of them is real.

The fake kind is the kind Renard was selling. Debt cancelled, slate wiped, the flood un-happened, Mateo free, the mother's kitchen refurnished — a magic eraser dragged across nine years. I had dreamed about that eraser on maybe four thousand nights, steering through fog, and I am not going to pretend the dream didn't almost win. My hand shook holding that pen. Tomás saw it shake. I'll carry that too.

But the real kind — the kind Ilsa Berríos knew something about, cutting a rope with herself on the wrong end of it — the real kind isn't given. It's built. It's built out of the exact weight of what you did, carried openly, in front of witnesses, until the carrying itself becomes a kind of payment. The villagers upstream weren't asking me to be innocent. They were asking me to show up. And showing up means arriving with clean hands, and clean hands means hands that haven't signed away the truth to buy back a kitchen table.

I tore the contract in half.

Then, because I am thorough when it matters, I tore it into quarters, and eighths, and fed the pieces into the wind off the glacier, where they scattered white-on-white like a flock of small stupid birds. Renard's men watched them go. One of them, I swear, almost smiled.

"You understand," Renard said quietly, "what happens now."

"I understand what happens now." I handed him back his pen. It had gone cold in my fist, which felt right. "Now you spend three days climbing a trail we've already broken, chasing people who know the route, in weather that's about to close the pass, to retrieve a map that's about to become worthless anyway — because the moment those villages testify, your dam is dead in court, and no piece of oilskin changes that. You're not hunting a document anymore, Renard. You're hunting time. And time's the one creditor nobody buys off."

For one moment — I'll grant him this — something human moved behind his face. Not regret. Recalculation. The look of a man discovering the item he came to purchase has appreciated beyond his budget.

"Walk away," I said. "You've got a launch, a payroll, and a career. None of us have anything but each other and a bad memory of you. Guess which of those survives the winter."

He looked past me, then, at the others. At Mara, upright through sheer spite, fever-bright. At Luz, who stared at him with the flat hatred only a fourteen-year-old can sustain without blinking. At Benicio Araya, who said nothing at all, which was somehow louder than anything. At Tomás, who stepped forward — one step, small and enormous — and stood at my shoulder like he'd been assigned there.

Renard gathered his map case. "The offer stands until the pass closes," he said.

"So does the mountain," I said.

* * *

We crossed the bridge in the dark, plank by plank, with the river shouting underneath, and on the far side Tomás fell into step beside me and walked in silence for a long time, which for Tomás is a speech.

"You wanted to sign," he said finally. Not accusing. Just noting, the way he noted sandbars.

"Yeah."

"Why didn't you?"

I thought about it honestly, because he deserved honest. "Because a man offered to pay my debts once before," I said. "My father's creditors. A cousin with money and conditions. And I learned that whoever holds the eraser gets to decide what's written next." I put a hand on his shoulder, briefly, awkwardly — I was still learning the mechanics of it. "Nobody writes you next, kid. Not him, not me. You write you."

Ahead of us the trail bent upward into the black throat of the pass, and above it, faint as breath on glass, the first stars were coming out over the Cordillera del Viento. Somewhere behind us, lanterns were moving. We had perhaps half a night, maybe less, and a summit window measured in hours, and eleven people's worth of strength left among eight bodies.

"Juniper," Mara called softly from the rear. "Look at the sky. That's not stars coming up over the ridge."

She was right. It was lantern-light — many lanterns, strung along the high shoulder of the pass like a rosary, moving fast.

Not Renard's men. Renard's men were behind us.

Somebody else knew the old route. And they were coming down to meet us.
