## 16. The Bridge Fight

The bridge was older than any of us and it showed. The Porter's Road had carried us three days up from the glacier ruins, switchbacking through a country of black rock and thin yellow grass, until the mountain simply gave up pretending to be solid and split open into a gorge so deep the river at its bottom sounded like a rumor. Spanning that gap: a rope bridge, maybe sixty meters of braided hide and cable, sagging in the middle like a tired hammock, its planks missing more often than not.

Ilsa Berríos looked at it the way a woman looks at an old dog she knows is dying.

"El Paso del Cordel," she said. "My grandmother crossed this when she was a girl. It has been waiting for someone heavy ever since."

"Comforting," I said.

"It is not meant to comfort. It is meant to be true." She turned to survey our little procession — me, Mara riding double with Tomás because her fever had broken but her strength hadn't come back, Luz walking beside them with her chin up and her eyes doing arithmetic on every shadow, and old Aurelio the muleteer bringing up the rear with four mules who were smarter than all of us put together, because they did not want to cross that bridge at all.

Behind us, half a day back and closing, Renard's party. We'd bought ourselves time with the raid at dawn, freed Luz and the spokesman Mateo, and spent the whole day paying for it in altitude. Every meter of climbing was a meter Renard's men didn't have to climb, except they were fresh and we were not, and the snowline was coming down to meet us like a lid.

"We cross light," I said. "Mules first or last?"

"Last," Ilsa said. "A mule on a rope bridge is a negotiation, not a crossing."

We got everyone across by late afternoon — Mara white-knuckled and silent, Tomás talking to her the whole way in a low steady stream about nothing, about river fish and engine oil, anything to keep her mind off the void breathing up between the planks. Luz crossed like she'd done it a hundred times, which maybe she had; mountain kids grow up with bridges like these the way river kids grow up with sandbars. Mateo went across carrying the testimony in his head, all those names and dates and village boundaries, and I watched him walk and thought: there goes the dam project, sixty meters at a time.

Then the mules. Aurelio coaxed, hauled, sang to them, cursed their grandmothers. Two went across. The third made it halfway and froze, and it took forty minutes and most of my dignity to get him moving again. The fourth — a gray gelding with opinions — refused entirely, and by then the light was going copper and then gray, and Ilsa stood at the far end looking back across the gorge with an expression I'd learned to read over the past week.

"They are closer than half a day," she said.

"How close?"

"Close enough that we do not sleep."

* * *

We didn't. We camped fifty meters past the bridge in a fold of rock where a fire would be invisible from the far side, and I set watches, and I sat awake during mine listening to the wind come up the gorge and thinking about what Ilsa had told us around the fire two nights ago — how the route itself was made by people running from men exactly like Renard, centuries ago, how every safe passage on that map was a scar somebody else had earned. History rhyming. She'd said it like a warning. I was starting to hear it like instructions.

It was full dark, maybe two hours past midnight, when the dogs started.

Renard's party had dogs. We'd heard them before, baying up the switchbacks, and each time the sound had faded behind us and we'd let ourselves believe. This time it didn't fade. It came up the trail below the bridge with the particular confidence of animals that have caught what they're chasing, and under the baying there were voices, and lantern-light bloomed in the dark on the far side like a bad idea catching fire.

I woke the others without words. You learn that on a riverboat — hand signals, pressure on a shoulder, the difference between *get up quietly* and *get up now*. This was the second kind.

They reached the near anchorage within minutes. Lanterns swung. A voice — not Renard's, one of his lieutenants, a man named Corvo whom I'd last seen offering to buy my engine parts at a price no honest person pays — called across the gorge, pleasant as a bank clerk.

"Señora Villalobos! The señor makes a final courtesy. Cross back, bring the case, and everyone walks away. Including the children. He is a reasonable man, the señor. He prefers reasonable outcomes."

I looked at Mara. Mara looked at the case — the oilskin case, the map, the thing we had climbed a mountain with, the thing I had already decided in some wordless part of myself that I would burn before I handed over, though I hadn't said it out loud yet even to myself. She shook her head once.

I leaned over the edge of our rocks and shouted back into the dark. "Tell your señor the river says no!"

There was a pause. Then Corvo laughed, and the laugh was worse than the dogs.

"As you wish. But the bridge is ours now, señora. And you are on the wrong side of it."

Lanterns converged on the near anchorage. I heard the ring of a blade being tested against rope, and my stomach dropped, because I understood the play a half-second too late: they weren't coming across. They were going to cut the bridge behind us and leave us stranded on the mountain with winter coming down, no supplies, no way forward or back, to freeze or starve or crawl down to wherever Renard decided to collect us. A reasonable outcome. No blood on the consortium's hands. Just weather.

Except cutting a rope bridge takes longer than angry men think it does, especially an old one anchored in stone, and Ilsa Berríos was already moving.

She'd been quiet all evening, sitting apart, and I'd thought it was grief or fatigue. It was neither. It was arithmetic. While Corvo was making his speech she had untied the small knife from her belt — the one she used for cutting kindling and trimming mule hooves, a working tool, nothing dramatic — and she had looked at the bridge's far anchorage, the one on *our* side, the one holding the whole structure taut, and she had measured something in her face that I recognized because I measure it every time I read water before a rapid.

"Ilsa," I said. "What are you doing."

"What my grandmother's people did," she said, "when the empire's men came up the valley. You leave the road behind you unusable. You make the mountain your ally instead of theirs." She looked at me, and her eyes in the starlight were calm as deep water. "The anchorage is old. The cables are held by a wooden drum and a lock-pin. One cut, here—" she touched a point on the main cable where it fed into the drum "—and the bridge falls into the gorge. They cannot follow tonight. Perhaps not this season."

"And you can't cut it from here," I said. "You have to be out on it."

"The pin is at the third plank," she said. "Yes."

"No," said Tomás.

"No," said Luz, sharper.

"Yes," said Ilsa, gently, the way you agree with children who haven't yet learned what things cost. "And you will all be well past the bend before I finish, and I will follow along the ledge — there is a ledge, an old one, the ancestors' path — and we will meet above the icefall by morning."

She was lying. I knew she was lying because I am an excellent liar and I know the grain of a lie when I hear one, the way it's sanded smooth on purpose. There was a ledge, maybe. In the dark. Under new snow. For a woman of seventy years with bad knees.

The dogs were surging onto the bridge. That decided it, if a decision was ever really available. Renard's men had gotten impatient with the cutting and were coming across after all — six, seven shapes on the swaying cables, lanterns bobbing, the whole span groaning like a ship in weather — and Ilsa slipped out of the rocks and onto the planks before anyone could grab her.

What happened next happened fast and is still happening, somewhere in me, slower.

Tomás went after her. Of course he did. I grabbed for him and got a fistful of coat and lost him, because a boy raised on the streets of San Vega moves through a crowd the way water moves through a net. Luz screamed his name and the name cracked across the gorge and one of the men on the bridge turned his lantern toward the sound, and then everything was noise and swinging light.

I went out onto the bridge after both of them, because apparently I had also failed to learn what things cost.

A rope bridge at night in the wind is not a place, it's an argument. The span bucked under us. Planks tilted into nothing. Below, the river-sound rose up like applause from a very large and unfriendly audience. Ahead, Ilsa had reached the third plank and was kneeling at the anchorage drum, sawing at the lock-pin with her kindling knife, unhurried, precise, a woman doing a chore. Behind her, Tomás clung to the side cables ten meters out, frozen between courage and terror. Behind *him*, the first of Renard's men — big, lantern in his teeth, machete out — was twenty meters away and gaining, because he weighed twice what Ilsa did and the bridge answered weight.

"Juniper!" Mara's voice from the rocks, raw. "The cable — they're cutting the far side too—"

Of course they were. Corvo wasn't stupid. If the bridge was falling either way, they wanted us on it when it went.

I stopped thinking like a person and started thinking like a pilot. Current, load, trim. The bridge was a hull; the men on it were cargo badly distributed; and the anchorage drum was the rudder post, and whoever controlled the drum controlled which way the whole structure swung. Ilsa had the knife. What she needed was time and weight on the right side.

"Tomás!" I roared. "To me! Now!" And I hauled on the side cable, hard, in rhythm, throwing my weight against the span's natural swing until the bridge began to roll — a slow sickening pendulum, planks tilting fifteen degrees, twenty — and the big man with the machete lost his lantern to the gorge and his footing a half-second later, and hung there swearing in the dark like a man hanging from a yardarm.

Tomás crawled to me through the rolling dark. I got an arm around him and pressed him flat against the planks.

"Ilsa!" I shouted. "Now or never!"

She didn't hurry. That's what I'll remember. The pin parted with a crack like a rifle shot, and the whole bridge dropped a foot and rolled hard to port, and the screaming started — from the men on the span, from the men on the far shore, from Luz on the rocks, maybe from me, I honestly don't know.

"Go!" Ilsa shouted, standing up on the dying bridge, waving us toward the rocks. "Both of you! Go!"

We went. Hand over hand, plank to plank, the span lurching and unraveling beneath us, and I want to tell you I was brave and deliberate but the truth is my body did it while my mind stood aside and watched, the way it does in rapids. We hit the rocks. Hands pulled us in — Mara, Luz, Mateo, Aurelio, all of them hauling like a deck crew in a squall.

I turned back to the bridge.

Ilsa was at the anchorage, knife out, cutting the last strand of the main cable, and two of Renard's men had reached her — the ones who hadn't fallen, who'd kept coming through all of it, because men like that don't know when a thing is finished. She didn't run. She looked at them, and then — I swear this — she looked across the gorge at us, and she smiled, a quick dry twist, the smile of a woman who has just won an argument.

Then she cut the cable.

The bridge died all at once. The whole span folded inward and downward with a sound like the world clearing its throat, cables whipping, planks cartwheeling into the dark, and Ilsa Berríos went with it, upright, unafraid, taking two of Renard's men with her into the gorge and the river-sound below.

The dark afterward was total. Even the lanterns were gone.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a long time. The wind filled in where the voices had been.

Luz broke first — not crying, worse, a kind of silent shaking, and Tomás held onto her the way he'd held onto Mara on the crossing, talking low and steady about nothing, about anything, and I understood that he'd learned that trick from watching adults fail at comforting him, and had decided to be better at it than we were.

Mara found my hand in the dark and gripped it. Her palm was fever-hot again. "She knew," she whispered. "She knew the ledge wasn't real."

"She knew," I said.

"Why?"

Because history rhymes, I almost said. Because her grandmother's people built this road running from men like Renard, and she was not going to let them catch us on it. Because some people spend their whole lives keeping a door open and will lie down in the doorway when the wolves come. But what I said was:

"Because it was hers to give. And she gave it."

Aurelio appeared out of the dark leading the two mules we'd already brought across, and reported the rest of the ledger in the flat voice of a man reading accounts: the other two mules, the feed, most of the food, the tent canvas, the spare rope — all of it on the far side with Renard's party now, or scattered down the trail where the mules had bolted when the dogs came. What remained was what we carried: some dried meat, a bag of flour, Mara's medicine, the engine parts, the case.

Sixty meters of bridge, gone. Two men of Renard's, gone. Ilsa Berríos, guide, historian, keeper of the ancestors' road, gone into the same gorge her grandmother's people had crossed fleeing the same kind of men.

Every mile we gained from here was going to cost like this. I understood that now. Grief wasn't waiting for us at the end of the journey; it was the toll, collected at each gate, and we would pay it or stop moving.

"We can't stay," Mateo said quietly. "When the light comes, they'll find the ledge paths. There are always ledge paths. We need to be above the icefall by dawn."

Above the icefall. Where Ilsa had promised to meet us.

We walked. All night, single file, Luz's hand locked in Tomás's, Mara leaning on my shoulder by turns, the stars wheeling cold and indifferent overhead. Somewhere behind us, faint, the dogs were quiet at last, and I chose to believe it was because there was no longer anywhere for them to go.

Just before dawn, at the top of the icefall where the old path met the high route, we found a cairn that hadn't been there before — stones stacked fresh, deliberate, in the old style Ilsa had pointed out to us in the ruins under the glacier. On top of it, wedged against the wind, a strip of hide with markings burned into it. Route signs. The ancestors' script.

Tomás read them aloud, slowly, sounding them out the way she'd taught him only days ago. His voice cracked in the middle and he kept going anyway.

"*The road remembers,*" he said. "*Walk on.*"

Mara wept. Luz wept. I stood there holding a dead woman's directions and felt something in my chest that wasn't grief alone, though grief was most of it — the rest was a fierce, furious gratitude, the kind that doesn't kneel, it stands.

Then the eastern sky went gray, then pink, and we saw, far below on the trail we'd abandoned, tiny as insects, lanterns moving. Renard's men, finding the wreck of the bridge, beginning to hunt for another way across.

And higher up, where the sky met the ridge line, a different color altogether: a thin white veil on the peaks that hadn't been there yesterday, thickening even as we watched.

The first snow. Early. Coming down to meet us.

"Walk on," I said, and shouldered the pack, and led them up into it.
