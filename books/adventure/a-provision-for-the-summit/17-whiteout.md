## 17. Whiteout

**Juniper**

The mountain stopped pretending to be a place and became a white argument instead.

We'd made maybe four miles past the bridge before the sky came down on us like a dropped ceiling. One moment I could see the switchbacks below us, the black thread of the river far beneath; the next, the world was a fistful of static, and the wind was speaking a language that had no words for *mercy*.

"Snow cave," Ilsa had said — no, Ilsa wasn't with us anymore. That was the wound we were all carrying, wrapped up in silence like a hot coal passed hand to hand so nobody had to hold it long. It was Mara who said it now, shouting over the wind, pointing at a bulge of wind-packed drift against the cliff face where the slope curled inward like a cupped palm.

I'd dug snow caves before. Once, as a girl, for fun, which tells you something about the childhood I ran from. You don't dig a cave so much as negotiate one — tunneling into the drift at an upward angle so the cold pools out the entrance and your body heat does the rest of the work. The trick is patience, and I have never once in my life been accused of having any.

But grief makes you patient, or it makes you stupid, and I suppose I got to choose which flavor that afternoon.

It took two hours. By the time I broke through into a chamber big enough for five people to sit without touching — though we touched anyway, because warmth is a currency and we were all broke — my hands were claws and Tomás had to pull me inside by the collar like a kitten by the scruff.

The storm sealed the entrance behind us within the hour. And then there was nothing to do but be alive at each other, which is harder work than it sounds.

* * *

**Tomás**

Day one, nobody talked.

I counted what we had, because counting is something I'm good at. Sixteen biscuits, if you didn't count the crumbs, and I counted the crumbs too, because I've been hungry before in ways these adults haven't, or they hide it better. Half a wheel of cheese gone sweaty in its cloth. A tin of rendered fat. Mara's fever medicine, nine pills, which she kept trying not to take and Juniper kept making her take, in the voice she used when she wasn't asking.

Juniper's voice has gears. Most people only find out about the low ones when it's too late.

Luz would have liked her, I thought, and then I thought about Luz, and then I stopped thinking on purpose, because thinking about Luz in a hole in a mountain while men who took her were somewhere behind us in the storm — that was a road with no bottom.

So I watched instead. Watching is free.

Mara slept and sweated and slept. The muleteers' boy, Esteban, who'd stayed with us past San Vega because he had nowhere else and no sense of self-preservation, whittled a stick into nothing. Juniper sat facing the sealed entrance like she expected the weather to apologize.

On day two, the quiet broke.

It broke because Juniper started talking, and what came out of her mouth was so far from anything I expected that I forgot to pretend I wasn't listening.

* * *

**Mara**

Fever is honest in a way sobriety never manages. On the second day I floated somewhere above my own body, watching the cave through warm water, and I heard Juniper Villalobos say:

"I killed eleven people."

Esteban's knife stopped moving. Tomás went very still, the way animals go still.

"Not on purpose," she said, to the wall of snow, because looking at us was apparently more than the story could bear. "That's the part I've spent nine years hiding behind. Not on purpose. As if that's a whole sentence."

I knew some of this. River towns talk, and I'd lived on the Cobre long enough before the expedition to have heard the shape of the rumor — the Villalobos girl, the flood year, the family that scattered like spilled grain. Rumors are bones. She was giving us the blood.

"There was a sluice gate above our town," she said. "Old, badly maintained, owned by a sugar concern that had gone bankrupt and left it to rot. My father wrote letters about it for three years. I know because I helped him seal them." Her laugh was terrible. "I was seventeen and I thought sealing envelopes was civic duty. The spring melt came early that year. There was a warning system — a bell, a runner, a chain of riders down the valley. My job, that night, was the lower quarter. The riverfront. Where the poorest families lived, because cheap land floods first, that's the arithmetic nobody teaches you in school but every river kid knows."

She stopped. In the blue half-light of the cave her face looked carved, older than her years, which I understood — fevers do that, strip you down to the load-bearing parts.

"I decided the bell could wait. There was a boy. There's always a boy in these stories, isn't there? He'd asked me to meet him. I told myself ten minutes wouldn't matter. I told myself the water hadn't come up past the second marker yet." Her hands opened and closed in her lap. "The gate let go while I was kissing him in a boathouse. Eleven people drowned in the lower quarter. Two of them were children. One of them was Doña Ferreira, who taught me my letters, who was deaf, who never even heard the bell I finally rang."

Nobody moved. Even the storm seemed to lean in.

"My family never said I killed them. That would have been cruel, and they weren't cruel, mostly. What they said was worse. They said *it wasn't your fault*, in voices that meant *we will never look at you the same way again*. My mother stopped meeting my eyes. My brother took the family debt — the boat loans, the compensation payments — onto himself so I could leave, and I let him, because leaving was the only thing I could think of that felt like penance. Nine years of running up and down the same river, and I called it freedom because calling it punishment hurt too much."

Tomás spoke for the first time in hours. "Was it your fault?"

And here is the thing I will remember until I die, in whatever valley they drown or don't drown: she didn't flinch.

"No," she said. "Ten minutes wouldn't have saved them. The engineers' report later said the gate was failing regardless — the melt just chose the hour. But I needed it to be my fault for a long time, because if it was my fault, then the world made sense. Bad things happen because someone deserves them. If it wasn't anyone's fault, then it was just water, and water doesn't care, and I couldn't live in a world where water doesn't care." She looked at each of us, one by one. "Turns out I can. Took me a blizzard and a dead friend to learn it, but here we are."

* * *

**Tomás**

After that, the cave turned into a market. Stories for food. That's how Juniper put it — she handed out biscuits like a dealer laying cards and said, "Truth costs a biscuit. Lying costs two, and I count."

Esteban paid his biscuit for a story about a mule named Constancia who hated him specifically and had once eaten his hat off his head while he wore it. It was a good trade. We laughed until Mara coughed, and laughing in a snow cave with a fevered woman and no idea what was happening outside felt like stealing something.

Then it was my turn, and I'd known since the bridge that it would come to this eventually. Juniper looked at me across the little flame of the candle stub — we'd burned it down to almost nothing, saving it — and she didn't ask. That's the thing about her. She waits like the river waits.

"I stowed away on purpose," I said. "Not running from something. Running toward."

"Luz," Mara said softly.

"Our parents died in the fever year. Cholera, they said, though nobody ever confirmed anything, because nobody official comes to villages like ours unless they want something. Luz is two years older than me. When they died, she became — everything. Mother, father, the both of them rolled into one person who was sixteen and scared out of her mind and never once let me see it." I picked at the biscuit I wasn't eating. "Then the consortium men came. Buying land rights, they said. Surveying. Luz went to the meetings because she read faster than anyone in the village and she wanted to understand the papers before she signed anything. And then one morning she was gone, and the men were gone, and the village headman said she'd taken employment in the mining camp. Good wages. A future."

"You didn't believe him," Juniper said.

"The headman owed the consortium money." I shrugged. "Also, Luz would never have left without telling me. She woke me before dawn every day of her life to make me check the fish traps with her. She'd have woken me to say goodbye. So when the supply boats started running upriver for the survey, I hid in a crate." I looked at my hands. "I picked your boat because you were the smallest. I figured the smallest captain would notice a stowaway fastest, and being caught by someone kind beats starving in a hold."

Juniper snorted. "Flattery costs two biscuits."

"It wasn't flattery. It was research. I watched how you argued with the harbormaster about mooring fees and decided you were angry but fair, which is the best combination available on that river."

For a second — just a second — she smiled. Then it went away, the way her smiles do, folded up and put in a pocket.

"And Renard's men have her," Mara said. "You're sure?"

"I saw her." My voice cracked and I hated it. "At the bridge parley. Behind the launch, in the tent they keep prisoners in. She was thinner. Her hair was different. But she was standing the way she stands when she's counting exits." I made myself look up. "I should have told you all before now. I told myself it would distract from the map, from the summit, from — everything. But really I was afraid you'd decide she wasn't worth the risk. That I wasn't worth the risk. One boy's sister against three villages' testimony."

The candle flame leaned in the draft from the entrance, though the entrance was packed solid with snow, and I remember thinking even fire was listening.

Juniper reached across the space between us and put her hand flat on top of my head, the way you'd steady something valuable on a shelf.

"Listen to me, Tomás. We are not a cargo operation anymore. Nobody aboard this — this *expedition* — gets weighed against anybody else. Not Luz, not the villages, not me." Her jaw worked. "Especially not me. I spent nine years doing that math and the answer always came out wrong, because the math itself is wrong. You don't trade people. You spend yourself for them, if it comes to that, and you don't call it a loss."

"That's a terrible financial philosophy," Mara murmured, feverish, from her nest of blankets.

"It's why I'm poor," Juniper agreed. "Eat your biscuit."

* * *

**Mara**

The third day, the storm began to tire. We could hear it in the change of pitch against the entrance plug — the shriek dropping to a growl, the growl to a mutter, like an old man arguing with God and losing interest mid-sentence.

By then the stories had run through all of us. Esteban confessed he wasn't fourteen, he was twelve, and we fed him double rations out of spite against whoever had lied about his age to get him work. I told them about the distress call — the one I sent from the gorge, the one that brought Renard's launch upstream like sharks to a cut line. I'd been carrying that guilt since the oilskin case, and saying it aloud in the dark, to people who couldn't leave even if they wanted to, was its own strange surgery. Juniper listened and then said, "You called for help because people were dying. Renard answered because he's a vulture. The fault lands where the intent lives," and I cried, quietly, feverishly, and nobody made it strange.

Toward evening of the third day, Tomás dug at the entrance with the pan, testing the pack. Snow crumbled. Beyond it, the light had changed — gone from blind white to something with gold in it.

"Clearing," he said, and the word went around the cave like a struck bell.

We broke out into a world remade. The storm had erased the mountains and redrawn them higher, sharper, the peaks burning white under a sky scrubbed down to pure blue. Cold enough to crack teeth. Beautiful enough to forgive it.

Juniper stood at the lip of the cave, scanning the slopes below us, reading the terrain the way she read sandbars — and I saw her body change before I understood why. Every muscle in her back locked.

"Juniper?" I said.

She raised one hand for silence. Downslope, perhaps half a mile, in the bowl of a frozen streambed sheltered from the wind, there was smoke. Thin, disciplined, banked fires — the fires of men who knew someone might be watching. And beside the nearest fire, small figures moved between tents, figures that did not move freely.

One of them stood apart from the rest, arms crossed, face turned up toward our ridge. Even at that distance, even after everything, Tomás made a sound I'll hear for the rest of my life.

"That's her," he whispered. "That's Luz."

Juniper lowered the hand slowly. When she turned to us, her eyes had gone the flat bright color of river ice, and I knew — we all knew — that whatever she was about to say, it had already cost her the summit window, and she was going to say it anyway.

"They'll break camp at first light," she said. "So we move tonight."
