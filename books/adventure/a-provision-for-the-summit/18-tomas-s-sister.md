## 18. Tomás's Sister

The storm broke sometime in the small hours, the way storms do up there — not gradually but all at once, like a door flung open. I woke to silence so complete it rang in my ears, and to light coming through the snow cave's mouth that wasn't the gray gruel of the last three days but something clean and blue and merciless.

I crawled out into a world that had been scoured and polished overnight. The Cordillera del Viento stood around us like a jury that had finally finished deliberating. Every ridge was sharp-edged, every shadow cut with a knife, and the sky above was the deep, indifferent blue you only get at altitude — the kind of sky that makes you feel like an insect on a very old table.

"Juniper." Mara's voice behind me, hoarse from the fever she'd been fighting since the Porter's Road. She'd turned a corner on it two days ago, thank whatever gods mind riverboats, but her voice still sounded like gravel sifted through honey. "You should see this."

"I'm seeing it."

"No. You should *see* this."

She pointed down the mountainside, east and below, where the terrain stepped down in a long staircase of granite shelves toward a bowl of forest still holding its white burden. And there, in the middle of the bowl, sat a camp.

Even at this distance I knew what I was looking at. Black canvas. The consortium didn't buy any other color; I suppose they thought it looked serious. Tents in a neat grid, a cookfire's smoke standing straight up in the windless morning, tethered mules stamping little craters in the snow. Half a day's climb down. Maybe less, if you didn't care about arriving quiet.

And near the fire, moving slow, huddled in blankets that were the wrong color for prisoners — no, wait. Prisoners don't get blankets. But they weren't moving like free people either. Three figures, maybe four, sitting apart from the rest of the camp in a way that had nothing to do with cold and everything to do with guards. Two men with rifles slung across their backs paced a lazy circuit around them, the way you pace around something you've been told is valuable but not fragile.

"Oh," said Tomás.

He'd come out of the cave without me hearing him, which at fourteen you shouldn't be able to do, but he'd had nine years of practice being unheard, first as a stowaway and then as the quiet boy everyone forgot to count. He stood at the edge of the shelf with the sun full on his face and his whole body gone rigid, the way rigging goes just before something parts.

"That's them," he said. "That's Luz."

* * *

Let me back up, because I've been telling this story out of order in my own head for days and it's starting to tangle.

Three days in a snow cave on short rations does something to people. It strips away the polite scaffolding. We'd traded stories like food — that's what Mara called it, and she was right, because hunger of the body and hunger of the truth feed each other. I told them about the flood. All of it, the parts I'd spent nine years not saying: how I'd read the water wrong, or right, depending on who you asked, and how my reading hadn't saved anyone, and how my family's grief needed somewhere to go and I was the only one still standing to put it in. How I'd taken the Marisol downriver three days later and kept going for nine years, calling it freedom, knowing it was penance.

Tomás had answered with his own confession, delivered flat and fast, the way boys deliver things they've rehearsed until the rehearsal wore smooth: he hadn't stowed away on the supply run by accident. Renard's men had taken his sister Luz from the village of Arroyo Hondo when it burned — taken her, along with the villagers' spokesman and two others, as "persons of interest," which is what men with rifles call hostages when they want to sleep at night. Tomás had gotten aboard the Marisol because a boat going upstream toward the expedition was the only thread leading anywhere near where Luz had gone, and he'd meant to follow it alone if he had to.

He'd been eleven when they took her. He was fourteen now. He'd spent three years collecting information the way other children collect stones, and he'd never once said any of it out loud until that night in the cave.

Now he stood on the ledge looking down at a black camp in a white bowl, and every stone he'd ever collected was in his face.

"How many guards?" I asked, because someone had to ask practical questions, and apparently that job was mine forever.

"Two visible. There'll be more." His voice came out steady, which frightened me more than crying would have. "Renard keeps six men minimum. You saw that at the bridge."

I had seen it. I'd also seen Ilsa Berríos cut a rope bridge with two of Renard's men on it, and I'd seen her fall, and I carried that the way I carried the flood — folded flat against my ribs where it didn't show. Six men minus two left four, plus whoever Renard hired locally, plus Renard himself, who fought like a man doing arithmetic rather than a man doing violence, which made him worse.

"We're not doing anything stupid," I said.

Tomás didn't answer. That was answer enough.

* * *

We held council in the cave mouth, sitting on packs, breathing steam. Present: myself; Mara Quill, botanist, wounded, lucid, and dangerous in the way that people are dangerous when they've stopped fearing death and started finding it inconvenient; Tomás; and old Cipriano, our senior muleteer, who had lost his mules at the bridge and stayed anyway, and who had opinions about everything and shared roughly none of them unless asked twice.

Below us, half a day's hard descent, sat the camp. Above us, another day and a half of climbing, sat the summit pass — the window we'd been racing since San Vega. Ilsa had told us the pass closed for the season when the deep snows came, and the deep snows were already scratching at the door. Every hour the sky stayed clear was an hour we could climb. Every hour it stayed clear was also an hour Renard could see us, and an hour his radio — yes, they had a radio, the consortium always had a radio — could call ahead to whatever waited on the far side.

"The arithmetic is simple," Mara said. "We climb now, we probably make the pass. We detour down to that camp, we almost certainly don't."

"Thank you, Mara, very comforting."

"I'm not finished. The arithmetic is simple and it is also wrong." She shifted, wincing at her side, where the wound from the gorge had healed crooked and angry. "Because if we climb past that camp with those people sitting in it, we're not the found family we've been telling ourselves we are. We're just five survivors with good posture."

Cipriano spat over the edge, ceremonially. "The boy's sister," he said, which was all he said, which was all he needed to say.

I looked at Tomás. He was trying very hard to look like someone who would accept a refusal gracefully, and failing completely, and I loved him for it, and I hated what I was about to say, and I said it anyway.

"We're going down there."

Mara closed her eyes. Not in despair — in something closer to relief, though she'd have denied it under oath.

"But hear me," I went on, before anyone got sentimental. "We do this my way. Nobody plays hero. Nobody dies for a map or a sister or a principle. We go down, we look, and if there's no way to do it clean, we walk away and live to try again. Luz has been a prisoner for three years. She will still be a prisoner tomorrow. Dead rescuers help nobody."

Tomás nodded. Then, quietly, with the terrible precision of a child who has thought about this longer than any child should: "She's been a prisoner for three years because everyone who could have helped decided it wasn't clean."

The wind moved through the rocks above us, whistling low across the stone — the singing pass, Ilsa used to call it, before she became part of its history. I let the sound sit between us for a moment.

"You're right," I said. "So we'd better make it count."

* * *

We spent the day preparing, and I'll tell you what preparing looks like when you have no weapons worth the name, no numbers, and no element of surprise except the one the weather might lend you.

It looks like a woman studying a hillside through a cracked spyglass for four hours. It looks like a feverish botanist sketching the camp layout from memory and guesswork, annotating it with the calm of someone cataloguing alpine flora: *cookfire here, likely guard rotation there, mule lines downwind — noise.* It looks like an old man testing every length of rope twice and muttering to his saints. It looks like a boy teaching me, patiently, without once making me feel foolish, everything he knows about Renard's habits gathered over three years of listening in ports and mining camps: that Renard posts his sentries where they can smoke; that he trusts schedules more than instincts; that he sleeps badly and wakes early and is most dangerous in the hour before dawn, when his arithmetic is freshest.

"And when is he least dangerous?" I asked.

Tomás considered. "Never, really. But after dark he drinks exactly one glass of wine, because he says a clear head is a professional obligation. So — after the wine. Before he sleeps. There's a gap."

"A gap," I repeated. "Our entire plan rests on a gap the size of a glass of wine."

"It's a good wine," Tomás said gravely. "He talked about it once. From the valley. He's particular."

I laughed. I couldn't help it — the laugh came up out of me sideways, rough with fear, and Tomás looked briefly scandalized, and then, to my surprise, pleased. Nine years I'd been running the Marisol alone, and it had taken a blizzard and a war and a fourteen-year-old sommelier of enemy intelligence to get a laugh out of me at altitude.

Mara watched us both with an expression I couldn't read until she said, softly, "This is what family looks like, isn't it. Planning crimes together."

"Rescues," I said. "Crimes are when you take people. This is giving one back."

* * *

Dusk came early, as dusk does in a valley that never sees the sun straight on. We left camp at full dark — Cipriano staying high with the packs and Mara, over her furious objection, because Mara could barely walk on flat ground and everyone including Mara knew it. Her objection lasted eleven minutes, which I counted, and then she gripped my wrist hard and said, "Bring her back. Bring all of them back. And Juniper — if it comes to choosing between the mission and the boy, choose the boy. The mission can grieve. The boy can't afford to lose anyone else."

"He can't afford to lose me either."

"He doesn't know that yet," Mara said. "Give him time."

We went down through the trees single file, Tomás leading, because it turned out the boy who'd grown up in these foothills read winter forest the way I read water — by the lean of things, by the weight of silence, by the small wrongnesses that shout at you if you've learned the language. Snow squeaked under our boots in a rhythm I hated, because squeaking carries, so we walked on the wind-packed drifts where the surface held, and twice we stopped for ten minutes at a stretch because some animal somewhere had stopped, and Tomás wanted to know why.

The camp glow showed through the trees well before the camp did — orange light on black canvas, the smell of woodsmoke and, underneath it, mule and leather and men. We lay in a drift at the treeline and watched.

Four guards that we could count. Two by the fire, playing cards by lantern-light, rifles leaned against a log like afterthoughts. One walking the perimeter, unhurried, smoking. One sitting on a crate near the prisoners' shelter — a lean-to off the main tent, open-fronted, where four shapes lay wrapped in blankets. My heart did something complicated when I counted four. Three years ago there had been four taken from Arroyo Hondo. Four still alive. Whatever else the consortium was, they hadn't starved their leverage.

Luz was the second shape from the left. I knew it before Tomás breathed her name, because even lying down, even at eighty meters, she had the same stillness he had — the stillness of someone practicing being invisible. She was awake. She was watching the sentry on the crate with an expression I recognized from mirrors, from nine years ago, from the night I decided to leave: the expression of someone measuring a distance they intend to close.

"She's planning something," Tomás whispered. "Look at her eyes. She's counting his steps."

Of course she was. Three years a prisoner and she was counting steps. The Villalobos in me — the runner, the reader of currents, the woman who'd spent nearly a decade telling herself she owed the world nothing — sat up and took notice. You don't leave a person like that in a cage. You especially don't leave her there when her brother is lying in the snow beside you vibrating like a struck cable.

Here was the shape of it, as best we could read it through the trees: the perimeter walker circled on a loop of perhaps eight minutes, and the trees came within thirty meters of the lean-to on its blind side. The card players faced the fire. The crate-sitter faced the prisoners, but he was cold and bored and his attention slid, the way bored attention does. Between the walker's passes there was a window — narrow, human-sized, wine-gap sized.

"We're not fighting four men," I whispered to Tomás. "We're stealing one person from under four men's noses, and then we are running uphill faster than anything has ever run uphill in the history of this mountain."

"And the others?"

I looked at the other three shapes. The villagers' spokesman, old Mateo Herrera, whose testimony was worth more than any map. Two others whose names I'd learned in the cave and whose faces I was only now putting to them.

"All of them," I heard myself say. "If we're doing this, we're doing all of them. Four prisoners, one pass, one window." I exhaled slowly, watching my breath hang and fade. "God help us, we're doing all of them."

Tomás turned and looked at me in the dark, and I felt it land — the thing Mara had said, the thing the whole mountain seemed to have been waiting for me to say. The found family committing fully to its own. Not just the boy. Not just the convenient ones. Everyone in the cage, because a family that saves only its favorites is just a crew with better manners.

"When?" he asked.

I studied the fire, the walkers, the stars just beginning to burn through the clearing sky overhead — beautiful, treacherous stars, because every clear hour was a gift to Renard's radio as much as to our legs.

"Tomorrow night," I said. "Tonight we watch. We learn their loop until we could sing it. And then tomorrow, after the wine—" 

A lantern flared in the main tent. A figure stepped out into the firelight, tall, unhurried, adjusting his cuffs against the cold even at this distance, even here, even now.

Renard walked to the lean-to, crouched, and spoke to the prisoners. I saw Luz sit up. I saw him hold something out to her — paper, a letter, an offer, God knew what — and I saw her take it, and I saw her look at it for a long moment before she looked up at him and shook her head, slow and deliberate, the way you refuse a man you want to be certain understands.

Renard stood, tucked the paper away, and said something to the crate-sitter. The crate-sitter laughed.

Tomás's hand found my sleeve in the dark and gripped it.

"What did she say no to?" he whispered.

"I don't know," I said. "But tomorrow we're going to ask her ourselves."

Above us, the stars wheeled clear and cold, and the summit window hung open like a door — and down in the black camp, Renard poured his one glass of wine, and the clock inside my chest started running.
