## 19. The Summit Window

**Juniper**

There is a particular hour before dawn when the cold stops being weather and becomes an opinion about you personally. I'd been awake in it for two hours, lying against Ilsa's pack with my knife open across my knee, listening to the mountain decide whether to kill us.

Above us, maybe four hundred meters up the shoulder of scree, Renard's camp glowed like a boil — three tents, a cook fire banked low, two sentries walking a lazy circuit that took them past the prisoners' shelter every eleven minutes. I'd counted. Eleven minutes, give or take the time it takes a bored man to relieve himself behind a rock. Bored men are predictable. It's one of the few mercies this world offers.

Beside me, Tomás was counting too. He'd been counting since midnight, lips moving, fingers twitching, a boy doing arithmetic with his whole body because his sister was on the other side of the sum.

"Eleven minutes," he whispered.

"I know."

"The sentry on the left drinks from his flask every third pass. He's slow after."

"You've been watching him drink?"

"He's watching us starve. Seems fair."

I almost laughed, which would have been a disaster, so I turned it into something that sounded like coughing. Below us in the snow cave, the others were waking without being told — Mara binding her fevered arm tight, the villagers' muleteers checking rope, old Ilsa's nephew Ferro running the whetstone along his machete with the patience of a man sharpening more than steel.

The plan was simple, which is another way of saying it was terrible. Simple plans are what you make when you have no supplies, no mules, half a day's lead gone to nothing, and a window of good weather that Ilsa — before she fell, before the bridge, before we lost her — had read in the clouds like a woman reading a letter from home.

"The pass closes tomorrow night," she'd said. "After that, the Cordillera keeps you until spring. If you mean to cross, you cross *through* them, not around."

So: through.

We went up the scree in the last dark, twelve of us strung out on a rope like beads on a string someone might drop. The glacier above the camp groaned once, long and deep, the sound a house makes settling. Nobody looked up. Looking up at glaciers is how you learn things you can't unlearn.

Tomás moved ahead of me, small and quick as a river bird, and I thought — not for the first time — that the boy who'd stowed away in my cargo hold nine chapters of misery ago had become something I didn't have a word for. Not cargo. Not passenger. Crew.

The sentries changed at first light. We hit them between changes, which was either brilliant planning or luck wearing brilliant planning's coat. Ferro took the left one down quiet; the right one got half a shout out before Mara's muleteer put him in the snow. Then we were among the tents, and the whole camp came apart like a card house in wind.

I kicked in the prisoners' shelter expecting the worst and found Luz first — sixteen years old, wrists raw, standing with her back straight as a mast, and beside her a gray-bearded man I knew from Mara's descriptions: Aurelio Campos, spokesman for the three villages, the man whose testimony was worth more than any map ever drawn.

"Juniper Villalobos," he said, like he'd been expecting me. "Mara said you'd come."

"Mara's optimistic."

"She said you'd complain the whole way and come anyway." He was already moving, testing his legs. "She was right about both."

Luz saw Tomás over my shoulder and made a sound I'll carry to my grave — not a word, just a note, high and cracked, the sound of a thing long-broken fitting back together. They collided. He held her the way you hold something you've already lost once and refuse to lose twice, and I stood there in the wreck of Renard's camp with snow starting to sift down and thought: *this*. This is what I ran from for nine years. Not blame. This.

Then a tent flap tore open and a consortium man came out shooting, and sentiment had to wait its turn.

* * *

**Tomás**

Here is what nobody tells you about fear: it is loud, but joy is louder, if you let it be.

I had Luz's arm over my shoulder and we were running, all of us, up the glacier's spine while the sky went from iron to rose behind the eastern peaks, and my legs were shaking and my lungs were full of knives and I was — I keep trying to find the honest word for it — I was *happy*. Happy the way the river is happy in flood season, which is to say dangerous and glad about it.

Juniper had given me the rear guard position, which she said was because I knew the ground from our scouting run yesterday, but which I understood was because she trusted me to count heads. So I counted. Twelve became thirteen with Luz and Señor Campos. Thirteen stayed thirteen through the icefall, threading the blue seracs where Juniper called the safe line from memory — she'd memorized it in one look, the way she reads sandbars, the way some people are simply built to know where the water wants to go.

Behind us, the camp woke properly. A whistle blew — three notes, then two, Renard's signal code, which I'd heard enough nights through the rocks to know by heart. Dogs, too. They had dogs. I hadn't told Juniper about the dogs because telling her meant admitting I'd been close enough to hear them, closer than agreed, and she'd have locked me in the snow cave if such a thing existed.

"They're coming," I said now, because honesty had gotten expensive lately but we could still afford it. "Two minutes. Maybe less."

"How many?" Juniper didn't turn around. She never turns around when the answer might be bad. She just asks, like asking is armor.

"Eight that I saw. Maybe ten."

"And Renard?"

I hesitated.

"Tomás."

"He wasn't in the camp. His tent was there but—" I'd looked inside. I shouldn't have looked inside. "His bed was cold. He slept somewhere else. Maybe ahead of us."

Juniper stopped so fast the rope went taut. Ahead of us. On the glacier. Between us and the pass.

"No," she said finally. "No, he's behind. Men like Renard don't walk point. They arrive after the work's done and take the credit." But her eyes went up the icefield anyway, reading it the way she reads water, and I watched her not believe herself.

The sun cleared the ridge then, all at once, and the whole glacier caught fire with light — white gold, blinding, beautiful — and Luz squeezed my hand and said, "Look, Tomás. Look where we are." As if we weren't fugitives. As if we were pilgrims.

Maybe both. Maybe that's the same thing sometimes.

* * *

**Juniper**

The crevasse field nearly ended us, and then, in the manner of disasters, saved us instead.

Renard's men — eight of them, moving fast and angry, dogs lunging on short leads — hit the icefield twenty minutes behind us and made the mistake of following our line exactly. Which was fine, right up until the bridge of snow over the second crevasse, the one I'd tested with my full weight and judged sound, decided that eight men in a hurry weighed differently than one tired woman.

It went with a crack like a rifle. The lead dog dropped screaming into blue nothing, and the man on its lead went to his armpits before his partners hauled him out, and after that they had to rope up and find their own way, and we gained forty minutes we hadn't earned.

"Thank you," Mara panted beside me, meaning the crevasse, meaning God, meaning whatever gets people up mountains.

"Don't thank it yet. It's fickle." Rivers and glaciers, same temperament. Same rules: respect the current, never trust the surface, and always, always know where your exit is.

The pass itself opened ahead of us by midmorning — a notch in the ridge like a door left ajar, snow pluming off its lip in banners. Ilsa's window. Blue sky above, and below us the western clouds already stacking up gray and heavy as wet wool. We had hours. Maybe five. Maybe fewer. Weather in the Cordillera doesn't knock.

Aurelio Campos walked beside me for a while. He was older than I'd guessed, and slower, but he climbed like a man with somewhere to be.

"You're the captain," he said. "Of the boat."

"Was. Is. The Marisol's in pieces in San Vega, waiting for me to be worth reassembling."

"Mara told me what you're carrying. What you burned your route for." He glanced back down the glacier, where Renard's party crawled like ants on sugar. "The testimony matters more than the paper. You know that. Whatever happens at the top—"

"Don't." I said it harder than I meant to. "Don't give me the speech where losing everything is fine because the truth survives. I've heard it. I've almost believed it twice. Ask me again when we're down the other side."

He smiled, old and unhurried. "That's not what I was going to say. I was going to say: whatever happens at the top, you won't be carrying it alone anymore. That's the difference between you nine years ago and you today. Yes? Nine years ago you'd have handed me the case and kept walking."

I didn't answer, because the answer was yes, and yes felt like a door opening in a wall I'd built load-bearing.

Then the shooting started.

Not from behind. From *ahead* — from the flanks of the pass itself, two figures on the ridgeline above the notch, rifles cracking flat echoes off the ice. Renard's men. The ones who'd slept cold. He hadn't been behind us at all. He'd cut the angle in the night and taken the high ground, patient as frost, and now the door was ajar and someone was standing in it.

"Down!" I screamed, and thirteen people flattened against the ice as bullets stitched white holes in the snow around us.

* * *

**Tomás**

We hid behind a serac the size of a chapel while the world tried to shoot us, and I did the only useful thing I know how to do, which is think like somebody smaller and hungrier than everyone else.

The riflemen were above the notch. Two of them. That meant they couldn't come down to us without leaving the pass open behind them. It also meant they couldn't see the gully on our left — the one I'd found yesterday, the ugly steep one full of rotten rock, the one Juniper had looked at and said, "Never, unless we're dying."

"We're dying," I said.

She looked at me. Snow in her eyelashes, blood on her sleeve where Ferro had clipped her diving for cover — his or hers, unclear. "That gully's a death trap."

"So's standing here."

For a second she grinned, fierce and wild, and I understood suddenly why rivers obeyed her. "Tomás Villalobos Quill, you're going to make a hell of a captain someday."

"I'm keeping the name Quill, then."

"Ask Mara first. Now move!"

We went up the death trap under covering fire from Ferro and the muleteers, Luz's hand locked in mine, rock breaking loose under us in slabs that rang away down the ice. Twice I fell. Twice Luz hauled me up by the collar like I weighed nothing, and I thought: *she's been prisoner for weeks and she's stronger than me.* I thought: *Renard took her and could not bend her.* I thought a lot of things, climbing. Mostly I climbed.

The gully topped out fifty meters shy of the notch, below the riflemen's ledge. From there it was exposed scree, no cover, thirty seconds of open sky between us and the pass.

Juniper counted us down on her fingers. Three. Two.

And that's when the glacier spoke.

Not groaned — *spoke*, a vast cracking syllable from the icefall below, and every head on the mountain turned to watch a serac the size of a cathedral lean, shudder, and begin to fall. Whether it was the gunfire or the warming sun or just the mountain's own opinion, no one would ever know. It came down in thunder, and the avalanche it fathered swept the lower slope where Renard's main party clung, and the screaming — human and dog together — rose over the ice like birds.

The riflemen above us forgot we existed. We didn't forget them. Juniper was already moving, knife in her teeth, and I was behind her, and Luz was behind me, and we took the ledge before the echo died.

One rifleman threw down his gun and sat in the snow with his hands up, young and gray-faced. The other reached for his belt and stopped when Luz stepped past me, picked up his fallen rifle, and worked the bolt with a click that sounded very final.

"Luz," I said.

"I'm not going to shoot him, Tomás." She slung the rifle over her shoulder. "I'm going to carry it. There's a difference, and he should think about it on the way down."

The pass stood open above us. The notch. The crest. Blue sky beyond it, and the far side of the Cordillera, and home, or whatever home was going to be.

But below, through the settling powder of the avalanche, figures were rising out of the snow. Fewer than before. Moving worse. And at their center, untouched, immaculate, coat buttoned as though he'd dressed that morning for a business meeting on a glacier, Renard raised his head and looked directly up at us.

Even at four hundred meters, I swear he smiled.

* * *

**Juniper**

He'd lost half his men to the mountain and it hadn't cost him a cent of his own skin, and I hated him with a cleanliness I hadn't managed since Port Salinas. Hating him felt almost restful. Familiar. Like tying off to a dock you know.

He started up toward the pass with the survivors of his party strung out behind him, and there was no more trickery available to either of us — no gullies, no avalanches owed, no dark left to hide in. Just the climb, him below and us above, and the weather falling out of the west like a debt coming due.

I put the oilskin case's strap tighter across my chest and looked at my people: Mara gray-lipped and grinning, Luz with a dead man's rifle, Tomás breathing hard and standing tall, Aurelio Campos with his testimony and his terrible calm, six muleteers, one chastened rifleman we hadn't shot. Thirteen souls and one map, and a door of blue light at the top of the world.

"He'll reach the crest an hour behind us," Mara said. "Maybe less."

"Then we'd better be through it and gone before he arrives." I turned my face to the notch, to the wind already carrying the smell of the storm behind it. "Everyone climbs. Nobody waits. Nobody looks back except to count."

Tomás fell in beside me, and Luz on his other side, and we started the last pitch with the sun high and the snow beginning to whisper against the ice — soft, at first, the way trouble always starts.

Below us, faint over the wind, I heard Renard call my name. Just my name, once, polite as an invoice.

I didn't answer. I climbed.
