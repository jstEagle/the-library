## 20. Burning the Map

There is a particular quality to the light at eleven thousand feet that I imagine the dead get to enjoy: everything sharp, everything honest, nothing forgiving. The sun came up over the Cordillera del Viento like a coin flipped by a god with a gambling problem, and it showed me exactly what I'd feared it would.

Renard's men had us at the crest of the pass. Not behind us — *at* us, spread across the saddle in a loose crescent, six of them with rifles slung casual as market baskets, and Renard himself standing in the middle like a man who'd arrived early for an auction he'd already rigged.

Behind us: the glacier, blue-white and indifferent, running down toward the valley where three villages were waiting to learn whether they'd still exist in five years' time.

Between us: about forty meters of scree, thin air, and one oilskin case.

"Captain Villalobos." Renard's breath made small clouds when he spoke, which was somehow more annoying than if he hadn't been breathing at all. "You've given us a remarkable chase. My compliments."

"I didn't do it for you," I said.

"No. You did it for them." He gestured — that same unhurried sweep he used on the river, back when his black launch shadowed us through the narrows, back when I still thought of him as a problem instead of a weather system. Tomás stood at my left shoulder, Luz's hand locked in his, both of them breathing hard from the climb down from Renard's camp. Mara leaned on Ilsa's muleteer stick, which we carried now the way you carry a flag. Behind her, the villagers' spokesman, old Mateo Herrera, stood with his hands folded like a man in church, except the church was a frozen pass and the congregation was armed.

"You should know," Renard continued, "that my employers have revised their offer. In light of the inconvenience."

"There's always a revision with you people."

"The debt in Port Salinas. Your brother's signature on it, your name underneath. Gone. A clean slate, Captain. And passage for all of you — safe passage — anywhere the Marisol wants to run." He smiled. It was a good smile. It had probably gotten him through customs in four countries. "All this, for a piece of oiled canvas."

I looked at the case under my arm.

Here's what nobody tells you about nine years of running: you get very good at weighing things. Sandbars against current. Fuel against distance. What a man says against what his hands are doing. I'd spent nearly a decade making those calculations in the dark, alone at the wheel, and every single one of them had been about keeping myself moving, because stopping meant being found, and being found meant answering for the flood, for the debt, for the sister who wouldn't write and the mother who wrote too much.

And now here was the biggest weight of my life sitting in my arms, and every calculation pointed the same direction. Give him the map. Take the deal. Free my brother. Save these people's skins. The truth could be told without paper — Mara herself had said so, back at the bridge, before the bridge took Ilsa from us. Testimony, affidavits, the spokesman alive and breathing. What was ink on skin compared to that?

Except.

"Mateo," I said, without turning around. "If I hand this over, and then you go to the capital and testify — what happens?"

The old man's voice came out steady, though I saw his knuckles whiten around each other. "Nothing happens. That is what happens. We have testified before, señorita. When the surveyors came asking questions. When the men in black boats measured our water. Our words went into the air and the air kept them." He shrugged, a small, terrible motion. "Paper is different. Paper can be contradicted by other paper. But paper in *our* hands — a map showing what they intend, drawn by their own expedition — that, they cannot argue with. That is why they want it. Not because it helps them. Because it convicts them."

"And if I give it up," I said, "in exchange for debts erased and safe passage — what does that make your testimony?"

"Worthless," Mara said quietly. She'd come up beside me, fever-flushed but clear-eyed, her bandaged shoulder held stiff. "It makes us bought. Every word out of our mouths afterward, they'll say we sold it. They'll be right." Her jaw worked. "I've been telling myself for two hundred miles that the truth doesn't need the map. I was wrong. The truth needs to *belong* to someone. If it belongs to them, even as ash, it belongs to them."

Renard watched this exchange with the patience of a man watching weather he'd already forecast. "Sentiment," he said, almost gently. "It's very moving, Captain. But sentiment doesn't pay interest. Your brother's creditors are not sentimental people. I've met them."

I thought about Emilio. Nine years since I'd seen his face, and I could still hear him saying *you always run, Juni, you run like water.* I thought about the ledger in Port Salinas with my family name bleeding down its columns. I thought about how easy it would be — God, how *easy* — to set this case down, shake his hand, walk away clean.

Then I thought about Ilsa Berríos going off that rope bridge into the dark with two of Renard's men, buying us forty minutes with her life, and shouting — I will hear it until I die — *"¡La ruta no se vende!"* The route is not sold.

"Mr. Renard," I said, "do you know what a riverboat captain does when she runs aground?"

His smile flickered. "I suspect you're going to tell me."

"She doesn't panic. She doesn't pay anyone to pull her off. She gets out in the water herself and she looks at what's holding her." I crouched, slow, keeping my eyes on his, and laid the oilskin case on the scree between us. His men's rifles shifted. I ignored them. "Because the thing holding you is never what you think it is. Usually it's just fear wearing a rock's clothing."

"Captain—"

I opened the case.

* * *

The map came out into the high cold light like something alive. All those weeks it had traveled with us — sewn into a flour sack, strapped under a mule, pressed against Mara's fevered side — and now it lay flat on the stones of the pass, and the wind got interested immediately, tugging at its corners.

Elias Thorne's fine drafting. The ancient route overlaid in red: the porter's road, the singing pass, the village under the ice, the bridge. And in Thorne's own annotation, in that careful cartographer's hand, the thing that had started all of this: *Proposed impoundment elevation — see consortium schedule C.* Three villages. Underwater. Written down neat as a recipe.

Renard took one step forward. Just one. His men didn't, because I'd positioned myself — without quite admitting I was doing it — directly over the map, and everyone on that mountain understood that a rifle crack at this range would put a hole in the document too.

"Juniper Villalobos," Renard said, and for the first time since Port Salinas there was something under the polish. Something quick and wet. "Think about what you're doing."

"Oh, I've thought about very little else," I said. "That's the trouble with me. I think and think and then I do something worse." I reached into my coat pocket and brought out the storm matches — Ilsa's storm matches, waxed and stubborn, the last thing of hers we'd salvaged. "You offered me a clean slate. Here's the thing about slates, Mr. Renard. You can wipe them all you want. The writing's still in you."

Tomás moved first. I'll swear to my grave I never signaled him, but the boy read me the way he'd learned to read water in the Gorge of Whistling Stones — ahead of the current, seeing the line before it existed. He struck the match against the box and cupped the flame, and Luz crowded in beside him, shielding it with both their bodies, two children guarding a fire the size of a moth.

"Last chance," Renard said. "Triple the original bounty. Your brother walks free. Or you burn the only leverage any of you will ever have, and my employers send someone less reasonable than me next time."

"There's a *less reasonable* than you?" I said. "God help the mountains."

I took the match from Tomás.

For one heartbeat — one long, glacial heartbeat — I held it above the map and let myself feel the whole weight of the choice. My brother's face. The debt. Nine years of penance dressed up as freedom. Everything Renard was offering was everything I'd told myself I wanted since the night I pushed the Marisol off the bank at Port Salinas and didn't look back, except I'd looked back constantly, the way you do, the way runners always do.

But Ilsa hadn't looked back. Ilsa had cut the lines and fallen, and the route had gone on without her, carried in the feet of the living.

"The route lives in us now," I said. Not to Renard. To all of them — Mara, Mateo, the kids, the wind. "Every switchback. Every shelter. Every stone that sings. He can't buy what's in our heads, and he can't drown what's in our hearts, and neither can his consortium, and neither can the snow." I looked down at the map one last time. "Thank you for carrying us," I told it. "Rest now."

I dropped the match.

The oilskin caught slower than paper — it wanted to resist, being built exactly for this, built to keep things safe from fire and water — and then it didn't resist anymore. Flame walked across Thorne's red lines like dawn coming up a valley, and the proposed impoundment elevation curled and blackened, and the villages that were scheduled to become a lake stayed villages, at least on paper, forever.

Renard made a sound. I want to remember it as a shout but it wasn't; it was smaller than that, thinner, the sound of a man watching his career burn at eleven thousand feet. He actually took another step forward, one hand out, as if he could snatch the burning thing back, and Mara stepped into his path — feverish, half-crippled, unarmed — and said, "No."

And he stopped. Because whatever else Renard was, he was not a man who shot botanists in front of witnesses on a mountain pass. Exposed men follow rules. It's the only armor they have left.

The map burned. The wind took the ashes and scattered them east, over the glacier, over the valley, a gray snowfall falling upward. Tomás watched it go with his arm around his sister, and Luz — who I'd known for three days and who had the sharpest eyes of any of us — watched *me*, and whatever she saw in my face must have satisfied her, because she nodded once, solemn as a judge.

"There," I said to Renard. My hands were shaking. I let them. "Now you're holding nothing. Go explain that to your employers. I hear they're not sentimental people."

* * *

He didn't retreat right away. Men like Renard never do; they perform the arithmetic first, out loud, hoping the numbers change while they talk.

"You understand," he said, straightening his coat, restoring the polish piece by piece like a man rebuilding a wall, "that this changes nothing legally. The consortium has copies of the survey data. Thorne's field notes survived. This was theater, Captain. Pretty theater."

"Then why," Mara asked sweetly, "did you chase us across a mountain range for it?"

He had no answer for that, which was answer enough. But he gave it one more try, because they always do. He turned to Mateo Herrera. "Señor. You're a reasonable man. Without the map, your testimony is a story. Stories lose in court."

Mateo smiled, and it was the first time I'd seen him do it, and I understood suddenly why the villages had chosen him to speak for them. "Perhaps," he said. "But stories told by many mouths, in many villages, written down by lawyers in the capital before your men can reach them — those stories have a name, Mr. Renard. We call them *witnesses*. There are more of us than there are of you. There always have been. That is why men like you build dams instead of armies."

Something moved behind Renard's eyes — not defeat, not yet, but the recognition of it arriving, the way you recognize a sandbar a moment too late to turn the wheel. He looked at his six men on the scree. He looked at the burned patch where the map had been. He looked, last of all, at me, and I gave him my best riverboat smile, the one I use on debt collectors and inspectors and other forms of flood.

"Port Salinas," he said quietly. "Your brother. None of that goes away, Captain. Debts don't burn."

"No," I agreed. "They don't. But I'm done paying them for other people." I jerked my chin westward, toward the descent, toward the long trail down to the valley and the river beyond. "Go on, Mr. Renard. The pass closes within hours, and I'd hate for the consortium to have to explain to your widow what happened."

For a moment I thought he'd push it. Six rifles against our sticks and our fury and our frostbite — the arithmetic wasn't impossible for him, just expensive, and Renard struck me as a man whose employers audited expenses.

He touched his hat brim. Actually touched it, the courteous monster. "Captain Villalobos. It has been genuinely instructive." Then he turned and spoke low to his men, and they began to withdraw down the eastern shoulder, back the way they'd come, their figures shrinking against the white until the wind folded them away.

Nobody cheered. We were too tired, and cheering costs breath, and breath was currency up here. But Tomás sat down right there on the scree and laughed — a wild, cracked, thirteen-year-old laugh that bounced off the ice walls and came back doubled — and after a second Luz joined him, and then I felt my own mouth doing something it hadn't done in nine years, and Mara put her good hand on my shoulder and said, "You know he'll be back."

"He'll be back," I agreed. "But he'll be back with nothing. And we'll be home."

* * *

We crossed the crest within the hour.

The snows were already coming — we could see them massing on the western peaks like a fleet under sail, the first flakes arriving sparse and deliberate, scouts for the army behind. The pass would seal behind us, exactly as Ilsa had promised it would, sealing away the bridge and the fight and the ashes of the map and, I suspected, some portion of the woman I'd been when I started up this mountain.

At the top, before the descent, we stopped. It wasn't planned. We just stopped, all of us, the way rivers stop at a bend to look at where they've been.

Mara unwrapped the bundle she'd been carrying since the bridge — nobody had asked, and I hadn't let myself wonder — and inside was a scrap of Ilsa's scarf, singed at one end, that she'd pulled from the rope-bridge timbers. She tied it to the stone cairn at the summit, where travelers had left offerings for longer than any map remembered. Old Ilsa Berríos, guide of the high routes, keeper of the singing stones. The wind took the scarf's ends and snapped them like a flag.

"She'd have hated the ceremony," Mara said.

"She'd have hated that we stopped moving," I said. "Come on. Downhill. While the window holds."

We went down into the valley, seven of us where nine had started, carrying testimony instead of paper, carrying each other instead of cargo. The snow followed us down like it wanted to come along, and for once I didn't mind something following me.

Because below us, past the tree line, past the last white fields, I could see it — a brown thread through the green, catching the light.

The Río Cobre.

Home water. And this time, I wasn't running toward it or away from it.

I was bringing everybody.
