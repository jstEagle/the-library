## 21. Descent

The pass closed behind us the way a door closes behind a funeral — slowly, with a sound like something enormous deciding.

I watched it happen from the shoulder of the mountain, half a mile below the crest, where the trail bent around a spur of black rock. The snow we'd crossed at dawn was already erasing itself. Wind came off the Cordillera del Viento in long white breaths, filling our bootprints, smoothing the drifts where Renard's men had floundered, covering the place where Juniper had knelt with the oilskin case and struck a match and held it steady while the map curled and went to ash. From this distance it looked like nothing. A woman kneeling in snow, a small flame, a small smoke. The most important bonfire I have ever witnessed, and it wouldn't have warmed your hands.

Tomás stood beside me with Luz's arm through his, both of them watching the white wall swallow the summit. He didn't cry. He'd done his crying in the snow cave, three days of it, rationed out between stories the way we rationed the dried meat — a little each day so there would be some left for tomorrow. Now he just watched, and his jaw was set in a way that made him look suddenly older, and I thought: *that is what it costs, to stop being a stowaway.* You become cargo that has opinions.

"Will it hold?" Luz asked me. Not the snow. The burning. She'd been a prisoner of Renard's for two weeks; she had earned the right to doubt everything.

"The route?" I said. "It holds better than paper ever did."

"No." She nodded back up the mountain, toward the smoke that wasn't smoke anymore. "Her. The captain. Will she regret it?"

I considered lying, because I'd been considering lying since the bridge, since I'd told her — told all of them — *give it up, give him the case, the truth doesn't need an oilskin.* I had argued for surrender with the fever still in my blood and my own guilt sitting on my chest like a cat that wouldn't be shooed. It had been easy to say. It had been the coward's arithmetic dressed up as wisdom: one map against five lives, no contest. And then Juniper had torn up Renard's contract on the bridge with her hands shaking, and I'd understood that she wasn't braver than me. She'd just done the math honestly, and honest math included the villages.

"She'll regret it," I said, "the way you regret anything you do right. In small doses. At two in the morning. And then she'll get up and make coffee and keep going."

Luz looked at me for a moment, and then she laughed — a short, cracked sound, the first laugh any of us had managed above the snowline — and squeezed her brother's arm, and we turned downhill together.

* * *

You would think descent would be easier than ascent. Nobody tells you that going down a mountain is its own kind of fight, that your knees become traitors, that gravity stops being a law and becomes a bully. We came down the leeward side of the pass in a chain, roped together where Ilsa Berríos's route demanded it — and yes, I say *her* route now, because somewhere between the glacier ruins and the bridge I stopped thinking of it as an ancient highland road and started thinking of it as hers, the way you claim a dead friend's habits by repeating them.

We were nine. Let me count us, because counting became a ritual, and rituals are how you carry the dead without dropping them.

Juniper Villalobos, who had not slept properly in eleven days and piloted our little column down scree slopes the way she once read sandbars — by instinct, by the set of her shoulders, by some conversation she was having with the mountain that none of the rest of us could hear. Tomás and Luz, hand in hand when the footing allowed, apart when pride reasserted itself, which for Tomás was roughly every twenty minutes. Old Mateo Quispe, the villagers' spokesman, whom we'd pulled out of Renard's camp with frostbitten fingers and a voice like a landslide — he'd spent two weeks refusing to tell the consortium anything, and now he couldn't stop talking, reciting names, dates, boundaries, the whole litany of the valley he intended to defend in a courtroom in the capital. Three more villagers from the burned settlement, walking with the particular silence of people whose homes are ash. The muleteer's boy, Pablito, fourteen and indestructible, who had adopted us somewhere below San Vega and refused to be un-adopted.

And me. Mara Quill, botanist, injured party, author of the distress call that had brought every wolf in the valley down on our heads. My wound had gone from feverish to merely furious; the leg worked if I insulted it enough. I catalogued alpine plants as we descended because it was the only prayer I knew — *Saxifraga, Valeriana, gentian gone to seed under the first snow* — naming things as if naming them could keep them.

It can't. But it keeps *you*, which turns out to matter more.

We lost the light early and camped in the lee of a moraine, and that night Mateo asked Juniper how far it was to Ilsa's village, and Juniper looked at me before answering, because Ilsa had been mine to know. Two days' walk, I told him. Maybe three, with my leg. Mateo nodded slowly, and then he said the thing that rearranged the rest of the journey:

"Then we bury her there. Properly. Her own ground, her own rite. A guide who dies on a mountain should not be carried off it like luggage."

Nobody argued. There was nothing to argue with. We had cut Ilsa loose at the rope bridge — or rather, she had cut herself, taking two of Renard's men with her, choosing the fall so the rest of us wouldn't have to choose it. We hadn't even had a body. Just the sound the bridge made when the last strand parted, which I will not describe, because I have decided some sounds don't get written down.

But we had her pack. We had her rope, coiled and scarred. We had the small tin cup she drank from and the carved wooden bird she kept in her breast pocket, which Tomás had found snagged on a rock fifty feet below the bridge when he went down — against everyone's orders, because that boy has never once obeyed an order in his life — and climbed back up with, white-faced and holding it like a rescued chick.

"You go back for the bird," Luz had told him afterward, "but not for breakfast. Explain that to me someday."

"Someday," Tomás had said, with the magnificent evasiveness of a twelve-year-old, and that had been the end of it.

* * *

The second day down, the mountain changed its mind about killing us.

That's the only way I can put it. The wind dropped. The clouds lifted like a curtain being raised by a stagehand with a sense of mercy, and suddenly we could see the whole leeward face of the Cordillera spilling away beneath us — brown and gold and then, far below, the impossible green of the valley, threaded with a river that caught the sun and threw it back. Our river. The Río Cobre, or its headwaters anyway, running home ahead of us the way rivers do, impatient, certain, never once looking over its shoulder.

Juniper stood at the overlook for a long time. I limped up beside her.

"That's the water your boat used to run," I said.

"That's the water they want to drown," she said. Then, quieter: "Mara. When we get down. When this goes to court, or whatever it becomes — the papers, the lawyers, whatever Mateo's cousin does in the capital—" She rubbed her face with both hands. "I'm a riverboat captain. I don't testify. I don't do rooms."

"You did a room with Renard," I said. "You tore up his contract in front of six armed men. That's testifying. It's just the version where the courtroom tries to shoot you."

She almost smiled. Almost. "The map's gone. Everything we saw up there — the carvings, the ruins under the ice, the whole proof of it — it's in our heads now. Nine people's memories. Is that enough? Tell me the truth. You're the scientist."

I thought about it honestly, because she'd asked for honesty and because lying to Juniper Villalobos had stopped being possible somewhere around the snow cave, when she'd told us about the flood — the levee, the warnings nobody heeded, the brother who blamed her, the nine years of penance she'd mistaken for freedom. Truth had become our currency up there. We'd traded it like food.

"It's enough," I said. "Better than enough. Paper burns — you proved that. But nine witnesses who walked the route, who saw the carvings, who buried a guide on sacred ground? A consortium can lose a document. They can't lose us without committing several additional crimes, and Renard's already committed so many that his own employers are going to feed him to the lawyers just to stop the bleeding." I shrugged, and immediately regretted it, because my shoulder had opinions too. "Besides. Mateo remembers every boundary stone in that valley by name. I've heard him recite them in his sleep. The dam project isn't fighting a map anymore, Juniper. It's fighting a story, and stories with grandfathers in them always win."

Below us, Pablito whooped — he'd found a patch of wild strawberries, late-season, half-frozen, absurdly sweet — and the sound rolled down the mountainside, and for a moment the whole ragged column of us was laughing, nine survivors eating frozen strawberries on the roof of the world, and I understood that this was the thing the consortium could never buy, burn, or dam. Not the route. Not the map. This. Us, laughing, descending, carrying each other.

* * *

Ilsa Berríos's village sat in a fold of the mountains like a coin in a palm — thirty houses of stone and timber, terraced fields gone gold and stubbled, smoke rising straight in the windless cold. Dogs announced us from half a mile out. By the time we reached the first terrace, the whole village had come out to stand along the path, silent, and I realized with a lurch that they already knew. Of course they already knew. Mountain news travels faster than mountain weather.

An old man stepped forward — Ilsa's brother, we learned later, though the resemblance was mostly in the eyes, that same flinty assessment that had measured us on the Porter's Road and found us worth the risk. He looked at our faces, at our number, at the empty space where a ninth-and-tenth should have been, and he didn't ask. He just said, in the valley tongue, and Pablito translated in a whisper:

"How did she go?"

And Juniper answered — not me, though Ilsa had been more mine than hers. Juniper, who had known the woman four days and trusted her with all our lives. "Facing uphill," she said. "Cutting the rope so the rest of us could cross. She chose it. She was smiling."

Whether that last part was true I cannot swear. The light was bad, the wind was screaming, and I was watching the bridge strands part. But I have decided to believe it, and I've learned on this mountain that belief is a kind of testimony too.

The old man closed his eyes. When he opened them, he said: "Then she died as a guide. Come. There is work, and there is food, and there is the rite. In that order. She would insist on that order."

* * *

I will tell you about the rite, because it deserves telling, and because I am the one writing this down and I refuse to let it be a footnote.

They did not weep, or rather, they wept the way the mountain does — everywhere at once, quietly, as a condition of the landscape rather than an event upon it. They washed her rope in the stream and hung it to dry across the door of her house, where it would stay, they said, until the next child of the village took it up. They filled her tin cup with chicha and set it on the windowsill facing the pass, so she could drink on her way home. The wooden bird — Tomás carried it forward himself, walking up alone through the assembled village with his chin up and his fists clenched, and placed it in the old man's hands, and explained, in his terrible formal Spanish, exactly where he had found it and exactly how far down he had climbed to get it, and the old man listened to every word as if it were a legal deposition, and then he put his hand on Tomás's head and said something that made the boy go red to his ears.

"What did he say?" Luz whispered.

Pablito grinned. "He said the bird came home carried by a boy with a guide's eyes. And that Ilsa would have charged him double for the climb."

The laughter that went through the village was the good kind — grief and love in the same breath, indistinguishable, which is the only honest way to hold them.

At dusk they carried her belongings to the high field above the houses, where the ancestors were buried facing the mountains, and they burned sweet grass, and Mateo Quispe — frostbitten, exhausted, a man from three valleys over with no formal standing among these people — stepped forward uninvited and spoke the names of the drowned villages, the ones the old empire flooded centuries ago, the ones the new consortium meant to flood again. He spoke them into the smoke. And the villagers answered, name for name, and I stood there with my useless leg and my useless science and understood that this — this call and answer, this litany repeated across four hundred years — was the real map. The oilskin had only ever been a copy.

Juniper stood beside me through all of it. She didn't speak. But at the end, when the fire had burned down to a red eye in the dark, she took off her hat — I had never seen her without it, not in fog, not in rain, not at gunpoint — and held it to her chest, and stood bareheaded under the stars like a woman arriving somewhere after a very long voyage.

Later, at the fire in the old man's house, she sat next to me with a bowl of stew she'd barely touched.

"I keep thinking," she said, "about what she said to me. On the Porter's Road. She looked at that glimpse of the map and she said, *your people ran this road once, carrying everything they owned, running from men with contracts.* And I told her my people were boat people." She stared into the fire. "I think she knew I was wrong before I did."

"Mm," I said, which is the botanist's contribution to emotional breakthroughs.

"Port Salinas," she said. "My brother's there. The debt's there. Nine years of there." She turned the bowl in her hands. "When this is done — the testimony, the lawyers, all of it — the Marisol's engine parts are in San Vega, and spring comes eventually, and..." She trailed off, which for Juniper Villalobos was equivalent to another woman sobbing.

"And you're going to steer her home," I finished for her. "Yes. Obviously. We all knew that before you did. Tomás has been calling it 'when we go back downriver' for a week. Present tense planning, Juniper. You've been outvoted by a twelve-year-old."

She laughed — really laughed, rusty and startled, the sound of a lock turning — and put her hat back on, and I thought: *there she is. The runner, dropping anchor.*

* * *

We left the village on the third morning, provisioned like royalty, blessed like family, and followed the river down out of the high country as the air thickened and warmed and the world remembered it was green. Mateo talked the entire way — depositions, essentially, rehearsed aloud, with the rest of us correcting dates and elevations like a very disorganized court stenographer corps. Somewhere below the tree line, Luz fell into step beside me.

"They'll want statements," she said. "In the capital. From all of us. Even me." Her hands were steady, but her voice wasn't quite. "Two weeks in Renard's camp. I heard things. Names. Which officials are paid, which villages are on the list after ours."

"Then you're not just a witness," I said. "You're evidence."

She considered that. "Tomás won't let me out of his sight. He thinks I'm going to evaporate."

"He stowed away on a riverboat and crossed a mountain range to find you. Evaporation is not on the table. He'll probably follow you to the courthouse and hide under the bench."

"He hid in a crate of survey equipment for two days," she said, and now she was smiling, wet-eyed and smiling, grief and love in the same breath, the village rite traveling with us whether we meant it to or not. "He said it smelled like machine oil and adventure."

Ahead of us, the valley opened wide, and the river widened with it, and there — moored at a landing below San Vega, tarped and stripped and patient as a promise — lay the Marisol, waiting for her engine, her captain, and her strange accumulating crew. Juniper stopped on the path and looked at her boat for a long moment, and I watched her take a breath like a swimmer about to go under on purpose.

"Well, Captain," I said. "Shall we go get your ship ready for spring?"

She settled her hat, and started down toward the water, toward Port Salinas, toward the brother and the debt and the forgiveness she'd sworn she wouldn't demand — and called back over her shoulder, in a voice that carried the way river voices do:

"Get aboard, Dr. Quill. We're going home."
