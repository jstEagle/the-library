## 22. Home Water

The Marisol came apart on a porter's road at the top of the world, and it stands to reason she'd come back together in a boatyard at the bottom of one. That's the thing about rivers — they only run one way, but people are free to be contrary about it.

We reassembled her in San Vega over six weeks of spring, in a yard that smelled of tar and cut pine and somebody's neighbor's cooking, which is to say it smelled like living. Mateo Ferro, who owns the yard and half the gossip in town, charged me a fair rate and doubled his questions, and I paid both without complaint because I'd learned something over the winter about what things cost.

The engine went back together last. Of course it did. The engine always knows it's the heart of the operation and behaves accordingly. Tomás spent three days elbow-deep in her guts with Luz holding the lamp and reading the manual aloud in a voice that made it sound like scripture, which, aboard the Marisol, it arguably was. When she caught on the fourth crank and then caught properly — that low diesel heartbeat I've known for nine years better than my own pulse — Tomás whooped so loud the yard dogs started up all down the street, and Luz laughed, and I stood there with grease on my hands and something in my throat I decided not to examine too closely.

"You're grinning," Mara said from her chair in the shade, her leg still in its brace, a botany journal open on her knee that she hadn't turned a page of in ten minutes.

"I'm inspecting my investment."

"You're grinning like a woman whose boat just came back from the dead."

"Same thing," I said, and let the grin stay.

* * *

We steamed out of San Vega on a morning so clear it felt like showing off. The Río Cobre ran high with snowmelt, brown-green and muscular, pushing against the hull like an old dog leaning into your leg. Everything downstream looked smaller than I remembered and more itself — the terraced hillsides, the cattle standing hip-deep in the shallows looking philosophic, the kids who ran along the bank shouting the name of the boat because kids always do, as if a riverboat might otherwise pass unannounced.

Tomás had the wheel for the first stretch. He'd earned it in the gorge and then again in the whiteout, though he didn't know about the second time; you don't tell a boy he earned anything in a snow cave where three days felt like three years and everybody came out the other side carrying each other's worst stories like contraband. He steered with both hands and total seriousness, correcting for the current a half-beat before it needed correcting, and I sat on the rail behind him and let him have it.

Luz sat beside him. She'd been beside him every waking hour since the raid on Renard's camp, since I'd cut her bonds myself and she'd looked at me with her brother's exact eyes and asked, "Is he all right?" before she asked anything about herself. Fourteen years old and asking first about somebody else. The villages upstream had produced worse ambassadors than that, and the consortium had produced considerably worse everything.

Renard himself was done. The testimony did what testimony does when it arrives in court attached to a spokesman, a botanist, a cartographer's field notes, and forty villagers willing to swear to the same set of facts: it sank. The dam project was suspended pending investigation, which in the language of investigations means dead, and Renard's employers had disowned him with the speed of men wiping their fingerprints off a knife. Last we heard he was answering questions in the capital, the kind of questions that don't have polite answers. I don't wish him harm, exactly. I just stopped spending any part of my day thinking about him, which is its own kind of verdict.

Elias Thorne sent a letter from the capital, long and careful, full of the word *restitution* and the phrase *historical wrongs* and, buried in the third paragraph like a man hoping not to be noticed entering a room, an actual apology. Mara read it twice and filed it under documents that matter legally but not morally. "He found the route," she said. "He also sold it. Both things are true and only one of them is going in the book." She was writing a book. Of course she was writing a book. Botanists map plants the way I map sandbars, obsessively and with commentary, and somewhere in those pages the valley's villages were going to become famous for their orchids instead of infamous for being in the way.

And Ilsa Berríos — well. We'd mourned her in her own village's rite on the far side of the pass, as promised, with her family and her neighbors and a fire that burned two nights, and her name was carved into the lintel of her house where names go so the house remembers. But there's a second mourning, the private kind, the kind you do alone at the wheel at four in the morning when the fog comes off the water and you'd swear you hear whistling stones. I do that one in shifts. So does everyone else aboard. Nobody talks about it. Everybody knows.

* * *

Port Salinas appeared around the long bend at midday, and I'll confess something: I took the wheel back from Tomás two miles early, under the pretense of checking the fuel line.

Nine years. Nine years since I'd run this bend heading the other direction, with the flood behind me and my family's voices in my ears and my brother's share of the debt folded into my pocket like a stone. Nine years of ports I could leave. Port Salinas was the port I'd left *from*, which is a different category entirely, the way a wound is different from a scar.

The town hadn't changed much, which felt like an insult. Same crooked pier, same customs shed with its peeling green paint, same laundry strung between balconies over the water street, drying in the wind like flags of small defeated nations. The fish market was loud. The church bell was late, as always. A dog slept on the warm stones of the ramp with the confidence of a creature who has never once been asked to leave.

I brought her in slow. Port Salinas pilots know I know how to bring a boat in fast, and I wanted them to watch me do it slow instead, wanted them to see a captain with nothing to prove and nowhere else to be. The fenders kissed the pilings. The lines went over. And then I was standing on the pier of the town I'd abandoned, and the ground wasn't moving, and neither was I.

"He's at the chandlery," Luz said. She'd been ahead of me the whole way downriver on this particular piece of intelligence, because fourteen-year-olds hear everything, and what she'd heard was that Rafael Villalobos still kept the chandlery his father had kept, and still closed it at noon to eat lunch facing the water, and still, according to the woman who sold fruit two doors down, sometimes watched the boats come in "like a man waiting for weather."

My brother. Who had blamed me for the flood — rightly, in the way that grief is always right, and wrongly, in the way that blame never quite lands where it aims. Who had inherited our mother's house and our father's debts and my share of both, because running away doesn't cancel accounts, it just transfers them.

"Come with me," I said, and then corrected myself, because I'd spent nine years issuing instructions and calling it independence. "If you want."

Mara closed her journal. Tomás wiped his hands on a rag with tremendous ceremony. Luz took her brother's hand, and the four of us walked up the water street like the world's least convincing delegation, past the fruit seller who stared, past two old men playing dominoes who didn't stare because dominoes come first, always, even for history.

* * *

Rafael was exactly where the fruit seller said he'd be, at the little table by the window, a plate of rice and beans going cold in front of him while he watched the pier through glass gone cloudy with salt spray.

He saw us coming up the street and I watched him do the arithmetic — my face, older by nine years and rearranged by weather; the boy and girl flanking me; the pale woman with the walking brace — and I watched him arrive at the answer and go very still, the way you go still when a sandbar appears where the chart says deep water.

"Juniper," he said, when I pushed the door open. Not a question. My brother has never once in his life asked a question he wasn't prepared to survive the answer to.

"Rafa."

We stood there in his chandlery among the rope coils and the brass fittings and the smell of canvas and turpentine, and I realized I had rehearsed this moment approximately four hundred times across nine years and four hundred miles, and in none of the rehearsals had I managed to say anything except his name.

So I said his name again, and then I put the envelope on the counter.

It was heavy. It should have been. Nine years of fares and cargo runs and one bounty — reduced, after the consortium's lawyers got involved, to a settlement that Mara called insulting and I called enough — folded into banknotes and a promissory note countersigned by a bank in San Vega. Every peso of what I owed him. What our father's death had left split between us, and what my leaving had dumped whole onto him.

"That's the debt," I said. "All of it. With interest, which I calculated twice because I didn't believe the first number."

He didn't touch the envelope. He looked at it the way you look at a snake that might be dead.

"You didn't have to—"

"I know I didn't have to. That's rather the point." My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "Rafa. I'm not here to buy anything. I'm not here to settle anything. The money's real and it's yours and it's been yours for nine years, and I'm sorry it took a flood and a mountain range and a blizzard for me to understand that owing someone isn't the same as being owned by them." I made myself keep going, because stopping was the old habit, the runner's habit, the door-left-open-behind-me habit. "I failed you. I failed Mamá's memory. I let you believe I chose the river over this family, and the truth is uglier and simpler: I couldn't stand to be looked at by people who knew what I'd done, so I went where nobody knew my face. That's not courage. That's just drowning slower."

The clock above the door ticked. Somewhere outside, the dog on the ramp barked at a gull and lost the argument.

"The flood wasn't your fault," Rafael said finally, in the voice of a man setting down luggage he'd carried so long his shoulders had grown around it. "I've known that for eight years. I've known it since the second year, when I found Papá's ledger and saw the levee inspection he'd skipped to save money. You were nineteen and you were hauling sandbags until your hands bled. I blamed you because you were the one who survived it visibly." He laughed, short and terrible. "You ran. I stayed and became the kind of man who blames a nineteen-year-old for a river. Between the two of us, Captain, I'm not sure who owes whom."

"Both," I said. "That's the trick of it. Both of us owe, and neither of us gets to decide the other's account."

He came around the counter then, and my brother — taller than me, grayer than he should be at forty-one, with our father's heavy hands — hugged me in the middle of his own chandlery, hard enough to creak ribs, the way Villalobos men hug, which is to say like they're trying to make up for several decades of not doing it.

"I watched the boats come in," he said into my hair. "Every noon. For nine years. Esperanza thought I was insane."

"Esperanza thinks everyone's insane. She told me so on the pier just now, through the medium of staring."

He pulled back and looked at the envelope, and then he did something I hadn't planned for and hadn't rehearsed and will carry to my grave: he tore it open, counted nothing, and slid it into the drawer under the register without a word, accepting it the way you accept rain — as weather, as fact, as something that falls on brothers alike.

"Lunch," he said. "There's enough rice if I add eggs. All of you. Now, before I wake up."

* * *

We ate at his table by the window, all five of us crammed into a space built for two, and Luz told the story of the snow cave with such dramatic embellishment that Tomás turned the color of a signal flag, and Mara corrected the botanical details, and Rafael laughed until he had to hold the table, and I sat in my brother's kitchen in the town I'd run from and understood, with the simple finality of an anchor finding bottom, that I was not going anywhere.

Not because the river was done with me. The river is never done with anyone; that's its charm and its cruelty. But because belonging isn't a place you find. It's a place you stop leaving. Nine years I'd treated every mooring line as a temporary arrangement, every friendship as cargo I'd eventually have to unload, every kindness as a debt accruing interest I'd have to outrun. And then a stowaway boy had spotted sandbars I couldn't see, and a wounded stranger had trusted me with the truth, and an old guide had cut a bridge and fallen so that strangers could live, and somewhere in all of that I'd stopped being a woman passing through and started being a woman others passed *with*.

After lunch, Rafael walked us down to the pier. The Marisol sat on the dark water looking, as always, slightly smug.

"So," he said. "You'll winter here? There's space at the north dock. I could talk to Haro about the rates."

"I'll winter here," I said. "And summer here. And I'll run cargo up to San Vega and down to the delta and everywhere the Cobre goes, and every trip will start and end at this pier, and you're going to get sick of seeing my smoke on the horizon."

"Impossible," said my brother, and hugged me again, briefly, fiercely, and went back up the hill to his shop, pausing once at the corner to look back, which he would deny forever.

* * *

Evening came down gold on the water. Tomás and Luz were on the foredeck arguing about whether the Marisol needed a figurehead — Luz said yes, a river otter, carved by her own hand, Tomás said absolutely not, we are a serious commercial vessel — and Mara sat on the pier with her brace off and her feet nearly in the water, writing, and I stood at the wheel doing nothing at all, which is the captain's most difficult duty and her finest.

A boat came downriver toward the docks, a small freighter riding low, and its pilot gave me two blasts of the horn — the greeting, the *evening, sister, safe water* — and I sounded mine back, and the exchange rang across the water the way it has rung on this river for a hundred years, and I was part of it. Part of it. Past tense of exile, present tense of home.

Tomás appeared at my elbow. "Course for tomorrow?" he asked, notebook already out, because the boy keeps records now, ship's records, proper ones, in handwriting better than mine.

"Upstream at dawn," I said. "San Vega by Thursday. Cargo's flour and roofing tin and one crate of books for Señora Quill, who claims she ordered none of them and will read all of them."

"And after San Vega?"

"After San Vega, wherever the river wants." I looked at the water street, the laundry lines, the light going amber in my brother's window, the dog asleep on the ramp who had, sometime during the afternoon, relocated to the foot of my gangplank with the air of a creature making a decision. "But we'll sleep here tonight. And tomorrow night. And the night after that."

Tomás wrote it down. Then he hesitated, pen hovering, in the way he does when he's about to ask something that matters.

"Juniper. Is this — are we —" He gestured at the boat, the pier, the town, the whole improbable arrangement of us. "Is this home now?"

I thought about the flood, and the fog, and the whistling stones, and a bridge in the mountains, and a map burning to ash in cold bright air while a man who owned everything learned he had nothing. I thought about how the route lived on in the memories of the people who'd crossed it, and how maybe that was true of everything worth keeping.

"It's whatever we keep choosing," I said. "And I'm choosing it."

He nodded gravely, wrote *home* in the logbook, and underlined it twice, and went forward to help his sister defend the honor of serious commercial vessels.

I stayed at the wheel a while longer, watching the first stars come out over the Cordillera del Viento, far off and white-capped and finally, blessedly, behind me. The current tugged gently at the hull, the way it always would, patient as gravity, certain as debt.

Let it tug. I'd dropped anchor.
