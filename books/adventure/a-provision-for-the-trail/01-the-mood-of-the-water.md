## 1. The Mood of the Water

The Sable River told you things before it showed you them, and Mara Halloran had learned, over seven years and ten thousand miles, to listen.

She stood at the Kestrel's wheel with her boots bare on the wet planking, feeling the boat's vibration come up through her soles the way a mother reads a sleeping child. The river was in one of its gray moods this morning — not angry, not yet, but sulking. A low haze lay on the water like breath held too long, and the current had gone quiet in that particular way that meant it was gathering itself somewhere downstream, coiling around some obstruction she couldn't see but would feel soon enough.

"Bar's shifted," Odile said from the bow, where she stood with a sounding pole, reading the water the old way, by color and texture. "See how it goes glassy there? Past the willow snag."

"I see it." Mara eased the wheel two spokes to port without looking down at it. Her hands knew the Kestrel's moods better than they knew their own scars.

The bar had been there last spring — a tongue of sand reaching out from the eastern bank like a lazy animal's paw — but rivers never kept anything where you left it. The flood season moved whole landscapes while people slept. A pilot who trusted last year's chart was a pilot who'd die surprised, and Mara Halloran did not intend to die surprised. She intended, if she could manage it, to die very old, very far from any river, having never once been recognized.

The Kestrel came off the bar with six inches to spare. Mara felt the sand kiss the hull — a soft dragging whisper through the keel, there and gone — and then they were past, sliding into the deep channel where the water ran brown-green and cold-smelling, heavy with silt from the high country.

"Six inches," Odile said, and there was something in her voice that might have been admiration or might have been reproach. With Odile it was often both at once. "You cut that close."

"The channel cuts close. I just go where it does." Mara wiped her palms on her trousers and finally let herself look away from the water ahead. The deck of the Kestrel was forty feet of weathered oak and iron, patched in nine places, rigged for cargo rather than speed: bales of dyed wool from the hill weavers lashed under oilcloth, crates of dried fish, a consignment of copper nails that clinked when the boat rolled. Ordinary freight. Honest freight, or honest enough. Nothing aboard that any harbor-master would look at twice, which was exactly how she liked it.

"You could have taken the outside channel," Odile said. She'd shipped her pole and come aft, arms crossed against the morning damp. She was twenty-six and built like the mast of a small ship, all vertical stubbornness, with a red scarf knotted at her throat that she'd worn every day since Mara had fished her out of a flooded slum in Corvane four years ago. "Deeper water. Safer."

"The outside channel adds half a day, and half a day is coin."

"Half a day is also not dying on a sandbar."

Mara smiled, or made the shape of one. "Odile. When have I ever died on a sandbar?"

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I give before breakfast."

Below them, the river unspooled southward through country that grew softer and greener as they went — hills giving way to bottomland, bottomland to the drowned forests and oxbow lakes where the Sable forgot its own course and wandered. Somewhere down there, three days' run if the water cooperated, lay Vessia's old capital. The city had a name. Everyone pretended it didn't anymore; since the Republic drowned — in debt, in revolution, in fire, take your pick of stories — the maps called it nothing, and the pilots called it *the Old City*, and everyone agreed not to say what had happened there, the way you agree not to mention a death in a house you're trying to sell.

Mara called it nothing at all. She called it *downriver*, and she did not go there.

They passed the mouth of the Ferren that afternoon, where the smaller river came in tea-colored and slow, and Mara took the Kestrel wide around the confluence because the mixing waters made whirlpools there that could spin a light boat like a leaf. Odile watched her do it and said nothing, which was its own kind of commentary. By evening they'd made the long straight reach below Harrow Bend, and Mara ordered anchor dropped in the lee of a gravel point, because running the Sable after dark in falling light was how boats became reefs.

* * *

Supper was salt pork and river greens Odile had gathered at the noon stop, cooked on the little iron stove in the cabin that smelled permanently of lamp oil and tar. Mara ate standing up, as she always did, one eye on the window slit where the water showed silver-black between the trees.

"You're quiet," Odile said.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're quiet like someone chewing on something." Odile pushed her bowl aside and leaned back against the bulkhead. "We'll pass the Old City day after tomorrow. There's still time to change the route. Take the Cane Fork cutoff, come at Greyharbor from the north. Costs us two days, maybe three—"

"No."

"Mara—"

"We have a delivery date in Greyharbor and a hull that doesn't love headwinds. We run the main channel, we pass the city at mid-river where the traffic is, and nobody looks at us. That's the plan. It's been the plan for seven years."

"And it works," Odile said. "I'm not saying it doesn't work. I'm saying I've watched you do this every season since I've been aboard, and every season you get quieter the closer we come, and you don't sleep, and you stand at that wheel like you're waiting for the river to hand you something. So I'm asking. Not as crew. As—" She stopped, jaw working. "As the person who owes you her life, asking the person who gave it why she won't spend one night in a bed."

Mara set her bowl down. Outside, a night bird called across the water, three notes descending, like something being counted down.

"There's nothing in the Old City for me," she said. "That's all."

"That's not all."

"Odile."

"I know. I know, you don't talk about before. Seven years, I've known you, and I know your river better than I know my own face, and I don't know where you were born. I don't know who taught you the water. I don't know why a woman who reads current like scripture flinches every time someone says the word *Vessia*." Odile's voice wasn't angry. That was the worst of it. It was patient, and patience from Odile was a thing with edges. "I'm not asking for the story. I'm asking if you're all right."

The lantern swayed a little with the current's pull on the anchor line. Mara looked at the flame instead of at the girl, because the flame was easier.

"I'm all right," she said, and heard how it sounded, and hated it.

"You read the river," Odile said quietly, "like it's written just for you. And everything else you read like it's going to bite you."

Mara picked up her bowl again. "Wash-up's yours," she said, and went out onto the deck.

* * *

The night was warm enough that she didn't go below. She sat on the cargo hatch with her back against the deckhouse, a blanket around her shoulders she wouldn't admit to needing, and watched the river work in the dark.

This was the hour she loved, or the closest thing to love she allowed herself. The Sable at night was a living document. You could hear the bars shifting — a soft underwater sigh, sand moving in the current's grip. You could hear snags grinding against each other somewhere upstream, wood worrying wood. Fish rose and took insects off the surface film with small precise kisses. The whole river was talking, and none of it was about her, and that was the mercy of it. The river didn't care where she'd been born. The river didn't remember faces. The river asked only that you pay attention, and attention was the one discipline Mara Halloran had never failed at.

Except, said a small dry voice in her skull, except once.

She closed her eyes and the past came up the way it always did — not as pictures first but as water. Green water, cold even in summer, the kind of cold that lived in the deep channels below the Old City's quays. The sound of oars stopping. A voice calling a name across the water — her name, spoken in a tone she'd spent seven years forgetting, and failing.

*Captain.* Just that. *Captain, they knew we were coming.*

Seven years ago. Another boat, another crew, another name for herself. They had run the night approach into the Old City with false papers and a bribed watchman, and somewhere between the second quay and the customs house the dark had filled with men who were not confused, not searching, not guessing — men who came straight to them, straight down the one route only five people alive had known. Straight to the hold where the cargo was. Straight to the people.

Mara had gone into the water. That was the whole of her heroism: she had gone into the green cold water while the shouting started, and the river had carried her away the way rivers carry everything, indiscriminately, without malice or favor, and by dawn she was twelve miles downstream clinging to a drift log with the fires of the customs house burning behind her and no crew, no boat, no name worth keeping, and a warrant with her face on it nailed to every post in the Republic's remains.

Three of them had died. She'd learned the names later, pieced together over years, each one a splinter she worked deeper rather than out. Doss Meraval. The twins, Anselm and Brac. And the fifth — the fifth had simply vanished, clean as a knife pulled from a wound, and Mara had never learned whether he'd died with the others or whether he was the reason the others died. Whether the route had been sold, and by whom, and for how much.

She opened her eyes. The river went on talking its indifferent language. Downstream, faint as memory, the sky above the southern horizon held a glow that wasn't stars — the Old City's lamps, or its fires, or just the haze of too many people living too close together. Three days away. Two days now, after tomorrow's run.

Every year she told herself she'd stop flinching. Every year the flinch arrived on schedule, punctual as the flood.

"Can't sleep either?"

Odile came out onto the deck, drying her hands on her trousers, and stood beside the hatch without sitting, which was Odile's way of leaving a door open without walking through it.

"River's loud tonight," Mara said.

"River's always loud. You just usually pretend it isn't." Odile tipped her head back at the sky. Clouds were moving in from the west, fat and low, blotting out the stars one constellation at a time. "Monsoon's early this year. Harbor-master at Greyharbor told me the rains came to the high country two weeks ahead of schedule. Whole season's going to be wrong."

"Seasons are always wrong. That's what makes them seasons."

"Mm." Odile looked at her sidelong. "Day after tomorrow. Mid-river. Nobody looks at us."

"Nobody looks at us."

"Right." She nodded slowly, and Mara knew the conversation wasn't over — it was never over, it was just anchored, waiting for a shift in current. "Well. If the water brings anything up, Captain, you'll see it before any of us. You always do."

Mara watched her go below, and then she sat alone with the blanket and the dark and the river's endless murmuring, and she thought: *the water never brings anything up. That's the bargain. The water keeps what it takes.*

She was wrong. She would learn, within the week, how wrong she was — but that night she only knew the ache in her chest that had no name she'd allow it, and the gray mood on the river that matched her own so perfectly it felt less like weather than like company.

The Sable slid southward in the dark, toward the city whose name she refused, carrying her the way it carried everything — patiently, and without asking permission, toward whatever it had decided to show her next.
