## 2. What the Flood Brings Up

The rain came in off the western hills the way it always did in the third month of the monsoon — not falling so much as arriving, a gray wall of it that walked across the water toward the Kestrel like something with intent. Mara Halloran stood at the wheel well before it reached her, because she had learned years ago that the river telegraphed its moods upstream, and the Sable had been muttering since dawn.

"Sheet the cargo," she called down to the deck. "Double-lash anything that floats."

Odile Ferren looked up from the winch, rain already beading in her close-cropped hair, and gave the particular nod she gave when she thought an order was unnecessary but wasn't foolish enough to say so. She was twenty-three, Odile, all elbows and opinions, and she had never seen Vessia whole. Sometimes Mara envied her that the way she envied fish their blindness to water.

The Kestrel took the first punch of the storm broadside and shrugged it off. Mara loved her for that. A hundred and ten feet of riverboat, shallow-drafted, flat-bottomed, built in the Vessian yards before the yards burned, and she handled a flood the way an old woman handles gossip — with weary, unshakable competence. Mara worked her up the middle of the channel where the current ran strongest but cleanest, letting the brown water carry them, reading the surface the way other people read letters.

That was the trick of the Sable, and of every river: it wrote itself on its own skin. A long smooth tongue of water meant depth. A line of standing waves meant a bar shifting beneath. A place where the current seemed to hesitate, to gather itself and think — that meant debris, a snag, something drowned and hungry for hulls. Mara read it all without thinking, the way she breathed, the way she kept her left hand moving on the wheel in small corrections no one ever noticed. Seven years on this river. Seven years of never stopping anywhere longer than a cargo took to unload.

The rain hit them full in the face then, and the world contracted to the wheel, the deck, the sound of water hammering wood. Somewhere behind her, Odile was shouting at the young one — Pell, sixteen, all eagerness and no ballast — to get below and stop admiring the weather.

"Captain!" Odile's voice, half-swallowed by the storm. "You want to run for the cut at Marrow Bend?"

Marrow Bend had a lee shore and a decent anchorage and, more to the point, a harbor-master who owed Mara nothing and remembered everything. "No," she said. "We ride it in the channel. The cut'll be choked with flotsam by now — we'd tear the wheel off trying to make it."

"You sure?"

"I'm sure the river's faster than we are. Let it do the work."

That was the whole philosophy, if Mara had one. Don't fight what carries you. Steer, don't struggle. Keep moving. She had built a life on it, plank by plank, and if the life had no rooms in it — no place she could shut a door and say *mine, and I'll be here tomorrow* — well, rooms could be searched. Rooms could be staked out. A boat that never slept in the same cove twice a season was the closest thing to a locked door the world had left her.

They rode the storm for six hours. It was, in its way, the best part of Mara's year, though she'd have cut out her tongue before admitting it. There was nothing in six hours of hard rain and hard water but the work, and the work asked nothing of her past. The river did not care what her face had looked like at twenty-six. The river did not remember Vessia.

*You do*, said a voice in the back of her skull, quiet as bilgewater. *That's the problem.*

* * *

The storm passed at dusk, trailing ragged veils of rain eastward, and the Sable came up swollen and furious, running high and brown and full of the country it had swallowed. Whole logs went by, root-balls like torn-up hair. A fence post, then a hundred fence posts. A drowned pig, bloated and dignified, sailing stern-first like an admiral. A thatched roof, entire, with a bird perched on its ridgepole riding it downstream toward whatever sea received such things.

"River's up four feet since morning," Odile said, coming to stand at the rail beside Mara. The evening light came through the breaking clouds in long copper bars, and the water under them looked like hammered metal. "Ferry landing at Lowmarsh'll be under. Hope nobody was counting on us."

"Nobody counts on us. That's the business model."

Odile snorted. She leaned her forearms on the rail, and Mara watched her watch the flood with the unguarded appetite of the young — the same girl who'd been a dock-rat at Greyharbor when Mara found her, stealing rope, of all things, and stealing it badly.

"You ever get tired of it?" Odile asked. "The moving?"

"Moving's how we eat."

"That's not what I asked."

Mara kept her eyes on the water. "No," she said, which was true, and did not say the rest, which was also true: *I'd stop tomorrow if stopping didn't kill me.* Odile had asked versions of this question for two years now. Mara had given versions of this answer. It was one of the small dishonesties that made the boat livable, and she kept it the way she kept the hull — patched, painted over, never mentioned.

Below them, the flood rolled on, carrying its harvest. And it was Odile, with a young person's eyes, who saw it first.

"Captain. Sandbar. Off the starboard — there's something hung up on it."

Mara followed her pointing finger. The bar lay just off the eastern bank where the river bent and shallowed, a long gray tongue of new sand that the flood had almost drowned, water sliding over it in a thin bright sheet. And wedged at its upstream tip, half-buried, canted at an angle like a coffin set carelessly into a hillside, was a dark shape.

"Driftwood," Mara said.

"That's not driftwood."

It was not driftwood. Even at two hundred yards Mara could see the lines of it — too regular, too square. Man-made. The current broke around it in a white V, holding it fast, and the sand was building up against it even as they watched, the river burying whatever it had brought.

Mara should have steered past. That was the sensible thing; that was seven years of doctrine. A strange object on a sandbar was somebody else's problem, somebody else's salvage claim, somebody else's trouble. She had a cargo of salt fish and lamp oil and a schedule that was really just a habit of never lingering.

"Bring us alongside," she heard herself say. "Easy. Off the downstream tip."

Odile glanced at her — one of those quick, cataloguing glances she thought Mara didn't notice — and went to work.

* * *

They anchored in the slack water behind the bar and took the skiff in, Mara at the oars, Odile in the bow with a boathook, Pell left aboard with strict instructions not to touch anything and a certainty he'd touch everything. The water over the bar was shin-deep and warm as bathwater, and it sucked at their boots as they waded the last stretch, the evening going violet around them, mosquitoes rising in clouds from the new sand.

The shape was a chest.

It was the size of a coffin, honestly, iron-banded, its wood blackened and swollen with long submersion, streamers of weed hanging off it like funeral ribbons. It had been down a long time. The sand had taken it to the waist, and the river had scoured a hollow around it, and the whole arrangement had the look of something deliberately buried that the flood had exhumed in a single night of violence — as if the Sable had reached down into some drowned place and pulled this one thing up to show them.

Odile hooked the boathook under the lid's edge and pulled, and the chest groaned — an actual groan, a low wooden complaint that made the hairs on Mara's arms stand up.

"Sealed," Odile said. "There's a lock, but it's — captain, look at the lid."

Mara looked.

Stamped into the iron band across the lid, half obscured by weed and silt, was a seal. She knew it before she'd finished wiping the mud away with her thumb. She knew it the way you know a face in a nightmare — instantly, totally, with your body arriving at the knowledge before your mind can stop it.

A river in a circle. The seal of the Republic of Vessia.

The republic was seven years drowned. Its seal was on a chest sitting in the middle of Mara's river, and her thumb was still on it, and she could not for the life of her make her hand move.

"Captain?" Odile said. "You know what that is."

"Everyone knows what that is." Her voice came out flat and strange. "It's on every customs house from here to the delta. Half of them are ruins now, but the seals are still there. They'll outlast the rust."

"It's a government chest." Odile crouched, tracing the iron band with a finger, her earlier appetite for salvage gone quiet. "An official one. How does an official Vessian chest end up in a sandbar two hundred miles from what used to be Vessia?"

"Rivers move things."

"Rivers move trees." Odile looked up at her, and in the last light her eyes were very steady. "This was put somewhere on purpose. And then it came up anyway."

Mara made herself step back. Made herself look at the bar, the bank, the drowned country the flood had swallowed — the fence posts, the roof, all the small domestic debris of other people's lives torn loose and set adrift. The river gave things back in flood season. Everyone knew that. Boots, bodies, Bibles, babies' cradles. The Sable kept nothing; that was the first thing they taught you about her. But she gave back on her own schedule, in her own order, and she did not care what a person was ready to see.

"We should burn it," Odile said. "Or sink it. Or leave it."

"We should open it."

"Captain—"

"We found it, Odile. Salvage law's salvage law, even now. Especially now." The words came out of her with a logic that felt borrowed, secondhand, as if someone reasonable were speaking through her mouth while the rest of her stood back and watched. "If it's treasure, it's a season's wages. If it's papers, paper's worth money to collectors. Either way we don't leave it for the next boat to find."

"And if it's neither?"

Mara didn't answer that. She took the pry bar from the skiff instead.

* * *

The lock was Vessian work and gave up grudgingly, and then not at all, and finally Odile smashed the hasp with the flat of the boathook's hook, three hard blows that rang out over the water like small gunshots. The lid came up with a suck of river mud and a breath of air that had been sealed since before either of them could guess — a smell of wet iron and rot and, beneath it, faint and impossible, something like lamp oil and paper.

Inside, packed in tarred cloth, were dispatches.

Dozens of them. Sealed cylinders of oilskin, each stamped with the river-in-circle, each wrapped tight enough that the flood had not touched what lay within. Under them, flat and dry and wrapped in waxed leather, was a ledger — a manifest, its pages foxed at the edges but legible, the ink brown with age but sharp as the day it dried.

Odile held the lantern. Mara held the manifest. And the river ran on around them, talking its endless brown talk, while she read.

It was a shipping manifest. That was the joke of it, if there was a joke. Cargo: sealed dispatches, quantity listed, weight listed. Destination: a place whose name had been changed twice since the ink dried. And then the section that made her hands go cold on the paper — the personnel section, because Vessian bureaucracy had accounted for everything, had numbered and listed and docketed everything, had built its beautiful drowning republic out of lists just like this one.

*Escorted by,* the manifest said. And then the names.

Mara knew the first name the way she knew her own heartbeat, because it had been her own heartbeat, once, on a different boat with a different name. *Joss Meric.* Her boatswain. Her boatswain who had gone into the water at the Meridian crossing with the cargo hold open to the sky, and who had not come up.

The second name. *Anthe Ferrule.* Her navigator. Dead the same night, the same water, the same sold-out route.

The third. *Den Sarles.* Fourth. *Imri Voss.* Fifth, sixth, seventh — she read them all, every one, and every one was a face, and every face was twenty-six years old and laughing or shouting or afraid, and every one of them had been under the water for seven years while the man who sold them went on breathing.

And then the last name on the escort list, the name of the one who had signed for the cargo, whose signature was still there in the confident downstroke of a woman who believed the republic would last forever:

*Captain M. Halloran. Kestrel.*

Her own name. Her own boat. A route she had never run, a cargo she had never carried, a signature that was almost hers — close enough to pass at a glance, wrong in ways only she would ever know — dated eleven days before the night her crew died.

The lantern hissed. A mosquito whined at her ear and she didn't swat it. Odile was saying something — her name, probably, *Captain, Captain* — from a long way off, from the far side of seven years.

Because this was not her route. This was not her cargo. This manifest was a forgery, a careful, bureaucratic, Vessian forgery with her name stitched into it like a hook into a bait — and it had been waiting in the water, in the dark, under the sand, all this time. Waiting for the flood. Waiting for her.

The past didn't stay buried. She'd told Odile that for years, in her way, in the shape of the life she'd built. But she had never understood — not truly, not in her bones — that the past didn't just surface. It came up *addressed*.

"Captain." Odile's hand on her arm, real, warm, here. "Mara. What is it? What's in there?"

Mara looked at the chest, at the seal with its patient drowned river, at the dispatches packed beneath the manifest like rounds in a magazine. Whatever those cylinders held, someone had gone to great lengths to keep them dry, and someone else had gone to great lengths to sink them, and the river — indifferent, enormous, the only honest thing in her life — had decided tonight that she would be the one to find them.

"Get the skiff," Mara said. Her voice was steady. She was almost proud of that. "We're taking all of it aboard."

Odile hesitated, one boot in the water, the lantern light jumping across her face. "Mara. That seal. That's not salvage. That's a *warrant* waiting to happen, and you know it better than anyone."

"I know," Mara said, and lifted the manifest, and her dead crew's names slid in the lantern light like fish turning in dark water. "That's exactly why we're taking it."
