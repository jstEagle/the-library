## 3. A Stranger on the Levee

Tomas Vey saw the chest before he saw the woman, and the sight of it stopped his breath the way cold water does — a fist against the ribs, a sudden knowledge that the bottom is farther down than you thought.

It sat on the trading post's back dock at Halfway Light, propped against a bollard with its lid open to the gray morning, and even waterlogged and warped it wore its history like a uniform. Vessian oak. Government make. The seal pressed into a lead medallion bolted to the front — the drowned Republic's open hand holding a quill, the mark that had once meant law and now meant nothing but memory, and not the kind anyone wanted.

He had spent eleven years teaching himself not to look twice at things. A salvager who looked twice got greedy, and a greedy salvager got dead in the shallows between Greyharbor and the delta, where the river kept everything and forgave nothing. But he looked twice at this. He looked three times. His hands, wrapped around a mug of chicory he'd bought mostly for the warmth, went still on the tin.

The trading post at Halfway Light was less a building than an argument between the river and the land — a plank levee, a roofed shed of cargo and rumor, a cookstove that smoked sideways in the wind off the water. Barges tied up four deep. Deckhands argued over freight rates in three languages. And through all of it, the chest sat open like a mouth, and no one else seemed to hear what it was saying.

Inside, he could see paper. Bundles of it, swollen and furred with mold, laid out on oilcloth while their owner — a lean woman with river-copper skin and a deckhand's scarf knotted at her throat — turned each piece toward the weak sun with the care of someone handling bones.

Tomas knew that care. He had invented half of it himself, back when he'd been someone else, back when his hands had worn ink instead of rope callus.

*Archivist,* he thought, and then corrected himself: *No. Not anymore. That country's gone under.*

But the hands remembered. The hands had spent six years in the Hall of Currents sorting dispatches by wax color and watermark, and they recognized, even across forty feet of damp morning air, the particular way government paper buckled when it had been submerged and dried and submerged again. Those were not merchant ledgers. Those were dispatches. Sealed ones, or they had been.

And there was only one reason sealed Vessian dispatches would be riding the Sable River in the hold of a cargo boat in the seventh year after the fall.

Someone had gone looking. Or the river had gone looking for them.

* * *

He watched her work for the better part of an hour, and everything he learned made him more certain and more afraid.

She was careful in the way of people who had been hunted. She'd positioned the chest so her body blocked the view from the levee. She'd spread an oilcloth that read, from any distance, as a salvage haul being sorted for resale — drowned cargo, common enough after monsoon season. But she touched the papers wrong for resale. Resale meant quick appraisal and rough handling; these she lifted by the edges, squared their corners, and set them down in an order that wasn't order yet but wanted to be. Cataloguing instinct. He'd have bet his punt and both sets of tools on it.

Her boat was moored downstream of the post: a river steamer of maybe ninety feet, black-hulled, single stack, with *KESTREL* painted on the bow in letters that had been freshened recently enough to matter. A good boat kept by someone who understood that a boat was the only honesty left on the river. Two figures worked her decks — a big man coiling line with the absent strength of habit, and a younger one, sharp-faced, who kept glancing upriver with an expression Tomas recognized from his own mirror: the arithmetic of how fast can we leave.

He asked around without asking. Bought a second chicory for the levee boss, a heavy woman named Brassa who ran Halfway Light the way a spider runs a web, feeling every vibration.

"Kestrel," Brassa said, sucking her teeth. "Runs cargo. Runs it quiet. Captain doesn't drink, doesn't gamble, doesn't gossip. Pays cash." She shrugged. "In seven years I've heard her name maybe twice."

"Seven years," Tomas said. "Same crew?"

"Same two, near enough. The big one came aboard five, six years back. The girl before that." Brassa's eyes flicked to him, weighing. "You asking because you want to sell her something or because you want to know something?"

"Both," Tomas said honestly. It was the safest answer, because it was true, and because it told her nothing.

Brassa laughed, short and hard. "Then you're like everyone else on this river." She leaned in, lowering her voice to the register traders used for real information. "One thing. There was a man through here yesterday, grey coat, city boots, asking after boats headed upstream. Paid too well for answers about who'd salvaged what after the flood crested. City boots on a mud levee, you understand? Wrong clothes for the questions."

Tomas felt the morning go colder. "Did he say what he was looking for?"

"He said 'government property.'" Brassa spat over the rail, eloquently. "Which on this river means somebody's going to pay for it, one way or another."

* * *

He decided to approach her at midday, when the levee was busiest, because a woman who didn't trust could be approached only where witnesses made violence expensive. He brought nothing but himself and a carefully arranged ignorance; he'd learned long ago that the best key into a locked room was to seem to be holding none.

"Captain." He stopped a respectful distance from her sorting cloth. Up close she was younger than her bearing, thirty perhaps, with a scar through one eyebrow old enough to have faded to silver, and eyes the flat green-brown of the river in dry season — eyes that measured him and found him unmeasured. "Forgive the interruption. Tomas Vey. Salvage and recovery, licensed out of Greyharbor."

"I'm not selling."

"No," he agreed. "You're cataloguing."

Something moved behind her eyes — a shutter half-drawn, half-opened. "Salvagers don't use that word."

"This one does." He crouched, slowly, keeping his hands visible, and nodded at the papers without touching. "That's a dispatch case. Vessian Foreign Office pattern, third issue. Lead seal medallion, not wax — wax melts, lead survives flooding. They used them for material that had to be readable after recovery from a wreck." He paused. "Or after a sinking that wasn't an accident."

The silence stretched. Down the levee, a crane groaned and swung a net of rice sacks. The woman did not move, but Tomas had the sense of a current reversing beneath still water.

"You know your seals," she said finally.

"I used to work with documents." He let that sit, incomplete on purpose. "Captain, I'm going to say something you won't like, and I'd ask you to hear all of it before you decide whether to throw me off your dock. Your hull is leaking."

"We're not leaking."

"Your stern seam, port side, below the waterline — yes, you are. I watched you come in this morning. You're taking on maybe a thumb-width an hour, pumping longer than you should, and your bilge smell has that sweet-rot note of oak giving up between the frames. Monsoon debris did it, or a bar you kissed harder than you meant to. Either way, it won't kill you today. It'll kill you in ten days, somewhere with no levee and no pump worth the name." He stood. "I can fix it. Properly, not a patch — re-caulk the run, sister the frame if it needs it. Two days' work. I've got the tools and the pitch aboard my punt."

"And the price?" Her voice was level, but he heard the wire in it, the sound a cable makes just before it parts.

"A look inside the chest. One hour. I don't take anything. I don't copy anything. I look, and I tell you what those papers are, because I think you already suspect and I think you need to hear it said by someone who knows."

"You think I can't read my own salvage?"

"I think," Tomas said quietly, "that you read them like a mourner, not a reader. And mourners miss things."

For a moment he thought she would strike him. Her hand actually started to rise — he watched the decision travel up her arm and die at the shoulder. When she spoke again her voice had dropped into something colder and older than anger.

"Get off my dock, Mr. Vey."

"It's a public levee—"

"It's mine until my lines are cast off, and everything within reach of my lines is mine. Including my business, my bilge, and my dead." She stepped forward, and this close he could see the sleeplessness banked behind her composure, the way a fire banks behind a closed stove door. "I don't know what you are. Archivist, agent, bounty man, ghost — I don't care. But nobody who looks at those papers the way you looked at them wants just to *help*. So here's my price for your silence: walk away, and I'll consider not telling the levee boss you've been sniffing after government property with city manners."

"They're not my city," Tomas said. "They're yours too. Or am I wrong about the way you square a page's corners?"

That landed. He saw it land, saw the flinch she couldn't quite swallow, and hated himself a little for aiming there — and knew he'd aim there again, because some doors only opened from the inside and the only tool for those was truth.

"The Republic's been under the water seven years," he went on, softer. "So have we, Captain, most of us. Some of us just haven't admitted it. I'm not your enemy. I might be the only man on this river who knows what you're holding and isn't trying to take it from you."

"Everyone says that right before they try."

"Yes," he agreed. "That's the trouble with surviving. Everyone's said everything right before they tried." He stepped back off her sorting cloth, raised both hands slightly — surrender, or blessing, or simply distance. "Two days, Captain. That's what your hull gives you. My punt's the grey one, third tie-off, with the copper patch on the port gunwale. If you change your mind, I'll be there. If you don't—" he glanced at the chest, at the mold-furred bundles sleeping in their oilcloth, "—then I hope whatever's in there is worth more than the boat you'll lose carrying it."

He walked away before she could answer, which was itself a kind of answer, and he felt her eyes between his shoulders the whole length of the levee like a hand pressed flat against his spine.

* * *

He did not leave with the evening barges, though leaving would have been the intelligent thing. Intelligence had never governed him where the Republic was concerned; that office belonged to older, worse machinery.

Instead he sat on his punt's bow with a tin plate of fried plantain and watched the Kestrel's lanterns come up one by one, warm squares reflected double in the black water. The big deckhand cooked something that smelled of pepper and onions. The young one stood the bow watch, a silhouette against the last grey light. And aft, in the lamplight spilling from a cabin door, the captain sat alone with a single sheet of paper held close to the flame, reading it the way people read letters from the dead.

Tomas knew what she was seeing, or near enough. He'd spent six years in the Hall of Currents watching paper become fate — routing orders, interdiction lists, the quiet bureaucratic murder of names typed onto manifests. He had processed the paperwork of drownings and told himself he was only filing. Then the Republic fell, and the files burned or flooded or scattered, and he'd taken up salvage because dragging the river for other people's losses felt like penance performed at arm's length.

Now the river had dragged something up and handed it to the one person alive with the right to hate what it said.

*Corvel Sarn.* He hadn't let himself finish the thought on the levee, but it finished itself now, unbidden, in the dark. Because he had seen manifests like hers before — or their siblings — in the last months of the war, in the restricted annex, under the third-issue seal. Interdiction results. Purchased intelligence. And one name recurring in the margins as broker, as facilitator, as the man whose signature meant a smuggling route had been sold and a crew had been delivered: *Sarn, C. — consideration rendered.*

He'd been young. He'd filed it. He'd told himself the tribunal would sort it all out after the war, and then there was no tribunal, and there was no war, only a river and seven years and a hundred small salvages of his own conscience.

If that chest held what he believed it held, it wasn't salvage. It was testimony. And testimony needed two things to live: a witness, and a vessel to carry it somewhere that still had the authority to listen. The tribunal seat at Vessel's Rest was reopening sessions before dry season — everyone on the upper river talked about it, justice crawling back into session like a snake waking from flood sleep.

And the Kestrel, if he was right about her hull, had ten days.

Tomas finished the plantain without tasting it. Below him the river moved in its sleep, thick and brown and patient, holding everything it had ever taken. Somewhere under that surface lay the Meridian, and a crew whose names he had filed and forgotten, and a debt with his name written in the margin beside Sarn's — smaller handwriting, but the same ledger.

The lanterns on the Kestrel went out one by one. Last of all the aft cabin light died, but not before he saw the captain fold the paper along its existing creases — carefully, precisely, the way archives fold things meant to be kept — and put it away.

Third tie-off, grey punt, copper patch. He'd told her where to find him. Now he watched the dark boat ride at anchor and understood that he had done something more dangerous than offer a bargain: he had told a hunted woman that he existed.

Downstream, far off, a lamp moved against the current where no barge should be running at night — a single light, steady, unhurried, working its way upriver toward Halfway Light. City boots on a mud levee. Government property.

Tomas sat very still in the dark and began, quietly, to plan.
