## 4. Seven Years of Sandbars

The chest sat in the hold like a passenger who hadn't paid.

Mara could feel it through the deck planks as she walked her evening rounds — a weight where no weight should be, a silence that had texture. She'd lashed it under the tarpaulin behind the spare line, and still she caught herself glancing at that corner of the hold the way a man glances at a dog he knows has been in the meat locker. Nothing moved. Nothing ever moved. That was the trouble with it.

She climbed to the pilothouse and found Odile already there, coiling the log line with the particular violence she reserved for questions she'd decided not to ask again.

"Anchor off Miller's Bend," Mara said. "We'll ride out the night on the hook."

"There's four hours of light left."

"And a bar downstream I don't trust after dark. We anchor."

Odile hung the coil on its peg harder than it needed hanging. "You've been running this river seven years," she said. "You know every sandbar by its first name. You don't get spooked by bars."

"I get careful by bars."

"You get *careful* by bars when there's cargo worth stealing. You get *spooked* when there's a Vessian seal in your hold." Odile turned, arms crossed, and Mara saw that she wasn't angry so much as frightened, which was worse. "I've been on this boat three years, Captain. Three years you've never once said why we don't stop at Vessia's old capital, why we take the long channel past Greyharbor even when it costs us a day, why you flinch every time somebody mentions the tribunal. I never asked because it was yours to keep. But now it's floating in our hold with your name written inside it."

Mara looked out the pilothouse window. The Sable ran wide and brown-gold in the last light, monsoon-swollen, carrying whole trees on its back like a bear shrugging off dogs. Somewhere under all that water were sandbars she knew the way other people knew their mother's face. Seven years of reading water instead of faces. It had worked. It had mostly worked.

"Drop the hook," she said quietly. "Then come back up. Bring the good coffee, not the tar we give the lock-keepers."

Odile held her gaze a long moment. Then she went.

* * *

By full dark the Kestrel swung gently at her anchor in the lee of Miller's Bend, lanterns hooded, the flood current murmuring against the hull like a crowd talking in another room. Odile came up with two tin cups and the good coffee, black and bitter and cut with chicory, and sat on the bench beside the wheel without being invited. Mara took the other end of the bench. Between them, the wheel turned idly in small degrees, answering currents they couldn't see.

"The Republic of Vessia fell eight years ago," Mara said, because starting anywhere else would have taken all night. "Not collapsed like a building falls. Collapsed like ice goes out in spring. Slow, then all at once, then just cold water everywhere."

"I was fourteen," Odile said. "I remember the coin stopped being worth anything. My father burned his ledgers."

"A lot of people burned things." Mara wrapped both hands around her cup. The tin was hot enough to hurt, and she let it. "I was running cargo out of Vessel's Rest back then. Legitimate cargo, mostly. The Kestrel was younger, and so was I, and I thought the worst thing that could happen to a boat was a hole in her hull."

The current spoke against the bow. Somewhere downriver an owl called twice and got no answer.

"When the regime came apart, the hunters didn't go away. They just changed employers. The men who used to hunt smugglers for the Republic started hunting them for whoever paid — merchant houses, river lords, the new tribunals cleaning house. And the old smuggling routes were suddenly worth money. Not the cargo. The *routes*. Who could move what, past whose eyes, along which drowned channels. There were men who'd spent ten years learning the Sable's secrets, and those secrets were suddenly inventory."

"You were one of them."

"I was one of them." Mara drank. The coffee tasted like burnt grain and honesty. "There were nine of us who mattered. Nine names on the Kestrel's articles. Doss Merrow, my engineer — a fat, gentle man who could hear a bearing going bad before the bearing knew it itself. His wife Liss, who ran the galley and kept the books and cheated at cards so badly it was practically charity. The Calloway brothers, Tam and Ruel, who'd grown up diving wrecks and could hold their breath long enough to worry you. Old Sef the pilot, who'd taught me half of everything I know about reading water. A boy named Wick who signed on at sixteen to escape his uncle and cried the first time we ran a night passage and swore he hadn't, and we all pretended to believe him. And Anselm."

She stopped. The name sat in the air between them like the chest sat in the hold.

"Anselm Reyes," she said, when she could. "My mate. I pulled him out of a debtor's cell in Greyharbor and gave him a berth and taught him the channels myself, because he had a head for figures and I trusted him with everything I had. He knew every route. Every client. Every safe cove between here and the delta."

Odile had gone very still. "What happened?"

"What always happens. Somebody sold us." Mara heard her own voice go flat, the way it did when she was carrying something too heavy to let it sway. "It was the autumn after the fall. We had a run planned — medicine, mostly, and some of the new scrip the merchant houses were issuing, moving it upriver for people who couldn't be seen buying it. Nine days' passage. We'd made the run four times that year without a whisper. The fifth time, the hunters were waiting at the Narrows below Greyharbor. Not patrolling. *Waiting.* They knew our draft. They knew our lights. They knew we'd come down the drowned channel past the old tollhouse, because nobody else used it anymore."

The wheel ticked against its stops. Mara realized her hands had gone white around the cup and made herself loosen them.

"They fired the Meridian first — a trader running parallel with us, innocent people, nothing to do with any of it. Burning ship in the channel so we couldn't turn. Then they boxed us against the shoal. We ran aground trying to clear them, and they boarded, and—" Her voice did something she hated, a small hitch, a crack in the armor. She drank coffee over it. "Doss died in the engine room keeping steam up so the rest of us might have a chance. Liss wouldn't leave him. The Calloways went into the water to cut the hunters' lines loose from their launch and didn't come up. Sef took two bullets standing at my shoulder, telling me which way to steer, right up until he couldn't talk anymore. Wick—"

She set the cup down on the bench. Her hand wasn't steady enough to hold it and pretend otherwise.

"Wick was seventeen. He surrendered. They shot him anyway, because the bounty was per head and dead men can't argue about the count." The current pushed at the hull, patient, enormous. "Anselm and I went over the side and swam for the oxbow marshes. We got separated in the reeds. I never saw him again. For seven years I told myself he drowned, and I grieved him like a drowned man, and I built him a little grave in my head next to the others. Because the alternative was worse."

"That someone sold the route."

"That Anselm *was* someone." Mara looked out the dark window, at the black water sliding past, at the far shore where fireflies stitched themselves along the treeline like slow sparks off a grinder. "The warrant came down within the month. *Mara Halloran, master of the vessel Kestrel, wanted for questioning in the matter of the Meridian and the deaths attendant thereto.* Questioning. As if I'd burned my own crew. Someone had sold the route *and* sold my name with it, and the hunters needed a survivor to hang it on, and I was the one who crawled out of the marsh. I've been moving ever since. Cargo up, cargo down. Never the same berth twice in a season. Never Vessia's old capital, because that's where the tribunal sits and where warrants are read aloud at the harbor office every morning like scripture. Seven years of sandbars, Odile. Seven years of knowing exactly where the water is shallow and never once daring to find out whether I still have ground under me anywhere else."

Odile was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had lost its edge. "And the chest?"

"Has a manifest inside it." Mara reached into her coat and brought out the folded paper she'd taken from the dispatch tube — she'd told herself she wouldn't look at it again, and she'd been lying to herself the way she lied to lock-keepers about her cargo. She smoothed it on her knee by lantern-light. "Nine names. Crew of a vessel called the *Lanternfish*, listed as assets liquidated under contract — which is regime language for *bought and betrayed*. Eight of the names are strangers to me. The ninth is mine. Mara Halloran. Listed not as captured. Not as escaped. As *outstanding*, with a price beside it that hasn't changed in seven years, which means nobody's collected, which means somewhere there is a piece of paper with my name on it that has outlived a republic."

"But how does a chest with your name in it wash up on a sandbar?"

"That's what I keep asking the dark." Mara refolded the manifest along its worn creases. "Dispatches too. Sealed. Official wax. I haven't broken them. Partly because breaking sealed dispatches is a crime even in a country that doesn't exist anymore, and partly—" She stopped. Made herself finish. "Partly because I'm afraid of what's in them. Seven years I've survived by not looking back. That chest is the past with a handle on it, and every instinct I have says throw it in the river and let the Sable keep what the Sable keeps."

"But?"

"But there are eight names in there I don't know, and one name I do, and if someone sold my crew once, they may have sold others since. And they're still alive to do it." She picked the cup back up. It had gone cold. She drank anyway. "That's the arithmetic I can't put down. Whoever packed that chest, sealed it, lost it to the flood — they were working recently. Wax doesn't rot that fast. Which means somewhere upstream or down, the machine that killed my crew is still turning, and it has my name in its gears."

Odile looked at her for a while, and then out at the water, and then back. "Tomorrow we make for Greyharbor," she said. "You need a chandler anyway — we're short on pitch, and I don't like the sound the stern seam's been making since the flood came up. And Greyharbor's neutral. Harbor-masters there don't read warrants, they read tariffs."

Mara almost smiled. "Since when do you care about pitch?"

"Since tonight." Odile stood, gathered the cups, and paused at the pilothouse door. "For what it's worth, Captain — I'm glad you told me. And I'm glad you didn't sink the chest."

"So am I," Mara said. "Ask me again tomorrow."

Odile went down the ladder, and the boat settled into the sounds of night: water on wood, rope on cleat, the small ticking of the hull cooling. Mara stayed at the wheel a while longer, watching the fireflies stitch the far bank, feeling the current lean on the anchored hull like a hand testing a door.

Seven years of sandbars. And now the river itself had reached down into its drowned pockets and handed her back the one thing she'd paid everything to lose.

Below her feet, through the deck planks, the chest waited in the dark with its wax seals and its nine names, and Mara Halloran stood her midnight watch over it like a woman guarding a wound she was finally ready to let someone clean.

She didn't sleep. Toward morning, in the grey hour when the river smoke lies flat on the water, she heard the stern seam groan — a long, wet, unhappy note she knew too well, the sound a hull makes when it's begun to lose an argument with the water. She knelt and pressed her palm to the planking and felt the dampness blooming through the caulking like a bruise.

The Kestrel was leaking. And forty miles downstream, at a trading post where a quiet salvager had asked too many questions about a chest, the day was waiting to begin.
