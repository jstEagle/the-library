## 5. The Leak

Odile heard the river change before she heard the boat.

That was the thing about seven years on the Sable — you stopped listening with your ears and started listening with your feet, with the soles of your boots against the deck planking, with the small tremors that traveled up through the Kestrel's bones and into yours. The river had a voice for every mood. It murmured when it was lazy, growled over bars, went silent and glassy in the deep channels where the drowned towns lay. And in monsoon season, swollen brown and muscular, it made a sound like a crowd muttering before a riot.

But this morning, standing at the rail with her hands around a tin cup of chicory coffee gone cold, Odile felt something new under her feet. A shiver. A wrongness in the rhythm of the hull's breathing.

She set down the cup and went aft to where Mara stood at the wheel, one hand resting easy on the spoke, reading the water the way a priest reads scripture — every boil and seam of current a sentence, every floating log a warning punctuated properly.

"Captain," Odile said. "She's talking funny."

Mara didn't look away from the channel ahead. "Boats talk funny all the time."

"Not like this." Odile hesitated, then said what she'd been afraid to say plainly: "It's coming from below. Like she's got a toothache."

That got her a glance. Mara's eyes were grey as the sky pressing down on them, and they held the particular stillness of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. "Debris field ahead," she said. "Half a village came downriver last night, if the driftwood's any measure. Roof beams. Fence posts." Her jaw tightened. "A door, I think. I saw a door go by an hour ago, painted blue, with the hinges still on."

"A whole house."

"Houses float when rivers take them." Mara turned back to the wheel. "Keep your ears open. If she starts talking worse, wake me even if I'm sleeping."

"You're not sleeping."

"No," Mara agreed, "I'm not."

* * *

The debris found them two hours past noon, in a stretch of river the charts called the Long Narrows and the boatmen called something less printable. The flood had turned the Sable into a moving country — islands of reed mat and uprooted trees drifting with the current, whole rafts of cane tangled together, and beneath it all, invisible, the things that sank slow: fence posts weighted with wire, millstones, the occasional drowned animal turning belly-up like a pale moon.

Mara took them through it standing, though standing was against her own rules in anything rougher than chop. She threaded the Kestrel between a floating granary roof and a dead cottonwood the size of a chapel, calling depths from memory because the leadsman's readings meant nothing in water this fast. Odile worked the bow pole, fending off logs, feeling each impact travel down the shaft and into her shoulders.

It was going well. That was the terrible part. It was going beautifully, Mara reading the chaos like a familiar page, and Odile had just let herself believe they'd come through clean when the hull spoke again — louder this time, a groan that rose through the deck and became, unmistakably, a scream of timber.

Then the sound every riverboat crew knows and dreads: not the knock of debris against planking but the wet, grinding *shudder* of something finding the seams.

"Starboard side, amidships," Odile shouted, before she even knew she'd seen it — a fence post, half-sunk, its buried end spiked with old nails, sliding past just under the surface exactly where no post had any business being.

Mara's voice cut through everything, calm and absolute: "Hard to port. Odile, get below."

The bilge told the story fast enough. Not a gush — the Kestrel was too well-built, too loved, for a gush — but a thin, insistent tongue of brown water sliding along the limber boards, gathering in the low corner of the hold like it owned the place. Odile crouched with a lantern and watched it come, and felt something in her chest tighten that had nothing to do with the water itself.

Because she knew what Mara knew: the Kestrel couldn't afford this. Couldn't afford a haul-out, couldn't afford a shipwright, couldn't afford the week in dry dock that a proper repair demanded. The boat's accounts lived in Mara's head, and Odile had seen the arithmetic there too — the shrinking margins, the cargoes taken at rates Mara would have sneered at three years ago, the repairs deferred and deferred and deferred until deferral had become a kind of religion.

The pump handle was cold and slick. Odile threw her coat over a crate, spat on her palms, and began to work.

* * *

By evening they had wedged the Kestrel into the lee of a derelict lock — some old canal works abandoned before the Republic fell, its stone gates leaning together like drunkards telling secrets. The flood had turned the lock chamber into a slack-water pool, and Mara brought the boat in with the delicacy of a woman setting a cracked egg into a nest.

They pumped through the night, Odile and Mara trading off in shifts while the rain came down in ropes. By dawn the leak was holding steady — not gaining, not losing, a stalemate — and Mara stood in the hold with the lantern raised, studying the weeping seam with an expression Odile had learned to fear. It wasn't worry. Worry would have been fine. It was calculation, the cold kind, the kind that ended with numbers nobody liked.

"It needs caulking from outside," Mara said finally. "Reaming the seam and driving new oakum. Can't be done afloat. Can't be done beached here — the bank's all silt, she'd list and strain herself worse." She lowered the lantern. "There's a yard at Greyharbor."

"Greyharbor's four days downstream," Odile said. "And we can't make four days pumping like this if the seam lets go in open water."

"I know what Greyharbor is," Mara said, in a voice that closed doors.

Odile looked at the seam. She looked at the water sliding in, patient as debt. And she thought about the salvager — the quiet man from the levee at Tallow Bend, the one who'd offered help with exactly this kind of trouble in exchange for a look inside that cursed chest, and whom Mara had sent off with a look that could have tarred a hull.

"Tomas Vey," she said.

"No."

"He salvages wrecks for a living, Captain. He'll have oakum, mauls, irons, probably a diving rig. He's moored not six miles upstream — we passed his barge yesterday, or did you think I didn't notice you steering wide around it?"

"I noticed." Mara set the lantern down with exaggerated care, which was how Odile knew the fury underneath. "And I know what he wants for his help. He wants into that chest, Odile. And I don't know why, and I don't trust men who come out of Vessia smiling."

"He didn't smile once."

"That's what worries me."

* * *

Odile waited until Mara took the dawn watch, then did the thing she'd been turning over all night like a stone in her pocket.

The trading post at Tallow Bend kept a relay — a boy with a mule who carried messages up and down the towpath for coins, no questions asked, because asking questions was bad for business. Odile rowed the skiff across the flooded field that had been the towpath's neighbor, found the boy dozing in a lean-to that smelled of mule and wet straw, and paid him double to run upstream.

The note took her three tries. She wasn't much of a writer. In the end she kept it simple:

*Hull seam split in the Narrows. Holding at the old lock below Tallow Bend. Captain won't ask. Come anyway. — O.*

She watched the boy splash away through the flooded fields and felt a strange doubling in her chest — guilt and relief braided tight together. Because this was treachery, technically. Small treachery. The kind you did for love. Mara wanted to face the river alone, always alone, wearing her competence like armor plate, and Odile had spent seven years watching that armor rust at the joints.

Sometimes love meant disobeying orders. She'd decided that a long time ago, though she'd never yet had to say it out loud.

* * *

Tomas Vey came downstream in the late afternoon, poling a flat-bottomed skiff loaded with gear, and he arrived the way he seemed to do everything — without announcement, without flourish, simply present, as if he'd been there all along and everyone else had only just noticed.

Mara met him at the gangplank with her arms crossed and the weather of her face set to storm.

"I didn't send for you."

"No," Tomas said. He was a broad, weathered man with the stillness of deep water, and he didn't flinch from her glare; he simply received it, the way a piling receives a boat bumping against it. "The boy who carried the note had a cousin who works my barge. Word travels."

"Odile."

"Odile," he agreed, and something almost dry touched his voice. "You have loyal people, Captain. They disobey you thoughtfully."

Mara's jaw worked. Odile, hovering at the rail with a coil of rope she had no use for, braced for the explosion. But what came instead was worse and better — Mara looking at the gear in the skiff, the mauls and irons and the canvas-wrapped bundle of fresh oakum, and doing arithmetic again. Odile could nearly hear the abacus clicking.

"The price hasn't changed," Mara said flatly. "You don't touch the chest. You don't see inside it. You patch my hull and you go."

"I'll patch your hull," Tomas said. "What you do after is your affair."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I've got." He stepped onto the deck, uninvited, and stood there a moment as if testing whether the Kestrel would object to him. She didn't. Boats rarely objected to Tomas Vey; he had the kind of weight that settled rather than disturbed. "Show me the seam."

* * *

He worked through the evening and into the dark, and Odile watched him the way she'd watch any craftsman — hungrily, taking notes with her eyes. He went over the side in the slack water of the lock chamber with a lantern in a glass box, and for long stretches all Odile could see was the yellow glow wandering beneath the hull like a fish made of light, and the sound of mallet blows transmitted through the water and the timbers, dull and rhythmic, a heartbeat from inside the boat.

Mara didn't watch. Mara sat in the pilothouse with the lamp turned low, ostensibly reviewing cargo manifests, actually staring at nothing. Every ten minutes, by Odile's count, her eyes flicked toward the chest lashed down in the hold under canvas and chain — the chest they'd pulled from the sandbar, the chest with the Vessian seal, the chest that had changed the weather inside their little world ever since.

Twice, Odile caught Tomas surfacing for air and looking at that same spot on the deck above him. He knew where it was. Of course he knew. He'd known since Tallow Bend.

Around midnight the mallets stopped. Tomas climbed aboard, streaming, took the towel Odile handed him, and said to the night in general: "She'll hold. I've driven oakum into the worst of it and pitched over the seam. It's a mend, not a cure — she needs proper yard work before dry season, or the next bar you kiss will open her up for good."

"How long will the mend hold?" Mara asked from the pilothouse doorway, without preamble, without thanks.

"In calm water? Weeks. In the Narrows at flood?" He wrung out his beard. "I wouldn't gamble on it. But then, you strike me as a woman who gambles on worse odds daily."

Silence stretched. Rain ticked on the deck. Somewhere out in the flooded timber, a night bird made a sound like a hinge.

"You'll stay ashore tonight," Mara said. "In the lock-keeper's house. You'll leave at first light."

"There's no floor left in the lock-keeper's house."

"Then you'll stay uncomfortably."

"Captain." Tomas folded the towel with the precision of a man who had once handled documents for a living — Odile saw it now, the archivist's neatness in his hands, whatever he claimed to be these days. "Your boat is bleeding. Your crew is exhausted. Your helmswoman has been pumping bilge for thirty hours. Send me off at first light if you must, but let me sleep aboard tonight, near my tools, in case the seam weeps in the dark. That's not a bargain. That's seamanship."

Odile held her breath. She watched Mara stand in the lamplight with exhaustion carved into her face, watched the war behind her eyes — control against necessity, pride against the plain fact of the leaking hull, the stranger against the growing, unbearable suspicion that the stranger might be the only person on this river who understood what was in that chest.

"One night," Mara said at last. "You sleep in the hold, by the pump. You don't open anything. You don't read anything. And at first light—"

"At first light I'm gone. I understand." He gathered his tools with those careful hands. Then, pausing, he added quietly: "For what it's worth, Captain — whoever built this boat built her to survive. Someone kept her alive a long time after that, too. That someone has my respect."

Mara said nothing. But she didn't repeat the order to leave, either, and Odile, banking the galley stove, counted that as a victory of a kind — small, grudging, bought with a hole in the hull.

* * *

Odile woke once in the black hours and went below to check the pump, out of habit older than sense.

The hold was dim, lit by a single lantern turned low. Tomas sat awake against the bulkhead, arms crossed, not sleeping at all despite his day of labor. His eyes were open and fixed on the chest — not touching it, not approaching it, just looking at it the way a man looks at a grave he dug himself.

"You should sleep," Odile whispered.

"Soon." He didn't look away from the chest. "Do you know what's in it?"

"No. And I don't want to, mostly." Odile leaned against the pump handle, suddenly tired down to her marrow. "But I know what it's doing to her. Seven years I've watched the captain outrun something. Now it's caught up, sitting right there in our hold, and she can't outrun it anymore because it's riding with us." She glanced at him. "You know what's in it, don't you. Or you know enough."

Tomas was quiet a long moment. The lantern flame breathed. Under the deck plates, the river pressed patiently against the patched seam, and the oakum held, and the boat breathed around them like something wounded but stubbornly alive.

"I know enough to be ashamed," he said finally. "That's all I'll say tonight. Ask me again when your captain asks me herself."

Odile studied him — the tired lines of his face, the archivist's hands, the shame he wore like ballast — and made a decision she didn't fully understand yet.

"First light, she's going to tell you to leave," she said. "And you're going to want to leave, because staying means trouble. But I'm telling you now, salvager: this boat doesn't make it to dry season without you. Neither does she." She nodded toward the pilothouse, toward the silhouette of Mara at the wheel even now, unable to stop working, unable to stop steering. "So figure out how to be worth the trouble. Fast."

Above them, rain resumed on the deck, soft and endless, and the river muttered its crowd-murmur in the dark, carrying down toward Greyharbor everything the flood had torn loose — doors, houses, names, the past — all of it drifting toward the sea, except what had washed up on their sandbar, except what lay chained under canvas three feet from where they sat, waiting for morning the way a verdict waits.
