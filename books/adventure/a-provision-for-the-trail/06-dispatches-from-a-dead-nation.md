## 6. Dispatches from a Dead Nation

Tomas Vey had spent the night on the Kestrel's deck because Mara Halloran had told him he could stay one night and no more, and because a man who had learned to read the fine print of other people's mercy knew better than to overdraw an account.

He lay on his bedroll beneath the cargo awning with the patched hull breathing under him — that faint tidal flex of timber taking water and giving it back — and listened to the river work at the derelict lock where they'd sheltered. The old lock's gates had rotted open years ago; the current came through in long slow exhalations, dragging weed against stone, and somewhere upstream a night bird made its one dry note over and over like a clerk stamping papers.

Clerk. The word found him even here, forty miles from any archive, in a country that had never heard of Vessia.

He had not slept. He had spent the dark hours doing what he had done every night for eleven years: arranging facts into rows and pretending the rows were peace.

* * *

Mara was awake before dawn. He heard her boots on the deck plates, heard her pause when she saw him sitting up, and then continue past without comment, which was its own kind of sentence. She went forward to check the patch — his patch, the one Odile had sent for him to make — pressing her palm flat against the caulking the way a physician checks a fevered brow.

"It held," she said, not turning around.

"They usually do, if you set them right."

"Mm." She straightened. "You'll want coffee before you go, or you'll want it after?"

The question was a door left open exactly the width of a man's shoulders. Tomas rose, folded his bedroll with archival precision, and said, "Before. If it's no trouble."

"Nothing on this boat is trouble until I decide it is." She went aft to the galley box, and while her back was turned he allowed himself to look at the chest.

It sat amidships where they'd lashed it, wrapped now in oilcloth against the damp, but the Vessian seal showed through where the cloth had worked loose — that double-headed river-serpent biting its own tail, embossed in wax gone the color of dried blood. Eleven years since he'd last seen that seal. He had seen it ten thousand times before that. He had pressed it himself, once, as a junior archivist trusted with the ceremonial kit on the Minister's name-day, and remembered how the wax took the impression too eagerly, how the serpent's tails always blurred.

This one hadn't blurred. This one had been pressed by someone who cared about the work.

Odile came up from below, yawning, and stopped dead when she saw him. The deckhand was perhaps twenty-five, all elbows and suspicion, hair cut short with what looked like a fish knife. She looked at Tomas the way one looks at weather that has come in uninvited.

"Coffee," Mara said from the galley. "Three cups."

"We only have two cups."

"Then someone's drinking from the pot."

It was, Tomas understood, the closest thing to hospitality the Kestrel offered. He sat down carefully on a cargo crate and did not touch the chest, though every nerve in his hands wanted to.

* * *

They drank coffee watching the light come up gray-gold through the lock's broken crown. Mist stood on the water in columns. Downstream, the Sable ran wide and brown and patient, carrying everything toward Greyharbor and the sea and forgetting nothing.

"You looked at it," Mara said at last. It wasn't a question. "Last night. While we slept."

"No."

"Don't lie to me on my own deck."

"I didn't touch it," Tomas said. "I didn't need to. I could read the seal from where I'm sitting." He turned his cup slowly. "Double-headed serpent, third issue of the Ministry of Rivers and Interior Passage, pressed after the fourth year of the Emergency — the wax is the gray-tallow blend they used when beeswax got requisitioned for the army. There's a counter-mark under the left head. You can't see it from here. I can see it because I know it's there."

Mara set her cup down. The sound it made against the deck plate was very small and very loud.

"Say the rest of it," she said.

"I was an archivist." The words came out of him with the strange flatness of things rehearsed for years and never performed. "Ministry of Rivers and Interior Passage. Records Division, Sub-bureau Four — manifests, transit licenses, sealed dispatches. For six years I catalogued the paperwork of a country drowning itself, and then the country finished drowning, and the paperwork floated downstream like everything else." He looked at the mist, not at her. "I know your chest, Captain Halloran, the way a priest knows a psalm. And I know what's likely inside it. Which is why I followed you."

"Likely."

"A sealed dispatch case, third issue, post-Emergency four. Those carried only two kinds of cargo. Interdiction orders — hunter warrants, route seizures — or payment authorizations." His voice stayed level. He was proud of that, in a distant, sick way. "Given that yours was wedged in a floodbar with half a drowned barge, my guess is payment. The hunters' pay barges went down in the last spring of the war, three of them, on the middle Sable. Everyone assumed the records went down with them."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I've wasted your coffee."

Odile snorted. Mara didn't. She was looking at the chest now, and Tomas watched her face do something complicated — hope and dread fighting for the same ground — before she shut it away behind the captain's mask he was beginning to recognize.

"If I let you open it," she said, "you tell me everything you see. Everything. No archivist's discretion, no 'this portion is unclear.' You don't get to curate my dead."

"Agreed."

"And if there's nothing in it but numbers—"

"There won't be." He heard his own certainty and hated how much it sounded like longing. "Numbers are how they wrote everything, Captain. That's the trick of it. No one ever signed an order that said *kill them*. They signed orders that said *authorize disbursement*. The killing was just what the money did once it was loose."

* * *

She made him wait until the sun was fully up. He understood that too — it wasn't cruelty, it was procedure, the same instinct that made her check the patch twice and count her lines before casting off. A captain doesn't open strange cargo in the dark.

They cleared space amidships. Odile brought a hatchet and Mara waved it off; the oilcloth came away with a knife, respectfully, the way you'd unwrap a body. The chest itself was black lacquer over iron banding, swollen with its years underwater, and the lock was fused with corrosion. It took twenty minutes of careful work with a drift pin and Mara's smallest pry bar, and during those twenty minutes nobody spoke, and the river spoke enough for all of them.

The lid came free with a sigh of trapped air that smelled of river mud and old wax and something else — something Tomas's body recognized before his mind did. Ink. Vessian state ink, the iron-gall blend with the ash additive that made it survive water better than paper did. He had breathed that smell for six years. It went into him like a key into a lock he'd hoped was rusted shut.

Inside, packed in rotted wool: dispatch tubes. Nine of them. Sealed, serialized, intact.

Tomas lifted the first one and his hands were steady, which surprised him. He broke the seal — the gray tallow wax parting along the line he predicted, serpent's tails crisp — and drew out the roll within.

Paper survives water poorly, but these had been oiled. The ink had feathered at the edges. It didn't matter. He had spent six years reading this hand, this format, this bureaucratic liturgy, and it came back to him the way a language comes back to a child.

"*By authority of the Ministry of Rivers and Interior Passage,*" he read aloud, because Mara had ordered everything and because his own voice was the only way he could be sure he wasn't dreaming it. "*Authorization for disbursement, Special Operations Account Seven. To the bearer or designated agent, sum of four hundred crowns Vessian, against services rendered in the interdiction of unauthorized passage on the middle reach.*"

"Services rendered," Mara repeated.

"That's the standard formula." He unrolled further. "*Services certified by:*" — and here the paper had a signature block, and the ink had feathered, but the name was written large, in the confident hand of a man who signed many documents and feared none of them —

Tomas stopped.

He had known, in the abstract, what might be in the chest. He had built the whole argument of the last two weeks on probabilities, on serial ranges, on the drowning of the pay barges. He had not, until this exact moment, let himself imagine a specific name.

"What," Mara said. Not a question either. Her voice had gone quiet the way water goes quiet over deep holes.

Tomas read the name. He read it aloud because he had agreed to everything, and because some part of him wanted to say it in front of witnesses so it would become real and he could stop carrying it alone.

"Corvel Sarn."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough for the mist to burn off the water entirely.

"Say it again," Mara said.

"Corvel Sarn. Certified by Corvel Sarn, Special Liaison, Middle Reach Operations." Tomas turned the roll so she could see, though he knew she couldn't read the Vessian ministerial shorthand — few outside the service ever had, another small cruelty of the design. But she stared at it anyway, the way you stare at a face in a crowd that you haven't seen in seven years.

"He's alive," she said.

"He's more than alive." And here it came, the second half of the thing Tomas had been carrying, the thing he'd followed her boat for and dreaded delivering. "Captain — do you follow the news out of the Protectorate? The successor states?"

"I follow cargo rates."

"Then you may not know. After the collapse, when the tribunal was seated at Vessel's Rest — the rump government, the ones trying the old regime's hunters and informers — they needed witnesses. Cooperators. Men from inside who could name names." He rolled the dispatch closed with exaggerated care, buying seconds. "Corvel Sarn came forward in the second year. He gave them the middle-reach network. Names, routes, accounts. In exchange for cooperation, immunity. He testified for eleven days. They called him the man who bought the map of the darkness." Tomas's mouth tasted of ash. "He's a trader now. Grain and river salvage, out of Greyharbor, I'm told. Respected. The tribunal uses him as their star witness whenever a new case opens. When they want to show the world that justice works, they put Corvel Sarn on the stand."

Mara had gone very still. It was the stillness of the river before a bank collapses.

"The man who sold my crew," she said, each word placed like a foot on rotten ice, "is the tribunal's honored witness."

"Yes."

"And they gave him immunity."

"For testimony. Yes."

Odile said, from the rail, "That's — that's not justice, that's—"

"That's arithmetic," Tomas said. "It's always arithmetic. That's the whole disease." He looked down at the dispatch in his hands, at the confident signature, and felt the old shame rise in him like bilgewater. Because he recognized the format of this document completely, and recognition meant memory, and memory meant—

"Read the rest," Mara said. "All nine. Everything."

So he did. It took most of the morning. Tube by tube, seal by seal, he broke them open and read aloud: disbursement authorizations spanning eight months of the war's last year, payments routed to agents whose code-designations he could partially reconstruct, and — in the fifth tube — the thing that made Mara sit down on her own cargo crate as if her legs had been quietly removed.

An interdiction order. Route designation: the smuggler's channel above the Meridian shoals. Date: eleven days before the Meridian burned.

"*Source of intelligence,*" Tomas read, and his voice finally cracked, "*certified reliable by C. Sarn, Special Liaison.*"

Below it, in the distribution block, a list of the hunter units tasked. Below that, the authorization number. And below that, in the corner, the processing stamp of the Records Division, Sub-bureau Four — the stamp that had passed across a junior archivist's desk on an ordinary gray afternoon, the stamp of a young man who had read the order, understood perfectly what it meant, and filed it.

Tomas had not let himself remember that afternoon in eleven years. Now it arrived whole: the smell of the heating pipes, the weight of the order in his hands, the moment — three seconds, maybe four — when he had looked at the route designation and thought, *that channel runs past the Meridian*, and thought, *someone will die for this*, and then had stamped it anyway, because the alternative was a cell in the Ministry basement, and he was twenty-three, and twenty-three-year-olds believe they will find a better moment later.

There had been no better moment. There was only this one, seven years and a river away, holding the proof of his own cowardice in both hands.

"Tomas." Mara's voice, from far away. "Your face. What is it?"

"Nothing," he said, which was the same lie, the same stamp, the same three seconds of cowardice, repeated faithfully across a decade. "The processing stamp. It's — it confirms authenticity. That matters, for the tribunal. A chain of custody."

It was even true. Truth was easy that way; it always had room in it for a smaller lie.

* * *

Mara stood at the rail for a long time after the tubes were rewrapped. The afternoon wind had come up, pushing whitecaps down the reach, and the Kestrel pulled at her lines against the derelict lock like a dog that smelled home.

"Four hundred crowns," she said finally. "Per operation. Do you know what I'd have paid for one clean run out of Vessia with my crew alive? Anything. Anything in the world." She turned. "And he sold them for four hundred, and testified his way into a pardon, and now he sells grain."

"Yes."

"He's thriving."

The word landed in Tomas like a splinter driven to the bone. *Thriving.* He thought of the archives burning — officially an accident, unofficially everyone's guess — and of the ledgers he'd saved by carrying them out in a grain sack, and of the eleven years since, salvaging other people's drowned property because he couldn't salvage his own decency. He was not thriving. He was preserved. There was a difference, and the difference had a name, and the name was on the dispatch in the chest.

"He'll fight this," Tomas said. "You understand that. The moment this chest surfaces, Sarn has every reason to bury it — and us with it. He has money, protection, and a tribunal that loves him. We have a leaking boat and—" he gestured at himself, at Odile, at the whole improvised armament of their alliance, "—this."

"Then we'd best not surface it yet." Mara's eyes had changed. The grief was still there, but underneath it something had caught fire, banked low, the way coal holds through a night. "Greyharbor first. Quietly. I want to know what he looks like now, where he drinks, who guards him. Seven years I've run from that warrant. I'm done running from it. If the truth's going to cost me, I'll spend it myself."

Tomas nodded, and said nothing about the processing stamp, and the lie sat in his chest like ballast.

But that night, as the Kestrel cast off from the derelict lock and slid out into the darkening reach, he took out his salvager's notebook — the one with the waterproof pages — and in the back, in a cipher only a Sub-bureau Four man would recognize, he began to write down everything he had sworn to tell her and hadn't yet.

Because the chest proved Sarn had bought the betrayal.

Only Tomas could prove who had processed the order — and why a junior archivist's stamp was on it at all.

The river carried them downstream toward Greyharbor, and Tomas Vey wrote fast, before his courage, like the water level, could drop any further.
