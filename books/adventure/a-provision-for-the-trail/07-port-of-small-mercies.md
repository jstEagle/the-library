## 7. Port of Small Mercies

Greyharbor announced itself the way it always did: by smell. Woodsmoke and tar first, then the green rot of the tidal flats, then the fry-oil and fish-gut sweetness of the market row — a layered stench that Mara Halloran had learned, over seven years, to read the way other captains read weather. The town was awake before the Kestrel was even tied off, lanterns swinging along the levee like a string of low orange stars, and somewhere ahead a woman was singing a work song with a rhythm meant for hauling nets, though it was too early for nets and too late for hauling.

"Starboard line!" Mara called, and Odile threw it, and the rope hissed through her hands as she took the turn around the bollard, and the Kestrel eased against the pilings with a groan that Mara felt in her own ribs. The boat had been talking to her all the way down from the oxbows — a new complaint in the hull, low and wet, somewhere aft of the cargo hold. She'd have to have a caulker look at it. She'd have to have coin to pay a caulker. The two problems circled each other like gulls over a kill.

"You're frowning at the dock," Odile said, coming up beside her with the mooring lines coiled over one shoulder.

"I'm frowning at the price of dock space."

"You're frowning at everything since the chest came aboard."

Mara didn't answer that, because it was true, and because the chest was sitting in her hold right now, wrapped in oilcloth, wedged behind a false bulkhead that had never once in the Kestrel's life held anything worth hiding until it held this. Fourteen days since the flood had coughed it up onto a sandbar like a bone. Fourteen days of telling herself she'd decide what to do with it at Greyharbor, and fourteen days of knowing she was lying, because Greyharbor wasn't a decision. It was a delay with a market.

"Go get breakfast," she said. "Both of us. And listen."

Odile's mouth went flat. She was twenty-three and had the kind of face that couldn't keep a secret from anyone who knew how to look, which was why Mara had taught her to stop trying and simply stop talking instead. "Listening," she said. "That's the job now."

"Listening is always the job. Everything else is just moving cargo."

* * *

Greyharbor's market row ran along the levee for a quarter mile, stalls under striped canvas, everything the river brought and everything the river took sold side by side. Mara walked it with her hood up and her collar high, not because anyone in Greyharbor knew her face — she'd made sure of that, over the years, by being forgettable here on purpose — but because habit was a kind of armor too, and she'd learned to wear it even in safe water. Odile drifted a few steps behind, doing what Mara had asked without being told twice: not shopping. Listening.

The chest's manifest was folded in Mara's breast pocket. She had read it so many times in the dark of her cabin that the paper had gone soft at the creases, the ink beginning to ghost through to the other side. Eleven names. Eleven names in a clerk's careful Vessian hand, each with a route number beside it, each with a price. She knew eight of them the way you know the names of the dead — slowly, and then all at once, and then forever. Bren Aldous, who'd taught her to read shoal-water by the color of the foam. The Ivo twins, who'd argued about everything except each other. Captain Sef, who'd given a nineteen-year-old deck girl her first command of a lock passage and never once made her feel like a girl doing a man's work.

And at the bottom, in the same hand, the same ink: *M. Halloran — route pending — price deferred.* Deferred. As if her death were a bill that could be carried to the next quarter.

She bought nothing. She stood at a fry stall and ate nothing, holding the paper cone of fish Odile pressed into her hands, and she listened.

It came to her in pieces, the way bad news always did. A barge hand two stalls down, complaining to his neighbor about the new money in town — "some southern trader's agent, buying up silence, if you ask me, and I didn't ask" — and the neighbor laughing and saying that was Greyharbor for you, everything for sale, always had been. Then a pair of pilots at the chandlery window, voices dropping when she came near, which was its own kind of message. Then, clearest of all, in the back room of the Ropewalk tavern where the door stood open to the noon heat: a smooth voice with a southern lilt, saying, not loudly, to someone who was being paid to listen:

"—the chest, sealed, contents unexamined. And the woman who carries it. Halloran. The price is the same for either, but both together is double, and there's a bonus if the woman's unmarked. He wants her able to talk."

Mara set the paper cone of fish down on the nearest barrel and walked away at a pace she was proud of, because every part of her wanted to run.

* * *

The Kestrel's cabin was hot in the afternoon, the river sun coming through the stern windows like a held breath. Mara sat at her table with the manifest spread flat and her hands on either side of it, and Odile sat across from her with her boots off and her knees drawn up, which was what she did when she was scared and wouldn't say so.

"Double for both," Odile said. "Unmarked."

"That's the shape of it."

"And you're sitting here reading that paper again like it's going to grow a third column."

Mara almost smiled. "It might. There's a lot of paper in that chest, Odile. Dispatches. Sealed orders. Enough names and numbers to hang a man twice over, if the right eyes read them."

"Whose eyes?"

"The tribunal's. Vessel's Rest." She said it and heard how it sounded — like a word in a language she'd sworn off. Vessel's Rest, the old capital, the marble town on the bluff where the Republic had held its tribunals before the Republic drowned in everything but name, and where the new government had set up its courts in the same halls because marble was expensive and grief was free. Seven years she'd given that town a berth of thirty miles, wide as a funeral. Seven years she'd told herself the warrant didn't matter because she'd never let it touch her.

"You're not seriously—" Odile unfolded, dropped her feet to the floorboards. "Mara. Look at this honestly. A man is paying for that chest. A man with enough money to buy silence in a town that invented the trade. Every day it sits in our hold, we're carrying a price on our backs down a river full of people who need the money. You could end that today. There's a deep hole off the second bend below the lock — forty feet if it's a yard. Wrap it in chain, it'll never come up. Nobody finds it, nobody gets paid, nobody dies."

"And the names?"

Odile stopped. Outside, a gull screamed at another gull about something neither would ever get.

"The names are dead," Odile said, quieter. "You've told me that yourself. A hundred times. The river keeps them. Sinking a box doesn't kill them any deader."

"The river keeps them," Mara said. "But the river doesn't *say* them." She turned the manifest around and pushed it across the table, and Odile looked at it, because Odile always looked, even when looking hurt her. "Eleven names. Eight I knew. This one—" her finger came down on Bren Aldous, and her voice did something she hated, went rough at the edges — "this one pulled me out of the water at Cutter's Bend when I was seventeen and too proud to admit I couldn't swim. And this one—" the Ivo twins, both names, one price, split between them like everything else they'd shared — "these two died because somebody sold their route for less than I make in a season hauling grain. Less, Odile. That's what their lives were priced at, by the man who bought them, and now that man is a *respectable trader* with an agent in Greyharbor offering me a bonus if I come in *unmarked*."

"So we sink it and run. That's not forgetting them. That's staying alive to remember them."

"Isn't it, though?" Mara leaned back, and the cabin seemed to tilt with her, or she with it. "I've been running for seven years. I know every sandbar between here and the headwaters, I can read the Sable in the dark by the sound of it, and I have never once — not once — stopped moving long enough to say their names out loud. Not at a grave. Not at a table. Not even to you, and you're the closest thing to crew I've let aboard since. I say *them*. I say *my people*. I don't say Bren. I don't say Sef." She looked down at the paper. "That chest is the first thing in seven years that says them for me. And you want me to wrap it in chain."

Odile was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had the flat practicality she used when she was about to be right in a way Mara didn't want to hear.

"Then say them. Say them now, out loud, at this table, and then sink the chest. Say them and mean them and keep them, and keep your boat, and keep your neck. Because here's what I see, Captain, and you can throw me off the boat if you want: you've got a hull that's talking, a hold full of paper worth double to a killer, a price on your head with a bonus for unmarked, and one plan, and the plan is to sail up to the people who wrote the warrant on you and hand them the evidence and *hope*. That's not a plan. That's a prayer with cargo."

"It's the truth."

"The truth sank the *Meridian*," Odile said, and then went white, because that was the name neither of them used, the boat Mara's old crew had died on, and she'd said it the way you say a curse — to make a point you can't make any other way.

Mara stood. Not fast, not angry — she was too tired for anger, had been too tired for it for fourteen days — but she stood, and Odile stood too, chin up, refusing to take it back.

"Get some sleep," Mara said. "We decide at dawn."

"You always decide at dawn. It's how you avoid deciding at night."

"Get some sleep, Odile."

* * *

She didn't sleep. She went up on the roof of the cabin instead, where the night air came off the river cool and smelling of silt, and she sat with her back against the chimney and watched the lanterns of Greyharbor burn down one by one along the levee. The town went quiet in layers — the market first, then the taverns, then the late traffic on the water, until all that was left was the lap of the current against the hull and, somewhere across the river, a dog with opinions.

The chest was ten feet below her, behind the false bulkhead. She could feel it the way you feel a stone in your boot — not painful, exactly, just *there*, insisting.

*Say them,* Odile had said. So she did. Alone, under her breath, to the river, in the order they'd lived rather than the order they'd died, which was the only order that had ever seemed fair. Bren Aldous. Tam and Rell Ivo. Captain Sef Marrow. Old Wenna, who'd cooked for three boats in her day and cursed in two languages. Pell, who was nineteen. Doss. Hark. Little Anselm, who'd signed on for one run and stayed for six years. She said all eight, and then she said the other three, the ones from the manifest she hadn't known, strangers dead on her behalf, bought at the same cheap rate, and saying those felt somehow worse — grief with no memory under it, just the shape of a debt.

The river took the names the way it took everything, without comment.

And that was the trouble, she thought. That was the whole trouble. The river took them and kept them and said nothing, and she had spent seven years doing the same, and Odile was right that it wasn't living, but Odile was wrong about what would fix it, because you couldn't sink a truth and keep it. She'd tried that already, in a way. She'd sunk herself — her name, her history, her face — under seven years of cargo and current, and all that had done was leave her a woman who could read any water on the Sable except her own.

A lantern came along the levee. Mara watched it without much interest until it stopped directly across from the Kestrel's berth, and a figure stood there, hooded against the river damp, not moving, not hailing, just — waiting. Then the figure raised one hand, not a wave, more a *look*, and turned the lantern so its light fell full on its own face for exactly three seconds.

Tomas Vey. The salvager from the trading post, the quiet man with the archivist's hands and the salvager's shoulders, who had offered to help with her hull in exchange for a look inside the chest and been refused, and who had somehow, impossibly, followed her boat eighty miles of river to a town where she was worth double.

He set the lantern down on the levee stones, deliberately, where anyone could see it — a message in itself, *I'm not hiding* — and then he crouched and wrote something in the dust of the walk with one finger, and stood, and walked away without looking back, and the lantern stayed burning there like an offering.

Mara sat a long time before she went down to look. When she finally crouched at the rail and read what he'd written, the letters neat as a ledger line, she felt the night go cold all the way through her oilskin.

*They know which berth. Move tonight.*
