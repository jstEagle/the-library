## 8. The Man Who Bought Loyalty

Tomas Vey had spent the night in the Kestrel's hold with a lamp turned low and his hands buried to the wrist in oakum, and by dawn he had learned what he had come aboard to learn: that Mara Halloran's boat was dying from the inside out, and so was he.

The patched seam at the garboard strake had held through the night — his work, and good work, the kind of work a man did with his whole body when his mind wanted nothing so much as to be elsewhere. But holding the seam meant lying against the hull's inner skin for hours, ear to the wood, listening to the river breathe through it. And somewhere past midnight, with the water whispering its one word over and over — *through, through, through* — Tomas had stopped thinking about caulking and started thinking about paper.

Paper was his trade. Had been. In the Archive of Vessia, in the years when the Republic still pretended to be a republic, Tomas Vey had been a second-class registrar with clean fingernails and a stamping hand so precise his supervisor had once called it *calligraphic*. He processed dispatches. He sealed them, countersigned them, entered them into the great ledgers whose leather spines still haunted him like a row of closed mouths. He had told himself, then, that ink was not rope. That a man who filed orders was not the man who carried them out. He had told himself this the way a drowning man tells himself the water is only up to his knees.

And last night, by lamplight, while Odile slept in the deckhouse and Mara stood her watch alone at the wheel because she trusted no one else to stand it, Tomas had opened the chest again — Mara had left it unguarded, which was either trust or a test, and he suspected it was a test — and he had read the third dispatch from the top.

He knew the seal-work before he knew the words. The double-pressed wax, the hairline crack radiating from the counter-stamp where the die had been set a half-degree off true — he had *made* that impression himself, or one exactly like it, on an evening seven years ago when the rain had sounded very much like the rain falling now on the Kestrel's deck above him. Dispatch 4471-C. Route interdiction authorization. He remembered the route number because he had looked it up afterward, once, in a moment of weakness he had never permitted himself to repeat: a smuggling lane on the Sable, marked for hunter interception, three names attached to the vessel manifest.

The *Meridian*.

He had not known, then, whose crew those names belonged to. He had told himself that too. The ledgers were full of names; names were the river's business, and rivers drowned people every season without anyone signing anything. It was only later — much later, after the collapse, after the Archive burned and the tribunal rose from its ashes hungry for someone to blame who wasn't already dead — that he had learned what 4471-C had bought, and whom.

Now he sat in the grey light of morning with oakum under his nails and the confession swelling in his throat like floodwater behind a bad lock gate, and he understood that there were two ways this day could go. He could say nothing. He could patch boats beside this woman until the dry season came, earn his keep, keep his silence, and let the truth stay filed where he had always kept it — neatly, precisely, calligraphically — in the ledger of things Tomas Vey would not look at.

Or he could open the ledger.

* * *

Mara found him on deck at first light, and she did not look like a woman who had slept. She looked like the river itself in low water: hard-edged, all shoal and current, nothing wasted. She handed him a tin cup of coffee boiled down to something like tar and stood at the rail beside him, watching the oxbow where they'd tied up for the night repairs. Mist lay on the water in long grey ropes. Somewhere upstream a kingfisher rattled out its dry laugh and fell silent, as if embarrassed.

"The seam held," she said.

"It'll hold better if we careen her at the next lock. There's a derelict about six miles down — old canal works, stone apron. We can beach her on the fall and get at the planking properly."

"You've done this before."

"I've done worse." He drank the coffee. It tasted like punishment, which felt appropriate. "Mara."

She turned. In the mist-light her face was unreadable, but her body wasn't; he had learned in their few days of wary alliance that she stood differently when she expected bad news — weight back, like a woman braced against a wave she'd seen coming a long way off. She stood that way now.

"You read the chest," she said. Not a question.

"I read the chest."

"Then you know what's in it."

"I know more than what's in it." His mouth had gone dry around the coffee's bitterness. "That's what I need to tell you. And you're going to want to put me off this boat, and I'll go if you tell me to go, but you're going to hear it first. You've earned that much. More than that."

The kingfisher laughed again, far off. Mara said nothing. She waited the way she waited at a tricky bend — patient, total, giving the water room to show its hand. Tomas set down the cup because his hands had begun to shake, and he would not let her see them shake and also hear what he had to say; a man got to choose one shame at a time.

"Before I was a salvager," he said, "I was an archivist. Second-class registrar, Central Archive, Vessia. Eleven years. I processed dispatches for the Interior Bureau — routing, sealing, entry. Thousands of them. I never wrote one. I never decided anything. I told myself that mattered." He heard how the sentence sagged under its own weight and made himself finish it anyway. "It didn't matter. A lock doesn't have to want the flood to come through. It just has to be open."

Mara's weight shifted forward, off her heels. "Go on."

"Dispatch 4471-C. Dated the ninth of Rainmonth, seven years ago. Interdiction authorization for a smuggling route on the middle Sable. Three names on the intercept list." He made himself look at her. "I sealed it. My stamp is on it. I remember the evening — it was raining, the same kind of rain, and my supervisor brought the file down personally instead of sending a runner, which should have told me something, and I pressed the wax and cracked the counter-seal because the die was set off true, and I entered it in the ledger, and I went home and ate dinner." His voice did something ugly on the last words, thinned and frayed, and he let it. "I went home and ate dinner, Mara."

The mist moved between them. On the water, a log the size of a coffin slid past the mooring line, turning slowly, going wherever the river took it.

"I didn't know the names," he said. "That's not a defense. I'm telling you the shape of the cowardice, not excusing it. I could have known. The route number was in the file. The vessel was in the file. I chose not to look, because looking would have made me responsible, and I had built eleven years on not being responsible. And then the Meridian went down with her crew, and the woman who captained her sister boat vanished off every registry on the river, and two years later the Archive burned and I walked out of Vessia with nothing but my hands, and I told myself the fire had settled my accounts." He shook his head. "Fires don't settle accounts. They just destroy the evidence. I was the evidence."

For a long moment Mara did not move at all. Then she reached down, took up his coffee cup, drank from it herself — a small, terrible courtesy, claiming the tar he couldn't finish — and set it back on the rail.

"The third dispatch," she said. Her voice was level. Level the way deep water is level. "The one with the payment schedule."

"Yes."

"That's yours too."

"The seal-work, yes. I recognized it last night. Same die, same flaw. Whoever bought your crew's route used our bureau seals — which means it went through the Interior chain, which means—" He stopped. Breathed. "Which means it may have gone through my hands twice. Once as the order, once as the payment. I don't remember the payment voucher. I processed four hundred documents a week. But I can't tell you I didn't touch it, and I won't tell you that, because you've spent seven years being lied to by people whose whole trade was telling people what they wanted to hear."

Something crossed her face then — not the rage he had braced for. Something worse. Recognition. The look of a woman finding the exact shape of an old wound in a stranger's body.

"Do you know what I did when the hunters came?" she said quietly. "We were running the Narrows at night, darkened, no lights, because the route was supposed to be clean. Sable had been quiet for a month. Someone had *bought* that quiet, and I didn't know it, and my helmsman — a boy named Ferren, nineteen, could read water like scripture — he died not knowing why the cutter was waiting for us. They all died not knowing why." Her hand found the rail and gripped it. "Seven years I've asked the river why. And the answer was sitting in a filing cabinet in Vessia with a neat little stamp on it. Your stamp."

"Yes."

"And you followed my boat downstream. You offered to fix my hull. You've been sleeping ten feet from me, eating my coffee, learning my river — knowing this."

"Yes." The word cost him everything he had. "Because I thought if I could help you carry the chest to the tribunal, maybe the scales would—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked across the deck like a hawser parting. "Don't you dare talk to me about scales. You don't get to balance. There is no balancing. Ferren was nineteen. Do you understand? There is no ledger in the world big enough."

"I know."

"You *know*." She laughed, and it was the worst sound he had ever heard from her, worse than anger — a dry, airless thing with no floor under it. "You know. Everyone knows. The clerks knew. The supervisors knew. The whole Republic ran on men who knew, filing papers they knew would kill people, and then the Republic drowned and they all became salvagers and fishermen and honest traders, and the river forgave them because the river forgets. Except it doesn't, does it. It brings things *up*." She gestured at the hold, at the chest below, at the whole sodden resurrection of the past. "That's what the flood was. That's what this is."

Tomas stood in it. He did not defend himself. He had prepared defenses on the walk down from the trading post — he had rehearsed them, actually, in the privacy of his own skull, smooth sentences about duty and ignorance and the machinery of states — and now, hearing Ferren's age spoken aloud in the mist, he understood that every one of those sentences was oakum stuffed into a seam that needed new planking. So he gave her the only thing he had left.

"When the tribunal summons witnesses," he said, "they'll need someone who can authenticate the seal-work. Prove the dispatches are genuine, prove the counter-forgery if Sarn's people produce one. There are perhaps three living archivists who could do it. I am one. And I will do it, Mara. Under my own name. With my own record read into the proceedings — including 4471-C, including my silence, including all of it. I'll hand them the ledger with myself in it."

"Why?" The question came out raw. "Why would you burn yourself?"

"Because silence is how they win. It's how they won the first time. One clerk at a time, choosing not to look." He met her eyes and held them, though it felt like holding a hot iron. "You've carried your dead for seven years. Let me carry mine. It's the least unequal trade I have to offer."

Mara looked at him for a long time. The mist was thinning; the sun was coming up somewhere behind it, a pale coin behind gauze, and the light found the grey in her hair and the lines the river had written at the corners of her eyes. When she finally spoke, her voice had changed — not warm, nothing like warm, but no longer aimed at him like a weapon.

"If you're lying to me," she said, "if any part of this is a play to get close to that chest for your own reasons — Sarn's reasons, the tribunal's reasons, anyone's — I will put you ashore at the next lock, and I will not stop the boat, and whatever the river does with you after that is between you and the river."

"That's fair."

"It's not fair. Fairness is for people who haven't lost a crew." She pushed off the rail and turned toward the deckhouse, then stopped, her back to him, and he saw her shoulders rise and fall once, twice, the way a woman breathes when she is deciding not to drown something. "Odile trusts you. That's the only reason you're still standing on my deck. Don't make a liar out of her too."

She went below. Tomas stayed at the rail a while longer, watching the mist burn off the oxbow, and found that his hands had stopped shaking — not because the fear was gone, but because confession, like caulking, was work, and finished work held its shape.

It was Odile who broke the morning open. She came up the companionway two steps at a time with the third dispatch in her fist and an expression Tomas had learned to dread on the faces of people bringing him news — the tight, careful blankness of someone carrying something breakable and terrible.

"Tomas." She thrust the paper at him. "The payment schedule. Look at the dates. Look at the *last* date."

He took it. Read it. And felt the bottom drop out of the morning, because the final entry on the schedule — the retainer, the ongoing payment, the money that had bought a crew's death — was dated not seven years ago.

It was dated eleven weeks ago.

The payments hadn't stopped when the Republic fell. They had never stopped at all. Which meant Corvel Sarn was not merely alive and respectable.

He was still paying.
