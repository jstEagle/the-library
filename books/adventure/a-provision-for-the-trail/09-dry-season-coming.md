## 9. Dry Season Coming

Mara knew the river was dying the way she knew a fever coming on in her own body — not by any single sign but by a wrongness in the whole of it, a heat without sweat, a silence where there should have been voice.

The Sable had been singing to her for seven years. In flood it roared; in the long middle of the wet season it muttered and chuckled over its bars like an old woman counting coins. But now, three weeks past the last hard rain, it had begun to whisper. She stood at the wheel before dawn with the mist peeling off the water in rags and listened, and the whisper told her everything: the channel off Gullard's Point had narrowed by half since the spring run. The bar below the drowned church at Selby's Landing had grown a new spine of silt, dark and smooth as a whale's back, waiting just under the surface for a hull like hers.

She could read the water better than most people read their own children. That had never once saved her from herself.

"You're doing it again," Odile said behind her, arriving with two cups of chicory and handing one over without asking. "That thing where you stare at the river like it owes you money."

"It does."

"Everything owes you money. That's your tragedy." Odile leaned on the rail beside her, small and wiry, her gray-streaked braid tied back with tarred twine. Her eyes went where Mara's had gone — downriver, toward the low gold line of the eastern hills, toward Vessel's Rest, four hundred miles of narrowing water upstream. "How bad?"

"The dry season's early. Two weeks early, maybe three." Mara drank. The chicory was bitter and hot and honest, which was more than she could say for anything else in her life just now. "The channels above the Split will start closing inside ten days. After that, nothing draws more than four feet gets through until the rains come back."

"And we draw how much, loaded?"

"Four and a half."

Odile said nothing for a while. The Kestrel breathed beneath them, her patched seam holding — Tomas's work, resin and canvas and a patience Mara still hadn't decided whether to trust — but breathing all the same, that faint flex of planking around the repair that spoke of a wound closed but not healed. Like everything else on this boat.

"So we run it now," Odile said finally. "Light load, high water enough, we make Vessel's Rest before the bars close. We've done tighter runs than that."

"We've done tighter runs with a sound hull and no one hunting us."

"No one's hunting us."

Mara looked at her. Odile held the look for exactly two seconds, then became very interested in her coffee.

"Greyharbor," Mara said. "The man who bumped me at the chandlery. The skiff that paced us for an hour below the Split and then turned off like it had gotten what it came for. You were there. You saw both."

"I saw a lot of things. Doesn't mean they add up to hunting."

"They add up to *measuring*, Odile. Somebody's measuring my draft and counting my crew." She set her cup on the rail. Below them, in the gray light, the river slid past, lower every hour, exposing ribs of red clay along the banks like something being slowly flayed. "Sarn knows I have the chest. He's known since Greyharbor. He isn't sending cutters yet because he doesn't have to. He's letting the river do his work for him."

* * *

They took on no cargo at Ferrow Bend. It cost her money she didn't have — the ferry contract north would have paid for their coal and then some — but the Kestrel rode light, riding high, her strakes showing a hand's width of extra freeboard, and Mara stood at the rail watching the loading dock fall away and felt the old familiar arithmetic begin to grind in her chest.

Every mile upstream was a mile the water gave up. The Sable in dry season was a miser counting out its channels coin by coin, and by the time they reached the Split it would have counted out almost nothing at all.

Tomas found her at midday in the engine bay, up to his elbows in the bilge pump, which had developed a cough. He worked quietly, the way he did everything — a big, deliberate man with salvage-scarred hands and a face that gave away nothing he didn't choose to give away. Seven weeks ago he'd been a stranger on a levee. Now he was elbow-deep in her boat uninvited, and the strangest part was how little that bothered her anymore.

"Pump's fine," he said. "Impeller's worn. I can dress it tonight, buy you another season."

"We may not have a season."

"I heard you and Odile on the roof deck this morning." He didn't look up. "Sound carries on a light boat."

"Then you know the problem."

"I know the problem." He sat back on his heels, wiping his hands on a rag that had been white during the previous administration. "You want me to tell you it's impossible so you can stop wanting it. I've watched you do that twice now. Once about the chest, once about Vessel's Rest."

Mara leaned against the bulkhead and crossed her arms. "And what would you know about what I want?"

"Nothing. That's the point." He looked up at her then, and his eyes were steady and tired and older than his face. "I only know what the chest is worth, and what it costs to carry it, and that those numbers change every day the water drops. In Greyharbor it was worth danger. At the Split it'll be worth blood. Above the Split, if the channels close with us on the wrong side of them—" He shrugged. "Then it's worth everything. Yours, Odile's, mine, and the boat's."

"You could put off at Ferrow Bend," she said. "Take the coach road north. Be in Vessel's Rest in nine days, dry-shod, with your testimony intact and your skin attached. Nobody would think less of you."

It was a test, and she hated herself a little for making it, and hated him a little more for failing it so completely — because he just shook his head and went back to the impeller.

"The road has checkpoints," he said. "The river has bars. I'd rather drown honestly than be searched politely." A pause, and then, quieter: "Besides. I owe the dead a trip upstream. You're just the one carrying the fare."

She left him to his work. There was nothing to say to that that wouldn't have opened something she couldn't close again, not with the clock running.

* * *

That night they anchored above the Split, in the wide slow pool below the ruins of the tollhouse, and Mara climbed to the roof deck alone and let the dark come down.

The stars over the Sable were merciless — no haze now, no cloud, the dry season stripping the sky bare the way it stripped the banks, everything exposed, nothing hidden. She lay back against the wheelhouse roof and found the Wanderer's Chain, the constellation her father had taught her, the one river pilots steered by when the fog ate the shorelines. *Follow the broken links home,* he used to say. She'd followed them for seven years and they had never once led anywhere but onward.

Below her, through the deck boards, she could hear the sounds of the boat settling into the night: Odile's boots crossing the cargo deck on her rounds, the tick of the cooling engine, the creak of the patched seam flexing with the current — the Kestrel talking in her sleep, dreaming of deeper water.

Ten days. Maybe fewer. The channels above the Split braided through forty miles of shoal country, and in low water the safe passages swung daily, sometimes hourly, as bars built and collapsed. A pilot who knew the water could thread them. A pilot who knew the water and was running light and desperate might. But the Kestrel's hull was compromised, her pump was elderly, and somewhere behind them a man named Corvel Sarn was spending his respectable fortune to make sure that either the river or the law got her before the tribunal did.

And ahead — ahead was Vessel's Rest, and the tribunal, and the names in the chest. *Halloran, M., pilot.* Her own name on a manifest seven years old, typed by some clerk in a ministry that no longer existed, filed under a case that had never been closed because closing it would have required admitting what had been done.

She could still sink it. The thought arrived the way it always did, patient and reasonable, wearing the voice of survival. Drop the dispatches over the side in the deep pool below the tollhouse, weighted, gone. Sarn's price on her head evaporates. The warrant stays — but warrants she'd lived with. Warrants were weather. She could outrun weather for another seven years. Another seventeen. The river was wide and her face, despite everything, was only worth remembering in certain ports.

But the names.

She thought of them the way she always did, in order, the roll call she never spoke aloud: Dossen at the wheel. The Ferrow twins in the engine room. Little Anselm, sixteen years old, who'd signed on to see the capital and died in the shallows of the Meridian channel with a hunter's bullet in him. Eleven names, and every one of them sold for money by a man who now testified at tribunals in a good coat.

If she sank the chest, the names stayed dead in the way that mattered — unavenged, unrecorded, officially nothing. If she carried it upstream, she might lose everything else: the boat, Odile, her own neck, seven years of careful motion. Survival and justice, competing for the same narrow water, and only enough draft for one.

The river whispered against the hull. Lower again. She could hear it dropping even now, the tone of it changed, the pool draining toward morning.

* * *

At first light she called them together on the cargo deck — Odile, Tomas, and old Piet from the engine room, who'd been with her five years and asked no questions, which was why she kept him. The mist was thin this morning, burning off fast, and the exposed mudbanks gleamed like bronze.

"I'm going to say this once," Mara said, "and then we either commit or we don't. The window above the Split closes in eight days, maybe less. We make the run light, we make it fast, and we don't stop except to wood and water. If the hull lets go in the shoals, we beach her, walk out, and take the chest overland. Nobody dies for the boat. Agreed?"

Piet nodded immediately. He'd have nodded if she'd proposed sailing to the moon.

Tomas nodded slowly. "Overland with the chest is four days' walk through hill country," he said. "Sarn's men travel roads, not hills. It's the fallback. Good."

Which left Odile, arms folded, jaw set, looking at Mara with an expression she'd seen maybe three times in eleven years — the expression Odile wore when she was about to be loyal in the hardest possible direction.

"Mara." Her voice was low. "I've been thinking about what happens if we don't make it. Not the dying. The other thing. What happens to the chest, to the proof, if we go down in the shoals or get taken at a checkpoint. All of it goes to the bottom or into Sarn's pocket, and the names go with it, and nobody ever—"

"That's why we don't get taken."

"But if we do." Odile stepped closer. "There's a copy. That's all I'm saying. Before the Split, we copy every page — Tomas reads archivist script, I've got a steady hand, and there's oilcloth in the stores. One set goes in the chest. The other goes somewhere that isn't this boat. So that whatever happens between here and Vessel's Rest, the truth survives the trip."

Mara looked at her, and then at Tomas, and understood that they had talked about this already, the two of them, somewhere in the night watches, deciding how to bring her along to her own rescue. She should have been angry. She found, instead, that she was tired in a way that anger couldn't reach, and underneath the tiredness, something that felt almost like gratitude.

"Copy everything," she said. "Twice. One copy goes ashore at the last anchorage before the shoals, buried, marked on my chart and nowhere else." She looked at the water sliding past the hull, low and clear and shrinking, already too thin for the boat it used to be able to carry. "If the river wants the truth, it can dig for it."

Odile grinned — quick, fierce, gone in a second. "Aye, Captain."

They broke to their stations. The anchor came up dripping red mud. And Mara took the wheel and turned the Kestrel's nose upstream, into the narrowing water, toward the Split and whatever waited on the far side of it.

They had made perhaps six miles, the banks sliding by golden in the early sun, when Odile hailed from the bow. "Smoke! Dead ahead, on the point — steam launch, anchored in the channel mouth."

Mara raised the glass. The launch flew no colors. It sat squarely athwart the main channel above the Split — the channel, the only one drawing over three feet — and on its deck, small in the glass but unmistakable, men moved with the unhurried confidence of people who were not lost and not passing through.

Waiting. For a boat that drew four and a half feet, with nowhere else to go.

Mara lowered the glass slowly. Behind her she heard Tomas come up onto the roof deck, and felt rather than saw him follow her gaze to the point, and heard the breath go out of him.

"Well," he said quietly. "He's stopped letting the river do his work."

Mara spun the wheel, and the Kestrel began to turn toward the western bank, toward water too shallow for anyone to follow — toward the kind of running that, once started, had only one ending.
