## 10. Sarn's Bargain

The man came aboard at dusk, when Greyharbor's lamps were just catching and the river had gone the color of old pewter.

Mara saw him first from the wheelhouse — a figure standing on the levee with the stillness of someone who had been waiting a long time and did not mind waiting longer. He wore a grey coat cut in the Vessian style, long-skirted and buttoned to the throat, though the fashion had died with the Republic seven years ago. Wearing it now was either a joke or a message. She suspected the latter.

He didn't wave. He didn't call out. He simply stood there while the lamplighter made his rounds behind him, and when Mara finally came down the gangplank he inclined his head as if she had done him an honor.

"Captain Halloran."

"Not that name," she said.

"Forgive me." He smiled without warmth. "Old habits. The Republic trained us all to use full names — it was considered efficient." He produced a card from his breast pocket, white stock, unmarked except for a small embossed anchor at the corner. No seal. No name. "You may call me Master Quill. Most people do."

"That's not a name either."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "It isn't."

* * *

She should have sent him off the levee. Every instinct she'd sharpened over seven years told her to do exactly that — turn, walk back up the plank, order Odile to cast off, and be three miles downstream before this Quill finished adjusting his cuffs. But the Kestrel was tied fast for the night, her patched seam weeping a slow brown thread into the harbor water, and low water was coming, and the tribunal seat at Vessel's Rest sat four days upstream through channels that would soon be ribs of mud. Running wasn't simple anymore. Nothing had been simple since the chest came up out of the flood.

So she let him talk, and hated herself a little for it.

"You've had a difficult season," Quill said, strolling along the levee beside her as though they were old friends taking the evening air. Behind them the trading post clattered with the last of the day's business; ahead, the river ran wide and slow toward the sea it never quite reached. "The hull trouble. The debris in the narrows. The cutter that's been asking after you at every lock between here and the falls."

"I don't discuss my boat with strangers."

"Of course not. Discretion is a captain's first virtue." He paused at the rail overlooking the water and looked down at the dark surface with an expression of genuine appreciation. "The Sable is remarkable, isn't it? It takes everything. Floods take the fields, the roads, the dead who aren't buried deep enough. And then the dry season comes and gives it all back — rearranged. A man's whole life can surface forty miles from where he lost it." He glanced at her sideways. "Or a chest can."

Mara kept her face still. Somewhere behind her ribs her heart had begun to knock, slow and heavy, like a fist on a door.

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"No," Quill said. "You wouldn't."

* * *

He named a number that night that would have bought the Kestrel twice over, and then he named a second thing that wasn't money at all.

They had ended up in the stern of the Kestrel, because Mara refused to conduct whatever this was inside her own cabin, and because the open deck gave her the sightlines she wanted — the gangplank, the levee, the shadowed mouth of the alley where Odile had gone to buy lamp oil and was taking far too long about it. Quill sat across from her on an overturned crate like a man visiting a friend's parlor, hands folded, grey coat immaculate despite the river damp.

"The pardon," he said, "is real. I want to be clear about that, Captain, because I know you've heard promises before. This isn't a promise. It's paper — drawn, sealed, and waiting in a strongbox in Vessel's Rest. Full remission of the warrant issued against one Mara Halloran, formerly of the Vessian river service, for crimes arising from the events of the autumn of the collapse. Signed by two sitting magistrates of the tribunal." He let that sit in the air between them. "You could dock at the old capital tomorrow. You could walk the Marble Hall. You could sleep a whole night in one place without calculating the tide."

The words went into her like cold water into a crack. Seven years. Seven years of skirting ports, of anchoring in the reeds outside towns whose harbors remembered her face, of teaching Odile to do the market runs because Mara Halloran's name on a manifest drew eyes. A whole night in one place. She hadn't dared imagine it in so long that imagining it now felt like a muscle cramping from disuse.

"And the boat," Quill continued, watching her the way a fisherman watches a float. "A new hull. River-built, this year, out of the Corvel Sarn yards at Millbrook. Sixty feet, shallow draft, coppered seams. Named whatever you like. Crewed however you like. Yours outright, papers clean, no liens, no history." He smiled again, and this time there was something almost kind in it, which was worse. "I understand your current vessel is dying. I'm told the seam below the cargo hold has been patched twice this season. I'm told she groans in her sleep."

"She doesn't sleep," Mara said. "And she's none of your business."

"Everything on this river is Mr. Sarn's business, Captain. That's rather the point of Mr. Sarn."

* * *

There it was. The name, laid down between them like a card turned face-up.

Corvel Sarn. She'd heard the name whispered at three landings this season alone — the respectable trader, the man with warehouses in four towns and a seat reserved for him at every table that mattered, the survivor of the collapse who had come out of the drowning with his ledgers dry and his fortunes doubled. She'd heard the name and felt nothing but the ordinary contempt one feels for any man who profits from a catastrophe. She had not known. Until Tomas had spread those dispatches across her cabin table two nights ago, his archivist's fingers trembling slightly as he traced the countersignatures, she had not known that the same voice that bought her crew's route from a traitor and fed it to the hunters — the same hand that signed the orders that put the Meridian on the rocks and nine good people in the river — now signed bills of lading and charity subscriptions and letters to magistrates.

Now she knew. And here was Sarn's man, sitting on her deck, offering to make her whole.

"And what does Mr. Sarn want," she said carefully, "in exchange for his generosity?"

Quill reached into his coat and produced, of all things, a small lacquered box, which he set on the crate between them and opened. Inside, nested in green felt, lay a single wax impression — a counter-seal, crisp and red, bearing the anchor-and-key of the Vessian archive office.

"A formality," he said. "Mr. Sarn is prepared to accept that you came by the chest honestly — salvage, flood-borne, no fault of yours. The tribunal will hear no charges regarding its possession. In return: the chest itself, contents intact, delivered to Mr. Sarn's agent at Vessel's Rest. And—" he lifted one finger, delicate as a surgeon, "—a statement. Written, witnessed, in your own hand. That the documents were removed from the archive before the collapse by an employee of that office. One Tomas Vey. That he sold them to you, or planted them on you, or however you prefer to phrase it — the wording is flexible. The substance is not. Mr. Sarn requires that the provenance of these papers point to the archivist."

The river slapped against the hull. Somewhere up the levee a dog started barking and thought better of it.

"He's the leak," Mara said. "That's the story. Your master's story. The archivist stole the dispatches, the archivist is the source, the archivist goes to the tribunal in chains, and the respectable trader keeps his name clean."

"Mr. Sarn's name needs no keeping clean," Quill said mildly. "But archives are full of disgruntled men, Captain. Men who held grudges, who took souvenirs, who spent years looking for a way to make their grievances profitable. If such a man surfaced with stolen papers and a tale to tell—" he shrugged elegantly, "—well. Who would believe him? A drowned clerk's word against a sealed statement from a pardoned captain? You see how tidy it is. Everyone is satisfied. You get your life back. Mr. Sarn gets his privacy. The archivist gets what he earned."

"What he earned," Mara repeated.

"Silence has a price, Captain. He paid it for seven years. Now the bill comes due. That's not cruelty. That's arithmetic."

* * *

Arithmetic. She turned the word over after Quill left, turning it the way she'd turn a stone found in a net, looking at what lived underneath.

Because here was the devil's ledger, and she could read figures well enough. On one side: a pardon with two magistrates' seals. A new boat, coppered and clean, no history. The end of running. The end of flinching at her own name in port towns. Seven years of exile, bought back in a single signature. Everything she wanted. Everything she had told herself, in the small hours at anchor, she would trade almost anything for.

On the other side: a quiet man with ink-stained fingers who had patched her hull in the rain without being asked, who had stood in her cabin over those dispatches and gone grey as ash when he understood what they proved, who had confessed — confessed, unprompted, voice cracking like a boy's — that he had processed the original orders, that his hands had moved the paper that killed her crew, that he had stayed silent ever since and could no longer bear it. A man carrying a debt so old and so heavy that he'd waded into a knife fight to pay an installment of it.

If she signed, the tribunal would hang that debt around his neck and drop him through the trapdoor of it. And Sarn — Sarn would go on buying loyalty and selling men, and the names of her dead would stay buried under his respectability forever, and she would sail a new boat on a clean river with a clean name and know, every single morning for the rest of her life, exactly what the cleanliness had cost and whose neck it had been measured against.

Almost anything, she had told herself. Almost.

The trouble with arithmetic, she thought, staring at the lamp flame until her eyes watered, was that it always assumed everything could be counted. It couldn't. Some things had no number. Nine names on a wrecked boat's roster. The sound of Tomas saying *I knew, and I said nothing.* Odile's face in the lamplight, older than her years, asking nothing. The Kestrel herself, groaning at her moorings like an old woman in a drafty house, dying by inches and refusing to admit it.

What was a pardon worth if it purchased silence? What was a new boat worth if the old debt floated free?

* * *

She didn't sleep. Toward the middle of the night she climbed down into the hold with a lantern and knelt by the chest where it sat wrapped in oilcloth behind the spare line, and she put her palm flat on the wet-cold wood, and she thought about the names inside it. Her name among them. *M. Halloran, pilot, route confirmed.* Typed in a dead nation's neat bureaucratic hand, as if she had been a cargo item, a line in a manifest, something to be scheduled and disposed of.

Sarn had scheduled her once already. He had spent her crew like coin and moved on to bigger ledgers, and now that the river had coughed the receipts back up, he wanted to buy the receipts. Not destroy them — she'd understood that much from Quill's careful phrasing. Buy them. Because destroyed papers raised questions, but owned papers raised none. He would lock her crew's death warrant in his own strongbox and keep it as insurance, the way he seemed to keep everything.

And he'd offered her a fair price. That was the obscene part. The pardon was real — she believed that. The boat was real. He wasn't cheating her. He was simply pricing the only thing she had left that couldn't be priced, and assuming, as men like Sarn always assumed, that everyone had a figure.

Footsteps on the deck above. Soft ones — she knew Odile's walk the way she knew the Kestrel's engine note, and these weren't quite right. Too careful. Too deliberate.

Mara closed the hold hatch quietly and climbed up into the dark.

Odile stood at the rail, lantern unlit, looking out at the black water. She didn't startle when Mara came up beside her. She'd known she was coming; that was plain. There was something set in the girl's shoulders tonight, something braced, the way a person stands when they've decided to say a thing they've been swallowing for years.

"You met him," Odile said. Not a question. "The grey coat. I saw him leave."

"You were supposed to be buying lamp oil."

"I bought lamp oil." A pause. The river breathed beneath them. "Mara. Whatever he offered you — and I can guess, because men like that only ever offer the same two things, your past and your future — you need to hear something first. Before you decide anything."

Mara turned to look at her. In seven years Odile had never once used that tone. It was the tone of someone stepping off a ledge.

"There are things I've done," Odile said, "to keep this boat safe. Things you don't know about. And if you're going to sell a man's neck to buy your name back, then you deserve to know exactly what kind of company you're keeping when you do it."

The lamp oil sat forgotten in its sack at her feet, and the river ran dark toward the sea, and Mara Halloran felt the ground shift under her the way it always did when the truth was coming — slow at first, then all at once, like a sandbar giving way.

"Tell me," she said.
