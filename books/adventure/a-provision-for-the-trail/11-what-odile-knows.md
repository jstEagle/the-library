## 11. What Odile Knows

Greyharbor smelled of tar and rotting fruit and the particular sweetness of a town that had learned to survive by not asking questions. Odile Fenn stood at the Kestrel's rail with a coil of line in her hands, pretending to work it, watching the harbor-masters' office across the quay the way she had watched it for seven years — casually, in glances, the way you watch a dog you've fed and don't entirely trust.

The office was low and stone, its roof green with moss, its windows fogged with pipe smoke even at this hour. Inside it sat Master Corvel Sarn's man — not Sarn himself, never himself; Sarn was upstream now, respectable, marble-halled — but a factor named Brull who kept the ledgers and took the reports and paid out small coins for small kindnesses. Odile knew his window. She knew the hour he took his tea. She knew that if she walked in before the third bell and asked after cargo rates, he would ask after her captain, and she would answer, and then she would hate herself for an hour or a day, depending on how much she'd drunk the night before.

Seven years. Seven years of walking into that office.

She worked the line through her palms until the rope burned, and still the knowing didn't leave her. It sat in her chest like ballast.

Behind her, Mara was arguing with a chandler about the price of pine pitch, her voice carrying that flat, river-worn authority that made men lower their prices just to end the conversation. The chandler's boy stacked pitch barrels on the dock with the exaggerated care of someone being watched by a woman who could hear a lie through a hull. Mara Halloran could, too. That was the trouble. That had always been the trouble.

Odile looked at her captain — the set of her shoulders against the grey morning, the way her hand rested on the rail as if the boat were an extension of her own arm — and felt the weight shift from ballast to anchor, dragging her down toward the one conversation she had spent seven years avoiding.

Because Sarn's agent had come to her last night. Not Brull. Someone new, soft-spoken, with clean boots that had no business on Greyharbor's mud. He had known things. He had known the Kestrel's draft, her last three cargoes, the name Odile's mother had given her before Odile had ever set foot on water. And he had made an offer that wasn't an offer: the chest, or the captain, or Odile's own name added to a list she did not want to be on.

She had told him nothing. But her hands had shaken all night, and shaking hands tell their own stories, and a man like that reads hands the way Mara reads water.

* * *

Mara sent her below to check the bilge — routine, or meant to look routine, though Odile had noticed the way her captain's eyes went to the seam line twice in an hour, counting drips. The pump ran longer than it should have. The Kestrel was talking to them in the only language she had left, and what she was saying was: *I am tired, and I am taking on more than I can hold.*

Odile sat on the engine housing in the dim, smelling of bilgewater and lamp oil, and listened to the boat breathe. Above her, footsteps crossed the deck. Tomas Vey was aboard again — Mara had let him stay, which was its own kind of weather change, and Odile hadn't decided yet whether it was a storm coming or a storm passing.

The hatch opened. Light fell down the ladder in a dusty column, and Mara came down it sideways, ducking under the low beams, and stood there looking at her.

"You've been to the harbor-masters' office," Mara said.

Not a question. Odile's stomach dropped through the hull, through the mud, through whatever lay beneath rivers.

"Twice since we tied up," Mara went on. Her voice was level, which was worse than shouting. "Brull's boy saw you. So did the fishwife who hates me for reasons neither of us has ever established. You want to tell me what cargo rates look like these days, Odie? Because we both know you can't read a rate card without me."

The lamp swung gently with the boat's motion. Shadows moved across Mara's face like clouds crossing water.

"I can explain," Odile said.

"Then do."

* * *

It is strange, Odile thought later, what the body does when the truth finally comes up for air. Hers wanted to run. Seven years of habit said *run*, said *lie*, said *laugh it off and pour another*. Instead she stayed sitting on the cold iron of the engine housing, and she heard herself say:

"I've been reporting your movements to Greyharbor for six years. Eleven months. Since the winter the Meridian wreck was stripped."

The words came out flat and terrible, like items read off a manifest.

Mara didn't move. Somewhere above them, Tomas's footsteps stopped — he'd heard, of course he'd heard, sound carried through this old boat like gossip through a small town — and then, deliberately, resumed, moving aft. Giving them the illusion of privacy. Odile would thank him for that later, if there was a later in which thanks were possible.

"Say it again," Mara said quietly. "All of it. Because what I think you just said cannot be what you said."

"It is." Odile's voice cracked and she hated it and kept going anyway. "Every port we've made since Greyharbor became our home water, I've gone to the harbor-master — first old Mistress Cade, then Brull when he bought the ledger — and I've told them where the Kestrel would be. Where we'd been. What we carried, sometimes. Not everything. Never the smuggling runs, never the names of people who helped us. Just enough."

"Just enough," Mara repeated. "Enough for what?"

"For them to keep the bounty hunters off us." The truth, once started, poured like the river through a broken levee. "You think we've been lucky, Mara? Seven years with a warrant on your head and nobody's ever boarded us? Nobody's ever searched the hold? You think the river loves you that much?" She laughed, and it came out wrong, wet and ugly. "The river doesn't love anybody. I bought you safe passage. Every season. With lies."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight and grain, like silt settling.

Mara lowered herself onto the crate across from her, slowly, the way she moved when she was keeping her hands busy so they wouldn't do something else. In the lamplight Odile could see every year the river had carved into her face, and none of it was surprise. That was the worst part. Some part of her captain had always known.

"How long have you known?" Odile whispered.

"That something was buying us quiet? Four years." Mara's jaw worked. "I told myself it was Cade's sentimentality. An old woman who liked my mother's cooking. Then Cade died and Brull took over and the quiet kept coming, and I told myself it was Sarn's indifference — that a warrant seven years stale wasn't worth paper." She looked up, and her eyes were the color of deep water over rocks. "I didn't let myself know, Odile. There's a difference between not knowing and refusing to, and I chose the second one, because the alternative was asking myself who on this boat had access to enough of my plans to sell them, and I couldn't afford the answer. So I didn't ask. We're a pair, you and I. Two cowards holding the same rope."

"Don't—" Odile stood, and the engine housing clanged behind her. "Don't make us the same. I did it to protect you."

"You did it to control me." Mara said it without heat, which made it land harder than any blow. "You decided what I couldn't survive knowing. Same as you hid the leak reports last spring. Same as you told me the Kestrel's seams were fine in high water when you'd already measured the give yourself. You love this boat and you love me, and you've spent seven years managing us both like cargo — keep it steady, keep it dark, don't let the captain see the water rising."

"And I was right!" Odile's voice broke loose at last, all the hoarded years behind it. "You want to know what happens when you find out where we're going, Mara? You turn the boat around. You point her at Vessia like a knife and you get us all killed for a name on a chest. I have watched you steer straight at storms because turning away felt like losing. Somebody on this boat has to lie to you, or you'll drown us all in the truth!"

The words hung in the bilge-dark, echoing off iron.

Mara was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone somewhere Odile rarely heard it — down beneath command, down to the bedrock.

"The crew of the Meridian died because somebody managed information," she said. "Somebody decided what the others could bear to know. Somebody loved them, maybe. I've wondered about it for seven years — whether the person who sold our route hated us, or thought they were saving some piece of us, keeping some of us out of the hunters' path by feeding them the rest." Her hands opened, empty. "I used to hope it was the second. Now I'm sitting in my own bilge listening to my oldest friend tell me she fed the harbor-masters my life to keep me safe, and I understand exactly how it starts. It starts with love. It starts with *you'll thank me*. And it ends with a burned boat and eleven names nobody says anymore."

Odile's eyes stung. "I never sold anything to hunters. Never. Ask Brull, ask his ledgers—"

"Oh, I believe you." Mara rose. "That's what makes it bearable. You're no traitor, Odie. You're something more common and more dangerous — you're loyal in the wrong direction. You protected the boat instead of the crew. You protected *me* instead of *us*." She climbed two rungs of the ladder, then stopped, her back to Odile, her hand white-knuckled on the rail. "Sarn's man came to see you. Last night. The one with the clean boots."

Odile went cold. "You knew that too?"

"Tomas saw him leave the levee. Clean boots in Greyharbor are worth noticing." Mara turned her head, just enough. "What did he offer?"

"Everything," Odile said miserably. "A pardon. A new boat. Or else my name on a list. He gave me until the tide turns tomorrow."

"And you were going to carry that alone too." It wasn't a question. Mara climbed out into the grey light, and her voice drifted back down the hatch, tired and clear as a bell over water. "Tide turns at noon tomorrow. That gives us one night to decide what kind of liars we're going to be. Come topside when you're done hating yourself. Bring the logbook — the real one, the one you think I don't know you keep."

* * *

Odile sat alone in the bilge while the Kestrel rocked her, patient as a mother, and let herself feel the whole shape of it at last: six years of small treacheries, each one reasonable, each one a single plank laid across open water, until she looked down and found she'd built a road to a place she never meant to go.

She had been nineteen when she started. The Meridian was two years sunk, the warrant fresh, the bounty on Mara's head large enough to tempt honest men. They'd come into Greyharbor half-starved, and Mistress Cade had summoned Odile alone — not Mara, never Mara — and shown her a list of bounty boats working the lower Sable, and said, *Your captain will run if she knows. She'll run until the river kills her. Give me her ports, girl, and I'll keep the wolves leashed.* And Odile, twenty years old and terrified of losing the only family the flood had left her, had said yes.

Cade had probably meant it kindly. That was the poison of it. Every link in the chain had been forged by someone meaning well — Cade protecting a reckless captain, Brull protecting his ledger, Odile protecting her people, each of them lying a little, loving a lot, until loyalty and betrayal were the same rope with different hands on it.

And now Sarn held the other end.

She climbed the ladder into the grey afternoon. Mara stood at the wheel housing with the chest open at her feet — the Vessian dispatches spread out on an oilcloth, Tomas bent over them, his salvager's hands uncharacteristically gentle with the brittle paper. Neither of them looked up right away, and in that moment Odile saw them clearly: two people standing over the past, deciding what to do with it, and herself on the outside of the circle she had spent seven years guarding.

She fetched the real logbook from beneath her bunk — the one with the true courses, the private marks, six years of small betrayals recorded in her own neat hand because she'd never been able to stop documenting, as if writing it down might someday count as confessing.

When she handed it to Mara, her captain weighed it in her palm like something that might explode.

"Noon tomorrow," Odile said. "That's when my time runs out. And Mara — the man with the clean boots, he wasn't working alone. I counted three sets of tracks leaving the levee, going downstream toward the cut. Somebody's boat is waiting below Greyharbor."

Mara looked at the water, then at the falling gauge tied to the pilings, where the river had dropped another hand's width since morning. Dry season coming early. Narrow channels. A cutter somewhere south of them, and a tide turning at noon.

"Then we don't wait for noon," Mara said.
