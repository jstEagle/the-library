## 12. Night Run

Tomas had learned to read water in the dark the way other men learned scripture — slowly, by failure, with a great deal of kneeling.

The archivists of Vessia had taught him that every document had a grain, a direction in which its fibers ran, and that a careful hand could feel a forgery through paper alone. The river taught him the same lesson at higher volume. Water over a shoal sang differently than water over deep channel: a hiss instead of a hush, a broken syllable where there should have been a long vowel. On a moonless night like this one, with cloud so low it seemed to drag its belly on the treetops, that song was all he had. There was no light to steer by. There was only sound, and the smell of wet clay coming off the banks, and the Kestrel's hull talking underneath his boots in a language of groans and creaks that he was beginning, against all sense, to love.

"Sound's changing," Odile said from the bow. She stood with her weight forward, one hand on the jackstaff, her head cocked like a wading bird's. "Hear it? That flat spot off the starboard side. We're drifting toward the bar."

"I hear it."

"You don't hear it or you're agreeing with me because I'm scary?"

"Both," Tomas said, and put the wheel over two spokes.

Behind him, in the pilothouse doorway, Mara said nothing. She hadn't said much since Greyharbor. She stood with her arms folded, watching the black water as if she could bully it into showing itself, and when she did speak it was only numbers — depths, headings, distances — clipped and exact, a woman rationing out her own voice like hardtack. But she had given him the wheel tonight. That was its own kind of sentence, and he was still deciding whether it was a sentence of trust or of sentencing.

The cutter had come upriver an hour after sundown.

They'd seen its lantern first — a single white eye swinging at the far bend, too steady to be a fisherman, too fast for cargo. Sarn's boat. Odile had named it without being told: *the Vesper*, Greyharbor registry, shallow draft, twin sweeps, a crew that got paid by results rather than hours. It would have the shoal stretch charted. What it wouldn't have was a pilot who knew these waters by ear, or a captain willing to run them blind on a moonless night, which was precisely why Mara had killed their own running lights, turned the Kestrel's bow toward the worst four miles of river between here and the oxbow, and said, in a voice that allowed no discussion: "He'll expect us to anchor and hide. So we don't."

Now the river narrowed. The banks came close enough that Tomas could smell the willows, green and rank, and the current picked up its pace beneath the hull like something eager to be done with them.

* * *

The first hour was almost gentle.

Almost. The word deserved its qualifiers. The Kestrel rolled through the dark with the ponderous patience of a beast that knows it is old, and Tomas steered by the water's voice, calling depth to Odile in the bow while she answered with hand signals he could barely see — a raised fist for deep, a flat palm for shallow, a chopping motion for *stop, stop, stop*. They had invented half the vocabulary that afternoon, standing in the derelict lock where they'd patched her hull, drawing shapes in the air like children playing at soldiers. It should not have worked. Three people who had known each other less than a season, two of them carrying secrets heavy enough to sink a smaller boat, improvising a language in the dark — it should not have worked, and yet it did, and Tomas found something in that which frightened him more than the shoals did.

Because it felt like a crew. And he knew what happened to crews on this river when someone sold them.

"Hold your line," Mara said behind him. Not a rebuke. A reading. "You're chasing the deep water. Don't. The deep water bends east here and the bar reaches out to meet it. Stay ugly and straight."

"Ugly and straight," Tomas repeated.

"That's how she likes it." He heard rather than saw her smile. "Boats are like people that way. The ones that look graceful are usually lying about their draft."

Odile snorted from the bow. Somewhere below, the patched seam along the Kestrel's port side gave a long complaint — the same seam that had split in the debris-choked water above the lock, the same seam Tomas had spent a day and a half caulking with oakum and pitch and prayers to no particular god. It held. It complained the way an old man complains getting out of a chair: loudly, and without any intention of actually refusing.

Then the river stopped being gentle.

It happened the way weather happens — no announcement, just a change in the quality of the dark ahead. The willow banks fell away and the water spread wide and shallow across a drowned field, and the current braided itself into a dozen threads, each one hunting for the deepest path, none of them committed. The sound changed too. Where before there had been one continuous hush, now there were voices — hisses and gurgles and the soft wet slap of water finding sand — overlapping, contradicting, arguing.

"The braid," Mara said quietly. "This is the part I hate."

"You've run it before?"

"Twice. In daylight. In high water." A pause. "Never blind. Never this low."

Tomas eased the throttle back until the engine was barely breathing. Ahead, the white lantern of the Vesper had vanished behind the bend, but he could feel it back there the way you feel a hand hovering near your shoulder — Sarn's crew poling carefully through the easy channel, taking their time, certain that nothing afloat would be fool enough to cross the braid at night.

Which meant speed was their only cover, and slowness their only survival, and both of those things lived in the same hull.

"Talk to me," Mara said. "Both of you. Everything you hear."

So they talked. For the next hour, the three of them narrated the river to each other in fragments — Odile calling the shape of the current by the way it bent the willow tips on the bank; Tomas reading the shallows by pitch, high hiss to port, deeper chuckle to starboard; Mara stitching it together into a course, correcting, adjusting, occasionally overriding them both with the flat authority of a woman who had buried friends in water exactly like this. And the strange thing, the thing Tomas would remember afterward whenever he thought about what loyalty actually was, was how quickly the three voices stopped sounding like three voices. They braided the way the current braided, separate threads making one strong rope.

"Fist!" Odile called suddenly, arm up. "Fist, dead ahead, thirty yards!"

Tomas threw the wheel hard over. The Kestrel leaned, groaned, swung — and slid past the bar with barely a boat-length to spare, close enough that he could hear the sand hissing under the hull like a whispered insult.

"Depth under the keel?" Mara asked.

"Enough," Tomas said.

"Enough isn't a number."

"It's the only number I've got."

Another pause, and then, dry as August: "Welcome to river work, archivist."

* * *

They lost the Vesper's lantern entirely in the second hour, and that was worse than seeing it.

A thing you can see has a position. A thing you can't see has intentions. Tomas kept glancing back over his shoulder at the empty black astern, imagining the cutter sliding silent down some channel he hadn't found, sweeps muffled, running lights dead, doing exactly what they were doing. The river offered a hundred ways to be ambushed and no way to know which one was occupied.

His hands ached on the wheel. He realized he'd been gripping it hard enough to whiten his knuckles, and made himself loosen, finger by finger, the way he'd once made himself stay calm in the archive cellars when the regime's inspectors came through — because panic, he had learned young, was a luxury the careful man paid for later at interest.

"You're thinking about the cellar," Mara said.

He startled. In the dark, in the engine noise, she shouldn't have been able to read him. "How—"

"Your shoulders go up around your ears. You did it at the trading post when the chest came up. You do it when you're somewhere you can't control." She moved closer, her shoulder nearly touching his, her eyes still on the water. "I'm not asking. Just noting it. For the record."

"For the record," Tomas echoed, and almost laughed. Of everything she might have said, she'd reached for the archivist's own phrase, and there was something in that — mockery, maybe, or mercy, or both braided together like the current outside. "You know what I keep thinking? Down in the cellars, we used to say the records were neutral. Paper doesn't take sides. Ink doesn't lie." He shook his head. "Seven years I believed that. Then I find out the ink I filed helped kill a crew, and I understand that paper takes whatever side the hand holding the pen is paid to take."

"And now?"

"Now I'm holding the pen." He nodded toward the cabin below, where the chest sat wrapped in oilcloth, full of dispatches bearing Corvel Sarn's bought-and-paid-for orders. "And so is he. And the tribunal gets to decide whose handwriting they believe."

"They'll believe yours," Mara said simply. "In the end. Because yours costs you something and his doesn't. Tribunals aren't stupid. They're just slow."

Ahead, Odile raised her hand — not a signal but a halt, palm out, fingers spread. "Listen," she breathed.

Tomas listened. Under the engine's mutter, under the river's many voices, there was something new: a rhythmic creak, far off to the west, wooden and patient. Oar-locks. Or sweeps. Something long and manned, moving through water that wanted to be quiet.

"West channel," Odile whispered. "They're in the west channel. That's the easy water."

Mara was quiet a moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed — lower, faster, alive in a way it hadn't been all night. "They took the west channel because their charts say the east braid is impassable at this stage. Which means right now, Captain Sarn's people think we're either anchored in the reeds or already gone to ground." Her teeth showed briefly in the dark. "We're going to give them the east braid. Full throttle. If we ground, we ground, and we fight it out on a sandbar like honest fools. But if we don't—"

"We come out below the braid with four miles on them," Tomas finished.

"And they get to spend their night wondering where we went." She clapped him once on the shoulder — a blow, really, affectionate the way a fall is affectionate — and said, "Give her everything she has, Vey. She'll give it back."

* * *

What followed, Tomas would never entirely be able to describe, and he was a man who described things for a living.

He opened the throttle and the Kestrel gathered herself like a decision. The old boat surged into the braided shallows with the black water peeling away from her bow in two long wings of froth that were somehow visible even in the dark, pale seams in the black cloth of the river, and the hull began to talk — not complain now but *sing*, a rising groan of timber and pitch, the patched seam shrieking its objections, the whole frame flexing like a living ribcage. Shoal water hissed beneath them. Once, twice, three times the keel kissed sand and lifted free again, each touch a sickening lurch that sent Tomas's stomach into his throat — and each time Mara was there, calling the correction before the scrape finished, her hand over his on the wheel, adding her strength to his, turning the boat by main force through water that had no business holding her.

Odile stood at the bow with spray flying off her arms, laughing. Actually laughing — a wild, unguarded sound Tomas had never heard from her, a girl's laugh escaping a woman who had survived too much to afford one. "She's flying!" Odile shouted into the wind. "Captain, she's *flying*!"

"She's *grounding* if you don't shut up and call me water!" Mara shouted back, and Odile laughed harder, and Tomas — white-knuckled, heart slamming, terror and joy fused into a single bright wire down his spine — found that he was grinning too, helplessly, the grin of a man falling who has suddenly discovered he enjoys the air.

The east braid spat them out.

One moment the world was chaos — hiss and groan and the lurching stutter of a keel skating over sand — and the next the river gathered itself back into one deep channel and the Kestrel settled into it like a coin into a pocket, riding smooth and deep and fast, the current taking her stern and hurrying her along. Behind them the braid stretched away invisible, a maze they had crossed in the dark that Sarn's charts said could not be crossed.

Tomas eased the throttle back to a civilized roar. His arms were shaking. He noticed it only now that he'd stopped needing them.

"Well," Mara said.

Nobody answered. There didn't seem to be anything to add. Odile climbed back over the rail and collapsed onto the deck planking, still breathless, and Tomas became aware that all three of them were looking at each other in the dark — not at the river, not at the chest, not at the past — at each other, and that something had quietly rearranged itself among them, the way cargo shifts in a hold and changes how a boat rides.

"Coffee," Mara said at last. "Somebody make coffee. We've got four miles of easy water and then the oxbow, and I intend to arrive at the Meridian awake enough to—" She stopped. The name of the wreck hung in the air between them, and Tomas watched her decide not to flinch from it. "Awake enough to do what needs doing there."

* * *

The oxbow opened before them an hour before dawn, a broad still lake of flooded forest where the old river had abandoned its loop and gone mad, drowning a mile of timber in water so calm it reflected the clouds perfectly — a second sky beneath the first, upside down and indistinguishable from it. The Kestrel slid off the current and into that mirror-world, and the engine noise fell away, and the silence was so complete that Tomas could hear the drip of water from the jackstaff, could hear Odile breathing, could hear his own pulse slow.

And there, black against the marginally blacker trees, lay the Meridian.

He felt Mara see it. He couldn't have said how — she made no sound, moved not at all — but the quality of her stillness changed, the way a document changes when you realize the seal on it is forged. Seven years of avoidance stood at the end of that flooded channel, half-sunk, ribs above waterline like the carcass of something enormous, and the woman beside him had spent seven years arranging her whole life so she would never have to look at it directly.

"Anchor's in the locker aft," she said. Her voice was level. Almost. "Drop it where you like. We're done running."

Tomas set the hook and killed the engine, and the Kestrel swung gently on her chain in the still water, and the three of them stood in the growing grey light and looked at the wreck of the boat where Mara's crew had died — the boat whose betrayal was documented in the chest below decks, in ink processed once, long ago, by a junior archivist named Tomas Vey who had read the orders and filed them and said nothing.

Odile broke the silence first, softly: "Captain. You want us to come with you? When you go out there?"

Mara looked at the Meridian for a long moment. When she turned, there was something in her face Tomas had never seen on it — not the armor, not the command, but underneath them, raw and new, like ground the flood had only just uncovered.

"No," she said. "This part I walk alone. Get the chest up on deck. Both of you. By the time I'm back, I want every page counted and ready — because tomorrow we start walking to Vessel's Rest, and I'm done letting dead men do my talking for me."

She went over the side into the tender, oars in hand, and rowed out across the mirrored water toward the wreck, growing smaller and smaller between the two skies, and Tomas stood at the rail and watched her go, and understood that whatever happened at the tribunal, whatever Sarn's money and Sarn's men could buy — the hardest crossing of this entire river had just begun, and it was four hundred feet of still water wide, and she was rowing it alone.
