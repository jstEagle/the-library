## 14. Grounded

The river told them first.

Odile felt it in the way the Kestrel answered her hand on the wheel — a sluggishness in the turns, a heaviness in the wake, as though the water itself had grown reluctant to carry them. By midmorning she could see it too: the current thinning, the brown water going glassy and shallow over bars that had been safe crossings three days ago. The reeds along the banks stood higher than they should, their roots exposed like old men's knuckles, and everywhere there was the smell of drying mud, that mineral sweetness that meant the river was letting go of its summer.

"Dry season's early," Mara said, standing at the rail with her arms crossed, watching the water the way other people watched enemies. "Two weeks early, maybe more."

"You're sure?"

"I'm always sure about the river." A pause. "It's everything else I get wrong."

They were two days out from Greyharbor, running light, with the chest's contents sewn into oilcloth bundles lashed beneath the pilothouse floor and Tomas's knife wound healing clean under Odile's careful bandaging. Four days to Vessel's Rest by water, if the water cooperated. It was not cooperating. All morning the soundings had been coming back shorter than the chart allowed, and Odile had taken to reading the surface for the telltale ripple-lines that marked a bar's shoulder — the faint seam where moving water dragged over sand, visible only when the light came low and flat across it.

By noon the light was flat and mean, and the seams were everywhere.

* * *

It happened an hour past midday, in a stretch of channel the charts called the Long Reach, where the Sable spread wide and braided through a dozen shifting channels. Mara took them down the easternmost cut because it showed the deepest color, and for a while it held them — ten feet, eight, six — and then the Kestrel shuddered once, gently, almost apologetically, and stopped.

Not a crash. Worse than a crash. A settling, the hull finding its resting place in the sand with a long grinding sigh that Odile felt in her teeth and her spine and somewhere behind her sternum, in the place where seven years of this boat lived.

"Everyone off the wheel," Mara said quietly, and her voice was so level it frightened Odile more than shouting would have. "Tomas, take the pole off the bow deck. We'll try to walk her off."

They tried. They walked her aft, walked her port, ran the engine hard ahead and hard astern until the exhaust smoke hung over the boat in a blue haze and the propeller churned nothing but mud and bubbles. Tomas poled until his shoulders shook. The Kestrel sat. The river dropped around them, visibly, measurably — Odile could mark it against the pilings of a drowned fish weir sticking up downstream, a hand's width gone in an hour, another in the next.

By late afternoon Mara stopped trying. She stood at the bow rail looking down at the water line exposed on the hull, a pale stripe of dried silt already forming above the surface, marking how far they'd fallen. She didn't say anything. That was the worst part. Odile had served under captains who cursed, who threw things, who wept; she would have known what to do with any of them. But Mara just stood there in the failing light with her hands folded behind her back like a woman at a funeral, watching her own life run out from under her.

"Captain," Odile said finally.

"Don't." Mara didn't turn. "Don't call me that right now."

"Then what do I call you?"

Mara was quiet a long moment. "I don't know," she said. "That's the problem, isn't it. I don't know who I am without her."

Odile looked at the boat — at the patched seam Tomas had caulked at the derelict lock, at the scarred rail, at the wheel she'd steered through eleven seasons of flood and shoal and one bad night on the Meridian's water. The Kestrel wasn't a boat. She'd understood that for years, but she'd never once said it aloud, because some truths you keep below the waterline or they sink you.

"She'll float again," Odile said.

"When?" Mara turned then, and her face in the last light was older than Odile had ever seen it. "The dry season's here, Odile. Early. Look at her — she's high and dry on a falling bar, and every day we sit here she settles deeper, and by the time the rains come back she'll be cracked like kindling unless someone works her seams all season. And we have four days' walk to Vessel's Rest and a tribunal that adjourns when the roads close, and—" She stopped herself. Breathed. When Mara breathed like that, deliberate and slow, it meant she was putting something away, locking it in a chest below decks. "We can't stay. You know we can't stay."

"I know."

"So say it."

Odile looked at the boat again. At the pale stripe rising on her hull. "You have to leave her," she said. "We have to leave her."

Mara nodded once, sharp, like breaking a bone to set it, and went below without another word.

* * *

They spent the evening making her ready to be left alone.

It was strange work, tender work, the kind of work you did for a sick person rather than a vessel. They pumped her bilges dry. They rigged awnings over her decks against the sun, which would warp bare wood faster than rain ever could. Tomas — moving stiffly, favoring his bandaged side — slushed the exposed planking above the new waterline with tallow and ash to keep the sun from checking it, working methodically along the hull from a ladder propped against the bow, and Odile hauled his materials and held the ladder and pretended not to notice when he paused, once, with his palm flat against the planking, the way you'd touch a sleeping person's shoulder.

Mara stayed below for most of it. When she came up at dusk she carried the ship's papers wrapped in oilcloth, the logbook, the small strongbox with their remaining coin, and the brass nameplate from the Meridian, which she had carried since the oxbow and which Odile had never once asked her about. She stowed these in the pack she'd be carrying, and then she stood in the middle of her own deck and looked around at it all — the coiled lines, the furled canvas, the lamp hanging in the pilothouse window that Odile lit every night at anchor, seven years, without fail.

"Leave the lamp," Mara said.

"It'll burn out by morning."

"Leave it anyway." Her voice was rough. "Boats shouldn't sit dark."

So Odile left it, and its small yellow glow followed them as they climbed down the ladder to the bar, and when Odile looked back from the mud the Kestrel sat above the waterline with her lamp burning like a house where someone was expected home, and Odile had to walk several steps before she trusted her voice again.

* * *

They made camp that night on the highest part of the bar, among the exposed roots of a dead cottonwood, because none of them could bear to sleep aboard and none of them could quite bring themselves to go beyond sight of her either. Tomas built a small fire. The river ran past them in the dark, thinner now, whispering over the shallows, and somewhere downstream an owl worked the exposed mudflats where the fish had nowhere left to hide.

Odile sat closest to the fire because she always did, tending it, and she watched Mara sit apart with her knees drawn up, staring at the boat, and she thought: *now*. If there was ever a time for the truth she'd been carrying since Greyharbor — since longer than Greyharbor, since years — it was now, when everything else had been stripped away and there was nothing left between the three of them and the dark.

But the truth wouldn't come. It sat in her chest like a stone swallowed whole, and instead she heard herself say, "First winter I crewed for you, you told me a boat is just a promise you make to the water. You remember that?"

Mara didn't look over. "I say a lot of things."

"You said you make the promise every morning and the water forgives you every night for the days you can't keep it." Odile poked the fire. Sparks went up and died. "I've been thinking maybe people are like that too. Maybe you forgive yourself every morning for the promises you broke, and someday the water—"

"That's not how it works, Odile."

"No," Odile said. "Maybe not. But it's how you get through the night."

Mara was silent. Across the fire, Tomas lay propped on his good side, awake, listening the way he listened to everything — completely, without seeming to. The owl called again, closer. The Kestrel's lamp burned steady in the distance, a fixed star that happened to be made by human hands.

"We leave at first light," Mara said at last. "Four days' walk. We travel light, we move fast, we don't stop except to drink. The chest's contents come with me — I carry them. Nobody argues about that."

"Nobody was going to argue," Odile said.

"And when this is done—" Mara stopped. Started again. "When this is done, whatever's left of it, I'm coming back for her. Whatever it costs. Whatever's left of me. I want you both to hear it, because I've broken a lot of promises on this river and I'm tired of being the only one keeping count."

"We hear you," Odile said.

And then, because the moment was open, because the fire was low and the night was honest, Odile heard the stone in her chest begin to move, and she said, before she could stop herself:

"There's something I have to tell you. Before we walk into whatever's waiting at Vessel's Rest. Something I should have told you years ago."

Mara turned. Even Tomas shifted, propping himself up on an elbow. The fire popped. Downstream the owl went silent, as if it too were listening.

Odile looked into the flames, because she couldn't look at Mara, and said, "It's about the harbor-masters. About Greyharbor. About why no bounty hunter ever found us in seven years, when every other smuggler on the Sable got caught inside three."

The silence stretched. She could feel Mara go very still beside the fire, the stillness of a woman who has learned that stillness is armor, and Odile knew there was no taking it back now, no sinking it below the waterline, no patching the seam. The words were already loose in the world, the way the flood had loosed the chest, the way the dry season had loosed the river's hold on the Kestrel — everything surfacing now, everything coming up at once, whether they were ready or not.

"Mara," Odile said quietly. "There are things I've been reporting about this boat. For years. And you need to hear them before we take one more step toward that tribunal."
