## 15. Four Days' Walk

The chest was too heavy to carry and too dangerous to leave, so Tomas took it apart with a pry bar and a prayer, working by the grey first light while the Kestrel sat groaning in the mud behind them.

The bar came up from a falling shoal the way a bone comes up from shallow water — pale, bent, already half-swallowed. Odile had tried everything: poles, winch, the desperate arithmetic of shifting cargo aft and forward and aft again. The river had simply decided, the way rivers do, and no argument of theirs would move it. Four days' walk to Vessel's Rest. Maybe five, if the rain came back. Maybe never, if Sarn's men found the boat first.

So they emptied her.

The dispatches went into an oilskin packet, wrapped twice and bound with cord. The manifest — the list of names, Mara's own name among them, written in a dead clerk's careful hand — went into a second packet, and that one Tomas carried against his own chest, under his coat, where a knife would find it before the rain did. The chest itself they broke apart and burned in the cookstove, feeding the Vessian seal into the fire piece by piece. Mara watched the lacquered crest curl and blacken without expression, though her hands, Tomas noticed, stayed closed the whole time, as if holding something that was no longer there.

"The boat," Odile said, for the third time.

"The boat will keep," Mara said. "Boats keep. That's the whole point of them."

Tomas said nothing. He had spent six years among records and knew the difference between things that keep and things that merely endure, and he suspected the Kestrel was the second kind, at least for now. He suspected Mara knew it too, and that this was why she would not look back at the mudflats as they climbed the levee and turned upstream, toward the hills and the tribunal seat and whatever waited for them there.

He carried the pry bar instead of a weapon. It was, he reflected as the town of Greyharbor sank behind the morning haze, a fitting implement for a man like him — a tool for opening things that should perhaps have stayed shut.

* * *

The road to Vessel's Rest was not a road so much as a rumor of one: a raised causeway of packed clay that the floods remade each year and the carters abandoned each dry season, so that it existed now as a spine of cracked earth running through country gone gold and brittle with the dying of the rains. Grasshoppers ticked in the grass like small clocks. The air smelled of dust and crushed mint and, faintly, of smoke from farms too far away to see.

They walked in the pattern Mara set without ever announcing it: herself ahead, reading the country the way she read water; Odile in the middle with the oilskin packet slung across her back; Tomas at the rear, where a man's shadow would fall first and a man's hesitation would show soonest. He was the slowest of them and he knew it. Six years in archive rooms had made his body an afterthought, a thing that carried his eyes from shelf to shelf. His shoulders ached by the second hour. By the fourth his boots had raised blisters he could feel with every step, small hot arguments against the whole enterprise.

"You walk like a man reading a ledger," Odile said, dropping back beside him as they forded a dry creek. "Left, right, left, right. No rhythm."

"I have rhythm," Tomas said. "It's just very old."

She snorted, but she slowed her pace to match his, and he understood that this was its own kind of ledger entry — a debt noted, not yet paid.

He had begun, somewhere on the second day, to think of the walk as payment. It was a comfortable fiction and he did not trust it. Blood was the only currency Sarn's regime had ever accepted at face value, and he had paid it nothing yet, not really — not in the way Mara's crew had paid, in the way the names on the manifest had paid. He had processed the orders. He had stamped them. He had gone home to his narrow room above the archive and eaten his supper and slept, while somewhere downriver the Meridian burned and eleven people who trusted their route to secrecy died for a man's ambition.

The knife wound, when it finally came, would feel almost like relief. He understood that in advance, the way you understand the ending of a story from its first page. But understanding did not make the waiting shorter.

They camped that night in the lee of a fallen milestone, an old Vessian marker with its inscription weathered to a ghost — he could make out the word *PROVINCE* and nothing else. Mara built no fire. They ate hard bread and dried fish and drank from a skin that tasted of the leather it was made from, and the stars came out in the fierce, unfiltered way they only did over dry country, so many and so sharp that the sky seemed less like a roof than an open wound.

"Tell me about the archive," Odile said. She said it lightly, the way you toss a stone into dark water to hear how deep it goes.

Tomas turned his water skin in his hands. "It was a beautiful building," he said. "That's the thing no one tells you about evil. It gets the best architects. Marble floors you could see your face in. Climate-controlled vaults for the documents, so nothing would rot, so nothing would be *lost*. We were very proud of never losing anything." He laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry sound with no floor under it. "Six years, and I never once lost a document. I can tell you exactly where the order for the Meridian's interception was filed. Shelf nine, case forty-one, third sub-series. I could walk there in my sleep. I have walked there in my sleep."

Mara said nothing. She sat with her back to the milestone, facing the dark country to the north, and he knew she was not looking at the stars but listening — to the grass, to the road behind them, to the world's small machinery of warning.

"Third sub-series," Odile repeated softly.

"The regime loved taxonomy," Tomas said. "It made atrocity feel like housekeeping."

* * *

They saw the riders on the third afternoon, and the seeing was almost an accident: a shimmer of heat-haze off the causeway, and then the shimmer resolving, briefly, into four shapes on the far side of a flooded borrow-pit, moving parallel to them and half a mile out. Mara dropped flat behind a stand of dry cane without a word, and Odile and Tomas went down beside her, and the four shapes went on shimmering, and then they were gone — or seemed to be, which in the flat gold country amounted to the same thing and to nothing at all.

"Scouts," Mara said. "Not the blade. The blade doesn't show itself."

"Then we've got a day," Odile said. "Maybe two."

"Maybe less." Mara lay with her chin on her forearm, studying the ground as if it were a chart. "They'll know we can't leave the causeway — the wet country south of here would take us a week. So they don't have to find us. They only have to be ahead of us." She looked up at Tomas, and there was something in her face he had not seen before, something almost gentle, which frightened him more than the riders had. "There's a cut in the levee about six miles on. Old flood channel, runs through a stand of dead cypress. If we cut through there we save half a day and lose our backs to the open. Or we stay on the causeway and walk straight into whatever they've set."

"Through the cypress," Tomas said.

Mara's eyebrows rose a fraction. "You don't know what's in the cypress."

"I know what's on the causeway." He shrugged, and his blistered feet throbbed as if in agreement. "I've spent my whole life choosing the known evil. I'd like to try the other one."

Something that was almost a smile crossed Mara's face and was gone. "Odile. Your feet?"

"Same as always," Odile said. "Attached, complaining."

They moved. The cut in the levee was exactly where Mara said it would be — she carried the whole river country in her head the way Tomas had once carried shelf nine, case forty-one — and they dropped through it into a world of standing grey water and cypress dead since the floods of some earlier year, their trunks pale as bone, their roots making a labyrinth of the shallows. The light came down in shafts through the bare branches and lay on the water in long copper bars. It was beautiful in the way that drowned things are beautiful, and silent in a way that made Tomas's skin crawl, because the birds had all gone quiet at once, and birds, he had learned in six years of watching the river from a salvager's deck, only went quiet like that for one reason.

"They're here," he said.

Mara was already turning. "How many—"

The first man came out from behind a cypress trunk ten feet away, and there was no time for anything. Tomas saw the knife — a short river-blade, the kind ferrymen carried — and saw it turn, not toward him, toward Odile, toward the packet on her back, and his body did what his conscience had been trying to do for six years, which was to put itself in the way.

He had the pry bar. It was a stupid weapon and it saved him once, a wild swing that caught the man's wrist and turned the first stroke wide. Then they were grappling in the shallows, and the water was cold and full of dead leaves, and the second man was there, and the world became very simple: weight, and breath, and the bright animal arithmetic of staying alive. He heard Mara shout. He heard Odile scream something that was not a scream but a warning. He felt the knife go in below his ribs on the right side, a punch more than a pain, a deep hot wrongness, and he did the only thing his body knew, which was to close his arms around the man who held it and fall backward into the water, taking the knife deeper and the man with him, so that the blade was pinned between their bodies and could not be drawn for another stroke.

They came apart in the shallows. The man ran. Tomas understood distantly that Mara had done something to the second man — he heard the crack of the pry bar, or perhaps it was his own pry bar, he had lost track of it — and then Odile was kneeling over him in the copper light, and her hands were pressing hard below his ribs, and the pain arrived all at once, enormous, like the river coming through a broken levee.

"Stay down," Odile was saying. "Stay down, you idiot, stay down—"

"It's not deep," Tomas said. He was proud of how steady it came out. Then he looked down at the blood spreading through the water around him, black in the shadow of the cypresses, and revised his estimate. "It might be deep."

Mara crouched at his other side, tearing the hem of her shirt into strips with quick, economical motions, and her face was the face he had seen her wear in the worst water, the face of a woman who had decided that nothing on this river was going to take anything else from her, not today. "You fool," she said, and her voice cracked on the word. "You absolute fool. It was coming at Odile."

"I know," Tomas said.

"Why?"

And because the truth was the only currency he had ever really dealt in, and because he was lying in cold water with his life running out of him and no time left for anything but exactness, Tomas told it plainly.

"Because I stamped the orders," he said. "Shelf nine, case forty-one. I read them. I knew what they meant, and I filed them, and I went home, and I slept. I have owed your crew six years of interest, Mara. This is the first payment. It's not enough. But it's a start."

Mara's hands stopped. For one long moment the only sound was the water dripping from the cypress branches, and the far-off tick of grasshoppers, and Odile's breath, ragged with effort as she kept the pressure on.

Then Mara tied off the first bandage, hard enough to make him gasp, and said, "Then you'll pay the rest at Vessel's Rest. Alive. Do you understand me? You don't get to settle this account in a ditch. You'll stand in the marble hall and say what you just said to me, in front of the tribunal, with your name attached. That's the payment. That's the only payment they'll take."

Tomas looked up at her through the lattice of dead branches, at the copper light and the fierce grey eyes, and felt something loosen in his chest that had been clenched for six years — not the wound, though that was there too, hot and insistent — but the older thing, the silence itself, the long archive-dark of it.

"Then get me there," he said.

They got him up between them, one arm over each of their shoulders, and the oilskin packet passed from Odile's back to Mara's, and they went on north through the dead cypress while the light failed, three people and a wound, carrying the names of the dead toward a hall of marble. Behind them, far off on the causeway, Tomas heard a whistle — two notes, then three, the sound of men signaling to other men.

The blade, Mara had said, doesn't show itself.

It had shown itself. Which meant the rest of it was already ahead of them, waiting at Vessel's Rest, and the tribunal had not yet adjourned, and four days had become three, and three were becoming two.

"Walk faster," Tomas said, through his teeth, to no one in particular. "I'm bleeding on the schedule."
