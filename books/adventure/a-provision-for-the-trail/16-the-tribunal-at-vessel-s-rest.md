## 16. The Tribunal at Vessel's Rest

They came down out of the hills on the fourth morning, when the mist still lay in the valley like milk poured into a bowl, and Vessel's Rest appeared all at once below them — white marble rising out of the river fog as though the city had been built by people who expected the water to apologize and go away.

Mara stopped at the crest of the switchback road and looked at it for a long time.

She had seen the capital of the drowned Republic only twice before in her life: once as a girl, from the deck of her father's barge, when the tribunal dome had been gilded and the levees crowded with banners; and once seven years ago, at night, from a stolen skiff running dark past the harbor forts with a warrant behind her and three dead crewmen behind that. From the river, the city had looked like a jaw. From up here, in the grey light, it looked almost gentle — a tiered town climbing its hill above the confluence, the tribunal hall crowning everything, its dome stripped of gold now, bare lead gone green with weather, like something that had been robbed and never quite recovered its dignity.

"You're staring," Odile said beside her.

"I'm deciding whether to turn around."

"You won't." Odile shifted the pack on her shoulders. She'd carried it nearly the whole way without complaint, though the strap had worn a red groove into her collarbone that Mara kept noticing and pretending not to. "You didn't walk four days through tick-brush and rock slide to admire the view from the top."

Tomas said nothing. He was walking more carefully than he had been two days ago, favoring his left side where Sarn's man's knife had opened him along the ribs. The wound was bound tight under his shirt, and he'd refused Mara's offer to re-dress it that morning with a small shake of the head that meant *not here, not on the open road*. But she had seen him press his palm against it at every rest stop, seen the way his breath went shallow on the climbs, and she knew what he was carrying besides the satchel of dispatches: a fever he wouldn't name, working its way up under his defenses like water under a hull seam.

"Four days," Mara said, mostly to herself. "And we made it in four days."

"We made it," Odile agreed, "in four days, with one knife wound, no boat, and a chest's worth of paper that smells like pond water. I'd call that a triumph if my feet would let me feel anything."

They went down into the city.

* * *

Vessel's Rest smelled different from Greyharbor. Greyharbor smelled of tar and fish and the sweet rot of the rendering yards; the old capital smelled of stone dust and cut grass and, faintly, everywhere, the river itself — the wide brown confluence that gave the town its reason for existing. The streets were paved, actually paved, which after years of mud-board landings felt obscene. People moved through them with the unhurried confidence of those who lived somewhere permanent. Mara caught herself walking close to walls, keeping her face angled away, an old habit from ports where her description had been nailed to customs-house doors.

No one looked at her. That was somehow worse.

The tribunal hall stood at the top of the Processional, a long stair of white steps flanked by cypress, and even from the bottom Mara could see that something was wrong with it. Not wrong, exactly. *Finished.* Banners were coming down. Workmen were folding scaffolding. A clerk's cart stood loaded with document cases, and two guards at the doors wore the loose posture of men whose posting was ending.

"They're closing," Tomas said quietly. He'd seen it too.

"They can't close."

"The tribunals sit for a season," he said, and there was something in his voice — not apology, exactly, but the flat tone of a man delivering facts he'd have preferred not to know. "I told you. When the rains end, they adjourn. It's in the charter. Justice keeps office hours, Captain. It always has."

Mara started up the stairs anyway.

Her legs ached from the march and her boots had given up any pretense of waterproofing somewhere on the second day, but she took the steps at a pace that made Odile curse behind her and made Tomas fall back to a grim, measured climb, one hand pressed to his side. Halfway up, a workman carrying a rolled banner stepped into her path without looking, and she shouldered past him hard enough that he stumbled and shouted something after her about madwomen, and she did not stop.

The doors of the tribunal hall were tall enough to admit a mast. They stood half-open, and through the gap came the sound of a nation packing itself away: the scrape of furniture, the rustle of paper, a clerk's voice calling inventory numbers to another clerk's answering voice, bored and rhythmic as a chant.

Inside, the great chamber was dim and vast, lit by high clerestory windows that threw bars of pale light across ranks of empty benches. At the far end, beneath the blank space where the Republic's seal had been chiseled off the wall — Mara could see the scar of it, a rough rectangle of newer stone — a handful of clerks worked among stacked ledgers. One of them looked up as they entered, a thin man with ink-stained fingers and spectacles pushed up into thinning hair.

"The tribunal is adjourned," he said. "Reconvenes with the spring rains. If you have petitions for the docket, the intake office in the lower city—"

"I don't have a petition." Mara crossed the floor, her boots loud in the emptiness. "I have evidence. Evidence of crimes tried and misjudged by this tribunal seven years ago. Crimes against a crew of the *Meridian*, and against me."

The clerk's expression did not change, which was itself a kind of answer. She had seen that face before — on harbormasters, on customs officers, on every gatekeeper of every institution she'd ever needed something from. It was the face of a man whose job was to be a wall.

"Evidence is received through petition," he said. "Petitions are received through the intake office. The intake office closes at the end of the week."

"Then receive it now."

"Madam—"

"Mara Halloran." She said it plainly, watching for the flicker. There it was — a fractional stillness around his eyes. He knew the name. Of course he did; everyone who mattered in this country's paperwork knew it. "Captain of the *Kestrel*. Subject of warrant 4471-C, issued by this tribunal's predecessor court. I've spent seven years running from this building. I walked four days through the dry hills carrying proof that the verdict in the *Meridian* case was bought and paid for. You are going to hear me out, or you are going to watch me stand in your empty courtroom until someone with authority comes back in the spring, and I promise you I will still be standing here."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above, a pigeon muttered in the rafters.

The clerk removed his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve with the deliberate slowness of a man buying time, and put them back on. "Halloran," he said again, softer. "The *Meridian* case. You're the smuggler."

"I'm the survivor."

"The tribunal found—"

"The tribunal found what it was paid to find." She heard her own voice rise and forced it level. Behind her, she felt Tomas shift his weight, and Odile go very still, the way she always did when Mara's temper came near the surface. "Corvel Sarn sold our route to the regime's hunters. Seven of my crew died on the *Meridian* when the hunters boarded her. The tribunal called it negligence — mine. The warrant's been on my neck ever since. And the man who arranged all of it—" she pulled the satchel around from her back and set it on the nearest bench, untying the oilcloth with hands that wanted to shake and didn't "—signed these."

The dispatches came out onto the wood one by one. Water-stained, swollen, their seals intact. The clerk leaned over them despite himself, and Mara watched his eyes move, watched the boredom drain out of his face degree by degree as he read.

"These seals," he said slowly. "These are archive seals. Provenance class. Where did you—"

"Fished out of a flood. That doesn't matter. What matters is the signature block on the third one. Read the authorization line."

He read it. His lips moved slightly. Then he straightened up, and Mara saw the wall come back up — saw it happen, brick by brick, the way a man closes shutters before a storm he's decided not to warn anyone about.

"This is not admissible," he said.

"It's signed."

"It's unsigned provenance. Salvaged material, no chain of custody, no authentication of seal-work, no living witness to the transactions described." He began stacking the dispatches, pushing them back toward her across the bench like something unpleasant he'd been asked to touch. "Do you understand what you're holding? These could be genuine. They could also be very good forgeries, produced by any of a dozen ex-regime clerks who fled south with their seal matrices. This tribunal has been burned by forged documents before. We do not act on paper alone. Not anymore."

"Then authenticate them."

"With what expertise?" He spread his ink-stained hands. "The archivists who could read this seal-work are scattered or dead or — worse. Do you know how many former regime functionaries have come through these halls since the collapse, each one with documents proving their own innocence and someone else's guilt? I have drawers full of them. Drawers." His voice cracked, just slightly, on the word. "Paper proves nothing, Captain Halloran. Paper is the cheapest thing in the world to lie with. It takes a person — a witness, under oath, with a face and a name and something to lose — to make paper mean anything."

Beside her, very quietly, Tomas made a small sound. Mara didn't look at him. She couldn't afford to look at him, because she knew what she'd see — and because some cold, quick part of her mind had already leapt ahead to the thought she wasn't ready to think: *he read those seals on the river. He told me what the matrix marks meant. He knew the archive classes.*

"And the man named in these dispatches," she said instead. "Corvel Sarn. Is he known to this tribunal?"

Something moved behind the clerk's eyes then — something that was almost fear, and almost shame, and entirely familiar to her.

"Trader Corvel Sarn," the clerk said carefully, "is scheduled to appear before the spring session. As a witness for the prosecution in the matter of the harbor records burnings. He is a respected member of the trading community. He has testified before this tribunal on several occasions."

"He's testifying *for* you."

"He is assisting the tribunal." The clerk gathered his ledger to his chest like a shield. "Captain, I will tell you what I tell every petitioner who arrives at the wrong season with the right story. Come back with the rains. Bring a witness. Bring a magistrate's endorsement from any provincial seat. Bring something that isn't only paper. Until then—" he glanced at the satchel, at the water-swollen dispatches, and for one moment his voice dropped, went human, went tired. "Until then, keep those safe. And keep yourself safe. Because if those say what I think they say, and Sarn learns they exist, you will wish the warrant was the worst thing hunting you."

* * *

Outside, the afternoon had turned golden, and the Processional steps were warm underfoot, and the city went on being beautiful in a way that felt like mockery.

Odile sat down heavily three steps from the top and put her face in her hands. "Drawers," she said. "He has *drawers* full of people like us."

"People with less patience," Mara said. Her voice came out flat. She sat down too, because her legs had decided the matter without consulting her, and looked out over the rooftops to the river, where the confluence ran broad and brown and indifferent, catching the low sun in long copper streaks. Somewhere down that water, four hundred miles of falling channels away, the *Kestrel* sat grounded on a bar with her seams open, drying out like a dead thing. Her boat. Her whole life, built plank by plank over seven years of running, and it was probably already past saving.

"Tomas." She said it without turning. "You knew the seal classes. On the river, the first night — you read the matrix marks like a priest reads scripture. Tell me why a salvager knows provenance classification."

The silence behind her lasted long enough that she did turn.

Tomas stood a few steps below them, one hand pressed to his bandaged side, his face grey with more than the fever. He was looking at the tribunal doors as though they might open again and swallow him, and when he finally spoke, his voice was rough.

"Not here," he said. "There's a tavern at the foot of the stairs. Buy me a drink first. I'm going to need it."

"That bad?"

"Worse." He managed something that was almost a smile, and failed at it. "You asked me once why a man with my debts would help you carry a chest toward the people who want you hanged. I owe you the real answer. I've owed it since Greyharbor. But I want to give it sitting down, and I want to give it before—" he glanced up at the bare scar on the pediment above the doors, where the old seal had been chiseled away "—before you hear it from someone else. Because in this city, Captain, everyone's past eventually walks into the room."

Odile looked between them, her dark eyes narrowing. "What did you do?"

"What the clerk said." Tomas started down the steps, slow, careful, each one a small negotiation with his wound. "Paper proves nothing. It takes a person. A witness, under oath, with a face and a name and something to lose." He paused, three steps down, not looking back. "I'm the person, Mara. I'm the thing your chest needs. And what that costs you — what it costs *us* — you'll want to decide with a drink in your hand."

Mara stayed on the step a moment longer, watching him descend into the golden light, and understood with a sinking, river-bottom certainty that whatever he was about to confess, the tribunal's walls were only the first of them.

She got up and followed.
