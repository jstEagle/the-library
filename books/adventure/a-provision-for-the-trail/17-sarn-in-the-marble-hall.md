## 17. Sarn in the Marble Hall

The tribunal at Vessel's Rest sat in a hall that had been a granary before it was anything else, and the grain had never entirely left. Tomas felt it in the floorboards, a faint sweet dust that rose with every footfall, so that the marble-faced courtroom smelled faintly of bread, of harvest, of a country that had once fed itself and now fed on its own paperwork. He stood near the back with his hat in his hands, and he watched the clerk's bench the way he had once watched a river gauge: for the small movements that meant the water was about to change.

The hall was half full. Tribunal season was dying the way the river was dying — slowly, then all at once — and everyone with business before the bench had come in the last days to have it heard before the adjournment froze the docket like low water freezing a channel. Farmers with boundary disputes. A widow with a salvage claim. Two men arguing over a drowned barge. Ordinary grief, ordinary greed, the small sediment of a functioning state.

And at the front, in the third row of the gallery, sat a man in a grey coat that cost more than the Kestrel's engine.

Tomas knew him before he knew him. That was the strange thing about Corvel Sarn — he had been a name on documents for so long that when the man finally assembled himself out of the crowd, Tomas's body recognized him first. The shoulders went tight. The breath went shallow. Seven years of filing cabinets stood up inside him and began to scream.

He was not a large man. That was the first obscenity of him. Tomas had built him, over the long walk upstream and the four days of mud and the knife wound that still pulled at his side with every step, into something enormous — a figure with a regime's whole weight behind it, a man made of orders and seals and other people's deaths. But the flesh of him was ordinary. Medium height, going soft at the jaw, with the careful, unlined face of someone who had never once been caught in weather. His hands rested on his knees like a man waiting for a ferry. His eyes moved over the hall with the mild, proprietary patience of a landlord checking his rooms.

He was here as a witness. Tomas had heard it that morning from the docket clerk, a boy with ink-stained cuffs who read the day's names aloud in the bored voice of someone reciting weather. *In the matter of recovered Vessian state correspondence, the tribunal will hear testimony from Corvel Sarn, licensed trader, resident of Greyharbor, formerly of the Office of Provisioning.* Formerly of the Office of Provisioning. As if the office were a town he'd moved away from. As if it were a place you could leave.

Mara stood at the petitioner's rail with Odile beside her, and between them on the rail's worn oak lay the chest's contents, laid out in their numbered order the way Tomas had arranged them on the Kestrel's chart table a hundred miles and a lifetime ago. The dispatches. The manifest. The payment authorizations with their column of names. Mara's name among them, and eleven others, and beside each name a figure, and beside each figure a date, and beside each date the small, dry initials of the clerk who had processed the order into the world.

*T.V.*

Tomas's own hands, his own initials, his own ink. He had told her on the walk — all of it, the whole confession, spilled out in the rain somewhere past the drowned orchards while Odile walked ten paces behind and pretended the rain was louder than it was. He had told her what he had been, what he had processed, what he had not asked. She had listened without a word and then said, *You'll testify to all of it. Not just the parts that help us. All of it.* And he had said yes, and meant it, and the meaning had not made him lighter. It had made him precise. That was all confession ever did, he suspected. It did not forgive you. It sharpened you.

But the tribunal had adjourned for the midday meal, and the hall had emptied, and now it was refilling, and Sarn had risen from the third row and was walking — unhurried, coat unbuttoned, a man strolling through his own orchard — toward the petitioner's rail.

Tomas moved. He didn't decide to move; his feet simply committed him to it, carrying him down the aisle with his hat still crushed in one hand, and he arrived at the rail a half-step behind Mara, close enough to smell the river still in her coat, close enough to see her knuckles whiten on the oak.

"Captain Halloran." Sarn's voice was warm. That was the second obscenity. It was a good voice, a voice for reading aloud, for toasting, for the long slow persuasion of a business dinner. "I'd hoped to find you here. They told me at Greyharbor you'd gone upstream, and I confess I worried. The channels are closing. A woman alone, with a boat that's seen better years." His gaze slid past her, touched the documents on the rail, and returned. "And this. The famous chest. May I?"

He didn't wait for permission. He reached out — and Mara's hand came down flat on the manifest before his fingers arrived, not fast, not angry, just there, the way a lockgate comes down.

"Petitioner's exhibits," she said. "You're welcome to look when the tribunal looks."

"Of course. Of course." He withdrew the hand gracefully, palms open, a man demonstrating he had nothing in them. "I only wanted to say — before we do this in front of the assessors, with all the machinery and the oaths — that I take no pleasure in it. Truly. I remember the old days, Captain. I remember the river when it was dangerous, and I remember the people who made it so, and I have spent seven years trying to build something quieter on top of all that. A man hates to see the old wounds opened. Even the ones that were never his."

"The ones that were never his," Mara repeated. Her voice was level. Tomas, standing at her shoulder, could feel the effort of the levelness the way you feel a hull holding against a current.

"No one is served by relitigating a drowned country," Sarn said. "The Republic is gone. Its debts are gone. Its crimes—" he spread his hands, a small benediction, "—were everyone's. I was a clerk in a ministry. You were a smuggler. We were both rowing in the same flood. The only question the tribunal needs to answer is a simple one, and I'll answer it under oath, and it will cost us both an afternoon: where did these documents come from, and are they what they claim to be?"

"And what will you say they are?"

"A forgery." He said it gently, almost kindly, the way a doctor delivers a diagnosis he's delivered a thousand times. "A skilled one. I'll grant it skill. The paper's right, the ink's right, the form is right — someone has done their homework, or has access to genuine blanks. But the seals, Captain." He shook his head with real regret. "The seals are the tell. Every document in the old Provisioning Office passed through a specific authentication protocol, and the counter-seals on these were applied in a pattern that no working archivist would have used. It's the error of an intelligent amateur. Someone reconstructing a system they never worked inside." He looked at her with something that was almost tenderness. "You were on the river when the Republic fell. You never saw the inside of the archive. I understand why you'd try. I understand the grief that would drive it. But grief is not evidence, and the tribunal will see that."

The hall had gone quiet around them. Not empty — the farmers and the widow and the salvage-claim men were still in their benches, and they had all gone very still, the way a room goes still when it smells blood under the bread-smell. Odile's hand had found the rail on Mara's other side.

"You've examined the seals," Mara said. "Already."

"I was given the opportunity this morning, when my testimony was entered." A small shrug. "The clerk was thorough. I admire that in an institution."

*The clerk.* Tomas felt the words land somewhere below his ribs. The clerk who could reopen the case was Sarn's old colleague — Mara had said it on the walk, said it like a woman naming a reef she'd have to sail past. But this was worse than a reef. This was the reef moving. Sarn had not come to testify against the documents. He had come to testify against *her* — to stand in the marble-faced hall with his honest grey coat and his regretful voice and bury the chest under his own expertise, and with it the manifest, and with it eleven dead names, and with it the warrant, the running, the seven years, the whole drowned history of Mara Halloran, filed forever under *forgery, malicious.*

And the trap was good. That was what made Tomas's hands cold. It was not a bribe, not a threat, not a knife in an alley — Sarn had learned, as the whole old regime had learned, that the cleanest murder is a procedure. He would destroy her with accuracy. He would be right about everything except the one thing that mattered, and no one in this hall would ever know the difference, because the one person who *could* know the difference was standing at the back with his hat in his hands and a knife wound in his side and no standing whatsoever before this bench.

No one knew him. He had walked into Vessel's Rest four days ago as a salvager, a nobody, a man with river mud on his boots. His name was on none of the petitions. His testimony had not been entered. The tribunal's season was closing like a lockgate, and the water behind it was going still.

Sarn was still talking. "—and I want to be clear that I bear you no ill will. If the tribunal finds the documents genuine, I will accept it. I am here to serve the record, nothing more." He paused, and for one moment the warmth thinned and something older showed through, something with the patience of a man who had signed orders and never once watched what the orders did. "But I will tell you what I told the assessors this morning. The dead do not need vindication, Captain. They need to be let alone. There is a mercy in letting the river keep what it has taken."

"The river doesn't keep things," Mara said quietly. "It brings them up. I've seen it do it."

Something flickered in Sarn's face then — the first hairline crack in the lacquer, gone as fast as it came, but Tomas saw it and understood it, because he had spent seven years reading other men's marginalia and he knew what a hand did when it touched a page it had hoped was lost. Sarn had known what the flood might bring up. Sarn had been waiting for this chest the way a man waits outside a locked room.

Then the bell rang for afternoon session, and the hall began to move, and Sarn inclined his head with perfect courtesy and withdrew to the third row, and the assessors filed in behind the bench in their plain dark robes, and the clerk with ink-stained cuffs called the first case in his bored voice.

* * *

They did not hear the case that afternoon. There was a procedural motion first — entered by the clerk, seconded by no one, requiring the tribunal to rule on the *provenance and authentication* of the exhibits before any testimony regarding their *contents* could be heard. It was elegant. It was devastating. It meant that before Mara could say a single name aloud, before the manifest could be read into any record anywhere, the tribunal would first decide whether the chest was real — and the only expert testimony on authentication in the entire docket was Corvel Sarn's.

Tomas stood in the corridor outside the hall and listened to the motion read, and did the arithmetic he had been doing all day, and watched it come out the same way every time.

He was the missing witness. Not *a* witness. The missing one. The hole in the record shaped exactly like himself.

Because Sarn had made one error, and it was the error of a man who had spent seven years believing the archive had drowned with everything else. The counter-seals on those documents were genuine. Tomas knew it the way he knew his own signature — knew it in his fingers, in the specific pressure and angle of the seal-die, in the little asymmetry of the Provisioning Office's second matrix that every clerk learned to feel for and no one outside the office had ever seen. The seals had been applied in the standard rotation, in the standard order, by hands that included his own. Sarn had described an error that did not exist, in loving technical detail, to a tribunal that had no way to check it.

Unless.

Unless someone stood up in that marble-faced hall and said: *I worked in that office. I processed those orders. I know the rotation, I know the dies, I know the hand that struck those seals, because one of the hands was mine.* And said it knowing what it cost — knowing it put him in the record beside the dead, knowing it made him the man who had stayed silent for seven years, knowing that Sarn's lawyers would take his confession and use it to drag every document he'd ever touched into the mud, and knowing, too, that the moment he spoke, Sarn would know exactly who had survived the archive, and what else he might remember, and where he might be found.

The trap closed on Mara. And the only door out of it was him, walking through it in full view of the man who had built it.

He found Mara and Odile in the corridor's end window, where the afternoon light came in the color of the river below — low, brown-gold, carrying the first leaves of the dry season. Mara was standing with her arms folded, looking out at the water, and Odile was standing with her, not speaking, the two of them arranged like figures on a figurehead.

"He's right about one thing," Mara said, without turning. "The seals are the whole case. If the tribunal rules them forged, the manifest dies with them. Every name. Everything." Her jaw worked. "And I can't authenticate them. I was on the river. I never saw the inside of the archive. He knows that. He built the whole motion on it."

"He did," Tomas said.

"Then we need—" She stopped. Turned. Looked at him, and he watched the understanding arrive in her face the way the flood had arrived in the river, all at once and from far upstream. "Tomas."

"The counter-seals were struck in the standard rotation," he said. "Second matrix, third impression, left-biased strike. The Provisioning Office die had a flaw in the laurel — a broken vein in the third leaf from the left. It came from a repair in the year the Republic was already dying, and only working clerks ever saw it. Sarn described a rotation that never existed." His voice was steady. He was surprised by that. "He's lying to the tribunal with a technical lie, Mara. The one kind of lie I can prove is a lie."

Odile had gone very still. "You'd have to say what you were," she said. "Out loud. On the record. In front of him."

"Yes."

"He'll come for you."

"He's already come for everyone I ever filed a paper about." Tomas looked out the window at the river, at the brown-gold water carrying the season away, at the far bank where the channels were closing one by one like eyes. "There's a mercy in letting the river keep what it's taken. That's what he said. Seven years ago I believed something like it. I called it *procedure*. I called it *not my department*. I stood in an archive and processed the deaths of eleven people and told myself the record was the record and my hands were only hands." He turned back to them. "Tomorrow the tribunal rules on the seals. If no one speaks, they rule on Sarn's word, and Mara Halloran becomes a forger in the last record that will ever hold her name. I'm the only witness in this building — maybe in the world — who can stop that. The trap closed today. It opened in the same breath. It's been open for seven years. I just never walked through."

Mara was looking at him with an expression he had no file for. Not gratitude — he didn't want that, and she didn't have it to give. Something more like recognition. One survivor of the same drowned country, finally sighting another across the water.

"The assessors will want your credentials," she said at last. "Your name, your office, your dates of service. All of it goes in the record."

"I know."

"Sarn will be sitting ten feet away."

"I know."

"And when this is over — whatever happens in there — he'll know exactly what you are and exactly where you stand."

"Yes," Tomas said. "That's rather the point."

Below them the river ran on, dropping by inches every day, carrying the dry season toward them like a debt coming due. In the hall behind them the clerk was reading the closing docket in his bored voice, and somewhere in the third row a man in a grey coat was waiting, patient as a landlord, to bury eleven names under a technical lie.

Tomas put his hat back on his head.

"First session tomorrow," he said. "Call me as a witness. And Mara — call me by my full name. The one from the archive. I've spent seven years being a salvager." He looked at the door of the marble hall, at the bread-smell and the blood-smell underneath it. "It's time someone in that room knew what else I've been."
