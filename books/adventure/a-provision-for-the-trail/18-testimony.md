## 18. Testimony

The tribunal hall at Vessel's Rest had been built to outlast the Republic that raised it, and it showed. Marble the color of old teeth. Columns wide as river barges. A ceiling so high that the morning light coming through the clerestory windows gave up halfway down and left the floor in a permanent gray dusk, like standing at the bottom of a clear lake. Mara had avoided this building for seven years by avoiding an entire country. Now she stood in its antechamber with a satchel of dispatches against her ribs and her hands steady only because she had ordered them to be.

The clerk — Pellam, Sarn's old colleague, the one whose face had gone carefully blank when Tomas named him yesterday — had reopened the session under protest, and the protest was audible in everything he did. He moved slowly. He consulted his seals at length. He let the benches fill with whoever cared to come, which turned out to be more people than Mara expected: traders off the wintering boats, salvagers, a knot of Vessian exiles who still wore the republic's faded blue at their collars like a scar they were proud of.

Tomas stood beside her in his good coat, the one with the mended cuff, and did not fidget. His knife wound had been bound two days ago; she could see him favoring the arm when he thought no one watched. Odile sat behind them on the public bench, arms crossed, jaw set, radiating the particular fury of a woman who had run out of things to throw at the situation.

"They'll try to make it about you," Tomas said quietly. "Not the chest. You."

"I know."

"Let them. Every question they ask about your warrant is a question they're not asking about the seal-work."

Mara looked at him. "You've rehearsed this."

"I've been an archivist for twenty years." Something dry moved through his voice, not quite humor. "Rehearsing is most of what we do."

The doors opened. The crier called the session. And down the center aisle of the marble hall, unhurried, dressed in a trader's charcoal wool with a silver chain of office at his throat, came Corvel Sarn.

* * *

She had imagined this moment for seven years, and every version of it had involved violence. She had imagined shouting. She had imagined him flinching. She had imagined, on the worst nights, anchored in some reed-choked backwater with the crew's names going around her head like water around a stone, that she would put her hands on his throat and squeeze until the river took back what it was owed.

What she felt instead, watching him take his seat among the witnesses like a man settling into his own parlor, was cold. Clean and total, like the first frost on the Kestrel's rails. He was older. Thicker through the shoulders. His hair had gone silver at the temples in a way that money buys. But the face was the same face from the night at Tarn's Landing, the night her route died, the face that had smiled at her across a table while hunters were already moving upriver toward her people.

He caught her looking. He inclined his head — courteous, almost warm — the way one merchant greets another at a fair.

That was the thing about Corvel Sarn. He had never once needed to pretend he hadn't done it. He simply lived in a world where doing it cost nothing.

"State your name and office for the record," said the presiding magistrate, an old woman named Ivenna whose voice carried like a hull-bell.

"Tomas Vey," Tomas said, walking forward. "Formerly second archivist of the Central Registry at Vessia-capital. Currently a salvager working the middle Sable."

"You understand that anything you say here becomes part of the permanent record."

"Yes," Tomas said. "That is rather the point."

A ripple went through the benches. Pellam's pen stopped moving.

Mara had heard Tomas tell stories before — river stories, salvage stories, the low amused recitations of a man who had spent years making himself unremarkable. This was not that man. This man stood in the well of the hall with his wounded arm held close and spoke in the flat, precise cadence of the Registry, each sentence a filed document.

"On the fourteenth of Rainsmonth, seven years ago," he began, "the Central Registry processed a requisition order authorizing payment from discretionary funds to an informant designated in the ledger as *Lantern*. The order concerned the sale of smuggling route documentation — waypoints, safe anchorages, cargo schedules — pertaining to a vessel operating under letters of passage issued during the final months of the Republic. The vessel was the *Meridian*. The route led through the Narrows at Coldspring Bend. The hunters who received the information intercepted the *Meridian* there."

The hall had gone very quiet. Somewhere above them, water dripped from the clerestory onto stone, patient as a metronome.

"I know the details of this order," Tomas continued, "because I copied it. Fair copy, into the permanent ledger, in my own hand. I knew what the route documentation was. I recognized the anchorage codes because I had catalogued them myself, two years earlier, from a captured smuggler's chart-book. I knew that *Lantern* was being paid to sell men and women to the regime's hunters. And I made my fair copy, and I initialed it, and I closed the ledger, and I said nothing."

"Why?" asked Magistrate Ivenna. Not cruelly. Genuinely.

"Because I was afraid," Tomas said. "Because the man who brought the order to the counter was Corvel Sarn, then deputy registrar, and he looked at me while I copied it, and he smiled, and I understood that my silence was also being purchased — not with coin, but with my life. And I told myself that copying was not signing. That ink is not intent. I told myself that for seven years, and I have come here today to stop telling myself that."

He turned, deliberately, and faced the benches where the exiles in their faded blue sat.

"The chest recovered from the Sable sandbar contains eleven dispatches bearing the Registry's true seal, including the *Lantern* requisition in my own hand. It also contains a twelfth document — a counter-sealed affidavit attesting that the chest is a forgery commissioned by one Mara Halloran to defame a respectable trader. That affidavit bears a seal purporting to be mine." He paused. "It is not mine. And I can prove it."

* * *

They gave him the documents, and the hall leaned in.

Sarn's counsel — a sleek man named Duvane whom Mara suspected had been retained before the tribunal even agreed to hear the case — objected twice, was overruled twice, and spent the third objection studying his notes with great intensity. Pellam had stopped pretending to be bored. He sat forward over his ledger like a man watching water rise past a mark he'd carved himself.

Tomas laid the true seal impression beside the forged one and walked the hall through it the way he must once have walked apprentices through it: gently, thoroughly, without mercy.

"The Registry die was cut in three pieces and assembled, so that no single engraver could produce a working counterfeit. Look at the interlace where the river-band crosses the keel-line. In the true seal, the keel passes *over* the band — always, in every impression ever struck, because of how the dies seat against each other. In this forgery, the keel passes under." He tapped the wax with one finger. "Whoever cut this worked from impressions, not from the die itself. Impressions flatten depth into surface. They cannot tell you what lies over what. Only someone who has studied hundreds of true impressions would know to look — and only someone who has never touched the die could get it wrong."

"And you have touched the die?" Duvane asked, rising.

"I assembled it," Tomas said. "In the last week of the Republic, when the Registry was being evacuated, I was ordered to break it down for transport. I carried the keel-piece myself. It is the heaviest piece. I remember the weight of it better than I remember my mother's face."

He straightened. "There is a second error. The affidavit is dated the third of Drymonth, seven years ago. The Registry's dating convention changed that same season — the old form wrote the month before the day; the new form reversed it. This document uses the new form with an old-form date stamp beneath it. No genuine Registry clerk would make that mistake, because the change was drilled into us for a full season before it took effect. But a forger working from mixed samples would find it almost irresistible." He looked, at last, directly at Corvel Sarn. "Almost irresistible. As almost everything is, if you have never had to live inside the consequences."

Sarn did not flinch. That was the terrible thing. He sat with his hands folded and his silver chain catching the gray light, and he watched Tomas dismantle him with the expression of a man reviewing a shipment manifest for errors — interested, faintly bored, already composing the correction.

But his counsel had gone pale. And Pellam's pen had stopped again, and this time it did not start.

* * *

They called her after the midday recess, and she walked the length of the hall feeling every eye like a hand on her back.

"State your name."

"Mara Halloran. Captain of the riverboat *Kestrel*."

Duvane was on his feet immediately. "Magistrate, this witness stands under an outstanding warrant from the provisional authority—"

"The provisional authority," Magistrate Ivenna said, "dissolved fourteen months ago. Its warrants are subject to review by this tribunal, which is precisely why this witness is here. Sit down, counselor."

Mara waited for the ripple to settle. Then she did the thing she had decided to do on the walk from the derelict lock, the thing she had practiced saying to the dark of her cabin until the words stopped tasting like blood.

"I want to read names into the record."

Ivenna considered her. "Proceed."

Mara opened the small notebook she had carried for seven years without ever reading aloud from it — the one with the water-blurred entries, the one she'd copied out fresh on the *Kestrel*'s deck two nights ago by lamplight while Odile pretended to sleep and Tomas kept watch.

"Bren Halloran. My father. Pilot and owner of the *Meridian*. He taught me to read water before he taught me letters." Her voice held. She had not been sure it would. "Sefa Idris, cook, who fed half the levee poor at Greyharbor out of our own stores and lied to me about how much it cost. Anselm Voss, engineer, who could keep any engine alive and chose to stay below when the hunters boarded, to buy the others time. The twins, Piet and Luce Ammerling, deckhands, seventeen years old. They'd signed on for one season to earn their pilot's fees. One season."

The hall was absolutely silent now. Even the drip from the clerestory seemed to have stopped.

"Denna Marsh, mate. She was the one who found the route. She trusted the wrong person with it, and she died knowing it, and I have hated her a little for seven years for trusting, and loved her more for dying, and I am tired of carrying both." Mara's thumb pressed hard against the notebook's spine. "And Joss Calloway, cabin boy, twelve years old. He hid in the coal bunker. They found him anyway."

She closed the notebook.

"For seven years I have not said these names aloud, because saying them felt like spending them, and I had nothing left to buy with. I ran instead. I ran up and down this river and I told myself that motion was mourning, that staying ahead of my warrant was a kind of memorial." She looked at the bench where Odile sat, and Odile's eyes were bright and furious and wet. "It was not. It was just running. These people were sold, magistrate — sold by a man who is sitting in this hall wearing a chain of office — and the least they are owed is to be spoken of in a place with marble in it, in a voice that does not shake. So. Bren Halloran. Sefa Idris. Anselm Voss. Piet Ammerling. Luce Ammerling. Denna Marsh. Joss Calloway. Let the record show they lived, and that they were betrayed, and that someone finally came back to say so."

She stepped back from the rail. Her legs felt strange, lighter than they should, as if some cargo she'd been hauling since Coldspring Bend had finally been set down on a dock that could hold it.

Corvel Sarn was still watching her. And now — only now, only for a moment — the merchant's courtesy dropped away, and what looked out of his face was the thing she had seen across the table at Tarn's Landing seven years ago. Not guilt. Appraisal. The look of a man recalculating.

Then he smiled, rose, adjusted his chain of office, and addressed the magistrate in a voice like warmed oil.

"Magistrate, I will waive my right to remain silent. I have nothing to hide from this tribunal. In fact—" he spread his hands, generous, wounded, magnificent, "—I welcome the opportunity to address these accusations personally. Tomorrow, if it pleases the court."

"It pleases the court," said Ivenna, in a tone that suggested it pleased her about as much as bilge water.

The gavel fell. The hall emptied slowly, the exiles in their faded blue filing out past Mara, and one old woman paused to touch her sleeve without a word, and Mara found she could not speak at all.

Behind her, Tomas said quietly: "He's going to testify."

"I heard."

"He wouldn't waive silence unless he had something. Sarn never spends unless he expects return."

Mara looked down the aisle to where Sarn had been, but he was already gone, swallowed by the doors, and outside the high windows the light was failing early, and far off toward the riverfront she could hear — faint, then less faint — the sound of voices rising, and a bell beginning to ring the alarm for fire.
