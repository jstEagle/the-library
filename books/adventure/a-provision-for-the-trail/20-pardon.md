## 20. Pardon

The tribunal hall at Vessel's Rest smelled of cold marble and lamp oil, and Mara Halloran stood in it for three hours without sitting down because no one had told her she could.

She had imagined this room for seven years — in the worst hours of night anchors, in the gray space between sleeping and waking when the river went quiet enough to hear her own name spoken in a dead man's voice. In her imaginings it had always been vast, echoing, full of robed figures whose faces she couldn't see. The reality was smaller. A chamber with a vaulted ceiling stained by an old roof leak, benches worn smooth by generations of petitioners, clerks moving between desks with the unhurried air of men who knew the season was ending and their work with it. The great seal of the restored Republic hung on the far wall — the twin oars crossed over the wave — but someone had hung it slightly crooked, and no one had bothered to straighten it.

Mara found that almost funny. Almost.

Tomas sat beside her on the petitioner's bench, his arm bound and slung, the knife wound in his shoulder dressed in clean linen that Odile had bullied out of a tribunal medic. He had gone pale twice during the walk up from the riverfront inn, and both times he had refused to stop, saying only, "I've waited longer than you have," which wasn't strictly true, but she hadn't argued. His color was better now. His eyes stayed fixed on the front of the chamber, where the chest sat on a table under the eye of two bailiffs who looked like they'd rather be anywhere else.

The chest itself looked smaller than it had any right to. Water-stained oak, iron bands rusted to lace, the Vessian seal cracked down its face where the flood had done half the tribunal's work already. It had been opened once more last night, under clerk's supervision, while Mara watched a young archivist copy out the dispatches in a hand so careful it seemed apologetic. She had read her own name again — *M. Halloran, master, vessel Kestrel* — written in ink that had outlived the nation that wrote it, and felt nothing she could describe. Not rage. Not grief. Something flatter and stranger, like looking at a chart of water she'd memorized long ago.

"Master Halloran."

The voice came from the bench at the front, and Mara realized with a small start that the proceedings had begun around her like weather forming. The presiding magistrate was a woman of perhaps sixty, gray-haired, with the kind of face that had stopped expressing things decades ago for professional reasons. Magistrate Ondrel. Mara had learned the name yesterday and forgotten it twice.

"That is you," Ondrel said. It wasn't quite a question.

"It is."

"You stand accused under warrant seven-four-one-one, issued by the Provisional Authority of Vessia in the last year of the old regime. The charge is smuggling of contraband materiel and conspiracy against the lawful government." The magistrate said it the way a person reads a shipping manifest. "You will understand that this tribunal does not retry the past. It reviews evidence as it bears on present law. Do you follow me?"

"I follow you."

"Then we'll proceed." Ondrel turned a page. Somewhere behind Mara, a door opened and closed, letting in a draft that smelled of the river — low and green and dropping, the dry season breathing down the valley. "Clerk Duvane has prepared a summary of the documentary evidence recovered from the sealed chest. Clerk Duvane."

Mara watched the clerk rise. This was the man Sarn's people had tried to reach first — the one whose signature, Tomas had explained in the dark of the inn, appeared on the routing slips of every order Corvel Sarn had ever laundered through the archive. Duvane was a narrow man with ink-stained fingers and the haunted look of someone who had spent the past week discovering what his own careful filing had been used for. He read for a long time. Names of vessels. Names of routes. Names of payments. When he reached the entry for the run that had killed her crew — the *Meridian*'s route, sold whole, priced per head like cargo — his voice did not waver, and Mara understood that he had made himself unable to waver, the way she had once made herself unable to look at certain stretches of river.

Then Tomas was called.

He stood slowly, favoring the shoulder, and gave his name and his old title — *junior archivist, third registry, Ministry of Rivers and Reaches* — in a voice so level that only Mara heard the effort underneath it. She had heard him testify yesterday too, in the closed session, when he had laid out the seal-work piece by piece: how Sarn's counter-seal mimicked the archive's authentication mark, how the forgery was good enough to fool any clerk alive except the one who had cut the original dies, how Tomas himself had processed eleven such orders in the final year and filed them without question because questioning was not survivable then. He had said that last part looking straight at the bench, and his voice had finally broken on the word *filed*, one crack, quickly mended, and the whole chamber had pretended not to notice because that was the merciful thing.

Today he was asked fewer questions. Today the questions were mostly about Mara.

"Were you aware of the sale of the Meridian's route?"

"No."

"Did you profit from any transaction described in these documents?"

"No."

"The warrant against you describes a conspiracy. Do you consider yourself a conspirator?"

Mara thought about the answer before giving it. The chamber waited. Outside, faintly, she could hear the river — the sound of it lower every day, pulling back from the levees like a tide going out for good.

"I consider myself," she said, "a captain who lost her crew to a man the government paid, and then spent seven years running from the government that paid him. If that's conspiracy, I'm guilty. If it isn't, I'd like my name back."

A murmur moved through the benches. Ondrel let it die on its own.

* * *

The verdict came after the midday recess, delivered in language so old-fashioned it might have been carved rather than spoken. Mara stood where she was told to stand, hands at her sides, and listened to the Republic of Vessia — restored, reformed, renamed twice in her absence — formally acknowledge that the warrant issued against her had been procured through corruption, that the underlying charges were void, and that she, Mara Halloran, was by this instrument fully pardoned, her record expunged, her rights of citizenship restored should she wish to claim them.

*Should she wish to claim them.* As if citizenship were a parcel left at a trading post, collectable at her convenience.

The clerk brought the document forward on a board. It was heavy paper, cream-colored, with a ribbon and a wax seal still warm from the press. Someone had written her name at the top in beautiful calligraphy. Someone had spent real time on the flourishes.

"Sign the receipt," Duvane murmured, sliding a pen toward her. "Not the pardon. The pardon is yours regardless. The receipt just says you took possession."

Mara signed. Her handwriting looked wrong on the page — a working hand among all that ornament, letters shaped by years of logbooks and bills of lading. She had signed for cargo ten thousand times. She had never signed for herself.

The bailiffs came for the chest while the ink was still wet. Two of them lifted it onto a wheeled cart with the practiced indifference of men handling anything that weighed enough to matter, and rolled it toward the records vault beneath the hall. Mara watched it go. Seven years of running, four days of walking with its contents strapped to her back through country where Sarn's men were hunting them, one knife wound, one grounded boat, one wreck walked in the flooded oxbow with her heart in her throat — and now the thing was property again, catalogued and shelved, going down into the dark to become paper among paper.

She found she was gripping the edge of the bench. She made herself let go.

"That's it?" Odile said quietly, at her other side. Odile had come in late and stood along the wall, arms crossed, wearing her shore clothes with the discomfort of a cat in rain. "That's the whole thing? They don't even say their sorry?"

"They said pardon," Mara said. "That's the shape their sorry comes in."

Odile snorted. But her eyes stayed on the cart until the vault door swallowed it, and her jaw worked once, twice, the way it did when she was chewing words she'd decided not to say. Finally she said, anyway: "Bren would've wanted to see it. Rell too. They'd have filled the back benches and heckled the magistrate."

Mara didn't answer. She had named them yesterday, all six of them, aloud, in this room, in a voice that had come out steadier than she deserved — Bren Oduya, Rell Vashti, old Sefa, the twins, little Anselm who'd been nineteen. She had named them and the names had gone into the record, into the permanent archive of the Republic, and there was a version of her that found that worth everything. That version was quiet right now. Right now she mostly felt the weight of the paper in her hand and the strange lightness underneath it, like a boat riding too high in the water, ballast gone.

Free. The word kept arriving and refusing to land.

* * *

They walked out into the afternoon together, the three of them, into a street that smelled of woodsmoke and drying mud. Vessel's Rest in the dry season was a city holding its breath — the river down to a brown ribbon between wide bars of exposed gravel, barges hauled out and propped, the riverfront taverns doing thin business off clerks and soldiers instead of boat crews. Scaffolding climbed the near tower of the tribunal hall; the Republic was forever restoring itself, plastering over the cracks of the old regime, and the scaffolds never quite came down before new ones went up elsewhere.

They ate at a riverside table outside a cookshop because Odile refused to sit indoors, and because Mara wanted, irrationally, to see the water while she held the pardon. The document lay on the table between the plates, weighted down with a salt cellar. Passersby glanced at the seal and looked away with the particular blankness of people who have learned not to be curious about official papers.

"So," Odile said, tearing bread. "Seven years I've watched you not stop anywhere. Now there's nowhere you can't stop." She chewed. Swallowed. "Where are we stopping?"

Mara looked at the river. It lay low and sluggish in its bed, the color of old tea, braid bars showing their spines where in spring the current ran twelve feet deep. Upstream, somewhere beyond the shoals and the derelict locks, the Kestrel sat where the dry season had caught her — hard aground on the falling bar below the second narrows, listed to port, her seams open, her bilge full of the last rain. Four days' walk away and a world away besides. The surveyor the tribunal had sent — because the boat had been entered into the record as evidence-adjacent property, because everything in Mara's life lately became paperwork eventually — had spent an hour aboard her two days ago and emerged shaking his head, using words like *hogged* and *unrecoverable* and *salvage value only.*

"She's not stopping anywhere," Mara said. "She's stuck."

"Mara—"

"Don't." It came out harder than she meant. She softened it with an exhale. "Don't make it a speech, Odile. I haven't got the shape for a speech today."

Odile held her gaze for a moment, then nodded slowly and went back to the bread, and Mara was grateful, because the truth was that she had spent the entire morning standing in a marble hall being given back her life and had discovered, somewhere between the signing and the vault door closing, that she didn't know what the life was for. For seven years the warrant had been the shape of everything. Every choice of channel, every refusal of a berth, every dawn departure — the warrant was the reason, and the reason had held the days together the way a keel holds a hull. Take it away and the days just drifted. She was a captain with no cargo contract, no crew beyond two, and a boat the surveyor had effectively pronounced dead.

Tomas had barely touched his food. He sat with his sling arm cradled, watching the river with an expression she'd learned to read over these weeks — the salvager's squint, measuring depths, estimating what the water would give back and when.

"The rains turn in six weeks," he said. "Maybe five. Early monsoon this year, if the birds are honest."

"The birds are liars," Odile said.

"The birds are more reliable than the surveyor." He glanced at Mara. "Hogged hulls come back. I've raised worse."

"She's not a wreck, Tomas. She's grounded on a bar that's growing every day. By the time the water comes back she'll have silted in to the gunwales."

"Then we dig her out before it comes back." He said *we* easily, without ceremony, the way he'd been saying it since the night run, and each time it landed in Mara's chest like a stone dropped into a well she couldn't see the bottom of. "Six weeks is enough. Shoring, pumps, caulk. I know a lock-keeper downstream who owes me and owns a windlass."

"With what money?" Odile asked bluntly. "Patching her cost you your last season's take. You told me so yourself, drunk, at Greyharbor, which I'm sure you remember fondly."

Tomas smiled faintly and didn't answer, and something in the way he didn't answer made Mara look at him more closely. He turned his cup in his good hand, watching the river, and said nothing about money at all.

A boy came down the street then, weaving between tables with a folded paper in his fist, calling a name — not hers, thank the drowned gods, some merchant's — and the ordinary commerce of the town resumed around them. But the sight of the running boy put a taste in Mara's mouth she couldn't wash out. Somewhere south, Corvel Sarn was running too, or hiding, or negotiating, and the tribunal's writ ended at the city stones. They had taken his counter-seal apart in open session. They had stripped his contracts. They had not, in any way that mattered to the river, caught him. Justice, it turned out, was a thing you served imperfectly and then walked home from, and the walking home was the part nobody wrote ballads about.

Magistrate Ondrel had said one thing at the end, after the formalities, in a voice pitched only for the petitioner's bench. *The Republic owes you more than paper, Captain. Paper is what we have. Spend it wisely.*

Mara looked down at the pardon, at her name in its beautiful calligraphy, and wondered what exactly it purchased. Passage anywhere. Berth anywhere. A life that could hold still.

And the Kestrel dying on a bar four days upstream, and six names in a vault, and a traitor loose in the world, and a partner with a ruined shoulder and a secret arithmetic behind his eyes, and Odile watching her across the table with the patient ferocity of a woman waiting for her captain to give the next order.

"Well?" Odile said at last. "We're free. First free decision of your adult life, Captain. Make it a good one."

Mara opened her mouth — and stopped, because Tomas had gone very still, his gaze fixed on something down the street, and the look on his face was the look of a man reading a document he had hoped never to see again.

Down by the landing stages, nailed to the harbor-master's post where the salvage notices went, a fresh paper fluttered in the dry-season wind. Even at this distance Mara could make out the heading, because salvage notices were printed large: *VESSEL DECLARED LOST — OFFERED AT AUCTION.*

Below it, in smaller type, the lot number, and the name.

*Kestrel.*
