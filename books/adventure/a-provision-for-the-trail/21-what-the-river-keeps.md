## 21. What the River Keeps

The clerk at the salvage office in Vessel's Rest had a voice like dry paper and a stamp he clearly loved more than most men love their children.

"Declared lost," he read, tapping the ledger. "Grounded on the Serrin Bar, fourteenth of the falling season. Owner failed to claim within the statutory period." He looked up over his spectacles at Tomas, and then, past Tomas's shoulder, at the window, where the river lay low and brown and patient in the autumn light. "You understand she's salvage now. Salvage price. Which means the tribunal washes its hands, and whatever she becomes is your trouble."

"That's the idea," Tomas said.

He counted out the pension in coin because the clerk did not trust paper, and Tomas understood that too — paper was what the old regime had run on, paper and seals and signatures, and everyone in Vessel's Rest had learned in the last seven years what paper was worth when the water came up. The coins made a small, honest sound on the counter. The clerk stamped the bill of sale with the full weight of his affection, and the Kestrel, one hundred and twelve feet of grounded oak and iron, became Tomas Vey's trouble for the price of three years' pension and a ferry ride downstream.

He stood on the levee afterward and looked at her.

She lay heeled over on the falling bar like a woman asleep on her side, mud to the second strake, her rail listing toward the channel as if leaning in to listen to the water that had abandoned her. Someone — Odile, he guessed, or Mara before she'd walked upstream and given the tribunal her name — had tied a lantern to the stub of the foremast, and it swung in the wind with nothing left to light. Gulls had claimed the pilothouse roof. The hull was scarred where the bar had held her, and there was a long gray bruise along her starboard side where the shoal had ground her during the night run, and her name was all but gone from the bow, worn to a ghost of paint that you had to know was there to see.

*Declared lost.* Tomas had spent eleven years in the Vessian archives, and he had learned that the phrase meant nothing. The archives were full of declared losses — ships, men, whole provinces, the Republic itself, declared dissolved on the ninth of Thawmonth by men who were already running. The river did not read the ledgers. The river kept what it kept, and gave back what it chose, and the Kestrel was still here, still holding her shape, still refusing, with the mute obstinacy of good timber, to be finished.

He walked out across the bar at low water and put his hand flat against her hull, below the waterline, where the mud was cold. The oak was sound. He could feel it, the way you could feel a book's spine and know whether it had been cared for. Someone had cared for this boat for a long time. Someone had run her hard and patched her mean and loved her without ever saying so, and the love was in the wood the way a reader's fingerprints were in a well-thumbed page.

"Salvage price," he said to her, and the gulls objected, and he took that as consent.

* * *

Odile arrived the next morning with a mule, two barrels of pitch, a bundle of caulking irons wrapped in oilcloth, and the expression of a woman who had been arguing with herself for four days and had lost.

"You're a fool," she said, from the levee. "You know that. You paid pension money for a boat with a split garboard and no engine worth the name, and the woman who owns her heart — " she stopped, recalibrated, started again — "the woman who *loved* her is up at Vessel's Rest testifying in front of marble, and when she comes back down that river she is going to find some archivist squatting on her deck like a heron, and she is going to put you over the side."

"Probably," Tomas agreed.

"So why?"

He considered the question while he coiled the towline the ferryman had thrown him. There were true answers and there were useful ones, and after the last two months he had developed a professional distaste for anything less than the whole truth. "Because I processed the orders that put her crew on the Meridian," he said. "Because I filed them, sealed them, and went home to my supper. Because a man can't caulk over that. But a man can caulk a boat." He shrugged. "It isn't enough. It's what I have."

Odile looked at him for a long moment. The wind came off the river and moved her hair, and somewhere down the bar a heron — the actual bird — lifted off on slow wings.

"Well," she said finally. "You're still a fool. But you're the useful kind." She swung her pack down off her shoulder and handed him the caulking irons. "Muck out the bilge first. I'm not driving oakum into a seam full of last season's river."

* * *

The work was the work, and the work did not care about grief or guilt or the fall of nations, and that was, Tomas came to understand, the whole secret of it.

They mucked the bilge for three days. It was not work for the squeamish: the Kestrel's belly had taken in the Sable through the split seam and held it, and what the flood had left behind was a black, breathing silt that smelled of drowned reeds and old diesel and something faintly, stubbornly sweet — resin, maybe, from the cargo of the last honest run. Odile worked the pump and the bucket and sang Vessian work songs under her breath in a voice that would have gotten her thrown out of any choir in the Republic, and Tomas crawled the length of the keel with a lantern and a scraper, cataloguing the damage the way he had once catalogued damage of another kind.

The split garboard was worse than the survey said. Eight feet of seam, sprung along a scar where she'd hit something in the debris-choked water — a fence post, a drowned tree, some farmer's whole life come down the river in the flood. But the frames around it were sound, and the planking on either side was sound, and Odile, lying on her back in six inches of muck with her arms raised, pronounced judgment like a magistrate.

"She can be saved. Barely. By us. If the dry season holds." She wiped her face and left a stripe of black across her forehead that she would wear, Tomas suspected, until the boat was done with them. "Which it won't. So we don't stop."

They did not stop. The dry season settled over the Sable country like a hand pressing down — hard blue mornings, dust gold in the light, the river dropping a hand's width every day, exposing sandbars that gleamed like the backs of sleeping animals. The mosquitoes died off. The heat came at noon and left by four. And every morning Tomas was on the bar at first light with the pitch kettle going, and every evening he fell asleep in the pilothouse bunk — Mara's bunk, though he did not let himself think of it that way, though the blanket still smelled faintly of her pipe tobacco, though he lay awake the first week listening to the river tick against the hull and understood that he was sleeping in a dead woman's bed in every sense except the one that mattered, because she was alive, she was testifying, she had walked into the marble hall with a chest full of names and refused to flinch.

He had seen her do it. That was the thing he carried now, heavier than the guilt, heavier than the knife scar on his ribs where Sarn's man had caught him on the overland walk: he had stood in that hall and watched Mara Halloran say her dead crew's names out loud, one by one, in a voice like a hull taking water — steady, and going down, and not stopping — and he had understood that he would spend the rest of his life trying to be worth the silence he had broken.

The caulking was a kind of prayer. He did not have another word for it.

* * *

The seam took oakum and pitch and patience. Week one, they drove the loose caulking and found the rot behind it — two strakes soft at the butt ends, and Odile cursed the original shipwright with a fluency that suggested a personal history. Week two, a timber man from the ferry landing brought white oak upriver on a barge, and they steamed the new strakes over a fire of driftwood in the manner of shipwrights a hundred years dead, because there was no sawmill within a week's walk and no money for one anyway. The steam smelled of the forest. The wood bent. Tomas held it against the frames while Odile fastened it, and the boat took the new timber into herself the way a body takes a graft, and on the third day of that week Tomas dreamed, for the first time since the tribunal, of the archive.

In the dream he was at his old desk, and the orders were in front of him, and the seal was unbroken, and he was holding the knife — the archivist's letter knife, bone-handled, for slitting dispatch covers — and he could not make himself cut the seal. And then the desk was a hull plank, and the seal was a seam, and he was driving oakum into it with a mallet and the iron rang, rang, rang, and he woke in the pilothouse with the mallet's rhythm still in his ears and the real river ticking against the real hull, and he lay there and laughed until Odile banged on the deck below and told him to sleep or work.

Week three, they re-caulked the whole starboard run, seam by seam, and the boat began to smell of pitch instead of mud. Week four, Odile went upriver to Greyharbor for fastenings and came back with copper nails, a new lantern for the foremast, and news.

"Sarn's gone to ground," she said, sitting on the rail with her boots swinging, passing down a bag of oranges like a peace offering, which after four weeks of bilge and beans it absolutely was. "Nobody's seen him since the fire. The tribunal's posted a warrant — a real one, this time, with a face on it and everything." She peeled an orange with her thumbnail and handed him half. "Half the river thinks he's dead. Other half thinks he bought his way onto a boat going south. Both halves are buying drinks over it."

"And Mara?"

Odile was quiet a moment, looking down the river toward Vessel's Rest, though it was a hundred miles off and below the curve of the world. "Still there. Helping the clerk rebuild the records the fire ate. She says — " Odile stopped, and started again, and Tomas heard in the restart the sound of a woman choosing honesty, which he was learning to recognize the way he'd once learned to recognize forged seals. "She says she's not coming back until the boat floats. Her words. She said, *tell him if she's not seaworthy, I'm not setting foot on her, and tell him I'll know.*"

"I'll know," Tomas repeated.

"That's what she said."

He looked down the length of the Kestrel, heeled in the mud with her new strakes pale against the old, and felt something that was not quite fear and not quite hope and was probably, he decided, the same thing under two names.

"Then we'd better make her seaworthy," he said.

* * *

Week five, they righted her.

It took the ferry landing's whole population, two mules, a block and tackle, and a full spring tide — the last high water of the dry season, when the river, though low, remembered itself for an afternoon. Odile organized the levee men with the authority of someone who had been buying their drinks all season, and at the top of the tide they hauled on the tackles and the Kestrel groaned — a long, articulate sound, wood and iron complaining in a language Tomas was by then nearly fluent in — and came off the bar by inches, and then by feet, and then all at once, with a suck of mud and a slap of water, floated.

The levee men cheered. Odile whooped. Tomas stood knee-deep at the bow line with the rope burning his palms and did not cheer, because his throat had closed, because the boat was afloat and light on the water, riding up to her marks, and the river came back around her hull and touched her everywhere at once, the way water forgives, and he stood there with his mouth open like a man at a funeral who has seen the coffin breathe.

She took water, that first hour. Of course she did. Two seams wept, slow as a candle, and Odile found them by lantern light with her ear against the planking like a physician, and they drove them tight over the next three days, and on the fourth day the bilge was dry at dawn, and Odile stood in it and pronounced the word they had both been not saying for five weeks.

"Tight," she said. "She's tight."

They pumped her out, scrubbed her decks, and rehung the rudder, which had survived the grounding better than anyone deserved. Tomas repainted the name on the bow. He did it at dusk, by lantern light, because he wanted to do it alone, and he worked the letters slowly — *K* and *E* and the rest — in white over the ghost of the old paint, and when he was done he sat on the bow chock with the brush still in his hand and the letters drying behind him, and the river ran dark and quiet past the hull, and he thought about what the river kept.

It kept the flood and the drought both. It kept the Meridian and everything the Meridian meant. It kept, somewhere in its long gray memory, a chest that had surfaced after seven years to put a traitor's name in front of a tribunal. It kept the names of drowned crews, whether anyone said them aloud or not — that was what he had learned in the archive, and unlearned, and learned again: the record exists whether or not anyone reads it, and reading it is the only thing that makes it matter. The river had read them. The river had kept them. And now it had given the boat back, patched and paid for, floating at her mooring with her name fresh and her bilge dry, and the only thing the river had not yet given back was the woman who belonged to her.

Week six, the rains came early.

They came at night, at first — a warm wind out of the south, a smell of green and iron, and then, an hour past midnight, the sound on the pilothouse roof that Tomas had not heard since the monsoon: rain, real rain, the river's own weather coming home. He was up and out on deck before he was fully awake, standing in it with his face turned up, and Odile came out too, and they stood in the dark and the downpour and watched the river rise.

It rose fast. It always did, this time of year — the upcountry rains coming down all at once, the Sable shrugging off its dry-season shyness, lifting the Kestrel on her mooring lines inch by inch, and the sound of the water against the hull changed, deepened, went from a tick to a voice.

"She'll float free by morning," Odile said.

"She floats now."

"You know what I mean." Odile pushed her wet hair out of her eyes and looked at him. In the lantern light her face was unreadable and then, deliberately, readable. "She'll come downriver as soon as the channel's safe. Week, maybe less. And she's going to stand on that levee and look at her boat, and you're going to be standing on it, and I have spent six weeks listening to you not talk about that, and I'm telling you now: figure out what you're going to say to her."

Tomas looked down the river, black and swollen and running hard, carrying the first of the season's debris past the mooring — branches, leaves, a fence post, some farmer's whole life on its way to the sea. The rain came down. The boat rose.

"I'm going to say the bilge is dry," he said.

Odile snorted. "That's not — "

"And that the name's repainted. And that she can have the boat back whenever she wants it." He turned the lantern down, and the rain filled the silence, and he found that the rest came easily, the way the truth did now, when he let it. "And that I'm not squatting. That I bought her because I couldn't fix what I broke, and a boat is a thing I know how to fix, and — and that if she wants me gone at Greyharbor or anywhere else, I'll go. But I'd rather stay. I'd rather crew for her. I'd rather spend whatever years I've got left learning to read this river the way she reads it, because she reads it better than anyone I've ever — " He stopped. The rain drummed. Somewhere upriver, thunder said its one word.

Odile was grinning at him through the wet. "You rehearsed that."

"Every night for six weeks."

"It's terrible."

"It's honest."

"It's both," she said, and clapped him on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him, and went below, and Tomas stood alone in the rain on the deck of a boat the ledgers had declared lost, listening to the river rise.

By dawn the mooring lines were humming, the Kestrel rode light and eager at her moorings, and far down the swollen channel, around the bend where the Sable came down from Vessel's Rest, a shape was moving against the current — small yet, no more than a fleck of white in the gray morning, but coming on, upstream against the freshet, the way nothing came upstream unless it meant to.
