## 22. A Provision for the Trail

The rain came back the way it always did on the Sable — not all at once but in pieces, a whisper in the high country first, then a rumor in the tributaries, then a gray weight settling over everything like a hand laid gently on the shoulder of the world. Mara Halloran stood at the rail of the *Kestrel* and let it land on her face, drop by drop, warm as breath.

The river was rising. She could hear it doing it.

Seven years she had listened to that sound as a warning. The swell of the Sable meant debris, meant snags and drowned fences and whole trees traveling with their roots still clutching the banks they'd been torn from. Rising water meant danger, meant work, meant the constant arithmetic of a captain who could never afford to be wrong. But this morning, moored at the new dock below Vessel's Rest, with the tribunal's marble halls gleaming wet up the hill behind her and a pardon folded in oilcloth inside her coat, the sound was only a sound. Water coming home to water. The river filling its own long throat and saying, in the language it had spoken since before there were names on any of it: *I am not finished yet.*

"You're going to rust if you keep standing out there," Odile said from the deckhouse door, a mug of chicory steaming in her fist. "Boat too."

"The boat doesn't rust. She's wood and stubbornness."

"Like her captain." Odile held out the mug. Mara took it. The coffee was terrible, overboiled, made from grounds already used once — Odile's economy had survived the winter intact even if nothing else about her had softened much. Her hair was shorter now, hacked off with a belt knife during the worst week of the walk upstream, and it made her look younger and older at once, the way grief does when it finally stops hiding.

Below them, between the hull and the pilings, the water slid past green-brown and muscular, carrying leaves, carrying sticks, carrying — Mara watched one pass — a small wooden bird, carved and painted blue, riding the current with perfect indifference to everything it had ever meant to anyone. It went under the dock and out the other side and was gone downstream, toward Greyharbor, toward the sea, toward whatever waited at the end of rivers that wasn't waiting at all.

"Six weeks," Odile said. "Six weeks we scraped and caulked and cursed, and you know what I kept thinking? I kept thinking, this is the first time in seven years I've worked on this boat without being afraid someone's watching us do it."

Mara drank her terrible coffee. "And now?"

"Now I keep looking over my shoulder anyway. Habit's got deeper roots than fear." Odile shrugged. "It'll take time."

"That's all right," Mara said, and heard, with a small private astonishment, how true it was. "We've got time."

* * *

They provisioned through the morning while the rain thickened and thinned like a sleeper's breathing.

This was ritual, and Mara honored it as ritual. Hardtack from the factor who'd stopped charging her the stranger's price sometime around the third week of the trial. Salt pork in a firkin, waxed paper over the top. Rice in oilcloth sacks, doubled against damp. A coil of new tarred line, bright as something just born. Lamp oil, pitch, a spare bilge pump — Tomas had insisted, and she'd let him insist, because a man who'd spent his life pulling things out of rivers knew better than anyone what rivers liked to put into boats.

Tomas worked beside her in the drizzle, checking each item off a list he'd written in the small precise hand of a former archivist, and every so often Mara caught him pausing over an entry not because he'd forgotten it but because he seemed to want to feel the weight of it — the fact of provisioning, the plain animal satisfaction of preparing for a journey you intended to survive.

"You're smiling at the rice," she said.

"I'm smiling at the manifest." He tapped the page. "Do you know how many manifests I processed in eleven years at the archive? Thousands. Tens of thousands. Barrels of tallow, crates of nails, bales of hemp — and every line item a small lie or a small truth, and me stamping them all the same, *received, received, received.*" He looked up at her, rain running off the brim of his hat. "This is the first one I've written where every word is mine and every word is honest. It feels — I don't have the word."

"Provisional," Mara said.

He laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, the kind she'd learned over the winter that he rationed like everything else he owned. "Provisional. Yes. Everything worth having is."

They stowed together, and the boat took their offerings the way she always had, down into her hold, into the dark places that smelled of oakum and old journeys. Mara ran her palm along the new caulking as she worked, seam by seam, feeling the smooth ridges where Tomas and Odile had driven oakum and poured pitch, six weeks of their hands living in the grain of her planking. The *Kestrel* had been declared lost. Salvage price, the harbor-master had said, shaking his head at the grounded hulk on the falling bar, half her stern buried in sand, her name-boards bleached blind. A dead boat. And then three people had come down the bank with scrapers and mauls and a caulking iron worn smooth by other hands before theirs, and the river had risen, and the dead boat had lifted on the first good swell like a sleeper turning over, and floated.

Mara had cried, a little, standing waist-deep in the floodwater holding the bow line. She didn't mention it. Neither had Odile, who'd been standing next to her crying considerably more.

* * *

In the afternoon the rain settled in earnest, a soft steady fall that turned the dock boards silver and sent the gulls inland to sulk under the warehouse eaves. The traffic on the river picked up with the rising water — barges from the upper settlements, a log raft riding low and patient, a mail skiff beating upstream under a patched lugsail. Somewhere north, beyond the headwaters, the drowned Republic's old borders were dissolving into something newer and clumsier and, everyone hoped, kinder. Downstream, Greyharbor was rebuilding its burned riverfront plank by plank, and the harbor-masters there had sent word — actual formal word, under seal — that Captain Halloran's berth fees for life were waived, with apologies.

Apologies. From institutions. Mara turned the word over in her mind like a coin she suspected of being counterfeit, and found, to her surprise, that she believed it.

She went below to the aft cabin, her cabin, the smallest room on the boat and the only one that had ever been entirely hers, and stood looking at the trunk in the corner.

It had ridden with her seven years and never been opened. That was the arrangement she'd made with herself back at the beginning, in the first raw weeks after the warrant, after the Meridian, after the funerals she'd attended alone in a churchyard above Greyharbor with the hunters' boats passing on the river below: *You can carry it, but you can't open it.* The trunk held everything she'd taken from the house in Vessia's capital the night she fled — her mother's shawl, a stack of letters tied in ribbon, a dress she'd worn to a celebration that turned into a wake, a tin of tea gone stale as dust, a carved comb her sister had given her the last birthday either of them had believed in birthdays. Seven years of cargo, and she'd carried it through every lock and shoal and customs inspection without once lifting the lid, because opening it would mean admitting she had a past, and having a past meant having an address, and having an address meant the hunters would find her, and beyond all of that — beneath all of that, truer than any of it — opening it would mean the people in those letters were really gone.

She'd opened it once since. In the oxbow, the night after she walked the wreck of the Meridian and pried loose the nameplate with her crew's names, she'd sat in the cabin with the lantern turned low and lifted the lid, and taken out only one thing: the letters. She'd read them by lamplight while the rain came down on the flooded oxbow and the dead boat groaned beside them, and she'd read her sister's handwriting until she could hear the voice behind it again, and then she'd put them back and closed the lid, and slept better than she had in years.

Now she knelt and opened it again, fully, in daylight.

The smell rose up out of it — cedar and camphor and time — and it hit her like weather, like a season she'd forgotten existed. She took things out one at a time and set them on the bunk. The shawl, soft as smoke. The dress, absurdly fine, moth-nibbled at the hem. The comb. The tea; she'd brew it anyway, stale or no, tomorrow morning, and drink it on deck, and it would taste like a kitchen she could almost see. At the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp all these years without her quite intending it, a small frame: a sketch of the *Kestrel* herself, done by young Ferro Rask the summer before the end, the boat running the Narrows with spray coming off her bow, drawn in charcoal on the back of a freight manifest. Everyone in the sketch was laughing. You couldn't see faces, but you could see it — the lean of the hull, the flung lines, the whole composition drunk with speed and joy.

Mara sat on the bunk with the sketch in her lap for a long time, listening to the rain.

Then she got up, found a nail, and hung it on the cabin wall where she'd see it every morning.

* * *

The room was on Rope Walk, above a chandler's shop, third floor, two windows facing the river.

She'd walked past it a dozen times during the six weeks of repairs, telling herself she was only noticing it the way a captain notices everything along a waterfront — professionally, idly, the way you note a mooring buoy or a snag. Two windows facing the water. A stair outside, so she wouldn't have to go through the shop at odd hours. An old woman named Mistress Quennel who owned the building and asked exactly one question about prospective tenants, which was whether they paid on time.

"It's not a house," Mara told Odile that evening, standing at the foot of the outside stair in the last gray light, the key cold in her fist. "It's four walls and a roof. It's a place to keep the trunk."

"The trunk's on the boat."

"The *other* trunk. The one I haven't bought yet. The one with things in it that aren't survival." She heard herself and stopped, and looked up the stair, and the two windows looked back at her, lamplit now, empty, patient. "Seven years, Odile. Do you know what I've done with every port? I've provisioned and I've left. Before dawn, usually. Before anyone could learn my face well enough to love it or sell it. Stopping cost everything. That was the rule. Stopping meant roots, roots meant records, records meant the hunters at your hatch at midnight."

"And now?"

"And now the tribunal says the warrant's ash. And now Sarn is running south with nothing but his name and whatever gold he could carry, and every port on this river knows his face instead of mine." She laughed, short and disbelieving. "He traded places with me. All those years I dreamed of putting him in my skin — the running, the looking over the shoulder — and the tribunal did it with a piece of paper."

Odile leaned against the stair rail. Below them the river murmured against the pilings, higher tonight than this morning, the rise gathering strength somewhere far upstream in the dark. "So are you taking it? The room?"

Mara climbed three steps. Stopped. Climbed three more.

"I'm taking it," she said, "and I'm leaving the boat at the dock, and I'm sleeping ashore tonight, and tomorrow morning I'm going to drink terrible stale tea on that balcony and watch the river come up, and then—" she reached the landing, fitted the key, felt the lock turn under her hand with a small clean click that sounded, absurdly, like a verdict — "and then we cast off and run cargo like ordinary people. Because the river will still be here tomorrow. That's the whole discovery, Odile. That's the thing I couldn't learn in seven years of running. The river will still be here tomorrow. It always is. That's what a river *is.*"

The room smelled of cedar shavings and lamp oil and someone else's long ago cooking. It was small. Through the two windows the water lay dimming under the rain, and the *Kestrel* rode at her mooring below, lamplit at the ports, her refitted hull sitting high and easy on the swell, and Mara stood at the window a long while with her hand flat against the cool glass, watching her boat watch the river.

* * *

Dawn came up rinsed and gold, the rain gone east overnight, the sky scrubbed to a hard clean blue that made the wet rooftops shine like scales.

Mara brewed the stale tea in Mistress Quennel's dented kettle and carried it out to the balcony, and it tasted exactly like the kitchen she'd almost remembered, which nearly undid her, and she let it nearly undo her, standing in the sun with steam on her face, and then it didn't undo her at all. Below, Odile was already on deck, coiling line with the fierce economy of a woman who'd decided mornings were not going to defeat her. Tomas came up the dock with bread and a sack of oranges and a newspaper from Vessel's Rest folded under his arm, and even at this distance she could see him reading it as he walked, hunting the shipping columns, the tribunal notices, the small print of the world they'd helped turn right-side-up.

There was a notice in it, he told her later, about a trader named Corvel Sarn, sighted south of the border towns, traveling under another name, buying passage on boats that didn't ask questions. There would be others like it, year after year, notices and rumors, a man growing grayer and smaller in the world's memory. Mara read the paragraph twice and handed the paper back.

"He'll get old," she said. "That's all. He'll get old somewhere warm and nobody will ever buy loyalty from him again, because everyone finally knows what it costs." She looked at the river. "I used to think I wanted him dragged back in chains. Now I think the chains were the cheap part. The expensive part was making sure the record says what happened. That part's done. Let him run."

At midmorning they loaded the last of it — the hardtack, the pork, the rice, the oil, the spare pump, a crate of nails, a bolt of canvas, and, wrapped separately and stowed in the aft cabin beneath the sketch of the boat running the Narrows, the chest's emptied shell itself, which the tribunal had released to her with a receipt and a dry remark that she might as well have it, since she was the only person alive who'd know what to do with a box that famous. Mara meant to use it for tools.

Odile cast off the bow line. Tomas took the stern. Mara stood at the wheel with the current already leaning on the hull, eager, insistent, the whole risen river shouldering past the docks toward the sea, and she gave the *Kestrel* a touch of helm and felt her answer — quick, alive, glad — and steered out into the channel.

Downstream lay Greyharbor and its new planks, and the delta, and the sea. Upstream lay the Narrows and the high country and the small hard landings where people paid in eggs and news. The whole Sable ran shining in both directions, and for the first time in seven years neither direction was away from anything.

"Where first?" Odile called.

Mara thought about it properly, hands light on the wheel, wind finding the wet flag at the masthead. Then she grinned, and it felt strange on her face, and then it felt like the oldest thing she owned.

"Up," she said. "We'll start by going home the long way."
