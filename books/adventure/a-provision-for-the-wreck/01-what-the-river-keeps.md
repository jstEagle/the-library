## 1. What the River Keeps

The Vell came down loud that spring, the way it always did when the high snow gave up all at once. From the boathouse porch I could hear it before I saw it—a low, grinding roar, like a millstone turning somewhere under the world. By midmorning the water had climbed over the second stair of the landing and was worrying at the pilings with brown, muscular hands, carrying whole trees sideways, a fence gate, half a roof of pale blue shingle that some family upriver would be mourning by now.

I stood on the porch with a mug of barley tea going cold in my hands and watched the river work, and I thought, as I did every spring: *this year it will not give anything back.*

The river had given me my living once, or taken it—depending on how you kept your accounts. Eight years since I'd last been Cyrus Amberdale anywhere but inside my own skull, where the name sat like a stone I'd swallowed and never passed. Here in Vell's Landing I was Maren Vale, boatwright and occasional ferryman, a woman of no particular history who mended nets for the fisherfolk and asked no questions and was asked none. The town liked me fine on those terms. Invisibility, I had learned, was less a cloak than a discipline. You practiced it daily, the way a climber practices knots—until the fingers know the work without the mind.

The scream came out of the fog around the middle bend just after noon.

I heard it twice before I understood it—not a bird, not a branch. A person. I dropped the mug, took the gaff hook off its pegs, and went down the landing at a run, boots skidding on wet planks, the floodwater already lapping at my knees where the stairs used to be.

She came around the bend tangled in the top of a drowned alder, arms hooked over the trunk like a woman clinging to the neck of a beast that meant to drown her. Gray water broke over her back in sheets. Her coat had gone heavy as armor; every time the current rolled the tree, she went under and came up slower.

I got the skiff out through the side door of the boathouse, which is why I'd built the side door, and rowed into the flood with my shoulders burning and the gaff across my thighs. The current took the bow and tried to keep it. I let it carry me wide, then dug in crosswise and swung us alongside the alder.

"Give me your hand," I shouted. "Not the tree. Me."

She looked at me with eyes gone white-rimmed with cold, and for one long moment she did nothing at all—the particular paralysis of the nearly dead—and then her hand shot out and closed on my wrist with a grip that had nothing left in it but refusal. I hauled. She came over the gunwale in a rush of river, a tangle of limbs and soaked wool, and we both went down into the bilge together while the skiff spun lazily in the eddy behind the alder.

She was young—younger than me by ten years, maybe more—with dark hair plastered to her cheeks and lips gone the color of slate. A traveler's coat, good quality once, city-made. Not a local. Not a fisherman. Nobody from Vell's Landing had any business being in the flood at all.

"Boathouse," I said, when she'd coughed up half the Vell. "Can you hold a bucket?"

She could. We bailed ourselves back across the current, and I got her stripped of the coat and wrapped in the wool blanket I kept for exactly this, though in eight years I had never once needed it. I built up the little stove in the corner and put tea in her hands and watched her shake.

"Name?" I asked.

"Ilsa." Her voice was raw gravel. "Ilsa Reyne."

"Reyne. What were you doing in the river?"

"Crossing at the old ford." She said it without flinching, which was either honesty or very good practice. "The ferryman wouldn't take me. The water was lower this morning."

"The water is never lower in spring. That's not how rivers work."

"No," she said, and looked into her tea. "I suppose it isn't."

I should have turned her out then, dried and fed and pointed toward the inn with a warning about fools and fords. Instead I found myself studying her—the way she catalogued the boathouse without seeming to, her eyes ticking from the tool rack to the loft ladder to the locked chest-bench under the window where I kept my gear. I knew that look. I had worn that look, once, on mountains, reading clients to see which of them would panic at altitude and which would simply lie about it afterward.

*Later,* I told myself. *Deal with her later.*

* * *

It was the dog that found the chest—or what served this town for a dog, a brindled river-terrier belonging to the chandlery, who came down the bank at dusk barking his fool head off at something wedged in the shallows below the willows.

I went down with a lantern because the terrier's owner was lame and someone had to. The flood had begun its retreat, dropping a hand's width since noon, and it had left its leavings strung along the mud like a tide line: branches, reeds, a dead ewe, a barrel stave. And there, snagged in the root-fan of the willow, half-buried in silt, an iron-bound chest the size of a sea chest, its lid still shut.

I crouched over it for a long moment, telling myself it was salvage. Salvage law in Vell's Landing was simple: the river gives, the finder keeps, minus a tithe to the chandlery if the chandler's dog does the finding. I had pulled drowned cargo out of this river three springs running—bolts of cloth, a crate of candles, once a strongbox full of somebody's ruined letters. This was nothing new. This was Maren Vale's ordinary business.

But even before I waded in and set my shoulder against it, even before the terrier stopped barking and went suddenly, strangely quiet, some animal part of me had already read the shape of the thing and disliked it. It rode too deep in the water. It was built too well. Iron corners, iron straps, a lock plate with no keyhole rusted shut but *sealed*—waxed, I realized when I got it onto the bank and scraped the mud away. Someone had sealed this chest deliberately, the way you seal a thing you mean to survive a long journey or a long burial.

And stamped into the wax, pressed deep and perfect, protected all these years under whatever dark water it had traveled:

A mountain peak above a broken crown.

I did not move for what felt like a full minute. The lantern hissed. The river muttered against the bank, patient as a creditor.

I knew that crest. You do not forget a thing like that, not if you were there when the nation under it died—not in fire, not in war, but in paperwork, in red ink, in the quiet stroke of cartographers' pens that erased Karveth from every map between the Serpent's Spine and the coast. Twenty years gone, and the world had agreed so thoroughly to forget it that most people under thirty had never heard the name. But I had been twenty-four years old and hungry for glory when I signed on to guide the Karveth Recovery Expedition into the high passes, looking for whatever the dead country had left behind in its fortresses and its archives. And I had come down alone, with three clients dead in a storm that no forecast ever recorded, and my name blackened from border to border, and the passes sealed by decree against anyone foolish enough to ask why.

That seal had been on the expedition's document cases. Every one of them. Peak above broken crown, pressed in gray wax.

My hands were shaking. I made them stop, the way I'd taught them to on ice slopes—*not now, not yet, do the work first*—and I pried at the lid until the wax crumbled and the hasps gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age but dry as bone: papers. Dozens of them. Survey charts in a hand I recognized before I could stop myself from recognizing it—the beautiful, cramped drafting of the expedition's cartographer, a quiet man named Reyne who had walked into the mountains and never walked out. Route maps of the Karveth passes. Supply manifests. And beneath them, in a folder tied with government cord, documents bearing stamps and signatures I had only ever seen in nightmares: requisitions, orders, correspondence between men whose names meant nothing to the world and everything to me.

I did not read them there on the bank. Some instinct older than sense told me not to. I wrapped the oilcloth back over the papers, closed the chest, and carried it up to the boathouse through the blue dark with the terrier trotting at my heel, and only when the door was barred and the lamp trimmed did I sit down on the floor with the folder in my lap and open it properly.

The storm that killed my clients had been bought. Ordered. Scheduled. There was a ledger entry for it—*weather provision, Serpent's Spine, itemized*—paid to a syndicate of river-and-road men whose name I had heard whispered in dockside taverns for years without understanding what they sold. They sold weather. They sold washed-out bridges and missing forecasts and guides who took the blame. The disaster that ended my life had been a line item, budgeted like feed for porters, arranged so that no one would ever reach what the original expedition had found in the high passes—and so that no one would ever ask what happened to the first ones who tried.

*A provision for the wreck.* That was the phrase, in one letter, in a clerk's tidy hand. As if my three dead clients were a wreck to be provided for. As if I were.

I sat on the floor of my own boathouse until the candle burned down to a puddle, and somewhere in that time I stopped being Maren Vale. I could feel it happening, the alias sloughing off like a rope burned through. Eight years of careful invisibility, undone by a box the river decided to give back.

Because here was the arithmetic I could not escape, sitting in the dark with the papers in my lap: chests like this did not float loose. Sealed chests with government cord and syndicate ledgers inside did not wash down the Vell by accident. Someone had put this in the water—upstream, recently, in a hurry—or lost it, and either way, someone would come looking for it. Men who sealed things in wax and paid for storms were not men who accepted loss gracefully.

And upstairs in my loft, sleeping under my spare blanket with her hair drying in dark waves, was a woman who had lied to me about the ford, who read my boathouse like a surveyor reads terrain, and whose surname was the same as a dead cartographer's.

I banked the stove. I did not sleep. Near dawn I took the papers out of the chest and sewed them into the heart of a coil of climbing rope, the way I'd once sewn lead shot into sledge runners, and I put the empty chest back where a searching eye would find it, and I told myself I was being prudent, nothing more. A boatwright securing salvage. Maren Vale, minding her own affairs.

But when the light came up gray over the flooded landing, and I heard footsteps on the planks above—light, deliberate, unhurried—I knew better. The river had given something back after all. And it had given me with it.

I climbed the ladder to meet Ilsa Reyne's eyes, and hers were already open, already watching, as if she had been waiting all night for me to come up and admit who I was.
