## 2. The Client Who Lies Well

The woman arrived on the morning ferry, and Cyrus Amberdale—Maren Vale, these eight years, Maren Vale until the river forgot—watched her step off the gangplank the way a fox watches a snare being set: without moving, without blinking, cataloguing everything.

She was not dressed for mountains. That was the first wrongness. She wore a traveling coat of good grey wool, cut close, and boots that had never known anything rougher than a city pavement, and she carried herself with the particular economy of someone who had been taught to move well by people who charged for the lesson. Her hair was dark, pinned back without ornament. Her eyes did the work that jewelry does for other women.

She stood at the end of the dock while the ferry unloaded crates of salt fish and bolts of dyed cloth, and she looked up—not at the town, not at the harbor, but at the Serpent's Spine itself, where it shouldered up out of the morning haze to the north, white-capped even in spring, its ridgeline coiling away east like something long and patient that had decided to lie down across the horizon.

Cyrus knew that look. It was the look of someone measuring a distance they intended to cross.

*Damn*, Cyrus thought, and went back to mending nets.

* * *

She came to the boathouse at midmorning, when the light through the slatted walls laid stripes across the water like bars. She did not knock so much as arrive, pausing in the doorway with the confidence of a person accustomed to doors opening.

"Maren Vale," she said. Not a question. "I'm told you're the only guide on this stretch of coast who'll take clients above the tree line."

"I'm told a lot of things." Cyrus kept working the net needle through the mesh. "Most of them flattery."

"Ilsa Reyne." She extended no hand; Cyrus offered none. A small negotiation, concluded instantly, both parties satisfied. "I need a guide up the Spine. Three weeks, perhaps four. I'm surveying mining claims for interests out of Corvane."

"Corvane's interests usually send men with ledgers and bad knees."

"Corvane's interests sent me because I don't have either."

It was a good answer. Too good. Cyrus set down the net needle at last and looked at her properly. Up close there were small truths the coat couldn't hide: calluses on her right palm, old ones, from rope or rein; a thin scar along her jaw gone silver with age; the way her weight sat evenly on both feet, balanced, always balanced, the stance of someone who'd learned early that ground could be taken from under you.

"You want me to take you into the high passes," Cyrus said, "in spring, when the meltwater's still chewing the trails apart, to look at rocks."

"To look at what's under the rocks."

"The mining claims are under the rocks?"

"Everything worth having is under the rocks, Ms. Vale." Her mouth curved, but her eyes didn't join it. "I'm told you know the northern approaches better than any map shows."

And there it was. The hook beneath the bait, glinting.

"There are no maps of the northern approaches," Cyrus said. "That's rather the point of them."

"Then you'll be guiding me by memory. Which is why I asked for you specifically."

Cyrus rose slowly. Outside, a gull screamed once over the water and thought better of it. "Who told you about me?"

"A man in Corvane. A shipping agent. He said you came here years ago and never left, and that people who come to Vell's Landing and never leave are usually running from something, and that people who run to the edge of the Serpent's Spine are usually mountain folk at heart." She paused. "He said you were good."

"He said a great deal for a shipping agent."

"He was paid to."

Honesty, then—or a shape of it, worn like the coat, tailored to fit. Cyrus moved past her to the window, needing the water in view, the flat grey reach of the harbor where nothing could hide except what the river brought down. Two days ago it had brought a chest stamped with a dead nation's seal, and Cyrus had spent two sleepless nights since telling themselves that a chest in a flooded river was driftwood, coincidence, the sea's indifferent litter. That nobody knew. That nobody could know.

But this woman stood in the doorway asking for the northern routes by name, and Cyrus felt the coil tightening behind the ribs—the old sensation, eight years dormant, of being watched by something patient.

"The Karveth passes are closed," Cyrus said, watching her reflection in the glass for the flinch. "By treaty. By law. By the simple fact that everyone who knew them is dead or scattered."

Her reflection didn't flinch. It went very still instead, the stillness of a held breath. "I didn't mention Karveth."

"No," Cyrus agreed softly. "You didn't."

* * *

They walked the harbor road afterward, because Cyrus wanted witnesses around them, open sky, the crowd of the fish market as cover. Ilsa Reyne kept pace easily, hands clasped behind her back like a scholar on a stroll, and talked about mining. She talked about mining very well—assay values, seam depths, the new smelting contracts out of Corvane—and every third sentence contained a small error that no genuine surveyor would make. She called galena by a name used only in textbooks. She spoke of the Spine's ore bodies as if they lay on the southern slopes, where everyone knew the rock was worthless granite.

She was lying, and lying competently, which meant she had prepared, which meant she had studied, which meant someone had briefed her—and yet the errors were deliberate too, Cyrus realized with a cold slide of understanding. They were tests. She was watching to see if Cyrus corrected her. Every mistake was a question: *Do you know this country? How well? Were you there?*

So Cyrus played the game back. Corrected nothing. Nodded along like a guide who knew trails and nothing else, and watched her frustration gather behind her eyes like weather.

At the mouth of the market they stopped, and she turned to face the mountains again, and for a moment—for just one unguarded moment—the performance dropped. What looked out of her face then was not greed or curiosity or corporate purpose. It was grief. Old, worn-smooth grief, the kind that has been carried so long it has shaped the body around itself, the way a river valley is shaped by water long gone.

"My father surveyed those passes," she said quietly, to the mountains, not to Cyrus. "Before the war. Before any of it."

Cyrus's heart performed a slow, treacherous stumble. "Your father was Karvethi?"

"My father was a cartographer." A pause, precise as a knife laid flat. "That's all I'll say on a public street."

She turned back, and the mask was up again, smooth as the harbor water. "Three weeks. Double your going rate. I pay half now, half at the summit approach. And I want the route you took eight years ago—the Recovery Expedition route. I want to see where the storm caught them."

The words landed like stones dropped into still water. *Where the storm caught them.* Everyone in two countries knew that story; it had followed Cyrus across the border like a dog with a scent. Three clients dead in a white hell that no forecast had predicted, a guide who walked out alive, a name blackened from the guild rolls in Corvane to the taverns of Vell's Landing. *Where the storm caught them.* As if the storm were a place. As if you could visit it.

"Why?" Cyrus asked. "Why that route, of all routes?"

"Because my father drew it," she said. "And because I intend to finish his survey."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I'll find another guide." She smiled, and it was almost warm, and therefore entirely false. "Though none of them will know the way. You're the only one left, Ms. Vale. Isn't that what the river keeps telling everyone?"

* * *

That night Cyrus did not sleep. The boathouse creaked with the tide, and the lamp burned low, and Cyrus sat with the rope coil in their lap—the one from the loft, heavy with more than hemp—and did not open it, because opening it would change nothing and closing it again would take an hour of shaking hands.

*Ilsa Reyne.* The name sat wrong, the way a borrowed coat sits wrong on unfamiliar shoulders. And her questions had been wrong, and her errors had been wrong, and her grief had been the only true thing about her, which was somehow the most alarming part of all. Liars who grieve are the most dangerous kind. They have something real underneath, and real things can be leveraged, broken, aimed.

And she had said *the river*. Casually. A figure of speech, surely. Except that two days ago the river had given up a sealed chest bearing the crest of a nation erased from every map, and Cyrus had pulled it from the shallows alone, before dawn, telling no one—or telling no one they remembered telling—and the chest had made a sound against the rocks that carried further than sound should carry in fog.

Someone knew. Someone had always known. Eight years of Maren Vale, of nets and tides and careful invisibility, and within forty-eight hours of the river's gift a stranger stood in the doorway asking for the exact route of the exact expedition that had ended Cyrus's life.

The coil tightened. Cyrus understood, finally, with the clarity that comes only at night, that hiding had ended—not tomorrow, not when the syndicate's letters arrived or the strangers came asking, but two days ago, at the moment gloved fingers closed around wet rope in the grey water. Visibility was not coming. It had arrived, wearing a grey wool coat and a competent lie, and it was offering double rate.

Cyrus looked at the door, where the woman had stood. Looked north, through the wall, toward the Spine, white and silent and keeping its secrets the way it had kept them for twenty years.

Then, quietly, aloud, to the empty boathouse:

"All right, Ilsa Reyne. Let's find out what you're really carrying."

Outside, far down the dark harbor road, a lantern paused—just briefly—at the gate, then moved on.
