## 3. Blacklist

Eight years before the river gave up its dead, Cyrus Amberdale still believed the mountains could be trusted.

They had taught him otherwise gently at first, in the small lessons every guide learns: the cornice that holds until it doesn't, the blue ice under fresh snow, the way weather in the Serpent's Spine can be born and buried inside a single afternoon. He had learned to read clouds the way other men read faces. He was twenty-six years old and he thought that was enough.

The Karveth Recovery Expedition left Vell's Landing on the ninth of Thawmonth, six clients and two porters besides himself, bound for the high passes of a country that no longer appeared on maps. Karveth had been erased—its borders dissolved, its villages renamed or abandoned—but its mountains remained, indifferent to politics, and somewhere in those mountains, so the story went, lay the records of an earlier survey: a scientific party that had gone up nineteen years before and never come down. The clients were scholars and investors, men of soft hands and expensive gear, and they paid Cyrus a sum that made his mother weep when he told her.

He remembered the morning they crossed into the old Karveth territory because the light had been wrong. Not dangerous-wrong—the kind of wrong that painters chase. The whole valley stood washed in a copper haze, as though the sun itself were grieving something it couldn't name. One of the porters, an old man named Sef who had been born on the Karveth side, stopped at the boundary stone—a leaning slab with the crest half worn away—and touched it with two fingers, the way you'd touch a grave.

"You feel that?" he said quietly.

Cyrus felt only cold air coming down off the ice. "Feel what?"

Sef looked at him a long moment. "Nothing," he said. "That's what I feel. Nothing. A country should have a sound, boy. Even empty ones." Then he shouldered his load and walked on, and Cyrus, young and impatient, filed the moment away as superstition.

* * *

The first week was textbook. They climbed through larch forest still silvered with frost, past shrines whose carved figures had been defaced by soldiers who came after the erasure, up switchbacks cut by mule trains that hadn't run in a generation. The clients were loud and delighted. Halvard Brene, the eldest, a mining magnate with a laugh like gravel in a drum, kept asking questions about the survey party they were meant to find. What were they looking for? Why so high? Why had no one gone after them?

"Bad luck and bad politics," Cyrus told him. "Karveth closed its borders, then stopped existing. Nobody goes looking for men in a country that isn't there."

Brene smiled at that, but it was a strange smile, brief and private, like a man checking a coin he already knows is counterfeit. Cyrus noticed. He noticed everything; that was his trade. But noticing is not the same as understanding, and he was too young to know the difference.

On the sixth day they reached the shoulder below the Col of the Nine Winds, and there Cyrus found something that did not belong. Anchors. Old ones—pitons driven into the rock and rusted to the color of dried blood, with frayed rope-ends still knotted through two of them. Someone had camped here. Someone had fixed lines across the col. The knots were professional work, expedition-grade, tied in a style he recognized from the manuals of the old Karveth Alpine Corps.

He stood there longer than he should have, wind pulling at his collar, feeling the copper light of that first morning settle over him again like a hand on the back of his neck. Nineteen years of weather, and the pitons still held. Whoever had placed them had expected to come back this way.

"Guide!" Brene called from below. "Is it passable?"

"It's passable," Cyrus called back, and turned from the anchors, and that was the last easy decision he would make for eight years.

* * *

The storm came on the eighth night, above the cloud line, in the teeth of a forecast that promised ten days of clear high pressure. Cyrus had read the dispatches himself in Vell's Landing—he was not a man who gambled on weather—and every service, every source, had agreed: stable, dry, cold. Perfect climbing weather.

What came instead out of the northwest was a wall. No front, no warning, no gradual thickening of cloud. One moment the stars were hard and close overhead; the next, the world ended at arm's length and the wind arrived like something released from a cage, screaming down the col with a force that tore the fly off their mess tent and sent the porters crawling on their bellies to hold the supply line. The temperature fell thirty degrees in an hour. Snow came sideways, dry as salt, packing into every seam.

Cyrus did everything right. That was the sentence that would haunt him longest—not that he failed, but that he succeeded, completely, in every measure a guide can control. He got the tents reanchored in the dark with frozen hands. He got the stove lit in the cook shelter and the clients zipped into their bags with hot stones wrapped in cloth. He calculated rations, checked pulse and breath, kept them talking through the long hours so sleep wouldn't slide into something colder. He was, by every standard ever written, a good guide in a bad place.

By dawn, three of them were dead anyway.

Halvard Brene. The young surveyor, Tam Ostrewe. And quiet Mistress Ellery, who had come along mostly, Cyrus suspected, to be near Brene. They had not frozen in their tents—that was the thing no one could explain at first. Their tent had been found collapsed, its anchor lines cut. Not torn. Cut, clean, all four, while the neighboring tents' lines held fast through winds that should have taken everything on the mountain. And the cook shelter's stove, which Cyrus had lit and banked himself, had been found cold, its fuel valve opened wide, its flame starved out sometime in the night while three men slept beside it breathing carbon monoxide instead of air.

Cut lines. An opened valve. In a storm that no forecast in the world had predicted.

Cyrus sat in the wreckage of the camp with Sef's mittened hand on his shoulder and understood, in the slow, sick way one understands a betrayal, that none of it was accident. But understanding is not proof, and proof is not power, and he was a mountain guide with no name worth anything beyond the next valley.

* * *

The descent took four days. He brought the living down carefully, respectfully, and the dead wrapped in tent canvas on improvised sleds, refusing help at the worst sections because some animal part of him believed that if he carried enough weight, the ledger might balance. It did not balance. Nothing balanced.

At the border post below the passes, the questions began, and Cyrus learned the second lesson of that year: the truth has no currency among men who have already decided what happened.

A storm that never existed became, in the official account, a storm Cyrus ignored. The cut lines became worn equipment—his responsibility, his inspection. The stove became his negligence. The inquiry was conducted by men in good coats who never once looked up at the mountain, and when Cyrus produced the weather dispatches, the presiding magistrate examined them with great care and observed that documents could be forged, couldn't they, guide? And what motive might a struggling young man have to forge them?

Motive. They gave him one, of course. Insurance settlements. Professional jealousy. Some invented grudge against Brene. It didn't matter what; it mattered only that the story required a villain, and a foreign-born guide with no family money and no political friends was the cheapest villain available.

The verdict crossed the first border ahead of him. By the time he reached the second, innkeepers were turning him out, and a warrant waited in the district capital, and men in plain clothes asked questions about where he slept. His license was revoked in absentia by a guild he had served since he was fifteen. His mother sold the house to pay the advocates and died before the appeal was heard—or perhaps of hearing about it; the family never agreed on which.

Sef found him once, in a dockside tavern in Vell's Landing, in the last weeks before Cyrus understood he would have to become someone else entirely. The old porter bought him a drink and sat with him in silence for a long time.

"They wanted them dead," Sef said at last. "All three. You know that now."

"I know it."

"And you know why the storm wasn't in any forecast."

"I know someone paid to keep it out of them."

Sef nodded slowly, turning his cup. "Then hear an old man. A man who knows a thing that powerful will kill again to keep it buried. You can spend your life shouting the truth from rooftops, and they will bury you under the same stone they buried Karveth under. Or—" he tapped the table twice, softly, "—you can go quiet. Change your name. Get small. Live."

"I didn't do it," Cyrus said. His voice cracked on the second word, and he hated that, hated it more than the verdicts, more than the cut ropes.

"No," Sef agreed. "You didn't. But the world doesn't pay for innocence, boy. It only charges for it." He finished his drink and stood. "Get small. Live. And if the mountain ever gives the truth back to you—" he paused at the door, silhouetted against the gray harbor light, "—you'll know what it cost you to carry it the first time. Decide then what it's worth."

* * *

Eight years later, Cyrus Amberdale lived under the name Maren Vale in a boathouse at the edge of Vell's Landing, and if anyone had asked—which no one did—they'd have said Maren Vale was a competent, unremarkable river guide who kept to himself and asked no questions. Small. Quiet. Alive.

But there were nights, in the season when the floodwater ran high and brown and full of the mountains' leavings, when Cyrus stood at the water's edge and watched what the current brought down: branches, fence rails, a child's boot, once a door still on its hinges. The river kept things. The river returned things. And every spring Cyrus caught himself searching the flotsam for something specific without admitting what—some scrap, some document, some drowned fragment of the truth that had ruined him, delivered back by water the way justice never would be by men.

It was on one of those nights, eight years almost to the month after three bodies came down off the Col of the Nine Winds, that Cyrus saw the chest.

It rode the floodline in the shallows below the boathouse, snagged against a fallen alder, turning slowly in the current—dark wood, iron-banded, water streaming off its lid. And stamped into that lid, deep enough to survive the river, was a crest: a nine-pointed crown above crossed peaks.

Cyrus knew that crest. He had touched it once, half worn away, on a leaning boundary stone, while an old man said *a country should have a sound.*

The chest of the Karveth Recovery Expedition had been listed as lost with its owners. Cyrus had signed the inventory himself.

And now the river was handing it back.
