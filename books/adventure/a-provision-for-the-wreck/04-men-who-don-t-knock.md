## 4. Men Who Don't Knock

The morning the men came to my boathouse, I was three miles upriver gutting a fish I had no appetite to eat.

I remember that detail afterward with a strange tenderness—the knife, the cold slick of the scales, the grey water sliding past my boots. Ordinary things. The last ordinary things before the world tilted and showed me its underside again. I had gone out before dawn because the flood had begun to fall, and falling water gives back what it takes, and I wanted to know what else the Serpent had coughed up. Fences. A cradle, empty, God help whoever had owned it. Half a bridge from two towns away. I walked the mudline with a lantern and told myself I was only curious, only thorough, only a man named Maren Vale who had nothing in his pockets but a hook knife and a few coins.

I was lying to myself even then. I was looking for more of it. More of Karveth.

I found nothing but fish bones and river glass, and by the time I came home the light was wrong.

You learn to read light the way a sailor reads water. My boathouse sat at the end of a rotted pier, its door facing east, and at that hour the morning sun should have struck the hasp and thrown a long bright blade across the planks. There was no blade. The door stood a hand's width open, and the light that came through it was the flat, spent light of a room already searched.

I did not go in. That is the first thing eight years of hiding teaches you—not courage, not cleverness, but the animal grammar of retreat. I set my lantern down in the reeds, quiet as snowfall, and I watched.

Two men. They moved the way soldiers move when they think no one is watching, which is to say they moved efficiently and without apology. The tall one went through my gear shelf with a fisherman's economy, turning each coil of line, each tin of flies, weighing each item in his palm before setting it back exactly where it had been. That frightened me more than ransacking would have. Ransacking is rage or greed; this was procedure. The short one knelt at the water hatch where I kept my spare oars and pried up the boards with a blade, and the sound of it carried across the water like a knuckle cracking in a church.

They were looking for the chest.

The chest was gone. I had sunk it in the oxbow pond two nights before, wrapped in net and chain, in water deep enough that even a determined diver would need light and leisure to find it. But the documents—the papers I had read by candlelight with my hands shaking until the wax ran—the documents were not in the chest. They had not been in the chest since the second night, when I had understood that a chest is the first place any searcher looks and the last place anything should be kept. I had unstitched the false lining of my thickest mooring rope, the one I never used, the one coiled on its peg like a sleeping animal, and fed the oilskin packet down into its heart, and sewn it shut again with waxed thread. Then I had sat in the dark listening to the river and feeling like a man who had swallowed a coal.

So the men in my boathouse found nothing. They were thorough anyway. The tall one lifted my cot and looked beneath it. The short one ran his blade along the seams of the walls, listening for the hollow note of a hiding place, and I stood in the reeds with the cold water soaking through my boots and thought, *he has done this before, many times, in many houses, and somewhere there are people who never came home to find him.*

When they were finished they did not leave at once. That was the part I would turn over for nights afterward. They stood in the middle of my floor, in the middle of my borrowed life, and the tall one said something I could not hear, and the short one laughed—a short, unamused bark, the laugh of a man who finds nothing funny—and then they walked out and down the pier and along the river path toward town, unhurried, their boots leaving prints in the mud that the river would never see fit to erase.

I gave them a quarter hour. Then I went in.

* * *

My boathouse smelled of them. That sounds like poetry but it is plain fact—strangers leave a smell behind them, tobacco and boot leather and something under it, something like hot iron, like the air around a forge or a freshly fired pistol. I stood in my own doorway breathing my own air and tasting them in it, and my hands, which had been steady through the whole watching, began to shake.

I checked the rope first. Of course I checked the rope first. I cut the stitching with my hook knife and drew out the oilskin packet and held it up to the light, turning it, looking for the telltale smudge of another man's thumb. It was clean. The wax seal of Karveth glared up at me from the topmost page—the twin peaks and the falling star, the crest they had burned into my memory on a mountainside eight years ago while the snow buried three men I had sworn to bring home.

*You were never careful enough,* I told myself. *Careful men do not pull royal seals out of rivers.*

I had told myself the flood was chance. I had told myself the chest was flotsam, that Karveth was twenty years drowned and its secrets with it. But men who search a boathouse with a blade and a patient laugh are not chasing chance. Somewhere upstream, someone had known what the river was going to give up before the river knew it. Or—worse, and more likely—someone had put it in the river to be found, and had been watching ever since to see who would find it.

I restitched the rope with hands I forced to be slow. Slow hands are steady hands; my first mentor taught me that on my first mountain, back when I was Cyrus Amberdale and the name still fit me like a coat. Then I sat down on my cot, which had been lifted and replaced by strangers, and I did the arithmetic of my position like a man counting out coins he already knows will not cover the debt.

Option one: run. Take the packet, take the skiff, take the long way around the lake and be gone before dark. I had done it before. I could do it again. I would arrive in some other border town under some other name, and I would be Maren Vale there too, and in five years I would be someone else again, a sequence of borrowed men stretching out to the grave, none of them ever me.

Option two: burn the papers. Feed the whole oilskin packet to my stove and watch the twin peaks curl and blacken, and be safe, and be nothing, forever.

Option three.

I did not let myself think option three. Not yet. I made tea instead, because a man's hands want work, and while the water heated I heard a knock at my door.

Two knocks. Courteous. Even.

Men who don't knock had knocked.

* * *

He was younger than the other two, and better dressed, and that was its own kind of message. The searchers had been muscle; this one was a mouth. He stood on my pier with his hands folded in front of him like a clerk come about an invoice, and he smiled at me with the particular warmth of a man who has already decided how this conversation ends and is merely being polite about the middle of it.

"Maren Vale," he said. He said the name the way a jeweler holds a stone to the light, checking for flaws. "I've been looking forward to meeting you. Everyone says you keep to yourself. A man who keeps to himself. Rare, these days."

"River's high," I said. "Keeps a man busy."

"It does. It's been generous this spring, I hear." He drifted along the pier, reading it—my nets, my skiff, the peg where the rope coil hung, and I watched his eyes touch the rope and move on, and I understood that he knew exactly what he was looking at and wanted me to know he knew. "Generous enough to give things back. Odd things. Old things."

I said nothing. The river said nothing. Somewhere under the water the chest sat in its chains, patient as a grave.

"You found something," he said. It was not a question, and he did not dress it as one. "Something that washed down from the high country. Something with a seal on it." He turned and looked at me fully then, and his smile was gone, and what was underneath it was worse—not cruelty, exactly, but a kind of administrative patience, the patience of an organization that had erased a nation from the maps and considered it a job well done. "My employers want it returned. That's all. Return what the river gave up, and this stays a quiet spring in a quiet town. Keep it, and—" he glanced back toward Vell's Landing, the smoke of its chimneys standing up straight in the still air, "—well. Names get spoken. Names get checked. You'd be surprised how far back the records go, Mr. Vale. How *thorough* the records are."

There it was. Not a threat against my body—bodies are cheap, and men like him know that a dead man's secrets die with him. A threat against the alias. Against the eight years of careful nothing I had built around the wound of my real name. He was not offering to kill me. He was offering to make me *Cyrus Amberdale* again, publicly, in front of the magistrates of two countries, and let the warrants do the rest.

"I don't know what you mean," I said.

"Of course not." He nodded, satisfied, and produced from his coat a small card of grey stock, blank except for a single mark pressed into it: the twin peaks and the falling star. He set it on my pier post beneath a stone, the way a man feeds a dog he expects to see again. "You have until the water drops. It drops fast this time of year. Good day, Mr. Vale."

He walked back along the river path without hurrying. He never once looked over his shoulder, which told me he was certain no one would dare.

* * *

I sat up all that night with the packet in my lap and the stove cold, because I did not trust my hands near a flame.

The candle burned down and I let it. I read the papers again by moonlight off the water, though I knew them now nearly by heart—the requisitions, the weather ledgers with their forged entries, the letter in a syndicate hand ordering the Recovery Expedition's route closed "by any means, with no surviving testimony." Three men dead in a storm that never existed. My name dragged through two border courts like something pulled behind a boat. And beneath it all, the reason: something found in the high passes that certain men had paid a great deal to make sure no one ever found again.

I thought about running. I laid the whole plan out in my head, the skiff, the lake, the southern road, and it went together cleanly, the way escape plans always do, the way mine had gone together eight years ago when I had traded my name for my life and called the trade wisdom.

Then I looked at the grey card on the pier post, visible through the open door, the falling star pale in the moonlight, and I understood that running was over. They had my address. They had my alias in their mouths like a stone. I could flee tonight and be found in the next town, or the one after, and every year of hiding would be unwound thread by thread until there was nothing left of Maren Vale to hide inside. The choice they had so kindly left me was not between safety and danger.

It was between their truth and mine.

I got up in the grey hour before dawn and took down the rope coil and held it, feeling the papers through the hemp like a heartbeat. And I found that the fear in me had changed its shape—not gone, nothing so kind as gone, but hardened, compacted, the way snow becomes ice becomes something you can cut steps into and climb.

I would not run. I would not burn them. I would go up the Serpent's Spine, the route that had eaten my name, and I would deliver the dead their testimony if it killed me—and if it did not kill me, then God help the men who had forged the storm, because I was done being weathered over.

There was only one problem, and it was sitting in my taproom not two days since, asking questions about old Karveth routes with eyes too sharp for a mining surveyor.

Ilsa Reyne. I needed to know exactly who she was—before whoever sent those men sent someone worse.

I put on my coat and went to find her.
