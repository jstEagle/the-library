## 5. Ilsa's Map

Ilsa Reyne had learned to lie the way other children learned to pray: nightly, in whispers, with her hands folded over something precious.

For eleven days she had lied to Maren Vale. She had lied about mining claims, about a husband waiting in Corvane, about the reason her boots were already broken in along the toe like boots that had walked scree before. She had lied with the easy fluency of money, which was the only fluency her father had ever left her. And for eleven days the guide had listened with that flat grey patience, saying yes and perhaps and we'll see when the season turns, and Ilsa had begun to believe—almost—that she had found the one person on this border dumber than she was ruthless.

Then, on the twelfth morning, she woke to find a rope coil sitting on the table of her rented room, and the coil was wrong.

She knew rope. Her father had taught her knots before letters, had braided her hair in a fisherman's loop when there was no ribbon, and she knew that a coil stored wet did not sit with its end tucked *inward*, neat as a signature. Someone had opened it and closed it again. Someone had wanted her to know they'd opened it.

Beneath the coil, weighted by a river-smoothed stone, lay a single line of paper in a hand she did not know:

*The boathouse. Dawn. Come alone or don't come.*

Ilsa sat on the edge of the narrow bed and let the fear move through her the way cold moved through wet wool—slowly, thoroughly, into every seam. She had known this moment would come. She had crossed two borders and spent a small fortune to make it come. Knowing did not keep her hands from shaking.

She dressed in the dark. She took the map out of the false heel of her boot—not because she meant to show it, but because she could not bear to leave it behind—and she went down through the sleeping town toward the river.

* * *

Vell's Landing at dawn was a town holding its breath under water. Mist stood in the streets like the ghosts of the drowned, and the river ran high and brown and loud, still swollen from the spring melt, still carrying somebody's fence posts and half a roof downstream toward the delta. The boathouse crouched at the end of its rotting pier with its door already open, and inside, lamplight the color of old honey fell across a workbench, coiled tackle, and Maren Vale.

Except it wasn't Maren Vale. Ilsa had understood that since the second week—the way *Vale* watched water instead of scenery, the way their hands bore rope scars in the pattern of a guide who'd handled lines above the cloudline, not a ferryman's soft loops. But understanding a thing and hearing it said were different animals, and when the figure by the bench turned and spoke, the voice had shed its ferryman's drawl entirely.

"Sit down, Miss Reyne. Or should I say—" a pause, delicate as a blade laid flat against skin, "—whatever your name is."

"Ilsa Reyne is my name." She stayed standing near the door, backlit by the grey mouth of the morning. "The rest of it you'll have to earn."

"Earn." The guide—Cyrus, she thought; the name arrived in her mind unbidden, dredged up from eight years of grief like a body from a lake—huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "That's rich. You hire me with a purse full of lies, ask me questions about passes no miner has ever heard of, and now you want to talk about earning." They lifted the lamp slightly, and the light caught their face: sharp-boned, weathered, eyes the grey-green of deep glacier water. "The chest came out of the river stamped with Karveth. The documents inside name the Recovery Expedition. And three weeks later a wealthy stranger walks into Vell's Landing asking after the Serpent's Spine. So. One of us is going to explain themselves, and it isn't going to be me first."

"You pulled the chest out yourself?" Ilsa asked softly.

"The river gave it up. That's all."

"That's never all." She stepped forward, out of the doorway, letting the mist close behind her. "You looked at that seal and you knew what it was. I watched your face when I mentioned the Spine—you went white around the mouth. You've seen that crest before, up close, in places where seeing it cost people their lives." She set her palm flat on the workbench, steadying herself. "So let's not do each other the insult of pretending. You're not Maren Vale. And I'm not here for mining claims."

The lamplight hissed. Outside, the river kept up its low, ceaseless argument with the banks.

"Say what you are, then," Cyrus said quietly.

Ilsa reached down and unlaced her left boot. It was an absurd gesture, intimate and strange, and she felt the guide's confusion sharpen into attention as she drew out the false heel—a sliver of oiled leather sewn over a hollow—and lifted free the thing she had carried across two borders against her skin.

It was a map. Hand-drawn on rag paper gone soft as cloth with handling, ink faded to the brown of dried blood, covered edge to edge in a fine, patient script: contour lines, elevation figures, route notes in two languages, one of them dead. The passes of the Karveth range, drawn by someone who had walked them until the mountains lived behind his eyes.

"My father's," she said. "Edmun Reyne. Cartographer. He was contracted to the Recovery Expedition eight years ago." She laid it on the workbench between them, smoothing it flat with both hands, and watched Cyrus look at it the way starving people look at bread. "He walked those passes twice. He mapped the routes the syndicate wanted buried, and then he wrote down why they wanted them buried, and then he got on a boat at Corvane harbor to bring the originals home, and he never arrived. No body. No wreckage. Nothing. For eight years the world has told me my father vanished." Her voice held steady; she had practiced it being steady for years. "Last autumn I found the man who booked his passage. He remembered my father boarded. He also remembered two men in grey coats boarding after him, and neither man ever registered a return."

Silence. The lamp guttered and recovered.

"You think he's dead," Cyrus said.

"I know he's dead. What I don't know yet is where they put him, or how many others went into the ground with him, or how high up the chain the order came from. The map tells part of it." She turned the paper over. On the reverse, in smaller script, cramped as though written in haste or hiding: coordinates, dates, and a single repeated phrase in Karvethi that Ilsa had translated a hundred times by lantern light. *"They cut the anchors. Ask the guide."*

She looked up. "Ask the guide," she said. "My father wrote that. Eight years ago, before anyone knew anything was wrong. Which means either he suspected the disaster before it happened—or he survived long enough afterward to learn the truth. Either way, the only person alive who can tell me which is standing in front of me wearing a dead woman's name."

* * *

Cyrus Amberdale—for so Ilsa now thought of them, the alias falling away like ice off a warming ledge—did not speak for a long time. They stood with both hands braced on the workbench, head bowed, and Ilsa watched the war move across their face: the instinct to run warring with something older and more exhausted underneath it.

"They cut the stove line too," Cyrus said at last. Their voice had changed—flatter, stripped of performance. "Tampered valve. It vented while we slept. Two men died of the cold before we even understood what was happening. Then the storm came in early, off-schedule, from a direction storms don't come from, and the anchor lines on the upper tents failed. All of them. Clean cuts, later—I checked, later, when it was too late to matter to anyone but me." They laughed, short and terrible. "Three clients dead. Me, the only survivor fit to stand trial. And a forecast office in Corvane swearing there was never any storm system within two hundred miles."

"And you ran."

"I stopped existing." Cyrus straightened. "Maren Vale was born in a ferryman's bed in a border town nobody maps. Eight years of hauling other people's cargo and keeping my head below the ridgeline. And then your chest comes floating down the river like a message in a bottle, and three weeks later you walk into my life asking after the exact passes that ruined me, and I have spent eleven nights trying to decide whether you're a syndicate hunter or a ghost." Their eyes came up and fixed on hers, and there was nothing soft in them, but there was nothing cruel either—only a terrible, level weariness. "So which is it, Miss Reyne? Because if you're theirs, you should know I've hidden the documents somewhere even the river couldn't find them, and if you're a ghost—"

"I'm worse than either." Ilsa heard her own voice crack and let it. "I'm a daughter. I spent my inheritance buying passage and forged papers and a guide's silence, and I have lied to every soul between here and Corvane, and I would lie to you too if lying would get me up that mountain faster. But it won't. Not alone." She pressed her finger to the map, to the fine web of routes threading the forbidden country. "This is the only copy of these passes in existence outside the heads of dead men. I carry it in my memory as well—if you burn it tonight, I can redraw every line. My father made sure of that. He taught me the mountains the way other fathers teach scripture." She swallowed. "I need a guide who knows the Spine. You need someone the syndicate isn't hunting. Between us we might actually reach the high camps alive. Apart, we're both corpses with excellent secrets."

Cyrus studied her for a long moment. Then, slowly, they reached across the workbench—not for the map, but for her hand, turning it palm-up in the lamplight. They looked at the calluses there, the rope-wear, the honest labor of a woman who had rowed her own boats and carried her own packs and lied with her whole body for years.

"Scars," Cyrus said quietly. "You have them too."

"I told you. I'm not here for mining claims."

Something shifted in the guide's face—not trust, not yet, but the recognition of a fellow creature from the same hard country. They released her hand and stood looking down at the map spread between them, the dead cartographer's careful lines glowing amber in the lamplight, the mountains rising off the paper like a promise neither of them had been allowed to keep.

"There's a problem," Cyrus said finally. "Your chest, your documents, your father's murder—it's all evidence. Evidence needs a tribunal, and tribunals need witnesses, and witnesses need to survive the ascent. If we go up that mountain, we go up knowing what happened last time. Knowing they'll be watching. Knowing they don't send warnings twice." They tapped the map, once, on the highest pass. "And there's one more thing you should hear before you commit. Yesterday a courier left a note in my boathouse. *Return what the river gave up.* Which means they know the chest surfaced. Which means they know it's in Vell's Landing. Which means—"

The door of the boathouse swung open without a knock.

A porter—one of the two Ilsa had hired through the harbormaster, a broad silent Kellishman named Doss—stood filling the frame, rain streaming off his oilskins, his face the color of tallow.

"Beg pardon," he said. "But there's a man at the pier asking after the expedition. Says he's come about the mining survey." He hesitated, and his voice dropped. "Says his name is Broch. And miss—he's carrying gear for the high passes. Rope, pitons, a stove." A beat. "New stove. Still in the crate."
