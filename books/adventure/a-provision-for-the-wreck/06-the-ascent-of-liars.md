## 6. The Ascent of Liars

They left Vell's Landing before dawn, when the river mist still lay over the town like a sheet drawn up to its chin. Cyrus counted the party twice from the back of the line and twice more at the first switchback: Ilsa Reyne in her too-new boots; the porters, Tam and Ovid, brothers out of the mill villages, broad-shouldered and quiet; and Broch.

Broch had come to them the way bad weather comes—no announcement, no negotiation, just a presence on the horizon that eventually arrived. He'd appeared at the boathouse three days after the strangers tore it apart, offering his services as a hired hand with a smile like a door left ajar. He was big through the chest, soft-spoken, competent with rope. His references were forged, but they were *well* forged, which told Cyrus everything: whoever sent him had money and patience.

Cyrus let him come. A plant you've identified is worth more than one you haven't.

The trail climbed out of the river valley through alder scrub gone silver with dew, then into pine, where the light came down in green coins and the air smelled of resin and cold stone. By midmorning the town was a rumor below them, and the Serpent's Spine stood up ahead, white fangs against a sky the color of unpolished steel.

"You've been quiet," Ilsa said, falling into step beside Cyrus. She'd stopped pretending to be a mining surveyor somewhere around the second day of planning, though neither of them said so aloud. "Quiet even for you."

"Counting."

"What?"

"Clients. Porters. Liars." Cyrus adjusted the strap of their pack. "I like to know my numbers."

Ilsa glanced back down the line, to where Broch walked with an easy, unhurried stride, hands loose at his sides, eyes doing what good eyes do—reading the terrain, reading the people. "And what's your count?"

"One too many." Cyrus lengthened their stride and left her to chew on it.

By noon they reached the treeline, and by late afternoon the weather that should have been a week away arrived instead like an uninvited guest who'd been waiting outside the whole time. The wind swung north without warning. Clouds boiled up out of the western passes, gray going to bruise-purple, and the temperature dropped so fast that Tam's breath began to smoke.

"That's wrong," Ovid said, squinting at the sky. "That's autumn weather. That's a month early."

Cyrus looked at the clouds for a long time. They had the flat, layered look of a system that intended to stay, and beneath it, faint as a scar under paint, ran the particular green-black tint that old guides called *the mountain's mood*. Eight years ago, Cyrus had ignored that tint because the forecasts said otherwise. The forecasts had been bought.

"We camp at the bridge," Cyrus said. "Crossing at first light, ahead of whatever this is."

* * *

Broch sat apart from the fire, the way he always did—not far enough to seem unfriendly, just far enough that the light didn't quite reach his face. He ate his ration slowly and listened.

He was good at listening. It was most of his trade. The syndicate paid him for two things: proximity and reporting, and only rarely, if the situation required it, for the third thing. He turned the third thing over in his mind now, the way a man turns a coin he hasn't yet decided to spend.

His orders were clear enough. They'd been given to him in a room above a tannery in Corvess, by a man with ink-stained fingers and a voice like dry paper. *Follow them up. Find the documents. If the documents can be recovered cleanly, recover them.* And then the pause, the small professional pause that meant the rest: *If not—if there are complications—see that nothing comes back down.*

Nothing. Not papers. Not maps. Not the woman Reyne, whose father's cartography had cost the syndicate dearly twenty years ago and would cost it more now. Not the porters, who were nobody, who were collateral the way weather is collateral. And not the guide.

Especially not the guide. Because Maren Vale was not Maren Vale, and the man with the ink-stained fingers knew it, and knew that Cyrus Amberdale, once the documents surfaced, would be the one voice in all the borderlands with the standing to make them matter.

Across the fire, Vale was checking the anchor points on the bridge approach with a lantern held low, moving with the economy of someone who had done ten thousand small preparations and buried most of them deep. Broch watched the hands. Good hands. Steady. The kind of hands that had once led eleven people into the high Karveth passes and brought four out.

*Four,* Broch thought, and almost smiled. That was the elegant part. The guide was already ruined; already half a ghost. When the mountain took the rest, no tribunal in the world would bother asking why. Cyrus Amberdale's name carried its own verdict. All Broch had to do was let gravity do its work and stand well clear of the paperwork.

The wind rose through the night. In his bedroll, Broch listened to it worrying the pines and thought, with something close to contentment, that the weather was cooperating better than expected.

* * *

The bridge was gone by morning.

Not damaged—*gone*. The spring flood had undercut the near abutment sometime in the night, and the span lay downstream in pieces, splintered planks scattered along the shingle like the bones of something large. The river that had given Cyrus the chest had now taken the road.

They stood at the edge and looked at the water running high and brown and fast, carrying ice-scoured debris, and no one spoke for a while.

"There's the long way," Ovid offered finally. "South fork, the miners' track. Adds six days. Maybe eight, with this weather coming."

"Eight days puts us in the teeth of the storm front," Cyrus said. "On exposed ground. With a client who's never been above the treeline in winter conditions." They turned and looked west, where the mountains folded together into a dark, crowded defile that no map in Vell's Landing marked with anything but blank space and a warning symbol. "Or there's the other way."

Ilsa went very still beside them.

"The Karveth route," she said.

"It runs parallel. Higher, but shorter, and it crosses the ridge before the storm line does. My father—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "The old surveys show it's passable in early season."

"It's passable," Cyrus said. "It's also closed. Border edict, both sides. Twenty years of edict." They looked at the broken bridge, then at the sky, then at each member of the party in turn—the porters nervous, Ilsa carefully expressionless, Broch wearing the mild, reasonable face of a man prepared to defer to expertise. "We go up the miners' track, we gamble eight days against a front I don't trust. We go the Karveth route, we break the law and walk country that's killed better parties than this one. Those are the choices. I know which one I'm making. Anyone who wants to turn back, now's the time, and I'll write you a full refund with my compliments."

Tam and Ovid looked at each other. Money talked louder than edicts in the mill villages, and they shook their heads.

"I came to survey claims," Ilsa said, deadpan, and something passed between her and Cyrus that Broch, watching from slightly behind, noted and filed.

"Broch?"

The big man shrugged pleasantly. "You're the guide. Mountains don't care whose flag they fly under." He smiled. "Besides, I've never seen the high country. Might as well be the forbidden part."

*Later,* Broch thought, watching them repack, *this will be remembered as the moment it became easy.* A closed route. No patrols. No witnesses for miles in any direction. Whatever happened up there—a fall, a crevasse, a storm that came early and stayed late—would happen beyond the reach of anyone who might ask questions. The syndicate could not have designed better terrain if they'd built it themselves.

Perhaps, he reflected, they had.

* * *

The Karveth route began at a cairn so old its stones had grown lichen faces, and climbed immediately into country that felt less entered than *trespassed upon*. There were no blazes, no cairns after the first mile, no cut steps—only the land itself, and the memory of it that Cyrus carried in their legs.

Because the legs remembered. That was the terrible thing. Eight years, two borders, a new name, and still Cyrus's feet found the old line without being asked: here the trail bent around the buttress, here it traversed the scree shelf, here it ducked beneath the overhang where the rock had fallen in some earlier century. The body kept its own map, and the body had never agreed to forget.

Ilsa walked close behind them, and several times Cyrus caught her glancing at features—the notch in the ridgeline, the twin boulders like a split tooth—and nodding to herself, confirming something against the map she claimed lived in her head. Her father's map. Drawn by a man the syndicate had erased as thoroughly as they'd erased his nation.

By afternoon they were above the cloud line, walking through a world reduced to three colors: white snow, gray rock, and the deep blue-black of the sky overhead, thin and cold as blade steel. The silence up there was total. Not peaceful—*attentive*. The silence of a courtroom waiting for testimony.

It was Tam who broke it.

"Guide." His voice came out strange, flattened by the altitude. "What happened up here? To the others? The ones from before."

Cyrus stopped. Below them the cloud sea stretched to the horizon, featureless, hiding every road back down. Ahead the pass opened between two walls of rock, and somewhere beyond it lay the high camps where three clients had died in a storm that existed nowhere but in the reports written afterward.

"A storm," Cyrus said. "That's what the record says."

"And what does the guide say?"

Cyrus looked at the sky, at the first threads of cloud already reaching up from the west like fingers testing a latch. They thought of the chest in the river. The seal. The documents hidden in a rope coil in a boathouse that men had searched and men would search again. They thought of how many times they had rehearsed the truth in the dark and never once said it aloud in daylight.

"The guide says," Cyrus said quietly, "that when we get to the high camp, you should look at the anchor points. Look at them closely. And then ask me again."

No one answered. The wind moved through the pass with a sound like paper turning, page after page after page, and far to the west, where the weather was building itself out of cold and malice, the light had begun to go the color of old bruises.

Broch brought up the rear, and no one watched him check the knots on his own pack straps—checking, among other things, that the knife rode where it always rode, high on the right hip, under the flap, dry.

The mountain, he thought, was going to do most of the work.

But not all of it.
