## 7. The Pass That Remembers

The cloud line took them on the third morning above the washout, and after that the world was simple: white stone, white sky, and the sound of their own breathing.

Cyrus had climbed this once before. Eight years ago they had come up through this same corridor of grey scree with six clients and a cartographer who hummed old Karvethi marching songs under his breath, and every step now landed in a footprint memory had already dug. The mountain had not changed. The mountain never changed. It was only the people on it who came back different, or didn't come back at all.

They moved in a loose line—Cyrus first, breaking trail through the wind-crusted snow, then Ilsa Reyne, who climbed better than she admitted to, then the porters Sef and young Tam with the rope coils riding their shoulders like sleeping animals, and Broch at the rear, where he had insisted on being since the bridge went out.

*Where he can watch everyone's hands,* Cyrus thought, and did not say it.

The air up here had a taste to it, thin and mineral, like sucking on a coin. It thinned thought as well as breath. Cyrus counted steps without meaning to—an old habit from the bad year—and watched the sun crawl across a sky so clean it looked scrubbed. No weather coming. Not today. The barometer in their breast pocket had held steady since dawn, the needle resting easy as a sleeping child.

Which made it worse, somehow, when the camp appeared.

* * *

Sef saw it first—a dark geometry against the snowfield below the col, half-swallowed by drift. He called out something in his own tongue, then in trade-speech: "Wreck. Old wreck."

Cyrus knew before they reached it. Knew the way one knows a face across a crowded room, before the details resolve—the set of the shoulders, the angle of the jaw. They had been steering around this place for eight years, giving the whole shoulder of the mountain a wide berth, taking the longer eastern line down to Vell's Landing because some part of them could not bear to walk past it again.

Now the mountain had folded the route back onto itself, and there was no way around.

The tents came first. Two of them, collapsed into the snow like shed skins, the canvas rotted to lace in places but still holding the shape of what had crushed them. Then the cook shelter, its frame snapped mid-ridgepole. Then the small scatter of things that time had not bothered to digest: an iron pot fused to a ring of rust. A boot, upright, filled with eight years of snowmelt and ice. A tin cup sitting exactly where a hand had set it down.

Nobody spoke. Even the wind seemed to lower its voice, moving through the wreck in a long hush, lifting fine snow off the tent fabric in little ghosts.

"This is it," Ilsa said quietly. Not a question. She stood very still, her gloved hands at her sides, and her eyes were moving over the camp the way her father's maps must have taught her to read ground—methodically, hungrily, grieving. "This is where they stopped."

"This is where they died," Cyrus said. The words came out flat, scraped of everything. "All but one."

Broch stood apart, arms crossed, watching Cyrus instead of the camp. Cyrus felt the weight of that gaze between the shoulder blades like a hand pressed flat against their coat.

"Storm did all this?" Tam asked. He was twenty, maybe, and had signed on for wages and a story to tell in the low valleys, and he kept looking at the boot as if it might stand up.

"There was no storm," Cyrus said.

And because there was no longer any point pretending—not here, not with Ilsa standing among the bones of her father's expedition—they knelt at the nearest tent and began, gently, to uncover what the snow had kept.

* * *

The anchor lines were the first thing.

A high camp like this one lived or died by its anchors: lines run out from the tent corners to pitons driven deep in the rock, so that wind could howl all it liked and the canvas would hold. Cyrus remembered driving these very pitons—or ones like them—hammer ringing off stone while the cartographer, Aldric Reyne, laughed about how the mountain sang back in Karvethi.

Cyrus brushed the ice away from the nearest line and the breath went out of them in a slow, cold stream.

The rope ended in a cut.

Not frayed. Not worn through by eight years of abrasion against stone—abrasion left a telltale fuzzing, a gradual unraveling, and besides, the cut end sat *under* the drift line, sealed beneath seasons of snowfall, untouched since the night it happened. The blade had gone through in a single stroke, clean as a butcher's work, leaving a smooth pale oval of severed fiber.

Cyrus moved to the second anchor. Cut. The third, on the far side, half-buried: cut. All four lines, all four tents' worth where they could reach them, sheared in the dark while men slept inside.

"They said a katabatic wind came off the col," Cyrus heard themself saying, voice strange and distant, as if someone else were narrating. "Tore the camp apart at two in the morning. Three clients dead in the first hour—exposure, falls in the dark. The rest of us survived by luck and the cook shelter. I told the tribunal the forecast had shown nothing. They said forecasts fail. They said a guide of my experience should have read the sky." A pause. "I was twenty-six. I believed them. For years I believed I'd missed something."

"You didn't miss anything," Ilsa said. Her voice had gone very quiet and very hard, the voice of someone holding a door shut against a flood. "Look at the stove."

The cook shelter's iron stove had been dragged clear of the collapse, or perhaps it had fallen that way. Its firebox door hung open. Cyrus pried it wider with the point of an ice axe and reached in, gloved, and drew out the burner assembly.

Someone had worked the fuel valve nearly shut. Not closed—closed would starve the flame visibly, would leave the shelter cold and the tea unbrewed and questions asked. Nearly shut was cleverer. The stove would light, would burn a thin blue whisper of a flame, would seem fine at a glance. And in a shelter full of exhausted climbers sealing every gap against the cold, a flame that small drank the air faster than it gave heat. Carbon monoxide does not announce itself. It comes like a kind guest. It makes you sleepy, and sleepy men do not check their anchors, do not hear a knife working through rope in the dark, do not wake when the wind arrives to finish the job.

"The storm," Cyrus said, "was scheduled."

Sef crossed himself with two fingers, an old valley gesture. Tam had gone the color of the snow. Only Broch did not react at all, and his stillness was itself a kind of answer, and Cyrus filed it away in the place where dread lived now, a growing ledger.

"My father found this too," Ilsa said. She was crouched at the far tent, and she lifted something from the ice with both hands, careful as lifting a bird. A leather map case, water-stained, cracked with age—but empty. "He sent his finished maps down the mountain with the supply runner two days before. But he always kept working copies. He'd never have left them." She turned the empty case over. "Someone wanted them. Someone searched him for them."

"And then the storm came early," Cyrus said, "and he walked off the mountain and out of every record there is."

The wind moved through the wreck again, and for a moment Cyrus could hear it carrying other sounds—hammer blows, humming, a man laughing about singing stone—and they had to close their eyes against the weight of it. Eight years. Eight years of eating shame like bread, of flinching at their own name, of building a smaller and smaller life called Maren Vale until Maren Vale was all that was left, and all of it built on a lie some syndicate had hammered together out of cut rope and a throttled stove.

"We should mark it," Tam said softly. "For the families."

"Yes," Cyrus said. "We will. When we come back down—with witnesses enough that the marking sticks."

* * *

They made the col by late afternoon, and the crevasse field beyond it caught the low sun and threw it back in blue blades.

Cyrus roped them in pairs—Sef with Tam, Ilsa with Broch, themself running point on a separate short line, probing the snow bridges ahead with the axe. The field was an old one, mostly bridged over, the kind of ground a guide could read like print if the guide had grown up reading it. Cyrus read it. Most of it.

Most.

Tam went through at the field's heart, where two bridges met and the snow between them had rotted to sugar. There was no drama to it—one moment the young porter was a dark figure on white, the next the white opened and took him, and his cry came up out of the earth sounding wrong, too close, as if he were shouting up a well beside Cyrus's own feet.

The rope snapped taut. Sef, anchored by his own pair-line to Ilsa and Broch behind him, lurched and held, boots plowing furrows, and Tam swung once against the crevasse wall and hung, forty feet down, in a shaft of blue light.

"On rope!" Cyrus was already moving, already driving a picket, already shouting the old litany—*feet braced, lean back, don't look down*—hands doing the work while the mind stood apart and screamed. Because Cyrus knew this. Had *lived* this. Eight years ago, in the dark, in the wind, a client named Oskar Venn had gone into ice exactly like this, and the rope had held, and the rescue had failed anyway, and Cyrus had spent eight years learning the exact weight of that failure in grams.

"Hold the line! Ilsa, take Sef's anchor—Broch, coil out, we're lowering a loop!"

Broch did not move.

It was only a moment. Later, Cyrus would measure it honestly: perhaps two seconds, perhaps three. A heartbeat and a half of stillness in which the big man stood with the spare rope in his hands and did nothing at all—did not kneel, did not uncoil, did not even shift his weight—and his eyes were not on Tam in the hole but on Cyrus, calculating, weighing something on scales only he could see.

Then he moved. He dropped to his knee and fed rope fast and clean, textbook-perfect, and if Cyrus had blinked they might have missed the hesitation entirely.

But Cyrus had spent eight years learning to read mountains, and mountains taught you that the dangerous thing was never the avalanche you saw coming. It was the slope that held still one beat too long before it moved.

They got Tam out. It took forty minutes of brutal, wordless labor—loop lowered, harness improvised from the hauling line, Sef hauling until the tendons in his neck stood out like cables, Tam's frozen hands scrabbling at the lip until Cyrus lay flat and gripped his wrists and pulled him up onto the snow, gasping, weeping, alive. Alive. Cyrus pressed their forehead briefly against the boy's shoulder and let themselves feel it, one full breath of it, before standing.

Everyone gets brought home this time, they had promised themself on the riverbank in another life. Everyone.

They camped that night in the lee of the col, close-packed, watches doubled. Tam slept like the dead sleep, exhausted past dreaming. Sef prayed quietly. Ilsa sat sharpening her knife and looking at the stars as though consulting them.

Cyrus took the last watch, and Broch took the one before it, and near midnight, in the flat silver light, Cyrus found fresh tracks leading out of camp and back in—Broch's stride, unmistakable, going fifty yards toward the eastern skyline where a man with strong lungs and a signal mirror could talk to someone on a peak three miles south.

The tracks came back. The question was: what had they carried out?

Below them, far down the southern flank, almost too faint to be sure of, a light answered the stars. Once. Twice. Then darkness, patient as a held breath.

Cyrus sat back against the pack wall, axe across their knees, and watched the dark where the light had been, and understood with perfect clarity that whatever the syndicate had sent Broch up the mountain to do, retrieving documents had never been the half of it.

No witnesses descend.

Above the col, the pass waited—the pass that remembered everything—and tomorrow they would walk into its throat with a killer at their backs and nowhere left to go but through.
