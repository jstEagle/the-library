## 8. Whiteout

The storm did not arrive. It *condensed*—out of nothing, out of the grey throat of the sky, as though the mountain had been holding its breath for twenty years and finally exhaled.

Ilsa Reyne felt it first in her teeth. A pressure, a low singing ache, the same sensation she'd known as a child when her father would stop mid-sentence on their walks outside Karveth City and turn his face north, reading weather in the way other men read letters. *Snow coming, little map,* he'd say. *The kind that erases.* She had thought it a game then, his private poetry. Now she understood he had been teaching her the only inheritance he could safely leave: how to know when the world was about to be rewritten.

"Shelter," Cyrus said, and there was no argument in the word. It was not a suggestion. It was the voice of a person who had heard this exact silence before and knew what followed it.

They had perhaps four hundred yards of visibility, then three hundred, then the white came down like a curtain drawn by an impatient hand. Ilsa lost sight of Tam almost immediately—the young porter's orange pack dissolving into the murk—and heard Cyrus bellow his name with a rawness that startled her, a crack in all that careful control. Tam answered. Halim answered behind him. And Broch answered last, his voice coming from somewhere too far to the left, too casual, as if he'd been lagging on purpose. As if he'd wanted to see what they would do without him watching.

*He's counting us,* Ilsa thought. *He's been counting us since Vell's Landing.*

* * *

The shelter was older than any of them—a Karvethi waystation, half-swallowed by the slope, its stone walls furred with ice, its roof beam blackened by some fire lit decades ago and never fully forgiven. Cyrus got them inside by touch alone, hauling each of them through the low door like sacks of grain, and then spent ten full minutes with the lamp turned down to a blue whisper, checking the walls, the roofline, the single window slit, before allowing the flame higher.

Ilsa watched her do it. Watched the guide move through the small stone room with the economy of someone relearning a house they'd once lived in. Cyrus's hands knew where the old iron hooks were. Knew which corner drained. Knew that the third floor slab, when tapped, rang hollow over a cache space the Karvethi guides had used for emergency stores.

Cyrus knew this place because Cyrus had been here before. Eight years ago. With her father.

The realization settled over Ilsa like a second layer of snow.

They melted snow over a spirit stove, and the four of them ate in a silence broken only by the wind worrying at the window slit—a sound like fingernails, like patience. Tam was shaking. Not from cold; from the crevasse. From the fall that should have killed him, and from the rope that had come a heartbeat—or two heartbeats, or three—later than it should have.

Ilsa had seen it. She was almost certain she had seen it. Broch, standing at the lip with the coil in his hands, and that pause—that terrible, thoughtful pause, the pause of a man doing arithmetic rather than rescue work—before the rope went out.

She looked at Broch now across the lamplight. He sat against the far wall, rubbing his hands, his broad face arranged into weary good humor. "Weather like this," he said, "you'd think someone up there has opinions about our business."

No one laughed. The wind filled the space where laughter should have gone.

* * *

It was Tam who broke first, because Tam was young and honest and had nearly died. "The rope," he said suddenly, staring at his own hands. "At the crevasse. The rope."

Halim looked at him. "You slipped. You caught the edge. It happens."

"It happened slow." Tam's voice climbed. "I was hanging there and I could hear him—" He stopped, because Broch had gone very still, and stillness, Ilsa was learning, was its own kind of confession.

"The rope came when it came," Broch said mildly. "Storm wind. Bad footing."

"You didn't slip," Tam said to the room, to no one, to himself. "I stepped on crust that held all morning. I've crossed worse. I *know* how it happened, but I can't make it make sense—"

"I can."

Every head turned. Ilsa hadn't planned to speak. The words simply arrived, carried up from somewhere below her fear, from the place where her father's maps were inked into her skin in lines no one could confiscate.

She stood. Her legs felt borrowed. "I can make it make sense. Because I've been asking myself the same question since Vell's Landing. Why does a mining surveyor need the Karveth routes? Why does he ask about anchor points? Why does he walk at the back of the line and count us every morning?" She turned to Broch. "So let me save you the arithmetic. There are four of us. After today, how many were you ordered to bring down?"

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, the storm dragged its great white weight across the roof.

Broch smiled. It was the worst thing Ilsa had ever seen—not cruelty in it, but *relief*, the smile of a man setting down a load he'd carried too long. "Vale," he said to Cyrus, conversationally, "you always did hire talkative company."

"Cyrus," Ilsa said. "That's the name. Isn't it. Cyrus Amberdale." She watched the alias die in the guide's eyes, watched something older and harder surface beneath it. "And you're not a surveyor. You're the syndicate's broom. You sweep up after them. That's what they call people like you, isn't it? My father told me about them. Men who don't leave footprints."

Something moved in Broch's hand—toward his belt, toward the long knife he wore the way miners wear lamps, casually, professionally—and Cyrus moved faster than Ilsa would have believed possible, kicking the spirit stove between them so that flame and boiling water erupted across the stones. In the scramble, in the steam and shouting, Broch came up with the knife anyway, because men like Broch always came up with the knife.

What happened next took less time than the telling of it ever would. Tam lunged. Halim grabbed for a pole. Cyrus closed with Broch against the wall, and the two of them went around the small room like a storm in a bottle—elbow, blade, boot, breath—and then the window slit burst inward under their combined weight, admitting a fist of white wind, and Broch twisted free with the knife raised and his heel found the old ice-slicked threshold stone, the one Cyrus's feet had known and his had not—

—and the mountain took him.

There was no scream. That was the part Ilsa knew she would carry forever: there was no scream, just the sound of fabric catching once on the sill, and then nothing, and then the wind closing over the gap like water.

Cyrus knelt at the broken window for a long moment, one arm out into the blinding cold, finding nothing. Then drew it back. Then said, very quietly, in a voice Ilsa had never heard before—unarmored, ruined—"He hesitated at the crevasse. I saw it too. I told myself I'd imagined it." A breath. "I've imagined it before. Eight years ago. I told myself the same lie twice."

* * *

Afterward, no one slept. They rebuilt the window barricade with packs and stones while the storm screamed its one long note, and when the work was done there was nothing left between them and the truth except the truth itself.

"He was going to kill us," Tam whispered. "All of us. On the mountain. So it would look like—" He couldn't finish.

"Like my expedition," Cyrus said. "Like eight years ago. Three dead, blamed on weather that never existed. The syndicate doesn't send killers up mountains, Tam. It sends *weather*. It lets the mountain do the work and takes no fingerprints home." Cyrus fed the stove another measure of spirit, and the flame stood up straight and unsteady, like a small brave animal. "I pulled a chest out of the river in Vell's Landing with papers in it proving exactly that. Which is why we're being swept."

Ilsa sat down slowly on the cold stone. Her whole body was trembling now, and she understood it wasn't fear. It was the shaking of a structure that has held too long and is finally permitted to fail.

"My father didn't vanish," she said.

The others went still.

"Everyone says vanished. Disappeared. Lost on the Recovery Expedition, poor man, the mountain keeps its secrets." She laughed, and hated the sound of it. "The mountain doesn't keep secrets. *Men* keep secrets. My father sent a letter from the high camp—dated nine days after the 'storm.' Nine days. A dead man writing to his daughter about contour lines." Her voice cracked down the middle. "He'd found something up there. Something the passes were hiding, or something someone needed the passes to hide. And two weeks later the letter stopped, and the syndicate's story started, and every map of Karveth was pulled from every archive in two nations, and a guide named Amberdale was fed to the wolves so no one would ever go looking."

Cyrus had gone white. "He wrote to *you*?"

"Every week. Coded, mostly—survey notes, boring as prayer. But the last letter said—" Ilsa pressed her palms flat against her thighs, against the skin where, beneath wool and linen, the ink lived. "He said, *if anything keeps me, remember that I never write down what matters most. I carry it where no thief looks.*"

She began unlacing her left cuff.

"What matters most," she said, "is the route. The true route through the Serpent's Spine—all of it, every pass, every shelter, every anchor point, including the ones the syndicate cut and burned and pretended never existed. Because the original expedition wasn't looking for ore, whatever they told the investors. They were following my father's maps to something the old kingdom died protecting." She rolled the cuff to the elbow, and turned her forearm to the lamplight, and let them see.

The skin was a document. Fine blue-black lines ran from wrist to inner elbow—contours, elevations, cairn marks, a whole country written in a cartographer's careful hand, the handwriting Ilsa had known since before she could read print. Twenty years of hiding it under sleeves and lies. Twenty years of being a woman who flinched when strangers touched her arms.

Tam made a small sound. Halim murmured something in his own tongue, reverent.

"They killed him for this," Ilsa said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her; she had expected it to shatter. "For ink. For lines on skin. And I have spent my whole life deciding whether to show it to anyone, because showing it means the next Broch comes, and the one after him, until someone succeeds." She looked at Cyrus—at Cyrus Amberdale, disgraced, renamed, kneeling in the ruins of a Karvethi shelter with the storm howling down off the pass that had eaten their mutual past. "But I'm done deciding alone. You lost your name to their story. I lost my father to it. And tomorrow, if the sky clears even a hand's width, we walk down through terrain no living person is supposed to know—with your chest's papers and my father's map and whatever's waiting at the end of both."

"And if the sky doesn't clear?" Halim asked gently.

Ilsa looked at the barricaded window, at the white fury beyond it, and felt something settle in her chest that was not peace but was adjacent to it—the feeling of a decision already made.

"Then we wait," she said. "We're guides' children, all of us, one way or another. And the mountain remembers everything. It's time somebody remembered back."

Outside, the wind shifted—dropped, for one impossible moment, to a silence so complete they could hear the snow settling on the roof, soft as pages turning.

Then it rose again, colder than before, and somewhere far below them in the white, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of voices climbing.
