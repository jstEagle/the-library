## 9. Descent

The storm broke at dawn the way a fever breaks—suddenly, and leaving everything it touched changed. Cyrus stood in the mouth of the shelter and watched the clouds peel off the Serpent's Spine like bandages from a wound, revealing slopes scoured white and blue, the air so clean and cold it hurt to breathe it. Below them, the mountain went on falling for what looked like miles: ice fields, rock ribs, the dark stitch of the treeline far down where the world remembered how to be green.

Behind Cyrus, the shelter was a shambles of blood and broken gear. The scorch mark where Broch's stove had flared still blackened the wall. The place where Broch had gone over the edge was marked by nothing at all—snow had already begun to fill the trench of his passage, smoothing it over, the mountain tidying itself the way it always did, erasing men as casually as it erased footprints.

Ilsa emerged, wrapped in the last dry blanket, her face hollowed by the night. She did not look at the edge. She looked at Cyrus.

"How long do we have?"

"Until they find the lower camp and realize their man isn't answering." Cyrus checked the sky, the wind, the quality of the light—the old liturgy, performed by rote. "A day. Maybe less if they have a radio."

"And the way down?"

Cyrus turned to face her fully. "You said your father's map is in your head. I need it now. All of it."

Ilsa's jaw tightened. She had confessed it in the dark, in the worst hour of the night, with Broch's blood still freezing on the floor between them: her father had not abandoned the expedition, had not deserted, had not wandered into a whiteout of his own making. He had been murdered for his maps—killed in Vell's Landing by men who wanted the Karveth passes to stay empty, and his daughter had carried his knowledge out of the fire the only way she could, inked into memory, recited to herself for twenty years like a prayer against forgetting.

"I can give you the descent," she said. "But you have to understand what you're asking. My father mapped routes that were never meant to be walked. The Karvethi used them for smuggling, for escape, for things they didn't want on any road. They're not trails. They're *secrets*."

"Good," Cyrus said. "So are ours."

* * *

They left within the hour—Cyrus, Ilsa, and the two porters, Sef and old Tammas, whose arm hung wrong in its sling since the crevasse but who had refused, with a stubbornness Cyrus was beginning to revere, to be left behind. They took what the storm had left them: two tents, half the rope, a stove with a cracked burner, and the documents, wrapped in oilcloth and strapped to Cyrus's chest beneath the pack, riding against the heartbeat like a second, colder heart.

The route Ilsa described did not exist on any map Cyrus had ever bought or stolen. She called directions from memory the way a blind woman navigates a familiar room—*left at the split rock, down through the notch where the ice never melts, the shelf runs east for two hundred paces and then it doesn't.* And the mountain, impossibly, obeyed her. The split rock was there, fanged with ice. The notch was there, a blue throat of old snow. The shelf was there, narrow as a coffin lid, running east above a drop that went down and down into cloud.

"She walked this," Tammas said wonderingly, watching Ilsa move ahead of them with a certainty that had nothing to do with sight. "In her head. All these years."

"She had nothing else of him," Ilsa said, not turning around. "So yes. I walked it."

Cyrus followed, and felt something in the following that was almost like awe. For eight years Cyrus had known every published route of the Serpent's Spine, had dreamed them, had been ruined by one of them—and here was a woman walking the *unpublished* mountain, the true one, the one the syndicate had killed to keep blank. The Karvethi had known these passes the way a thief knows a house. And now, for a few desperate hours, so did they.

* * *

The second team found their trail at midday.

Cyrus saw it from the shelf—a smudge of movement far above and behind, where the storm shelter crouched against the rock, dark figures moving with the unhurried competence of professionals. Four of them. Maybe five. They paused at the shelter, at the edge, reading the mountain the way Cyrus read it, and then they began to descend. Not fast. *Sure.* That was the frightening thing. They were not chasing. They were herding.

"They'll cut the angle," Cyrus said. "They know where we have to come down. There's only one way off this face that isn't suicide, and they'll be waiting at the bottom of it."

"Then we don't take it," Ilsa said.

"There is no *then*. There's the route or there's the void."

"There's the *chimney*." Ilsa's eyes had gone distant, reading pages only she could see. "My father marked it. A gully behind the east rib—steep, ugly, full of loose rock, but it drops straight into the forest and comes out a mile west of the main route. He wrote that no Karvethi guide would take a client up it, but any of them would take a friend *down*."

"Loose rock," Tammas repeated, with the flat despair of a man who had already fallen once this expedition.

"Loose rock," Cyrus agreed, and looked at Sef, and at Tammas's arm, and made the arithmetic that guides make and hate. "Then we go now, and we go carefully, and nobody runs."

* * *

The chimney was everything Ilsa had promised and worse. It was a wound in the mountain's flank, choked with rubble that shifted under every placement, that sang and slithered and sometimes *moved*—whole sheets of it sighing downslope when Sef's boot found the wrong stone. They descended in a rhythm Cyrus set with their voice, one at a time on the bad pitches, rope on for the worst of them, the sun crawling down the sky above the eastern rib like something fleeing.

It was on the last bad pitch—a wall of rubble forty feet high, the final barrier before the slope gentled into scree and then, miracle of miracles, the first stunted pines—that Cyrus heard the rock start to move above them.

Not a stone. A *section*. The whole upper face of the chimney had been hanging on the frost all day, and Tammas, climbing with one arm, had set his weight on the keystone of it.

"Tammas—*off*—"

The old porter threw himself sideways and the mountain came down.

It was not a large slide, as slides go. It was forty feet of rock and old snow, and it caught Tammas at the shoulder and carried him fifteen feet down the gully and pinned him against a pine trunk at the bottom, half-buried, and Cyrus was already moving—down the pitch without setting protection, without rope, without the careful arithmetic that had kept them alive all day, hands and boots finding holds by instinct older than thought, the slide's dust still hanging in the air like a held breath.

And in the middle of the descent, one terrible instant, Cyrus stopped.

Because the documents were on Cyrus's chest, and the pitch was rotten, and a fall here—a fall with the oilcloth packet—was the end of everything. The proof. The names. The whole engineered disaster laid out in ink. Eight years of ruin, and Ilsa's murdered father, and the three dead clients whose faces Cyrus still saw in every whiteout—all of it riding against Cyrus's heart, and the smart move, the *guide's* move, the move that had been beaten into Cyrus by eight years of exile, was to back off, to anchor, to protect the packet first and dig for the man second.

Cyrus had made that calculation once before. Eight years ago, on the glacier, in the wind, with three clients dying and the evidence of what had killed them strapped to a sled. *Secure the documents. The documents are the mission.* Someone else had made it for them, and Cyrus had obeyed, and the obedience had cost everything.

"Tammas." Cyrus dug. "Tammas, I've got you."

The old man's face was grey, one eye swollen shut, blood in his beard. His good hand found Cyrus's wrist and gripped it with astonishing strength.

"You went past the packet," he rasped. "I felt it. You went *past* it."

"Shut up," Cyrus said, and hauled him free of the rock, and the packet rode against Cyrus's chest, intact, irrelevant, alive.

* * *

They came out of the treeline at dusk, into the long meadow country below the Spine, where the snow gave way to wet grass and the air smelled, unbelievably, of pine and thawing earth. Behind and above, the mountain stood against the last light, and somewhere on it the second team was still descending—Cyrus could feel them the way one feels weather, a pressure on the back of the neck.

They had a day's lead. Perhaps. They had a porter with a broken arm and another with a wrenched knee, a cracked stove, half a rope, and a route no living person should have known, delivered by a dead man's daughter.

And they had the documents. And they had each other, which was more than the mountain had ever let Cyrus keep before.

Sef built a fire, small and screened, because wet and cold kill as surely as syndicates. Ilsa sat with Tammas, splinting the arm with a tent pole, her hands steady, her eyes on the dark wall of the trees. Cyrus stood watch at the meadow's edge and looked back at the Spine, at the passes that had eaten a nation and a career and nearly, tonight, an old man's life.

Eight years ago, Cyrus had come down from that mountain alone, carrying proof and shame in equal measure, and had buried the proof and kept the shame. Tonight everyone was coming down. That was all. That was everything. The arithmetic had changed, and Cyrus had changed it, one burned minute at a time.

The fire crackled. Somewhere in the dark, far off, a branch snapped—not deer, not wind. Cyrus did not wake the others. There would be time enough for fear in Vell's Landing, where the tribunal sat, where the warrants waited, where the truth would finally have to stand up in the light and give its name.

Cyrus touched the packet through the shirt, over the heartbeat.

"Hold on," Cyrus murmured to it, to the dead, to the living. "We're almost home."

And down in the valley, at the meadow's far edge where the forest gave way to the river road, a lantern swung—once, twice, deliberately—the signal of men who had found the trail after all.
