## 10. A Provision for the Wreck

The river had given the chest back to Vell's Landing in pieces, and in a way, so had the mountain. Cyrus stood on the tribunal steps at dawn with the documents wrapped in oilcloth against their chest, watching the fog burn off the water, and understood that whatever happened inside that building, they would not walk out as Maren Vale.

The town smelled of pitch and frying fish and the green rot of the spring flood, the same smells it had worn eight years ago when Cyrus had first crept into it under a name not their own. Back then the anonymity had felt like a bandage. Now it felt like a burial shroud they were finally allowed to cut open.

Ilsa waited at the foot of the steps, dressed in travel-worn wool that no longer pretended to be anything but what it was. Her hands were steady. On her left forearm, beneath the pushed-up sleeve, the inked lines of her father's maps ran from wrist to elbow like a river system of their own—passes and elevations and the small careful annotations of a man who had loved a country enough to die for its geography.

"They'll want you to say it quietly," she said. "Tribunals like quiet."

"I know."

"You could still take the ferry north. The warrants haven't been sworn yet. I've asked around."

Cyrus looked at her. Below them, the river slid past the pilings, brown and patient, keeping whatever it kept. "Eight years ago three people died on that mountain because men with ledgers decided a country was worth more than a rope team. If I go north, they win by default. I've done my default already." They touched the oilcloth bundle. "Besides. Maren Vale has nothing left to lose. That's rather the point of her."

Ilsa almost smiled. It was a thin thing, weather-cracked, but real. "Then let's go ruin some very respectable people's morning."

* * *

The tribunal chamber had once been a customs house, and it retained the architecture of judgment: high windows, a magistrate's bench of black walnut gone silver with age, a rail of scarred oak where petitioners had gripped until the wood shone. Three magistrates sat behind the bench—a border panel, one from each of the nations that shared the Serpent's Spine, plus a neutral chair from the Concordat whose face suggested she had heard every kind of lie and expected this morning to be no different.

The clerk read the charges against Cyrus Amberdale before Cyrus had even surrendered the alias aloud: negligent homicide in the deaths of three clients of the Karveth Recovery Expedition; flight across two borders; false registration of identity in the territory of Vell's Landing.

"How do you answer?" asked the senior magistrate, a heavy woman with a voice like gravel poured slow.

"Guilty," Cyrus said, "to the last charge only. And I'm here to prove the first two were manufactured."

A murmur moved through the gallery like wind through scree. The syndicate had sent observers—Cyrus recognized two faces from the boathouse night, from Broch's descriptions dragged down off the mountain, from the ledger pages themselves. A man named Corvane sat in the third row with the composure of someone who owned the room he was renting. He caught Cyrus's eye and inclined his head slightly, a courtesy between professionals. *We can still buy you*, the gesture said.

Cyrus turned to the bench and unwrapped the oilcloth.

What came out was not dramatic. That was the thing about truth, Cyrus thought—it rarely looked like fire. It looked like paper. Survey reports bearing the Karveth cartographic office's seal, dated six weeks before the nation was struck from every map. A mining consortium's internal ledger, listing payments to a weather service that had never issued a forecast for the upper passes—and to an expedition outfitter who had supplied the Karveth Recovery Expedition with anchor line rated for half the load it claimed. Photographs of the pass where the original survey party had found the vein of ore so rich it would have made Karveth solvent for a century—the vein the consortium needed buried along with the country.

And last, laid down gently, the testimony of the mountain itself: the cut anchor lines Cyrus had carried down on their own back, frayed ends clean-sliced, preserved in wax.

"The storm that killed my clients," Cyrus said, "never existed. No forecast recorded it. No other expedition in the range logged it. What did exist was a sabotage kit and a payment schedule, and I have both here. I led those people up the mountain. Their deaths are mine to carry—I chose the route, I trusted the equipment, I lived when they didn't. But I did not kill them, and the men who did are sitting in your gallery."

Corvane did not move. But beside him, one of his companions did—a small motion, a hand sliding inside a coat—and two constables who had been waiting, Cyrus realized, since before dawn, crossed the gallery and took him by the arms. Not arrest. Escort. But the meaning traveled the room all the same: the ledger had names in it, and the Concordat's neutral magistrate had spent the night reading them.

The hearing lasted nine hours. Cyrus testified through all of them—about the flood, the chest, the seal that had risen out of the water like a drowned man's hand; about the boathouse and the courier's warning; about Broch, whose body had never been found in the white and whose orders, reconstructed from the syndicate's own correspondence, had been to ensure no witnesses descended. About Ilsa Reyne, who rose when called and gave her father's name to the record for the first time in twenty years, and watched a court stenographer write it down as though writing it down could raise the dead.

It couldn't. But it could make the killing of him mean something, and some mornings that had to be enough.

* * *

The verdicts came at dusk, delivered in the flat cadence of law.

The false-registration charge stood; Cyrus pleaded to it and accepted a fine that would take most of what remained of Maren Vale's savings. The homicide charges were referred onward—two nations wanted Cyrus Amberdale for questioning, and neither cared much that the questioning might end in exoneration, because process was process and borders were borders. Warrants would be sworn within the week. Cyrus could expect to spend a season or more giving depositions in cities they had spent eight years avoiding.

"Do you understand these terms?" the senior magistrate asked.

"Yes," Cyrus said. And then, because the moment demanded more than yes: "I'd like the record to show I'm not surrendering to them. I'm choosing them. There's a difference, and I need it written down."

The magistrate looked at Cyrus for a long moment over the silvered bench. "The record will show it," she said.

Outside, the fog had come back off the river, and the lamps of Vell's Landing burned soft halos along the waterfront. Ilsa stood waiting under the eave of the customs house, a packed bag at her feet. She looked lighter—not happy, nothing so simple, but unburdened in the way of someone who has set down a weight and found her spine still works.

"The northern warrant is the aggressive one," she said. "Their prosecutors are thorough. You'll be in deposition rooms until your hair goes grey."

"I have a great deal to tell them."

"And after?"

Cyrus considered the question honestly, which was itself a novelty. After. After the depositions, after the trials of the men in the ledger, after the syndicate finished tearing itself apart the way it now visibly was—three of its partners already turning crown's evidence, Corvane's escort becoming something less gentle by the hour. After the maps went into archives and the pass got a new name on new maps, and Karveth, dead twenty years, became a footnote that at least told the truth.

"After," Cyrus said, "I thought I might guide again. Properly. Under my own name, on routes I choose, with clients I vet myself." A breath of laughter escaped them, surprising in its warmth. "Though I may insist on a clause in every contract. A provision for the wreck, you might call it—if the mountain takes us, my family gets the truth, whatever it costs anyone."

Ilsa pulled up her sleeve and looked at the inked rivers of her father's hand. "He used to say a map was a promise. That whoever drew it owed the traveler honesty." She rolled the sleeve down. "Come north with me when the warrants do. Testify there too. I know a road that isn't on any map anymore, and I remember every mile of it."

They shook hands on it, formally, like guides sealing an expedition—which, in a way, they were.

Cyrus walked down to the water alone. The river moved dark and unhurried past the pilings, carrying silt and spring melt and everything else it kept. Eight years ago it had taken a reputation; a month ago it had given back a chest; and now it held only water, going where water goes.

"Maren Vale's boat is for sale," Cyrus told it. "You can keep the name."

Somewhere upstream, in the high white silence of the Serpent's Spine, the pass waited under snow that would melt and reveal whatever it still had to reveal. Cyrus Amberdale stood on the dock in full view of the lamplight, under their own name, wanted by two governments, free for exactly as long as it took the paperwork to catch up—and for the first time in eight years, that felt like standing on solid ground.

The warrants would come. Let them. Some things were worth being found for.
