## 1. Fathoms and Miles

I measure my life in miles, not years. Years are for people who stay put — people with gardens, mortgages, and photographs of themselves aging in the same kitchen. I have a duffel bag older than most marriages and a habit of leaving towns before anyone learns to spell my name right. Delacroix. Two syllables of trouble, apparently, wherever I go.

The miles, though, those I keep honest. Forty-one thousand nautical since I got my first commercial ticket at nineteen. I could lay them end to end like rope and moor myself to something, if I wanted. I don't want. Moorings are how ships rot.

Cape Verren was mile forty-one thousand and change, and it was supposed to be a simple job: survey work on a wreck off the point, salvage assessment for an insurance outfit that paid late but paid. The *Corvane*, a coastal freighter that went down in '09 with a hold full of machinery nobody had ever been in a hurry to recover. Cold water, gray water, water with opinions. My kind of office.

What it wasn't supposed to be was interesting.

* * *

The morning of the third day, I went down at slack tide with a slate, a torch, and the particular optimism of a man who has done this four thousand times and only nearly died twice. The *Corvane* sat on her starboard side in ninety feet of green murk, hull furred with anemones, deck crusted in mussels so thick she looked like she'd grown a beard out of shame.

Ninety feet is nothing. Ninety feet is a walk to the corner store, if the corner store were dark, crushing, and full of things that used to be people's livelihoods. I've worked three hundred feet. I've worked places where the water was so cold your lips went stupid and you had to write notes to yourself because your own voice inside your skull started slurring. Ninety feet should have been a vacation.

Except somebody had been there before me. Recently.

The first sign was the anchor line — not mine, not the insurance company's. A clean synthetic line, new within the season, running down into the silt cloud near the freighter's stern hold. The second sign was the hatch. The aft cargo hatch had been dogged shut from the outside. Welded, actually, and the welds were fresh enough that the rust hadn't even gotten its boots on yet. Somebody had sealed a ship that had been sitting open on the seafloor for fifteen years, and they'd done it underwater, which takes equipment, planning, and a reason.

I ran my glove along the weld seam. Even through the neoprene I could feel how deliberate it was. This wasn't looters patching a hole. Looters open things. Only one kind of organization closes things.

I wrote on my slate: *WHO WELDS A WRECK SHUT?* and then, because the cold makes a man philosophical, *AND WHO PAYS FOR THE GAS?*

That was when the current shifted and brought me the sound. Down there you don't hear much — your own breathing, the crackle of the reef going about its day. But propellers carry through water like gossip through a small town, and this one was close, and it wasn't fishing-boat idle. It was the heavy, patient churn of something naval.

I surfaced beside my boat to find two things I hadn't ordered: a gray patrol cutter sitting four hundred yards off my stern, and a rigid inflatable making straight for me with two men aboard who wore their uniforms the way some men wear bad news.

* * *

Now, I've been run off dive sites before. It comes with the trade. Coastal authorities, harbor masters, one memorable incident in the tropics involving a resort owner, a shotgun, and a misunderstanding about a wedding ring. You learn the choreography. They bluster, you pack up, everybody pretends the ocean belongs to someone, which is the funniest joke ever told on salt water.

These two didn't bluster.

The inflatable came alongside and the younger of the officers caught my gunwale with a gloved hand — navy gloves, not the kind you buy at a chandlery — and looked at me the way a customs officer looks at a suitcase he already knows about.

"Delacroix." He said it like a diagnosis. "Ansel Delacroix."

"That's what the boat says." It did. Painted on the transom, because I'm not a secretive man, just a private one, and there's a difference I spend a lot of time explaining to people with badges.

"You're contracted for survey work on the *Corvane*."

"I am. Though I notice you know more about my contract than I told the harbor office. Which means either you read minds or you read paperwork, and your face suggests paperwork."

The older officer — lieutenant commander by his lace, weathered, with the kind of tan that comes from standing on bridge wings rather than beaches — let the younger one do the talking. He just watched me. There was a particular quality to the watching. I've seen it before, twenty years ago, from men on a pier in dress whites telling a sixteen-year-old boy that his brother's body hadn't been recovered and wouldn't be, and that the incident file was closed, and that further inquiries would be — and here I remember the exact phrase, because phrases like that tattoo themselves — *not constructive.*

"You're to cease operations immediately," the younger one said. "This site is closed pending a security review. Your permit's revoked as of this morning."

"My permit was issued six weeks ago."

"And it's revoked as of this morning." He handed over a folded sheet. Official letterhead. The revocation was dated, stamped, signed by someone whose signature looked like a seismograph reading during an earthquake. No reason given. There was a line where a reason would go, and instead there was a reference number, which is the bureaucratic way of saying *because we said so, and the saying-so has a serial number.*

"What's the security concern?" I asked. "She's a freighter full of dead machinery. Unless the machinery joined a union down there, I can't see the threat."

"The site is closed."

"See, that's the part I'm having trouble with." I kept my voice pleasant, the way you keep a knife pleasant while you're cleaning it. "Because I was down there this morning, and somebody welded the aft cargo hatch shut. Recently. That's not security review work. That's containment work. So either you tell me what's in that hold, or I start asking around, and I'll tell you now, I ask around the way other men breathe. Constantly, and without being asked."

Something moved behind the older officer's eyes. Not anger. Recognition. He knew what I was doing, because he'd done it himself once, probably, back before rank sandblasted the curiosity off him.

"Mister Delacroix." His voice was quiet, and quiet is worse than loud. Loud is for show. Quiet is for meaning. "You're a long way from anywhere, alone, in a boat with a single engine. The water off Cape Verren has taken better-equipped men than you."

It took me a second to understand what he'd actually said, because the words were so ordinary and the meaning was so far from ordinary. Then I understood, and something in my chest went very still and very old.

"Is that a threat?"

"It's a safety advisory," he said. "The sea is dangerous. We'd hate to see anything happen to you." And then, stepping back into the inflatable, almost as an afterthought, almost gently: "Some wrecks stay closed for good reasons, Delacroix. Take the advice. Go home."

They pulled away. The cutter turned a slow, expensive circle and headed north, and the wake came rolling under my hull a minute later, rocking me like a hand on the shoulder from someone I didn't want touching me.

* * *

I sat in the cockpit for a long time after that, drinking coffee gone bitter in the thermos, watching the water over the *Corvane* do its flat, innocent impression of nothing happening.

Here's the thing about grief that nobody warns you: it doesn't age. Everything else ages. Your knees, your eyesight, your patience for other people's nonsense. But grief just sits there in you like ballast, exactly the weight it was on the day it was loaded, and every so often the sea of your life rolls a certain way and it all shifts to one side and suddenly you're listing hard and taking water.

Tomas Delacroix went into the water twenty years ago this spring, and he did not come out. Twenty-two years old, certified, careful, the responsible brother, the one who checked my gear and made me check his — which is why it never made sense, none of it ever made sense, that Tomas of all people didn't surface. He was diving a contract job off Cape Verren, same waters, different wreck, and the official account was equipment failure, and the official account was delivered by men in uniform whose faces did the thing faces do when they're reciting something they've been told to recite.

I was sixteen. I spent that whole summer diving the search grid on my own ticket, illegally, half-trained, until they confiscated my gear and escorted me off the water and told me, again, that further inquiries would be not constructive.

Not constructive. As if grief were a building project and my brother's death was a zoning problem.

I learned to live around it the way you learn to live around a hole in a deck plate. You mark it. You step over it. You don't look down. And for twenty years I didn't look down — I just dove. Every job, every coast, every mile. People assumed it was because I loved the work, and I do, God help me, I love it, the silence and the weightlessness and the way the world below forgives nothing and hides everything. But the truth is uglier and simpler: I never stopped searching. Not consciously. Consciously I told myself I was a man who liked being paid to swim. Unconsciously I was a boy sweeping a grid with a torch, looking for a shape in the silt, and every wreck I entered was the same wreck, and every year the water stayed the same temperature as the day it took him.

And now, today, two officers had come out of nowhere to seal a hatch I'd found freshly welded, revoke a permit without cause, and advise me — politely, quietly, with the whole gray ocean standing witness — that the water off Cape Verren takes people.

The same waters. The same phrasing. Twenty years apart.

You can tell a man coincidence twice, maybe three times, before he stops buying.

* * *

I weighed anchor at noon, mostly because staying anchored there felt like agreeing with them, and partly because I had a plan forming, and plans need fuel, and fuel needs a harbor, and harbors need taverns, and taverns — well. Taverns are where the truth goes to drink.

The run back to Cape Verren's little port town took two hours against a chop that slapped the hull like it had a grievance. I used the time the way I use all empty time: cataloguing. It's a diver's habit. Below, you catalogue everything — gauges, depth, bottom time, the exact angle of light — because the men who don't catalogue become statistics. Above water I catalogue facts, and I laid them out on the deck of my mind and walked around them.

Fact: the *Corvane*'s aft hold was sealed with fresh welds by someone with military-grade equipment.

Fact: the permit revocation came within hours of my finding it, which meant they were watching the site, which meant they'd been watching before I arrived.

Fact: the older officer knew my name before I gave it, and my name isn't on any survey manifest worth a naval officer's attention.

Fact: twenty years ago, in these same waters, my brother died, and the file closed like a hatch dogged shut from the outside.

I stood at the wheel with the wind trying to take my hat and thought: *somebody, somewhere, keeps a list of things that must stay drowned.* And I thought: *my brother might be on it.*

There's a moment in every dive — every real dive, past the fun depth, into the cold and the dark — when the noise of the surface falls away and you hear yourself breathing, and you have to decide, consciously, deliberately, whether to keep going down. Most people turn back. The ones who don't are either fools or divers, and the difference between us is mostly paperwork.

I felt that moment arrive right there at the wheel, in broad daylight, dry as a sermon. Keep going down, or turn back. Take the advice. Go home. Find another contract, another coast, another forty thousand miles of stepping over the hole in the deck plate.

Or.

Or find out why the navy welds hatches shut on a ship full of nothing, and why my family name made an officer's mouth go quiet, and what exactly it is about Cape Verren that requires guarding after all these years.

I'm not a brave man. I want that on the record. Brave men run toward things; I've spent my whole life running along things, parallel, sideways, keeping the danger at a comfortable oblique angle. But there's a species of stubbornness that looks like courage from a distance, and I've got a double helping of it, inherited fair and square from a mother who once argued with a hurricane about the placement of her clothesline.

The harbor lights came up out of the dusk, strung along the quay like a necklace someone had dropped. I throttled down and watched them grow, and I made my decision the way I make all my decisions: slowly, reluctantly, and then all at once, with no exit ramp.

Tomorrow I'd pull the harbor records. Every wreck, every closure order, every incident report filed off this cape in the last twenty-five years. Somewhere in that paper trail was the reason for the welds, and I'd bet my boat — the one asset I own outright, the closest thing I have to a soul — that somewhere in that paper trail was also a date, and a name, and a word I'd been circling for twenty years without landing on.

I tied off at the quay, shouldered the duffel, and paused with one foot on the dock.

Behind me, the water lay black and smooth over the *Corvane*, over whatever was in her hold, over everything the tide had ever been paid to keep. It looked peaceful. Water always does. That's the first lie it tells you.

"Twenty years," I told it. "You can keep him a little longer. But I'm coming back down, and this time I'm not looking for a body."

The water said nothing, which is its second-favorite lie.

I walked up the quay toward the light and the noise of the taverns, toward whatever was waiting there — though if you'd told me then that a dying man with a map was about to hand me the thread that would unravel the whole twenty years, I'd have called you a liar, bought you a drink, and gone back to worrying about my permit.

The sea takes. I've always known that. What I learned that night, over bad stew and worse whiskey, was that sometimes it also gives things back — and it never, ever gives them back gently.
