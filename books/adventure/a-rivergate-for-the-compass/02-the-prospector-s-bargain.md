## 2. The Prospector's Bargain

The Salt Lark smelled of tar, spilled beer, and the particular despair of men who had come to Cape Verren to make their fortunes and were now drinking what little remained of them. I fit right in. I'd taken a corner table with my back to the wall and a view of the door, which is not paranoia in my line of work so much as good posture.

I was three fingers into something the barman called whiskey and my tongue called an apology when the old man came in out of the rain.

I noticed him for the wrong reasons at first — because he moved like a man carrying cargo that didn't belong to him anymore, some weight his body had stopped agreeing to hold. He was gaunt in the way that isn't diet but disease, cheekbones like hull ribs showing through weathered canvas skin. His coat had been good once, twenty years and two owners ago. He stood just inside the door a long moment, letting the lamplight find him, and I watched him do a slow inventory of the room the way you'd survey water before a dive: checking for hazards, checking for exits, checking whether the bottom would hold.

His eyes snagged on me and stayed.

I've been looked at by a lot of people in a lot of taverns. Most of them look through me, or past me, or at the empty chair beside me hoping it stays empty. This man looked *at* me, deliberate as a sounding line dropped straight down, and whatever he found on the bottom made him cross the room.

"You're the diver," he said. Not a question. He lowered himself into the chair across from mine without being invited, and the effort of sitting down cost him something he tried not to show on his face.

"I'm a diver," I allowed. "Whether I'm *the* diver depends on who's asking."

"Elias Wren." He said it like a man laying a card face-up. "And I'm asking because they told me at the chandlery there's only one man fool enough to dive the Verren shelf this time of year, and that he drinks here, and that he sits facing the door like he's expecting trouble."

"Your sources are thorough."

"My sources are dying." He said it flat, no self-pity in it, and that's what told me he wasn't exaggerating. Men who are lying about dying milk it. Men who mean it set it down like a tool they're finished with. "Sit still, Delacroix. I know your name too. Ansel Delacroix. Brother Tomas. Lost off this coast — or near enough to it — twenty years back. You've spent every season since pulling other people's junk out of cold water, and every winter asking the water questions it won't answer."

The whiskey went tasteless in my mouth. "Who told you all that?"

"The same people who told me about the wreck you've been diving. The one the navy's so tender about." His smile was thin as ice on a spring creek. "You've been asking questions. I heard. Questions have a way of traveling in this town. Like rats. Like plague."

I should have ended the conversation there. Any sensible man would have. The navy had already leaned on me once this week — two officers with creases in their trousers sharp enough to draw blood, telling me my salvage permit covered wreckage and not curiosity, and smiling while they said it, which is always worse. A sensible man hears *the navy is interested* and develops a sudden passion for fishing, or religion, or anywhere else.

But I was twenty years tired, and grief makes a poor counselor, and there was something in the old man's eyes —

I'll get to the eyes. They matter. But first he put his hand inside his coat, slow, telegraphing the movement so I wouldn't reach for the knife I don't carry, and drew out a oilskin packet wrapped in twine. He set it on the table between us like a man setting down a debt.

"Open it."

I didn't touch it. "What is it?"

"A map."

"I've seen maps."

"Not like this one." He unwound the twine himself, fingers clumsy with a tremor he couldn't disguise, and folded back the oilcloth. Inside was a sheet of heavy paper gone soft at the edges, covered in a hand-drawn cartography so fine it looked engraved — contour lines, elevations, a river valley rendered in ink by someone who understood both rivers and valleys down to their bones. And across the top, in the same sure hand: *Rivergate.*

The name landed somewhere under my sternum and sat there.

"Rivergate," I said carefully. "There's no Rivergate. There's a reservoir."

"There's a reservoir *named after* nothing," Elias said. "They drowned the name along with everything else. But there was a Rivergate. Township. Four hundred souls, a church, a schoolhouse, a sawmill, and a graveyard that never got moved because moving graves costs money and dead men don't sue." He tapped the map with one yellowed nail. "I walked that valley before the water came. Every inch of it. I drew this from memory afterward, and my memory has never once been wrong about country I've walked."

"Why are you showing me this?"

"Because in nine days—" He stopped. Coughed, deep and wet, into a rag that came away spotted dark, and folded it away with the practiced discretion of a man who'd been doing this for months. "Because in nine days, none of this matters anymore unless it's in the right hands. And my hands are spoken for."

"Spoken for by what?"

"By the ground, eventually. Sooner than that if the weather turns." He leaned forward, and up close I could smell the sickness on him, sweetish and wrong, under the woodsmoke and rain. "Delacroix. I'm going to make you an offer. I want you to listen to all of it before you say no, because you're going to want to say no fast, and I've paid for the privilege of five minutes of your stubbornness with the last coin I intend to spend tonight."

I looked at the door. I looked at the barman, who was suddenly very interested in polishing glasses at the far end of the room. I looked at the map, at that word *Rivergate* sitting above contour lines of a place that officially did not exist.

"Five minutes," I said. Which was, I realized later, the first promise I made him.

* * *

Here is what Elias Wren bought with his five minutes:

He talked, and I listened, and the tavern noise seemed to arrange itself around us like water around a stone. He'd prospected the high country for forty years, he said. Knew the passes better than the survey office did. Twenty years ago — and his voice didn't change register when he said it, which somehow made it worse — he'd been working claims in the range east of Verren when the government came for Rivergate valley. Dam project. Eminent domain. Standard story.

Except the townsfolk hadn't sold. Except there had been a fire. Except eleven men from the township went into the official record as *departed prior to proceedings*, which is a remarkable trick for men whose families swore they'd never left, and except that a young prospector named Elias Wren had seen things that week in the high timber that the navy's investigators never asked him about, because nobody asked him anything, because asking questions in those mountains that autumn was a way of joining the departed.

"So you ran," I said.

"I *left*," he corrected. "Running implies you might stop. I left and I kept leaving for twenty years. Different valleys, different names, different women who deserved better than a man who flinched at navy uniforms." He took a pull of beer without tasting it. "And then this summer I got sick, and sick men stop running, because the ground catches up with them regardless. And I found out something that made the running worse than useless."

"What?"

"That the reservoir's coming down. Emergency drainage — dam fault, they say, though I'd wager my last tooth the fault is older and more human than concrete. Nine days, maybe ten, and the valley floor sees daylight for the first time in twenty years." His eyes came up and fixed on mine. "And certain parties intend to be standing on that valley floor when it does. With shovels. With fire, if it comes to it. Making sure the ground gives up nothing but mud."

"And you want—"

"I want my daughter to have what I couldn't deliver myself." He slid the map across the table until it touched my knuckles. It was warm from his coat, which unsettled me more than anything else he'd said. "Mara Wren. Kestrel Crossing, over the range. She runs the forge there — farrier work, horseshoes, the kind of honest iron her father never gave her. She hasn't seen me since she was fourteen, and she'll spit at my name, and she'll be right to. But this map goes to her. Not to the navy, not to a newspaper, not to any grave-robber or hero or fool with good intentions. To *her*. Before the passes close. After that, the snow takes the choice away from everyone."

"Why her? What is it to her?"

"It's hers by blood. That's all I can tell you tonight, and it's more than I've told anyone in twenty years." He coughed again, shorter this time, angrier at his own body. "Delacroix. I'll pay you. Name a figure. I've got sixty dollars and a mule and a watch that doesn't run, and you can have all of it, and I'll die owing you the difference."

"I don't want your money."

"Everybody wants money."

"Not for this." I pushed the map back toward him. "Elias. Listen to me. I dive wrecks. I haul up engine parts and brass fittings and the occasional dead man's boot, and I drink the proceeds in rooms exactly like this one. I don't carry messages over mountain ranges for dying strangers. I especially don't do it when the navy's already told me twice this month to keep my nose on my own side of the waterline. Whatever's buried in that valley, it's buried deeper than I dig. Find yourself a courier. Find a priest. Find anybody whose life isn't already a locked room."

He took the rebuke the way stones take rain. Then he said, quietly:

"Your brother dove that water."

The tavern went very far away.

"Tomas Delacroix went into the reservoir eight years ago — no. Nineteen years ago, that's right, a year after the flooding, when the navy still issued diving permits to civilians and hadn't learned to seal the whole shoreline. Hired quiet, paid quiet, by a prospector who needed something recovered from the flooded valley. Never surfaced. Navy ruled it equipment failure, closed the file, sealed the site, and posted the shoreline against salvage." Elias's voice stayed level, almost gentle, which was its own kind of cruelty. "You've dived that water every season since. Looking. Don't insult us both by denying it — I watched you once, from the ridge, years ago. You swim that reservoir like a man visiting a grave. Because you are."

My hands were flat on the table. I don't remember putting them there. "If you say another word about my brother—"

"He was hired by me."

The sentence went through me like a hook through the gill. The tavern noise came back all at once, too loud, and then receded again.

"You're lying," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's.

"I've done many things I'm ashamed of, boy, but lying about Tomas Delacroix isn't one of them. He was a good diver. Better than good. He came down from the north country with recommendations and a laugh like a dog getting up, and I hired him to bring something up out of that drowned church, and I waited three days at the shore camp, and he never came up, and I have carried that alongside everything else ever since." His eyes were wet now, and he let them be. "So understand what I'm offering you. Not a job. Not charity. A thread. Your brother's death and my cowardice are tied together in the same knot, and the map is the end of the rope, and I am handing you the end of the rope because I haven't got the strength to climb it myself."

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to; my legs simply concluded the conversation was over and acted accordingly. I put two coins on the table for the whiskey I hadn't tasted and turned for the door.

"Ansel."

Nobody had called me by my first name since my mother died. I stopped. I hated myself for stopping.

"The passes close in twelve days," Elias Wren said behind me. "I'll be dead in six. You can walk out that door and go back to your cold water and your locked room, and I will die knowing I tried, and Mara will die never knowing, and the valley will give up its dead to whatever man stands on it first. Or you can take the map, and take my mule, and take the worst bargain of your life, and maybe — *maybe* — the water gives something back for once."

I turned around. I meant to tell him no. I had the whole speech loaded — about self-preservation, about sense, about how a man who measures his life in miles traveled can't afford detours into other people's graves.

Then I looked at his eyes.

That's the thing I said I'd get to. They were brown, ordinary brown, river-mud brown, but they had a clarity in them that sick men aren't supposed to have — the clarity of a man who has already done all his crying and all his bargaining with God and arrived at a ledger balanced to the final entry. He wasn't begging. He wasn't even hoping, quite. He was simply *offering*, the way you offer water to a stranger in a drought, without any certainty they'll take it, because withholding it would be the one thing you couldn't live with.

Tomas had eyes like that. That's the truth of why I sat back down. Not the map, not the mystery, not the navy, not the knot of our shared dead. Just two pairs of brown eyes, twenty years apart, both willing to hand a stranger everything and ask nothing guaranteed in return.

"One condition," I said.

"Name it."

"When I find her — if I find her — I tell her the truth. All of it. Including the part where her father hired my brother and got him killed. She gets the whole ugly ledger, not the edited version."

Something moved through the old man's face — pain, relief, gratitude, hard to say which, probably all three fighting for the window. "She'd smell a lie anyway," he said. "Ironworker's nose. Yes. All of it."

"And I keep the map after delivery. If she doesn't want it, it comes back to me, and I decide what happens next. No conditions from beyond any graves."

"Done." He pushed the oilskin packet across the table, and this time I took it. It weighed almost nothing. Things that change your life usually do. "There's a mule stabled at the Harbormaster's Lane livery, gray, bad-tempered, answers to Biscuit when she feels like it. Saddlebags are packed. There's sixty dollars sewn into the left pannier — it's yours, argue with me and I'll haunt you."

"You can't afford to haunt anyone. You'll be too busy haunting yourself."

For the first time that night, Elias Wren laughed. It was a terrible sound, wet and rusty, and it turned heads at the bar, and he didn't care a bit. "There he is," he said. "Tomas's brother. I wondered where he'd gone." He stood, slowly, using the table, using the wall, using the last of whatever kept him upright, and shrugged into his ruined coat. At the door he paused, rain silvering the light around him, and looked back.

"Kestrel Crossing," he said. "Ask for the forge. And Ansel — the girl spits first and thinks second. Let her."

Then he was gone into the rain, and I sat alone with a map to a drowned town, sixty dollars I didn't want, a bad-tempered mule waiting in a livery, and the growing understanding that I had just been talked into the exact thing I'd sworn off five minutes earlier.

I ordered another whiskey. Drank it properly this time, all the way down, feeling it burn a line from my throat to my stomach like a fuse.

Twelve days. Over the range, ahead of the snow, to a woman who would spit at her father's name and had every right to. Carrying a dead man's map through country where the navy had already told me twice to mind my own waters.

I should have felt afraid. Mostly I felt something I hadn't felt in twenty years, and it took me a while to name it, sitting alone in the Salt Lark with the rain coming down outside.

It was momentum.

I paid, I pulled my collar up, and I went out into the wet to collect a mule named Biscuit — and if I'd known then what was waiting for me in Kestrel Crossing, I'd have walked faster.
