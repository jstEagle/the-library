## 3. What the Navy Won't Discuss

The Harbor Registry Office smelled of mildew and old coffee and the particular despair of men who had spent thirty years alphabetizing other people's disasters.

I knew the place the way I knew my own hands. I'd been in and out of it since I was nineteen years old, first with Tomas beside me, then without him, pulling charts and wreck surveys and tide tables for every job that paid and a few that didn't. The clerk behind the counter — a narrow man named Prewitt with spectacles thick as portholes — looked up when I came in and sighed the sigh of a man who already regretted whatever was about to happen to him.

"Delacroix," he said. "You're not supposed to be here."

"Good morning to you too, Prewitt."

"You were ordered off the *Corvus* site yesterday. By naval officers." He said it the way you'd say *by wolves*. "There's a memorandum. There's always a memorandum now. If you're here to request anything touching that site, I'm required to tell you the file is restricted."

"I'm not here about the site," I said, which was true, in the way that most useful lies are true. "I'm here about an old chart. Pre-dam survey work. Cape Verren basin."

Prewitt's face did something complicated. Relief and suspicion fighting for the same real estate.

"That's not restricted," he said slowly.

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

He fetched me the key to the map room himself, which he had never done in twenty years, because Prewitt guarded those keys like a dragon guards gold, and I understood immediately that he wanted me out of his sight and behind a door before anyone else saw me standing at his counter. He was a small man in a large machine, and he had survived this long by knowing exactly how much trouble he could afford to be near.

I took the key. I said thank you. He didn't answer, just went back to his ledger and began writing something with tremendous concentration, and I understood that too: whatever he wrote next would be his alibi.

* * *

The map room was a long stone cellar under the registry, lit by caged bulbs that buzzed like trapped insects. Drawers ran floor to ceiling along both walls, thousands of them, each holding the drowned geography of the coast — soundings, township plats, river surveys, the patient paperwork of a country that had spent two centuries measuring its own edges.

I started where any sensible man starts: with the basin itself. Cape Verren, pre-construction. And there it was, in a surveyor's hand so fine it looked embroidered — the valley.

I want to be honest about what I felt, because what I felt wasn't shock. It was recognition, the dull click of a lock whose key you've been carrying for years without knowing it. The *Corvus* sat in forty fathoms of cold green water off a shelf I'd mapped myself over a dozen dives. And beneath that shelf, drawn in ink faded to the color of weak tea, was a town.

Rivergate.

Streets. A grid of them, neat as stitching. A mill on the river bend. A schoolhouse marked with a tiny square. And a church, set back from the water, with a steeple drawn as a small defiant line — the kind of detail a surveyor adds not because the contract requires it but because he liked the church.

I stood there a long time with my finger on that steeple.

Tomas died diving a bell. That was the story the navy gave us, the story they gave everyone: salvage diver lost during a routine operation, body unrecovered, site sealed for safety. Twenty years I'd assumed the wreck was somewhere out in the deep channel, some freighter or patrol boat, some ordinary iron coffin. I'd dived half the coast looking for him in all the wrong places, the way a man searches a house for glasses sitting on his own head.

But the *Corvus* site — the site they'd thrown me off of with guns and threats and a commander's voice like a hatch closing — sat directly above Rivergate.

Not near. Above.

* * *

Here is what I knew about dams, because a salvage man learns the engineering whether he wants to or not: you don't flood a valley by accident. You buy it. You condemn it. You move the graves — there's always a clause about moving the graves, because even governments flinch at drowning their own dead. There are hearings. Compensation schedules. Relocation orders signed and stamped and filed.

So I went looking for the paperwork.

It took me four hours, and I'll spare you the tedium except to note one thing: the filing was *too good*. Every document I needed was exactly where it should have been, cross-referenced cleanly, which told me someone had curated this collection the way a curator arranges a museum — everything visible, nothing accidental. You don't hide a thing by burying it. You hide it by surrounding it with so much orderly truth that no one thinks to dig.

The dam authorization was dated thirty-one years back. Standard language. Eminent domain, public benefit, reservoir capacity figures in acre-feet. The township of Rivergate, population — and here the clerk's pen had pressed hard enough to emboss the page — *two hundred and six souls*, listed as fully compensated and relocated.

Two hundred and six souls. Eleven of whom, according to a separate casualty abstract filed eleven months later, had died in a structure fire during "resettlement operations."

A fire. In a valley that was about to be flooded anyway. Eleven people dead in a building the water was going to take regardless, and the government had still found it necessary to file a report.

I pulled the report.

Or rather, I pulled what remained of it. Four pages. Three of them almost entirely black — redaction bars laid across the text like railroad ties, so dense that whole paragraphs had become simply absence. What survived the censor's pen read like a message from a drowning man:

*"…responded to the scene at approximately…"*

*"…structure determined to be a total loss…"*

*"…remains recovered at the site numbered…"* — and then a bar, and then — *"…eleven (11) in total, consistent with…"*

And then, on the final page, in the signature block, where a report like this should have carried the name of a county coroner or a fire marshal, a military rank instead:

*Investigating Officer: CDR A. Vane, United States Navy.*

Below it, a second signature. *Concurred.*

No coroner. No fire marshal. No county anything. A navy commander had investigated a civilian fire in a civilian township and his findings had been concurred with and closed and filed, and the file had been stamped — I turned it over, though I already knew what I'd find — *CLOSED. NO FURTHER ACTION.*

I sat down on a step stool among the drawers and read it again. Then a third time. Not because the words changed, but because I kept hoping I'd misread the shape of the thing, the way you reread a doctor's letter hoping the diagnosis will soften on second contact.

It didn't soften. It sharpened.

* * *

"Delacroix."

Prewitt's voice, from the top of the stairs, pitched low. Not calling me up. Warning me down.

I climbed toward him and found him wringing his hands like laundry. Behind him, through the registry's front window, I could see a navy staff car idling at the curb, gray as a shark's belly.

"They called ahead," he whispered. "An hour ago. Asking whether anyone had requested basin materials today." His eyes did the arithmetic for me: *you, Delacroix. They're asking about you.* "I haven't logged you in yet. I can lose the slip. But you need to go out the cellar door and you need to not come back for a while, and I need you to understand that if you do come back, I will deny ever having seen you, and I will mean it."

"Prewitt—"

"Don't." He held up a hand. "Don't thank me, don't ask me why, don't make me complicit past the point where I can sleep. Just — listen to me, because I've worked this office thirty-one years and I know what a redaction bar means." He leaned close, and his breath was coffee and fear. "A bar means somebody above decided the truth was a security risk. One bar, maybe two, that's embarrassment. Routine. But a *page*, Delacroix? Pages? That's not embarrassment. That's a wound they're dressing. And men who dress wounds—" he straightened, finished, "—come back to check the bandages."

He pressed something into my palm as he said it. Folded paper, warm from his pocket.

Then he turned me around and pointed at the cellar door, and I went, because I am many kinds of fool but not the kind who argues with a man saving his neck and mine in the same motion.

* * *

The cellar door let out into the alley behind the fishmongers, all brine and gulls and the honest stink of the working waterfront. I walked three blocks at a pace I hoped read as bored before I allowed myself to stop in the lee of a net shed and open what Prewitt had given me.

It was a carbon copy — a flimsy, we called them, thin as onion skin — of a requisition log. Dated nine days after the fire. Nine days after eleven people burned in a valley scheduled for inundation.

*Requisitioning Office, Naval District 4. Issued to: CDR A. VANE. One (1) lot: structural demolition charges, delayed-fuse type, qty 40. Purpose: PREPARATORY CLEARANCE, RIVERGATE SITE.*

Preparatory clearance. Forty demolition charges, issued to the same officer who'd investigated the fire, nine days after eleven civilians died in it — to clear structures that were, per the dam authorization, going to be underwater within the year anyway.

You don't blow up buildings you're about to drown. Unless what you're destroying matters more than the water does. Unless there's something in those buildings — papers, records, testimony, bodies arranged in ways that contradict reports — that has to *not exist* even at the bottom of a lake. Especially at the bottom of a lake. Water keeps things. Any diver could tell them that. Water is the world's best evidence locker, and every salvage man alive knows how to get a search warrant for it.

They hadn't flooded Rivergate to build a dam.

They'd built a dam to flood Rivergate.

I stood in the net shed's shadow with the flimsy shaking slightly in my hand — from the wind, I told myself, though the wind was coming off the harbor gentle as a lullaby — and I thought about Commander Vane's voice on the deck of that cutter yesterday. *Mr. Delacroix. You'll surface now.* Not angry. Worse than angry. Procedural. A man reading from a script he'd been keeping oiled for twenty years, waiting for someone to trip the wire.

And I thought about Tomas.

Twenty years ago, a navy salvage operation, officially routine, working waters directly above a murdered town. My brother, young and strong and greedy for dive hours because dive hours meant rating points, volunteering for everything. A bell tower on a surveyor's chart, drawn with a surveyor's affection. A body never recovered from a site sealed by the same service that had razed the town beneath it.

I had spent two decades believing my brother's death was an accident that grief had made feel like a conspiracy. It's a comfortable arrangement, that belief. Accidents are terrible but they're *dumb*. They don't mean anything. You can carry one.

This was not carrying weight anymore. This was being carried — dragged, rather, by the collar, backward through time, toward a valley I'd never seen and a fire I'd never heard of and a name I'd only just learned.

*Aldous Vane.*

I said it aloud once, quietly, to taste it. Names have teeth. This one had molars.

* * *

That night I sat in my room above the chandlery with the flimsy and my memory of the chart spread out together, and I did what divers do: I planned the dive. Old habit. You can't look at water without wanting to know what's under it.

Forty fathoms. Cold, dark, silted. A town street grid I could navigate by the survey plat. A church with a steeple — a belfry, probably, if the roof had held, and stone towers hold when timber doesn't. Somewhere in that drowned grid, forty demolition charges had gone off twenty-odd years ago, and whatever they'd been meant to destroy was either dust or — if the charges had failed, if the fuse timing had been wrong, if the water had gotten there first —

Water gets everywhere first. Water is patient. Water wins.

If even one thing survived down there — one strongbox, one file cabinet, one bundle of papers wrapped in oilcloth by someone who saw the fire coming — then the bottom of that reservoir held the whole rotten story, intact, in the dark, waiting for the one profession trained to go get it.

Which raised the obvious question, the one I turned over last, the way you save the bitterest pill: why throw me off the site now? After twenty years of quiet? Why post guards on a wreck that had been left alone since before I grew a beard?

Unless something was changing. Unless the water was about to give up what it kept.

I didn't have the answer yet. But I had a name, a chart, a stolen requisition, and a clerk who'd risked his neck to press paper into my hand — and I had learned long ago on the bottom of cold harbors that when enough small strange things align, they aren't strange anymore. They're a heading.

I blew out the lamp. Outside, the harbor breathed against the pilings, and somewhere beyond the headlands, forty fathoms down, a drowned town waited with its mouth full of truth.

In the morning I'd start asking about Elias Wren. Because a dying prospector who'd spent twenty years running from something, and a commander named Vane who'd spent twenty years guarding something, sounded to me like two ends of the same rope.

And I intended to climb it.
