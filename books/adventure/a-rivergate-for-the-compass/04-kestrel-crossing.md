## 4. Kestrel Crossing

The road to Kestrel Crossing climbs the way grief does — gradually, then all at once, until you look back and can't see where you started.

I'd been on it three days. The lowlands had given up their fog somewhere past the second ridge, and by the time the pines thickened and the air went thin enough to taste of iron, I'd stopped thinking of myself as a man running an errand and started thinking of myself as a courier with a corpse's instructions. Elias Wren had died the morning after he made me promise, quietly, in the cot at the back of the Salt & Anchor, with his hand still curled around empty air as if the map might come back to him if he held on long enough.

It didn't. It came to me instead, folded in oilcloth against my chest, and every mile uphill it seemed to get heavier. Not the paper. The promise.

Kestrel Crossing announced itself before I saw it: a smell of coal smoke and hot iron riding down the wind, and under that the cleaner smell of snowmelt off the high peaks. Then the town itself, strung along a river gorge like laundry on a line — two streets, a church with a leaning steeple, a general store whose porch sagged with men who watched me ride in with the particular attention mountain towns pay strangers this late in the season. The kind of attention that asks *are you trouble* without using words.

"No," I told them, or my face did. They went back to their pipes.

I asked after a farrier at the store. The clerk, a woman with flour to her elbows, jerked her chin eastward. "Wren's forge. Down past the millrace. You'll hear it before you see it."

She was right. I heard it from fifty yards — not hammering, exactly. Hammering has a rhythm to it, a conversation between striker and iron. This was something else. This was iron being argued with, iron being *punished*, each blow landing like a verdict. I came around the millrace and found the forge: a stone building with a shake roof, doors thrown wide despite the cold, and inside, working alone at the anvil, a woman built like the work she did.

Mara Wren was maybe thirty, dark-haired, sweat-darkened shirt rolled to the elbow, forearms that could have been carved from the same stock as her father's weathered hands. She didn't hear me over the hammering. She was shoeing a draft horse that dwarfed her, holding its hoof between her knees like it weighed nothing, and when she finished the last nail and set the foot down, she straightened, pushed a strand of hair off her face with the back of her wrist, and only then registered me standing in her doorway.

Her eyes did the arithmetic strangers' eyes always do. Height, hands, the salt still in my coat from Cape Verren. Diver, they said. Trouble, possibly.

"Farrier work's booked through the week," she said. Her voice was low and even, a voice that had learned not to waste breath.

"I'm not here for shoes."

"Then you're lost."

"Looking for Mara Wren."

Something moved behind her face — quick, guarded, gone. "You found her. State your business or move along. That horse needs feeding and so do I."

"Elias Wren sent me."

I've seen people take blows before. In the water, when things go wrong, you learn what shock looks like — the body going still while everything inside it scrambles. That's what crossed her face. But she covered it fast, and what she covered it with was contempt, which is what most of us cover grief with when we've had years to practice.

"Elias Wren," she repeated, "sent no one anywhere for fifteen years. Elias Wren sends nothing. Not letters. Not money. Not so much as a postcard saying *still alive, don't wait up.* So whoever gave you his name is either lying or—"

"He's dead."

The word landed in the quiet between the forge's heat and the mountain cold pouring through the door. She stood very still. Somewhere behind us the millrace churned on, indifferent.

"How?" she said finally.

"His lungs. Twenty years of hardrock dust and cold camps." I hesitated. "He knew it was coming. He had time to settle things."

"Did he." She picked up the rasp and began cleaning it with short, vicious strokes, though there was nothing on it. "Settle things. That would be a novelty."

"I'll come back when—"

"Don't bother." She set the rasp down and looked at me straight, and her eyes were her father's eyes, which must have been a peculiar cruelty to live with. "Whatever he wanted, whatever he promised you, I'm sure there's money in it somewhere, because that's how he thought of everyone, including me. Take it and go back down the hill."

"There's no money."

"Then what is there?"

"A map."

The word hung there. I hadn't meant to say it yet. I'd meant to do this gently, or as gently as anything gets done with strangers and dead men between them. But she said *what is there* and looked at me with those inherited eyes, and the truth came out ahead of my caution, the way it always does.

She laughed. It wasn't a kind sound. "A map. Of course. A treasure map. Did he tell you about the gold too? He used to tell me about the gold when I was small. The mother lode he'd find someday, the stake that would fix everything, the claim just over the next ridge. I believed him until I was twelve and he walked out with his pack and his promises and never—" She stopped herself. Breathed. "Get out of my forge."

"Mara—"

"*Out.*"

I should have gone. Any sensible man would have gone. But I'd spent twenty years being sensible about Tomas, staying out of sealed wrecks, keeping my questions small enough to drown quietly, and I was tired of it. Tired all the way through.

"It's not a treasure map," I said. "There's no gold on it. There are graves on it."

That stopped her. Not softened her — stopped her, the way ice stops a wheel.

"You're lying to sell it."

"Why would I lie? He's dead. There's nothing left to sell except the truth, and nobody pays for that." I pulled the oilcloth packet from inside my coat. "He made me promise to bring it to you before the passes closed. I didn't want the job. I took it anyway, and I've spent three days climbing toward a woman who I was warned hates him, and now I've met her and I understand why, and I'd still rather be anywhere else. So take it or don't. But don't call it a lie until you've looked at his handwriting."

I held it out. For a long moment she didn't move. The fire ticked in the forge. The horse shifted and blew warm air across both our necks.

Then she crossed the floor, snatched the packet out of my hand like it might bite her, and unfolded it on the anvil, weighting the corners with a horseshoe and a pair of tongs.

I watched her face while she read it. First confusion — the map itself is strange, drawn in Elias's cramped hand, showing a valley and a reservoir where no valley should be, water marked over rooftops. Then recognition, arriving in stages, like light coming up through deep water. Her finger stopped on a notation in the margin. Small letters. *E.W., his hand, no mistake.*

"That's his writing," she said. Barely aloud.

"Yes."

"And this—" Her finger had moved to the center of the map, to a cluster of marks beside a shape that could only be a church. Beside them, scrawled and underscored twice, one word: *Rivergate.* And beside the word, a date.

I saw her go pale. Not the dramatic pallor of storybooks — the real kind, blood leaving the face in a hurry because the body has decided something more important than color is happening.

"That date," she said. "That's the week he left."

"You're sure?"

"I'm sure." Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone walking on river ice, testing each step. "I'm sure because I counted. Fifteen years I counted, off and on, trying to make sense of it. He left the third week of October. My mother got his note the day after my birthday. And this date—" she tapped the map, once, hard, "—is four days before he walked out. Four days. Whatever this is, whatever Rivergate is, he saw it or learned it or *did* it right before he vanished on us, and he never said one word, not one, in fifteen years."

"Do you know what Rivergate is?"

"No." She was already moving, pulling a battered ledger book off a shelf — accounts, I thought, farrier's accounts — and flipping through it with shaking hands until she found a page near the back where she'd kept clippings, or tried to. "Nobody knows. Nobody talks about it. When I was a girl there was a man in town, old Sorel, who came from down-valley, and once, drunk, he said the name and another man broke a chair over his head. Over a *word*. I asked my mother and she said some places die and you let them." She looked up at me. "What did my father drag you into?"

"I was hoping you could tell me."

We stood there in the ringing heat of the forge, two strangers joined by a dead man's handwriting, and outside the light was starting to fail against the peaks, and somewhere above the tree line the first thin rags of cloud were assembling themselves into winter.

Mara looked down at the map again. At the graves. At the date. At the word *Rivergate*, underscored twice, the ink pressed so hard it had nearly torn the paper — the mark of a man writing something he needed to survive being erased.

"Fifteen years," she said again, but differently now. Quieter. The bitterness was still in it, but underneath the bitterness something else was setting, the way iron sets — fast, and permanent, and shaped by the blow that made it. "He let me hate him. All this time, he let me hate him, because hating him was safer than whatever this is."

She folded the map along its old creases, precisely, and put her hand flat on top of it.

"He's dead," she said. "So he doesn't get to explain. Which means I'm going to find out anyway. Whatever it costs." She looked up, and her father's eyes had gone hard as horseshoe iron. "You said the passes close soon. How soon?"

"Weeks. Maybe less. Storms are early this year."

"Then you're not leaving tonight." She was already reaching for the lamp, turning up the wick, throwing the forge into warmer light. "Sit down. Tell me everything he said to you, every word, and don't leave anything out because you think it's kinder. And then tomorrow we're going to talk about what a man would have to be, to carry a map full of graves around for fifteen years and never once send it home."

I sat down on her bench, in the smell of coal and hot metal, and began to talk.

And that was when, through the window over the anvil, I saw the two riders come down the far street — moving slow, checking doorways, asking at the store. Strangers. Wrong bearing. One of them wore his coat the way soldiers wear coats, and the other turned his head as they passed the forge, and I caught the gleam of an officer's insignia pin catching the lamplight before the angle cut it off.

Mara saw me watching. Saw my face change.

"What?" she said.

"Maybe," I said, "we talk faster than we planned."
