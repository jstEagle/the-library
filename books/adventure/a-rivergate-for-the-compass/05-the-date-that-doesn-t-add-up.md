## 5. The Date That Doesn't Add Up

I didn't sleep. I want that on the record, because Ansel Delacroix slept like a man who had made his peace with hard ground, and I lay awake in my own loft above the forge doing arithmetic with a dead man's handwriting.

The map was spread on my workbench where I'd left it, pinned at the corners with horseshoe nails because I didn't own anything as delicate as a paperweight. My father's hand crawled across it in that slanted, hurried script I'd spent twenty years trying to forget and forty minutes failing to. *Elias Wren* had written me exactly four letters in my life — two of them money orders with no note, one of them an apology so vague it could have been addressed to anyone, and one of them, when I was nine, explaining that horses were honest in a way men weren't, which I had taken at the time to be wisdom and now understood to be a confession.

And here he was again, writing to me from beyond whatever line he'd finally crossed, except this time he wasn't writing to me at all. He was writing to himself. Reminders. Marginalia. The private shorthand of a man keeping track of something he couldn't afford to forget.

I'd read the notes a dozen times by lamplight. *Below the third bend, past the drowned fence line.* *The bell still hangs.* And then, in smaller letters, pressed so hard the nib had nearly torn the paper: *the eleven. Don't lose count of the eleven.*

Eleven what? Eleven claims? Eleven ounces? Prospectors counted everything — paces, pans, days between meals. But you don't press your pen through paper for gold.

I came down before dawn and built the forge fire too hot, out of habit and spite, and when Ansel appeared in the doorway smelling like my cheap coffee and looking like a man who'd been awake almost as long as I had, I was already talking.

"He left in October," I said. "Nineteen years ago. The second week."

Ansel leaned against the doorframe. He had that quality some quiet men have, of making silence into a question.

"I know because it was the week of the Founders' Day dance. He promised to take me. I was twelve. He said he'd be back before the first snow." I turned the map toward him without touching it, the way you'd turn a knife. "So tell me about Rivergate."

Something moved behind his eyes — not surprise exactly. Confirmation. Like I'd handed him a piece he'd been carrying loose in his pocket.

"Rivergate's gone," he said. "Drowned. There's a reservoir up there now, government water, navy seals on the old roads. Nobody goes in."

"When did it drown?"

He was quiet long enough that I knew the answer mattered. "Twenty years back. Give or take. There was an incident first — nobody says what kind. Then the dam, then the water."

"The second week of October," I said, "nineteen years ago, my father walked out of this house with his prospecting gear and never came back. Not before the first snow. Not ever. He sent money sometimes. He wrote like a man dictating from a moving train." I put my finger on the date scrawled beside the word *Rivergate* — Elias dated everything, even his lies, a bookkeeper's tic. "That's the same week, Ansel. Same month, same year, within days. He didn't leave home because of gold. He left home because of *that*."

Ansel crossed the room and looked down at the map, and I watched him read it the way I'd watched him do everything — carefully, like the world might change if he blinked. His finger stopped on the marginalia.

"*The eleven,*" he read aloud. "*Don't lose count.*"

"Graves," I said. It came out of me flat and certain, a thing I hadn't known until I said it. "Those are graves. Or names. You don't count gold like that. You count the dead like that."

* * *

We spent the morning at my kitchen table, which is where all the worst discoveries of my life have happened, and by noon we had taken my father apart like a clock we suspected of lying.

Here is what I knew about Elias Wren, or thought I knew: he was a prospector, a fair-to-middling one, the kind who found enough to keep prospecting and never enough to stop. He was gentle with animals and hopeless with people. He taught me to shoe a horse before he taught me to read cursive, and when he did teach me to read cursive, it was mostly so I could help him copy out his claim notes in duplicate. *Always in duplicate, Mara-girl. Paper burns, but two papers burn twice as slow.*

I used to think that was just him being him — careful, doubled-up, a man who trusted nothing once. Now I sat across from a stranger with salt-scoured hands and understood that my father had lived his whole life like a man carrying something breakable through a crowd.

Because look at the map. Look at it properly. A prospector's map shows veins, washes, tailings, water. This one showed a town. Street grids. A church drawn in careful little strokes. A schoolhouse. A mill. He'd mapped Rivergate the way a surveyor maps it, or the way a man maps a place he can't stop seeing when he closes his eyes.

And the notes — I read them aloud, because saying them made them worse and I wanted worse, I wanted to be angry at something specific:

"*Past the drowned fence line, south side, the low ground. Counted twice, comes to eleven.*"

"*The bell still hangs. Left what matters where the sound carries.*"

"*If I go under, find Mara. She counts true.*"

That last one undid me a little, I'll admit it. *She counts true.* I was twelve years old and he trusted me with numbers and nothing else, and then he left anyway, and I spent nineteen years deciding that meant I wasn't worth staying for.

"He witnessed something," Ansel said. He said it simply, the way he said most things, no decoration. "A massacre, maybe. Eleven dead. He ran, and he kept the proof, and he kept running for twenty years."

"Prospectors don't witness massacres."

"Prospectors are everywhere nobody else wants to be. If soldiers cleared a valley, a prospector in the high country would see it." He turned his cup in his hands. "There's a wreck off Cape Verren the navy won't let divers near. I worked it for years. They threw me off last month like I was carrying plague. I've been wondering why a sunken freighter needs that kind of guarding." He looked up. "Your father's map might answer that too. Rivergate flooded. Things sink. Things get moved underwater where nobody looks."

The thought landed in my stomach like a dropped hammer. My father hadn't just run. He'd run *toward* the thing everyone else fled, circling it, watching it, for twenty years. Duplicating his notes. Keeping count.

"Why not go to the law?" I asked. "Why not newspapers, magistrates, anything?"

"You met him," Ansel said gently. "What do you think?"

I had met him. Three weeks ago, in a dockside tavern that smelled of tar and bad decisions, a dying man had gripped my wrist with a strength that embarrassed his body and made me promise things. I'd thought he was delirious. I'd thought the map was a madman's treasure and the promise was a dying man's vanity.

Now I remembered how he'd watched the door. How he'd chosen the corner seat with his back to the wall. How he'd flinched — actually flinched — when two men in good boots walked in wearing coats a size too military for their manners.

"He was afraid," I said. "Not of dying. Of being found."

"And he gave the map to you instead of burning it," Ansel said. "Which means near the end, he decided it mattered more than his fear. Or more than—" He stopped.

"More than me," I finished. "You can say it. He had twenty years to bring this to me and he waited until he was dying and there was a diver standing next to him to do the carrying." I stood up, because sitting felt like agreeing. "Do you know what that's like? To find out your whole childhood grievance was real? I hated him for leaving. I built my whole self on hating him for leaving, and it turns out he left because he saw eleven people murdered and spent two decades too scared to be the man who knew it — and somehow that's *worse*, Ansel. Somehow that's so much worse."

"It's not worse," he said. "It's just heavier."

"Well, I'm strong," I snapped, and immediately heard my mother in it, and hated that too.

* * *

But anger doesn't hold its shape. By afternoon it had cooled into something more useful and more terrible: urgency.

Because here's the arithmetic I'd been doing all night, and it finally added up wrong in a way I couldn't ignore. My father died three weeks ago with a map and a secret and a lifetime of running behind him. Men in military bearing had come to Kestrel Crossing asking after Elias Wren within days of my taking possession of that map. Ansel told me about the aide he'd recognized, the questions asked wrong, the way the innkeeper's boy had been pumped for gossip. Whatever my father had carried, someone wanted it badly enough to send riders into the mountains after a dead man's debts.

"They're not chasing gold," I said. We were in the forge by then, because I can't think in a parlor. I pumped the bellows while I talked, letting the work hold me together. "They're chasing the ledger. The proof. My father kept duplicates — always duplicates — which means somewhere there's a second set of notes, maybe the original, maybe the thing itself. And they think we have it, or they think we know where it is."

"Do we?"

"I don't know!" The bellows roared; I made myself ease off. "That's the point, Ansel. I don't know what my father knew. I know him only as the man who left, and now I'm finding out he was also the man who stayed — stayed near it, watched it, counted it — and I will never ask him why, because he's dead, and the only way left to ask him is to follow this map to the bottom of it."

Ansel was quiet. Outside, the light was going the color of old tin, and the peaks above Kestrel Crossing had that white-rimmed look they get when the snow is deciding. I'd lived under those peaks my whole life. I knew their moods better than my father's face.

"The passes close soon," he said.

"I know when the passes close. I shoe the horses that fail to beat them."

"Then we should talk about whether you're coming."

I set down the tongs. I looked at him — this lean, careful stranger with his dead brother and his sealed wrecks and his habit of saying hard things kindly — and I realized I'd already decided, probably the moment I saw *find Mara, she counts true* in my father's cramped hand. Some promises you inherit. This one had my name written on it in advance, by a man who'd known me better at twelve than I'd let him know me since.

"I'm coming," I said. "Not for you. For the eleven. Somebody counted them for twenty years and got killed for it, and I'm the one who counts true."

Ansel nodded slowly, and there was something in his face that wasn't quite a smile but lived in the same country.

Then he glanced past me, through the forge's open doors, toward the road.

"Mara," he said quietly. "How many strangers does your town usually hold this time of year?"

I followed his eyes. Two men stood at the far end of the street, talking to the innkeeper's boy, and even at this distance I could see the boots — good boots, wrong boots, the kind my father had flinched at.

"More than usual," I said, and reached for the lamp to kill the light.
