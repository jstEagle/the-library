## 6. Riders on the Ridge

Kestrel Crossing smelled of coal smoke and horse sweat and, by evening, of woodsmoke curling from chimneys whose owners had no idea their town was about to become interesting to the wrong people. I'd been in it three days. Three days was long enough to learn that Mara Wren could shoe a horse faster than any man in the county and shorter than any man would admit; three days was also, as it turned out, two days too many.

I should have known something was wrong when the questions started coming polite.

The West has a particular grammar for trouble. When a stranger wants your horse, he asks rude and you know where you stand. When a stranger wants information, he buys you a drink first. But when a stranger wants to know whether you've seen a dying prospector pass through — well. That's when he's wearing a uniform under his coat, even if the coat is wool instead of navy blue.

I heard about them before I saw them, which is the only way to hear about men like that and keep your teeth. The farrier's trade brings every mouth in town past the forge eventually, and Mara's forge sat on the main street like a stove everyone gathered around whether they needed iron or not. So it was old Petey Hallow, who delivered ice and gossip in roughly equal measure, who leaned over his mare's flank and said, low, "Three fellows come in on the morning coach. Asking after Elias Wren."

"Prospector," I said. "Half the county owes him money."

"That's not how they asked." Petey ran a hand along the mare's neck, buying time or courage. "They asked had he *transited* through. Transited. Like he was freight." He glanced up at me. "And the one doing the talking, he didn't look at my face while he asked. He looked at my hands."

"What's wrong with your hands?"

"Nothing. That's what worried me."

* * *

I went to see for myself, because curiosity is the flaw that will someday kill me, and because I wanted to be wrong.

They were at the hotel porch, which in Kestrel Crossing is four chairs, a rail, and pretensions. Three men. Two of them were exactly what they were dressed to seem: trail-worn prospectors' agents, maybe, or land men, boots dusty, hats creased with the studied carelessness of men who'd been told to look civilian. The third stood apart from the other two the way an officer stands apart from his men — not by distance but by the fact that nobody spoke unless he'd finished speaking.

He was younger than the other two, clean-shaven, with the kind of neatness that doesn't survive actual wilderness. His coat was good wool, his boots were city boots, and his hands were folded behind his back in a parade rest so ingrained it looked like a skeletal condition.

I knew him.

Not by name. By silhouette. Twenty years of diving wrecks teaches you to memorize shapes — the shape of a hull against dark water, the shape of a thing before you can name it. I'd seen this man's shape once before, on the deck of a naval tender off Cape Verren, standing just behind the shoulder of the officer who ordered me off-site with a politeness that had knives in it. Aides don't get remembered, generally. They get remembered when they're attached to something you can't stop thinking about.

Commander Aldous Vane's aide. Standing on a hotel porch in a mountain town four hundred miles from salt water, asking about a dead-or-dying prospector.

I did what any sensible man does. I crossed the street, bought a paper I couldn't read in the failing light, and listened.

"—last seen at Verren, we understand," the aide was saying. His voice was mild. Mild voices are the worst kind. "An old man, ill. He'd have been looking for transport into the mountains. Someone may have carried his effects."

"Effects," repeated the second man, tasting the word.

"Papers. Maps. Nothing of value." A pause you could have parked a wagon in. "We're simply trying to return them to his family."

That was the sentence that told me everything, because it was wrong in the way military lies are always wrong: too complete. Civilians lie in fragments. Institutions lie in whole sentences with all the parts accounted for. Nobody hunting a lost father's papers says *nothing of value* unless they've already decided what value means and intend to collect it personally.

I folded my unread paper and walked back toward the forge, and my heart was going like a dive pump, steady and too fast, and underneath the fear there was something else, something with more teeth in it. Because if Vane's aide was here, then Vane knew about Elias Wren. Knew about the map. Which meant somewhere between a dockside tavern and a mountain pass, a dying man's last request had become a matter of official interest, and matters of official interest, in my experience, ended with men in the water.

* * *

Mara took it better than I did, if you define *better* as *immediately practical and slightly terrifying.*

"Vane," she said. She'd been heating a shoe and didn't put the tongs down. The forge light made her face half bronze, half shadow. "You're certain it's his man."

"Certain enough. I saw him on the tender. He stood where aides stand."

"And you think this Vane sent him for Father's map." She set the shoe in the slack tub and the hiss filled the pause she left. "For a map to a valley that's been under a lake for twenty years."

"When a powerful man sends soldiers after a piece of paper, Miss Wren, the paper is worth more than the soldiers."

She looked at me then, really looked, the way she looked at a hoof before deciding where to strike. "Say the rest of it."

"The rest of it is that your father spent twenty years running from whatever's drawn on that map, and the man who scared him into running is still spending money to keep it buried. And now the reservoir—" I stopped. I hadn't meant to say that part yet. I'd been turning it over since the harbor records, the notice tucked in the shipping pages like a confession hidden in a hymnal: *emergency drawdown scheduled, Rivergate Reservoir, pending inspection.* Nine weeks, maybe less. The valley floor coming up into daylight for the first time in twenty years, silt and foundations and whatever else Elias Wren had seen that made him run until his heart gave out in a tavern full of strangers.

"Drawdown," Mara said quietly. She'd read the same notice; I watched her arrive at it again in real time. "They're draining it."

"They're draining it. And Vane's aide arrives the same week. That's not coincidence, that's scheduling."

She pulled the shoe from the water, held it dripping, and I watched her make the decision the way I'd once watched her father make his — jaw setting first, eyes going somewhere private and coming back changed. "How fast can you ride?"

"Fast enough to regret it tomorrow."

"Good. We leave tonight."

* * *

Here is what I learned about leaving a town properly, which is to say I learned it from Mara because I'd have done it badly: you don't slip out the back like a thief, because thieves get chased. You leave like people with ordinary business. She sold nothing, packed nothing visible, told her apprentice — a gap-toothed boy named Corwin who worshipped her with the pure devotion available only to fourteen-year-olds — that she was riding out to the Hendricks place to shoe stock and would be back inside the week. Then she walked me past the hotel porch at dusk, arm through mine like a courting couple, close enough to the three strangers that I could smell the aide's bay rum over the coal smoke.

He looked at us. I felt the look go across my face like a lantern beam, and I gave him what every diver learns to give the surface: nothing. A man thinking about supper. A man whose business was a woman and a warm bed and a slow morning.

"Mister," Mara said pleasantly, tipping her chin at the aide as we passed, because she was Mara and subtlety was a tool she used when it suited her, "you'll want heavy coats if you're heading up-valley. Snow comes early this year."

"We're not heading anywhere," the aide said, smiling.

"No," she agreed. "You don't look like men who go places. You look like men things happen to."

I steered her onward before the conversation could ripen into a corpse.

* * *

The horses were hers — a rangy dun gelding called Biscuit who had opinions, and a black mare named Sorrel who had none, which made her the better animal by Mara's arithmetic. We saddled in the livery's back stalls by a single lamp turned low, and I'll confess my hands weren't steady. Not from cold. From the specific cold that comes when you understand the shape of the thing hunting you and it understands yours.

Elias's map rode against my chest, oilcloth-wrapped, in the pocket where a man keeps the thing he'd grab if the house burned. It had been there since the tavern. It felt heavier every day, the way lead feels heavier the deeper you swim.

"You could still give it to them," Mara said, cinching the girth. Her voice was flat, testing. "Hand it over, tell them where I live, go back to your wrecks. They might even let you."

"They might."

"But."

"But I promised a dying man." I swung up onto Biscuit, who expressed an opinion about my weight and settled. "And I've been ordered around by that man's uniform before. Off a wreck I had every right to dive. There's a number of ways to spend a life, Miss Wren, and I find I've got less patience for being told where not to look."

She mounted Sorrel in one motion, no wasted movement, a woman built by work. "Then we ride north through the mill yard and take the packer's trail. It adds six miles but nobody watches it, because nobody's stupid enough to ride it in November."

"And we are?"

"We're motivated." She turned the mare toward the door. "There's a difference."

* * *

We went out through the mill yard as the last light died, hooves muffled in sawdust leavings, past the sleeping stacks and the black shoulder of the kiln. The packer's trail climbed immediately, switchbacking through pine so thick the stars came to us in pieces, and for an hour the only sounds were leather and breath and the small honest noises of animals doing their work.

At the first ridge opening we stopped, because Mara stopped, and I'd already learned that when she stopped there was a reason.

Below us, Kestrel Crossing lay scattered with lamplight, small and warm and finished with us. And along the north road — the road we hadn't taken, the road out of town toward the low passes — moved three riders in single file, unhurried, spaced like men who'd done patrol work. Even at that distance I knew the spacing. You don't learn to ride like that driving cattle.

"They're watching the roads," I said.

"They're watching the easy roads." Mara's breath hung white. "Which means they think we're still in town, and they think we're stupid. Both those advantages expire by morning."

Above us, higher on the ridge we'd have to climb at dawn, something else: a pinpoint of light, there and gone. A lantern shuttered. Or a signal answered.

"They're on the ridge too," I said.

"Yes." She said it without drama, the way she'd report a cracked hoof. "So we don't camp. We ride the night line along the divide, cold, and we're through Gray Pass before they've finished their breakfast and their theories." She turned Sorrel uphill, into the dark, and looked back at me once. "My father crossed these mountains alone and sick, carrying whatever he carried, for twenty years. The least we can do is carry it one night without complaining."

I nudged Biscuit after her, and the map lay against my chest like a second heartbeat, and behind us the town lights shrank to nothing, and above us the ridge riders' lantern blinked its patient, military blink — but ahead, ahead there was only the trail climbing into the black, and snow-smell on the wind sharp as a whetted blade, and the narrowing window of the season closing around us like water over a diver's head.

We had perhaps three days before the passes shut. I counted them the way I counted fathoms: downward, and fast, and praying the air held.

Behind us, faint on the wind, a whistle sounded twice from the ridge — and was answered, much closer than I liked, from below.
