## 7. The High Trail

The trail out of Kestrel Crossing climbed the way a debt collector climbs stairs — patiently, without mercy, and with every intention of taking everything you had by the top.

By midmorning I'd stopped counting switchbacks. By noon I'd stopped counting anything except my own breath, which came in short, expensive installments. The air up here was thin enough that you could feel it rationing itself to you, a miserly landlord of oxygen, and my lungs were paying rent on both lungs' behalf while my legs filed complaints.

Mara Wren did not appear to be suffering. She rode ahead of me on her bay mare — a horse named Bracken, who had opinions about me and expressed them mostly through his tail — and she sat in the saddle the way other people sit in their own kitchens. Loose. Certain. Like the mountain was furniture she'd grown up bumping into.

"You're breathing wrong," she said, without turning around.

"I'm breathing at all," I said. "Under the circumstances, I consider that ambitious."

"You're gulping. Divers do that." She glanced back, and there was something almost like curiosity in it. "You take shallow sips and hold them. Up here you want long drinks. Slow. From the belly."

"Is that so."

"It is." She faced forward again. "My father taught me. He said a man who can't breathe slow will panic fast, and a man who panics fast dies slow."

I tried it. She was right, which was annoying, and she was her father's daughter, which was more annoying still, given how she felt about that particular fact. But I took the long drinks, from the belly, and the trail got fractionally less hostile, and we climbed.

* * *

We'd left Kestrel Crossing two hours before dawn, out the back of the forge with the lamps cold and the dogs next door mercifully asleep. Mara had packed like a woman who'd done this before: hardtack and dried apples, a coil of rope, a canvas shelter-half, a small iron pot, and a whetstone she used on a skinning knife with the absent tenderness of a woman stroking a cat. She'd banked her forge fire rather than douse it, so the town would think she was merely closed for the day. Clever. Sad, too, in a way I didn't examine too closely — a whole life arranged to look temporarily interrupted when it might never be resumed.

The map rode against my chest, wrapped in oilcloth inside my coat, over the shirt, under nothing. Elias Wren's careful lines. Rivergate. And that date beside it that had made Mara's face go still as pond ice.

The riders had been another reason for the early start. Three men had come asking after Elias Wren the evening before, wearing town clothes cut too neat and boots worn in a pattern no farmer ever wears them. One of them had Vane's aide's jaw — I'd seen that jaw across a wardroom table once, attached to a man who smiled the way a hatch cover closes. They'd asked their questions politely. That was the tell. Honest questions come rough; polite ones come pre-loaded.

So: two horses, one map, one promise to a dead man, and a window of good weather that the old-timers in Kestrel Crossing had estimated at somewhere between five days and a lie. We were going over Gray Pass ahead of the snows, or we weren't going anywhere at all except into the kind of story that ends with search parties and a newspaper item.

* * *

The trail topped out onto a shoulder of granite in the early afternoon, and for the first time since Kestrel Crossing I stopped moving and just looked.

Below us the foothills unrolled in brown and gold, stitched with creeks that caught the light like dropped wire. Behind us, already small, the town sat in its valley like a coin at the bottom of a pocket. Ahead, the range went up and up — gray walls, white shoulders, and between them the notch of Gray Pass, still a day and a half distant if the weather held its tongue.

"Beautiful," Mara said, "if you like looking down."

"I dive for a living," I said. "Looking down is the family business."

"Down into water, though. Water holds you." She swung down and walked Bracken forward, picking along the ledge where the footing was scree and argument. "This just drops you."

There was truth in that, and I felt it in my palms, which had gone damp on the reins. Twenty years underwater teaches you a certain faith — that the medium will carry you, that buoyancy is a kind of mercy. A cliff extends no such courtesy. It simply declines to catch you.

We led the horses through the bad stretch. Mara went first, testing stones with her heel before committing weight, and twice she stopped me with a raised hand — not a dramatic gesture, just a flat palm, a stop-sign made of fingers — and waited until some private calculation finished behind her eyes before waving me on. The second time, thirty seconds after we'd passed a shelf of rock, the shelf let go and went bounding down into the void with a sound like a cabinet full of dishes falling forever.

I looked at her.

She shrugged. "Rock does that when the freeze-thaw's been working it. You listen with your feet."

"Your feet listen."

"All of mine do. Yours should try it instead of clomping."

I clomped slightly less after that.

* * *

We made camp at dusk in a hollow below the ridgeline, where a stand of wind-bent pines broke the worst of the air. Mara built the fire small — "a candle, not a bonfire" — and set the iron pot on it with snow and a handful of dried apples and a strip of salt pork, producing something that was not food exactly but performed the duties of food under duress.

I took first watch because she'd done the navigating all day and because I wanted to be alone with the dark for a while, which is a thing I say to myself as though it were a comfort rather than a habit.

That was when I saw them.

Riders. Or rather — not riders. Movement. Along the ridgeline opposite, maybe a mile off and four hundred feet up, where the last light still touched the snowfields, something crossed a gap in the rocks. Then stopped. Then crossed again, lower, following our line of travel the way a shadow follows a man walking past a row of streetlamps.

Three shapes. Maybe four. Too far to count gear or faces. Close enough to count intentions.

I didn't move. I watched until the light failed completely and the shapes dissolved into the general dark, and then I sat very still and listened with my feet, and my feet reported nothing but cold.

When I crawled back to the fire, Mara was awake, sitting up with her blanket around her shoulders like a shawl, watching me read my own face.

"Well?" she said.

"You saw them too."

"Since midafternoon." She fed a stick to the flames. "They don't travel like drovers or prospectors. Prospectors argue about the route. These ride like they're being paid by the mile."

"They're being paid by something."

"Mm." She looked into the fire. "Vane."

It wasn't a question anymore. Somewhere between the forge and the pass, it had stopped being a question for both of us.

"We could lose them in the night," I said. "Cut east, drop into the timber, double back—"

"And be on their ground, in the dark, in terrain they've probably studied, with two horses and no room to maneuver." She shook her head. "No. We keep the trail, we keep the pace, and we get over the pass before they close the gap. High country favors whoever's ahead. You know that better than anyone — you work salvage. First diver to the wreck owns what's in it."

I stared at her. "Where did you learn that?"

"My father talked in his sleep." Something flickered across her face — the grief that wasn't anger yet, the anger that wasn't grief yet, whatever lived in the space between those two. "He talked a great deal in his sleep. It was the only time he talked at all, near the end of the letters."

* * *

She asked the question the second morning, on the long traverse above the tree line, where the world was reduced to rock, sky, and the sound of wind deciding what it thought of us.

We'd stopped to breathe — genuinely stopped, both of us bent over our knees like a pair of penitents — and when I straightened up she was looking at me with that directness she carried like a tool on her belt.

"What are you running from?"

"Same thing you are," I said. "Men with neat boots."

"No." She remounted, unhurried. "That's what we're running *toward away from*, if you follow me. It's not what's driving you. You didn't take my father's map for the money — you refused the money, he told me that much even in his letter, the stubborn fool. You took it because somebody asked you to carry something, and you're a man who carries things." She nudged Bracken forward. "So. What is it?"

I rode a while without answering. The trail traversed a face of red stone, and below us a turquoise lake sat in a cirque like a coin someone had pressed into the mountain's palm, and I looked at that water and felt the old pull, the one that had run my whole adult life.

"A brother," I said finally.

Mara said nothing. She'd learned, in two days on the trail with me, that silence was a lever she could lean on, and she leaned.

"Tomas. Younger than me by three years. Better swimmer, worse judge of weather, which tells you most of what you need to know about him." I kept my eyes on the trail. "He drowned twenty years ago. Off Cape Verren, working a wreck the navy had sealed. Except sealed isn't the word — sealed implies a lid. This was more like a fence around a piece of ocean, with gunboats at the corners, and a story about unexploded ordnance that nobody with a brain believed, because the ordnance, whatever it was, had been sitting there quiet for years and only became dangerous right around the time people started asking what the navy wanted with an empty stretch of seabed."

"And Tomas went in anyway."

"Tomas went in anyway." My voice came out flatter than I intended, which is how I knew I was close to the edge of something. "He was always doing that. Doors marked keep out were, to Tomas, doors marked interesting. They pulled him out two days later. Official cause: entanglement. Unofficially—" I shrugged. "Unofficially there's no unofficial version. There's just the official version and the hole where the truth should be, and I've spent twenty years diving around the edges of that hole."

"The same waters."

"The same waters. Every season. Working wrecks, running salvage, telling myself I was earning a living when what I was actually doing was circling. Like a shark that's forgotten what it's hungry for." I laughed, and it came out wrong, and I let it die. "The navy ordered me off-site last month. Men with neat boots, Mara. Same boots, different town. And then your father put a map in my hands with the word Rivergate on it, and I'm not a man who believes in coincidence, because I've seen what the bottom of the sea does to things people drop, and coincidences don't sink. They float. Somebody throws them."

Ahead of us the trail bent north, toward the pass, and Mara rode in silence for a hundred yards. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — quieter, stripped of its edge.

"He wrote me a letter. Last winter. First one in six years." Her knuckles were white on the reins, though her voice stayed level. "It said he'd found what he was looking for, and that he was sorry, and that I'd understand soon. No signature flourish, no apology for the birthdays. Just that. I threw it in the stove." She paused. "Then I took it back out. Half of it burned."

"The half with the words, or the half without?"

She almost smiled. "The half without. The words survived." She looked back at me, and for a moment the wind dropped and I saw her plainly — not the farrier, not the bitter daughter, just a woman hauling a dead man's contradictions up a mountain. "Your brother circled the truth for twenty years. Mine ran from it. Between the two of them they managed to hand it to us."

"That's either fate or poor planning."

"In my experience," Mara said, "they're the same thing viewed from different altitudes."

* * *

We crossed the high shoulder that afternoon, and the pass opened ahead of us like a door left ajar — a gray notch between two white shoulders, with the far country visible beyond it, faint and blue, the valleys descending toward the reservoir country, toward Rivergate, toward whatever had been waiting at the bottom of the water for twenty years.

And above us, patient as arithmetic, the riders kept their distance.

They'd gained during the day. Not much — a few hundred yards, taken in increments, the way you'd gain on a horse you didn't want to spook. They held the ridgeline parallel to ours, black specks against the snowfields, stopping when we stopped, moving when we moved. Once, when the light hit right, I counted four. One of them raised something to his face — glasses, or a spyglass, or just a hand shading his eyes — and I raised mine back, a friendly wave across the beautiful desolation, and got no answer, which was itself an answer.

"They'll push tonight," Mara said. We stood by the horses, looking up at the specks. "They'll want to be ahead of us at the pass. Cut us off in the narrows where there's one trail and no options."

"Then we don't sleep."

"We sleep in turns, and we leave at three." She was already unsaddling, her hands moving through the work with that economy I'd come to trust faster than I'd trusted anything in years. "There's a route my father marked on the map — a miner's track, west of the main trail. Steeper. Ugly. But it comes down on the far side of the pass without touching the narrows at all."

"You've read the map."

"I've had it memorized since I was nine. He drew these mountains on everything — grocery lists, letters, the margins of my schoolbooks. I used to think it was sentiment." She looked up at the pass, and her jaw set in a way I was beginning to recognize as the Wren family inheritance. "It was rehearsal."

We ate the hardtack. We drank the snowmelt. We took our watches in turns, and in the hour before three, with the stars hard and close overhead and the fire long dead, I stood looking up at the ridgeline one last time.

The riders were gone from it.

For a long moment that felt like mercy. Then I understood it for what it was — they hadn't turned back, they'd moved down, into the dark, onto the trail ahead of us — and I woke Mara with a hand on her shoulder and told her, and she listened the way she listened to rock, with her whole body, and then she began saddling the horses in the dark without a single wasted motion.

"Miner's track," she said. "West side. We climb ugly."

Above us, the pass waited, empty-looking and full.
