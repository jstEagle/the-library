## 8. Cold Camp

The storm came down on us like a debt collector — no warning, no negotiation, just the whole sky leaning on the pass until the trees bent sideways and the world went white. One moment we were picking our way along the shoulder of Gray Pass with the horses complaining about the grade, and the next I couldn't see Ansel ten feet ahead of me. I could hear him, though. He had a way of swearing that was almost musical, like a man who'd practiced.

"Left!" he bellowed into the wind. "There's a wall — rock overhang — follow my voice!"

I followed his voice. It seemed like as good a thing to follow as any, and better than most. The horses fought us the last hundred yards, ears flat, and then the wind dropped away all at once, the way it does when the mountain decides it's done playing with you, and we were standing under a shelf of granite with a floor of dry pine duff and a view of nothing but boiling white.

"Well," I said. "Cozy."

Ansel looked at me. Snow had packed itself into every crease of his face, and his beard — the scruffy, undecided thing he called a beard — had icicles in it. "You have a strange relationship with the word cozy."

"I grew up in a forge, Delacroix. This has a roof. By my standards it's a palace."

* * *

We got the horses settled in the lee of the entrance, rubbed them down with handfuls of duff, and gave them what grain we could spare, which wasn't much. Then we built a fire small enough to hide and big enough to matter — a fire like a secret whispered rather than shouted — and I dug through the saddlebags while Ansel melted snow in the tin pot.

That was when I found the notebook.

I'd seen it before, of course. Elias's field book, oilcloth-wrapped, riding in the same bag as the map. But I'd been treating it the way you treat a stranger's luggage — aware of it, not touching it. Now, with the storm sealing us in and nothing to do but wait out the weather or go mad waiting, I unwrapped it.

The cover was soft with handling. Twenty years of handling. My father's hands had worn this book the way a river wears a stone, and I felt something twist in my chest that I refused to name.

"What is it?" Ansel asked.

"His notes." I turned pages. Figures, mostly — ore weights, assay values, distances between claims. The handwriting of a man who'd learned arithmetic in a hurry and never went back to fix it. "Nothing but numbers. He was a prospector. This is prospector stuff."

Except it wasn't. Not all of it.

Because there, in the margins, squeezed into the white space around the ore figures, were other notes. Tiny ones. Written in a hand that got smaller and more cramped as the pages went on, as if the man writing them was afraid the words would take up too much room in the world.

I read one. Then another. Then I stopped reading silently, because some things need air.

"'Counted again,'" I read aloud. "'Eleven. Always eleven. The ledger does not lie even when men do.'"

Ansel looked up from the pot. "Read that again."

"'The ledger does not lie even when men do.'" I frowned. "Ledger of what? He never kept a ledger. The man kept money in a boot and called it a system."

"Keep reading."

So I did. I sat with my back against the granite and my boots nearly in the fire and I read my father's marginalia out loud to a stranger in a snowbound cave, which was not how I'd imagined spending any evening of my life, and certainly not this one.

"'They ask about the fire. I tell them what they want. The fire is the least of it.'" Page turn. "'Saw the bell come down. Thought that would be the end of it. It was not the end of it.'" Page turn. "'Eleven names. I carry them so no one else has to. It is heavier than gold and worth less at the assay office and I cannot put it down.'"

The cave was very quiet. The fire ticked. Somewhere above us the wind screamed over the pass, but in under the granite shelf it was just breath and flame and the sound of my own voice getting smaller.

"Ansel," I said. "What is he talking about?"

Ansel had gone still in the way he did sometimes — a stillness that wasn't calm but compression, like a spring being wound. "Read me the one about the ledger."

I hunted back through the pages. "'The ledger is safe where I put it. Safe is not the same as gone. Nothing in that valley is gone. It is only waiting for the water to give it back.'"

"Where did he put it?"

"He doesn't say." I flipped forward, backward. "He never says. It's all — sideways. Look at this one. 'T. D. went down for it. Pray the water is kinder than the men.'" I looked up. "T. D. Who's T. D.?"

And Ansel Delacroix, who had spent six days giving me half-answers wrapped in deflections and tied with a bow of silence, closed his eyes and said, "Tomas Delacroix."

The name sat in the firelight between us.

"My brother," he said.

* * *

Here is what I knew about Ansel Delacroix at that point, six days out of Kestrel Crossing: he could read water the way I read hoof and shoe; he ate like a man who expected the food to be taken away; he slept in shifts, always, even when there was no one to keep watch against; and he had a brother he mentioned exactly once, on the second night, in four words — "He drowned. Long ago." — and then changed the subject so hard I nearly got whiplash.

I'd filed it away. You learn to do that, running a forge. A horse that flinches at the left ear had a bad bridle once. A man who sleeps in shifts had something to watch for. I didn't know what. I knew it mattered.

Now I looked at him across a fire the size of a cupped hand and said, "Tell me."

"It's a long story."

"We're snowed into a cave on Gray Pass. I have nowhere to be until spring."

He almost smiled. It was the first time I'd seen anything close to one that wasn't wry or defensive. "You always this charming, or is it the altitude?"

"I'm never charming. It's the altitude. Talk."

* * *

He talked.

He told me about two brothers from the Cape Verren docks, a fisherman's sons, and how the younger one — Tomas, nineteen, strong as a winch and twice as cheerful — had gone into salvage work because it paid, and how the older one followed him into it because following Tomas was simply what Ansel did, the way a shadow follows a man, unexamined and loyal.

"He got a contract," Ansel said. "Private commission, well paid, no questions. A man came to the docks — older, mountain-tanned, nervous as a long-tailed cat — and hired Tomas to recover something from a flooded valley. Said it was family papers. Said his daughter needed them."

My hands went still in my lap. "Elias."

"Elias Wren." Ansel nodded slowly. "I didn't know the name then. Tomas took the job. Told me it was easy money — dive the old church, the papers were in the belfry, in and out in an afternoon." He fed a twig to the fire. Watched it catch. "He went down at midmorning. I was topside, tending his line. The line went slack."

The wind howled over the pass. Neither of us moved.

"They sealed the water," he said. "That same week. Navy cordon, no civilian diving, no explanation. I spent twenty years diving wrecks inside their restricted zones trying to get close enough to that valley to finish his dive. They threw me off sites. They fined me. Once they held me for three days." His jaw worked. "I thought it was grief, you understand? I thought I was a man who couldn't let his brother go. Turns out I was a man who couldn't let a lie go. I just didn't know whose."

"T. D. went down for it," I said quietly. "Pray the water is kinder than the men." I looked at the notebook in my hands. "My father hired your brother. And your brother died doing what — what I'm — " I stopped. The sentence had a cliff at the end of it and I didn't like the look of the drop.

"Doing what we're doing," Ansel finished for me, because he was the kind of man who walked up to cliffs.

I should have cried, I suppose. It felt like the moment for it. But the cold had gotten into everything, including, apparently, my tear ducts, and what came out instead was a sound I hadn't made in years — a laugh, short and cracked and real.

"What?" Ansel said.

"Nothing. It's just — " I gestured with the notebook. "Twenty years. Twenty years my father runs from whatever happened in that valley, and he can't outrun it, so he hires a diver to go back down for it. Twenty years you dive wrecks looking for your brother, and the thread that ties your brother to the bottom of that water is — my father. And the thread that ties my father to the whole rotten business is — your brother. And neither of them ever told us a damn thing, and now here we are, the two leftovers, carrying the same promise up the same mountain in a snowstorm."

"Leftovers," Ansel repeated.

"What would you call it?"

"I was going to say orphans, but ours are — " he paused, doing the arithmetic — "one dead and one useless, so leftovers might be more accurate."

And then he laughed.

I want to be clear about what that laugh was, because I don't think he'd done it in years and I don't think he knew how. It started as a cough, like the sound was rusty, and then it caught, and then it was real — a big, unguarded, startled laugh, the laugh of a man ambushed by his own relief. And I laughed with him, harder than the joke deserved, because that's how laughter works in cold places: it isn't about the joke. It's about being warm enough, for one moment, to make a sound like that and not be afraid of what hears you.

The horses shifted at the cave mouth. The fire burned down. We laughed until it hurt and then we sat there grinning at each other like idiots, two people with icicles in their hair and a dead man's notebook between them, and something shifted in the air — some load redistributing itself between us, the way weight redistributes on a pack animal when you finally cinch it right.

"Eleven," Ansel said, when we'd settled. He'd gone quiet again, but a different quiet. Thoughtful. "That number. It's been in the back of my head for days."

"From where?"

"The harbor records. Before I left Verren, I pulled everything I could find on the flooded township. Rivergate. The official count of the dead from the fire — the one they called accidental — was eleven." He looked at me across the flames. "Your father wasn't counting ore, Mara. He was counting graves."

I looked down at the notebook. At the cramped, frightened, stubborn hand that had raised me for six years and then vanished for twenty, and that I had hated with a clean, simple hate that had been the warmest thing I owned.

*Counted again. Eleven. Always eleven.*

"He carried their names," I said. "For twenty years. In a boot, probably, with the money."

"That sounds like him."

"You didn't know him."

"I knew a man who hired my brother because he couldn't live with himself otherwise, and then spent two decades paying for it in silence. I feel like I got the important parts."

I turned pages until I found the last entry — the most recent, the ink barely faded, written in a hand that shook. I read it aloud, because by then it seemed wrong to read anything silently.

"'If I do not finish it, my daughter will. She is stronger than I ever was. Forgive me, Mara. The map is true. The water gives back what it takes. Tell her the eleven were not forgotten. Tell her I was a coward who kept walking, and walking, and walking, and every mile was a prayer I did not know how to say.'"

The fire popped. I closed the notebook.

Ansel didn't say anything. He didn't offer comfort, which was — I'd learn this about him — his particular form of respect. He just reached over and set the tin pot of now-hot water near my hands, so I'd have something warm to hold, and looked back at the white wall of the storm.

"Thank you," I said, because I couldn't say the other ten thousand things.

"Don't mention it."

"Ansel." He glanced back. "Your brother. The commission — Elias paid him. But who told Tomas where the ledger was? Who knew it was in the church?"

Ansel frowned. "Elias, presumably. He drew the map. He — " He stopped. I watched the thought arrive in his face like weather. "He drew the map," he said slowly. "The map we're carrying. It shows the church. It shows the belfry."

"Yes."

"Mara, the map we're carrying is the dive plan. Your father drew the same map twice. Once for Tomas, twenty years ago." He looked at the oilcloth bundle in my saddlebag, at the paper inside it that had started all of this, and his voice went flat and careful. "And once for us."

Outside, the storm went on screaming. But under it — or maybe I imagined it, maybe it was just the wind finding a crack in the granite — I thought I heard something else, far off down the valley: a long, low sound, like metal, like a bell, waiting under twenty years of water for someone to give it back its voice.

"We should sleep in shifts," Ansel said.

"Of course we should," I said. "You always do."

"Not anymore," he said, and handed me the watch.
