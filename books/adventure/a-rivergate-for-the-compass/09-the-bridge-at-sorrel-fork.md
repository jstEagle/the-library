## 9. The Bridge at Sorrel Fork

The Sorrel Fork announced itself a full hour before we saw it — a gray ribbon of noise threading down through the pines, the kind of sound that in the low country means a creek and up here means a river with ambitions. Mara heard it first. She always heard things first, which I was learning was less a talent than a habit of a woman who'd grown up listening for a father's boots that never came.

"Water's high," she said, reining in at the edge of the tree line.

"Water's always high."

"This one's high and angry." She squinted down the slope. "There's the bridge."

It sat where bridges sit, spanning the gorge maybe forty feet above the water, a plank-and-timber affair built by men who'd assumed the river would stay reasonable. Snowmelt ran under it brown and muscular, carrying branches the size of fence posts, and the whole structure shivered slightly in the current's spray, like a horse that knows something's wrong.

"Cross it fast and don't look down," I said.

"You look down?"

"I look down. I'm a professional."

She gave me the look she gave me — the one that had started as contempt and had been quietly renegotiated somewhere on the switchbacks into something more like tolerance — and we came down off the ridge at a walk, because a trot on wet planks with a gorge underneath is how you end up as a story other people tell.

The bridge took us. I'll say that for it. It groaned like a dying man but it held, and on the far side the trail bent north along the river toward the pass, and I let myself feel, for the length of maybe half a mile, something adjacent to optimism.

That's when I looked back.

Mara saw my face and turned in her saddle. Down at the near end of the bridge, small with distance, riders had come out of the trees. Four of them. The same four, or their brothers, who'd been shadowing the ridgeline since Kestrel Crossing — the ones with the military bearing and the wrong questions, the ones whose questions about Elias Wren had been less *where is he* than *what did he leave behind*.

"They'll cross," Mara said.

"They'll cross."

"Then we ride."

But they didn't cross. That was the thing I couldn't make sense of at first, the thing my mind kept refusing like a bad coin. The riders dismounted at the bridge head. One of them walked out onto the planks a few steps, then back. And then another of them — I could see the motion even at that distance, unhurried, almost bureaucratic — bent down at the near abutment with something in his hands.

"Ansel," Mara said. "What are they doing?"

I didn't answer, because the smoke had already started.

* * *

They'd soaked the timbers in lamp oil, or something like it, and the fire went up the near end of the bridge the way fire goes up anything that's been drying in mountain sun all summer — fast, greedy, with a sound like tearing linen. In under a minute the whole span was burning, planks blackening and curling, and the riders stood back and watched their own work with the patience of men on a schedule.

"They're not trying to catch us," Mara said slowly. "They're trying to *keep* us."

"They're trying to slow us down." I was doing the arithmetic I didn't like. "A burned bridge means a ford. A ford in snowmelt means hours lost, maybe a horse, maybe more. And every hour is an hour Vane's people gain on us toward Rivergate."

"You think they know about the drainage."

"I think Vane's had twenty years to think about that valley, and nine days left to think about it now. He's not chasing us. He's herding us. There's a difference, and the difference is he doesn't much care if we live through it."

The fire burned itself down to a skeleton — black beams standing in the gorge like the ribs of something the river had eaten — and the riders mounted up and turned back the way they'd come. That bothered me worse than the fire. They weren't even going to wait and see. They'd done their work and left the river to do the rest.

Mara and I rode upstream along the bank for an hour, looking for the ford the trail maps promised and the river seemed to dispute. What we found was a wide gravel bar where the water spread out and, in a generous mood, might have been knee-deep in August. It was not August.

"Bottom's gravel," Mara said, dismounting and walking in to the hocks of her boots. "Firm enough. It's the current that'll kill you — it'll take your feet out from under and then it's a quarter mile of white water to the bend."

"You've forded rivers."

"I've shod horses that forded rivers. Close enough." She looked at me. "We cross one at a time. Rope between the saddles, me on the bank paying out slack. If the water takes you, I let the rope run and pull you in at the eddy below."

"And if the water takes you?"

"Then you'd better have been paying attention."

* * *

Mara went first, because she was going first whether I agreed or not, and watching her cross was a small education in the difference between courage and carelessness. She picked her line and held it, leaning upstream into the push of the current, letting the water break around her horse's chest, and the river did its best — it shoved, it spun the mare half around at the deepest channel, it ripped a hatbrim's worth of footing loose — and Mara talked to the horse the whole way through it, low and constant, the way you talk to something you refuse to let panic. They came out the far side soaked to the thigh and upright.

I crossed worse. The river caught my left stirrup in a pocket of current and rolled my horse's hindquarters downstream, and for three or four seconds that lasted a geological age I was occupied entirely with the project of not dying, and then the gravel came up under us and we staggered out dripping, and Mara didn't say anything, which was its own kind of mercy.

We were wringing out and re-saddling when I heard the shout.

It came from upstream — a cry cut short, the particular sound a man makes when the situation has left the region of words. I looked up and saw a horse in the water at the old crossing above the gravel bar, the one we'd rejected as too fast, and the horse was down, and a rider was in the current.

For a moment — I'll be honest, because a man who lies to himself about the small things can't be trusted with the large ones — I did nothing. The rider was one of theirs. One of the men hunting us, or sent by them, and every cold practical voice I owned said *let the river have him, he'd have let the river have you, it saves you a problem.*

Then I saw him come around the bend toward the white water, and I saw that he was young — young the way Tomas had been young, all arms and certainty and no idea how thin the ice of the world really was — and the cold practical voices went quiet, because there are debts a man pays without doing the accounting.

"Rope," I said, and Mara already had it.

I went into the water at the head of the eddy, which is where you enter if you want the river to hand you back. The cold hit like a plank across the chest — snowmelt doesn't feel like cold water, it feels like a decision being made about you — and I swam for the line of the flailing rider and let the current do the traveling.

He went under just as I reached him. I went down after, into the brown roar where nothing has edges, and my hand found cloth — wool jacket, army wool, I registered that even then — and I hauled, and the river argued, and we came up together with him coughing against my shoulder and me keeping his head back and my boots dragging for bottom that wasn't there yet. The rope caught me across the chest. Mara had read the eddy right. The bank came up to meet us in the shallows, and I crawled out with the boy half under me, both of us streaming, him retching river water and me retching mostly pride.

He was maybe nineteen. Wet black hair plastered to his forehead, a face that hadn't decided what it was yet, and the uniform under the soaked jacket — I'd been right about the wool — was army, private's stripes, the kind of soldier they hand a rifle and a story and don't ask questions of.

He looked at me. I looked at him. And then his eyes went past me to Mara, to the horses, to the map case strapped under my coat, and I watched something move through his face that I recognized, because I'd seen it in harbor offices and navy records rooms: the moment a man realizes the story he was given doesn't fit the facts in front of him.

"You're the thieves," he said. It came out half-drowned and half-wondering. "You're supposed to be thieves."

"Thieves," Mara repeated.

"Stolen government property. Two armed men, wanted for theft and — and assault." He was shivering now, the deep shiver of a body that has just been explained to by a river. "That's the warrant. That's what the captain read us. Thieves."

"And what did you think we'd stolen?" I asked.

He opened his mouth. Closed it. "I didn't — I never asked. You don't ask." He looked at the water, at the burned bridge downstream, at his own horse, which had made it to the far bank and stood there dripping and unbothered, traitor to both sides. "They told us you took something from Commander Vane. Papers. And that you'd kill to keep them."

Mara made a sound that was almost a laugh. "Kill. We've been running for a week and haven't so much as raised our voices at anyone."

"Except the bridge," I said. "Somebody raised fire to that."

"Not us." The boy — Reyes, I'd learn, though he didn't offer it then — shook his head, and the shaking had more in it than cold. "That was Sergeant Halle's order. Burn it behind the thieves. Slow them down." He swallowed. "He said it like it was nothing. Like it was a road repair."

I sat down on a rock, because my legs had opinions, and looked at this child the army had issued a story to, and I thought about Tomas, who'd been twenty-two when the navy handed him a job and a lie to carry it in, and who'd gone down into cold water with both and never come up.

"Listen to me," I said, and I kept my voice level, because a drowned man doesn't need more fright. "The papers we're carrying are twenty years old. They're not stolen from anyone. They belong to a dead man who spent his whole life running from what he saw in a valley called Rivergate, and they're going to his daughter, who is standing right behind you, wetter than you are and considerably less impressed by your uniform."

Reyes looked at Mara. Mara looked at Reyes, arms crossed, and said nothing, which from Mara was a full paragraph.

"Rivergate," he said. "That's the — that's the reservoir. That's the drainage." And then his face did the thing that faces do when a man adds numbers he's been told not to add. "We're not here for thieves. We're here for the *valley*."

"Now you've got it."

"But the captain said —"

"The captain," I said, "was told the same kind of story you were, one layer up. That's how it works. Everybody's holding a piece of paper that says the man below him is the problem, and the man at the top is holding the whole ledger." I didn't say *ledger*. It slipped out of my mouth before I could catch it, and I watched the word land on the boy like a dropped tool.

"The ledger," he repeated. "Sergeant Halle said that word once. When he thought I was asleep."

* * *

We gave him his boots back. That was Mara's doing, and she did it without ceremony — fished them out of the driftwood where they'd washed, set them down in front of him, and stood there while he put them on with fingers that wouldn't quite obey.

"You could keep me," he said. "Tie me up. I'm evidence now, aren't I? I know things."

"You're a drowned soldier who lost his horse in a ford," Mara said. "That's what you'll report, if you've got any sense. The rest you'll carry where it does some good."

"And where's that?"

"Wherever men ask questions," I said, "instead of reading them."

He stood there in his recovered boots, a nineteen-year-old holding a story that was too big for his pockets, and I could see the two futures fighting in his face — the one where he reported the theft as ordered and slept fine, and the one where he'd just been handed the weight of eleven names and a burned bridge and a river that had nearly kept him. I didn't push. You can't push a man into conscience. You can only fish him out of a river and let the cold do the talking.

"Downstream," he said finally. "There's a supply camp at the old mining road junction. Vane's main party is already at the valley. They're — they brought tools. Drums." He stopped, and his jaw worked. "Kerosene. A lot of kerosene."

Mara went very still beside me.

"How many days ahead of us?" I asked.

"Two. Maybe three, if the weather holds. But the drainage crew — the civilian crew — they're due in six days, and the commander wants the ground *clean* before anyone official walks on it." He looked at me, and for the first time he looked me straight on, and under all that youth there was something that hadn't been there when the river spat him out. "I was told you were the crime. Standing here, I'm starting to think you're the witnesses."

"Starting?"

"I'd like to not be part of the other side," he said, simply, the way a young man says a thing he hasn't yet learned to complicate. "I don't know how yet. But if you're going down into that valley —" he nodded north, toward the pass, toward Rivergate — "you should know they've got thirty men and you've got two horses and a map."

"And a ledger," Mara said.

"And a bell," I said, though I didn't yet know why I said it.

Reyes mounted the horse Mara flagged down from the far bank — his own, swimming back across the gravel bar like nothing had happened, because horses hold no grudges and fewer loyalties than men pretend to — and he turned downstream toward the camp where his sergeant waited, and I watched him go and did not believe I'd see him again, and was wrong, and would be glad to be wrong.

Mara stood beside me in the wind off the water, wringing out her braid.

"You saved a man who came to arrest us," she said.

"I saved a boy who was lied to. There's a difference, and the difference is going to matter."

"You think he'll talk?"

"I think," I said, "that Vane just made a mistake he can't take back. He sent a nineteen-year-old with a conscience into a river, and the river gave him back to us." I looked north, where the pass cut the sky, where a drowned valley waited with its church tower and its eleven names and whatever was left of my brother. "And now we've got three days, a burned bridge behind us, and thirty men between us and the truth. So we'd better ride."

Mara swung up into the saddle, and for a moment she looked back downstream, at the black ribs of the bridge standing in the gorge.

"They burned it to stop us," she said.

"No," I said, gathering my reins. "They burned it because they're afraid of what crosses bridges. Come on. We've got a valley to reach and a bell to ring."

She didn't ask me what I meant. She never did, when I didn't know myself. We turned north, and the river ran brown and loud behind us, carrying its branches, its secrets, and everything else it had ever been given, down and away.
