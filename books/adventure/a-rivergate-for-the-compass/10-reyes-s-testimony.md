## 10. Reyes's Testimony

We let the soldier keep his boots. I want that on the record, because everything else about that morning felt like a thing I'd have to answer for someday, and I'd like at least one line of it to read clean.

Ansel had fished him out of the Sorrel Fork with both hands and no hesitation, which told me something about Ansel Delacroix that his half-answers never had: the man's mercy lived below his words, down where the water was cold and clear. He hauled Reyes onto the far bank, rolled him onto his stomach, and pressed the river out of him while the rest of the riders watched from the burned bridge's near side, rifles slung, doing arithmetic they didn't like.

"Go home," Ansel told them, in that flat voice he used when he wanted to sound bored and didn't quite manage it.

They went. All but one.

* * *

Reyes sat where we put him, on a fallen aspen above the ford, dripping like something the river had decided against keeping. He was young — younger than me, maybe twenty-two, with the kind of face that hadn't yet learned what it was going to be guilty of. His boots squelched when he shifted. Ansel had taken his rifle and his sidearm but left the boots, and I remember thinking that was either kindness or a message, and that with Ansel it was usually both.

"You could have let me drown," Reyes said. It wasn't gratitude. It was an accusation aimed mostly at himself.

"Yes," Ansel said.

"Why didn't you?"

Ansel looked at him for a long moment. "Because I've pulled enough men out of that particular water," he said, and I understood he wasn't talking about the Fork.

I crouched down so we were eye to eye. Up close, the boy — man, I'll call him a man, he'd earned the word by the end of that hour — had river grit in the creases of his neck and hands that wouldn't stop trembling. Cold, or conscience. Probably both; in my experience they travel together.

"You were told we were thieves," I said. It wasn't a question. Thieves was what they'd been calling us since Kestrel Crossing, since the men with the wrong questions came asking after my father. *Two thieves and a dead man's map.* I'd heard the phrase secondhand from the livery boy and it had lodged in me like a splinter.

Reyes flinched. That was answer enough, but he gave me the words anyway. "Sergeant said you stole government property. Said there was a warrant." He swallowed. "Said you'd be armed."

"We're armed," Ansel said mildly, "with a map."

"And a promise," I said, because I was tired of letting Ansel carry all the weighty lines. He glanced at me — that quick sideways look he did, like a man checking a knot — and I thought, fine, let him look. The boy needed to hear it from somebody who hadn't spent twenty years underwater.

"Whose map?" Reyes asked. And then, before either of us answered, his own face supplied it. "Wren. Elias Wren." He said the name carefully, the way you handle something you've been warned is hot. "That's why we were sent. Not for theft."

"No," I said. "Not for theft."

He looked out at the river. The burned bridge smoked faintly across the water, a black skeleton letting off its last complaints. Somewhere upslope a jay was making an idiot of itself, and I remember being furious at the jay, at the ordinary bright morning of it all, because what was coming out of this soldier's mouth deserved weather. Deserved thunder at minimum.

"I'm going to tell you things," Reyes said slowly, "and then you're going to leave me here, and I'm going to walk back down that trail, and I don't know what happens to me after that. So I'd take it kindly if you didn't interrupt."

"We can do that," Ansel said.

I couldn't, entirely, but I kept it to one interruption, which for me is practically monastic discipline.

* * *

"The operation was called Rivergate Clearwater," Reyes began. "You know that much already, probably. Dam project. Flood the valley, power the coast, progress marches on." He recited it like catechism, flat and bitter. "Except the valley wasn't empty. There was a town. Four hundred people, give or take. Farms, a school, a church with a bell they were proud of — the survey notes mention the bell twice, which tells you how much the townsfolk loved it."

My father's handwriting rose up in my memory, those cramped marginalia I'd been reading by firelight in the cave two nights past. *The bell keeps the time now,* he'd written, under a sketch of a tower. At the time I'd taken it for sentiment. Sitting on that log, listening to a soldier quote a survey, I felt the sentence turn over and show me its underside.

"They were offered compensation," Reyes went on. "Most took it. Some held out. Eleven families held out, and the holdouts had lawyers, and the lawyers found problems with the seizure orders — irregular signatures, dates that didn't match, land parcels reassigned before the paperwork cleared. Enough problems that the whole project stalled for eight months." He rubbed his face with both hands. "And then there was a fire."

I interrupted then. I said I'd keep it to one. "A fire in winter. In a valley that gets forty feet of snow."

"A fire in the grange hall," he said, "on a night in late November, during a town meeting about the final eviction notices. Eleven people died. The official finding was accidental — a stove, bad flue, dry timber." He stopped. Started again, quieter. "The stove had been cold for three days. My grandfather was the one who wrote the incident report, years later, after he got out. He told me the flue was intact. Told me there were accelerant traces in the floorboards, and that the report he filed was rewritten before it ever reached a courthouse. He drank himself to death over it, my grandfather. Used to say the army doesn't burn evidence, it just floods it."

The jay finally shut up. Small mercies.

"Who signed the order?" Ansel asked. His voice had gone very still, the way water goes still right before it goes over the edge. I already knew the name. I think some part of me had known it since Kestrel Crossing, since the aide with the officer's bearing started asking after my father with questions shaped like courtesy and weighted like shackles.

"Commander Aldous Vane," Reyes said. "Then a major. He ran the seizure office. He was the one the holdout families dealt with, and he was the one who reported the fire, and he was the one who recommended the flooding schedule be accelerated 'to stabilize the site.'" He laughed, and there was nothing in it. "Stabilize. You flood a graveyard to stabilize it."

"Eleven," Ansel said. Just the number. But I heard what was underneath it, the same thing I'd heard in the cave when I read aloud my father's coded references to *the eleven*: a man counting, and the count not coming out even against the grief.

"Eleven," Reyes confirmed. "And a twelfth name that isn't on any list. A prospector who saw too much and carried the proof out of the valley on his own two feet, ahead of the cordons." He looked directly at me then, and I made myself hold it. "Elias Wren. He had a ledger. Copies of the seizure files, the original incident report, witness statements from three townsfolk who survived because they were late to the meeting. Vane has been looking for that ledger for twenty years. Every few years a new officer gets assigned to find it, and every few years the search quietly dies, because Wren was good at staying lost." He paused. "Until he got sick. Until he surfaced long enough to send for his daughter."

The word *daughter* landed on me like a dropped anvil. I stood up, mostly because sitting down had become unbearable, and walked three steps toward the river and stood there with my arms crossed like I was cold, which I was, though not only from the temperature.

Twenty years. Twenty years my father had been gone — gone without letters worth the name, gone with a postcard twice a year and a smell of pipe smoke in packages I stopped opening around my fourteenth birthday. I had built a whole architecture of resentment around that absence. He chose the hills over us. He chose gold over family. He was a coward with a wanderer's alibi. I had said worse than that, at the forge, to customers who nodded along because everyone in Kestrel Crossing had a parent-shaped hole and we compared them like scars.

And the truth was he'd been running. Running from a fire and eleven names and a ledger heavy enough to hang a commander, carrying it the way you carry a live coal — alone, because anyone close to it burns. He'd left us not because he didn't love us but because love was exactly what would get us killed, and he'd been too proud or too broken or too much my father to explain that in a letter. Or maybe he'd tried and the letters were intercepted. I didn't know yet. I knew only that every ugly sentence I'd ever constructed about Elias Wren now had a body under it, and the body was wearing a uniform I hadn't voted for.

"He never sold it," I said, turning back. "All these years. He could have sold it to a newspaper, to Vane himself, to anybody, and bought a life. He carried it instead."

"He carried it," Reyes agreed. "And twenty years ago he hired help to move it somewhere safe. A diver." He hesitated, and I watched a young man decide, in real time, whether to hand a stranger a knife or sheathe it. He handed it over. "A diver named Delacroix. First name Tomas."

* * *

I will tell you honestly what happened inside me at that name, because a wry narrator owes you at least one unguarded moment per chapter and this is mine.

The world did not go white. It went quiet — the specific quiet of deep water, pressure without sound. Beside me, Ansel did not move at all, which was how I knew the name had struck him somewhere structural. I had known about a brother. Half an answer, given on a switchback with the wind taking most of it: *lost him diving, twenty years back, navy sealed the wreck.* I had filed it away as grief, the private kind, the kind you don't poke.

Now the private kind turned out to have a public address. Rivergate. The bell. The ledger.

"Tomas Delacroix drowned in the reservoir," Reyes said gently, watching Ansel. "Officially. Training exercise, equipment failure. But my grandfather's papers mentioned a contract diver, hired through intermediaries, engaged to recover documents from the flooded township before the water reached full pool. The diver went down near the church. Never surfaced. The navy sealed the site within the week and listed it as an accident." He spread his hands. "Vane didn't kill your brother with his own hands, Mr. Delacroix. He built a machine that did, and then he flooded the machine."

Ansel said nothing. He stood at the edge of the ford with the current talking over the rocks and his face doing absolutely nothing, and I had been around him long enough by then to know that nothing was the loudest thing he could say. I crossed to him and put my hand on his forearm — the first time I'd touched him on purpose — and he didn't pull away, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough.

Because here is what I understood in that moment, standing in the splash zone of two catastrophes: my father and his brother had died — well, one died, one vanished into a life of exile — carrying the same errand. And now the errand had come to us, hand to hand, like a relay baton passed through twenty years of snow. Elias hires Tomas. Tomas drowns. Elias runs. Elias dies. Elias's map reaches Ansel. Ansel carries it to Elias's daughter. And the daughter walks beside him into the same valley, toward the same ledger, pursued by the same man.

Destiny, if you believe in it. Inheritance, if you don't. Either way, somebody upstream had decided we were the ones still standing.

"We're not turning around," I said. It came out steadier than I felt. "Whatever you were thinking, Delacroix — whatever noble retreat you were drafting — cancel it."

"I wasn't drafting anything," he said. And then, because he was incapable of leaving a sentence undefended: "I was counting days."

"Counting them to what?"

"To the drainage." He looked at me, and for once there was no half-answer anywhere in him. "The reservoir's scheduled for emergency drawdown. Nine days. When the water goes, the valley floor comes up for the first time in twenty years — and Vane will be there before anyone else, burning what the water didn't finish."

That was the first I heard of the deadline, and I want it noted that I learned it from Ansel and not from our prisoner, and that I was angry about the sequencing for a solid hour afterward. But anger keeps a body moving, so perhaps he knew what he was doing.

* * *

We released Reyes the way Ansel had promised: with his boots, his shame, and a canteen.

He stood at the treeline, half-turned back toward us, a soldier-shaped question mark. "When they ask me what happened at the ford," he said, "I'll tell them I lost you in the rapids. Two against six, night crossing, gone in the dark." He worked his jaw. "It might even be mostly true."

"It'll do," Ansel said.

"But listen." Reyes stepped back toward us, urgent now, voice dropping. "Vane isn't sending six men next time. He knows Wren's daughter is with you — the aide recognized her at the forge, that's why the pace changed. He'll bring enough to make a valley disappear twice." He glanced at the smoking bridge. "There are others like me. Young ones who signed up for bridges and flood surveys and got handed a manhunt instead. If it comes to it — if it comes to a choice between his order and what's true — some of us will choose true. Remember that."

"I will," I said, and meant it more than I've meant most things.

He went. We watched him until the trees took him, a small figure walking downhill with wet boots and straight shoulders, and I thought: that one will carry this too, now. That's how it works, apparently. Truth is less a weapon than a weight, and once it's in your hands you can't set it down without someone noticing the shape it left.

Ansel repacked the horses in silence. I stood a while longer at the ford, looking south, toward the high country and the drowned valley beyond it, and I let myself feel the full horror of it — the fire, the eleven, the stove that had been cold for three days, my father alone in the snow with a ledger and no one to tell. Horror is supposed to paralyze. Everyone says so. But what I felt that morning was closer to ballast: something heavy settling low in me, lowering my center of gravity, making me harder to knock over.

"Nine days," I said, swinging up into the saddle.

"Eight and a half, the way you ride," Ansel said, and put his heels to his horse, and we went up into the white country together — two heirs to a dead man's errand, carrying proof, pursued by the man who'd built the machine, racing a reservoir down to its bones.

Behind us the Sorrel Fork ran on over its rocks, indifferent and loud, like a jury that had already heard everything and was waiting to see what we'd do about it.
