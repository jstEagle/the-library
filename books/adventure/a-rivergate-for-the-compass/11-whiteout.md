## 11. Whiteout

The storm didn't announce itself. That's the thing about mountains — they don't knock. They just change their mind about you.

We'd made Gray Pass by midmorning, or what passed for midmorning under a sky the color of wet slate. The trail had been kind for two days, almost suspiciously so, and I'd let myself believe the range was done with us. Mara had said nothing, but she'd been watching the peaks the way a farrier watches a horse favoring a leg — not alarmed, just keeping accounts.

"Cloud's wrong," she said, around noon.

"Clouds are generally wrong," I said. "It's their whole temperament."

She didn't laugh. She squinted west, where the sky had gone the particular yellow-gray that old-timers call a snow-sky and I call a warning you'll wish you'd heeded. "That's not weather," she said. "That's a wall."

I wanted to argue. I'd gotten good at arguing with her over the past week — it was how we talked, mostly, a fence built of objections that we both leaned on without ever quite knocking down. But she was right, and we both knew it, and the wind chose that moment to swing around from the southwest carrying a smell like iron and cold stone.

The horses knew first. Brego threw his head and pulled at the lead rope, and Mara's mare, Juniper, went stiff-legged and wide-eyed, ears swiveling like they were trying to catch a sound arriving ahead of the air itself.

"We need shelter," Mara said. "Now. Not when it starts. Now."

I looked at the map of the pass in my head — Elias's map, which had gotten us this far with its confident little ink lines and its marginalia in a dead man's hand. There was supposed to be a miner's cabin somewhere below the saddle on the eastern side. *Old claim shack, roof holds,* Elias had written, in the same handwriting that had once broken his daughter's heart and was now, posthumously, trying to mend it.

"That's an hour down," I said.

"The storm's ten minutes out."

So we ran them. Which is to say we did the stupid thing, because the alternative was the stupid thing done slower. We pushed the horses off the saddle into the wind, and the wind took the temperature with it — I could feel the warmth draining out of the day like water out of a cracked hull. Snow came first as a few experimental flakes, sideways, testing us. Then as a curtain. Then as nothing at all, because there was no up or down anymore, just white moving in every direction including ones that shouldn't exist.

I've been underwater in zero visibility. I know what it is to lose the world and keep moving anyway, trusting a line in your hand. This was that, except the line was Mara's voice, shouting ahead of me, and the horses were screaming behind us, and then —

Then the horses weren't there.

I don't know how it happened. One moment the lead rope was taut in my fist, transmitting Brego's panic straight up my arm, and the next moment it was slack, and I whirled in the white and shouted his name and got nothing back but the wind eating the word whole. I stood there like a fool holding forty feet of rope attached to nothing, and Mara came stumbling back toward me, and her face said she'd lost Juniper too.

"They'll run for the treeline," she shouted. "Horses are smarter than we are in this. They'll find the trees and stand in them."

"And if they don't?"

"Then we're walking out of these mountains on our own feet, and I'd rather not discuss it!" She grabbed my sleeve. "Cabin. Move."

We moved. Or we moved in the sense that we fell downhill repeatedly in a generally easterly direction, which is the mountain man's definition of progress. The snow was past my knees in the drifts and the wind hit like something with intent. Twice I went down and twice Mara hauled me up, her grip astonishingly strong — forge hands, I reminded myself, hands that bend iron for a living — and the second time she kept hold of my arm after she'd gotten me vertical, and I understood that wasn't affection. It was inventory. She was making sure all her pieces were still attached.

I don't know how long. Twenty minutes. An hour. Time doesn't work in a whiteout; it just stops being one of the things available to you. And then, out of the roaring gray, a shape — a dark rectangle leaning at a drunk angle against the slope, half-swallowed by drift.

The cabin. Elias's cabin, twenty years abandoned, roof still holding, exactly as promised. The dead man kept his word even from under the ground. It seemed to be his principal occupation now.

* * *

The door was a plank on leather hinges and it fought us, and then we were inside, and inside was — dark, and small, and smelled of mouse and old smoke, and it was the finest palace ever raised by human hands. I got the door shut and braced it with a length of fallen rafter, and then we stood in the black listening to the storm try the walls and finding them, for now, sufficient.

Mara struck a light before I found mine. Her hands were shaking — I noticed it only because she held them up and looked at them with clinical disgust, the way she looked at everything that failed to meet her standards, including, frequently, me.

"Stove," she said. "There's a stove."

There was. There was also half a cord of split wood stacked against the wall, seasoned dry by two decades of mountain air, left by some prospector who'd planned to come back and never did. The mountains are full of such inheritances. We fed the stove until it roared, and the little room turned orange and almost warm, and we peeled off the outer layers of ice that had grown on us like a second set of clothes.

My fingers wouldn't work right for a while. Neither would hers. We sat close to the stove and didn't talk, because talking cost heat somehow, or felt like it did, and the storm hammered the roof with a sound like a giant sifting gravel.

It was Mara who broke the silence, eventually, in the way she broke most things: directly, and without warning.

"You never finished telling me about your brother."

I should have expected it. There'd been a version of the story on the high trail, three days back — Tomas, the wreck, the navy seal, the twenty years — delivered in the clipped voice I use for things I don't want examined. She'd let it lie since then. I'd mistaken her silence for disinterest. It was patience. She was a farrier; she knew you don't force a hoof. You wait for the animal to give you the foot.

"He died in the reservoir," I said. "At Rivergate. I told you that."

"You told me where." She poked the stove, sending up a constellation of sparks. "You didn't tell me how. Or why a salvage diver's brother was diving a navy-sealed lake in the first place. Or why you've spent twenty years diving the same water like you're looking for something." She glanced at me. "Or someone."

The fire popped. Outside, the wind found a loose shingle and worried it like a dog with a bone.

"Elias hired him," I said.

Her head came up fast. "What?"

"Tomas was a diver too. Better than me, if I'm honest — he had the patience for it, the stillness. I was always the one who needed to be moving." I watched the fire instead of her. It was easier. "Three weeks before he died, a man came to Cape Verren asking discreet questions about divers who could work deep, cold, unsupervised water. No permits. No questions. Good money. Tomas took the job. I thought it was foolhardy and I told him so, and he laughed and said the pay would buy us a boat of our own, and I —" My throat did something inconvenient. "I helped him load his gear."

Mara had gone very still. "Elias Wren hired my brother to dive Rivergate," she said slowly. "Twenty years ago."

"I didn't know it was Elias. Not then. I found out later, piecing it together — the description matched, the timing matched, and when he showed me the map in that tavern, I knew. The way he looked at me when I said my name. He knew who I was the whole time, Mara. He sought me out. A dying man arranging his last delivery, and he picked the brother of the diver he got killed."

"Got killed," she repeated. "You think —"

"There's a church in Rivergate." I heard my own voice go flat and distant, the voice I use on the deck of a boat when the water's bad. "Old stone church, the one building in the valley too stubborn to tear down before the flooding. There's a bell in the tower. Tomas's last message to me — a note left with his landlady, in case — well. In case. It said he'd found what the old man wanted. It said it was in the bell. And then he went down and he didn't come up, and the navy sealed the site within the week, and every question I asked for twenty years came back stamped, redacted, or ignored."

The storm filled the pause. Mara fed another log to the stove, unhurried, giving me the room the way she gave horses room.

"So you dive it," she said. "Every season."

"Every season the ice allows. Off the point near the tower. I tell myself I'm surveying. I tell myself it's salvage work, that the navy's seal won't hold forever, that someday the water gives him back." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Twenty years of telling myself stories. You want to know the truth? I dive it because it's the closest I can get to him. Because the water where he stopped is the only place he still exists, and I can't — I can't leave him alone in it."

The words hung there, and I waited for pity, which I'd have resented, or comfort, which I'd have deflected. Mara did neither. She looked into the fire for a long time, and when she spoke, her voice was rough.

"He was going into the bell. For the ledger."

"If that's what the ledger is. Whatever Elias buried in that church, Tomas found it, and it killed him, and I have spent twenty years diving circles around the proof without knowing it was proof." I shook my head. "And then a dying stranger buys me a drink and hands me a map, and I carry it a hundred miles through the mountains because I can't refuse a dead man's eyes, and the whole time — the whole time, Mara — I'm delivering my brother's errand. Finishing his job. Walking his route." I looked at her. "Your father didn't hire me to bring you a map. He hired me to finish what he started. What Tomas died starting."

She was quiet a long moment. Then she said, "He didn't get him killed."

"Mara —"

"No. Listen." She turned to face me fully, firelight on one side of her face, shadow on the other. "My father was a coward. I've had twenty years to build that case and I built it well — he ran, he drank, he sent nothing, not a letter, not a coin, not one damn word in two decades. I will stand by that verdict until someone shows me different." Her jaw tightened. "But a coward doesn't hire a diver to go back. A coward runs and stays run. Something in that valley mattered more to him than staying safe, and it cost him everything he had left, and he spent his last breath making sure it landed somewhere it could do damage." She jabbed a finger at the map roll, tucked safe inside my coat. "He couldn't carry it himself. So he carried it to us. Both of us. Children of his messes."

The stove ticked. Somewhere in the wall, a mouse expressed an opinion.

"My mother used to say grief shared is weight halved," I said finally. "I used to think that was the sort of thing mothers say because it rhymes with something."

"It doesn't rhyme."

"She wasn't big on accuracy." I fed the stove rather than look at her. "But she might have been onto something. Twenty years I've carried him alone, and it turns out he weighs less with two people under him. Who knew."

"Everyone," Mara said. "Everyone knew. You just don't ask for help. It's very annoying."

"You don't either."

"I'm aware. I contain multitudes of annoying." But she was almost smiling, and I was almost smiling, and for a moment the cabin was warm in a way that had nothing to do with the stove.

We spread our bedrolls on either side of the fire, backs to the wall, boots on, because you sleep booted in the high country or you don't wake with feet. The storm showed no sign of tiring. If anything it had settled in for the night, thumping the roof in long slow rhythms, and I lay there listening to it and thinking about bells.

"Mara," I said, into the dark. "When the reservoir drains — the drainage schedule, the reason Vane's people are crawling all over this range —"

"Nine days," she said. "You told me. The dam's compromised, they're dropping the water level for emergency inspection. First time in twenty years anyone's seen the valley floor."

"First time anyone's seen the church." I stared at the rafters. "Tomas's note said the ledger was in the bell. If it's still there — if twenty years of water hasn't taken it — then in nine days, that bell comes out of the water for the first time since he died. And Aldous Vane will have men standing on the shore waiting for it."

Silence. Then Mara sat up, and even in the dark I could feel the change in her, the way you feel a barometer falling.

"Then we can't be nine days behind," she said. "We have to be there first."

* * *

Morning came gray and quieter, the storm spending its last fury on the ridgelines above us while the valley below lay under three feet of new snow, featureless, erased. We dug out the door and stood blinking in a world remade, dazzling and silent and utterly indifferent to what had been decided in it overnight.

The horses were standing in the treeline fifty yards downslope, hip-deep in drift, Brego's saddle askew and Juniper grazing a snow-buried patch of bunchgrass with the untroubled air of an animal who has never once doubted herself. Mara made a sound I'd never heard from her before — half sob, half laugh — and went down to them at a run.

I followed more slowly, because I'd seen something else.

Down-valley, along the trail we'd have taken, there were tracks. Not ours. Not the horses'. Rider sign, several riders, cutting across the fresh snow on a line that bent — not toward the pass behind us, but ahead. Down. Toward the river country. Toward Rivergate.

They'd passed in the night, while we told each other the truth by stove-light. While we decided to hurry.

Vane's men weren't chasing us anymore.

They were racing us.
