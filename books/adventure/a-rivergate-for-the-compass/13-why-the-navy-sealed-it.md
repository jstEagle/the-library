## 13. Why the Navy Sealed It

The hermit's cabin smelled of pine smoke and old paper, which is to say it smelled like a life that had been thought about.

His name, he said, was Oren Fitch. He'd been the schoolteacher in Rivergate for eleven years before the water came, and he'd been up here on Gray Pass for twenty since. He was a small man made mostly of beard and wool, with hands that trembled until you put something in them — a cup, a log for the stove, a question worth answering. Then they went still as set stones.

Mara had told him who we were while I'd been seeing to the horses. Now she sat across from him at his table, Elias's map spread flat between them, weighted at the corners with river stones worn smooth by a drowned valley. She'd taken off her gloves and her fingers were white-knuckled against the wood, and I understood why. This man had known her father. Had maybe known him better than she ever had.

"You knew Elias Wren," she said. It wasn't a question, but Fitch treated it like one anyway.

"Knew him? Boy." He shook his head slowly. "I taught half this valley to read. Elias Wren sat in my third row and chewed his pencil down to splinters because he couldn't stop thinking long enough to hold it right. When the dam men came—" He stopped. Looked at me instead of her, which was its own kind of answer. "You're the diver."

"I'm the diver."

"Tomas Delacroix's brother."

The name landed in my chest the way names do when you've spent twenty years keeping them in a sealed room. I didn't say anything. Mara looked at me sideways, and I gave her nothing, because there wasn't anything yet to give. Just a door opening somewhere inside me that I'd bricked over so long ago I'd forgotten where the bricks were.

Fitch poured three cups of something that had started life as tea and ended it as punishment. "Sit," he said. "Both of you. What I have to tell you takes longer than standing legs allow."

* * *

He talked for two hours, and I'm going to give it to you straight, because that's how he gave it, and because I've learned that the truth wears different clothes depending on who's dressing it.

Rivergate hadn't wanted to sell. That was the whole of it, the seed of everything. The government wanted the valley for the reservoir — said the lowlands downstream needed flood control, said the power was for the good of the many, all the things governments say when they mean *we want what you have and we've already drawn the maps*. Most towns fold when the money's right or the pressure's hard enough. Rivergate had a sawmill, a church, a schoolhouse, and a stubbornness that ran four generations deep, and they folded not at all.

"So Vane came," Fitch said. "Not with soldiers at first. With papers. Eminent domain, condemnation proceedings, offers that insulted a man's intelligence and then doubled when he wouldn't sign. And when the town still held — when they got themselves a lawyer out of the capital and filed injunctions — the papers stopped working and other methods began."

"What kind of methods?" Mara asked.

"The kind that don't leave signatures." Fitch's eyes were steady and very old. "Barn fires. A mill dam breached in spring thaw. Livestock poisoned. Men in plain clothes asking questions at the tavern about who led the resistance, and finding out, and those men's barns burning the following week. Aldous Vane ran it like a campaign, because that's what it was. A war against eleven hundred farmers and millworkers, fought with lawyers and matches."

"And then the fire," I said.

"And then the fire." He set his cup down with a click that sounded final as a lock turning. "The night of the ninth of October. The church — the old meeting hall attached to it, where the town kept its records and its treasury and the ledger of every transaction, every petition, every payment Vane's office had made to informers inside the valley. Because yes, there were informers, and yes, the town council knew who some of them were, and they'd been writing it all down. Names. Dates. Amounts. Twenty years of evidence in one building, because Elias Wren convinced them to keep it all together where the whole town could swear to it."

"Elias," Mara said quietly.

"Your father was the one who organized it. The record-keeping. He understood early what the rest of us took too long to learn — that a man like Vane can survive losing a fight, but he cannot survive being *documented*." Fitch smiled without any warmth in it. "So the fire came for the meeting hall on a night when the council was meeting. Eleven people died. Eleven, and every one of them either a signer of the petitions or a keeper of the records. The county ruled it accidental — faulty flue, dry timber, tragic. The navy arrived within the week to 'assist with relocation,' which meant the survivors were scattered across three counties before anyone could hold a funeral properly, and the dam construction began the following spring over ground that still had graves in it nobody had moved."

"They flooded a graveyard," Mara said. Her voice was flat, which is how I've learned to recognize the worst anger — hers or mine. Flat as still water, and just as deep.

"They flooded everything," Fitch said. "That was rather the point. You can't investigate a fire under sixty feet of water. You can't exhume testimony from people you've paid to move away and never speak again. The valley became its own silence." He turned to me. "Except."

"Except," I said.

"Except Elias got out with copies. Not everything — but enough. The ledger itself, the original, he'd hidden somewhere he thought no one would ever look. Somewhere the water would guard it better than any vault." Fitch's mouth did something complicated. "And then he ran. Twenty years running, moving from claim to claim, camp to camp, always alone, always watching the road behind him. I got one letter a year, postmarked nowhere, unsigned. Until the letters stopped."

"He's dead," Mara said. "Three weeks ago. In a dockside room in Cape Verren, coughing his lungs out into a basin." She said it without looking at me, and I let her have it however she needed to say it. "He sent me a map."

"He'd have sent you more than a map if he could have." Fitch reached across the table and covered her hand with his trembling-still one. "He'd have sent you an apology twenty years long. But men like your father send what they can carry, and what he could carry was paper."

* * *

It was after midnight when I finally asked the question I'd been carrying since Cape Verren, since the wreck off the point, since the officers with their too-careful menace. I asked it the way you ask a thing you already know the answer to, because knowing and admitting are different countries and sometimes you need a guide between them.

"The wreck I was diving. Off Cape Verren. The one the navy sealed."

Fitch looked at me for a long moment. "What did you find down there?"

"Foundations. Cut stone. A bell housing, half-buried. Depth's wrong for a shipwreck and right for a building." I heard my own voice go quiet. "There's a church bell down there, Mr. Fitch. I've touched it."

"That's not Cape Verren," Fitch said gently. "That's Rivergate. Or it was. The reservoir they built over the valley — they call it the Verren Reservoir now, named for the cape, because naming a lake after a graveyard makes people ask fewer questions. Your wreck site sits above the old township. You've been diving your brother's grave for twenty years, Mr. Delacroix, and the navy has been letting you, because a salvage diver poking around drowned foundations looks like exactly what he is — a man chasing ghosts — right up until he starts asking questions with dates in them."

The room tilted, the way rooms do when the floor turns out to be a trapdoor. Tomas. My brother, who'd gone down off "Cape Verren" on a contract job twenty years ago and never come up, in water the navy closed to diving within the month. I'd spent two decades measuring my life in miles because years refused to be measured, and the whole time I'd been swimming laps over the thing that killed him.

"He wasn't on a contract job," I said. It wasn't a question either.

"No," Fitch said. "He wasn't. Elias hired him. A young diver, hungry, discreet, recommended by a friend of a friend. The job was simple: get into the flooded meeting hall through the belfry, recover the original ledger, bring it up. Elias had the copies, but copies can be dismissed. Originals convict." Fitch's hands were flat on the table now, trembling faintly. "Tomas Delacroix went down on a clear morning in June and did not come up. The navy recovered the body two days later and ruled it a diving accident — equipment failure, they said. And then they sealed the site, and Elias Wren lost the last of his courage somewhere in that same water, because he understood what the sealing meant. They hadn't just killed a witness. They'd shown him they could reach anywhere."

Mara's hand found mine across the table. I didn't look at her. If I looked at her I was going to come apart, and there was still talking to do.

"Why tell us now?" I managed. "Twenty years, and you sit up here on your mountain. Why open the door tonight?"

"Because the water's leaving," Fitch said.

* * *

He got up then, slow, joints cracking like green wood in a fire, and went to a chest beneath the window. From it he took a newspaper — folded soft with handling, weeks old, the print going gray.

"Emergency drainage order," he said. "Cracked spillway. Structural failure risk. The engineers want the reservoir drawn down before spring melt, and the bureaucracy, for once, moved fast — because a dam failure downstream would kill more people than the cover-up ever did, and even liars can do arithmetic." He laid the paper on the table. "Drawdown begins in nine days. In nine days, Mr. Delacroix, the valley floor will see sky for the first time in twenty years. The fences. The foundations. The church. The graves." He tapped the paper once. "Everything."

"And Vane," Mara said.

"Is not a man who lets history surface on its own schedule." Fitch sat back down. "I have friends — such as they are — who watch the roads. There's been traffic. Survey crews with no survey equipment. Men with federal credentials that don't check out. And Commander Aldous Vane himself, retired with honors, taking a sudden interest in watershed management. He means to be standing on that valley floor when the water goes down, and he means to have fire crews with him, and by the time the press arrives to photograph the great revealed valley, there'll be nothing left to reveal but ash and mud and a story about looters."

"The ledger," I said. "If the original's still down there—"

"It's still down there." Fitch said it with the certainty of a man who had helped hide it. "In the belfry. Where Tomas died trying to reach it. The navy sealed the site, but they never found it — I know because Elias spent ten years paying divers to try, and none would go near water the navy had closed. The ledger is still wedged in the stonework of a drowned church, waiting. And in nine days, the water that's been guarding it will be gone, and the only thing between Aldous Vane and a clean burn will be whoever gets there first."

The stove ticked. Outside, the wind worried the eaves, and somewhere far off a snow-slope settled with a sound like a long exhale. Nine days. I did the arithmetic the way I did everything — in miles. Gray Pass to the valley head was three days' hard travel if the weather held and four if it didn't, and the weather, judging by the wind, intended to have opinions.

Mara was already pulling the map toward her, her finger tracing the route, her jaw set in a way I recognized now as inheritance. Elias's jaw. Elias's stubbornness, four generations deep.

"We're not running anymore," she said.

No, I thought. We're not. All winter I'd thought of it as flight — the riders on the ridge, the burned bridge, the cold camps, all of it a chase with us on the wrong end of it. But a chase has two shapes, and I'd been reading it backward. We weren't fleeing something. We were racing toward it, toward a drowned church and a deadline and a brother's unfinished work sitting in sixty feet of dark water with a date on it now.

Nine days. For twenty years I'd measured my life in miles traveled because miles don't accuse you. But miles run out. Deadlines don't forgive. And somewhere below the shrinking waterline of the Verren Reservoir, a ledger and a dive knife waited in the belfry of a church that had once been full of stubborn people who wrote everything down.

I stood up, and my knees had decided before my head had finished voting.

"How fast can we move?" I asked Fitch.

He looked at me, then at Mara, then at the map, and the tremble went out of his hands entirely.

"Faster than the snow," he said. "If you leave before dawn."
