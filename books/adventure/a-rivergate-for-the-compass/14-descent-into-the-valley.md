## 14. Descent into the Valley

The snowmelt was running loud enough to swallow conversation, which suited me fine, because I didn't trust what I'd say.

We'd come down off Gray Pass through three days of thaw — the kind of spring that arrives in the high country all at once, like a debt collector. The trail had turned to porridge. The air smelled of wet stone and pine rot and something older underneath it, something I couldn't name until I saw the first fence post and understood that the smell was twenty years of drowned wood drying out in the sun for the first time.

Mara stopped when she saw it too. A fence line, running down the slope ahead of us, following a contour that made no sense for sheep or cattle — until you realized it had never marked a pasture at all. It had marked a property line. A town line. Somebody's idea, once, of where one man's life ended and his neighbor's began.

"It's still standing," she said. "After all that water."

"Water's patient," I said. "It doesn't tear things down. It just holds them."

She looked at me sideways. She'd learned by now to hear the second thing inside my first thing, the way you learn to hear a bell under a lake. "That's not comfort, Ansel. That's a threat."

"I know," I said. "Come on."

* * *

The valley opened below us the way a secret opens — slowly, then all at once.

We came around a shoulder of granite with the meltwater thundering in a chute beside us, and there it was: Rivergate. Or the skull of it. The reservoir sat low in its basin, drained down to maybe a third of what it had held my whole adult life, and what it had given back was a landscape I had no right to be seeing. Mudflats silvered with shallow water. The ghost-lines of streets, laid out in silt and old cobble, running straight as arguments toward a center that no longer existed. Here and there a rooftop broke the surface like a whale's back — tar paper and rotted shingle, black with two decades of submersion, but unmistakably roofs. Houses. Kitchens where somebody had burned breakfast and sworn about it. Porches where somebody had shelled peas on an August evening and complained about the heat, never dreaming they'd have wanted it back.

I've spent my life underwater. I know what drowned places look like from beneath — the green murk, the soft collapse of everything, the way a town becomes a rumor of itself. But this was different. This was a drowning reversed. The valley was exhaling. And every fence post and doorframe coming up out of the mud was a sentence finishing itself after twenty years of holding its breath.

"There." Mara pointed.

The church tower stood at the far end of the flats, and it was breaching. The waterline had dropped past its belfry windows, and the top eight feet of the steeple stood clear in the morning light, streaming, dripping, its weathervane cocked at an angle like a man craning his neck to see who'd finally come. The bell housing gaped dark above the water. I could see the bell itself — just its crown, just the curve of bronze catching sun.

Tomas had died under that tower. Somewhere in that dark water, in that belfry, my brother had gone down after a ledger and hadn't come up, and I'd spent twenty years diving other people's wrecks because I couldn't make myself dive that one.

"You're doing the thing," Mara said quietly.

"What thing?"

"The thing where your face goes somewhere I can't follow."

I worked my jaw. "My brother's under there."

"I know." She didn't say *I'm sorry*. She'd figured out early that I'd rather take a beating than pity. Instead she said, "Then let's go get him."

* * *

We came down through the mudflats careful as thieves, because we were thieves, sort of — thieves of the truth, which is the only kind the navy punishes harder than the ordinary kind. The going was slow. The ground sucked at our boots. In places the silt had dried into a crust that held, and in others it was still soup, and twice I went in to the knee and had to laugh so I wouldn't swear, and Mara laughed too, and the laughter had a strange echo out there among the rooftops, like the town itself was surprised to hear anything but water.

We passed a house with its front door standing open to the air. Inside, visible through the doorway, a staircase rose into darkness, and on the newel post someone had tied a strip of cloth — faded past color, but tied in a bow, deliberate, the way you tie a ribbon on a gift or mark a child's height. Twenty years underwater and the knot had held. Somebody had meant it to hold.

Mara touched the post once, lightly, and walked on without a word.

By midday we could read the town. Main Street ran along the old river course, storefronts surfacing in rows, their false fronts leaning together like drunks holding each other up. A wagon sat in what had been the road, its bed full of silt, one wheel upright and one collapsed. A child's tricycle lay on its side near a wellhead, red paint gone brown, and I looked at it exactly long enough to feel it in my chest and then made myself look away, because a man can drown in sympathy easier than in water, and I had work to do.

"The map says the church records office," Mara murmured, checking Elias's hand-drawn lines against the terrain. "He marked it here — see, the little square with the cross. And there's a note." She squinted at her father's cramped pencil. "*'Where he kept the true count.'*"

"The true count," I said. "Eleven."

"Eleven," she agreed.

We were still fifty yards from the church when I caught her arm and pulled us both down behind a drowned hay rake.

Smoke.

A thin thread of it rising from behind the church, where the ground climbed toward the old mill district. And under the meltwater roar, faint but unmistakable: the ring of iron on iron. Hammering. Voices. The clank of rigging.

"They're already here," Mara breathed.

"I told you Vane would come early."

"I hoped you'd be wrong. You're never wrong. It's exhausting."

We wormed forward through the mud and got our first look at the camp, and my stomach did something cold and slow, the way it does when a diver sees the wreck is worse than reported.

They'd built it in the lee of the church hill, maybe two hundred yards from the tower. Tents in military rows. A generator humming. Floodlights on poles, unlit against the daylight but ready for night work. Two heavy barges moored in the remaining shallows, fitted with winches and crane arms, and men moving across the flats in waders, driving stakes, stringing lines, marking out grids. Salvage gear — or dressed up as salvage gear. I knew navy equipment when I saw it, and this wasn't salvage. Salvage crews don't post sentries. Salvage crews don't run their lines in patterns designed to find paper and burn it.

And there, at the center of it, standing at a folding table with maps pinned under stones, a tall man in a civilian coat that fit him like a uniform anyway, gray at the temples, posture like a plumb line.

Commander Aldous Vane. Twenty years older than the file photographs, and recognizably the same man the way a knife is the same knife after sharpening.

"He came himself," Mara whispered. "He didn't send anyone. He came."

"Of course he did." I kept my voice flat. "You don't trust a job like this to hired hands. He buried these eleven men once with his own signature. He'll bury them again personally."

Below us, one of the barges swung its crane arm out over the water, and a diver went off the side in a splash that carried clean across the flats. Headed, I'd have bet my boat, for the church.

For the belfry.

For whatever Elias Wren had hidden there, and whatever else — whoever else — the water had kept all these years.

Mara's hand found my sleeve. Her knuckles were white. "Ansel. They'll find it first."

I watched the ripples spread from where the diver had gone under, spreading out and out until they lapped against the church tower itself, rocking the weathervane, ringing no sound at all from the bell that hung silent above them.

"No," I said, and heard my own voice come out steadier than any part of me felt. "They'll find it second. Tonight, when their lights are off and their sentries are bored — we go down before they do. I've been diving that water my whole life without knowing it. One more dive."

"And if the water's too cold? If the silt—"

"Mara." I turned and looked at her, mud to the knees, windburned, her father's map crushed in her fist like it was the man's hand itself. "Twenty years ago my brother went into that belfry for this. The least I owe him is to get there before the man who killed him does."

Below us, the hammering went on. Vane's men were building something at the edge of the tree line — I squinted, and my blood went to ice water.

Barrels. Rows of them. Fuel drums.

They weren't just here to search the valley.

They were here to erase it.
