## 15. The Ledger Below

The water inside the church was the color of old tea and twice as cold, and I stood at its edge for a long time before I let myself think of it as water at all.

It wasn't, really. Not anymore. The reservoir had dropped so far that what remained in the valley was less a lake than a wound — a shallow, silted remnant pooled in the lowest ground, held back by nothing but gravity and the drainage crew's schedule. Nine days, the hermit had said. In nine days the pumps would finish their work and the floor of Rivergate would stand naked under the sky for the first time in twenty years. Vane meant to be there first. So did we.

But first there was the church.

It rose out of the mud like something surfacing from a dream you'd tried hard to forget. The tower breached the waterline by fifteen feet, its shingles gone to black rot, its bell still hanging in the belfry — I could see it through the louvers, green with age, patient as a held breath. The nave below was flooded to a depth of maybe thirty feet, the windows long since shattered, the doors swollen shut or simply gone. The water inside was still. Still in the way that a room is still when it has been waiting.

Mara crouched beside me on the mudbank, wrapping my air line in careful coils. She hadn't said much since we'd crept down through the snowmelt past Vane's pickets — she'd counted them for me, six men, two fires, one lantern burning late in the command tent — but her hands were steady, and steady hands are worth more than speeches.

"You don't have to do this today," she said. "We could wait for dark."

"The ledger's been waiting twenty years," I said. "I've been waiting twenty years. Neither of us should have to wait for dark on top of that."

She almost smiled. "That's the worst logic I've ever heard from a man about to dive into a drowned church."

"It's not logic. It's spite. Spite keeps better than logic."

She checked my weights, my valves, the knife on my belt — a loaner, hers, because mine had been confiscated along with everything else when Reyes's patrol stopped us at Sorrel Fork before mercy went sideways and I fished one of them out of the river. Her knife was a good one, honed thin, the handle wrapped in leather she'd stitched herself. It sat against my hip like an argument I hoped not to need.

"Twenty minutes of air," she said. "If you're not up in twenty-five, I'm coming in after you, and I swim like a stone, so don't make me."

"Understood."

"And Ansel." She caught my wrist. Her eyes were gray as the water and just as deep. "Whatever's down there — whatever you find — you come back up. That's the promise I care about. Not Elias's. Mine."

I didn't trust my voice, so I nodded, and put the regulator in my mouth, and stepped off the edge into twenty years of cold.

* * *

The first thing the water took was sound. Then light, most of it — what came through the broken windows fell in pale slanted columns, thick with silt, turning slowly like things asleep. My lamp cut a cone through the murk and found pews first, drifted with sediment, soft-edged, dissolving. A hymnal lay open on one, its pages fused into a single gray block. Somewhere near the altar a carp had made itself a kingdom; it flicked away from my light with the indifference of a landlord.

I swam the center aisle.

I want to tell you I felt nothing. That I was a professional doing a job, measuring distances, checking my depth gauge, running the search pattern they teach you off Cape Verren: grid, sweep, mark, move. And some part of me was that professional, cold and competent, counting strokes. But another part of me knew — knew the way you know your own name — that this was the water. This was the church. Twenty years ago my brother had come down through water exactly like this, in a suit older than mine, carrying a promise from a dying prospector, looking for a ledger wedged somewhere above the flood line, and he had not come back up.

Tomas Delacroix, aged twenty-six, listed in the navy's files as a civilian contractor lost in a training accident. No wreck named. No site named. Just a line in a ledger of his own, the bureaucratic kind, the kind that erases men by filing them.

I had dived the waters off Cape Verren for twenty years telling myself he was out there somewhere, in the sealed zone, behind the navy's buoys, and that someday I'd find him. I had measured my life in miles traveled because miles could be crossed and years only accumulated, and I had never once said aloud what I actually believed: that Tomas had died here. In this valley. In this church. That the map Elias Wren had pressed into my hands in a dockside tavern was the same map — or its twin — that had sent my brother down into the dark.

The belfry stairs were gone. Of course they were. Twenty years of water finds every weakness in a structure and makes love to it until it comes apart. What remained was the stairwell shaft itself, open like a throat, rising through the tower toward the bell. I hovered at its base and looked up. My lamplight reached maybe twenty feet. Above that, darkness, and somewhere in it, if Elias's marginalia were honest — *the bell keeps the book, where the ringing can't reach* — the ledger.

I started up.

The shaft was narrow enough that I could brace shoulders and knees against opposite walls and climb hand over hand along the rotted stringers, kicking gently, letting buoyancy do the work the stairs no longer could. Silt bloomed around me with every touch, clouds of it, swallowing my light and giving it back amber. Fish scattered. Something small and skeletal drifted past my face — a bird, maybe, drowned on the wing the day the gates closed — and I pushed it aside and kept climbing.

Ten feet. Fifteen. The cold found the seams in my suit and began its patient work. I checked my gauge: nine minutes gone. Plenty. Plenty.

At the top of the shaft the tower opened into the belfry, and the bell hung above me like a drowned moon.

It was bigger than I'd expected, bronze gone green-black, suspended from a beam that had somehow refused the water's courtship. The rope was long gone. Around the bell's rim, where the clapper would swing, the silt had settled in a perfect collar, undisturbed. Nothing had touched this place. Nothing had rung this bell in twenty years, and the silence it had been keeping had settled over everything like snow.

*Where the ringing can't reach.* Elias had meant it as a joke, maybe, or a prayer. Hide the book where the sound of its own discovery can't follow it.

I swept my lamp around the belfry platform.

And there it was.

Wedged between the beam and the bell's mounting bracket, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, tied with wire that had rusted to lace — a bundle the size of a Bible. I knew before I touched it. You spend enough years handling salvage and you develop a sense for what things are before your hands confirm it, the way you know a body from a log before the light reaches it. The shape of it. The density. The way it sat in the water like it had grown there.

I hooked two fingers under the wire and pulled.

The oilcloth cracked. Inside, swollen and warped but whole, was a ledger. A real one — bound board covers, a cloth spine, pages of good rag paper that had survived where cheaper paper wouldn't have. Elias Wren had known his business. He'd wrapped it against the water, weighted it against the current, and hidden it where only a diver would ever look, and then he'd spent twenty years dying slowly in dockside taverns because he couldn't face coming back himself.

I held it against my chest and felt something in me crack along an old seam.

Because beside it — tucked into the same bracket, deliberate, placed — was a knife.

I knew that knife. I had bought that knife. Seventeen years old, flush with my first salvage pay, I'd walked into a chandler's off Harbor Row and bought two — matched pair, carbon steel, walnut handles, because Tomas and I did everything in pairs in those days and it had seemed right that our blades should match too. His had a notch cut in the left side of the grip, done drunk on his birthday, so he could tell them apart in the dark. He'd laughed and said, *Now yours is the pretty one and mine's the honest one.*

The notch was there. The walnut was bleached gray. The steel was filmed white with twenty years of mineral deposit, but it was sharp — someone had sharpened it last, recently, recently meaning twenty years ago, meaning Tomas had honed it before the dive, the way you do, the way you prepare, believing you'll come back up and need it again.

He had gotten here. He had found the ledger. He had put it in the bracket, or taken it down and rewrapped it, and he had set his knife beside it — set it down, deliberately, both hands needed for the bundle, or resting it while he worked the wire — and then something had happened. The cramp. The snag. The dark. Whatever took him had taken him in the seconds or minutes after he'd finished the job, after he'd done the thing he came to do, and he had died ten feet from success with his blade within arm's reach and his lungs full of this same tea-colored water.

Twenty years I had wondered what happened to him. Whether he'd panicked, whether he'd failed, whether he'd died badly in some meaningless corner of the sealed zone. And the answer was: he had died well. He had died having done it. The ledger was here because Tomas Delacroix had carried it here, and it had waited all this time for someone to finish the errand, and the someone was me, and I was holding proof in one hand and my brother's knife in the other and the water was very cold and very still and I was weeping into my mask like a child.

The tears mixed with the flood and vanished, which is the mercy of crying underwater: nobody sees it, not even you. You just feel your chest heave and the regulator chatter and the world go blurry at the edges, and you let it happen, because there is nowhere to hide and no reason to. Grief underwater has nowhere to go except through you.

I don't know how long I stayed there. Long enough that my gauge told me to stop being a fool. I tucked the ledger inside my suit, against my chest, where it rode like a second heartbeat. I slid Tomas's knife into my belt, next to Mara's, the honest one and the borrowed one. I took one last look at the bell — green, patient, keeping its twenty-year silence — and thought, *not much longer, old girl. Not much longer at all.*

Then I went down the throat of the stairwell, through the sleeping pews, past the carp's kingdom, and up into the light.

* * *

Mara had a fire going when I broke the surface, which tells you either how long I'd been down or how well she'd read me. She waded out to meet me, took the weight of my tank without being asked, and then saw my face and stopped.

"You found it."

I pulled the ledger out of my suit. Water had gotten to the edges despite everything; the cover was warped, the first few pages fused. But the bulk of it was legible. Names. Dates. Signatures. Payments authorized by a signature I'd seen redacted across a hundred harbor files: *A. Vane.*

Mara took it in both hands the way you take a newborn, or a live grenade, and turned to the first clean page. Her lips moved as she read. Then she looked up at me, and her eyes were wet, and furious, and certain.

"Eleven names," she said. "He wrote them all down. He signed for all of it."

"There's more," I said. My voice came out scraped. I drew Tomas's knife from my belt and held it out to her on my open palm, because some things you can't keep to yourself, and grief shared is weight halved, and I was tired of carrying it alone.

"My brother's," I said. "He got here first. Twenty years ago. He found the ledger, and he hid it right, and he died doing it." I swallowed. "Elias hired him. The hermit was right. My brother and your father were working the same job, and I've spent half my life diving over their grave without knowing it."

Mara looked at the knife for a long moment. Then she closed my fingers back over it, gently, the way you'd close a book you weren't ready to finish.

"Then it's yours to carry," she said. "Both of them. The proof and the blade." She glanced east, toward Vane's camp, where the lantern still burned in the command tent, and her jaw set in a way I was beginning to recognize as the prelude to trouble. "And we'd better decide fast what we're going to do with them. Because one of Vane's pickets just stood up, and he's looking this way."

Across the drowned valley, thin in the evening air, a whistle blew — twice, then once — and a lantern began to move along the ridgeline above the church.

They'd seen my bubbles.
