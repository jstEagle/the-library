## 16. Taken

The trouble with waiting is that it gives your hands nothing to do but shake.

I crouched in the lee of the drowned schoolhouse with the ledger wrapped in oilskin against my chest, and I told myself I was guarding it. That was the story I'd agreed to when Ansel laid out the plan on the ridge that morning — he dives, I watch, we trade. Simple as a fence gate. But the truth was the ledger was safer in my coat than I was, and we both knew it, and neither of us said it, because saying it would have made it true in the way that matters.

"Twenty minutes," he'd said. "If I'm not up in twenty minutes, you take it and go."

"And where exactly would I go?" I'd asked. "It's a bowl, Ansel. A bowl full of mud and navy men. There's nowhere to go but down or up, and up is where they are."

"Then go up anyway."

He'd smiled when he said it, that crooked diver's smile, the one that said he'd spent twenty years making peace with the water and had never once made peace with the land, and I hated him a little for smiling, and I loved him a little for it too, and I said nothing about either, because that's how it was with us. Half an answer, always half an answer. The other half lived in the water.

He went under at what I judged to be mid-morning, though the valley kept its own hours now, all of them gray. The bubbles rose from the church — the church, God, the whole tower of it standing in the shallows like a drowned man's finger pointing at heaven and finding it closed for renovations — and I settled in to wait, and I counted his bubbles the way I used to count my father's footsteps coming up the road, and I hated that too.

I'd gotten to four hundred and eleven when the world went wrong.

Not loudly. That's the thing about soldiers — the good ones don't announce themselves. One moment the schoolhouse was empty and the next there was a man standing in its doorway with a rifle slung casual as a mail sack, and then two more behind him, and the mud made no sound under their boots because they'd learned to walk the way I'd learned to shoe a horse: so the animal barely notices until it's done.

"Don't," the first one said, when I moved. Not unkindly. That was somehow worse.

I did the arithmetic fast, the way you do when there's nothing else left to count. Three of them, rifles, me with a ledger, a farrier's hammer I'd stopped carrying after Sorrel Fork, and a diver underwater who didn't know. The ledger was the whole point of me. I understood that the way you understand a nail's too long for the board — not with your head, with your hands.

"Looking for me?" I said. "You should've sent flowers."

The first one — young, sandy, the kind of face that hadn't decided yet what it believed — actually almost smiled. The one behind him didn't. The one behind him had the look of a man who'd been given a job and intended to do it the way a hammer intends to hit a nail, which is to say without philosophy.

"Ma'am," the hammer said, "you'll come with us."

"Will I." I stood up slow, keeping my coat closed with one hand like a woman modest about her figure instead of a woman hiding a nation's worst sin against its own people wrapped in oilskin. "And if I don't?"

"Then we'll carry you," he said, "and you'll be less comfortable, and we'll be less patient."

"You're already not patient. I can smell it on you. It smells like a man who's been standing in mud for three days waiting for two strangers to walk into a valley." I started walking toward them, because the only thing worse than being taken was being taken at the bottom of a scramble with my back to a wall. "Who's your commanding officer? Let me guess. Tall? Gray at the temples? Looks like a man who irons his face in the morning?"

The sandy one's eyes flickered. There it was. Vane.

"Walk," the hammer said.

I walked. And here is the thing I want written down somewhere, in some record that outlives us all: as they walked me up out of that bowl of drowned fences and rising rooftops, I did not look back at the church. I wanted to. Every step, I wanted to turn and look at those bubbles, count them, pray them — but a woman who looks back tells the men holding her rifle exactly where her heart is kept, and I had learned enough in one week on the high trail to know you don't show soldiers your heart. You show them your teeth.

So I looked forward, and I carried the ledger against my ribs, and I thought: not yet. Whatever they want, they don't know I have it. Not yet.

I was wrong about that within the hour, but I didn't know it yet, and ignorance, it turns out, is a kind of armor. Heavy, ill-fitting, but armor.

* * *

They kept me in a tent at the edge of Vane's camp — a real camp, not a bivouac, with generators and a mess line and crates stenciled with numbers that meant nothing and everything. The navy had come to the valley the way it always came to things: with paperwork and fuel and the quiet certainty that whatever was here belonged to it.

The sandy one brought me coffee. I'll give him that. He set it down on the crate they'd given me for a table and stood there holding his cap like a boy at a funeral.

"I'm Reyes," he said.

"I know. Ansel fished you out of the Sorrel Fork and gave you back your boots. He said you looked like a man who'd been lied to and was still deciding whether to be angry about it."

Something moved behind his eyes. "He said that?"

"He said you had an honest face, which from Ansel is roughly a marriage proposal."

Reyes almost laughed, then caught it and killed it, the way you kill a thing you've been ordered to kill. "Ma'am, I can't — there are things I'm not—"

"Allowed to say. I know how it works. My father spent twenty years not saying things. It killed him slower than a bullet and just as sure." I picked up the coffee. It was terrible. I drank it anyway, because my hands wanted a job. "So don't say things, Reyes. Just stand there and let me say them, and you can decide afterward which ones to report."

He glanced at the tent flap. The hammer was outside; I could see his shadow through the canvas, patient as a post.

"Commander Vane is going to want to talk to you," Reyes said.

"I assumed. He's had twenty years to rehearse. I hope I'm worth the rehearsal."

"Ma'am—"

"Mara. My name is Mara. If you're going to stand in a tent with me while men outside decide what to do with me, you can do it calling me by my name. It's the last courtesy anyone's going to offer me today, and I'd rather it came from you."

He looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him do the same arithmetic I'd done in the schoolhouse, the fast kind, the kind you do with your hands. Then he nodded, once, and went out, and I was alone with the coffee and the oilskin bundle under my coat and the growing, sick understanding that the bubbles at the church had stopped, or hadn't, or had — that I didn't know, that I wouldn't know, that Ansel was down there in the black cold water inside a drowned church where his brother died, and I was up here drinking terrible coffee in a navy tent, and the distance between those two facts was the whole width of my life.

I made myself stop. Terror, I have since learned, is a fire, and a fire in a stove will cook your supper and heat your house, and the same fire loose in a barn will burn everything you own. The trick — the whole trick, the trick I would spend the rest of my life learning — is to get it into the stove.

I looked around the tent. Crate, cot, lamp, my own boots' mud on the floor. And on the crate beside the coffee, where Reyes had set down his clipboard without thinking — because he was sandy and young and honest-faced and tired — a sheet of paper, half-covered in a neat military hand.

I read it upside down, which is a skill every farrier learns, because you spend your life reading invoices held by men who don't turn them around for you.

*Demolition inventory — structures, remaining. Incendiary allocation. Timing contingent on drainage completion. Disposal of "salvage debris." Attribution: looters. Press containment: in hand.*

Looters.

I read it three times. They were going to burn the valley. Not just the ledger — the church, the houses, the fences, whatever the water gave back, all of it, and then they were going to stand in front of whatever reporters Vane hadn't managed to smother and say: salvage looters, criminals, a fire of unknown origin, a tragedy of greed. They were going to take eleven dead people and make them the villains of their own massacre.

And the fire was already lit in my chest, and I didn't put it in the stove. I let it burn the barn. I let it burn everything. Because some things should burn.

* * *

Vane came at dusk.

I knew it was him before the tent flap moved, because the camp changed around him — voices dropping, boots straightening, the whole place pulling itself taut like a sheet on a bed. He came in alone, which surprised me. I'd expected the aide, the escort, the theater of it. Instead: one man, gray at the temples, with a face that had indeed been ironed, and eyes that did not so much look at you as take inventory of you.

"Miss Wren," he said.

"Commander Vane." I didn't get up. "You have me at a disadvantage. You know my name. I only know your reputation, which is apparently excellent at burying things."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not a smile. A measurement. "Your father was a difficult man," he said. "I imagine you're similar."

"My father was a coward for twenty years and brave for one week, and the brave week cost him everything, and you know it, because you're the reason." I stood then, because there are moments when sitting is surrender and I was not going to hand this man a single one for free. "He saw what you did at Rivergate. He carried the proof out of here in his teeth like a dog with a bone, and he ran until running killed him, and on his deathbed he gave the bone to strangers because he couldn't face his own daughter. That's what you did to my family. What did you think you were doing here?"

"Housekeeping," Vane said.

And I will say this for him: he didn't flinch. Most men, hit with twenty years of grief like that, flinch, or bluster, or reach for the nearest lie. Vane just looked at me the way a man looks at weather.

"The valley is being drained under emergency order," he went on, in the tone of a man reading a supply manifest. "When it's drained, it will be surveyed, cleared, and returned to productive use. What's left down there is debris. Some of it is dangerous. Some of it attracts — opportunists. Salvage looters. People who dig up tragedy and sell it by the pound." He stepped closer. He smelled of coffee and wool and nothing else, no fear, and that frightened me more than any smell could have. "You and Delacroix came down here for treasure. I understand. The water gives things back, and men can't resist picking through them. But what you're doing is theft from the dead, Miss Wren, and it's dangerous, and I'd hate to see you hurt."

"There it is," I said. "I was wondering how long you'd rehearse before you got to the part where the massacre becomes my fault."

His eyes narrowed — a fraction, a hair's breadth, but I'd spent ten years reading horses and twenty reading men, and I saw it. I'd landed somewhere.

"The ledger," he said quietly.

"Never heard of it."

"Delacroix is diving the church right now. My divers watched him go in. There is exactly one thing in that church worth diving for." He let that hang. "So either you have it, or he does, or it's still down there. And I have time, Miss Wren. I have generators, and fuel, and men, and time. You have a coat and a temper."

"And you have a piece of paper on a crate in a tent where a young man with an honest face left it lying upside down," I said, "talking about incendiary allocation and salvage looters."

The silence that followed was the longest of my life, and I have since had several longer ones, so I speak with some authority.

Vane did not shout. He did not call the guards. He looked at me for perhaps five seconds, and in those five seconds I watched a man decide, with the calm of an accountant, what I was worth and what I cost, and I understood — with a clarity that has never left me — that I was looking at the face of the thing that had killed eleven people in a valley and called it an accident. Not rage. Not evil, even, or not only. Arithmetic.

"Search her," he said to the tent flap, without raising his voice.

They took the ledger out of my coat with hands that were almost gentle, which I will resent until I die. The hammer held it up and Vane unwrapped the oilskin and looked at the ledger — my father's ledger, Elias Wren's ledger, twenty years in a belfry and twenty years in a coat and now in the hands of the man it accused — and his face did nothing at all.

"Names," he said, almost to himself. "Dates. Signatures." He closed it. "He was thorough. I'll grant him that."

"He was better than you," I said. "He was a coward, and he was still better than you, because he spent twenty years trying to fix what you spent twenty years burying."

Vane handed the ledger to the hammer. "Lock it with the demolition stores," he said, and I felt the floor of the world tilt, because I understood at once: the ledger was going into the fire. Tonight or tomorrow, with the church and the fences and the eleven graves, all of it one clean flame and one clean story.

"You'll be held for transport," he told me. "There's a magistrate in the low country who handles salvage theft. You'll be treated fairly."

"By men who burn evidence."

"By men who follow orders," Vane said, and for one strange moment he sounded tired, almost human, almost like a man who had been carrying something heavy for twenty years and had forgotten it was heavy because he'd never once set it down. "Everyone does, Miss Wren. That's the whole of it. That's always been the whole of it."

He went out. The lamp guttered. And I stood alone in a navy tent in a valley full of the dead, with no ledger, no diver, no horse, and no plan — and I found, to my genuine astonishment, that I was smiling.

Because he'd told me things. He'd told me where the ledger was going, and when. He'd told me the fire was scheduled, not improvised. He'd told me there was a magistrate and a transport and a timetable, and men on timetables, I had learned on the high trail, are men who can be made late.

And I had one asset left, one, the only one Vane hadn't inventoried: a sandy-haired soldier with an honest face and a debt to a diver who'd pulled him out of a freezing river and given him back his boots.

I sat down on the cot, and I put my terror in the stove, and I began to plan.

Somewhere out in the gray water, at the bottom of a drowned church, Ansel Delacroix was still counting his bubbles, and he thought I was watching them.

I had to get word to him before the water told him the truth: that the woman he'd trusted with half an answer was now the only answer there was.

I closed my eyes and listened to the camp — the generators, the boots, the hammer's shadow pacing outside the canvas — and I waited for the one sound I needed.

Footsteps. Young ones. Honest ones.

Reyes came back with supper at full dark, and I looked up at him, and I said, "Reyes. Sit down. I need to tell you what they're going to burn tomorrow — and then I need to tell you whose side you're on."
