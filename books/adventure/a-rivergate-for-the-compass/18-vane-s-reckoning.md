## 18. Vane's Reckoning

The bell was still ringing when the first lights came down the valley road.

I stood in the shadow of the church tower with Mara beside me, both of us soaked to the ribs, and watched those lights resolve into lanterns and headlamps and the yellow glare of a drainage crew's generator rig. Behind them, further up the switchbacks, came a second string of lights — slower, more orderly, moving with the particular unhurried authority of men who have never once had to hurry in their lives.

Federal investigators, Reyes had called them. He'd gotten word out two days ago, he said, through a chaplain at Fort Marrow who owed him. I'd had my doubts. I'd had doubts about everything since the moment I pulled that ledger out of the belfry, cold and swollen and heavier than any book has a right to be.

But here they came, all the same. Down into the valley that had been drowned for twenty years, walking on ground no living soul was supposed to walk on, drawn by a bell that hadn't rung since before the water rose.

"They heard it," Mara said. She wasn't whispering, exactly, but her voice had the hushed quality of someone standing in a cathedral. Which, I suppose, we were. "All the way up the valley. Sound carries over water."

"There's not much water left."

"Sound carries over graves, then." She looked at me. In the lantern light her face was hollowed out and fierce, and there was mud on her jaw from where she'd been knocked down during the escape, and I thought — not for the first time — that Elias Wren had made something remarkable and never known it. "It carried for twenty years, Ansel. It just needed somebody to pull the rope."

The bell had gone quiet now. My palms were still stinging from it. Somewhere below us, Vane's camp had erupted into noise — shouts, engine starts, the crackle of a radio being worked hard. They'd heard it too. They knew what it meant: witnesses, where there were supposed to be none.

"We should get down there," I said. "Before they come looking."

"You want to walk into their camp?"

"I want to be standing next to that ledger when the people who can do something about it arrive. If Vane gets his hands on it first—" I didn't finish. We both knew how that sentence ended. We'd seen what his men did to a bridge at Sorrel Fork just to close a door behind us.

Mara nodded once, and we started down through the ruins.

* * *

You don't really understand what drowning does to a place until you see it coming back.

We picked our way between rooftops that rose from the mud like the backs of sleeping animals. A fence line stood exposed, wire furred with silt, posts soft as cheese. There was a child's wagon half-buried near what must have been a garden, and Mara stopped for one second — one second only — and then kept walking, and I understood that she was doing arithmetic of her own, counting up all the lives that had been folded under this water while men in clean uniforms signed papers calling it progress.

Vane's camp sat on the flat above the old mill race. Three tents, two trucks, a portable generator humming like a hive. And men — eight of them, maybe nine, moving with the jumpy energy of soldiers who've been told one thing and are starting to suspect another.

They saw us coming and rifles came up, and I raised my hands and kept walking, because the one thing I'd learned in twenty years of diving is that panic sinks you faster than water ever will.

"Easy," I called. "We're the thieves, right? Come to turn ourselves in."

A lieutenant-type stepped forward, young, uncertain. "On the ground. Both of you. Now."

"Now seems unwise," I said. "Mud's freezing. And you're going to want us standing when your guests arrive."

"Guests?"

"The ones following your commander's lights down the mountain. The ones with federal credentials." I let my hands drop an inch, slow, theatrical. "Reyes will vouch for us. Where is Reyes?"

Something moved in the ranks. A face I knew — younger than the last time I'd seen it, which is a strange thing to say about a man you fished half-drowned out of a river four days ago, but shame ages people fast. Reyes pushed past the lieutenant, and the muzzle of a rifle tracked him as he did it, and nobody seemed to know whether that was allowed.

"You," he said to me. His voice cracked on it. "You're alive."

"Barely. It's a specialty of mine."

He looked at Mara. At me. At the ledger-shaped bulge in the oilskin bag slung across my chest. Then he turned to face the camp, squared his shoulders like a man stepping off a ledge, and said, loudly enough for everyone:

"They're not looters. Sir. They're witnesses. And those lights coming down the valley are investigators from the Department of Justice, and if we're holding them when those men arrive, every one of us goes down with Vane."

* * *

Commander Aldous Vane did not run.

I'll give him that. When he emerged from the command tent — silver at the temples, jaw like a hatchet, the same face I'd seen in a redacted photograph in a harbor records office, except older and harder around the eyes — he did not run, and he did not shout, and he did not do any of the things guilty men are supposed to do. He walked to the center of his camp as though it were a parade ground, and he looked at me the way a man looks at weather.

"Delacroix," he said.

"You know my name."

"I've known your name for twenty years." Something flickered behind the hatchet jaw. Not remorse. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man confirming an entry in a ledger of his own. "Your brother was a complication. You were supposed to stay in salt water where you belonged."

"Tomas wasn't a complication. He was a person. That's the difference between you and everyone in this valley, Commander. They were all persons."

"Sentiment," Vane said, "is not evidence."

"No," said a voice behind me. "But this is."

Mara had the oilskin bag open, and she held the ledger up in both hands — swollen, silt-stained, its cover warped into a shape like a wave frozen mid-break. Even ruined, even drowned, it was unmistakably a book of accounts. Names. Dates. Signatures. Twenty years underwater and still legible, because paper keeps faith longer than men do.

And that was when the investigators arrived.

There were four of them, plus a drainage engineer who took one look at the camp and started shouting about permits, and a woman with gray hair and a federal badge who introduced herself as Inspector Halloran and asked, in a voice like a court reporter's, whose authorization existed for an armed encampment inside an active emergency-drainage site.

Vane produced papers. Of course he did. Vane had always produced papers; that was his whole method — drown the truth in documentation until it stopped struggling. Halloran read them without expression and asked follow-up questions that had teeth in them, and I watched the commander's answers start arriving a half-beat late, and I thought: there it is. The water rising.

He felt it too. I could see him feel it.

And so he did the thing I'd been afraid of since the belfry. He turned to his men, and his voice went flat and parade-ground clear, and he said:

"Sergeant. Burn it."

* * *

For a second, nothing happened. The generator hummed. Snow ticked down out of the dark, fine and dry, the first flakes of the storm we'd outrun by hours instead of days. Somewhere in the trees above the camp, the ruined rooftops creaked as the temperature fell.

Then a corporal — not Reyes, a big man with a shaved head — took a step toward Mara and the ledger, and I moved without deciding to, putting myself between them, and Vane said, "That's an order, Sergeant," and the sergeant hesitated, and in that hesitation the whole night turned.

Because Reyes stepped forward too.

Not toward the ledger. Toward the sergeant. He planted himself in the big man's path, and he said, quietly, in a voice that carried anyway:

"Don't."

"Corporal, stand aside," Vane said.

"No, sir."

The word landed in the camp like a stone dropped down a well. No, sir. Two syllables, and twenty years of buried things came up with them.

Reyes faced the commander, and his hands were shaking, and his voice shook too, and he did not stop. "Sir, I read that book. I helped fish these two out of a river and I read pages of it by firelight before I gave it back, and I know what's in it. Eleven names. Eleven men and women who died in a fire you ruled accidental, on land you seized, and a diver hired to bring the proof up who never surfaced." His voice broke and he drove through it. "I was told they were thieves. They're not thieves. They're the children of the man who carried this ledger, and the brother of the man who died carrying it, and I am not going to burn it, sir. And neither," he said, turning to the squad, to the nine armed men standing in the snow with their orders curdling in their hands, "is anybody here. Look at them. Look at the badge. This isn't an order anymore. It's a confession with witnesses."

Nobody moved.

The sergeant with the shaved head looked at Reyes. He looked at Inspector Halloran, who had stopped asking questions and was watching with the stillness of a woman letting a trap finish closing. He looked at Vane.

And he set his rifle down in the mud. Deliberately. The way you'd set down something heavy you'd been carrying too long.

After that it went quickly, the way avalanches go quickly after the first crack. A second rifle down. A third. The lieutenant who'd ordered us onto the ground took off his cap, which I understood was some kind of surrender in soldier language. Within a minute the entire squad was standing unarmed in the falling snow, and Aldous Vane was alone in the middle of his own camp, holding orders that had evaporated into paper, surrounded by men who would not.

"Burn it," he said again, but quieter, and there was nothing behind the words anymore, no weight, no parade ground. Just an old man telling a story nobody believed.

Halloran stepped forward. "Commander Aldous Vane. You're detained pending investigation into the deaths of eleven residents of Rivergate township, the death of Thomas Delacroix, obstruction, tampering, and whatever else the United States Attorney decides to add before breakfast. Do you understand?"

Vane looked at her. Then at me. And for one strange moment his face did something almost human — almost puzzled — as though he genuinely could not find the flaw in his own arithmetic. As though twenty years of paperwork had convinced him that if a thing was filed correctly, it had never happened.

"The valley was worth more empty," he said. To no one. To the snow. "Eleven people. Do you know what the dam was worth? What the power was worth? They were going to be moved anyway. I just... accelerated the schedule."

"That's the confession," Mara said softly, to Halloran. "You got that, right? Everyone got that?"

Everyone got that.

They walked him out past the church tower, past the fence line and the buried wagon, up the valley road toward the lights waiting at the top. He went without resisting. At the edge of the camp he paused once and looked back at the water — the last black pool of it, shrinking in the cold, steaming faintly where the snow touched it — and whatever he saw there, he didn't share it.

The snows closed in behind him before the trucks reached the ridge. I watched the taillights shrink and vanish, and then the storm arrived in earnest, and the valley filled up with white, and the eleven names slept under fresh snow instead of standing water for the first time in twenty years.

* * *

Later — much later, in the command tent that Halloran's people had requisitioned, with coffee that tasted like boiled regret — Mara sat across from me with the ledger open between us, drying page by page over the stove, and neither of us said anything for a long time.

"He gave the order twice," she finally said. "Burn it. And nobody burned it."

"That's usually how it works," I said. "The whole machinery of a thing like this runs on people saying yes. Take away the yeses and it's just one angry old man in the snow."

"Reyes said yes for four days."

"And then he said no at exactly the right time. Timing's most of courage, Mara. The rest is just being too tired to keep lying."

She turned a page carefully, with tweezers borrowed from the drainage engineer's kit. Elias's handwriting marched down the margin — the same hand I'd carried across a mountain range, the same hand she'd grown up resenting. Eleven names. Dates. Payments. Initials that matched the initials on Vane's own requisitions.

"My father ran for twenty years carrying this," she said. "And I spent twenty years hating him for running."

"He wasn't running from you."

"I know." Her voice caught, and steadied. "I'm going to need a while to know it all the way down, though."

"Take the while. It's not going anywhere. Neither is he." I nodded at the ledger. "Neither are they."

Outside, the storm thickened. Somewhere up the valley, the drainage crew's pumps had gone quiet for the night, and in the quiet I could hear the river — the real river, the one they'd caged for twenty years — finding its old channel through the mud, running cold and free over ground that remembered it.

Mara looked up at me across the ledger, and there was something new in her face, tired and unguarded.

"The families," she said. "Somebody has to tell them. Somebody has to read the names."

"I know," I said. "I was thinking the same thing."

"Not you alone. Us." She closed the ledger gently, like tucking in a child. "My father started this. Your brother finished it. I think we're the ones left holding the map."

And I found that I was already nodding, already planning it — the letters, the journey back down out of the mountains, the doors we'd knock on — when Halloran's aide ducked into the tent with a message flimsy in his fist and an odd look on his face.

"Inspector says you'll want to see this," he said, handing it to Mara. "Came up with the morning supply run. It's addressed to both of you."

It was postmarked three weeks ago. Kestrel Crossing. And the handwriting on the envelope was Elias Wren's.
