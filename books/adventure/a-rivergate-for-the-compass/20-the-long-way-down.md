## 20. The Long Way Down

The valley drained slower than the engineers promised. Water has its own opinions about schedules, and the reservoir gave up Rivergate grudgingly, a foot a day, leaving tidelines of gray mud on the drowned fences like rings on a drunk's sleeve. By the fourth morning after Vane's arrest, the church tower stood clear to its belfry, and the rooftops of Main Street had surfaced like whales coming up to breathe.

I stood at the edge of what used to be the town square and tried to remember that I'd come here for justice. It was getting harder. Justice had been served, more or less, with paperwork and handcuffs and a young soldier named Reyes who'd found his spine somewhere in the white water at Sorrel Fork. What remained, now that the anger had burned down to embers, was something older and quieter and considerably heavier.

Tomas was still down there.

Not his body — twenty years of water sees to that, and I'd made my peace with the arithmetic of it long ago, or told myself I had. But his effects were. His knife, which I'd already brought up, wedged beside the ledger in the belfry like he'd known someone would come looking and wanted to leave a signpost. And whatever else a nineteen-year-old carries on his last dive: the brass compass Elias Wren paid him with, maybe. The coil of line. The little tin cross our mother gave him that he swore he'd lost years before but wore under his shirt anyway, because Tomas lied the way other people hummed — constantly, tunelessly, without malice.

"You don't have to do this," Mara said.

She was standing beside me with her arms crossed against the morning cold, breath steaming, watching the water lap at the church door. She'd said it three times now, each time a little differently, which is how Mara argued when she knew she'd already lost. First it was an observation. Then it was a plea. This last one was just her being honest about being scared for me.

"I know," I said.

"The water's freezing. The silt hasn't settled. Half those buildings are rotten through."

"I know."

"And you've already got what matters. The ledger's in a federal evidence bag. Reyes wrote everything down twice. There's nothing left down there but mud and memories."

"That's the point," I said.

She looked at me then, really looked, the way she did when she was deciding whether to hit me or hug me, and with Mara the margin between those options was always narrower than people assumed. "You're going down there to say goodbye," she said. Not a question.

"Something like that."

"Twenty years, Ansel. You could say goodbye from here. People do it from churches. From gravesides. From bars, mostly."

"I'm not people," I said, and immediately regretted how it sounded, because it sounded like pride, and it wasn't. It was the opposite of pride. It was the admission that I'd been carrying him wrong for two decades — carrying him like contraband, like a wound I kept picking at to prove it still hurt, instead of laying him down somewhere with honor. You don't smuggle your brother out of the water in pieces over twenty years. You go down once, properly, and you bring all of him home.

Mara studied me a moment longer. Then she shrugged out of her coat and started helping me check the gear, because that was her way of losing arguments gracefully — by making sure you survived winning them.

* * *

The old dive rig felt different on my back than it had two days ago, when I'd gone into the flooded church hunting proof. Then, every movement had been purpose, every stroke of the fins a kind of fury. Now there was nothing to find, nothing to prove, no clock running except the one in my own chest. Just cold green water and a brother at the bottom of it.

The reservoir had dropped enough that I didn't even need to descend far. The church roof had collapsed years ago — I'd seen it from below, a lattice of black beams like the ribs of some enormous dead animal — and the nave lay open to the sky now, or to whatever passed for sky forty feet down in water the color of old bottle glass. Light came through in shafts, bent and trembling, catching the silt I kicked up and turning it to gold smoke.

I went in through where the front door used to be.

Pews first, or what was left of them — soft gray shapes furred with sediment, dissolving slowly back into the chemistry of the lake. A hymnal so swollen with water it had become less a book than a brick, pages fused into a single pale block. I put my hand on one pew as I swam the center aisle, the way you'd trail your fingers along a banister in a house you grew up in, and the wood came away on my glove in soft rotted ribbons.

Twenty years ago, my brother had swum this same aisle. Down through the dark, following Elias Wren's map, hunting a ledger hidden in the belfry by men who'd trusted the water to keep their secret. He'd found it. That was the thing I kept returning to, the thing that broke me open every time I turned it over: he'd *found* it. Wedged it loose, tucked it against his chest, and then something happened — a beam shifted, a line snagged, his air ran short, the cold took him, the record would never say — and nineteen-year-old Tomas Delacroix died ten feet from the truth, holding it in his hands, unable to carry it up.

And then the navy sealed the wreck. Sealed the valley. Told me my brother died in a training accident off Cape Verren, showed me a form with a signature I'd spend half my life trying to trace, and let me grieve in the wrong direction for twenty years. Grief needs a grave the way a river needs banks. Without one it just spreads out and drowns everything.

Well. I had the grave now.

I reached the base of the bell tower and looked up. The belfry yawned above me, black mouth of it, the bell itself hanging silent on its rotted yoke — the same bell I'd rung two nights ago to bring the whole world down on Aldous Vane's head. Sound travels strangely underwater. When I'd pulled that rope, or rather the cable rigged to it, the tolling had come through the lake and through my own bones, and for one vertiginous moment I'd thought: *he can hear it. Twenty years down there and he can finally hear it.*

Now I rose through the tower's throat, slow, careful, my bubbles climbing past me like small silver souls getting a head start.

The belfry floor was knee-deep in silt and pigeon bones and the debris of two decades. I'd torn half of it apart looking for the ledger. What I wanted now was smaller. I worked methodically, the way Tomas taught me to work a site when we were boys diving the shallows off Cape Verren for lost fishing tackle and coins off the excursion boats — *slow hands, Ansel, fast eyes* — sweeping the silt aside in patient fans.

His line reel was there, aluminum gone to white corrosion, the handle seized solid. I took it. His mask sat in a corner of the frame, lens crazed, strap dissolved; I took that too. Under a fallen section of the yoke, half-buried, a canvas pouch that had been waterproof once and stubborn still: inside it, wrapped in oilcloth that Elias Wren must have provided, a brass compass with *T.D.* scratched into the case, and folded paper gone to pulp, and — my breath caught hard enough that my regulator chattered — a small tin cross on a chain, bright as the day our mother bought it off a peddler's cart, because Tomas had never lost it at all. He'd hidden it here. In the belfry. Before the dive. The way divers do, the way soldiers do, the way men do when they're about to do something dangerous and want to leave a piece of themselves safely above the waterline.

He'd planned to come back for it.

He'd planned to come back, and hand Elias his ledger, and collect his pay, and climb up to Kestrel Crossing or wherever the road went, and someday tell his little brother about the time he robbed a drowned church for a dead man's secrets. And the water had said no.

I knelt there in the silt of the belfry — knelt, though kneeling means nothing underwater, though there was no floor to kneel on that wasn't already the past — and I held the cross in my gloved palm and I said what I'd come to say.

It wasn't eloquent. Underwater, nobody's eloquent; the regulator eats your words and the water eats your silences. It was mostly his name. *Tomas.* Said over and over like a bell being struck softly, and then the things that follow a name when you finally let them: *I'm sorry I stopped looking. I'm sorry I believed them. I'm sorry I dove these waters for twenty years looking for you and never once thought to look here, in the one place you'd have gone, because they told me you died a hundred miles east and I believed them because believing them hurt less than doubting them.* And then, because grief shared is weight halved and grief spoken is weight quartered: *It wasn't your fault. Whatever happened down here, in the dark, with the ledger in your arms — it wasn't yours to carry, and I've been carrying it like it was mine, and we're both putting it down now.*

The light shifted above me. A shadow crossed the tower opening — Mara, leaning over the edge of the belfry platform they'd rigged, watching my bubbles rise. Even distorted by water she was unmistakable: the set of her shoulders, the way she leaned into things, weather and worry alike.

*I found somebody,* I told him. *You'd like her. She'd hate you for about a week and then defend you with a hammer.* 

I laughed underwater, which is a strange thing, all bubbles and no sound, and my mask fogged at the edges and I let it.

Then I gathered everything — the compass, the cross, the reel, the mask, the pulped papers that might have been Elias's instructions or might have been a letter Tomas never sent — and I clipped the pouch to my harness, and I took one last look around the belfry where my brother had spent the last minutes of his life in the company of a secret worth eleven lives.

The bell hung above me. I reached up, and with the flat of my glove, I touched it. Once. A single low note rolled out through the water, through the tower, through the ruined nave and the drowned street and the whole emerging valley, softer than the one I'd rung for Vane, meant for an audience of one.

*Come on up, Tommy,* I said. *We're going home.*

* * *

They talk about surfacing like it's nothing — a technical phase, decompression stops, equalize your ears, exhale on the way up. Nobody tells you that sometimes the last ten feet are the longest swim of your life, because the world you're rising into is not the world you descended from. You go down carrying a question and you come up carrying an answer, and answers weigh nothing and everything.

I broke the surface into gray morning light and cold clean air, and Mara's hands were on my harness before I'd gotten my mask clear, hauling me toward the ladder they'd lashed to the tower's flank.

"You were down long," she said. Her voice was steady but her knuckles weren't.

"I had farewells to make."

"And?"

I held up the pouch. Water streamed off the canvas. "All of him," I said. "Everything he left. It's coming home."

She looked at the pouch, and then at my face, and whatever she saw there made her stop asking questions, which for Mara was practically a religious experience. She helped me up onto the platform and sat me down against the bell housing, and I sat there dripping in the thin mountain sun while below us the valley went on giving itself up — a fence line here, a wagon there, the streets of a town that had been murdered and buried and was now, name by name, ledger line by ledger line, being called back into the light.

I expected to feel hollowed out. That's what I'd braced for — the empty aftermath, the way a salvage man feels when the hold comes up empty and the contract's dead. Instead I felt *lighter*. Not empty. Lighter. Like I'd been diving all these years with ballast I'd forgotten I was wearing, lead sewn into the lining of every day since Cape Verren, and someone had finally cut it loose, and the water was letting me go.

Twenty years I'd measured my life in miles. Miles from the wreck, miles from the records office, miles from Kestrel Crossing, miles up a frozen pass with a dying man's map and a stranger's daughter. Distance as anesthesia. As long as I was moving, I didn't have to arrive anywhere, especially not at the bottom of a flooded church.

But sitting there on the belfry platform with my brother's cross warm in my fist — warm, though it had been forty feet down in snowmelt five minutes before, and I choose to believe that was the sun and will hear no argument — I understood that the miles were over. Not the traveling. The *fleeing*. There's a difference, and it took me two decades and one drained reservoir to learn it.

"Tomas," I said aloud, testing the name in open air, saying it to the sky instead of the water for the first time in twenty years.

Below us, the river ran through the reopened valley, finding its old channel, running clear.

Mara sat down beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched, and said nothing at all, which was exactly right. Far down the valley road, a wagon was coming — federal men, probably, or the first of the families who'd received letters about the drainage, come to see the ground where their people had been. Word would travel now. It was already traveling. By tomorrow there'd be tents in the square, and names read aloud, and weeping, and the strange communal work of building a memorial out of mud and memory.

That was hers to help with. She'd told me as much last night, standing watch by the fire: *the eleven belong to everybody, Ansel. Somebody has to read the names.*

But the morning was ours yet, and the bell was quiet, and my brother was zipped into a canvas pouch against my chest instead of scattered across the bottom of a government lake.

"We should get down," Mara said eventually. "Before the road fills up."

"Yeah." I didn't move. "In a minute."

She let me have the minute. Then another.

And it was there, in that minute, watching the wagon grow larger on the valley road — a wagon, and behind it riders, and behind the riders a second wagon with women in it, heads bare against the cold — that I realized I recognized the lead driver. Gray now, heavyset, but I'd have known that profile anywhere, because I'd spent twenty years studying it in harbor photographs and navy yearbooks and the margins of redacted files.

Elias Wren had a brother. Nobody had ever mentioned it. And the man reining up below the church tower, looking up at the belfry with an expression I knew intimately because I'd been wearing it for two days straight, called out in a voice cracked with twenty years of the same question:

"Is it true? Are they reading the names?"
