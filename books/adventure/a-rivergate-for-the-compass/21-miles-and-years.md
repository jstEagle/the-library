## 21. Miles and Years

Spring came to Kestrel Crossing the way forgiveness comes — late, grudging, and smelling of mud.

I noticed it first in the forge. All winter I'd worked with the doors shut against the wind, hammering iron by lamplight, and now one morning I opened both doors wide just because I could, and the light came in across the floor like something returning from a long trip. The river ice broke up that same week with a sound like rifle fire, and the whole town turned out to watch it go, as if we'd all been holding our breath since October and only just remembered how to exhale.

Ansel was there on my doorstep when the last of the ice went through, carrying two cups of coffee and wearing the expression of a man who has rehearsed a speech and lost his nerve about it.

"You're up early," I said, taking the cup anyway.

"Couldn't sleep." He looked out at the water running brown and fast between the banks. "Kept thinking about the valley."

"Thinking or dreaming?"

"Both. Same thing lately." He drank. "The drainage crew's report came through. The river's running clear above the dam already. They say by midsummer you'll be able to see the old bridge foundations from the ridge."

I'd heard the same report and had done what I always did with news that mattered too much: said nothing and hammered something. But Ansel had learned to read my silences over the winter, God help him, and he waited me out with the patience of a man accustomed to waiting on weather.

"I filed the papers," I said finally. "On the claim."

"I know. I saw them posted at the land office."

"Elias's claim. His name's still on it, twenty years later. Nobody ever contested it because nobody ever wanted it." I turned the cup in my hands. "The clerk asked me if I understood that reopening a claim inside the former reservoir puts me in line for every survey, every hearing, every federal man with a clipboard who wants to walk the ground. I told him I'd been shot at for less."

"That's not strictly true," Ansel said. "Nobody shot at us. They burned a bridge and nearly drowned us, which is different in kind but not in effort."

"Wit. From the man who once described a whiteout as 'brisk.'"

"It *was* brisk. Brisk is a matter of degree."

He smiled when he said it, and I let him, because that was the arrangement we'd arrived at without ever discussing it: I supplied the fury, he supplied the dryness, and somewhere in the middle of it we had built something neither of us had planned on. Grief does that, I think, when two people carry compatible weights. You don't fall in love so much as discover you've been walking in step for miles.

* * *

The trial opened on a Tuesday in April, in the federal courthouse down in Verren, three hours' ride and a ferry crossing away. They put us up in a boarding house that smelled of cabbage and floor wax, and every morning we walked past the courthouse steps where the newspaper men had begun to gather like gulls over a fish gutting pile.

Vane's lawyers had tried everything in the months since the arrest. They'd argued the ledger was inadmissible — recovered illegally, they said, by trespassers. The judge had observed, with visible pleasure, that the ledger had been entered into evidence by federal investigators who found it sitting in plain view in a camp the defendants had no permit to occupy, and that if Commander Aldous Vane wished to complain about how evidence came to light, he was welcome to explain to the court why he'd been preparing to burn it. After that, the defense stopped talking about the ledger and started talking about jurisdiction, which is what guilty men talk about when the facts have left the room.

We sat in the gallery on the opening day. Reyes testified first — the young soldier whose boots we'd returned to him at Sorrel Fork, standing at the witness stand in his dress uniform with his jaw set like a man diving off a high rock. He gave it straight: the orders, the cover story, the night Vane ordered the church fired in the second week of the operation, twenty-two years ago. Eleven names. He said them all, slowly, and the courtroom went so quiet I could hear the clock over the door.

Elias Wren's name wasn't among the eleven. My father hadn't died at Rivergate. He'd died last autumn in a dockside tavern with a stranger's hand around his map, having spent two decades running from a thing he'd witnessed and never found the courage to face. I used to hate him for leaving. Sitting in that courtroom, listening to a soldier describe a fire I'd only known as a line in a redacted file, I found the hatred had gone soft and strange in me, more like sorrow than anything else. He'd carried the proof of a massacre in his pack for twenty years and never once opened it. I'd carried it four hundred miles in one winter and opened it in a snowbound cave by lamplight. I don't know which of us was braver. I suspect it was neither, and that courage is mostly a matter of what you can't afford to keep carrying.

The ledger itself was brought out on the third day. A clerk carried it to the bench in a canvas satchel like it might bite, and maybe it should have. Twenty-two years underwater, then wedged in a belfry above the flood line where Elias's money and Tomas Delacroix's life had bought it a hiding place. The pages were warped and stained the color of weak tea, but the ink held. Names. Dates. Payments authorized over Commander Vane's signature — payments to men who signed affidavits swearing the township's resistance was "agitator-driven," payments dated the week before the fire, payments dated the day after.

Beside me, Ansel didn't move through any of it. He sat with his hands flat on his knees and watched the ledger the way he watches water — measuring what's underneath.

Afterward, outside on the courthouse steps, a reporter asked me if I felt justice had been served. I told her justice hadn't been served yet; justice was going to take weeks, and there would be paperwork. She laughed and wrote it down. What I didn't tell her was that watching that ledger enter evidence had felt less like victory than like setting down a load so heavy you'd forgotten your own posture. I kept reaching for the weight of it for days afterward, the way a missing tooth keeps its own ache.

* * *

The claim hearing came in May, and this time the ground itself did the testifying.

They let us walk the valley floor before the surveyors finished their work — Mara Wren, claimant; Ansel Delacroix, listed on the filing as "consultant," a word that made him snort every time anyone said it aloud. The reservoir was down to pools and channels. The river ran through the middle of it, finding its old bed with an ease that shamed everyone involved, as if to say: *I always knew where I lived. It was you people who got confused.*

Drowned fences stood out of the mud in rows, black and skeletal. A wagon sat where it had been abandoned, its spokes fanned open like a pressed flower. And at the head of the valley, the church — our church now, I supposed, though the congregation was eleven graves and two living strangers — stood whole above the waterline, its tower streaked green with two decades of algae, its bell still hanging in the belfry where Ansel had rung it loud enough to bring the world down on Aldous Vane's head.

Ansel walked ahead of me toward it without a word, and I let him go alone. Some doors you knock on yourself.

I busied myself at the churchyard instead, where the survey crew had flagged eleven plots. The stones were readable again now that the water was off them — the moss would come back, but the carving held. I read every name twice. Then I knelt at the newest-appearing of them, a plot near the wall with no stone at all, only a depression in the ground that the hermit of Gray Pass had described to us in his quiet way, and I understood without being told that this was where they'd have put my father if he'd ever come home.

I talked to him there. Not a speech. I'm not built for speeches, and neither was he. I told him the claim was reopened under Wren. I told him the trial was going badly for Vane and would go worse. I told him I'd kept his note — the last one, the one he'd tucked into the map's fold, six words in a hand that shook: *Tell Mara the water rises anyway.* I'd thought for a year it was a riddle. Standing in the drained valley with the river running clear through the middle of it, I finally heard it plain. It wasn't a confession. It was permission. *Whatever they buried, girl, it comes back up. Don't be afraid of it.*

I buried the map at the church door that afternoon, folded small, wrapped in oilcloth, under a flat stone I set myself. I kept the note. Some things are for the record and some things are for the pocket, and a person learns the difference or spends her life losing both.

* * *

Ansel came back down from the tower as the light was going long and gold, and I saw the change in him before he said a word. He moved differently. For as long as I'd known him he'd walked like a man pacing a cell — all that restless measuring, miles instead of years, distance instead of time. Cape Verren to Kestrel Crossing, forty-one miles. Kestrel Crossing to Gray Pass, sixty. The wreck site to the church bell, ninety feet down. Everything in his life had been a measurement of how far he was from something.

But coming down those church steps he walked like a man in a place he intended to stay a while.

"There's a dive shop in Verren," he said, sitting beside me on the churchyard wall. "Fellow named Okafor runs it. He wrote to me in March. Wants a partner for the summer season — salvage work, honest wrecks, nothing sealed, nothing navy." He turned his hat in his hands. "Told him I'd think about it."

"And?"

"And I told him no."

I looked at him. "You loved that work."

"I loved hiding in it." He said it easily, without flinching, which is how I knew how long he'd been practicing. "Twenty years I went down into cold water because down there was the last place I'd been close to Tomas, and if I stayed close enough to the drowning, I never had to get on with the living. Miles were easier than days. Miles you can count without feeling them." He looked out at the river running through its old bed, catching the last of the sun. "Days are harder. Days you have to actually be in."

"What will you do instead?"

"Dunno. Something with my hands. Something above sea level." A pause, and then, in the tone of a man testing ice before putting weight on it: "Okafor also mentioned the Verren harbor board wants a diver to survey the new breakwater. Steady work. Local work." He glanced at me sidelong. "Kestrel Crossing is forty-one miles from Verren. I know because I counted, back when counting was the only thing I knew how to do."

"Forty-one," I agreed. "Give or take."

"Give or take," he said, and the grin that broke across his face then was the first wholly unguarded one I'd ever seen on him, and I thought: *there he is. Twenty years underwater and here he is.*

* * *

We rode back up out of the valley the next morning, and I confess I looked back exactly once — at the church standing in the morning haze with the river bright below it, the bell silent in the tower, the eleven names resting easy under their flags. The water had risen and the water had fallen and everything true had come up with the mud, just as my father promised. There was no sentiment in it that I could find. Only a kind of arithmetic, the plain kind: things buried come back, things spoken hold, things mended hold better.

At the top of the ridge we stopped, because stopping at the top of ridges is a habit the winter gave us and neither of us saw reason to break it. Below us the road ran down toward Kestrel Crossing, toward the forge with its doors thrown open, toward a summer with a trial to finish and a claim to work and a breakwater to survey and no passes closing anywhere on the calendar.

"Forty-one miles," Ansel said again, settling his hat.

"You'll wear a groove in the road."

"Probably." He gathered the reins. "Worth it."

And I turned my horse down toward home, counting — not miles, not anymore, not either of us — but days. Good ones, ahead of us, more than either of us had ever thought to want.

The river ran clear all the way down.
