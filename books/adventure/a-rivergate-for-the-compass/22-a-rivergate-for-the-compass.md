## 22. A Rivergate for the Compass

**Ansel**

I have spent most of my life measuring distance the way a drowning man measures air — in what it costs, in how much is left. Miles were never miles to me. They were fathoms turned sideways, each one something to be survived rather than crossed.

So it took me three days out of Rivergate to understand that I was traveling wrong.

We'd stopped at noon on a rise above the Sorrel Fork — the same river we'd forded at gunpoint in the fall, now running shallow and bright over its stones like it had no memory of trying to kill us — and I caught myself doing arithmetic. How far to the pass. How long before dark. How many hours of daylight left in the legs of these horses. The old habit, the ledger-keeping of a man who has always been going somewhere because stopping meant thinking.

Mara watched me do it. She has a way of watching that feels like being audited.

"You're counting again," she said.

"I'm estimating."

"You're counting." She handed me a strip of dried apple she'd been chewing on, which is either generosity or an insult depending on your mood. "The trial doesn't start for six weeks. Nobody's chasing us. There's no snow coming for a month if it comes at all. What exactly are you racing?"

I opened my mouth to say *nothing*, which would have been a lie, and then closed it again, because lying to Mara Wren is like lying to a farrier about a lame horse. She'll find it, and she'll make you look at it.

"I don't know how to travel any other way," I admitted.

She considered this with the seriousness she gives everything, which took some getting used to after twenty years of men who consider nothing. Then she said, "Well. We've got time to learn."

And that was the whole conversation. That's the thing about her — she doesn't decorate. But I sat there chewing terrible dried apple above a river I'd nearly died in, and something in my chest unclenched by a degree I didn't have a name for.

* * *

**Mara**

My father spent twenty years drawing maps of places he was running from, and I spent twenty years hating him for it, and now here I am, following his map for pleasure.

There's a joke in there somewhere. Ansel says I'll find it eventually; I say Ansel finds jokes where other people find weather.

The map itself has changed since winter. Not the paper — though it's softer now, creased along its folds until they're more suggestion than line, and one corner carries a brown stain I refuse to investigate too closely. What changed is what it means. In Kestrel Crossing it was a summons. On the high trail it was a weapon, or a shield, or a reason to keep breathing when the wind came off Gray Pass hard enough to peel paint. At Rivergate it was evidence, and then it was grief, and then it was buried at the church door under a cairn of river stones with eleven names read over it while the water fell away from the valley floor like a bandage coming off a wound.

What it is now is just lines. Ink on calfskin. A dead man's handwriting describing mountains that don't care.

And somehow that's the version I love best.

We followed it south out of the valley in April, not because the map said to but because it went that way, and neither of us had anywhere better to be, and saying that out loud felt so strange that we both had to laugh about it — the ugly, startled laughter of people discovering freedom is heavier than it looks. Ansel laughed like a man setting down luggage he'd forgotten he was carrying. I laughed because he looked ridiculous doing it.

The river ran clear the whole way down the reopened valley. Clear the way water runs when it hasn't been sitting still behind a dam for twenty years — quick, cold, opinionated. You could see every stone on the bottom. You could see the trout holding in the current, facing upstream, doing exactly what they were built to do.

I thought about my father then. Not the bitter version, the one I'd built out of abandonment and silence, but the man who must have stood beside this same water as a young surveyor, before the incident, before the fire, before the eleven. He'd drawn this valley when it was alive. Every map is a portrait of a place the cartographer loved enough to measure.

He just never learned you could come back.

* * *

**Ansel**

In May we reached the coast, and I did something I hadn't done since the navy sealed the wreck off Cape Verren: I hired a boat.

Not a salvage boat. No dive gear, no lift bags, no manifest of things to be pulled up and sold. Just a fisherman named Odo who charged us too much and talked too little, and a morning with no agenda except that Mara wanted to see the water where I'd worked, and I wanted — I don't know. To look at it without wanting anything out of it. To stand on a deck over two hundred feet of cold green dark and feel nothing but the deck.

For twenty years that stretch of ocean had been a wound I kept probing. Tomas went into it and didn't come out, and I went into it after him, season after season, telling myself I was working when really I was searching — diving the sealed wreck, diving the neighboring shoals, diving anything that might hold an answer, because an answer was the only thing I could bring home to a grave that had none.

Now I knew the answer. He died carrying Elias Wren's promise into the flooded valley, same as I almost did, same as I finally did. He died for eleven strangers and a ledger, and nobody told me, and the not-knowing had been its own kind of drowning.

Odo cut the engine a mile off the cape and rolled us gently in the swell. Below us, somewhere down in all that blue, lay the wreck with its navy seals rusting off the hatches. Federal investigators had been through it in March. They'd found what Vane wanted erased and what Tomas had died near: shipping manifests, correspondence, the machinery of a cover-up preserved in cold water like fruit in a jar. It would all be read aloud in a courtroom in the capital while I sat in the gallery next to a farrier who'd decided my business was her business.

"Is it strange?" Mara asked. She was leaning on the rail, hair whipping, watching the water with the expression she wears when she's deciding whether to shoe a horse or shoot it. "Being back here. Above it."

I thought about it honestly, which is another thing she's taught me — honesty as a reflex instead of a ration.

"It's quiet," I said. "First time it's ever been quiet."

Because that was true. The water wasn't calling me anymore. It wasn't asking for anything. My brother was down there, and I'd be back in June with a proper crew and a permit and a chaplain if he wanted one, to bring him up the right way, slow and respectful, the way you bring anyone home. But that was work with a shape and an end. This — this rolling, sunlit nothing — this was just the sea.

"Good," Mara said. And then, because she can't leave a thing alone: "You're smiling. It's unsettling. Do it more."

* * *

**Mara**

Here is what nobody tells you about arriving: there's no moment. You spend years — or in our case, one brutal winter — aimed at a single point on the horizon, and you tell yourself that when you reach it, everything will resolve. The bell will ring. The names will be read. The truth will surface, because memory, like water, always rises.

And then you get there, and the bell does ring, and the names are read, and Vane goes away in irons with snow closing behind him like a door, and the world does not resolve. It just continues. The valley keeps draining. The grass comes up through the mud, greedy and green. People rebuild fences on foundations their grandparents poured, and children swim in a reservoir that isn't there anymore, and somebody opens a tavern in the old Wren general store, and life pours into the wound like water finding level.

Arriving isn't a moment. It's a decision you make every morning.

I made it again today, standing in the shallows of the Rivergate channel while Ansel swam — actually swam, for no purpose, splashing like a boy — and I realized I'd made it without noticing. Somewhere between the cave at Cold Camp and the bell tower and the cairn at the church door, I had stopped traveling toward something and started traveling *with* someone, and those are not the same journey, and only one of them has an end.

My father's last note to me — the one I kept when I buried the map — says: *If you're reading this, you found the valley. Don't stay in it. Valleys are for rivers.*

I used to think that was just him being clever. Now I think it was the whole testament. Everything else — the map, the ledger, the twenty years of running — was him failing to follow his own advice. Rivers don't stay. That's the entire nature of them. He knew it and couldn't live it, and maybe that's the saddest sentence I know.

But I'm not him. And the man currently pretending to drown in four feet of clear water so I'll come in after him is not a valley either.

* * *

**Ansel**

June found us back at Rivergate for the raising of Tomas.

I won't write much about that day, because some things are for the water and the few people who stood at its edge, and not for any page. I'll say only this: the Navy sent an honor detail, which would have been an insult in October and was something close to grace in June, and Commander Reyes — no longer commander, actually, having declined the promotion with a letter everyone in the capital has read — stood at attention in the mud in his dress boots and saluted a salvage diver's flag for the better part of an hour.

Tomas came up slow, in the sling, wrapped in canvas, and the church bell rang while it happened. They'd rehung it in May — the original bell, raised from the tower, cleaned of twenty years of silt, mounted on a timber frame at the head of the valley. It doesn't ring often. Only for the dead, and for the anniversary, and apparently for brothers coming home.

Mara held my hand through all of it. She'd deny the specifics if asked. I've stopped needing specifics.

Afterward, walking down from the church through the new grass, she said, "Your brother carried a promise across this valley and died of it. You carried one over the mountains and lived. What do you carry now?"

I genuinely didn't know. I told her so.

"Good," she said. "Empty hands are underrated."

Then she threw a river stone at my head, which is how she says most things.

* * *

**Mara**

We left Rivergate the second week of July, and this time nobody burned a bridge behind us.

The plan — if you can call it that, and Ansel insists we can't, and honestly the absence of a plan is the entire luxury of the thing — is to follow the map backward. South and east, the way my father came north thirty years ago, before any of it. He marked springs on it. Forges, actually — old mountain smithies, long cold, that he'd noted in his surveyor's hand with little annotations like *good stone* and *water year-round*. A farrier reads a map differently than a diver does. Ansel looks at it and sees depths; I see iron and hearth-ash.

There's a forge two valleys over, the map says, abandoned since the flooding, that sits above the waterline and might have kept its chimney. If it did, I'm thinking about reopening it. A town is growing back down in that valley — real people, real horses, real work — and every settlement needs someone who can keep them shod. Ansel claims he knows boats and could run freight on the reopened river channel, which is either a business plan or an excuse to stay near water, and I've stopped asking which.

But that's later. First, the journey. First, the two of us and two horses and a soft old map, going slowly on purpose, which may be the strangest sentence either of us has ever spoken.

Last night we camped on a shoulder of the pass where you can see all the way back to the valley — a thin silver thread of river, the green scar of the old lakebed, the church bell tower small as a thumbprint. Ansel sat looking at it for a long time.

"I used to count miles," he said. "Every road was a number. Every number was a debt."

"And now?"

"Now I count days." He turned and looked at me with that crooked half-smile that took him all winter to learn. "And I want more of them."

Well. What do you say to that?

I said, "Then stop talking and help me with the tent," because I'm my father's daughter and sentiment makes me itch. But I said it while taking his hand, and he understood, because he understands now. That's the thing we built in all those cold camps and burning bridges and drowned valleys — not a grand passion, nothing the balladeers would bother with, but something rarer: two people who learned each other's silences well enough to live inside them.

Tomorrow we cross the pass. Tomorrow the map takes us somewhere my father only ever drew, never dared. The river runs clear behind us all the way down to the sea, and ahead of us the trail climbs into country neither of us has measured, and for the first time in either of our lives, there is nothing chasing us and nothing waiting and nothing owed.

Just miles. Just days. Just the compass in the hand and the river for a guide.

It's enough. It's more than enough. It's a beginning, and beginnings, unlike endings, don't need to be survived — only walked into, one clear mile at a time.
