## 1. The Debt in the Dust

The thief left footprints like an apology — small, neat, and gone before you could accept them.

I crouched over the third one that morning, out where the flats outside Coriander Wells went pale as old bone, and pressed two fingers into the sand beside it. Still damp at the edges. Ten minutes, maybe twelve. Nobody else was fool enough to be out here before the sun cleared the mesa, which meant the fool I wanted was still ahead of me, and still winning.

Two years. That's how long Wren Calloway had been running from me, and the honest arithmetic of it was this: she'd stolen a strongbox of assay scrip from the Consortium pay office, vanished with it clean as smoke off glass, and every time since then that I got close enough to smell her campfire, she was already three ridges gone. She never took more than she needed. She never hurt anyone. She once left a tin of peaches on my saddle horn with a note that said *You look tired* — which is either mockery or mercy, depending on how long you've been eating jerky in the flats, and I've been eating jerky in the flats for a very long time.

I followed the prints east, then north, then east again, and somewhere around the fourth mile they simply stopped being there. Not faded. Not blown over. *Stopped*, in the middle of hardpan, as if she'd been lifted up by a considerate god or had finally learned to walk without touching the earth. The wind came off the mesa carrying dust and the faint mineral stink of the Redglass Range far to the north, and I stood in it feeling the particular exhaustion of a woman who has chased a ghost so long she's started to leave offerings.

"Damn you, Calloway," I said, to nobody, because that's who was listening.

I watered my horse, Dunny, from my own canteen because the seep I'd counted on was dry — everything out here was dry lately, the whole country holding its breath for snow — and turned back toward Coriander Wells with nothing but heat and failure in my saddlebags.

* * *

Coriander Wells in late afternoon looks like what it is: a company town pretending otherwise. The Consortium built it, owns it, salts its water table with tailings and its streets with the kind of men who tip their hats to you and count your teeth while they do it. The assay office squats in the middle of it all like a fat spider, and above the assay office, in a room kept cooler than any church, sits Superintendent Harrow.

I tied Dunny at the rail, climbed the stairs, and let the clerk announce me, because Harrow liked his announcements. Liked his rituals. Liked, most of all, the fact that everyone who entered his office entered owing him something, and he kept a ledger of it — literally, an actual ledger, bound in green leather, that sat on his desk like a sleeping animal.

"Felix." He didn't look up when I came in. He was writing. He always wrote when I arrived; it was a way of telling me my time was worth less than his ink. "The Calloway matter."

"Gone again," I said. "Clean trail to the hardpan, then nothing."

"Nothing," he repeated, and set down his pen, and looked at me with those pale eyes that never seemed to blink so much as *file*. "Do you know what I admire about you, Underhill?"

"No."

"Persistence. It's rarer than talent and cheaper than loyalty." He opened the green ledger, turned pages with one dry finger, and stopped. I knew the page without looking. I'd seen it often enough that I could draw it from memory: a column of figures, a date eleven years old, and beneath it, in Harrow's own hand, the words *Underhill, T. — plot 44, Everlasting Rest, balance outstanding.*

My father's grave. Plot forty-four. The Consortium owned the cemetery at Coriander Wells the way it owned everything else — the water, the road, the air you'd swear was free until you tried to breathe it without permission — and when my father died, the ground he went into cost more than I'd made in a year of ranging. So I signed. A debt of service against a debt of coin, and Harrow had been holding the paper ever since, turning it over in conversation whenever he wanted something done that wasn't strictly legal and wasn't quite illegal either.

"He's still out there," Harrow said now, tapping the page. "Your father. Under consortium stone. And every month that Calloway stays loose, that balance stays open." He closed the book gently, the way you'd close a door on a sickroom. "How is your mother's cough, by the way? The winters here are hard on her."

It wasn't a question. It never was. It was a hand laid flat on the table between us, palm down, patient.

"She manages," I said.

"I'm sure she does. Strong women, your family." He leaned back. "Bring me Calloway, Felix. Bring me the box, or bring me her, I'm not particular about which, though I'll confess the box interests me more. There are men above me who ask about it monthly, and men above them who ask weekly, and I dislike answering questions I can't close."

"The box was assay scrip," I said. "Paper. Why does anyone care about paper after two years?"

For just a moment — half a heartbeat, no longer — something moved behind Harrow's eyes, some calculation shifting its weight, and then it was gone, filed away wherever he kept the things he didn't say.

"Because it's ours," he said. "That's reason enough in this territory. And because a debt unpaid teaches the wrong lesson to the other debtors." He smiled, and it was a good smile, well-made, the kind they must have taught him somewhere expensive. "You'll want to get ahead of the weather. First snows come early to the high country this year, or so the barometers say."

"I know the weather, Superintendent."

"I know you do. That's why I keep you." He picked his pen back up. "Send the clerk in on your way out. And Felix — the flats are big, but not big enough. Nothing out there is big enough, eventually."

I walked out past the clerk, down the stairs, into the street where the lamplighter was working his way along with his pole and his little flame, giving light to a town that hadn't earned it. My hands were steady. They're always steady; it's the rest of me that shakes, later, alone, doing the arithmetic I do every time I leave that office: years served against balance owed, months remaining against mother's cough, my life on one side of the column and my father's grave on the other, and the sums never coming out even.

Dunny lipped at my sleeve as I untied him. "Don't start," I told him. "You get fed regardless. You're the only one of us with no debts."

We rode out to the ranger cabin at the edge of town, where I lit the stove and ate beans and thought about a tin of peaches sitting on a saddlehorn two summers ago. *You look tired.* I'd laughed, then. Alone, in the dark, laughing at a thief's handwriting — because it was funny, and because nobody else was going to be kind to me that day, and a ghost had bothered to try.

Now the laugh wouldn't come. Now I just sat with the ledger page drawn behind my eyes, plot forty-four, balance outstanding, and wondered — the way you wonder about a tooth that doesn't quite hurt yet — why a superintendent who didn't blink cared more about a box of paper than a woman who'd taken it.

The knock came near midnight. Three raps, official spacing. I opened the door on one of Harrow's clerks, hat in hand, breath steaming, a telegram flimsy crushed in his fist.

"Superintendent's compliments," he said. "He needs you first thing. There's been—" he glanced over his shoulder at the dark, lowered his voice as though the night might file a report— "there's been a disappearance. Government survey team. Out past the Redglass fork. Whole camp gone, Mr. Underhill says, and not a track leaving it."

I took the flimsy. Read it twice. Six names, one canyon camp, instruments found running, meals found half-eaten, and a line at the bottom in Harrow's clipped hand: *Find them before the passes close. Everything depends on it.*

Everything, I thought. Everything always did, according to somebody, and somehow it was always me carrying it.

"Tell him I'll come at dawn," I said, and shut the door, and stood a long moment in the cold with the paper in my hand, feeling the first faint pull of a current I didn't yet know had my name on it.
