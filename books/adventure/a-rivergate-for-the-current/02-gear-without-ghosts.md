## 2. Gear Without Ghosts

The message came up from Coriander Wells on a Tuesday, which was already suspicious. Nothing official ever happened on a Tuesday. Official things waited for Mondays, when the clerks were fresh and the whiskey hadn't yet worked its way into the week's arithmetic.

I read it twice by lamplight in my cabin at the edge of the flats, while the wind did its usual work of trying to sand the roof off my head. *Survey team overdue. Canyon camp, Redglass Fork. Investigate. Report to Superintendent Harrow.* Signed with the Consortium's stamp, which meant it cost them nothing but ink and would likely cost me considerably more than that.

Six days, the wire said. Six days since anyone had heard from the government survey crew — five men and a woman, sent out from the territorial office to map the high country ahead of the mining concession filings. They had provisions for a month. They had a heliograph station on the ridge above their camp. They had, according to every record Harrow's office could produce, no reason whatsoever to go missing.

Which is exactly why I didn't believe a word of it before I'd even saddled up.

* * *

You learn things as a desert ranger that they don't put in the manuals. You learn that people vanish for three reasons: they're dead, they're lost, or they want to be gone. And you learn to tell the difference from a long way off, because the land keeps honest books even when people don't.

The Redglass Fork canyon ran narrow and red-walled, the kind of place where the rock holds the day's heat like a stove and gives it back grudgingly after sundown. I rode in at midmorning with my mule Biscuit complaining about the altitude of his opinions, and I knew something was wrong before I saw the first tent.

Wrong how? Wrong quiet. A working camp has a sound under its sound — canvas snapping, tin rattling, somebody swearing at a transit. This canyon had nothing but wind and my own hoofbeats coming back at me off the walls like an accusation.

Then the camp opened up around a bend, and I stopped Biscuit and just looked at it for a long time.

Five tents, pitched neat as church pews. The cook fire had been banked, not abandoned — someone had raked the coals over proper, the way you do when you mean to come back in an hour. The mess tarp was strung between two junipers. On it: plates. Half-eaten plates. Beans gone to leather, bread gone to stone, coffee dregs dried black in the cups. Nobody walks away from breakfast that isn't finished unless they walk away fast, or they never walked away at all.

And the instruments — this is the part that sat wrong in me then and sits wrong in me now. The theodolite was still set up on its tripod at the edge of the bench, lens cap off, leveled, aimed at the far wall of the canyon. Still running, if a machine can be said to run. Beside it, weighted under a stone against the wind, lay the field notebook, open to the day's page. The last entry was barometric readings, taken at 7:40 in the morning, six days ago. The handwriting was steady. No hurry in it at all.

I dismounted and walked the camp the way I'd been taught to walk anything: slow, hands loose, eyes doing the work. I found their gear. All of it. Bedrolls rolled. Rifles racked in the arms tent, all six accounted for, oiled and loaded. Boots by tent doors. One man's spare spectacles folded on his cot. Packs hung from the mess line, full, dry, untouched by weather.

Nobody leaves their boots. Nobody leaves their rifle in this country, where leaving your rifle is a polite way of writing your own obituary. And nobody — nobody — leaves their glasses, unless they expect to be back before dark or they've left in a state of mind where seeing clearly is no longer the point.

I stood in the middle of that tidy, impossible camp and felt the hair on my forearms lift, and not from the cold.

Because here's what the land told me, and the land doesn't lie: there were no tracks leaving.

Oh, there were tracks aplenty going about the business of the camp — boot prints crisscrossing between tents and cookfire and instrument station, six sets, worn into the hardpan by days of routine. But the canyon walls rose sheer on both sides, four hundred feet of red rock a lizard couldn't climb, and the only way out was down-canyon, the way I'd come, or up the talus fan at the head of the draw toward the high passes. And both of those ways were clean. Not scuffed-over clean. *Clean* clean. No prints newer than five days old anywhere except mine and Biscuit's.

Wind could do some of that, I'll grant. Wind in the Fork could scour hardpan in a night. But wind doesn't lift six grown people over four-hundred-foot walls, and wind doesn't bank a cookfire, and wind doesn't fold a man's spectacles.

I climbed the talus anyway, all the way to where it funneled into the pass approach, checking the gravel, the scree, the edges of the snow patches that lingered in the shadowed couloirs even this early in the season. Nothing. If six people had gone up that fan, they'd gone up light as ghosts, and ghosts don't carry bedrolls.

On the way back down I passed the heliograph station on the ridge spur — mirror still mounted, signal flags coiled, logbook showing routine transmissions right up until six days ago. Last entry: *All well. Weather holding. Expect resupply Thursday.*

All well. That was the phrase that kept turning over in me like a stone in a current. Somebody had written *all well* and then, apparently, ceased to exist.

* * *

Harrow received me in his office above the assay house, behind a desk so orderly it looked like it had been laid out for a funeral. He was a small, precise man with gray in his beard and ledgers in his eyes — the kind of man who believed everything in the world could be entered in a column and balanced, and who grew genuinely offended when it wouldn't.

"You found the camp," he said. It wasn't a question. His runners had already carried word ahead of me; news traveled faster than I did, which told me whose money paid the horses.

"I found the camp."

"And?"

"And it's the strangest thing I've seen in eleven years of ranging." I laid it out for him — the banked fire, the half-eaten meals, the racked rifles, the missing boots, the clean exits, the *all well*. I watched his face while I talked, and here's what I saw: not surprise. Concern, yes, properly arranged, but underneath it something quicker, something that flickered when I said *no tracks*, like a man watching a card he'd hoped was buried turn up in the deck.

"Desertion," he said at last. "Government men walking off their contract. It happens."

"With their boots?"

"People do foolish things, Underhill."

"Not six of them at once, leaving their rifles, in grizzly country, in October." I leaned forward. "Superintendent, those people didn't leave that camp. Or rather — they left it so carefully that leaving is exactly what it looks like they never did. There's a difference, and the difference matters."

He steepled his fingers. "What difference does it make to the Consortium whether they deserted or were abducted by spirits? Here is what matters." He turned a paper on his desk so I could read it upside down, which I suppose he thought was subtle. "The concession permits on the upper Redglass require a completed government survey before the winter filing deadline. If the passes close before that survey is finished and filed, the permits lapse. The company stands to lose a considerable investment."

"So find the surveyors, finish the survey, file the papers."

"So *you* find the surveyors," he said. "Before the snows. The high passes generally close by the third week of November. You have five weeks."

"That's not a search, that's a deadline."

"It's both. You're good at deadlines, Underhill. You have one hanging over you that's older than this one." He let that sit, the way he always let it sit, patient as erosion. My father's grave plot, up on the company hill, held against the debt my family had carried since the year the mine took him. Eleven years of my wages and my winters and my badge, all of it mortgaged against a square of ground and a headstone with our name spelled right. "Deliver the survey team, and the company will remember its friends."

Meaning: keep paying. Meaning: keep tracking thieves across the flats for men like him, forever, and call it friendship.

I should have said no. Some part of me, the part that had stood in that too-tidy canyon feeling the wrongness crawl up my arms, wanted to say no. But the other part — the part that thought about frost-heaved ground and a headstone and a debt with interest — that part kept its mouth shut and its hat on.

"I'll need supplies," I said. "And I'll start at the talus fan. Whatever went up that draw, I'm going to follow it."

Something moved behind Harrow's eyes then, quick and unreadable, gone before I could name it. "See that you do," he said. "Thoroughly. And Underhill — report only to me. These are delicate negotiations with the territorial office. No sense stirring rumors."

No sense stirring rumors. From a man whose entire livelihood was stirred rumor. I touched my hat brim and went out into the cold, and the whole ride back down through the assay yard I kept turning over one fact like a coin I suspected was shaved:

A government survey team vanishes without tracks, and the mining consortium that profits most from their absence is the first to know, the least surprised, and the most eager to send one ranger — just one, alone, reporting to no one but Harrow — to bring them back before anyone else comes looking.

Nothing about it added up. But debts don't require arithmetic to make sense; they only require payment. So I went home, sharpened my knife, restocked my kit, and made ready to chase six ghosts up into the Redglass Range before the snow closed the door behind them.

It wasn't until I was packing the next morning that I noticed the thing I'd missed in the camp — the detail that changed the shape of everything, though I wouldn't understand its shape for days yet.

The field notebook, the one weighted under the stone beside the theodolite. When I'd thumbed through it, checking dates, the pages had been crisp and dry. Every page.

But it had rained two nights before the team vanished. Hard. Any notebook lying open on that bench would have warped, spotted, curled.

Unless it had been opened, and written in, *after* the rain.

Somebody had staged that camp. Somebody patient, careful, and very good at making a disappearance look like nothing at all.

And whoever they were, they'd wanted me — or someone like me — to come looking slowly, and follow.
