## 3. What the Compass Won't Say

The trail into the Redglass Range began, as most lies do, with a small and reasonable truth.

I found it two days out from the canyon camp, where the government men had supposedly walked off into the night without boots, food, or sense. A single boot print in the alkali flat at the mouth of Dyer's Wash — left foot, government issue, heel worn on the outside edge like a man who walked with his weight rolled in. I crouched over it for the better part of an hour, and the longer I looked, the less I liked it.

Because it was clean.

Not fresh — I know fresh. Fresh prints have crumbled edges, a shine of disturbed dust, the small collapse of a heel lifting out. This one was a day old, maybe more, and yet it sat in the dust like a coin pressed into wax. No wind-fill. No collapse. The alkali out there is fine as flour and just as fickle; it erases a print in hours. This one had been preserved. Brushed around. Tended.

Somebody had made that print the way a shopkeeper arranges goods in a window.

I stood up, worked the ache out of my knee, and looked north at the Redglass Range, where the peaks were doing their morning trick of pretending to be farther away than they were. Somewhere up in that pink and hostile stone, six government surveyors had vanished so thoroughly that even their instruments kept running. And somebody down here was curating a trail for me.

"Well," I said to the empty wash, because talking to yourself is free and the desert doesn't judge, "aren't you clever."

I filled my canteens at the seep, checked the sun, and started up.

* * *

*They think we walked off a cliff, or into the arms of rustlers, or that we simply lost our minds at altitude. Harrow's men came within a mile of the cache two nights ago. I watched their lanterns from the ridge and thought: they are looking for a body, and we are trying to hand them a confession, and neither of us speaks the other's language.*

*Felix Underhill is on our trail. I know her step the way I know my own pulse. Two years I've run from that woman and she's never once taken a wrong turn out of anger. She reads ground like scripture. So we will give her scripture — but we will give it slowly, verse by verse, and pray she reads it to the end instead of burning the book.*

*The others say I'm a fool to leave signs at all. Marsh says let the mountain do the work. But the mountain will do its work soon enough — the snow is coming, and after the snow there is no confession, only a disappearance, and disappearances favor the man with the lawyers. No. She has to find us. She has to find us and she has to understand, and I can only control the first part.*

*God forgive me for what I stole. God forgive me more for being glad I stole it.*

* — W.C., from a journal kept in a tin box that has no business surviving this altitude*

* * *

The second day the trail climbed out of the wash and into the red stone proper, and it got stranger.

Whoever — whatever — had left the canyon in a hurry had left a trail a child could follow. Scuff marks on slickrock that no boot leaves naturally. A dropped ration tin, sitting upright, label out, in the exact center of the path. A strip of cloth tied to a juniper at precisely the height a man would notice it without stooping.

I'd spent eleven years reading sign for the Consortium, and I want to be clear about what I was seeing: this was not a trail. This was a *demonstration*. A real fleeing party leaves chaos — a burst trail at the start, mistakes, doubling back, the honest litter of fear. This was tidy. This was *edited*. Every hundred yards, another sentence in a language I was only beginning to suspect I'd been reading wrong my whole life.

By noon I'd stopped following the trail and started following the *gaps* in it.

That was the thing the compass wouldn't say. I'd set my bearing by the prints and the tins and the cloth strips, and the compass agreed with them all, cheerfully pointing me up-canyon toward the high passes. But the ground told a different story. There were fainter marks under the obvious ones — a heel dragged where someone had climbed, not walked; the ghost of a handhold polished on a ledge that the "trail" conveniently bypassed. Two trails, laid over each other. The loud one for the searchers. The quiet one for the people who'd actually gone.

And the loud one was built to be slow.

I proved it to myself at midafternoon, at the cairn.

It stood at the mouth of a side draw, a stack of stones maybe knee-high, the kind of marker prospectors and rangers both throw up without thinking. Except this one was wrong in a way that took me a shameful amount of time to name. The stones were stacked in a spiral, each one offset from the last, winding inward like a snail shell. Nobody builds a cairn that way. Cairns are practical — biggest at the bottom, flat stones, done in a minute by cold hands. This one had been *composed*. Somebody had sat in this draw, in the wind, and built a piece of argument out of rock.

I looked at it a long time. Then I looked up the draw, where the obvious trail continued, and then I looked at the quiet marks, which did not go up the draw at all but traversed west along the ledge line.

The cairn wasn't a marker. It was a message: *you are being led, and you know it, and we know you know it.*

"Slowly," I said out loud. "You want me to come slowly."

Because that was the other thing. The trail was built for a tracker of my caliber — and I say that without vanity; it's just a fact of my trade — to follow at a walk. Every sign was legible. Every legible sign cost time. Whoever laid it wanted me in the Redglass Range, wanted me committed, wanted me *invested* — but did not want me arriving. Not today. Not tomorrow. There was something up there that needed days to ripen, and I was being managed like a stubborn mule on a long switchback.

The question that sat down next to me on the warm stone and refused to leave was: *why?*

Either the surveyors had faked their disappearance and were buying time — in which case, time for what? — or someone else had faked it for them, and I was being herded away from the real answer. And underneath both possibilities, coiled and patient, was the one I didn't want to look at directly: Wren Calloway. Two years of chasing that thief, and every trail she'd ever left me had been exactly this — clever, legible, and one step shy of contemptuous. She'd once led me in a circle around Coriander Wells for three days that ended at the racetrack, of all the humiliations, with a note under my saddle blanket that said *you're getting better.*

If this was her handiwork, she wasn't running from me anymore.

She was *inviting* me.

I didn't like how that made my chest feel. I filed the feeling under *altitude* and climbed.

* * *

*She found the cairn. I know because I watched her find it — three hours behind me on the traverse, and she stopped, and she stood over that ridiculous snail-shell of stones with her hands on her hips for so long I started to laugh, up there in the wind, alone, which is a bad habit of mine.*

*Marsh wanted to know what was funny. I said: she's figured it out. He said: then we're finished. I said: no, you don't understand — she's figured it out and she's still coming. That's the whole woman in one gesture. Felix Underhill does not turn around. It is her finest quality and the reason I have to be very careful, because a person who doesn't turn around will follow a thing all the way to the bottom of it, and the bottom of this thing has my name on it.*

*I've left the next sign at the notch where the ledgers say the old survey benchmark stands. If she reads the ground the way I think she does, she'll find the original brass cap under the cairn they built to hide it, and she'll understand why six honest government men stopped being honest. If she doesn't — if she reads it as a trap and comes in shooting —*

*Well. I've spent two years leaving her notes. I suppose I'm finally writing one worth reading.*

* — W.C.*

* * *

I made the notch at last light, cold biting through my coat now, the peaks going the color of an old bruise. The obvious trail ended there — neatly, deliberately, at a cairn twice the size of the last one. A full stop. A period at the end of a sentence.

I almost camped. Every sensible fiber in me said: dark coming, storm building in the northwest, you've been managed for two days, rest and think.

Instead I did what the quiet trail told me. It skirted the big cairn, west, along a shelf of stone, to a pile of rubble against the cliff face that was rubble the way the cairns were cairns — *arranged*. I pulled the top stones off with numb fingers and found, set into the rock behind them, a brass survey cap, green with age, the old government stamp still legible: a benchmark from a survey done forty years ago, before the Consortium, before the concessions, before any of it.

And chiseled into the cliff above it, fresh, in a hand I recognized from a note I'd once found under a saddle blanket — quick, confident, faintly mocking even in stone —

*READ THE OLD NUMBERS. THEN DECIDE WHO'S LYING.*

I sat back on my heels with the wind pulling at my collar and the first flakes of snow starting to come down out of the dark, and I understood two things with perfect clarity.

The first was that the disappearance was a performance, staged by people who wanted an audience of exactly one, and I had a ticket I couldn't refund.

The second was that somewhere above me, in the dark and the coming storm, Wren Calloway was waiting to see if I'd read to the end.

I looked up at the black mass of the Redglass Range, where six liars and a thief were betting everything on my stubbornness, and I did the only thing I have ever known how to do.

I turned around, and I kept climbing.
