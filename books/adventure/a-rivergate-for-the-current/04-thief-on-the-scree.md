## 4. Thief on the Scree

The first thing you learn as a thief is that guilt is a luxury item. It weighs nothing in your pack and everything in your chest, and it never once helped anybody cross a scree slope before dark.

I'd stolen plenty of things before the ledgers. A watch here, a deed there, a whole season's payroll out from under a paymaster who deserved it. Small sins, clean hands. But the night I lifted the original survey ledgers out of Harrow's strongroom at Coriander Wells — three volumes bound in government calfskin, each page stamped and countersigned by men who had never once set boot on the land they were describing — I understood I'd crossed some line I couldn't see until I was on the far side of it. Not because of what I took. Because of what taking it meant: there was no version of my life after that which didn't end at Rivergate, in front of a magistrate, with the truth spread out on a table like a gutted fish.

You don't steal a thing like that to keep it. You steal it to give it away.

* * *

Here is what the ledgers said, for anyone who ever asked me why — and people did ask, usually while pointing a gun.

The consortium's mineral surveys claimed copper in the Redglass headwaters. Rich veins, assay numbers glowing off the page. Enough to justify voiding every water claim along the river settlements from Stonebridge down to the delta — enough to move four hundred people off land their grandmothers had drained and ditched and buried each other in. The permits were already drafted. The magistrate's signature was a formality scheduled for spring.

Except the copper wasn't there.

I know because I read the originals. The field notes — the ones written in cold tents by men with frozen fingers who actually swung the hammers — said gossan only. Iron stain, no ore body. The assays in the ledgers didn't match the assays in the reports Harrow filed. Somebody had sat at a desk in Coriander Wells with a pen and a bottle and improved the numbers by a factor of eleven.

And here's the part that still sits wrong in me, even now: the survey team knew. Six of them, government men and women, sworn to honest measurement, and when they saw what their work had become, they didn't shout about it. They went quiet. They came to *me* — imagine that, the thief being sought out by honest folk — because they'd heard I could move things without them being missed. Dr. Amara Voss, the team lead, found me in a river settlement tavern where I was drinking under a name I've since retired. She put a copy of the falsified report on the table between us and said, "We can't make this loud from inside. They'd bury us and the data both."

So we made it quiet instead. The disappearance was Voss's idea — a vanished survey team stops the clock on the permits better than any petition. A government team unaccounted for means inquiry, and inquiry means delay, and delay past first snow means the concession window closes. We faked it well, if I say so myself. Tents pitched, instruments running, meals half-eaten. I left the trail out of that canyon camp exactly as blank as they wanted it, and I'll admit I enjoyed the craft of it. Erasing six people is harder than stealing one ledger.

We came up into the Redglass Range to wait out the season and let the paperwork strangle itself. And then, three days ago, on a ridge above the tree line, I looked back down the valley with a spyglass out of pure habit —

— and saw her.

* * *

Felix Underhill.

Two years I'd been running from that woman, and she'd never once gotten close enough for me to see her face clearly. She was a story among thieves: the ranger who tracked a payroll thief across forty miles of salt flat with nothing but a dropped button and spite. The one who waited three days outside a fence because she'd noticed the guard dogs liked a certain whistler better than their own handler. You learned to respect Felix Underhill the way you respect weather.

And there she was, a speck of deliberate motion on the lower scree, moving the way she always moved — not fast, not slow, reading the ground like a letter someone had written to her specifically.

My first thought was professional: *she's been sent.* Of course she had. Harrow wouldn't trust a government inquiry; governments ask questions Harrow can't afford. He'd send his debt-bound ranger, the one whose father's grave he holds like a pawn ticket. I knew about the grave. I make it my business to know what levers people carry, and Felix Underhill carried hers openly, the way honest people do, not realizing it made the lever easier to find.

My second thought was less professional. My second thought was that after two years I was finally going to get a good look at her, and I hated myself a little for how much that thought wasn't dread.

* * *

That evening I stood at the mouth of our hidden camp — a hollow below the last stand of whitebark pine, stove-smoke baffled through green boughs, six exhausted surveyors pretending to be geological — and I told Voss the truth, because lying to her had stopped coming easily somewhere on the climb.

"She's coming up," I said. "Underhill. Three days behind us, maybe two now. She's reading our sign."

Voss looked at me over the map case she was re-stitching. "Can you lose her?"

"Anyone else? Already lost." I turned the spyglass over in my hands. "Her, I'm not sure. She doesn't chase sign. She chases *meaning*. I could lay a false trail down the eastern drainage and she'd follow it for half a day out of courtesy, and then she'd notice the cairns are built wrong or the boot prints are too clean, and she'd come back twice as certain."

"So we run."

"We can't run. Okafor's ankle won't take another descent, and if we abandon the instruments the whole performance unravels — a vanished team leaves its theodolite behind, that's a corpse story, and corpses bring bigger inquiries than missing men." I sat down on a stone. "There's a third option."

Voss waited. She's good at waiting. It's why I trusted her with the truth in the first place.

"We let her come," I said. "All the way. And when she gets here, we show her what we have."

"You understand what she is," Voss said. "She's Harrow's. Whatever they hold over her, it's iron."

"I know what she is." I looked east, toward the pass, toward the thin white line already forming on the high country weeks ahead of schedule. "I've spent two years learning what she is. That's rather the problem."

* * *

Here is the confession I'll only make once, and only here, in the privacy of my own skull: I left things for her.

Not obvious things. Nothing a tracker would call bait. But when we moved camp I let a boot print survive in the sheltered mud where any competent eye would find it — mine, too clean, too deliberate, the print of a woman walking slowly enough to be *read*. I built a cairn on the saddle where cairns aren't built, stones stacked in the river fashion instead of the mountain fashion, because the river people stack them three-and-two and every soul from Stonebridge knows it, and Felix Underhill has patrolled this range long enough to know it too. A message in the only language I could be sure she spoke: *this was done by someone who knows this country. Someone who wants you thinking.*

Was it mercy? Calculation? I genuinely could not tell you. Both, probably. Guilt makes a poor compass but it does keep pointing at something.

Because here's what gnawed at me on the cold nights: she didn't deserve what was coming for her. If Harrow sent cutters after us — and he would, the moment he guessed where we'd gone — Felix Underhill would be standing between them and us, holding a badge she'd earned and a debt she hadn't, and either way she'd lose. Deliver us, and she'd spend the rest of her life knowing she'd fed four hundred people to a lie. Refuse, and Harrow takes her father's grave the way he takes everything — quietly, legally, with paper.

I'd stolen a lot of things, but I'd never stolen a person's choice before. And that's what the ledgers had done to her, before she even knew they existed. Her choice was already in my pack, wrapped in oilcloth, and I was climbing higher into the mountains with it while she climbed after me.

* * *

On the fourth morning I went up alone to the saddle above the hollow, ostensibly to check the weather line, actually to look down the valley with the spyglass, and there she was again — closer now, close enough that I could see her stop at my cairn and go very still.

She stood over those stones for a long time. Then she did something I will remember on my deathbed, whatever deathbed that turns out to be: she reached out and touched the top stone, the way you'd touch a doorframe entering a stranger's house. Reading it. Reading *me*.

And then she looked up. Directly at the saddle. Directly, I would have sworn, at me — two miles of cold air between us, and I felt seen anyway, the way you feel a draft from a door you thought was shut.

I should have ducked. Any sensible thief ducks.

Instead I stood there, and I took off my hat — a fool's gesture, invisible at that distance, done purely for my own benefit — and I thought: *come on, then. Come and see.*

Voss asked me that night, one last time, whether I could lead her astray. I told her I'd decide tomorrow.

But we both knew. Somewhere between the salt flats and the snowline, between the strongroom and the saddle, the arithmetic had changed. Two years I'd measured Felix Underhill in miles of escape, and now I was measuring her in days of approach, and the number kept getting smaller, and I found I didn't want it to stop.

Tomorrow she'd reach the tree line. Tomorrow she'd see the smoke we'd stopped bothering to hide.

Tomorrow, one way or another, the thief and the ranger would finally stand face to face — and I had to decide which of us would be caught.
