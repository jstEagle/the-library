## 5. The Camp Above the Clouds

The trail lied to me for three days, and then it stopped lying all at once.

I'd followed it up through the redglass canyons where the stone sang when the wind moved over it — a thin, high whine like a wineglass rubbed wrong — and past the cairn that had been built wrong on purpose, stones stacked in a pattern no muleteer would use, no ranger either. Someone had wanted me to notice. Someone had wanted me to *wonder*. And wonder, I was learning, was slower than pursuit, which was the whole point of it.

By the fourth morning I was above the last of the pinyon, climbing through scrub that clung to the scree like a man clinging to an argument he's already lost. The air had gone thin and clean and cold enough to bite the inside of my nose. Below me the flats outside Coriander Wells had shrunk to a brown smudge, and beyond them the river country lay hidden under haze — all those little settlements with their little docks, none of them knowing what was being drawn up here in their name.

I crested a shoulder of granite just as the sun cleared the eastern ridge, and there it was.

A camp. Tucked into a fold of the mountain below treeline, half-shielded by a stand of wind-bent spruce, close enough that I could see woodsmoke rising straight up in the still air. Six tents. A canvas fly stretched between two of them, and under it, figures moving — small at this distance, unhurried. Not captives, then. Not starving. Cooking breakfast, if my nose could be believed, and it told me bacon, which is not the smell of men held against their will.

My hand went to the warrant in my breast pocket out of pure habit. Six government surveyors, vanished from their canyon camp with their instruments running and their supper half-eaten. My orders were simple: find them, bring them down, do it before the snows closed the passes and the consortium's concession permits expired like milk left out in August.

Simple. Except nothing about this had been simple since the day Harrow handed me the file and smiled with his teeth.

I sat down on the cold rock, out of sight of the camp, and let myself think for the first time in four days instead of just walking and watching. The boot print too clean to be three weeks old. The cairn built wrong. The way the "vanished" team had left their canyon camp tidy as a Sunday parlor — instruments running, meals half-eaten, but every valuable packed away, every ledger accounted for, if you knew what an accounting looked like. I did. I'd spent two years learning how one particular mind arranged a disappearance, and this bore her fingerprints the way a window bears the fingerprints of the brick that went through it.

Wren Calloway.

Two years I'd chased her across three territories. Two years of arriving at empty rooms that smelled faintly of her — cedar oil and gun grease — and finding notes sometimes, tucked under teacups or pinned to stable doors. *Getting warmer, Ranger.* Once, in a hotel in Salt Lick, she'd left me the entire take from a job untouched except for the cost of her room, with a receipt itemized in beautiful copperplate. She stole things nobody thought worth stealing and ignored things worth fortunes, and every lawman from here to the coast had a theory about her, and every theory was wrong because none of them included the possibility that she was simply better than we were.

And now she was down there, in that camp, presumably eating bacon.

I should have gone back. That was the correct move — ride hard for Coriander Wells, put the location in Harrow's hands, let him send men with rifles and warrants and no curiosity whatsoever. It would have been easy. It would have been *owed*.

Instead I found myself checking my rifle out of habit rather than intent, and walking down toward the smoke.

* * *

They heard me coming, of course. I made sure of it — crunched the last fifty yards of scree deliberately, so nobody would get jumpy and somebody would get dead. When I stepped under the canvas fly, six faces turned toward me, and every one of them had the look of people who'd been waiting for this moment the way you wait for a tooth to be pulled: dreading it, but wanting it over.

"Ranger Underhill," said the nearest man. He was fifty-odd, gray-bearded, sunburned to leather, with the ink-stained fingers of someone who'd spent his life drawing lines on paper. Chief Surveyor Anselm Pike. I recognized him from the file photographs, though the photographs hadn't shown how tired he was. He looked like a man who hadn't slept properly in a month and had decided, finally, that sleep was a luxury for people with cleaner consciences.

"You know my name," I said.

"We know a great deal about you," said a woman at the table behind him. She had a sextant beside her elbow and a map weighted down with stones, and she spoke with the flat patience of a schoolteacher addressing a promising but difficult student. "We've been leaving you a trail for nine days, Miss Underhill. We're glad you finally took the hint."

"I noticed the hint," I said. "The cairn was a nice touch. Very amateurish."

"That was Marta's work." Pike gestured at a young woman by the fire, who had the grace to look embarrassed. "She insisted. She said you'd never follow a trail that looked professional."

"She was right," I admitted. "Professional trails are for people who want to lose you."

"And which kind is this?" asked a voice from the far end of the fly, and my heart did something unprofessional and entirely involuntary, because I knew that voice the way you know a song you hate yourself for humming.

She stood up slowly, hands visible, palms out — Wren Calloway, in a surveyor's canvas coat two sizes too big for her, hair tied back with what looked suspiciously like instrument wire. Two years of chasing her, and the first thing I felt wasn't triumph or fury. It was the absurd, humiliating relief of a dog that has finally caught the cart.

"Hello, Felix," she said.

"Don't," I said. "Don't 'hello Felix' me. You don't get to be familiar. You're under arrest, Wren. All of you are. Theft of government property, obstruction of a territorial survey, and whatever charge fits faking your own disappearance, which I'm sure some clerk in Rivergate will invent before lunch."

"Before you read us our rights," Pike said quietly, "you should read these."

He nodded to the woman with the sextant, who pulled a ledger from under the map and set it on the table in front of me. Then another. Then a third, and a folder of loose reports, and a roll of survey maps tied with string.

I didn't touch them. Touching them meant something. "What are they?"

"The original field ledgers," Pike said. "Every assay, every core sample, every measurement taken in the Redglass Range this season. In our own hands, in our own writing. And next to them—" he tapped the folder "—the reports we supposedly filed. The ones with our names on them. The ones the consortium submitted to the territorial land office."

I opened the folder, because at some point a person has to look.

The numbers were wrong. Not subtly wrong — not the honest slop of fieldwork versus office arithmetic, which any surveyor expects. They were wrong the way a portrait is wrong when the painter has been paid to flatter. Copper assays inflated fourfold. Water tables marked as dry that I personally knew ran sweet, because I'd watered horses at them. And along the river bottoms — the settlements, the docks, the little towns that lived by the current — the mineral claims were drawn in fat confident strokes, swallowing homesteads whole.

"They falsified it," I said. My voice came out flat. "All of it."

"Every line," Pike said. "Harrow's office gave us the figures before we ever took a sample. We were told to confirm them. When we wouldn't, we were told our contracts would not be renewed and our findings would be filed regardless. So we filed nothing. We came up here, and we waited for the one person in this territory stubborn enough and honest enough to find us anyway."

"That's flattering," I said, "and it doesn't change what she did." I jerked my chin at Wren. "She stole those ledgers from the land office vault. That's a felony regardless of what's written in them."

"Yes," Wren said. No denial, no deflection, none of her usual dancing. She looked at me straight, and there was something in her face I'd never seen in two years of glimpses and taunting notes: she was afraid. Not of me. Of whether it would matter. "I stole them. I'll plead to it, Felix, gladly, in front of any magistrate you like. But they have to reach the court before the permits expire at dawn on the twenty-eighth. After that, Harrow's claims convert to title, and everything in these books becomes history instead of evidence."

"The court is four days' ride," I said. "The passes—"

"Are closing early this year. We know. We've been watching the sky same as you." Pike spread his hands. "Six of us can't make it down fast enough carrying instruments and supplies, and if we come down together we're six witnesses Harrow can bury in one avalanche. But one rider, light, with the ledgers in a saddlebag—" He stopped. He didn't finish it. He didn't have to.

"You're asking me to deliver stolen goods to a territorial court," I said, "on the word of a thief and six contract-breakers, against the consortium that owns—" I stopped too. Because the next word was *my father's grave*, and saying it out loud in front of strangers suddenly felt like undressing.

"Owes you," Wren said softly. "I know what Harrow holds over you, Felix. I've known for a year. Why do you think I kept letting you catch up?"

The camp went very quiet. Even the fire seemed to hold its crackle.

"You—" I stared at her. "Every time. Salt Lick. Dry Fork. The ferry at Cane Crossing. You *let* me—"

"I needed you to keep following," she said. "Because someday the trail you were following had to end somewhere true. And I couldn't hand you the truth in a hotel room with a note. You'd have thrown it in the stove. You had to walk up a mountain to get it. You had to *earn* it." She shrugged, and the oversized coat shifted on her shoulders. "Also, yes. I liked being chased by you. Both things can be true."

I looked down at the ledgers on the table. Honest numbers in tired hands. I looked at the map, and the fat greedy claim-lines crawling over the river towns. I thought about Harrow's smile, and the deed to a grave plot that had kept me on a chain for eleven years, and the way he'd said *family obligation is such a fine motivator, Miss Underhill*, the day he handed me this assignment.

Everything I owed was owed to a lie. Everything they'd stolen had been stolen from a theft.

"Twenty-eighth," I said finally. "Dawn. Permits expire."

"At dawn," Pike confirmed.

I picked up the ledgers. They weighed almost nothing. They weighed more than anything I'd ever carried.

"I'm not doing this for you," I told Wren.

"No," she agreed, and something eased in her face, some tension she'd been holding maybe for two years. "You're doing it for the river. That's why it counts."

Outside, far off to the north, the first clouds of the season were stacking up against the high peaks, white and orderly and patient as a debt coming due. I checked the sky, checked my horse, and started counting days.

Four. Maybe less, if the snow had opinions of its own.
