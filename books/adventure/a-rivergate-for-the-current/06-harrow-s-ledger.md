## 6. Harrow's Ledger

The supply runner came up the switchbacks two days after I'd stopped pretending I was still hunting them.

His name was Doss — a wiry, wind-scoured man who ran mules for the Consortium and had the particular talent of looking like he'd never once thought an original thought. He crested the last rise below treeline just past midmorning, leading three loaded animals, and I watched him come from the rocks above the hidden camp with my heart doing something complicated in my chest. Because there was only one reason Harrow would send a man this far, this late, with winter already breathing down the passes.

I went down to meet him before he could go up any farther.

"Felix Underhill." He said it like he was confirming a delivery address. "Got correspondence. And provisions."

"Correspondence," I said. "From Superintendent Harrow."

"That's the one." He dug into his coat and produced a letter sealed with the Consortium's wax — the red stamp of the pick-and-scales, pressed deep enough that the paper had dented under it. Harrow always did press too hard. "He said you'd want it read private."

"I'll bet he did."

Doss shrugged and began unloading feed for the mules, which was its own kind of message: he wasn't leaving soon, and he wasn't pretending otherwise. I carried the letter up above the camp, out of earshot of the surveyors, who were doing their best to look like men not hiding from their own government, and broke the seal.

Harrow's hand was as I remembered it — small, precise, economical, the handwriting of a man who never wasted ink or feeling.

*Underhill.*

*Your failure to report is noted. Your continued silence is also noted, and understood for what it is.*

*You will be pleased to learn your search is at an end. We have located the survey team through means that need not concern you. A recovery detail is already on the trail — six cutters, contracted through channels you would be wise not to ask about. Their instructions are to retrieve certain property belonging to the Consortium and to escort the team down for questioning regarding the circumstances of their disappearance.*

*The word "escort" is doing considerable work in that sentence, Underhill, and we both know it.*

*As to yourself: your father's account stands at four hundred and twelve dollars against plot 9-C, perpetual maintenance included. Deliver the woman Calloway and the ledgers she carries to the recovery detail or to Coriander Wells directly, and that account will be closed in full. The grave will be yours outright. No more seasons of chasing thieves across the flats to pay for dirt.*

*You have until the snow closes Stonebridge Pass. After that, the matter is out of my hands, and so are you.*

*Do not make me regret the faith I have placed in you. It is, I am told, the only faith anyone has placed in you in some years.*

*— R. Harrow*

I read it twice. The second time was not because I misunderstood anything the first time. It was because I wanted to see if the words changed when I knew what they cost.

They didn't. Four hundred and twelve dollars. My father's grave, itemized like a shipment of ore. Perpetual maintenance included — as if love were a service the Consortium rendered and I was behind on payments. Eleven years I'd been paying that debt, first as a girl running errands in the Wells, then as a ranger wearing their badge, and the number had never once gone down faster than it grew. It was a grave designed never to be paid off. I'd known that for years, in the way you know a thing you can't afford to say out loud.

Now Harrow was offering to erase it. All I had to do was hand over a woman whose worst crime was stealing the truth back from men who'd sold it.

* * *

I didn't burn the letter, though I thought about it. Instead I walked down into the camp, where Wren Calloway was crouched over the cook fire arguing with Okafor — no, that's wrong, the surveyor's name was Marsh — about whether you could stretch beans another day without mutiny. She looked up when I came in, and whatever was in my face must have shown, because the argument died and the other five found urgent business elsewhere among the tents.

"Well?" she said.

I held out the letter. She read it standing, firelight — such as it was, midday gray through the smoke-hole — moving over her face. When she finished, she folded it along Harrow's creases, neat as a bank clerk, and handed it back.

"Four hundred and twelve dollars," she said.

"You've got a good memory."

"I've got a good memory for what people are owed. It's most of what I do." She sat down on a supply crate and studied me. "So. This is the part where you decide, isn't it? I've been watching you circle it for two days."

"I'm not circling anything."

"You're a ranger tracking six fugitives you found three days ago and haven't reported. That's not a decision, Felix. That's a decision being very slowly made."

I wanted to argue. The trouble with Wren Calloway was that she was almost never wrong, and she knew it, and she wore that knowledge the way other people wore a knife — casually, where you could see it. Two years I'd chased her across the flats and the dry washes, and in all that time I'd built her into something simple: a thief, a job, a debt of my own that ran alongside the grave money. Then I'd climbed a mountain and found her sitting at a fire with the whole rotten architecture of the Consortium spread out on oilcloth, and simple had died somewhere on the scree.

"The cutters," I said instead. "How many days out?"

"If they left when Harrow wrote that letter? Four. Maybe five if the weather argues with them."

"And the snow?"

She glanced up, at the sky beyond the smoke-hole, where the clouds had been building all morning — the wrong color, the heavy bruised gray that meant the range was done negotiating. "Earlier than that, maybe. It's starting early this year. Everyone on this mountain knows it except the men coming up to kill us."

"They're not coming up to kill you."

"No," she agreed mildly. "They're coming up to *escort* us. For questioning." She let that sit. "Felix. Ask me the question you actually came down here to ask."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, wind moved through the pines with a sound like water getting ready to become something else.

"Why did you take the ledgers?" I said. "Two years ago. The Coriander Wells office. You could have taken the payroll box — it was sitting right there, Harrow told me himself, half a year of cutter wages in silver. You took paper instead. Paper nobody would even miss for a week. Why?"

"Because the payroll was theirs," she said simply. "The ledgers belonged to the river. My grandmother's name is in those books, Felix. Entry forty-one, Redglass survey, original mineral readings — and then entry forty-two, same rock, same assay, different numbers, initialed by a man who'd never been within fifty miles of the river. They didn't falsify one survey. They falsified eleven years of them. Every settlement from Stonebridge down to the delta is sitting on land the Consortium has already decided is 'barren,' which is the legal word they use right before the word 'available.'" She stood, and for a moment she wasn't a thief at all; she was something older and angrier, a current that had been dammed too long. "I stole the only copy of the truth that exists. That's why you never caught me. You can't catch a person carrying something everyone else wants buried."

"And now everyone wants it unburied," I said. "Or buried deeper."

"Now everyone wants it," she said. "Which is worse, for me. For you too." She stepped closer, close enough that I could smell woodsmoke and cold air on her, and her voice dropped to something just for me. "There's a version of the next four days where you walk down that pass, meet those cutters halfway, and hand me over. And in that version, in about a month, you're standing at a graveside in Coriander Wells that finally belongs to you, and it's quiet, and it's paid for, and every time you visit it you'll know exactly what you traded for the deed."

"That's not fair."

"No," she said. "It isn't. None of this is fair. Fairness is for people who can afford it — ask Harrow's ledger." Her eyes held mine, and there was no calculation in them now, no thief's cleverness. Just the question underneath everything. "But there's another version. And you already know which one you're in. You've known since the scree. You just haven't said it out loud yet."

* * *

I said it out loud that night, but not to her.

Snow started an hour after full dark — fat, deliberate flakes, the kind that mean business, falling straight down through the pines with no wind to excuse them. Early. Weeks early. By morning the pass would be closing whether anyone liked it or not, and every hour we waited was an hour the cutters gained and the court at Rivergate lost.

I sat up on the ridge above the camp with the letter in my pocket and my badge in my hand. I hadn't worn it since the camp; I'd kept it in my coat anyway, the way you keep a thing that's part of your weight. Consortium tin. Ranger, Redglass District. It had bought me authority, and shelter, and the right to chase one specific woman for two years, and it had never once bought me free.

Below me, the camp fires glowed small and stubborn against the snowfall. Wren moved between the tents, checking lines, settling people, and even at this distance I could pick her out of six silhouettes without trying. That was the tell, really. Two years of tracking her and I'd learned her walk better than my own.

My father had been a river man before the Consortium owned him — a bargeman on the lower water, back when the settlements still trusted the surveys. He'd drowned in a spring flood the year the falsified reports started, though nobody connected those facts then, least of all me. He was buried on Consortium land because there was nowhere else, and I had spent half my life paying rent on his bones to the men who'd helped empty his river.

Harrow was right about one thing. It was the only faith anyone had placed in me in some years.

But he was wrong about whose.

I turned the badge over once in my fingers, felt the cheap tin cold as river ice, and set it on a flat stone at the top of the ridge, face-down, where the snow was already beginning to cover it. Not thrown away. Just — set down. There's a difference, and I figured I'd earned the difference.

Then I went down into the camp, stamped the snow off my boots at Wren's fire, and said the words out loud at last:

"The pass closes tomorrow. The cutters are four days out unless they push hard, and Harrow doesn't hire patient men. So we leave tonight, ahead of the storm, ahead of them, down the White Pass to Rivergate with those ledgers — and when we get there, I'm going to stand in front of a magistrate and testify to everything I know about how the Consortium does its arithmetic." I pulled the letter from my pocket and fed it to her fire. "Starting with this."

Wren watched the paper curl and blacken, and when she looked up at me there was something in her face I'd spent two years crossing deserts trying to find.

"Took you long enough," she said.

Above us, the snow thickened, and somewhere down the dark pass, six men were already climbing.
