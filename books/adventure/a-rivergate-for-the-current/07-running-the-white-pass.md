## 7. Running the White Pass

**FELIX**

The storm came down the pass like a debt collector — no negotiation, no grace period, just weather with a warrant.

We broke camp above the clouds at first light, six surveyors, one thief, and one ranger who had officially stopped being a ranger somewhere around the moment she'd said *yes* instead of drawing her iron. I hadn't filed the paperwork on that yet. There wasn't a form for it anyway. There was only the descent: eleven miles of switchback through the Redglass Range, a frozen river crossing at the bottom, and somewhere behind us, Harrow's cutters already climbing.

I counted our pace in my head the way my father taught me to count cattle — by what you could lose. An old man named Osei with knees that sounded like gravel in a tin cup. A young instrument tech, Petra, who talked when she was scared and was therefore talking constantly. The team lead, Vasquez, gray-faced and steady, carrying the forged reports against her chest like a second heart. And Wren Calloway, who carried the ledgers in a oilskin wrap slung across her back, and who caught me looking at her and grinned like she knew exactly what I was thinking.

"You're doing the math," she said.

"I'm always doing the math."

"Eleven miles," she said. "Storm's maybe four hours behind us. Cutters are slower in snow — they'll take the east chute because it looks easier."

"It is easier."

"Right. So they'll be predictable." She adjusted the strap on her shoulder. "You could've been a strategist, Underhill. Wasted on chasing people like me."

"People like you are the only interesting thing that ever happened to this territory."

She laughed — a short, surprised sound, swallowed quickly by the wind — and we kept moving.

* * *

**WREN**

Here is what nobody tells you about stealing something important: after the theft, the object gets heavier every day. Not physically. Physically the ledgers weighed about as much as a sleeping cat. But morally they gained weight like wet wool, and by the time we reached the frozen river at the bottom of the pass, I felt like a pack animal carrying a cathedral.

Felix walked point. Of course she did. She'd been walking point her whole life — ahead of her family's debts, ahead of Harrow's orders, ahead of everyone who might have told her the truth about anything. Watching her work the trail was its own kind of confession. She read snow the way I read locks: patiently, respectfully, assuming the thing would try to kill her.

Two years I'd run from her. Two years of clean getaways and empty pockets and one increasingly absurd legend — *the ghost of Coriander Wells*, the papers called me, which flattered me enormously since mostly I'd just been very good at leaving before dawn. And now here she was, breaking trail for me, for us, for six strangers whose crime was telling the truth.

I kept waiting to feel like I'd tricked her into this. That was the guilt I hadn't confessed to anyone, not even the firelight version of myself: that some part of me had wanted her to find us. Had left the boot print too clean, the cairn built wrong, the little breadcrumbs up the whole red throat of the range. A thief who wants to be caught isn't a thief anymore. She's a letter waiting for a postman.

The river appeared below us around midday — a white seam stitched between black canyon walls, frozen hard enough, Vasquez hoped, to hold a loaded line of people.

"Frozen rivers don't hope," Felix said, when Vasquez said so out loud.

"Then what do they do?"

"They wait."

* * *

**PETRA**

I want it noted, wherever notes go when everything goes wrong, that I said the ice looked wrong. I said it twice. Nobody yelled at me; that's not the kind of crew this was. Vasquez just nodded and put Osei on the rope first because he was lightest, and Felix went second because she was Felix, and I went third because I'd stopped arguing with competence somewhere around the third day of faking our own disappearance.

The river under the ice was still moving. You could hear it — a low, patient sound, like something breathing below the floor of the world. We were halfway across, strung out on the rope, wind funnelling down the canyon hard enough to make my eyes water, when the ice spoke back.

It didn't crack so much as *open*. One moment solid white, the next a black seam yawning under Osei, and then he was gone — no scream, just a sound like a door closing, and the rope snapping taut, and all of us thrown down onto our bellies with the cold coming up through our coats like a living thing.

"Nobody stands!" Felix's voice cut through everything. "Wren — anchor! Petra, on my feet, pull when I say!"

I crawled. I don't remember deciding to crawl; my body did it without consulting me. Felix went down the rope hand over hand toward the hole, and I will see this until I die: her boots braced against the ice edge, her whole body leaning out over black water, hauling, while the current pulled Osei down and the ice groaned around her like it was considering taking her too.

"NOW!" she roared, and we pulled, all five of us, screaming, slipping, and Osei came up out of the river like something being born — blue, gasping, alive — and the three of us dragged him twenty yards to the far bank before any of us could breathe.

He was shaking so hard his teeth chattered in a rhythm, and Vasquez wrapped him in every dry thing we owned, and Felix stood there dripping from the elbows down, hands bleeding where the rope had burned them, looking at the hole in the river like it owed her an apology.

"That's one mile down," she said finally. "Ten to go."

* * *

**FELIX**

We made camp that night in the lee of a rockfall, because Osei couldn't walk another step and pride doesn't melt hypothermia. Small fire, screened by stone. Watches in pairs. The storm hit the upper pass behind us around dusk — we heard it before we saw it, a long white roar swallowing the country we'd come from, which meant the cutters were either trapped above us or pushing through it angry.

Both options bought us time. Neither bought enough.

First watch was mine. Second was Wren's, and instead of waking me for third, she sat down next to me at the fire with two cups of tea made from snow and the last of the pine needles, which tasted like punishment, and handed me one.

"You're off shift," she said.

"I'm fine."

"You pulled a man out of a frozen river today. You're allowed to be a person, Felix. Briefly. I hear it's reversible."

I took the tea. The fire popped. Down-canyon, the frozen river gleamed faintly, the scar of Osei's rescue already iced over again, patient, waiting.

"Why'd you leave the trail for me?" I asked. "Back in the range. The print, the cairn. You wanted to be followed."

She looked into the fire for a long time. "I've asked myself that every night since. Best answer I've got is — I got tired of running from someone I respected. It's exhausting, disrespecting your own taste."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed. "It's a confession wearing an answer's coat." She turned to look at me, firelight on half her face. "Two years, Felix. Do you know how many times I almost let you catch me? There was a night in Dry Forks when I left the window unlatched on purpose and then climbed out it anyway, because I hadn't finished what I started, and a thief who quits mid-job is just a criminal, but a thief who finishes—"

"—is a witness," I said.

Her mouth curved. "Is a witness."

The wind came down the canyon and the fire leaned hard, and for a moment neither of us moved, and I thought: *this is the thing Harrow can never put in a ledger.* What a person is worth when they're tired and cold and honest. No column for it.

"When this is done," Wren said quietly, "and if we live — I owe you two years of explanations. Maybe more. Maybe I owe you something better than explanations."

"Don't owe me anything," I said. "I'm done with owing. Start fresh or don't start."

"Fresh," she repeated, like she was testing the weight of it. "All right. Fresh. Hello. I'm Wren. I steal from liars."

"Felix. I arrest people for a consortium I hate, and today I stopped."

"Pleasure's mine."

* * *

**VASQUEZ**

I woke them both at grey dawn because the boy on last watch — Aldous, nineteen, all elbows — came crawling into camp saying he'd seen lights on the east rim. Lanterns, moving. Three, maybe four.

Felix was on her feet before he finished the sentence, and I watched her do the arithmetic on her face: eight miles to Rivergate, permits expiring at dawn tomorrow, cutters four hours behind and closing, and a storm at our backs like a wall falling.

"We split?" Wren asked.

"No." Felix was already kicking snow over the fire. "They expect splitting. We stay together, we move fast, and we take Stonebridge — it's the only crossing, which means it's also where they'll be waiting."

"So we walk into their ambush."

"We walk into it *early*, before they've set it properly." She shouldered her pack and looked at each of us in turn, and I have commanded survey crews through rockslides and flash floods, and I have never seen anyone look at people the way she looked at us then — like we were hers, like that was a fact she'd signed for. "One way or another, we cross that bridge today. Everybody ready?"

Osei stood up on legs that had nearly become river-bottom yesterday, and said, "Ready," and that settled it, the way those things do.

We went down toward the bridge and the morning, and behind us the storm came on like something with our names in its mouth.
