## 8. The Cutting at Stonebridge

The old stone bridge at Stonebridge was built before the Consortium had a name, back when the river people still trusted the government enough to pay for masonry. Two arches, granite blocks furred with lichen, spanning a gorge where the White River ran green and mean under early ice. It was the only crossing for forty miles in any direction, which meant it was either the safest place on the pass or the most dangerous, depending entirely on who got there first.

We didn't get there first.

I knew it before I saw them. That's the thing nobody tells you about two years of chasing one particular thief: you learn her rhythms so well they become your own. Wren Calloway moved through country like water finding its level — always testing, always reading. And all morning she'd been reading wrong. Taking the high line when the low line was faster. Pushing pace when every instinct of survival said rest. She wasn't hurrying toward Rivergate. She was hurrying away from something behind us, and she was doing it badly, which meant she wanted me to notice without wanting to say it aloud.

So when we crested the last rise above the gorge and I saw the bridge sitting there empty and innocent as a Sunday sermon, I already knew.

"Stop," I said.

The surveyors stopped. Six of them left from the seven who'd come down off the mountain, poor Dray Halloway still limping on the ankle the crevasse had tried to keep. They were gaunt and frostbitten and carrying forty pounds of evidence apiece, and they stopped because by now they'd learned that the desert ranger who'd been sent to arrest them had better instincts than their own team lead.

Wren didn't stop. She took three more steps down the scree, then turned, and in the flat grey light her face did something complicated — guilt and apology and defiance all fighting for the same ground.

"You knew," she said.

"I've known since the second cairn this morning." I came down beside her, boots loose in the shale. "You've been steering us like a barge with a drunk pilot. Question is why."

"Because there's no other way across." Her breath came out white and fast. "The ford upstream is iced to slush. The gorge walls are sheer for six miles. It's the bridge or we winter on this side of the river watching our evidence rot in a canvas pack while Harrow's men sit warm in Coriander Wells laughing at us."

"And you didn't say anything because?"

"Because if I said it, you'd do what you're about to do."

Which was look at the bridge properly. Not at the arches but at the approaches. At the cut bank on the far side where the trail narrowed between rock and drop-off. At the stand of dead pine just past the near abutment, close enough to cover, far enough back that nobody sheltering in it would show themselves to casual eyes.

At the thin thread of woodsmoke standing perfectly straight in the windless air above those pines, like a flag planted by someone who didn't care who saw it.

"They want us to cross," I said.

"They *need* us to cross," Wren said. "There's a difference, and the difference is that we don't have a choice, and they know we don't have a choice, and knowing that makes them sloppy." She said it like a woman arguing herself into courage, and I recognized the technique because I'd used it on myself more times than I could count.

Dray came up on my left, favoring his ankle. "How many?"

I studied the pines again. The smoke was too thin for a cook fire, too steady for a signal. "Four, maybe five. Cutters travel light and mean. They'll hold the far approach and let the bridge do the killing work — narrow frontage, no cover on the span, ice on the stones." I looked at Wren. "You should have told me at dawn."

"If I'd told you at dawn, you'd have spent six hours trying to invent a third option, and there isn't one." Her jaw set. "Felix. The ledgers have to reach Rivergate. Whatever else happens on that bridge — whatever happens to *us* — the ledgers reach Rivergate."

And there it was. The thing she'd been carrying since the camp above the clouds, wrapped up in practicality like a blade wrapped in oilcloth. She'd decided, somewhere on the mountain, that the mission outranked the people carrying it. I understood the arithmetic. I'd lived my whole life inside that kind of arithmetic — debts and duties, what you owe against what you're owed. I'd just spent nine days learning to hate it.

"Here's how it goes," I said. "The surveyors split. Three take the ledgers and go loud at the near end — make noise, make targets, give the cutters something to watch. The other three go quiet along the gorge rim and downclimb to the old fisherman's ledge under the first arch. There's a route. I've crossed here before."

"There's no ledge," Wren said.

"There's a ledge if you know where your hands go. My father showed me when I was fourteen. He also showed me which stones on the span are slick, which is information I'm now going to use differently than he intended." I pulled my coat tighter and looked at each of them in turn, these exhausted, stubborn, decent people who'd burned their careers to tell the truth. "Nobody crosses the top of the bridge. Nobody dies for a shortcut. We go under it, we come out on the far side, and we put ten miles between us and this gorge before dark."

"And you?" Wren asked quietly.

"I'm the loudest thing on this mountain. Always have been." I checked the action on my rifle, an old habit that felt suddenly new. "They came for the ledgers and for you. So the ledgers go under the bridge, and you go under the bridge, and I stand on top of it being extremely findable until everyone's across."

"No," Wren said.

"It's not a vote."

"Felix—"

"Wren." I made myself look at her, really look, the way I hadn't let myself since the firelight two nights ago when she'd told me about her brother and the flood and why she stole things that weren't money. "Two years I chased you because catching you was the only debt I could actually pay. This morning I finally understand that some debts aren't meant to be paid. They're meant to be *chosen*. Let me choose this one."

She stared at me, and something in her face cracked open — not tears, nothing so simple, but the place where tears would live if she allowed them. Then she grabbed the front of my coat and kissed me, hard and brief and furious, like punctuation at the end of an argument she'd lost.

"Don't die stupidly," she said. "Die cleverly or not at all."

"Noted."

* * *

It went wrong in the way things go wrong: quickly, and then all at once.

The decoys worked for eleven minutes. I counted, because counting kept the fear in a box. I stood on the near approach in full view, hat off, rifle slung, shouting down the gorge like a madwoman about territorial authority and lawful detention, and four cutters rose out of the dead pines like crows off a carcass — lean men in matched grey, moving with the unhurried competence of people paid by the job. Their leader was a broad, grey-bearded man with a scar through his eyebrow, and he looked at me standing alone on the bridge approach and smiled the smile of a man whose arithmetic has just balanced.

"Ranger Underhill," he called. "Superintendent Harrow sends his regards. He says your account is settled if the Calloway woman and the paper come back with us. Says you can even keep the badge."

"She's not here."

"Course she's not." He strolled forward, easy as Sunday. "That's why we're having this conversation instead of shooting. Where is she, Felix? Because we found the rim trail, and we found the ledge marks, and my boys are walking the far bank right now. You bought them maybe twenty minutes with this little theater. Was it worth it?"

Under the bridge, iron rings set into the granite by some long-dead stonemason — hand over hand, the river roaring green below, spray freezing on the stone. Twenty minutes. It might be enough. It had to be enough.

"Was it worth it?" I echoed, and laughed, and heard how unhinged the laugh sounded, and decided to lean into it. "Mister, I've spent two years chasing a thief across half a desert for a debt I never chose. Everything since Tuesday has been worth it."

He sighed, genuinely regretful, and nodded to his men, and that's when the shot came from the far bank — not at me, at the cliff base below the second arch, where a figure in a brown coat had just reached the fisherman's path. Rock spalled and sang. A scream that wasn't Wren's — thank God, thank God — but the plan died right there, because the remaining cutters on the far side stopped hunting the ledge and started hunting the noise, and the noise was everywhere, and then it was nowhere, and then it was *behind* me.

I heard the boots on the span too late. Turned with the rifle coming up. A cutter named — I'd learn later — Vess hit me with a shoulder rather than a bullet, because Harrow wanted me alive and deniable, and we went down together on the ice-slicked stones with the gorge yawning on either side. The rifle skated away. His knee found my ribs. Something in my side went hot and wet, and the world tilted, and through the ringing in my skull I saw the far end of the bridge, where Wren had come up off the ledge into exactly the trap she'd known was waiting, because the ledge route had been found and the ledgers were heavy and she would not — she *would* not — leave them in the river.

They took her at the far abutment. Four men, and she fought like a wildcat anyway, and I screamed her name and got a boot in the mouth for it, and the world went red and then grey.

When I could see straight, I was propped against the bridge parapet with my own coat pressed to my side, and the grey-bearded man was crouched in front of me with a canteen, and the bridge was empty except for blood — mine — and scuff marks pointing east, toward Coriander Wells. The ledgers gone. Wren gone. The surveyors scattered into the high country like startled birds, alive, at least; I could hear Dray's whistle signal fading up the rim, three notes, *we live, we hide*.

"Harrow says walk away clean," the cutter said. Not unkindly. That was the worst part — the professional gentleness of it. "Debt's forgiven either way. Grave plot's yours regardless. He's not a monster, the superintendent. He just wants his paperwork and his problem solved, and you lying here bleeding is neither. Go home, Ranger. Sleep it off. In a month none of this will have happened."

He left the canteen. He left my rifle, unloaded, ten feet away, an insult dressed as mercy. And then the grey coats were gone down the east trail with Wren Calloway walking between them, chin up, hands bound, and she did not look back, and I understood that she wasn't looking back because looking back would have broken her, and she needed to stay whole for whatever came next.

* * *

I lay on the cold stones for a long time. Long enough for the blood to stiffen in my shirt. Long enough for the ice on the river to groan and shift, the way it does when the current underneath refuses to quit.

Walk away clean. Debt forgiven. Father's grave secured, badge intact, a warm bunk at Coriander Wells and a life measured out in patrol reports until retirement. Everything I'd been grinding toward for eleven years, handed back to me on a bridge, free of charge, the moment I agreed to forget.

My father had been dead nineteen years and I had never once visited his grave. I'd paid for it every month of my adult life and never stood at it. Somewhere in those nineteen years the grave had stopped being about him and become a leash, and I'd worn it so long I'd mistaken it for a spine.

I sat up. The world objected. I sat up anyway.

Ten feet away lay my rifle, unloaded, gleaming with someone's contempt. I crawled to it, because crawling was what I had, and I checked the magazine out of pure habit, and of course it was empty, and I started laughing, which hurt, which made me laugh more.

Because here was the thing the grey-bearded man hadn't counted on. Here was the thing Harrow's tidy arithmetic couldn't hold. I didn't need bullets. I'd spent two years learning every trick Wren Calloway knew — every false cairn, every clean boot print, every way a person can move through official country without official permission. I knew the back ways into Coriander Wells better than the men who patrolled them, because I'd mapped half of them myself. I knew which constable owed me, which lockup had a rotten sill, which magistrate at Rivergate still read actual testimony instead of consortium summaries.

Harrow had taken the thief and the ledgers and my blood, and thought that settled accounts.

But I was the ranger. Finding people was the one thing I had ever been good at.

I got to my feet, swayed, held. East, toward Coriander Wells, toward the lockup where they'd hold her overnight before moving her north. Toward the courthouse at Rivergate where the permits died at dawn. Twelve hours, a busted rib, an empty gun, and a debt I was done paying.

I picked up the canteen, drank, and turned my face east.

"Keep her alive, Harrow," I said to the empty bridge, to the green river, to whoever was listening. "I'm coming to collect."
