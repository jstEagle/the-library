## 9. Rivergate

Rivergate at night smelled of coal smoke and river rot, and I loved it the way you can only love a place that owes you nothing.

I came in on the mail sledge, half-frozen, three days out from Stonebridge with a cracked rib and a face Harrow's man had redecorated. The town clung to the river like something determined not to be washed away — which, I supposed, was exactly what it was. Lamplight in the windows. Ice forming along the pilings in gray lace. Somewhere a dog was losing an argument with the dark.

The courthouse sat on the high bank, stone-faced and stubborn, its clock tower the only thing in Rivergate taller than the willows. Under it, in a cellar that had once been a jail and was now, by some legal sleight nobody bothered to explain, a consortium lockup, they were holding Wren Calloway and two of her surveyors.

And under my coat, wrapped in oilcloth against my ribs where the bruise throbbed worst, I carried the thing that mattered: nothing. That was the joke of it. The ledgers had gone with the cutters when they took Wren. All I carried was what lived in my head — six weeks of trail, camp, and confession, every page of forged mineral data Wren had made me read by firelight until I could recite it like scripture. Which, by then, I more or less could.

Harrow thought he'd taken everything at Stonebridge. He'd taken the paper and the thief and left me bleeding in the snow with an offer: walk away clean, debt forgiven, badge intact, your father sleeps undisturbed.

I'd walked away clean for about four hundred yards. Then I turned around.

* * *

The lockup's front door faced the square, all official brass and iron. Its back door faced an alley that smelled of tannery, and its back door had a lock older than the territory itself. I'd spent the ride down memorizing what Wren had told me of it — she'd been held there once before, years back, for lifting a magistrate's watch as a dare, and she described that lock the way other people describe old lovers. Fondly. With specific complaints.

"A Corbin pattern," she'd said, teeth chattering on the sled. "Left-handed wards, which flatters them, because it means whoever installed it assumed no one clever would ever come at it from the left."

So I came at it from the left, in the blue hour before dawn, with a pry bar borrowed from a boatyard and a lie ready for anyone who asked. Nobody asked. Rivergate at that hour belongs to the rivermen and the cats, and neither group is inclined to testify.

The lock fought me for eleven minutes. I know because I counted, and because counting kept me from thinking about what eleven minutes meant if a cutter patrol came down the alley. On the twelfth minute the wards gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and the door swung into darkness that smelled of wet stone and lamp oil.

Three cells. In the first, a surveyor named Aldous Finch snored like a landslide. In the second, his colleague Marta Voss sat awake, sharpening a spoon against the wall, and looked at me with the flat calm of a woman who has already decided how she'll spend her last hour.

"Underhill," she said. "You're late."

"I got hit with a rifle butt. It slowed my paperwork." I worked the third cell's lock while she watched. "Where are they keeping the ledgers?"

"Evidence room. Ground floor, east side. There's a clerk who sleeps at his desk — slept through my arrest, anyway, and I was loud."

"You're always loud."

"And yet here we both are." She nodded at the third cell, where Wren lay curled on the bare plank bed, awake — of course she was awake; Wren Calloway slept the way rivers freeze, only on the surface — and watching me with an expression I couldn't read by lamplight and didn't trust myself to read at all.

"You came back," she said.

"You say that like it was a hard decision."

"It was. I watched you make it." She stood, stiff, and put her hand flat against the bars between us. Her fingers were cold through the iron. "Felix. Once we start this, it doesn't stop at the courthouse. You understand that. Harrow won't forgive it twice."

"Harrow doesn't forgive anything. He settles accounts." I got the cell open. "I'm done being settled."

She kissed me then, quick and cold and certain, the way you drink from a stream you know you won't pass again, and if that sounds romantic, it wasn't entirely. It was also a signature. A contract signed in the only ink either of us trusted.

Then we went to steal the evidence.

* * *

The clerk did sleep at his desk, head tipped back, mouth open, dreaming whatever clerks dream. The evidence room key hung on a board behind him, labeled in a hand so neat it felt like mockery. I lifted it, and Wren — who had insisted on coming despite Marta's argument that she should run while the running was possible — went past me to the shelf where the oilcloth bundles sat in tidy rows, and stopped.

"They rewrapped them," she said softly. "Different string."

"Does it matter?"

"No. It matters that they looked." She untied the bundle, checked the ledger inside against some memory of its own spine and watermarks, and nodded once. "All present. They didn't dare destroy them — too many people knew they existed. So they kept them close and planned to lose them legally instead." She smiled without any humor in it. "You can forge a survey, Felix. You can't forge a court."

"That's what we're about to find out."

Dawn was coming up gray over the river when we climbed the courthouse steps from the inside, and the permits expired at first light. Not metaphorically — the concession language was brutal and precise, and Harrow's whole machine ran on precision. If the territorial magistrate didn't hear challenge before the sun cleared the far bluffs, the land belonged to the consortium, the settlements became trespassers, and everything Wren and five exhausted scientists had bled for would be a footnote in somebody's expense report.

The courtroom wasn't full. Courtrooms at dawn never are. But Magistrate Okonjo was there — she took petitions at first light during winter term, because she said the law shouldn't keep banker's hours — and so, God help us, was Harrow.

He stood near the rail in a good wool coat, hands clasped behind him, and when he saw me his face did something complicated and brief, like weather over the flats, and then settled into disappointment. That was always his best weapon. Harrow could make you feel like a child who'd broken a dish.

"Felix," he said quietly. "There's still time to be sensible."

"There usually is," I said. "That's what makes it expensive."

I laid the ledgers on the rail before the bench and told the courtroom what I knew. All of it. The falsified assays. The original field notes versus the filed reports, page by page, numbers I could recite because a thief had made me learn them by firelight. The canyon camp staged to look abandoned. The cutters hired to retrieve and silence. My own name, my own badge, offered back to me as payment for delivering Wren Calloway in irons.

My voice held. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only here: my voice held, though my hands didn't, and though every word cost me something specific. When I said my father's name — Elias Underhill, plot forty-one, Coriander Wells cemetery, held against debt these nine years — it cost the most. Because I understood, saying it aloud, that I was spending him. Trading the only grave I could afford for strangers on a river I'd never lived on.

Magistrate Okonjo listened the way stone listens, and then she began turning pages, and the room filled with the small dry sound of paper moving faster than snowfall.

Harrow's men came in at the quarter hour. Four of them, trying to look like spectators, failing the way hired men always fail at looking casual. Harrow raised one hand — not toward violence, never that, Harrow's hands stayed clean the way ledgers stay balanced, by arithmetic rather than honesty — and said, "Magistrate, this woman is a confessed accomplice. She admits to breaking into a lawful facility—"

"She admits to testifying," Okonjo said, not looking up. "Sit down, Mr. Harrow. The permits expire at sunrise, and I intend to rule before they do."

The sun cleared the bluffs. I watched it happen through the tall window, light coming up the river like something flooding, and Okonjo read her finding into it: the surveys suspended, the permits void pending inquiry, the ledgers entered into evidence, warrants issued — including, she added, glancing at Harrow over her spectacles, "for any party found to have employed force against witnesses."

The four spectators left quickly. Harrow stayed a moment longer. He looked at me across the emptying room, and whatever he saw in my face made him stop reaching for disappointment.

"Your father's plot," he said. "You understand what happens now."

"I do."

"He'll be moved to the common ground. Unmarked, after ninety days."

"I know." And I found, to my own surprise, that the grief was real but it wasn't the biggest thing in me anymore. It was just grief, sitting where grief sits, while something larger stood around it. "He taught me to read water, Mr. Harrow. Before the consortium bought his debts. He'd have testified too, if anyone had ever asked him what he knew about the river. I'm asking for both of us."

Harrow left. The doors swung. And Wren, who had stood through all of it at my shoulder without touching me, finally took my hand in front of the magistrate and the clerk and the two drowsy bailiffs, as if the whole territory needed to witness it.

Okonjo looked at our joined hands, then at me, and stripped my badge off the table where I'd laid it beside the ledgers.

"This stays with the court," she said. "Along with your standing in the service. What you did this morning was illegal, Ranger Underhill. Nearly all of it." She paused, and something moved behind the stone of her face — not quite a smile, but related to one, the way a tributary is related to a river. "It was also the first honest testimony this office has heard in a year. Get out of my courtroom before I have to decide which fact matters more."

We got out.

* * *

On the courthouse steps, the river ran gold with morning, and the settlement boats were already putting out — word travels fast when it means people keep their homes — and someone on the nearest barge was ringing a bell like a fool, like joy, like a thing that hadn't been allowed to ring in years.

Wren leaned into me, cracked rib and all, and I let her take some of my weight because she offered and because I was tired of carrying things alone.

"So," she said. "No badge. No debt. No grave. No job." She counted them off against my fingers, folding each one down. "You're completely ruined."

"Completely."

"The settlement's offering berths to anyone who can read water. Terrible pay. Honest work." She looked up at me, and the morning caught the crooked grin I'd chased across two years of flats and canyons, finally standing still. "There's a barge leaving on the afternoon current. Survey boat. The captain's a known criminal."

"What's she paying?"

"Everything she's got."

I looked at the river — at the current taking the ice downstream, patient and unstoppable, going exactly where it intended — and folded my last finger closed myself.

"Then I suppose," I said, "I'd better learn the boat."

Behind us, in the courtroom, the clerk was already stamping papers. Ahead of us, the bell kept ringing, and the current ran on, and for the first time in nine years, every hour of my life belonged to me.
