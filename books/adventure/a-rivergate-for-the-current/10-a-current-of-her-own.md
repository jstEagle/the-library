## 10. A Current of Her Own

### Felix

The magistrate's clerk read the suspension order aloud twice, because the first time nobody in the Rivergate courthouse believed him.

"Suspended pending inquiry," he said, and then, as if the words needed a second coat: "All concession permits in the Redglass drainage. Suspended. Pending inquiry."

The room did not cheer. That surprised me. I'd expected cheering — I'd earned some cheering, I figured, what with the testimony and the broken lockup door and the ledgers spread across the magistrate's bench like a hand of winning cards. But the river people in the gallery just sat very still, the way you sit when a long pain finally stops and you don't trust it yet. An old woman with hands like braided rope reached over and touched the sleeve of the man beside her, and he covered her fingers with his own, and that was the whole celebration. Two people touching, careful, like the moment might spook.

Harrow's men had already gone. They'd left during my testimony, one by one, quiet as men leaving a funeral they'd rather not be seen at. Harrow himself stayed to the end. He looked at me on his way out — not angry, which would have been easier. He looked at me the way a man looks at a ledger entry he can't reconcile, and then he put on his hat and walked into the rain, and somewhere behind him the consortium's claim on my father's grave dissolved like salt in current.

Debt-free. Jobless. Badge surrendered on the magistrate's desk between the inkwell and the forged survey reports, where it lay looking smaller than I remembered it being.

I stood outside the courthouse for a while under the eaves, watching the river. The Coriander runs wide and brown through Rivergate this time of year, carrying snowmelt and silt and the occasional drowned fence post from somebody's upstream misfortune. Two years I'd chased a thief along rivers like this one. It seemed only polite to finally look at one.

"You're going to catch your death standing there looking noble."

Wren Calloway came down the courthouse steps with her collar up and her hands in her pockets, moving like she still wasn't entirely sure no one was going to arrest her. There was a bruise going yellow-green along her jaw from Stonebridge, and she wore it like a medal she hadn't asked for.

"I'm not being noble," I said. "I'm being unemployed. It looks similar from a distance."

"Mm." She stopped beside me, close enough that our shoulders almost touched. "The barge leaves on the morning tide."

"The barge."

*The Kestrel's Ledger.* Her survey boat, impounded these two years, released that morning by the same inquiry that had suspended the permits. She'd named it before I ever knew her, back when she was an honest cartographer instead of a dishonest one, and she'd told me once — firelight, White Pass, both of us too tired to lie — that she'd stolen the ledgers partly because the name had started to feel like a dare.

"Six berths," she said. "Five taken. The river settlements want honest charts — depths, channels, ferry crossings, all of it, so when the consortium comes sniffing again there's paper on their side too. It doesn't pay much."

"It pays nothing, you mean."

"It pays in fish," she said. "And gratitude, which spoils faster."

I watched the water a while longer. My whole life I'd been paid in obligations — the grave plot, the badge, the debt that fit me like a coat I couldn't take off indoors. A berth on a leaky survey barge, salaried in fish, was the first thing anyone had ever offered me that I could refuse.

That was the part that made me say yes, if you want the truth of it. Not the fish. Not even her. The fact that I could say no, and she'd have shrugged and pushed off without me, and the world would have gone on turning either way.

"I get the stern bunk," I said. "You snore like a landslide."

"I do not snore."

"Wren. I spent two years listening to you breathe through walls. I have *documentation*."

She laughed — really laughed, the sound cracking out of her like ice off a hull — and pulled her collar higher against the rain, and we stood there together under the eaves while the river went about its business below us, unmeasured, unmapped, minding nobody.

* * *

### Wren

Here is what nobody tells you about getting what you wanted: it takes weeks to stop checking whether it's still there.

For two years I slept with the ledgers under my head, or in my coat, or strapped to my chest in a oilcloth wrap when there was no other place. I woke in the hidden camp above the clouds with my hand already on them. I woke in the consortium lockup with my wrists hurting from reaching for them. And now I wake on the *Kestrel's Ledger*, in a proper bunk, in a cabin that smells of tar and river, and my hand goes out into empty air and finds nothing, and for three heartbeats every morning I think: *stolen again.*

Then I remember. Given up. Freely, publicly, into the hands of the territorial inquiry, with Felix Underhill standing beside me on the courthouse steps like a woman planting a flag.

The ledgers are safer in the magistrate's vault than they ever were with me. I know this. Knowing it and believing it are different animals, and the second one is slower to tame.

Felix is on deck. She's always on deck before me now, which I suspect is deliberate — the ranger's habit of taking first watch repurposed, because habits like hers don't die, they just change employers. I come up through the hatch with coffee and find her at the bow with the sounding line coiled at her feet, squinting at the far bank where the morning fog is peeling off the water in rags.

"Report," I say, handing her a cup.

"Channel shifts east of the willow bar," she says. "Maybe four feet of new shoal since the spring chart. Somebody's ferry is going to have opinions about that." She drinks. Considers. "Also there's a heron who's been judging me since dawn, and I want it noted that I've done nothing to deserve it."

"Noted in the log." I lean on the rail next to her. Below us the river slides past, brown and patient, and somewhere under it lies the channel we'll spend the day measuring, foot by honest foot. "You know the crew's started a pool on how long you stay."

She doesn't look at me, but I feel her go still. "And?"

"And Marta's got two months. Old Sef has you gone by the first hard frost. The twins bet you'd quit inside a week and they lost their money already, so honestly the pool's mostly settled."

"What did *you* bet?"

"I didn't bet." I turn and look at her properly. Rain-faded ranger's coat, hair escaping its braid, a face that has learned to be wary of good weather. "I don't gamble on things I'm not prepared to lose."

Something moves through her expression — quick, quickly mastered, but I've spent two years reading this woman across canyons and courthouses, and I caught it. She looks back at the river.

"Frost is late this year," she said.

"That's what I heard."

* * *

### Felix

We worked the willow bar all morning, and I will tell you plainly: it was the best day's work I have ever done.

Not the hardest — the pass in the storm was harder, and the testimony was harder in a way that left marks. But there is a particular pleasure in measuring something true. Every sounding I dropped, every depth I called out and Wren recorded in her clean surveyor's hand, went into a chart that would keep some ferryman's children dry. No quota. No superintendent. No debt accruing quietly underneath everything like water under ice. Just the line, the lead, the river, and the numbers coming out the way the numbers actually were.

I hadn't known how much of my life had been spent making false numbers come out right. Standing on the bow with wet rope burning my palms, I felt like a woman who'd been walking slightly uphill her whole life and had only now noticed the grade.

At noon we tied up at Ferry Landing, one of the river settlements the falsified surveys would have drowned under a reservoir. The people came down to the barge the way they'd stood in the courthouse — carefully, not trusting it yet. But they came. An old man brought us smoked trout. Children hung over the rail asking if we were the ones who'd beaten the mining men, and Wren told them no, we were the ones who drew maps, which is better, because maps last longer than fights. A woman pressed a jar of honey into my hands and said, "You're the one who gave back the papers," and before I could correct her — it was Wren who took them, it was the surveyors who kept them, I'd only carried them and broken a door — she'd gone, and I stood holding honey I hadn't earned from a debt I no longer had.

Wren appeared at my elbow. "Let them," she said quietly.

"They've got the wrong thief."

"They've got the right one." She looked out at the landing — the drying nets, the stilt houses, the church bell with a crack in it that rang flat and nobody minded. "Two years I ran from you, Underhill. You want to know the worst part? Part of me hoped you'd catch me. Because you were the only person chasing me who seemed like she might listen."

"I shot at you twice."

"You missed twice. That's practically a conversation."

* * *

### Wren

Evening. We made camp on the barge, moored midstream off the willow bar, because Felix wanted a second set of soundings at slack water and because neither of us was in any hurry to be anywhere else.

The crew turned in early. Marta took the middle watch. And Felix and I ended up in the stern with a lantern and the last of the coffee, our backs against the rail, watching the first stars come out over the Redglass Range — distant now, blue-black, wearing their new snow like a fresh bandage.

"Does it hurt?" I asked her. "Looking at them."

She considered it honestly, which is one of the thousand things I never got to learn about her while she was chasing me. "Less every day," she said. "My father's up there under a stone the consortium paid for. For a while I thought giving that up meant giving him up." She turned her cup slowly. "But he taught me to read water. Before any of it. Before the debt, before the badge. He'd have liked this boat."

"He'd have liked the fish salary."

"He'd have thought I was an idiot."

"He'd have been right," I said, and she laughed, low, and leaned until her shoulder rested against mine, and neither of us moved away, and the river moved for both of us.

Later — much later, when the lantern had burned to a bead and Marta had come off watch grumbling about the cold — Felix spoke again, half-asleep, in the voice people use when they've stopped guarding the border between thinking and saying.

"There's a stretch above the forks," she murmured. "Old charts mark it as rapids. Ferryman at the Landing says it's been navigable since the quake, six years back. Nobody's surveyed it because nobody official cared."

I opened one eye. "Are you proposing an expedition, Ranger?"

"I'm proposing we draw a map," she said, "of something that's actually there."

I lay back against the rail and looked up at the stars doing their slow wheeling over the range, and I thought about the ledgers sleeping safe in a magistrate's vault, and the debt dissolved, and the woman beside me breathing easy — no snoring, whatever she claims — and I thought: *this is what it weighs, a life with nothing owed in it. It weighs exactly as much as you choose to carry.*

"All right," I said. "Above the forks. First clear week."

She was already asleep. But I'd swear the corner of her mouth moved, and the river carried us gently toward morning, and for the first time in two years I fell asleep without reaching for anything under my pillow.
