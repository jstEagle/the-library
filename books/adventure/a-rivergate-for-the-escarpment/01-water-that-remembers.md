## 1. Water That Remembers

The Rivergate in flood season was a liar with a beautiful face.

Cyrus Kavanagh had learned that the hard way, eleven years and two countries ago, and she repeated the lesson to herself every morning since like a prayer she didn't believe in but found useful anyway: *the river is not your friend, it is a colleague with poor boundaries.*

She stood at the tiller of the *Marrow*, her flat-bottomed cargo skiff, riding a brown torrent that had swallowed the channel markers sometime last week. The monsoon was late this year—everybody downstream said so, the way people say things they want to be wrong about—and when it finally arrived it came down the escarpment all at once, like a drunk uncle through a door. The water ran high enough that willows along the banks showed only their heads, bobbing politely, drowning with dignity.

"Left," said the river, in its way, by shoving the bow sideways.

Cyrus gave it left. Arguing with the Rivergate was a hobby for people with better insurance.

Her cargo today was forty bolts of undyed wool, three crates of glassware packed by someone who clearly hated glassware, and a sealed letter for a customs officer in Lowmark that she had agreed not to think about too closely. Honest work, mostly. Late-season runs paid triple because most sensible boatmen had hauled out for the floods, and Cyrus had never once been accused of being sensible. Reckless, yes. Unlucky, frequently. Sensible, never.

She checked the sky out of habit. Grey, low, the color of wet slate. Rain coming again by afternoon, which meant she wanted to be past the Sennet checkpoint before the patrol boats got bored. Bored patrols were worse than hostile ones. Hostile patrols wanted bribes; bored ones wanted conversation, and conversation led to names, and names were the one cargo Cyrus never carried twice.

*Never stop anywhere longer than a tide.* That was the rule. Not a suggestion, not a philosophy—a rule, carved into her skull alongside everything else from the bad years. A tide was long enough to trade, eat, patch a hull, sleep four hours. A tide was not long enough for anyone to learn your face well enough to describe it later. She'd watched men break that rule. She'd watched what came after. Some of them were still alive, technically, in the way that a lamp with no oil is still a lamp.

The Sennet checkpoint announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke and wet rope and the particular boiled-cabbage misery of soldiers who'd been stationed somewhere too long. Two patrol skiffs sat chained to a mooring post that was itself mostly underwater, and a chain—laughably, heroically—stretched across the channel at surface level, as if the river might respect it.

A soldier on the near skiff raised a hand. Not a wave. A *stop*.

Cyrus looked at the chain. She looked at the water over it—two feet of churning brown, maybe more where the current piled up against the drowned willows. She looked at the soldier's face, which was young and damp and already resigned to whatever happened next.

Then she swung the *Marrow* wide toward the far bank, where an old millrace cut through the flooded orchard, and threaded the needle between two apple trees that were up to their chins in the river.

It was not elegant. The port gunwale kissed a branch hard enough to shake loose three apples, one of which bounced off her shoulder and into the hold, where it would keep until lunch. But the *Marrow* slid over the submerged millrace wall with a sound like a whale clearing its throat, and then they were through, and behind her the young soldier stood in the rain holding his hand up to nobody, and Cyrus allowed herself the smallest smile, the kind she rationed like hardtack.

"Thank you for your hospitality," she told the river.

The river declined to comment.

* * *

By midday the rain arrived, right on schedule, and turned the world into a grey smear with occasional trees in it. Cyrus ran the *Marrow* down the middle of the flood, reading the water the way other people read letters—slowly, with her lips moving. A boil of current there meant a sunken fence. That long smooth tongue of water meant deep channel, follow it. The V-shaped wake off the point meant something alive and large, probably a raft of debris, possibly a drowned cow, definitely not worth investigating.

This was the part of the work she loved, if she was honest, which she tried not to be. There was a version of herself that existed only at the tiller in bad weather—the version with clean hands, metaphorically speaking, whose only problem was the next hundred yards of river. No ledgers. No tribunals. No faces from the rescue that had ended her career in two countries and started her exile in a third. Just water, and her, and the small honest arithmetic of staying afloat.

The loneliness, as always, waited politely for a lull.

It came around the bend below Harrow Weir, where the channel widened and the rain thinned to a drizzle, and the silence got big enough that her own thoughts could find her. *You're good at this,* the silence said. *You're so good at this, and you're doing it alone, and you've arranged your whole life so that nobody can ever be hurt by knowing you, which is a very clever way of making sure nobody ever knows you.*

"Shut up," Cyrus told the silence, and adjusted the tiller.

That was when the river spat the chest at her.

It came out of a side slough like something ejected—tumbling end over end, dark wood flashing, and then it struck the *Marrow*'s hull amidships with a *thunk* she felt in her boots. It scraped along the planking, caught briefly on the rudder fitting, and began to drift astern.

Cyrus's first instinct was correct and professional: let it go. Flotsam in flood season was a menu of troubles. Snagged nets. Dead livestock. Smugglers' drop-boxes, which were worse than dead livestock because livestock didn't have owners who came looking. Her rule about tides extended naturally to mysteries: *never pick up anything longer than you're willing to run from.*

She watched it turn in the wake. Heavy oak, iron-banded, the kind of construction that cost more than her boat. Water sheeted off its lid. And stenciled across the top, half scoured but unmistakable even through the mud—

A seal. A crowned bridge over three stars.

Cyrus's hands went still on the tiller, which the *Marrow* noticed immediately and punished with a lazy drift toward the willows.

Vessia.

There was no Vessia. Hadn't been for twenty years. The maps called it the Corven Territories now, or the Eastern Protectorates, depending on which government was doing the erasing that week, and schoolchildren learned it as a border dispute that had gotten out of hand, the way wars are described by the people who won them. But Cyrus had grown up two valleys from the old line, close enough that her grandmother had still sworn in Vessian when she burned her thumb, close enough that Cyrus remembered the refugees walking the escarpment road with everything they owned in pillowcases, moving like people who expected to be able to go home soon.

They hadn't gone home. Nobody went home. That was the whole point of erasing a country—you made sure.

The chest bumped against the rudder fitting again, patient as a debt collector.

"You are nothing," Cyrus informed it. "You are driftwood with delusions."

The chest did not argue. It just sat there in the rain, wearing a dead nation's seal, looking exactly like the kind of thing that got people arrested, disappeared, or—in the case of whoever had last owned it, judging by the fresh gouge along one corner, a gouge that looked suspiciously like an axe mark—worse.

Every instinct she'd spent eleven years sharpening told her the same thing: let it drift. Let it be someone else's history. You don't stop anywhere longer than a tide, and you certainly don't stop for a country that stopped existing before you learned to shave.

But her grandmother's voice, Vessian-flavored and thirty years dead, said something else entirely, and it said it in the tone she'd used for *Cyrus, we do not leave things on the mountain.*

"Oh, hell," Cyrus said.

She shipped the oars, brought the *Marrow* around in a slow flooding-current arc, and snagged the chest with the boathook on the second try. It came aboard heavier than it had any right to be, water pouring from its seams, and she wrestled it amidships and threw her oilcloth over it, mostly so she wouldn't have to look at it, which lasted approximately nine seconds.

Under the seal, she saw now, someone had scratched initials into the lid. *E.V.* And beneath those, smaller, hurried, gouged deep enough to survive the river:

*IF FOUND — NOT LOST — TAKEN*

Cyrus sat back on her heels in the rain and looked downriver, toward Lowmark, toward the checkpoints and the patrol boats and the long grey miles between here and anywhere safe, and felt the tide-clock in her chest begin ticking faster than it had ticked in years.

"Well," she said to the chest, to the river, to the dead nation stamped on the lid. "This is going to be a problem."

The Rivergate, colleague with poor boundaries, carried her onward and said nothing at all.
