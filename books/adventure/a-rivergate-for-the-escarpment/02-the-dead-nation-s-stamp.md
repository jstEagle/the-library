## 2. The Dead Nation's Stamp

The reed marsh off the old ferry cut was the kind of place Cyrus Kavanagh knew how to disappear into, which was fortunate, because disappearing was more or less her entire profession these days.

She poled the *Magpie* in through a gap in the reeds no wider than a coffin, let the current's pull slacken, and set the anchor—a bent plow blade on forty feet of chain, salvaged from a farm that no longer existed—into the soft black muck. The reeds closed behind her like a curtain drawn by careful hands. From the river, if anyone bothered to look, there was nothing here but marsh: ten thousand acres of it, breathing and hissing and full of birds with opinions.

Cyrus had opinions too. Hers were mostly about the chest.

It sat amidships under an oilskin, dripping river water onto her deck planks at a rate she found personally offensive. She'd lashed it down two miles back, when the flood current had first shoved it against her hull with the polite insistence of a drunk leaning on a friend, and she'd been telling herself ever since that she was only keeping it aboard because throwing cargo overboard in a flood season was bad luck.

She was a liar, and she knew it, and the chest knew it, and the chest sat there being smug about it.

"Nobody asked you," she told it.

The chest said nothing, being a chest, but its seal said plenty. Even crusted with silt, even half-scraped where the river had dragged it over who-knew-what on its long journey downstream, the stamp pressed into the lead was unmistakable to anyone who'd ever looked at an old map. A mountain split by a road. The escarpment crossing. Vessia.

A country that hadn't existed for twenty years, sending mail.

Cyrus crouched beside it and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since dawn. Floods ran downstream. This had come from upstream—from the high country, from the drowned valleys above the escarpment, from places that had been Vessian once and were now, according to every map worth bribing a clerk for, simply *administrative districts*. Something up there had held this chest for twenty years, and something recent had let it go. A collapse. A flood cutting into a bank. Or hands.

Hands was the thought that made her skin prickle. Because chests like this didn't drift loose by accident. Chests like this were either lost by people who wanted them found or abandoned by people who were dead, and both kinds of people made her life shorter just by existing near them.

She should open the river gates and let the current take it back. That was the rule. Her rule, the one she'd carved into her own skull after the second blacklist: *never stop anywhere longer than a tide; never carry anything with a story.* Cargo was bolts of cloth and sacks of grain and crates of nails, things that didn't care whose hands they ended up in. Things without opinions.

The chest dripped. Somewhere out in the reeds, a bittern made a sound like a saw going through wet wood, and Cyrus flinched, then hated herself for flinching.

"Oh, for pity's sake," she muttered, and went below for the pry bar.

* * *

The lock was good work—Vessian work, she realized, once she'd chipped away enough silt to see the maker's mark, a little stamped mountain with a road through it again, even on the hasp, even on the *lock*, because apparently that nation had put its emblem on everything right up until the day other nations decided it shouldn't have anything left to put emblems on. It took her twenty minutes and most of her patience, and when the lid came up at last with a groan of swollen wood, the smell that rose out of it was ink and oilcloth and time.

Ledgers. Eleven of them, wrapped in waxed canvas, their spines cracked with damp but their pages—when she drew the first one out and turned it toward the grey light leaking through the hatch—heartbreakingly legible. A clerk somewhere had loved this work. The columns marched straight as soldiers. The handwriting was small and exact, the hand of someone who understood that what he wrote might someday matter.

Cyrus read the first page. Then she read it again, because the first reading had to be wrong.

It was a shipping ledger. Ore shipments—copper, mostly, some silver—routed through the escarpment crossing, consigned to mining concerns whose names she recognized, because those same names sat now on the charter-houses of the very officials who'd signed the settlement that dissolved Vessia. Dated eleven years before the war. And in the margin, in that same small exact hand, a note:

*Third quarter short again. The Vessian tolls make the northern route unprofitable. If the tolls were removed—*

And beneath it, in different ink, different hand, heavier, the kind of hand that signed things rather than wrote them:

*Working on it.*

Cyrus sat back on her heels.

She'd carried dangerous cargo before. She'd run rifles through a blockade once, in another life, in the years before she'd learned better, and she knew what contraband felt like—the way it seemed to warm the deck under your feet, the way you caught yourself listening for engines that weren't there. This was that feeling, multiplied by something she didn't have a word for yet.

She opened the next ledger. Then a bundle of letters, tied with string gone soft as yarn. Then a folded order, heavy paper, an official seal she did recognize—the seal of the provisional authority that had governed the borderlands after the war—and read it twice with her heart going like a drum in a parade.

It was orders for a false-flag raid. Dated three weeks before the border incident that had started everything, the incident every schoolchild in three nations could recite: Vessian irregulars firing on a refugee column, the atrocity that had justified the war, the settlement, the erasure. The order authorized men dressed as Vessians. It named the column. It named the number of dead required.

Someone had written *approved* across it and signed a name. Cyrus stared at the signature for a long moment, because she knew it. Everyone on the river knew it. Magistrate Corven's name sat on warrants and tariffs and settlement notices all along the Rivergate, neat and official and everywhere, like weather.

"Well," she said aloud, to the marsh, to the birds, to nobody. "That's not cargo."

Her voice sounded strange in the reed-hush. She found she was holding the order the way you hold a bird—too tight and not tight enough—and she made herself set it down flat on the ledgers and wipe her palms on her trousers.

Here was the thing she knew, the thing every runner on the river knew in their bones: documents like these didn't sit still. They were not cargo. Cargo could be dropped, sold, forgotten. Documents like these had weight the way stars had weight—you didn't feel them, but everything bent around them. People had died for less than this. People had died for exactly this, probably, and recently, and upstream, and their chest had washed down to her hull because the river had decided—no, because *someone* had decided—that it needed to keep moving, and the river's idea of a courier was any fool with a boat.

She was not going to be any fool with a boat. She was going to finish her run, deliver her cloth and grain to Halfway Landing like an honest smuggler, and at the next settlement with a deep enough dock she was going to find a way to be rid of this chest that didn't involve her name anywhere near it. That was the plan. It was a good plan. It was the kind of plan that had kept her alive for ten years.

She was still rehearsing it, with the growing desperation of a woman arguing with a jury that isn't buying it, when she heard the engine.

* * *

It came up the ferry cut at a pace no honest boat used in floodwater—steady, unhurried, the engine note of something with armor plate and a crew that got paid whether the river cooperated or not. Cyrus killed her lantern, slid the oilskin over the chest, and lay flat in the bilge with her cheek against cold planking, looking up through the gap in the reeds.

The patrol skiff came around the bend gray-green and ugly, a mounted swivel gun forward, four men aft in rain capes. On its bow, stenciled in white paint gone chalky, the escarpment-and-road emblem of the provisional authority—the same seal that had been on the forged order, the same seal that was, she now understood, less a symbol of government than a logo for a mining concern with a flag attached.

The skiff slowed.

Cyrus stopped breathing, an old trick, one that bought maybe ninety seconds of stillness before her body started filing complaints.

One of the men stood and swept the reed line with a glass. She watched him do it, watched the lens catch the light, and thought with surreal clarity: *he's looking at the birds, he's looking at the water, he is not looking at me, I am a marsh, I am mud, I am nothing anyone ever bothered to map.*

The skiff drifted closer. Close enough that she could hear the water slapping its hull, close enough to count the rivets. The man with the glass lowered it. He said something to the helmsman, and the helmsman shook his head, and for one long moment the whole skiff hung there in the current like a held breath—

—and then the man pointed upstream, and the engine note climbed, and the gray-green hull swung away toward the high country, toward the drowned valleys, toward whatever had let the chest go.

Cyrus lay in the bilge until the sound faded to nothing and the bittern started up again, aggrieved, and then she lay there a while longer, because her arms had decided they weren't ready to work yet.

When she finally hauled herself up, she didn't go to the tiller. She went to the chest.

She lifted the top ledger out and weighed it in her hands. Two pounds of paper. She'd carried pianos. She'd carried a bull calf, once, bawling the whole reach between Two Sisters and the weir. Two pounds of paper should have been nothing.

But she kept seeing the margin note. *If the tolls were removed.* And beneath it: *Working on it.* Twenty years of graves filed neatly under a mining company's quarterly losses, and the men who'd done the filing were still signing warrants, still patrolling this river, still sweeping the reeds with their glasses looking for exactly this.

If she dumped the chest, the river would keep it or someone else would find it, and either way it would stop being her problem. That was true. She tested the truth of it the way she tested ice, putting her weight on it slowly, and found it wouldn't hold. Because the patrol hadn't been searching randomly. They'd been working upstream, methodical, and methodical meant informed, and informed meant the chest was already missed, and missed meant hunted, and hunted meant that sooner or later every boat on this river would be turned over board by board until the ledgers surfaced—or until the woman who'd been seen hauling something aboard at dawn did.

They'd already seen her. Of course they had. She'd pulled the thing aboard in open water in daylight. Somewhere in a report room, right now, a clerk was writing *female, alone, flat-bottomed cargo skiff, gray hull,* and clerks were the one species of predator Cyrus had never figured out how to outrun.

She looked at the ledger in her hands, at the small exact handwriting of a dead clerk who had known his work might matter someday, and felt the specific, sinking resentment of a person realizing they've already made a decision and are only now being consulted about it.

"I hate you," she informed the chest. "I want that on the record."

The chest, being a chest, declined to respond. But the light was failing, and the tide would turn within the hour, and tide-turn was when she moved—it was the one law she'd never broken—and so Cyrus Kavanagh did the thing she'd sworn off doing for ten years.

She stopped longer than a tide.

Not far. Not long. Just long enough to wait out the dark, to read every page by lantern-light until she knew what she was carrying, because a runner who doesn't know her cargo is just freight with a pulse. She boiled tea she didn't taste. She learned the names of villages that no longer appeared on maps. She learned the number of dead required.

And sometime past midnight, poling silently out of the reeds into the black ribbon of the flooded river, she noticed the fire.

It was small, a mile downriver on the near bank, tucked back from the water the way a careful person builds a fire—her kind of fire, a survivor's fire. Someone was camped there alone, in the rain, in flood season, watching the river.

Watching, she'd have bet her hull, for her.
