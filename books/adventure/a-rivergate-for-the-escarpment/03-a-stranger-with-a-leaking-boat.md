## 3. A Stranger with a Leaking Boat

Ilya Vosk had been ferrying drunks, smugglers, and one memorable escaped goat across the lower Rivergate for eleven years, and in all that time he had learned exactly three things about surviving: never lend money to a man with a better boat than yours, never trust a river that goes quiet, and never—*never*—light a fire you can see from the water.

So when he rounded the bend at Sallow Bend and saw a campfire burning cheerfully on the reed shelf like an invitation to a festival, he did what any sensible man would do.

He turned his boat toward it.

"You're going to get us killed," muttered his passenger, old Marta, who was not technically his passenger so much as a woman who had refused to disembark and whom he had stopped arguing with around mile six.

"That fire's got no business being there," Ilya said. "Nobody camps on the reed shelf. The ground drinks you by morning."

"Then why are we going?"

"Because nobody camps on the reed shelf." He shipped the oars and let the current carry them in. "Which means whoever's there doesn't know the river. And people who don't know the river either die fast or need help fast, and I've made a modest living off both."

What he did not say—what he would not have said under torture, which in his experience of Vessia was not a hypothetical category—was that he could smell something else on the night air. Woodsmoke, yes. But underneath it, faint as a hymn remembered wrong, something that smelled like *before*. Twenty years of exile teaches a man to stop trusting his own nose, but it does not teach him to stop using it.

The reed shelf rose out of the floodwater like the back of a sleeping animal. Ilya grounded his boat—his boat, the *Patient Widow*, hull weeping gently through two seams that he bailed each morning with the devotion other men gave to prayer—and climbed the muddy bank with Marta complaining behind him like a kettle left on.

The fire was small and well-built. That was the first thing he noticed. Not the blaze of a fool but the tidy flame of someone who knew exactly how much light she wanted and no more. Beside it sat a woman, broad-shouldered, weathered to the color of old rope, mending a tear in her sleeve with stitches neat enough to make a surgeon weep. A longboat lay half-hidden in the reeds behind her, riding low with cargo.

And beside the fire, open, unmistakable, catching the light—

A chest. Small, sodden, iron-banded. And stamped into its lid, worn but legible even across twenty years and a river's worth of drowning:

The double star of Vessia.

Ilya stopped walking. Marta walked into him.

"Oof. What—"

"Go home, Marta."

"This is not my home. My home is upstream, and you promised—"

"*Marta.*"

She looked past him at the fire, at the woman, at the seal, and whatever she saw in his face made her go quiet for the first time in recorded history. "Ah," she said softly. Then, because she was still Marta: "I'll wait in the boat. But if you drown, I'm keeping your oars."

She picked her way back down the bank. Ilya stood at the edge of the firelight with his heart doing something embarrassing in his chest.

The woman hadn't looked up. She'd seen him, of course—she'd probably seen him before he'd seen her—but she kept stitching, unhurried, the way a person is only unhurried when they're very confident or very tired, and Ilya suspected she was both.

"That's a Vessian seal," he said.

"Is it?" She didn't look up. Her accent was northern border-country, all flat vowels and clipped ends. Not Vessian. Not anything he could pin down, which meant she'd worked hard to sand herself smooth.

"It is," Ilya said. "I'd know it anywhere. I used to kiss it every morning at school. We kissed the flag too, but the flag was bigger and tasted like dye."

Now she looked up. Her eyes were gray-green, river-colored, and they went over him the way a customs officer goes over luggage. "You're a long way from school."

"Everything's a long way from school. That's the tragedy of growing up." He spread his hands. "Ilya Vosk. Ferryman. Formerly of Vessia, formerly of a great many places, currently of this mudbank, apparently."

"Cyrus." Nothing else. No surname, no nation, no trade. Just the name, offered like a coin tossed to a beggar—enough to move along.

"Cyrus," he repeated. "That's not a Vessian name."

"No."

"But that is a Vessian chest."

"The river gave it to me. Finders, keepers. It's an old law. Very binding. Practically scripture."

"The river also gives people dead goats," Ilya said, "and nobody keeps those." He crouched at the edge of the light—not inside it, he wasn't a madman, but near enough to feel the warmth, and after twenty years of exile there was a part of him that would have knelt at the edge of any fire just to be near warmth and another human voice. "May I?"

She considered him. He watched her consider him, watched the arithmetic run behind her eyes: threat, asset, nuisance, threat again. He knew that arithmetic. He ran it himself, daily, on everyone from tax collectors to his own reflection.

"You can sit," she said finally. "You can't touch the chest."

"Generous terms. I accept." He sat. Up close she smelled of tar and wet wool and the particular stubbornness of people who sleep outdoors by choice. "Where did the river find it?"

"Floodwater. Two nights ago. It came up against my hull like it had an appointment."

"And you fished it out."

"I nearly didn't."

"But you did." He let that sit between them. The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, the Rivergate moved with the deep, muscular patience of a thing that had drowned better men than either of them. "Do you know what's in it?"

"I know what's in it."

"Ledgers," Ilya said. "It'll be ledgers. Or letters. Something with signatures." His voice came out lower than he intended. "Vessia didn't keep much else worth sealing. We were a nation of accountants and orchard-keepers. Our great cultural achievements were plum brandy and meticulous bookkeeping."

Something flickered across her face—surprise, quickly filed away. "You know a lot about a country that hasn't existed in twenty years."

"I know everything about a country that hasn't existed in twenty years. That's the whole problem with me." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and looked at her straight on. "Cyrus. I'm going to say something now, and you're going to want to throw me in the river, and I'd ask you to remember that the river and I have an arrangement and it doesn't include Tuesdays."

"Go ahead."

"Whatever's in that chest, it's worth more than your cargo, more than your boat, more than both our lives put together and honestly more than mine twice, because my life has depreciated considerably." He held up a hand before she could speak. "And you cannot move it alone. The checkpoints downstream are doubled—the magistrate's men are pulling every skiff between here and Harrow Ford, and they're not looking for cargo, they're looking for a *woman*, singular, alone, with something she shouldn't have. Which describes you so precisely it's almost poetry."

Her stitching had stopped. "How do you know what they're looking for?"

"Because I ferry people, and people talk, and I listen, which is the entire skill set of my profession." He shrugged. "Also because when a warrant comes down describing 'an unidentified woman of athletic build traveling solo,' every ferryman on the river reads it aloud to his wife for a laugh. You're famous, Cyrus. Fame is very bad for your line of work."

For a long moment she said nothing. The firelight moved on her face, and Ilya saw what he'd guessed at from the bank: she was tired in a way that sleep didn't fix. He recognized it. It was the tiredness of a person who had been running so long that running had become the shape of her soul.

"I don't need help," she said.

"Of course you don't. Nobody needs help. Help is for people who plan to be alive next month." He smiled, and it cost him something, because the smile was the first honest thing he'd offered anyone in years. "Here's my offer, and then I'll leave you to your fire and your denial. My boat leaks. This is a known flaw and I've named the seams after my ex-wives. But I know the Rivergate from source to salt—I know which channels flood first, which checkpoint officers take bribes and which take trophies, where the reed beds hide a hull and where they hide a corpse. Twenty years I've been carrying other people's secrets across this water and keeping my mouth shut, because keeping my mouth shut is the one patriotic duty exile taught me well."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange—" His throat tightened, absurdly, and he had to look at the fire for a moment. "In exchange, when you read what's in those ledgers, you tell me. You tell me the names. The villages. Whatever the paper says happened to us. Because I was nine years old when they struck us off the maps, and I have spent my whole life being from nowhere, and if some clerk's handwriting survived the flood and my family didn't, then I want to *read it*, Cyrus. That's my price. Not gold. Not passage. The truth, shared. Considered payment in full."

The silence stretched. Out in the dark, the river breathed.

"No," Cyrus said.

Ilya blinked. "No?"

"No. You're kind, and you're clearly useful, and that's exactly why I can't use you. Everyone who helps me gets hurt. It's not a theory; it's a documented pattern." She tied off her stitch and bit the thread. "Go home, Ilya Vosk. Kiss your flag, drink your brandy, forget you saw the seal."

"My flag is underwater and my brandy is theoretical."

"Then forget faster."

He stood. It would have been wise to stand and walk away, and wisdom and Ilya had been estranged since childhood. "You know what the worst part of exile is?" he said. "Everyone assumes it's losing the country. It isn't. Losing the country is terrible, certainly, top-five worst things. But the worst part is learning that no one is coming. That you can wait your whole life at the shore and the boats that pass are all going somewhere else." He looked down at her—at the chest, at the seal, at the tired, armored, magnificent stubbornness of her. "Well. I waited twenty years, and tonight a boat finally came. It happens to be captained by a woman with the survival instincts of a candle in a windstorm, but it came."

"I said no."

"You did. Very clearly. Excellent boundaries. I respect it." He was already backing down the bank toward the *Patient Widow*, raising his voice cheerfully over the water. "I'll be fifty meters behind you tomorrow. If pirates attack, I'll intervene. If you change your mind, I'll pretend you always intended for me to come."

"That's not how following works!"

"It's exactly how following works! Ask any dog!" He pushed off into the current, Marta clucking beside him, and took up the oars. Behind him the fire shrank to an ember, then a spark, then nothing—but he could still find the place in the dark without trying, the way you can always find the place where a house used to be.

Fifty meters, he'd said. He gave it thirty. Old habits, old rivers, old nations: none of them ever quite let go.

By dawn, somewhere upstream, the sky had gone the bruised green-black that old ferrymen dread and young ones write songs about. Ilya felt the pressure drop against his ears like a hand closing, and looked at the horizon, and said a word in Vessian that his mother would have washed his mouth out for.

The monsoon had come early.

And somewhere ahead of him, alone on the water with a dead nation's secrets, was a woman who thought she didn't need anybody—which meant, by Ilya's arithmetic, that she needed everybody, starting with him, immediately.
