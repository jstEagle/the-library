## 4. The Magistrate's Warrant

The rain reached the capital three days after it reached the river, which meant that Magistrate Corven learned of the chest three days after the river had already decided the matter.

He was at breakfast when the courier arrived — a thin, dripping young man from the Office of Rivers, which was the kind of office that existed only so that other offices could blame it. Corven ate his breakfast the way he did everything: precisely, in order, and without any appearance of hurry. Soft-boiled egg. Toast cut into triangles. The morning's correspondence sorted into three piles: matters requiring his signature, matters requiring his signature and his attention, and matters that were, in the language of the civil service, *under observation* — which meant someone else's problem until it wasn't.

The courier's dispatch went into the second pile, then migrated to the first, and then Corven read it twice and set down his spoon.

*A vessel of unknown origin, sealed with the arms of the former Principality of Vessia, recovered from floodwater near the lower Rivergate. Contents unknown. Witness reports indicate the chest was taken aboard a cargo skiff, single operator, description as follows—*

The description was useless. *A woman, weathered, capable.* Corven had known a hundred women who fit that sentence, and he had signed papers that ended several of their careers, and two of their lives, without ever learning their names. The river produced people like that the way it produced driftwood — stripped, smooth, untraceable.

But the seal. The seal was not driftwood.

He rang the small brass bell that sat at his elbow and waited. His clerk, Pell, appeared in the doorway with the particular expression of a man who had been waiting to be rung for since dawn.

"Cancel my morning," Corven said. "All of it. The harbormaster's delegation can wait in the rain; it will do them good to remember what weather is."

"And the Vessian repatriation committee, sir? They've been asking for a slot since—"

"Especially them." Corven dabbed his mouth with his napkin, folded it into a shape that would have satisfied a drill instructor. "Especially them, Pell."

* * *

The Magistrate's offices occupied the top floor of the Customs House, which was a joke the architects had probably intended kindly: the man who had settled the Vessian question, installed above the very department that had once processed Vessian trade. Corven had kept the office exactly as he'd found it, right down to the water-stain on the ceiling shaped, if you squinted, like the old Vessian double-headed river hawk. He had been offered restoration funds four times. He had refused four times. A magistrate should be able to look at the past without redecorating it.

He spread the courier's dispatch on his desk and read it a third time, and this time he let himself think about the thing he had been not-thinking about for twenty years.

The Vessian settlement had been the making of him. Everyone knew that; Corven had never pretended otherwise, and his honesty about his own ambition was one of the things people mistook for integrity. He had been a junior assessor when the war ended, one of six officials assigned to administer the dissolution of a nation — its assets, its archives, its borders, its name. Five of the six had found the work grim and gone on to other things. Corven had found it *elegant*. A country, it turned out, was mostly paperwork, and paperwork could be settled. He had settled it so thoroughly, so fairly, so *visibly*, that the coalition had no choice but to promote him, and he had risen the way silt rises in a flood — gradually, inevitably, and on the backs of things the river had drowned.

In twenty years, no chest had ever washed ashore with a Vessian seal on it.

In twenty years, no one had ever asked what happened to the Vessian ledgers.

Corven had asked. Quietly, early, in his first year, with the innocence of a man simply doing due diligence. *Where are the original records?* And he had been told they were destroyed in the war, and he had accepted the answer the way one accepts all official answers, and he had never asked again, because he had understood — with the clarity that was his real gift — that some questions are only dangerous if they are asked twice.

Someone on the Rivergate was now asking the question with a crowbar.

"Pell," he said. "What do we know about flood salvage on the lower river?"

Pell was thirty, conscientious, and possessed of the fatal habit of knowing things. "Very little, sir, in an official capacity. Unofficially — a fair amount. The flood season scours the banks. Old wrecks surface. There are salvage crews, mostly unlicensed, and there are—"

"Skiffs."

"Skiffs, yes, sir. Cargo runners. The river people." Pell said *river people* the way one says *mushrooms* — with a certain respect and a certain suspicion. "They move goods the customs posts don't see. One of them, I'm told, is rather famous. Or infamous. There's a name that comes up in the harbormasters' gossip. Kavanagh."

Corven turned the name over. "Cyrus Kavanagh?"

"You know her, sir?"

"I know *of* her." Everyone knew of her. The blacklisted guide, the woman who'd run a rescue up the Kessel col and lost four of the six she'd gone up to save, who'd been struck from the rolls in two countries and had simply — continued. Corven had read her file years ago, out of professional habit, the way one reads the files of weather systems. *Resourceful. Unaffiliated. Does not respond to authority. Does not, notably, respond to anything.* "She's not political, Pell. She's a mule. A very good mule. Mules don't open chests."

"No, sir."

"Someone opens the chest for them. Or the mule develops opinions." He rose and went to the window. Below, the capital was doing its best impression of a drowned rat; the monsoon had come early and hard, and the gutters were running brown, and somewhere upriver a nation that did not exist was trying to be born again in a cedar box. "Get me the warrant forms. The general ones."

Pell hesitated. It was a small hesitation — half a second, the time it takes to swallow — but Corven heard it the way he heard everything.

"The general forms, sir?"

"The ones without a name in the name field."

* * *

Here is what Corven understood about power, and what he had tried, over twenty years, to teach the promising ones: power is not force. Force is what the desperate use. Power is *paperwork*. A man with a sword can be fought. A man with a warrant can be obeyed, and the beauty of it is that the warrant does not need to be right. It only needs to be broad.

He filled it in himself, in his own hand, because he believed a magistrate should be able to show his work. *Whereas certain documents of seditious and falsified provenance have surfaced from the floodwaters of the Rivergate; whereas the circulation of such documents constitutes a threat to the peace, the settlement, and the dignity of the state; let it be known that any party found in possession, transport, or concealment of the aforementioned materials is liable to detention, and that all officers of the river jurisdictions are authorized and instructed to detain and search without further warrant—*

He paused over the description field. *A woman, weathered, capable.* He wrote: *an unidentified woman, traveling alone by skiff, believed to be in possession of the materials.* And then, because he was thorough: *possibly known as Kavanagh.*

"Pell. Read this back to me."

Pell read it back. His voice was very level. At the end of it he said, "Sir, this authorizes detention of — it's very broad. Any woman on the river. Any boat. The harbormasters will—"

"The harbormasters," Corven said, "will be relieved to have instructions. Ambiguity is exhausting for small men, Pell. Give them a broad warrant and they can act without thinking, which is the state in which they are most effective." He held out the pen. "Countersign."

Pell did not take the pen.

It was, Corven reflected, almost admirable. The boy had been with him three years and had never once hesitated, and now he stood there with his hands at his sides like a man at the edge of cold water, and Corven knew exactly what was going on behind that conscientious face, because he had seen it before, in others, in mirrors, in the long ago. The boy was thinking about the Vessians. The boy was thinking: *if the documents are falsified, why the urgency; if they're genuine, why the breadth.* The boy was doing, at thirty, the arithmetic that Corven had done at twenty-five and had then, deliberately, stopped doing.

"Pell," Corven said, and his voice did not change at all, which was the trick of it. "Do you know what happened to Assessor Brandt?"

"The — Brandt who was on the dissolution commission, sir?"

"The same. Bright man. Conscientious. He hesitated over a requisition in the second year — a small thing, some archive transfers he wanted to verify. He wanted, I believe, to *ask twice*." Corven smiled, and it was a warm smile, genuinely warm; he had never learned to make the cold ones and had never needed to. "He spent the rest of his career inspecting tanneries in the Salt Marches. He is still there, I'm told. He has become, by all accounts, an expert on tanneries. A man can hesitate once, Pell, and spend forty years smelling cured hides. Or he can countersign, and in ten years be sitting in this chair, deciding what the rain washes up."

The rain ticked against the window. Somewhere below, the Vessian repatriation committee waited in the wet with their petitions and their grief, and Corven felt, as he always felt when he thought of them, nothing whatsoever, which he had long ago decided was the professional condition.

Pell took the pen. His signature came out slightly wrong — the *P* cramped, the final stroke short — but it was there, and it was his, and Corven watched him make it with the mild satisfaction of a man watching a young tree take its first tying-down.

"Good," Corven said. "Now. Copies to every river jurisdiction from the delta to the escarpment. Priority to the lower crossings — the flood will close the escarpment route within the fortnight, and whatever our mule intends to do with her chest, she will have to do it before then, or not at all." He turned back to the window. "And Pell — find out everything about the Kavanagh woman. Not the blacklist file. The other one. The rescue. I want to know who she lost, and how, and what it made her."

"You think it matters, sir?"

"I think," Corven said, watching the brown water run, "that everyone on that river is carrying something the flood gave them. I'd like to know what her flood was."

Pell went. Corven stood alone with the water-stain hawk on his ceiling and the warrant drying on his desk, and he allowed himself, for exactly the length of one cup of tea, to wonder what was in the ledgers. Then he finished his tea, and stopped wondering, and began instead to plan — because whatever was in them, it had surfaced, and things that surfaced could be sunk again, and sinking things was, when it came down to it, the family business.

Upriver, the rain was still falling. And a woman with weathered hands and no country was, at that very moment, deciding whether to run or to stand — and Corven, who did not know her name yet, had already begun to write it down.
