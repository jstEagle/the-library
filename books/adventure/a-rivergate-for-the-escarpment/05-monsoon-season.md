## 5. Monsoon Season

The storm came early, the way bad news always does.

Cyrus felt it in her teeth first—a pressure behind her jaw that she'd learned to read years before any barometer could be bothered to agree with her. The river had gone strange and glassy in the hour before dawn, holding its breath, and the birds had stopped arguing with each other, which on the Rivergate was like a tavern going quiet mid-brawl.

"Landfall by noon," she said.

Ilya Vosk looked up from mending a net he had no business mending, since it wasn't his net and he couldn't fish. "By noon? The almanac says—"

"The almanac says three days. The almanac is written by men who sleep indoors." She was already striking camp, hands moving through the old choreography: douse, fold, lash, stow. "We want high ground and a roof that's given up on being proud of itself."

"And you know where such a roof is."

"I know where everything is between here and the escarpment. That's what they pay me for." Nobody was paying her, currently, but the principle stood.

He didn't argue. That was one thing she'd grudgingly come to appreciate about Ilya Vosk in the two days since he'd attached himself to her wake like a burr with a leaking boat: he argued constantly, but never about survival. Survival he deferred to her without complaint, as if her competence were a fact of weather rather than a matter of opinion. Everything else—the ledgers, the seal, the question of who deserved to carry them—he contested hourly, with the tireless grievance of a man who'd been rehearsing an argument for twenty years and had finally found someone forced to listen.

They ran the boat hard up a side channel while the sky went the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom. The reeds bent flat. The air tasted metallic. And then the monsoon arrived, not as rain but as a decision—as though some vast authority upstream had finally signed the paperwork and opened every sluice in heaven at once.

* * *

The chapel sat on a knoll that hadn't been a knoll when it was built. It was Vessian—of course it was; this whole stretch of river had been Vessian once, before the maps were redrawn by men whose descendants now wanted Cyrus dead—and it had drowned so gradually that the water had simply moved in like a polite tenant. The floor lay six inches under floodwater. The pews floated gently at their moorings, knocking against each other in the dark like nervous congregation members. The bell tower still stood above the roofline, and its bell, impossibly, swung in the wind and rang—one low, mournful note every few minutes, as if reminding God it was still on the books.

"It rang for my grandmother's wedding," Ilya said, standing in the doorway with water streaming off him. His voice did something complicated. "And for the last service. Same bell. Different weather."

Cyrus hauled the chest inside, wedged the boat under the collapsed gallery where it wouldn't wash away, and got a fire going on a shelf of dry stone behind the altar, because even drowned chapels keep one dry corner—it seemed to be a rule of churches, like bad acoustics and uncomfortable kneelers. She cooked rice with dried fish and too much salt, because salt was cheap and grief made people hungry.

Ilya ate in silence for a while, watching the rain curtain past the doorway. Then he said, "You could ask, you know."

"Ask what?"

"What's in the ledgers. You've carried them four days and you haven't asked me to read you a single line. You treat them like—" he searched for it, "—like ballast."

"They are ballast. Heavy things that keep a boat honest." She fed another stick to the fire. "I know what's in them generally. Numbers. Signatures. The usual anatomy of a crime."

"You don't know." He set down his bowl. "You know the shape of it. Not the weight."

The wind found the bell again. *Donn.* A long pause, like a held breath. *Donn.*

"Ilya." She kept her voice level, guide-calm, the voice she used on clients when the crossing looked worse than it was. "I've carried dangerous papers before. Papers get people killed, and the killing never stops at the carrier. It walks backward through everyone who touched them. So I don't read them. Reading makes them mine."

"And not reading makes them whose?"

She didn't answer. Outside, the monsoon hammered the roof tiles loose one by one, each departure announced with a clatter and a splash.

He took the ledger out anyway. Of course he did. He'd been waiting four days for permission and had clearly decided that permission was a formality invented by people with something to lose.

"This one," he said, settling against a pew that bobbed faintly, "is from the Ministry of Interior. Vessian Interior, mind—not ours. Dated eight months before the war." He turned pages, his lips moving slightly, reading in his mother tongue first and translating after, like a man tasting food twice. "Grain shipments. Coal tonnage. Boring, boring—and then." He stopped. The firelight caught his face and did nothing kind with it. "A requisition order. For uniforms. Ossarine uniforms. Twelve hundred sets, delivered to a depot at Kelm's Ford."

"So?"

"So Kelm's Ford is where it started. Where they say Vessian irregulars attacked an Ossarine border post in the night. Twelve hundred dead, they printed. The atrocity that justified everything that came after." He looked up. "You can't stage an atrocity without bodies, Cyrus. And you can't dress your own dead men in the enemy's uniform unless someone ordered the uniforms first."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere in the flooded nave, two pews knocked together and knocked apart.

"Who signed the order?" she heard herself ask, and hated how much she wanted to know.

"A name you'd know if you followed politics. Deputy Minister Halloway." He pronounced it carefully, the Ossarine way. "There are letters after it. Correspondence. A man named Brant asking whether the 'theatrical element' has been arranged. Another man confirming the 'audience' will include foreign observers." He laughed, short and terrible. "Audience. They called the corpses an audience."

Cyrus stared into the fire. She had spent ten years perfecting the art of not caring about large abstractions—nations, borders, justice, all the heavy furniture of other people's lives that you could break your shin on in the dark. Abstractions were how she'd ended up blacklisted. Someone else's flag, someone else's rescue gone wrong, someone else's tribunal deciding her name was worth less than their embarrassment.

But these weren't abstractions. These were grain shipments. Somebody had counted sacks of grain in a village that no longer existed, in handwriting that slanted left, and then somebody else had ordered that village emptied or burned or renamed, and the counting hand had probably gone on counting other things afterward, because bureaucracies survive everything.

"Read me the villages," she said.

Ilya blinked. "What?"

"The ledgers will have supply routes. Village names. Read them."

So he did. Velka Porub. Sennitsa. The twin towns of Maly and Velyki Drayov, which shared a ferry and a feud and, apparently, a final invoice for lamp oil. Kravets Hollow, population entered in a careful clerk's hand as 311 souls, with a note in the margin—*schoolhouse roof repaired, spring*—dated five months before the war erased it.

"311," Ilya said quietly. "My cousin taught at that school."

Cyrus found that her eyes were hot and her jaw ached, and it took her an absurd moment to identify the sensation, because it had been so long since she'd allowed it. Grief. Not hers, exactly—or not only hers—but grief with nowhere to go, arriving uninvited like the floodwater, seeping up through the floorboards of a life built specifically to have no floorboards.

"I pulled a body out of a river once," she said, before she could stop herself. "After the rescue. After everything went wrong. A boy, maybe nineteen. And the worst part wasn't the body. It was that I kept thinking about his boots. Good boots. Someone had bought him good boots." She turned her hands toward the fire. "That's when I decided rivers were safer than people. Rivers drown you clean. People drown you in paperwork."

Ilya didn't offer comfort, which she appreciated. He just said, "Halloway's signature is on forty-one pages. Brant's on sixty-three. There's a third man—initials only, V.C.—who countersigned everything that mattered."

"V.C."

"Whoever he was, he was careful." Ilya closed the ledger with the gentleness of a man closing a coffin. "Careful men leave the most threads, though. They always think they're being clever."

The bell rang. *Donn.* And this time Cyrus thought she understood it—not mourning, exactly. Attendance. The bell marking who was still here, who remembered, who had come in out of the rain.

"We should sleep in shifts," she said, because the alternative was saying something true and enormous, and she wasn't ready. "Storm'll run two days. Then we move."

"Move where? You still haven't said."

She looked at the chest. At the floating pews. At this man who had lost a country and gained, apparently, inexhaustible stubbornness.

"There's a tribunal sitting at the escarpment crossing," she said. "End of flood season. Corven's coalition will want it quiet, ceremonial, over fast. Which means it's the one place in the world where those ledgers would have to be read aloud, in front of witnesses, where they couldn't be buried quietly afterward." She pulled her blanket around her shoulders. "It's also a suicide run upriver in peak flood, past Corven's gunboats, and I haven't agreed to it yet."

"But you're thinking about it."

"I'm thinking that 311 people had a schoolhouse roof repaired in the spring," Cyrus said, "and that somebody should say their names somewhere that counts."

Ilya smiled—for the first time since she'd met him, a smile that wasn't bitter—and began, softly, to recite names like a litany, while outside the storm redoubled and the drowned bell counted them both into morning.

She was almost asleep when he added, conversationally, "Of course, we'll need a better boat. Mine leaks."

"I know," Cyrus muttered into her collar. "I've seen it. It leaks like a sermon."

And somewhere below the wind and the water, she thought she heard the river laugh.
