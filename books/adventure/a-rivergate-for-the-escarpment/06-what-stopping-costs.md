## 6. What Stopping Costs

The smoke reached them before the news did.

Cyrus smelled it at dawn—woodsmoke with something wrong underneath it, a sweetness that meant tar and thatch going up together. She stood in the shallows of the drowned chapel's bell tower, where they'd moored for a second night against her better judgment, and watched the haze sit low over the river settlements downstream like a lid on a pot.

"That's Marrow Quay," Iyla said beside her. He said it the way you'd say a name at a funeral.

"It might not be."

"It is." He had the flat certainty of a man who'd watched his whole country burn once already and had learned to recognize the smell of his neighbors' houses specifically. "The wind's coming off it. You can smell the fish racks."

Cyrus could. She'd bought smoked eel from those racks two seasons running, back when she was nobody in particular moving cargo nobody asked about. The harbor master there was a woman named Odile Ferrant, broad as a barge and twice as hard to move, who had once looked at Cyrus's forged papers for a long ten seconds and then stamped them anyway, saying only, "River takes everyone eventually. Might as well be kind about the wait."

Odile had sheltered her through a fever once. Fed her. Asked nothing.

Cyrus counted backward from ten, which was a trick she used instead of panicking, and got to four before Iyla ruined it.

"They're burning the settlements," he said. "Because of us."

"Because of a chest."

"Because of *us*, Cyrus. The chest doesn't have a campfire. The chest never bought eel." He crouched at the tower window, squinting downstream. "Corven can't find us, so he's making sure no one else can hide us. That's not a hunt anymore. That's a lesson."

She wanted to argue. It was her oldest reflex, older than her knife hand—argue first, feel later, feel never if you could manage it. But the smoke kept rising, patient and gray, and arguing with smoke had never once worked.

* * *

They heard the details by midday, from a reed-cutter named Tam who poled up to their hiding spot because Ilya had known his father, which apparently still counted for something even after twenty years and one erased nation. Tam talked fast and chewed faster, working through a wedge of cheese like it owed him money.

"Soldiers in gray, no insignia, which everyone knows means magistrate's men pretending to be bandits, except bandits don't carry ledgers of their own." He swallowed. "They went quay to quay. Asking about a woman traveling alone, tall, dark hair, 'competent with boats'—that's the phrase, competent with boats, like it's a crime now. And asking who'd seen a Vessian."

"There are hundreds of Vessians on this stretch," Ilya said quietly.

"Were," Tam corrected, then winced at himself. "Sorry. They took some in for questioning. Most they let go. But Odile Ferrant—" He stopped chewing.

Cyrus felt the river go very still inside her chest.

"What about Odile Ferrant?" she asked, and made her voice do its job, which was to sound like a person asking about weather.

"They found her manifests. Ten years of them. Every stamp she ever gave a traveler with thin papers. They arrested her this morning. Took her upriver toward Corven's seat at Hallowford." Tam looked genuinely miserable, which Cyrus noted distantly, the way you note rain. "Everyone liked Odile. Everyone owed Odile. Nobody's saying her name out loud now, though. You understand."

"I understand," Cyrus said.

Tam left the rest of his cheese as an offering, which struck her as both generous and grimly practical, and poled away into the reeds.

Ilya waited until the sound of him faded. Then: "Say it."

"Say what?"

"The thing you're doing instead of saying it. You've got a face like a locked drawer."

Cyrus sat down on the chapel's flooded step, water lapping at her boots, and tried to assemble the thought properly because Ilya had this infuriating habit of waiting for her to be honest and being right to wait.

"When I was blacklisted," she said finally, "I learned that the safest thing you can be is a rumor. No fixed berth. No debts. No names given or taken. You keep moving and nothing sticks to you—not trouble, not gratitude, not people. I told myself that was discipline. Craft." She turned a pebble over in her fingers. "But it isn't free, is it. Somebody always pays the fare. Odile paid it. She stamped papers for a hundred ghosts like me and she did it because she thought kindness was just what harbors were for, and now she's in a wagon heading to Hallowford because one of her ghosts happened to be carrying the end of the world in a cedar box."

"You didn't arrest her."

"No. But I moored next to her quay. Twice." She flicked the pebble into the water. "My whole life I've believed that staying invisible was neutral. Like current—you don't blame the river for flowing around things. But Corven's men are burning fish racks, Ilya. Neutral current doesn't burn anything. I've been invisible *at* people this whole time, and I called it survival, and maybe it was, but it was also a bill I kept sending to other people's addresses."

Ilya sat down beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, which he did deliberately and without apology. He'd been doing more of that since the chapel. She hadn't decided how she felt about it, mostly because deciding would require looking at it directly.

"So what does the great Kavanagh rule become now?" he asked. "Never stop anywhere longer than a tide?"

She almost laughed. It came out as something between a laugh and a cough. "That rule kept me alive for ten years."

"And it nearly killed you three times that I've witnessed, and I've known you nine days." He fished a soggy map case from inside his coat—the good map, the old one, Vessian survey marks still inked beneath the newer borders like bones under skin. "So let's talk about stopping. Properly. On purpose. At the tribunal."

* * *

They planned it the way sailors plan anything: badly, loudly, and with diagrams drawn in charcoal on available surfaces. In this case the available surface was the chapel's remaining dry wall, which Ilya assured her had survived worse than her cartography.

"The escarpment crossing closes when the peak flood comes," Cyrus said, tapping the wall. "Every year, same week, give or take. The gorge narrows to a throat and the water goes through it like a cork out of a bottle. After that, nothing gets up to the tribunal until autumn. Not us, not the ledgers, not Corven's gunboats either—which cuts both ways."

"So we have a window."

"We have a window. Four days, maybe five if the storm stalls inland. The crossing is six days upstream at cargo pace." She drew a line with her thumb. "At the pace we'll actually be running, with Corven's skiffs behind us and half the river wanting reward money for our faces? Call it five days and a prayer."

"Prayers are free," Ilya said. "It's everything else that costs."

He traced the route with a charcoal stub: up the Rivergate proper for two days, visible, fast, drawing eyes; then the cut through the drowned orchards where the flood had turned somebody's life work into a shallow lake—slow, hidden, miserable; then the last open stretch below the escarpment where the water ran green and mean between walls of rock.

"Corven will expect us to hug the banks," Ilya said. "Small boat, small movements, hide at dawn, run at dusk."

"Corven will be right to expect it. It's what I'd do."

"So we don't do it." His charcoal stopped at the orchards. "We run the middle of the river. Broad daylight. Loud."

Cyrus stared at him. "You want us to be *visible*."

"I want us to be *announced*." There was something new in his voice, something she'd heard only once before, back in the chapel when he'd read the ledger names aloud. "Cyrus, listen. If we sneak up to that tribunal and hand over the chest quietly, Corven buries it. A quiet delivery is a rumor, and rumors die. But if every fishing skiff between here and the escarpment sees a leaking ferry flying a Vessian rag running straight up the throat of the river, refusing to hide—" He shrugged, and the shrug cost him something; she could see it did. "Then it's not a rumor. It's a story. Stories are harder to drown than documents. Believe me. My whole country is a document that got drowned."

She wanted to tell him he was being romantic. She wanted to tell herself that, too, because the truth was worse: he was being right, and she'd known it since he started talking, and agreeing with him meant admitting that her decade of careful invisibility had just been formally declared over.

"Your boat leaks," she said instead.

"My boat leaks *charmingly*. It leaks in a way that says humble ferryman, harmless, look away."

"It leaks in a way that says we sink in the escarpment rapids and the ledgers go to the bottom of the world forever."

"Then we fix the boat." He grinned, and it transformed his whole face, twenty years falling off it like ballast. "You're a guide. Guides fix boats. I've heard it said."

"By whom?"

"Tam. While eating your cheese. He said, and I quote, 'the tall one looks like she could fix a boat during an argument.'"

Cyrus looked at the wall, at the charcoal line running up through the drowned orchards to the escarpment, at the little X he'd drawn at the top that was supposed to be the tribunal and looked more like a star. Her hands wanted to be busy. Her hands were always happiest when they were busy, which was the entire architecture of her personality, if she was honest, and she was trying, lately, at Ilya's insistence, to be honest.

Four days. Six days of river. One leaking boat, one erased nation, one harbor master in a wagon heading somewhere bad, and one magistrate whose paperwork had teeth.

"Here's the plan," she said. "We patch the hull tonight. We run the orchard cut tomorrow at first light, before Corven's skiffs finish sweeping the main channel. We sleep in shifts, we eat while moving, and we do not—I am begging you—we do not stop anywhere longer than a tide."

"A tide," Ilya agreed solemnly. "One tide. For old times' sake."

"And Ilya." She picked up the charcoal. Below the escarpment, under the last stretch of green mean water, she wrote two words in her own blunt hand: *FOR ODILE.* "We're not sneaking. If we're going to stand still long enough to fight, we may as well do it where everyone can see."

He read the words. He didn't say anything clever, which from Ilya was practically a speech. He just put his hand briefly on her shoulder, the way you'd steady someone on a wet deck, and went to find the tar pot.

Outside, the smoke downstream had begun to thin. The river kept moving, indifferent as ever—but for the first time in ten years, Cyrus noticed, she wasn't moving with it.

She was moving against it. Upstream. On purpose.

And far off, past the drowned orchards, thunder rolled down off the escarpment like something clearing its throat to speak.
