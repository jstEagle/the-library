## 7. The Escarpment Road

The old Vessian trade road began where the river gave up pretending to be gentle, climbing out of the floodplain in switchbacks so steep the mule trains of Ilya's childhood had needed three days and a small theology of curses to make the ascent. Now it was mostly landslide, moss, and memory.

Ilya went first, because it was his road. He had told Cyrus this three times, and each time she had said nothing, which he had decided to interpret as agreement.

"Watch the third step," he called back. "The one that looks like a step."

"It looks like a step," Cyrus said.

"It's a lie. It's a hole wearing a step's clothes. Step where I step."

"You're enjoying this."

He was, a little. There was a particular pleasure in being the one who knew things — in leading, for once, instead of following a river that did what it liked. For twenty years Ilya Vosk had been a ferryman, which is to say a man who waited. He ferried other people's goods, other people's errands, other people's lives across forty meters of brown water, and he had told himself this was patience, when it was mostly fear with a rope tied to it.

Now he was climbing his own country's spine with a chest of its stolen truth on a woman's back, and his fear had changed shape entirely. It was no longer *they will find me*. It was *I will not be enough for this*.

He stepped where he had told her to step, and the ground held, and he allowed himself one breath of pride before the next bend showed him the landslide.

It was a bad one. Half the mountainside had come down years ago — he remembered the winter, the groan of it in the dark, the whole valley holding its breath — and the road simply stopped, buried under a fan of shattered stone forty meters wide and steeper than a sermon. Beyond it the road reappeared, smug and intact, as if it had merely stepped out for a moment.

"We go around," Cyrus said, coming up beside him. She set the chest down — she carried it like a grudge, close and low — and looked at the slide with the flat professional interest she brought to all disasters.

"Or over," Ilya said.

"Over that?"

"I've done it."

"When you were what, nineteen? Twenty?"

"Twelve," Ilya said, and heard his own voice go strange on the word.

Cyrus looked at him. She had a way of looking at people that made them feel audited, but kindly — an accountant who was sorry about the findings. "Twelve," she repeated.

"The war came through here." He said it the way you'd report weather. "The road was how the columns moved. I carried ammunition for the Third Vessian Rifles. Well — I carried things. Ammunition, mostly. A boy with a strong back and no beard is invisible to everyone, including, eventually, himself." He tested the first stone of the slide with his boot. It held. "I know this crossing. There's a line. You'll see it when we're on it. Until then, don't look down, and if you must look down, don't do it while moving."

"You're describing my entire profession," Cyrus said, "as a way of life."

"I'm describing *surviving*," Ilya said, "as a way of life. You'd know."

She didn't answer, but she shouldered the chest and followed him onto the stone.

* * *

The line, when they found it, was a seam of pale rock running diagonally across the slide like a vein in marble. Ilya walked it the way his grandfather had walked it, heel to toe, arms out, humming — he hadn't meant to hum, but the old crossing song came up out of him like water out of a spring, a tuneless Vessian counting-song his grandmother had used for measuring flour. *One for the road, two for the rain, three for the ones who don't come back again.*

He was on the fourth verse before he realized he was singing it aloud, in Vessian, and that Cyrus had gone very quiet behind him.

"What's that one?" she asked, when they were across and the slide was behind them and his heart had climbed down out of his throat.

"Flour song," Ilya said. "Measuring song. My grandmother sang it over everything. Bread, grain, soldiers' packs." He wiped his face, which was sweating, which was the reason for the wiping, certainly. "It counts to nine. There was never a tenth verse. She said the tenth was for after."

"After what?"

"After it was safe to finish the song."

Cyrus sat down on a fallen milestone — a genuine Vessian milestone, its carved numeral worn to a ghost — and looked back down the valley they'd climbed out of. The Rivergate was a silver stitch in green. Somewhere down there, Corven's men were burning river settlements and asking questions with a warrant in one hand.

"You never told me," she said. "The child soldier part. You told me you were a ferryman. You told me you knew the river. You let me think you were following me for a share of the truth, like some — some toll collector for history."

"I was a toll collector for history," Ilya said. "That's what I'm telling you now. The toll is this: I'm not just helping you deliver the ledgers, Kavanagh. I need them *read*. Publicly. Out loud. In a place where the people who burned my country have to stand in the room and hear the names."

"They'll be read at the tribunal. That's the whole point—"

"Read, or *received*?" Ilya heard his voice rise and made himself stop, made himself sit on the milestone across from her, knee to knee in the old road. "I've spent twenty years watching what courts do with truth. They take it, they stamp it, they file it in a room no one visits, and twenty years later a chest washes out of a river because even the river has a better memory than the law. I was nine years old when the forgery happened. Nine. There's a village called Serevets in one of those ledgers — the one they said attacked first, the one that gave Vessia its excuse for the war. I was born four kilometers from Serevets. There was no attack. There were no Vessian dead at the border post. There was a pay ledger and an order with a forged seal and a hundred men with rifles, and one of them let a nine-year-old carry his ammunition because his own son had died, and he used to give me honey candy and tell me it would all be over by winter." He stopped. The valley was very quiet. "It was over by winter. That was the problem."

Cyrus said nothing for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was gentler than he'd ever heard it. "I know what it is to have your name in other people's paperwork," she said. "Two countries' worth. I know how it feels — like the document is the person, and the person is just where they keep the document."

"Yes," Ilya said. "Exactly that."

"So we don't just deliver it," Cyrus said slowly, working it through the way she worked a rapid — reading, committing, no half-measures. "We make them *perform* it. Public reading. Names aloud. On the record, in front of witnesses."

"In front of *me*," Ilya said. "I want to be in the room. I want the tenth verse, Kavanagh. I want to finish the song."

* * *

They argued about it that night, because of course they did. The old waystation at Kilometer Twelve still had three walls and a roof that leaked with dignity, and they made a fire in the corner where the fourth wall had been, and ate river fish and hard bread, and argued.

"The tribunal is Corven's institution," Ilya said, poking the fire. "Same courts. Same seals. The same people who buried Vessia will be the ones unburying it, and they will bury it again the moment the weather changes. You're delivering a hostage to its jailers."

"And the alternative?" Cyrus lay back against her pack, hands behind her head, infuriatingly comfortable. "You burn it? Nail it to a door? You said it yourself — a chest in a room no one visits. At least a tribunal is a room people *have* to visit. Justice doesn't come from clean hands, Vosk. It comes from dirty hands doing the work in dirty rooms until the room gets cleaner. You don't get to wait for perfect. Nobody gets perfect. I got blacklisted by the same rescue bureaucracy that was supposed to save me, and I still run cargo for the people the river strands, because the system being rotten doesn't make the drowning less real."

"That's not bitterness," Ilya said. "That's a speech. You've been saving that."

"I've had a long time to polish it."

He laughed despite himself — a real laugh, surprising in its own chest, the first in longer than he wanted to count. The fire cracked. Somewhere up-slope, an owl asked its one question and got no answer, as owls do.

"Here's what I think," Cyrus said, quieter now. "I think you're right that they'll try to bury it. I think you're right that a sealed tribunal is a tomb with better furniture. So we don't give them the chance to be quiet about it. The reading happens in the open. On the escarpment steps, before the crossing closes. Everyone the flood season strands on that road — traders, pilgrims, Corven's own clerks — they all hear it. You can't file a thing the whole mountain has memorized."

Ilya looked at her across the fire. "You've thought about this."

"I've had a long time to think about everything," she said. "It's the one advantage of never stopping. You get very good at seeing the whole route." She paused, and something moved behind her eyes that she would have denied under oath. "And I think — I think a country that comes back from a ledger is a country worth the paperwork. I'd like to belong to one. Someday. If it would have me."

"It would," Ilya said, before he could weigh the words. "It will. It's a country of ferrymen and flour songs. It has room."

Cyrus looked at the fire and did not answer, which from her was a kind of yes.

He was still warm with it — with all of it, the argument, the plan, the dangerous soft thing they'd built between them out of fish bones and firelight — when he heard it. A sound up the road, wrong for the hour. Not an owl. Not a landslide.

Boots. More than two. And the thin, unmistakable clink of a rifle bolt being worked with care.

Ilya put out the fire with his boot and put his hand on Cyrus's arm, and in the dark he counted the shapes gathering on the old trade road of his childhood, and understood that Corven had not waited for the tribunal after all.

"Checkpoint," he breathed. "They're on the road ahead. And behind us, Kavanagh — there's only the slide."
