## 8. Corven's Offer

Magistrate Corven had always believed that the best interrogations happened over breakfast.

Not torture, not the dim cell and the swinging lamp—those were for men without imagination. No, the true instrument of statecraft was a table, two chairs, a pot of decent tea, and the appearance of choice. A prisoner who believed he was a guest would tell you anything. A prisoner who believed he was being *courted* would tell you things you hadn't thought to ask.

So it was that at half past six on a gray morning, at a river fork called the Tern, Cyrus Kavanagh found herself seated at a folding table on a gunboat's aft deck, with tea, honey cakes, and Magistrate Corven himself, who had removed his coat and rolled his sleeves like a man about to prune roses.

"You'll forgive the theater," he said, pouring. "I know you didn't come willingly. But my men can be heavy-handed, and I find heavy hands bruise the fruit."

Cyrus said nothing. She sat with her hands flat on the table—deliberately flat, he noted, so he wouldn't think she was reaching for a knife—and watched him with the flat gray patience of a woman who had stared down worse than a bureaucrat with good china.

Corven admired that, in a professional way. He'd spent twenty years learning to read people, and this one read like a locked ledger.

"You're wondering how I found you," he said.

"You didn't," she said. "I let you. A woman alone on a river fork with nowhere to run doesn't happen by accident."

He paused with the teapot tilted, then laughed—a genuine laugh, short and surprised. "Well. There it is." He set the pot down. "You know, I had you described to me as a smuggler. A runner. Cargo and silence. Nobody mentioned you had a spine."

"People describe me wrong all the time. It's useful."

"I imagine it is." He slid a cup toward her. "Drink. It's not poisoned. Poison is for people who frighten me, and you don't frighten me, Kavanagh. You *interest* me. There's a difference, and it matters to your morning."

She drank. He appreciated that too—cautious, a small sip first, testing, then a longer one. A woman who assessed before she committed. He'd built a career on such women. He'd also buried a career or two on them, but that was neither here nor there.

* * *

The Tern fork split the Rivergate into the deep channel and the shallow, and in flood season the shallow was a grave and the deep was a gauntlet. Corven had chosen the spot with care. Two gunboats sat anchored at the mouth of the deep channel like a pair of patient dogs, and the escarpment crossing—her only other road—would not open for another nine days, if it opened at all.

He had done his arithmetic. He was rather proud of his arithmetic.

"Let me tell you a story," he said, breaking a honey cake in half and offering it. She declined the cake. He ate his half without offense. "Once there was a junior magistrate in a border province, a young man of modest birth and immodest ambition, who was handed a settlement negotiation. Two governments, one mountain range full of ore, and a third nation sitting on the middle of it like a hen that wouldn't leave the nest. The young man was told to make the problem go away. He did. It made his career."

"The problem had a name," Cyrus said. "Vessia."

"The problem had a *solution*," Corven corrected mildly. "Names are what sentimental people give to solutions after the fact." He dabbed his lips with a napkin. "You have the ledgers. I know you have the ledgers. I know what's in them, because I signed three of the orders myself when I was too junior to know better and too clever to be expendable. So let us not waste the morning pretending this is about documents. It's about what documents *do*."

"And what do they do?"

"They rot," Corven said. "Given time, all of them rot. Empires, treaties, tribunals—paper is a slow fire, Kavanagh, and I have spent twenty years learning to be faster than it. You will not be. That is not an insult. It is simply the difference between us. I have an institution. You have a boat."

* * *

He let that sit while the river muttered past the hull. Somewhere upstream a heron complained. Corven had always liked rivers; they were honest in a way people weren't. They went where the fall of the land told them, and they never pretended otherwise.

"Here is my offer," he said, and produced a leather folio, which he opened and turned toward her with two fingers, the way a dealer shows a card.

Inside was a passport. Blank in the name field, filled everywhere else—her approximate age, a birthplace in the coastal provinces, the stamps of three borders she had never legally crossed. It was beautiful work. He'd had it made in four days, which was itself a small boast.

"A clean name," he said. "A clean paper. No warrants, no blacklists, no two countries that bar your entry. You could walk into any port on the coast and be nobody, which I understand is your life's ambition. There's a stipend attached—modest, but a stipend—and a letter of introduction to a shipping concern in Marrow Bay that asks no questions and pays on time. You take the chest, you take the boat, you go, and you are free. Genuinely free. Not running-free. *Free* free. There's a difference, and I suspect you've forgotten it."

Cyrus looked at the passport for a long moment. Long enough that Corven felt the familiar pleasure of a hook finding flesh.

"You've been running for what—ten years?" he said, conversationally. "Since the Kestrel Pass rescue."

Her cup stopped halfway to the table. It was a small thing, a half-inch of arrested motion, but Corven had built twenty years on half-inches.

"Ah," he said softly. "There it is."

"You don't know anything about Kestrel Pass."

"I know everything about Kestrel Pass," Corven said, and here he allowed himself the luxury of leaning forward, because this was the part he'd rehearsed, and he was rather good at it. "Eleven climbers. A storm. A guide who made the call to descend with six and leave five on the mountain, because the window was closing and the six were still alive. The five died. The six lived. The families of the five were wealthy, connected, and grieving, and they needed someone to be guilty, and the guide was foreign, and foreign and guilty rhyme in every language I know."

"Two countries blacklisted me."

"Two countries *were asked* to blacklist you," Corven said. "There's a difference, and it matters to your morning—I did promise you'd hear that phrase twice. The petitions were drafted in my office. I signed them. I was younger then and it was a favor to a colleague, and I have often thought, in the small hours, about how a signature is such a light thing to lift and such a heavy thing to put down on a person."

He watched her. Her face had not changed, but her breathing had—shallower, measured, the breathing of someone holding a door shut against a wind.

"So you see," he said, gently, and the gentleness was the knife, "I am the reason you've spent ten years sleeping in reeds. And I am the only man alive who can un-sign it. The petitions, the blacklists, the warrants—they all route through my office. I am not offering you a passport, Kavanagh. I am offering you an *amnesty*. Your name, restored. Your record, sealed. You could guide again. You could stand on a mountain and make the hard call and have it be a tragedy instead of a crime. Tell me you haven't wanted that every single night for ten years."

* * *

The silence went on long enough that the tea cooled.

Corven let it. He had learned—this was the whole secret of his profession, really, the thing they never put in the manuals—that power was mostly a matter of letting other people's silences do your arguing for you.

When Cyrus finally spoke, her voice was even. Almost warm.

"Five people died on Kestrel Pass because the weather service sold the storm forecast to a mining company that wanted the pass closed that week. I've spent ten years not knowing that. I found out three days ago, in a reed marsh, reading your ledgers." She set down the cup with a small, precise click. "Page forty. 'Weather service retainer, Kestrel district, autumn disbursements.' Your signature is on the disbursement schedule, Magistrate. You didn't just bury me. You *bought the mountain*."

For the first time in the morning, Corven felt the table tilt.

It was a small thing—a half-inch of arrested motion, one might say—but he had built twenty years on half-inches, and he recognized one coming back at him across the honey cakes.

"That's not in the ledgers," he said. "The disbursement schedules were never—"

"Then you've just confirmed it," Cyrus said, and for the first time she smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "Thank you. I hate guessing."

He recovered, because he always recovered; that was what he was *for*. He folded his hands. "You're bluffing."

"I'm a smuggler. Bluffing is the trade." She stood, unhurried, and Corven's guards shifted at the rail, and she looked at them the way one looks at furniture. "Here's what I'm considering, Magistrate, since you've gone to the trouble of the tea. A name. A port. Ten years of not running. Every night for ten years, yes—I've wanted it. You're right about that, and it costs you nothing to be right, does it? You buy things cheap and sell them dear."

She picked up the passport. She turned it over. She looked, for a long moment, at the blank where her new name would go, and Corven watched her do it and thought: *she's mine, she's mine, no one walks away from a blank page with their name on it.*

Then she set it back on the table, face down, the way one declines a hand of cards.

"Keep it," she said. "I'll consider your offer. I'm a careful woman; I consider everything. But I consider slowly, and I do my best thinking on the water."

"The crossing doesn't open for nine days."

"Then I have nine days to consider." She was already moving toward the rail, toward the skiff his men had, with a certain grim courtesy, tied alongside. "You should know one thing before I go, though. Free free." She glanced back at him. "You said it like it was a place. It's not. It's a direction. And you're not in it."

* * *

He watched the skiff pull away into the mist off the shallow channel—into the channel his charts called a grave, in a boat, alone, with a chest full of his career.

His subordinate, a nervous young man named Pell who had hesitated once over a warrant and been reminded of it daily since, cleared his throat. "Sir—shall we follow her? The gunboats—"

"No."

"But sir, the shallow channel, in this water, she'll—"

"Pell." Corven picked up the face-down passport and put it in his breast pocket, over his heart, where he kept the things he intended to use again. "Do you know what she did just now? She told me she'd consider. Which means she's already decided, and she wants me to spend nine days believing otherwise. Which means she's going for the crossing *early*, in the flood window, when my gunboats can't run the narrows and my warrants are worth the paper."

He stood, and looked out at the mist where the skiff had gone, and felt—beneath the professional irritation, beneath the genuine sting of being out-read at his own table—something almost like admiration, and something underneath *that* which he refused, with twenty years of practice, to name.

"Signal both boats," he said. "We're going to the escarpment. All of us. And Pell—"

"Sir?"

"Have the clerks bring the original settlement files. All of them. If she means to burn my career with a bonfire, I intend to be standing in the smoke deciding which way the wind blows." He buttoned his coat. "And pray the river is kinder to her than I will be, because if she drowns in that channel, we will never find the ledgers—and if she *doesn't* drown, I want to be at the tribunal steps before she is."

The gunboats weighed anchor. Upstream, past the fork, the escarpment waited in the rain like a held breath—and somewhere in the mist behind them, a leaking boat with two people and a dead nation's paperwork was already moving.
