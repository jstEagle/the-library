## 9. The Crossing

The river told them first.

Cyrus felt it through the hull before she heard it—a low, grinding hum, like the Rivergate clearing its throat. She'd run water for fifteen years, and she knew that sound the way other people knew their mothers' voices. It meant the escarpment gorge was full, which meant the crossing was open, which meant they had perhaps six hours before the peak flood shut it like a door.

"Six hours," she said, and Ilya, crouched over the chest with his hands wrapped around a mug of chicory he hadn't touched, looked up at her like a man being handed a sentence.

"You say that like it's good news."

"It's the only news." She kicked the last of the driftwood off their fire and watched the embers die in the wet grass. "After that, the crossing closes until the thaw. Corven's gunboats don't close. So we go now, or we go never, and I've had enough of *never* to last me."

Ilya stood slowly. He'd been favoring his left knee since the landslide on the old trade road, and he did it now without complaint, which Cyrus had learned was its own kind of argument. He put one hand flat on the chest—the Vessian seal worn but legible under the mud they'd never quite scrubbed away—and said nothing at all, which from Ilya was practically a speech.

They loaded the boat in silence. The leaking boat. His leaking boat, technically, though by now Cyrus had caulked so much of it herself that she thought of it as a joint venture neither party would admit to owning. The seam above the waterline still wept no matter what she did to it, a thin silver thread of river finding its way in, patient as a debt collector.

"She'll hold," Ilya said.

"She'll hold," Cyrus agreed, because there was no percentage in saying what they were both thinking.

* * *

The gorge took them an hour after dawn, when the light came down the escarpment walls in long gold slats and turned the floodwater the color of strong tea. Cyrus steered from the stern; Ilya rode amidships with the chest lashed between his boots and both hands on the gunwale, reading the water ahead of them the way he read everything—quietly, thoroughly, with the particular attention of a man who had lost things before and did not intend to lose them again.

"Riffle, then the S-bend," he called back. "Then the Narrows."

"I know the Narrows."

"Everyone knows the Narrows. That's why they're famous."

She almost smiled. Almost. The trouble with running rapids with someone you'd started to trust was that they made jokes at exactly the moments you needed your jaw clenched, and some traitorous part of her kept wanting to laugh.

The riffle was nothing. The S-bend was work—she threw her whole weight against the sweep, and the boat answered with the grudging obedience of an animal that has decided, provisionally, not to kill you. Then the walls closed in and the Narrows opened up beneath them, and the Rivergate stopped being a river and became a decision being made very quickly.

Water stood up in ridges. Spray hit her face like thrown gravel. Somewhere behind them—muffled by the roar, but unmistakable, the way grief is unmistakable—came the sound of engines.

"They're in the gorge," Ilya shouted.

"How many?"

"Two. Maybe three."

Three gunboats against one leaking ferryman's skiff. Cyrus did the arithmetic she'd been doing since the marsh, since the chapel, since the night the harbor master's face had appeared in her mind wearing irons: the numbers didn't work, and they were going anyway.

"Hold the chest," she said.

"I always hold the chest."

"Hold it like it's the last thing your country owns."

He looked at her then, spray streaming off his beard, and something passed across his face that wasn't fear. "It is," he said.

The first shot came off the canyon wall and hit the water a boat-length short, throwing up a white fist of spray. The second hit the gunwale. The third—

The third found Ilya.

He didn't cry out. That was the thing Cyrus would remember afterward, in the long hours of not sleeping: he didn't cry out. He simply folded forward over the chest, arms wrapping around it, the way a man shelters a lantern in wind, and the bullet that had been meant for the ledgers—or for him, or for both, because by then they were the same thing—took him through the shoulder instead.

"Ilya!"

"I'm fine!" A lie delivered with tremendous conviction. Blood was already spreading across his shirt like ink dropped in water. "It's fine! It went through! Steer the *boat*, Cyrus!"

So she steered the boat.

The Narrows spat them out into the lower reach, where the flood spread wide and brown and fast, and the gunboats came on behind them like hounds that had finally scented blood. Cyrus could see them clearly now: gray hulls, the magistrate's warrant pennant snapping at the mastheads, men at the bow rails with rifles braced. And at the rail of the lead boat, small with distance but unmistakable, a figure in a magistrate's coat who did not shoot again, because Corven wanted the chest intact and everyone aboard it alive enough to be useful.

That was their only advantage, and Cyrus spent it all at once.

She ran the skiff straight at the shallows along the northern bank—water too thin for a gunboat's draft, water that would tear the bottom out of anything that followed—and heard the hull begin to come apart beneath her even as the gunboats slewed to a stop, horns blaring, riflemen cursing at the depth gauge. The weeping seam had become a wound. The river poured in over Ilya's boots, over the chest, cold and insistent.

"We're sinking," Ilya observed.

"We're *arriving*," Cyrus corrected. "There's a difference. It's mostly attitude."

Ahead, around the last bend, the escarpment crossing rose out of the flood haze: the tribunal steps, pale stone descending into the brown water, and above them the long hall where, once every season, the river nations sat to hear what the river brought them. Banners hung limp in the rainless heat. There were people on the steps. Witnesses. Clerks. The whole machinery of law, idling, waiting for something worth its attention.

The skiff struck the shallows with a crack Cyrus felt in her teeth. She grabbed Ilya under the arms—he swore in Vessian, creatively—and hauled him and the chest over the side into waist-deep water, and together they dragged it up the stone steps while the boat behind them settled into the river with a sigh, going home at last.

Behind them, gunboats ground to a halt in open water, too deep to follow. Corven's voice carried across the flood, smooth as oiled paper, promising warrants, promising amnesty, promising everything except the truth.

And then a strange thing happened.

A clerk came down the tribunal steps. Not toward Corven's boats—toward Cyrus. A narrow man with ink-stained fingers and the expression of someone who has just done the largest arithmetic of his life and gotten an answer he can't un-know. He waded into the shallows past her, past bleeding Ilya, and held out both hands.

"The documents," he said. "Give them to me. I sit inside those doors. I have sat inside them for eleven years, filing the settlement papers, and I know—" his voice cracked, steadied— "I know what was filed isn't true. Give them to me, and they get read *inside*. Read aloud. On the record. Not carried in by a blacklisted guide and a dead nation's ghost, dismissed before the ink dries."

Cyrus looked at Ilya. Ilya, gray-faced, blood dripping off his elbow into the floodwater, looked back at her—and nodded once, slow, the way he'd nodded at the S-bend. Trusting her steering.

She lifted the chest lid one last time. The ledgers lay where they had lain through flood and gunfire and twenty drowned years, patient as the river itself.

"Names first," she said to the clerk. "Read the villages first. Make them hear the names before they hear the excuses."

The clerk took the ledgers against his chest the way Ilya had taken the bullet, and climbed the steps, and the great doors of the tribunal hall opened to let him in.

Corven was shouting now. It sounded, from this distance, remarkably like paperwork.

Cyrus sat down on the wet stone beside Ilya, put pressure on his shoulder with both hands, and watched the doors close. For the first time in ten years, she was standing still—and the world, astonishingly, kept moving without her.

Above the hall, a bell began to ring.

It rang the way bells do when a session is called out of order: urgently, wrongly, wonderfully. Ilya laughed—a wet, painful, disbelieving laugh—and leaned his head against her arm.

"That's our bell," he said. "Do you hear that? That's *Vessia's* bell."

Cyrus heard it. And far downstream, faint under the ringing, she could already imagine the sound of maps being redrawn.
