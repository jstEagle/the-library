## 10. A Rivergate for the Escarpment

The tribunal at the escarpment crossing had been built to process cargo, not history. Cyrus learned this on the third morning, when the clerk with the ink-stained cuffs—the one who had defected with the ledgers clutched to his chest like a rescued child—apologized for the seating.

"We were a toll station," he said, wringing his hands over a table that had, until recently, held manifests for timber and salt fish. "Nobody thought a nation would be un-erased at a weighbridge."

"Nobody thought a nation would be erased at one, either," Cyrus said. "And yet."

His name was Pellam, and he had not slept properly since the crossing. She could tell because he kept re-inking the same signature on the same form, so that the letters bled into each other like a word drowning. He was, she had decided, the bravest coward she had ever met—a man who had spent twenty years filing the paperwork of a lie and had finally, in one white-water moment, chosen to stop.

The ledgers lay open on the toll table now, weighted down with river stones because the tribunal had no paperweights and the escarpment wind had opinions. Clerks from three jurisdictions moved among them with the careful, reverent horror of people touching a relic they'd been taught was cursed. Every few hours someone would read a line aloud—*requisition order, Vessian granaries, diverted south, marked "spoiled"*—and the room would go quiet in a way that had weight, like water finding its level.

Cyrus sat by the window with her boots on and her bag between her feet, and watched the river.

The monsoon was dying the way monsoons do—not with a departure but a long, sulking retreat, the rain coming in shorter and shorter fits, the Rivergate dropping a hand's width a day and revealing, band by muddy band, everything it had swallowed. A fence. A rowboat. Half a chapel roof. The water was giving things back. It seemed only fair that the maps should follow.

* * *

Corven's coalition fractured the way rotten pilings do: quietly at first, then all at once.

The news came up the escarpment road with a grain merchant who also, it turned out, carried gossip, and delivered it in the toll station's common room over a stew that was mostly barley and ambition. Corven's deputy for the southern districts had resigned by letter—a letter that had been *leaked*, Cyrus noted with professional appreciation, which meant the man wanted credit for his conscience. Two mining charters had been suspended pending inquiry. A third signatory to the old settlement had taken a boat across the strait and was, per the merchant, "discovering religion, or at least claiming to."

"And Corven?" Ilya asked from his chair by the fire. He was still thin, still moving like a man whose body had been recently used as a shield, but his color was back and his voice had recovered its edge.

"Corven," the merchant said, with the relish of a man who had rehearsed this, "has requested a private hearing. Requested. Not demanded." He slurped his stew. "They say he's begun using the word *context*."

Ilya laughed—a short, unlovely sound, but real. "Twenty years of context," he said. "Now he wants some."

Cyrus said nothing. She had met men like Corven on two rivers and in two countries, and she knew they did not break like pilings. They broke like ice: sound right up until the moment they weren't, and dangerous the whole way down. But that was the tribunal's problem now. That was what standing still bought you—other people to hold the line while you slept.

She caught herself thinking *while you slept* and frowned into her cup. The pronouns were multiplying. *You. We. Here.* She'd have to watch that.

* * *

The map amendment was drafted on the ninth day, and Cyrus watched it happen because Ilya refused to watch it alone and refused, also, to explain why.

The cartographer was a young woman named Sefa with ink on her fingers and a steadiness that reminded Cyrus uncomfortably of herself at that age, before the rescue, before the blacklist, before the tide became a unit of residence. Sefa unrolled the current survey of the Rivergate delta—the official one, the one printed since the war—and laid a strip of blank paper along its eastern margin.

"Vessia," she said, and wrote it. Just that. Fourteen letters of ink on a strip of paper, and the room held its breath like the word was a keel being laid.

"It will take years," Sefa continued, practical, apologetic. "Boundaries. Repatriation. The mining charters alone will be litigated until we're all dead. The amendment today is provisional—it's a line and a name and a promise to keep drawing."

"It's a line and a name," Ilya said. His voice had gone rough. "Do you know what we had for twenty years? We had a footnote. *Formerly.* Formerly Vessia. We were a *formerly*." He put his finger on the blank strip, next to the wet ink, careful not to smudge it. "Now we're a *presently*."

Cyrus looked at the map—the river she knew like her own pulse, and beside it, in fresh black ink, a country that had not existed that morning. She thought about the chest, sodden and seal-stamped, knocking against her hull in the flood. The river had been carrying it the whole time, she realized. Down from the drowned archives, through the checkpoints, past every patrol, to the one person stubborn enough not to stop and the one person stubborn enough to follow.

The river remembered. It had just needed someone to take dictation.

* * *

Ilya's recovery was announced, in his fashion, by an insult.

"You row like a man with one arm and a grudge against the oar," he told her, on the morning he insisted on taking the ferry skiff out—his old boat, patched and re-patched, riding low but honest. "The current does the work. You fight it. You fight *everything*."

"The current," Cyrus said, "once tried to drown me."

"The current has never tried anything. It just is. It's people who try." He shipped his oar and let the boat drift in the slack water below the crossing, and for a while they sat in the kind of silence that only exists between people who have run rapids together. His shoulder wound had healed to a stiff ache; he rolled it now, wincing, testing.

"The tribunal's organizing the first return convoy," he said, too casually. "Spring, when the water settles. Exiles coming home. Old people, mostly. Families." He examined his oar blade with great interest. "They'll need a guide. Someone who knows the Rivergate at every level, and the escarpment road, and which checkpoints are run by honest men this month. Someone who—" he gestured at her with the oar, taking in all of her, the boots and the scars and the bag that never unpacked— "doesn't stop anywhere longer than a tide."

"I'm not that person anymore," Cyrus said. "I stopped."

"You stopped *once*," Ilya said. "Under protest. At gunpoint, essentially." He grinned, and the grin made him look twenty years younger and ten years more Vessian. "I'm asking you to stop *twice*. That's not a pattern. That's an anomaly. Even your own rule would allow it."

Cyrus looked out at the river. The flood had dropped far enough now to show the willow bank where she'd hidden the chest, the reed marsh where she'd pried it open with her knife and her heart in her throat, the drowned chapel steeple leaning out of the shallows like a man wading home. Her whole monsoon, laid out in geography. She had come down this river a woman with no country and no name she'd admit to, and she was sitting in it now with a country being inked into existence beside her and a name that three jurisdictions had stopped hunting and one tribunal had started thanking.

"I'm expensive," she said.

"I know. I've seen your rates."

"I'm worse than expensive. I'm *difficult*. Ask anyone I've ever worked for. Ask the two countries that banned me."

"I have," Ilya said. "I interviewed them both. Their stories don't match, which tells me the truth is more interesting than the charge sheet." He let the skiff swing with the current, stern toward home. "The convoy leaves in spring, Kavanagh. The exiles will need someone to show them the river that carried their country back to them." He shrugged, elaborately indifferent, a performance so bad it was almost touching. "Or don't. Keep running. But the river only goes so far before it's all sea, and sea doesn't have a home port."

Cyrus was quiet for a long moment. Then: "I want it in writing that I'm an anomaly."

"I'll draft it myself. I know a good clerk."

* * *

That evening, in the room the tribunal had given her—a room, with a door that locked from the inside and a bed that was hers until she said otherwise—Cyrus set her bag on the floor and looked at it for a long time.

It was a good bag. Oiled canvas, double-stitched, older than some of the clerks downstairs. It had been everything she owned for ten years and it had learned to be packed in under a minute, in the dark, to the sound of boots on a dock. It had never once been empty. An empty bag was a target; a packed bag was a promise you made to yourself that you could always go. She had built her whole life on the difference.

She knelt and opened it.

The first thing out was a coil of rope—went on the peg by the door, where rope lives. Then the oilskin wrap of tools, the flint, the spare knife she'd carried so long the handle had molded itself to a hand that wasn't hers anymore, or maybe finally was. The false papers went into the stove, and she watched the fire take her second-favorite alias with no particular grief. The real ones—her name, her name, the one from before—she put on the table, face up, where she could get used to it.

At the bottom, soft with age, was a child's wooden boat, no bigger than her palm, that she had not looked at in a decade and could not, tonight, explain. She set it on the windowsill, facing the river.

When the bag was empty she turned it over and shook it, and out drifted a single river stone, flat and grey, that she had no memory of picking up and every suspicion of why it was there. She put it on the table next to her name. A paperweight. For a life that finally had papers worth weighing down.

Outside, the rain had stopped altogether. The Rivergate ran low and clear under the first honest moon in months, and somewhere down its length, in the drowned dark, it was already gathering the next thing it would carry—some cargo, some chest, some stranger's whole country—toward whoever was fool enough to be on the water when it arrived.

Cyrus banked the stove, left the door unlocked, and slept past the tide.
