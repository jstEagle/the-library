## 1. Miles, Not Years

The tornado was a quarter mile wide and I was doing seventy to keep up with it, which is the closest thing to a stable relationship I've ever managed.

You learn things about a storm when you've chased them long enough. You learn that the ones that look calm are the ones that kill you. You learn that the sky over western Oklahoma in May can go the color of a bruise healing in reverse, green-black and swelling, and that when the hail starts hitting your windshield like God throwing gravel, you've already made three mistakes and you're working on a fourth. What you don't learn—what nobody teaches you, because there's no certification for it—is how to stop.

The odometer on the truck read 287,411 when the funnel touched down south of Elk City. I know because I look at it the way other men check their watches. I measure my life in miles the way accountants measure it in dollars, which, given what happened later that week, turned out to be a fitting symmetry.

"Marguerite." The voice in my ear was Denny's, my spotter, riding shotgun with his tablet and his perpetual air of a man watching someone else's funeral. "It's turning northeast. You want to reposition or let it go?"

"Reposition." I swung the truck onto the farm road, gravel popping under the tires like applause. "It's got good structure. Could cycle."

"Could also rain itself out in twenty minutes and we drive home with nothing."

"Then we drive home with nothing." I said it lightly, but Denny made the little sound he makes—the one that means *you've been saying that for six years and we both know it's a lie you tell yourself at night.* Denny had been chasing with me since before the debt, back when the miles were just miles and not a ledger. He'd stayed through the Vantablack years out of some stubborn loyalty I never asked for and never deserved, and he watched me now with the particular sadness of a man who can see the water rising around his friend and has already handed him the life vest twice.

The tornado crossed the road a half mile ahead of us, and I stopped, because even I have limits, and let it walk across the wheat like it owned the deed. Which, in a sense, it did. There's a moment in every chase—the moment the thing stops being weather and becomes a *presence*—and I've spent my whole adult life chasing that moment the way other men chase God or money or women who are bad for them. The funnel stood on the earth and the earth seemed to lean into it, and the light went strange and submarine, and for about ninety seconds I felt like a man with a purpose.

Then it roped out, thinned, and died, and the ninety seconds were over, and I was just a man in a truck with 287,433 miles on it and a phone that had started buzzing twenty minutes ago and hadn't stopped.

"That'll be them," Denny said.

"Could be my sister."

"It's not your sister. Your sister calls twice, gives up, and calls your creditor instead. That's the pattern." He was right, which was annoying, because Denny was usually right and had learned to wear it like a coat he couldn't take off. "You should take it."

"I'm driving."

"You're parked."

"I'm *emotionally* driving."

He almost smiled. It was the closest either of us came to tenderness that day. I picked up the phone, and the number had no name attached, because the Vantablack Club doesn't put its name on things. That's the whole aesthetic. Vantablack—the color that absorbs light, that eats it, that gives nothing back. They'd named themselves after the one substance in the universe that refuses to be seen, and then they bought a building in Oklahoma City with a lobby so dark you needed a flashlight to find the elevator, and they thought this was charming.

I answered. A voice I didn't recognize said, "Mr. Almeida. Thursday, seven o'clock. The Club would like to discuss your account."

My account. As if I were a magazine subscription.

"Discuss it how?"

"Seven o'clock," the voice said, with the patience of someone who has never once been told no by anyone who mattered. "Wear something that doesn't look borrowed."

I hung up. Denny was looking out the window at the dead storm, at the wheat lying flat in a two-mile scar, at the sky scrubbing itself blue again like nothing had happened.

"Renegotiation?" he asked.

"Renegotiation."

"Marguerite." He turned to me, and for a second I thought he was going to say something real, something that might have changed the shape of the next six months if I'd let it land. He didn't. He said, "Don't sign anything with a river in it."

I laughed. I want that on the record. I laughed, because it was absurd, because what kind of debt comes with a river in it?

* * *

The Vantablack Club occupied the top four floors of a tower downtown, and the top of the top was a room they called, without apparent irony, the Observatory. It had no windows. The walls were covered in a fabric so dark it seemed to have depth, like staring into a well, and the furniture was low and wide and upholstered in something that felt like animal but probably wasn't anymore. The lighting was the color of a candle two rooms away. Men who spent money like water do not like to be reminded that light is free.

I'd been in the room three times before. The first time, they'd lent me the money. The second time, they'd restructured it, which is a word that means *we have decided you will pay us differently and you will thank us.* The third time, I'd asked for an extension, and the man across the table—Mr. Latch, though I didn't know his name yet, then; he'd been just a pleasant face with a pleasant voice—had smiled and said, "Mr. Almeida, we don't extend. We *redirect.*"

I hadn't understood him then. I understood him now, because he was sitting across from me again, and this time he'd brought a folder.

"Marguerite," he said, using my first name the way a vet uses it on a dog, warmly, while holding the needle. "You look well. Storm season treating you?"

"Storm season treats everyone the same. That's the point of it."

"Ha." He laughed the way a man laughs at a joke he's already priced. He was maybe fifty, silver at the temples, a face like a handshake—firm, warm, forgettable. He wore a suit the color of the room, which I suspect was the point of the suit. "You know, I watched your footage. The Elk City one. The close approach. Remarkable nerve."

"It's not nerve. It's just mileage."

"Everything is mileage, if you think about it." He opened the folder. "Your principal stands at one million four hundred thousand. Interest accrued, you're at one point nine. Your sister's medical situation we've been generous about, I think you'd agree—"

"You're holding her debt as collateral. That's not generosity, that's a hostage."

"—and we've been *patient,*" he continued, as if I hadn't spoken, which was a skill I would come to know very well, "while you chase weather for a living, which I'll be honest with you, Marguerite, is not a profession our actuaries view as an income stream. It's a hobby that occasionally pays. So." He turned the folder around and slid it across the table. "The Club would like to offer you a redirection."

I didn't touch it. "I'm listening."

"A salvage contract. An expedition went dark on the Sereno River, up in the northern territories. The Halcyon Reach. Twenty-two souls, three weeks of silence, a significant quantity of Club-funded equipment sitting unattended in the wilderness. We need someone to go up there, assess the situation, and bring back what's recoverable. You'd be paid a finder's fee against your principal. Complete the contract, and we restructure the remainder on terms a human being could actually survive." He smiled. "You'd be out from under in three years instead of thirty. Possibly less, if the salvage is good."

"And if I say no?"

He spread his hands, an gesture of infinite regret. "Then Thursday's interest posts, and Friday's, and we continue this conversation in a room with better lighting, because by then you'll have nothing left to be delicate about." He said it kindly. That was the thing about Latch, though I didn't know he was Latch yet, didn't know his name would come to taste like copper in my mouth. He said everything kindly. Cruelty performed with warmth is still cruelty, but it's cruelty you agree to, and that's the trick, isn't it. That's the whole trick. They make you sign.

I looked at the folder. A river. A river in it, just like Denny said, and I laughed again, inside this time, where it couldn't be held against me.

"What happened to the expedition?" I asked.

"Unknown. Weather, most likely. The Sereno's a difficult system. Meltwater channels, glacial outwash, the calving season coming on. It closes up like a fist by midsummer." He folded his hands. "Which is why we need someone who reads terrain fast and doesn't waste time being sentimental. Someone who measures his life in miles." He let that sit. They'd done their homework. Of course they'd done their homework; the file probably had my odometer readings in it, charted by quarter, my whole life converted into a line on a graph. "You can outrun a storm, Marguerite. Can you outrun a deadline?"

"Nobody outruns a deadline. That's what makes it a deadline."

"Then we understand each other." He pushed a pen across the table. It was heavy, silver, the kind of pen that exists to make signing feel like an event. "The contract's standard. Radio check-ins with our operations desk, weekly. You report what you find. You recover what can be recovered. You bring back our people or our property, whichever presents itself first."

"Our people," I repeated. "The expedition's crewed by your people?"

"By people we *invested* in." He said it smoothly, and something in the smoothness caught on something in me, a burr I'd feel for months without knowing what it was. "Scientists are expensive, Marguerite. Someone has to own the risk."

I picked up the pen. I want to be honest here, because honesty is the one thing I have left to trade and I've learned to spend it carefully. I picked up the pen because of the debt, yes. Because of my sister, yes—Nadia, thirty-one, a nurse with an autoimmune condition that ate her savings the way the storm ate the wheat, whose loan the Club had bought specifically so they'd have something of mine that wasn't a truck. I picked up the pen for all the reasons a man picks up a pen in a dark room with a kind man across the table.

But I also picked it up because a small, ugly, honest part of me wanted the destination. Three weeks of silence. A river closing like a fist. Something lost in the far north that nobody had heard from. I'd spent fifteen years chasing things that could kill me because the chase was the only time I felt like the miles meant something, and here was a job that was nothing *but* destination—no storm to outrun, no road home until the road existed—and some part of me that I didn't examine until much later, until it was far too late, thought: *finally. Finally something I can't drive away from.*

I signed.

"Wonderful." Latch took the contract back and blotted it with a cloth he produced from nowhere, like a magician. "You'll leave within the week. There's a boat waiting at the last navigable town—Kestrel Landing, I believe the locals call it. Operations will radio you on channel nine, Wednesdays, six p.m. Miss two check-ins and the contract voids, and we're back to Thursday's interest." He stood, and so did I, because rooms like that one have gravity. At the door he put a hand on my shoulder, and his hand was warm and light, the hand of a man who had never carried anything heavier than paper.

"One more thing," he said, pleasantly. "If you find survivors—well. Assess their condition and report it accurately. Accuracy is the whole of salvage, Marguerite. Everything else is just mileage."

* * *

I drove home with the windows down because the truck smelled like the room, like dark fabric and money, and I wanted the green wheat smell and the road dust to scour it out of me. It was past midnight when I pulled up outside Nadia's apartment, because I'd learned that if you tell her news in person she can't hang up on you, and I sat in the cab for ten minutes composing the lie.

Here's what I'd learned about lying to my sister: you don't lie about the big thing. You lie about the small thing adjacent to the big thing, and the big thing walks in wearing the small thing's coat. So I knocked, and she opened the door in her scrubs, home late, hair down, looking tired in the way that made my chest hurt, and I said:

"I got a job. Supply run, up north. Six weeks, maybe eight. Pays well."

"Supply run for who?"

"A logistics company." True, technically. The Vantablack Club owned eleven shell companies and I'd signed with one of them; somewhere a corporation named something like Meridian Riverine Services LLC existed on paper and nowhere else. "River work."

"You hate boats."

"I don't hate boats. I don't know boats. It's different."

She looked at me the way she'd looked at me since we were kids, since our parents' funeral when she was nineteen and I was twenty-four and I'd driven us both there at a hundred and ten because I couldn't stand the idea of arriving, of the arriving being the end of something. She looked at me like that and said, "Marguerite. What did you sign?"

"Nothing bad."

"Nobody says *nothing bad* about something they signed. They say *it's fine,* they say *it's handled,* they say *don't worry about it.* Nobody says *nothing bad.*"

"Nadia—"

"Is it them? The ones who bought my loan?" Her voice went quiet, which with Nadia was worse than loud. "Is that who you're working for? Because of me?"

"No," I said, and it was the first lie of the season, and it came out smooth as water, and I hated it even as it left my mouth, and I would keep hating it, and I would keep telling worse ones, and that is the thing about debt that nobody puts in the contract: it doesn't just take your money. It takes the version of you that your sister still believes in, and it spends that too, and you let it.

She hugged me at the door, eventually, because she's my sister and she always does, and she held on a second longer than usual, the way you hold on to someone boarding a thing.

"Come back," she said. "Not fast. Just—come back."

I drove home the long way. I always drive the long way. Forty extra miles of empty road under a sky scrubbed clean by the day's storm, stars coming out over the wheat like a debt being paid in a currency nobody accepts anymore. The odometer ticked past 287,600 and I watched it do it, because watching it is the last habit I have left, the way some men check their pulse.

And I thought about what Latch had said. *Can you outrun a deadline?* And I thought about what I should have said, what the honest answer was, the one I'd learn on a river three thousand miles north with the water rising and the ice letting go: no. Nobody outruns a deadline. Nobody outruns anything, really. You just drive fast enough that the thing you're running from can't see your face.

Wednesday, six p.m., channel nine. I had four days to buy a boat, learn a river, and become a man who could be trusted with the truth.

I'd had worse odds. That's what I told myself, anyway, and for the first time in fifteen years of telling myself things at highway speed, I almost didn't believe it.
