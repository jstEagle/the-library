## 2. The Silence on Channel Nine

The check-in was scheduled for 0600, which meant that in the world of the Halcyon Reach expedition it would happen sometime between 0540 and 0730, depending on whether Dr. Halloran had slept, whether the generator had sulked overnight, and whether anyone had spilled coffee into the radio housing again. I knew this because I'd been their supply pilot for eleven weeks, and in eleven weeks I had learned that scientists treat punctuality the way rivers treat property lines—as a suggestion made by people who don't understand water.

So when 0600 came and went on a Tuesday morning in a motel outside Fort Nelson with a view of a parking lot and a vending machine humming like it was thinking about dying, I didn't worry. When 0630 passed, I drank my coffee. At 0700 I keyed the mic.

*Halcyon Reach, this is Almeida on supply run. Radio check.*

Static. The good kind, the kind that means nobody's home but the atmosphere is minding its own business.

I tried again at 0715. Then 0745. By 0830 I'd worked through the whole litany of innocent explanations—dead battery, broken antenna, hungover comms officer, bear in the equipment tent (it happens; bears are curious about anything that crackles)—and by 0900 I'd worked through the other litany too, the one you keep folded up small in your back pocket so it doesn't weigh anything until you need it.

At 0912 my satellite phone rang. The number had no name attached, just a string of digits that resolved in my head to *Vantablack*, because everything without a name these days resolved to Vantablack.

"Mr. Almeida." A woman's voice, smooth as poured concrete. "We see the expedition has missed its window."

"You see that from down there?" I said. "Impressive."

"We see everything we've paid to see." A pause with paperwork inside it. "Your contract includes contingency language. Section nine."

"I don't read sections. I read water."

"Then read this one. You're ordered upstream. Alone. You'll proceed to their last known position and establish contact. If contact is established, you resupply and report. If it is not—" she let the sentence sit there like a fish she didn't feel like cleaning yet, "—you will report that instead."

"And if I say the weather's wrong?"

"The weather is always wrong somewhere, Mr. Almeida. That's why we employ optimists."

She hung up before I could ask what an optimist was doing employing a man who'd spent his whole life betting against clear skies. Maybe that was the joke. Maybe the whole Club was a joke and I was the punchline being delivered upstream in a boat.

* * *

Here's what I want to be honest about, since honesty is going to be in short supply for the rest of this story: I almost said no.

Not out of principle. Principle is expensive and I was carrying debt the way a pack mule carries ore—without complaint, without hope, without any memory of having volunteered. No, I almost said no because every instinct I'd sharpened over twenty years of chasing storms said *don't go toward the silence*. Silence on a channel is like stillness in a supercell wall cloud. It's not peace. It's the atmosphere holding its breath, deciding where to put the tornado.

But section nine wasn't really about the expedition. Section nine was about Odette.

My sister lives in Tulsa in an apartment I pay three-quarters of, because her lungs decided at age nineteen that they were done negotiating with the world, and the world's medical system charges rent on breathing. The Vantablack Club had bought that debt too—not hers, mine, all of it consolidated into one elegant instrument—and somewhere in the fine print of that instrument was a clause I'd never had translated: collateral. Not the truck. Not the gear. Her. They'd never say it that way, of course. They'd say *family considerations factor into our risk assessment*. But I'd sat in that velvet room long enough to learn the dialect. When they said *considerations*, they meant *hostages*. When they said *optimists*, they meant men who bet on disasters and needed disasters to be well-supplied.

So I packed.

Packing for a supply run is a discipline. You weigh everything twice, once in pounds and once in regret. Dehydrated food for twelve, fuel, batteries, the spare impeller I'd been meaning to ship them since week six. But this time I added things I didn't understand, because the voice on the phone had told me to expect a delivery, and the delivery arrived at the motel office at noon in two hard cases with no return address.

Case one held instruments. A spectrometer the size of a bread loaf, its manual printed in four languages none of which explained what it actually did. Core sampling gear, diamond-tipped, far nicer than anything a supply pilot should be trusted with. A sealed canister labeled only with a hazard diamond and the word *ARCHIVE* in letters that had been typed, then retyped, then crossed out and handwritten, as if someone downstream couldn't decide how much they wanted me to know.

Case two held a satellite uplink, military surplus by the scuffs, and a laminated card with a frequency and a code phrase. The code phrase was *BLUE HORIZON CONFIRMS RECEIPT*. I stood in that parking lot holding a card that felt like it weighed forty pounds and understood, with the particular clarity that comes right before you do something stupid, that I was no longer delivering rice and diesel. I was delivering questions I hadn't been given answers to.

I put both cases in the boat anyway. That's the thing about dread—it doesn't stop your hands. It just rides along, quiet in the stern, taking notes.

* * *

I called Odette from the boat ramp at dusk, because if I called in the morning she'd hear the river behind me and ask questions, and Odette's questions have never once been survivable.

"Let me guess," she said, before hello finished leaving my mouth. "You're going somewhere."

"I'm going somewhere."

"Somewhere with mosquitoes."

"Odette."

"Somewhere cold, then. Somewhere you'll call me from in three weeks sounding like a man who's been talking to himself." A pause. In our family, pauses are load-bearing. "Margo. Where are you going?"

And here is the lie. I want to record it precisely, because later—much later—this exact sentence is going to come back through my life like a ricochet, and I want the record to show it left my mouth easily, smoothly, with the practiced warmth of a man who has lied to everyone he loves at least once per fiscal quarter:

"Resupply run up the Sereno. Two weeks, maybe less if the channels hold. Boring as watching paint freeze."

"Boring," she repeated.

"Aggressively boring."

"Mm." I could hear her settling back against whatever she leaned against when she was deciding whether to believe me—the kitchen counter, probably, the one with the burn mark from the time I set a dish towel on fire trying to impress her with flambéed bananas, age sixteen, the first fire I ever started on purpose. "You know what's funny? You're worse at lying now than you were at fifteen. At fifteen you at least committed."

"That's growth."

"That's exhaustion." Another pause, longer. "Come back, Margo. Not eventually. Come back."

"I always come back."

"Yeah," she said, and the word had a crack running through it, hairline, the kind you only see in ice when the light hits it right. "That's what the river says too."

I hung up and sat in the truck with the engine off and the parking lot lights coming on one by one, each with its little moth congregation, and I thought about how strange it is that the people who love us can hear the lie even when they can't name it, while the people who own us can't hear the truth even when we're shouting it.

Then I launched, because the light was going and the river doesn't care about either.

* * *

The first night on the Sereno I ran until the channels got too narrow to trust in the dark, then tied off on a gravel bar that some previous survey crew had marked with a stake and a strip of orange flagging gone gray as old bone. I ate rehydrated something-or-other and listened to the river work. Rivers at night sound like a room full of people whispering about you.

At 2200 I tried Channel Nine again. Habit, mostly. Also superstition—I'm a storm chaser; superstition is half my instrument panel.

*Halcyon Reach, this is Almeida. Radio check. Anyone listening, come back.*

Static. But underneath the static, or maybe I dreamed it, or maybe the static itself arranged it the way clouds arrange themselves into faces if you stare long enough—something rhythmic. Three pulses. Pause. Three pulses. Pause.

SOS is three short, three short, three short. This wasn't that. This was three pulses, period, repeating, steady as a metronome, and after a minute it stopped altogether, and I sat there with the handset pressed to my ear like a shell, listening for an ocean that wasn't there.

Could have been a loose antenna wire tapping in the wind. Could have been a compressor cycling. Could have been eleven scientists running a generator with the touch Halloran brings to machinery, which is to say the touch of a man who believes machines respond to moral character.

Or it could have been a signal meant for exactly one listener—a supply pilot alone on a dark river, ordered upstream by people who wanted him to find something, or wanted him to think he'd found something, or wanted the finding witnessed.

I turned the radio off. Sat in the dark. The river kept whispering.

Professionalism says: log the anomaly, note the bearing, sleep, run at first light. Dread says: they're alive, and alive is more complicated than dead, and you are sailing toward the most complicated thing you've ever been paid to touch.

I logged the anomaly. I noted the bearing. I did not sleep.

At first light the fog was standing on the water in columns, and somewhere ahead of me, past the drowned survey markers and the channel that the maps swore was wider than it was, the Sereno bent north into country where the trees gave up and the rock started keeping records of its own. I pushed off into it with two cases of instruments I didn't understand, a code phrase I hoped I'd never use, and my sister's voice riding in the stern next to the dread, saying *come back* in a way that meant *you won't*.

Three pulses. Pause. Three pulses.

I told myself it was the wind.

I have never once, before or since, believed myself less.
