## 3. Sereno Headwaters

The Sereno begins as a rumor and ends as a fact, and somewhere in between it stops being a river at all and becomes a negotiation.

I'd read the survey reports on the flight north—three of them, spanning eleven years, each one thinner than the last, as if the river itself were being redacted. The 2014 report described a navigable channel forty meters wide at the headwaters delta. The 2019 report said twenty-five. The most recent one, the one the Club's people had slipped into my contract folder without comment, said only: *conditions variable.* Which is accountant-speak for *we stopped paying anyone to look.*

So I came in expecting a fight. What I hadn't expected was that the fight would be beautiful.

* * *

Day one was easy, which should have been my first warning. Rivers that start easy are saving something up.

I ran the lower reaches with the throttle half-open, the boat—a shallow-draft salvage skiff the Club had requisitioned from a fishing outfit in Kivaliq, still smelling faintly of char and salmon—cutting through water so clear I could count the stones passing beneath me like coins dropped through green glass. The valley walls rose on either side, granite streaked with snowmelt that fell in threads so fine they seemed less like waterfalls than like the mountain sweating. The light came down at that high-latitude angle that makes everything look recently made, freshly unwrapped, a world still in its packaging.

I measured my progress the way I measure everything: in numbers. Sixty-two kilometers by noon. Fuel burn at nine liters an hour. Channel depth holding above a meter everywhere I probed with the sounding pole. Good numbers. Numbers I could radio home to my handlers, who wanted data the way creditors want interest—regularly, and without any indication of trouble.

"Supply run proceeding," I told them on the scheduled check-in, lying into the handset with the ease of long practice. "Conditions nominal."

Nominal. There's a word that has never once described anything true about me.

By late afternoon the valley began to narrow, and the river's personality changed. It wasn't gentler up here—it was *quieter*, which is different. The current picked up speed without showing off, sliding over gravel bars that hadn't been there when the surveys were drawn, because gravel bars don't stay put when a glacier upstream is melting faster than its own geology can account for. I grounded once, gently, on a shoal that no chart would ever admit to, backed off, and gave the river the respect of slowing down.

That's the thing they don't tell you about meltwater country: the map is a historical document. Every chart of this river is an obituary for a river that no longer exists. I was navigating by ghosts.

* * *

The first survey marker appeared on day two, and it was wrong in a way that took me a kilometer to understand.

It stood on the left bank where the channel bent hard around a bluff—a steel post, orange paint flaked to pink, topped with a faded reflector. Standard issue. Except the marker was standing in the middle of dry ground, a good thirty meters from the waterline, and according to my GPS the channel it marked had moved so far right that the post now commemorated nothing but an old idea of the river.

I idled past it. Then I saw the next one, also orphaned. Then a third, tipped sideways, its base exposed like a pulled tooth, the bank beneath it slumped away in a raw crescent of thawing permafrost. The whole left bank was letting go, slowly, the way a person lets go of a railing—finger by finger, without deciding to.

Eleven years of markers, walking uphill away from their own river. Somebody had come out here year after year and driven steel into ground that kept refusing to hold it, and you could read the whole history of the place in that stubbornness. You could also read what the Club thought of it: three reports, then none. When the landscape stops cooperating with the ledger, the ledger stops looking.

I passed a fourth marker that afternoon and noticed something that put a cold finger on the back of my neck. Fresh scratches on the paint. Recent. Someone had tied something to this post—not long ago—and taken it away again. A rope ghost, a scuff, a few fibers of synthetic line still caught in the seam of the bracket.

The Halcyon Reach expedition had come through here. Recently enough that the evidence hadn't weathered.

They'd been moving fast, too. The tie-off was the kind you do in seconds, not minutes—the knot of a person who doesn't plan to come back for whatever they left hanging.

* * *

The drowned station found me on day three, or maybe I found it; by then the distinction felt academic.

I smelled it before I saw it—diesel and wet rot, the specific perfume of abandoned infrastructure. The channel bent around a low moraine island and there it was: the Sereno Headwaters Research Station, or what the river had left of it. I knew it from the reports. Decommissioned six years ago when the funding model changed—which is to say, when studying a melting glacier stopped being profitable to the people who'd been betting on the melting.

Half the main building stood on higher ground, listing but intact, its corrugated skin rusted into abstract art. The other half—the boathouse, the lower lab—stood in the shallows, windows dark under a meter of water, the river running calmly through rooms where people had once calibrated instruments and argued about lunch. A dock reached out from the shore and simply stopped, its far end submerged, leading nowhere visible. A stairway descended into green water and ended in suggestion.

I tied off at the surviving dock and walked the station alone, and I'll be honest: it got to me. Not the decay—I've slept in worse. It was the *orderliness* of it. Whoever left hadn't fled. They'd shut things down properly, drained lines, tagged equipment, stacked chairs. They'd expected to come back. And the river had quietly decided otherwise, filling their rooms while nobody watched, keeping their furniture company, erasing them at the patient pace of water doing arithmetic.

In the upper lab I found the chalkboard still standing, half-wiped, someone's last calculations ghosting through the smears. Flow rates. Dates. A single word circled twice in a hand that had pressed hard: **EARLIER**.

Earlier than predicted. Earlier than modeled. Earlier than safe.

I stood there longer than I needed to, telling myself I was checking for supplies. I took two jerry cans of stabilized fuel that were still good and a case of ration bars, and I did not take the journal lying open on the desk, because taking it felt like reading someone's mail, and I already had enough of other people's secrets riding in this boat.

Outside, the light was going long and gold, and the glacier at the head of the valley had come into full view for the first time—a wall of blue-white ice five kilometers across, calved face sheer as a courthouse, glowing against the dark rock like something lit from inside. Beautiful is too small a word. It was the most expensive-looking thing I'd ever seen, and I've been inside the Vantablack Club.

It looked like a vault. I remember thinking that, specifically. *It looks like a vault.*

I didn't know yet how literally I'd be proven right.

* * *

Day four, the channels began to braid, and the river started playing games.

Braided sections are where meltwater rivers show their true character: instead of one channel, a dozen, splitting and rejoining around gravel islands, shifting with every surge of melt from the ice above. In the morning a route might be deep and clean. By evening it could be a gravel garden. Navigation became a matter of reading the water's face—dark green means depth, pale gray means a bar waiting to ruin your week—and committing, over and over, to choices you couldn't take back.

I ran aground twice more. The second time cost me forty minutes and some pride, poling off a bar while the boat groaned like it was writing a complaint to management. My hands were blistered. My shoulders had filed grievances. Somewhere behind me, downstream, were creditors, handlers, a sister whose name sat in a Club file as collateral, and a debt measured in a currency I'd stopped understanding years ago. Somewhere ahead of me was an expedition that had gone silent for three weeks, and a contract that said *retrieve*, written by people who used words like *asset recovery* when they meant *bring back what we paid for.*

And the river just kept doing its thing—gorgeous, indifferent, withholding. It showed me a mile of glassy, inviting water and then a wall of standing waves. It offered a wide channel and delivered a sandbar. It was, I decided somewhere in the fifth braided crossing, exactly like everyone I owed money to: beautiful at a distance, treacherous in detail, and utterly uninterested in my schedule.

I started talking to it. Out loud. "Fine. Fine. Your way." The river declined to respond, which was more courtesy than I'd gotten from Mr. Latch's office.

* * *

The campsite evidence turned up on day five, and it was the deliberate part that frightened me.

I'd pulled into a gravel bench above a rapid to make camp—tents don't pitch themselves, even mine, which mostly pitches itself—and while gathering driftwood I found the cache. Not hidden. *Arranged*. A waterproof duffel set neatly on a flat stone, weighted with a second stone, positioned under an overhang that would shed rain. On top of it, a strip of survey tape knotted in a pattern I didn't recognize—deliberate, coded, meant for eyes that knew the code.

Inside the duffel: a satellite phone, powered down and wrapped in foam. Two boxes of spares for instruments I couldn't name. A medical kit, unopened. And a folded sheet of paper, dry as bone, with a single typed line:

**IF YOU ARE READING THIS, DO NOT PROCEED PAST THE SECOND MORaine WITHOUT US.**

No signature. No date. No explanation of who *us* was, or why the instruction was capitalized like a shout, or why the rest of the sentence had been deleted—there was a struck-through fragment below it, blacked out with marker, heavy strokes, more ink than paper deserved.

I sat on the gravel with that paper in my hands and let myself think clearly for the first time since Kivaliq.

Here's what the evidence said. An expedition moving fast—fast enough to leave gear they'd need later, fast enough to code their messages. Moving *upstream-to-downstream* logic inverted: they'd cached this heading downriver, meaning they'd passed here heading up, toward the ice, carrying everything that mattered and abandoning what they could replace. People don't abandon functioning satellite phones unless they're afraid of being tracked by them. People don't black out their own instructions unless they're afraid of them being read.

People go quiet on purpose, sometimes. Silence isn't always a distress signal. Sometimes it's a decision.

My contract said the Halcyon Reach expedition had suffered a communications failure. My contract said *locate and retrieve personnel*. My contract was written by the same organization that had commissioned three river surveys and then stopped asking questions, and that had sent me upriver alone instead of with a team, and that had—now that I thought about it with the paper trembling slightly in my hands—been remarkably incurious about what, exactly, eleven scientists had been doing out here that required silence.

The fire burned down. The glacier at the head of the valley held the last light a long time, blue going violet going black, and I fed sticks to the flames and rehearsed lies I might need and tried not to hear how thin they sounded even in rehearsal.

Around midnight, the ice spoke. A crack rolled down the valley like a door slamming in a house the size of a country, followed by a boom I felt through the gravel, through my boots, through my sternum. Calving. Somewhere up in that shining wall, a piece of sixty-thousand-year-old weather had let go and fallen into the water, and the river carried the sound to me like a message addressed personally.

*Earlier,* the chalkboard had said. Everything up here was happening earlier.

I looked at the blacked-out paper. I looked at the dark shape of the ice. And I understood, in the way you understand things at midnight alone in wild country—with your whole body and none of your arguments—that whatever the Halcyon Reach expedition had found, they hadn't lost contact with the world.

They'd left it. On purpose. And the second moraine was one day's run upstream, and tomorrow I would pass it, and everything after that would belong to a different story than the one I'd been sold.

I packed the duffel back exactly as I'd found it, tape knot restored, and lay in the tent listening to the river run.

Somewhere upstream, a light moved along the far bank. Small, steady, wrong—no expedition camps a lantern on a bare bank at one in the morning. It traveled parallel to the water for a few hundred meters, unhurried, the way a man walks when he's counting things.

Then it went out, and the river kept talking to itself in the dark, and I did not sleep.
