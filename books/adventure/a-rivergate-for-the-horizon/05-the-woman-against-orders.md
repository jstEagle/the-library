## 5. The Woman Against Orders

The coordinates in my contract put the Halcyon Reach expedition's last known camp at the mouth of a lateral valley that didn't appear on any map I owned, which by day nine of this river I had learned to take personally. The Sereno had eaten three survey markers since breakfast. It ate things the way a rich man eats oysters—casually, without looking up, confident there would always be more.

I found the camp at four in the afternoon, when the light goes long and honest and makes everything look like evidence.

It was a good camp, or had been. Two large dome tents, one collapsed under snow-load and one still standing like a man pretending he's fine. A kitchen fly strung between ice axes. A weatherport half-buried at the treeline. And everywhere, the particular disorder of people who left in a hurry but left on purpose—boots paired neatly beside a pack that wasn't there, a stove disassembled into its component parts and laid out on a tarp with surgical care, a jar of pens standing upright in the snow like a small white forest.

Nobody abandons a camp like that in a panic. Panic looks different. Panic looks like Chapter Three of every disaster report ever written: gear scattered, food burned, a boot floating in the river pointing downstream like an accusation. This was something else. This was tidiness as a message. *We were here. We meant to go. Don't read too much into it.*

I beached the boat below the high-water line and stood for a while just listening. That's a thing storm chasers do that civilians don't understand—the listening. You learn to hear a supercell before you see it, a pressure change in your back teeth, a hush in the insects. Out here the equivalent was simpler: I listened for eleven scientists and heard wind, water, and one sound I couldn't file.

A metallic click. Rhythmic. Patient.

Somebody was racking a bolt action rifle at the speed of a man who has done it many times recently and expects to do it again.

"Tomas Reyes?" I called out, because my contract said his name and because shouting *hello* to an armed man in bear country felt like leaving money on the table.

Silence. Then, from behind the standing tent, a voice—low, hoarse, unhurried: "Who's asking?"

"Marguerite Almeida. Supply pilot. I'm contracted to bring you downriver."

Another silence, longer. When the voice came back it had a smile buried somewhere in it, the way a stone has a fossil. "Contracted by whom?"

And there it was, the question I'd been dreading since Oklahoma, since the velvet room, since Mr. Vantablack's people slid the amended contract across the table with its new clause about *recovery of personnel and property*. Property. I'd signed it anyway, because debt doesn't leave you a lot of room to be philosophical about nouns.

"Vantablack Group," I said. "Salvage and recovery division."

The laugh that came out of the trees was not a nice sound. "They sent one man? In one boat? For eleven of us?"

"They sent me," I said, which was true, and let him decide what it meant.

He stepped out from behind the tent then, and I got my first look at Tomas Reyes, the man whose rescue was worth more to certain parties than the lives of the other ten combined. He was shorter than I'd pictured, broad through the shoulders, with the deep tan and deeper squint of someone who'd spent years being underestimated by weather. He held the rifle low and easy, barrel down, which is either courtesy or contempt depending on the man. His parka was patched at both elbows with what looked like tent fabric. He'd been out here a while.

"You're the pilot," he said. "Channel Nine. You did our resupply runs last season."

"I did."

"You missed the June run."

"The Club pulled funding in May." I shrugged. "I assumed you knew."

"We knew." He looked at me the way you look at a bridge you're deciding whether to trust with your truck. "You want coffee? I've got real coffee. Halloran's stash. He'd hate that I'm drinking it."

"I want the truth," I said, "and then coffee."

"Wrong order," Reyes said. "Truth's easier after coffee. Come on."

* * *

We sat on overturned supply crates under the kitchen fly, two tin cups steaming in air cold enough to make the steam look solid. Reyes drank like a man rationing pleasure. I told him about the run upstream—the drowned station, the abandoned markers, the gear left deliberately in the shallows—and he listened without interrupting, which in my experience is either politeness or reconnaissance.

"Eleven of you," I said finally. "Where are they?"

"Safe."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got authority to give." He set down his cup. "Look, Almeida. I believe you're a decent man doing a bad job. But I need you to answer a question honestly, because everything depends on it. Did they tell you why we went dark?"

"Radio failure," I said. "Antenna damage in the icefall."

Reyes nodded slowly, the way you nod at a child's explanation of where the cookies went. "And did they tell you why the recovery contract specifies *me* by name? Not Halloran. Not the science team. Me."

"Personnel of particular value," I said. "Standard language."

"Is it?" He leaned forward. "Because I've been sitting here for three weeks reading the tea leaves of that contract language, and here's what I keep coming back to. If the Club wanted us rescued, they'd have sent a proper team the moment we went quiet. Instead they waited nineteen days, then sent one pilot with a clause about property recovery. You know what that tells me? It tells me somebody upstairs wasn't sure they wanted us back. They wanted to know where we were. There's a difference, and the difference is measured in bodies."

I drank my coffee instead of answering, which was an answer, and he took it as one.

"There's a fourth boat on this river," he said quietly. "Isn't there. Behind you."

My spine did something involuntary. "What makes you say that?"

"Because Ines spotted smoke on the far bank two days ago, and because the Club never sends one boat when it can send two, and because—" he tapped the crate between us— "you keep glancing downstream every ninety seconds like a man checking whether the bill has arrived."

I hadn't known I was doing that. That's the thing about lies; they don't live in your mouth, they live in your posture, and eventually your posture testifies against you.

"If there's another party on this river," Reyes said, "then whatever you were told your job is, your actual job is to find out where we are and report it. Am I close?"

"You're close enough to be dangerous," I said. "So let me ask mine. Why won't you come downriver? Eleven people, one functioning radio, calving season coming. Whatever you found up that valley, it isn't worth dying over. Nothing is. I've chased weather for fifteen years and I will tell you the single most reliable fact in my profession: nothing is worth dying over, because the dead don't get to enjoy being right."

For a moment I thought he might actually laugh. Instead he looked past me, up the valley, at the glacier wall glowing blue-white in the late light like something lit from inside.

"You know what permafrost is," he said. Not a question. "Frozen ground. Archive ground. Everything that dies up there stays—pollen, ash, animals, atmosphere, all of it, layered like pages. Sixty thousand years of pages, Almeida. And most of those pages are boring. Pollen, ash, pollen, ash. But some of them aren't. Some of them are signatures."

"Signatures of what?"

"Of who knew what, and when." He turned back to me, and his eyes had gone flat and bright, the eyes of a man holding something heavy at arm's length. "There's a section of core from the last century that shouldn't exist. Industrial chemistry in layers where there was no industry yet. Somebody sampled this ice decades ago, logged it, and buried the log. We found the old bore site. Someone was here before us—a survey team, private flag, no government markings. They knew. They've known for years what this archive proves, and they built a financial architecture on top of the lie, and now eleven scientists are sitting on top of the proof like it's a landmine."

The river said what rivers say, which is nothing, endlessly.

"So we voted," Reyes went on. "Halloran ran it clean. Go dark until we verify, until we can move the data somewhere it can't be bought, buried, or disappeared. Ten to one, silence wins. I was the ten." He smiled without humor. "Ines was the one. She thought hiding the truth was how truth gets killed—slowly, politely, by people who mean well. She may be right. She usually is, which is insufferable."

"And now?"

"Now I'm the one refusing rescue, and she's the one arguing we should trust you." He rubbed his jaw. "Democracy is exhausting."

"Who else knows?" I asked. "About the archive. Outside the crew."

And there it was—the crack, the first hairline fracture in the whole clean story of *expedition lost, brave pilot sent*. Because Reyes didn't answer right away. He looked at his cup. He looked up the valley. And when he finally spoke, his voice had dropped into a register I recognized from interrogation rooms and family funerals: the voice of a man naming a thing that gets heavier when spoken.

"Somebody tipped us," he said. "Before we ever launched. An anonymous packet, six weeks before departure. Bore-site coordinates, partial assay data, a warning: *the Club's interests extend to this latitude.* No name. No return address. Whoever it was knew the science, knew the Club, and knew enough to stay invisible." He met my eyes. "Someone who isn't crew has been watching this valley for years, Almeida. Maybe protecting it. Maybe cataloguing it for later. We don't know which, and that's the part that keeps me awake."

* * *

Full dark came down around six, early and total, the way it does at that latitude in autumn—like a lid. Reyes rigged a tarp shelter for me near the standing tent and refused, firmly, to tell me where the others had gone. "Up-valley," was all he'd give. "If you're what you say you are, you'll wait. If you're what I think you might be, better you don't know."

I lay in my bag listening to the glacier settle, that deep internal groaning like a house shifting on its foundation, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the velvet back room.

One: the Club had sent me upstream knowing—or suspecting—that the expedition was alive. You don't spend salvage money on corpses. Two: the recovery contract named Tomas Reyes specifically, and Reyes believed it was because of what he knew, not who he was. Three: there was a second boat on this river, or would be soon, and Mr. Latch's people didn't strike me as the camping-for-pleasure type. Four: somewhere above me, ten people were guarding sixty thousand years of weather like it was the last uncut diamond on earth, and one of them—a woman named Ines Okonkwo, according to the manifest I'd memorized—thought I might be worth trusting.

And five, the number I kept circling back to: my sister's name was on a document in a velvet room in a city I owed too much money to, listed under *collateral*, and every hour I spent learning the truth made the distance between my debt and my conscience narrower, until soon they'd be touching, and something would have to give.

Near midnight, a sound woke me—not the glacier, not the river. A footstep. Soft, deliberate, on frozen gravel outside the tarp.

I sat up. The tarp flap moved.

Not Reyes. Smaller frame, hooded, face shadowed, and moving with the particular economy of someone who has spent years walking on ice. She crouched at the edge of my shelter, keeping the dark behind her like a coat, and when she spoke her voice was quiet and level and carried the unmistakable weight of a decision already made.

"Marguerite Almeida," she said. "My name is Ines Okonkwo. I'm the one who voted against the silence. And I need to know—in the next thirty seconds, before the others wake up—whether you're the best liar on this river, or the only honest man on it."

She held out her hand, palm open, waiting.

Outside, far downstream, so faint it might have been imagination, a motor coughed once and caught.
