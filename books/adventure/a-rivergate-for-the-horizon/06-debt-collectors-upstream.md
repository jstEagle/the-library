## 6. Debt Collectors Upstream

I heard the second boat before I saw it, which is the only reason I'm alive to tell you this. The Sereno runs loud in the narrows—white water over granite makes a sound like applause from a crowd that wants something—and underneath that applause came a different note. An outboard running rich. Someone who hadn't tuned an engine since the last time someone else did it for them.

I killed my own motor and let the current take me into the shadow of a cutbank, and I sat there doing arithmetic. There was no arithmetic to do. One boat on the Sereno is a survey crew. Two boats is a question, and the question is *who told you where to look*.

The answer, of course, was me. Every check-in I'd made with my handlers, every position report relayed through the Club's dispatch like a man reading his own diary aloud to his creditors. I'd been so busy lying about what I'd found that I never considered I was broadcasting exactly where I was.

The boat came around the bend at a tourist's pace. Aluminum hull, new, the kind of gray that costs extra. Two men aboard and a third shape under a tarp that was shaped suspiciously like a generator. The man at the tiller wore a canvas jacket that had never been rained on, and he lifted one hand as they passed my hiding spot—not a wave, exactly. More like a man acknowledging a parking attendant.

"Almeida," he called, without slowing. "Latch. We'll make camp at the gravel bar past the narrows. Come eat something that isn't freeze-dried."

Then they were gone around the next bend, leaving me alone with the river's applause and the specific cold feeling of a man who has just learned that the trap has a name.

* * *

I want to be fair to Mr. Latch, because fairness is the weapon he'd use on me.

He had a fire going when I pulled in—a real fire, birch and alder, not a camp stove—and a pot of coffee that smelled like it had been brewed by someone's grandmother rather than reconstituted from a foil packet. He was a big man gone soft in the way of men who used to be dangerous and now outsource it, silver at the temples, reading glasses pushed up on his forehead like a prop he'd forgotten to remove. His two companions didn't have names he offered. One was built like a filing cabinet. The other watched my hands the way you watch weather.

"Marguerite Almeida." Latch said my name like he was tasting a wine he already owned the vineyard of. "Sit. You look like a man who's been eating his own cooking."

"I've been eating the river's cooking."

"And how is the river?"

"Withholding."

He laughed, warm and genuine, and poured me coffee into an enamel cup that was clean. That was the detail that scared me most, later—the clean cup. A man who brings clean cups upriver has planned this stop. A man who plans stops upriver is not support.

"So." He settled across the fire, knees apart, hands wrapped around his own cup. "Report."

And here is the thing I need you to understand about the moment before I lied: I hesitated. Not long. Maybe a second and a half. But Latch caught it—I watched him catch it, the way his eyes did a small pleasant thing, like a man noting a typo in a contract he intends to enforce anyway—and I understood that whatever I said next would be measured against that second and a half for the rest of my life, or whatever remained of it.

"Nothing upstream but water," I said. "Channels are closing early. Survey markers drowned at kilometer forty. The old research station's half underwater. If Halcyon Reach came through here, they came through fast and light."

"Mm." He sipped. "And the station. You went inside?"

"Some of it. The rest is river."

"Find anything?"

"Rust. Mold. A poster about ergonomics."

That got a laugh out of the filing cabinet, one syllable, immediately suppressed. Latch smiled at him fondly, then back at me, and the smile stayed exactly where it was while everything behind it moved.

"Ergonomics," he repeated. "You know, I read the incident reports from that station. Before your time. Ninety-four, ninety-five. Interesting outfit. Atmospheric chemistry, mostly. Ice cores." He let the word sit in the fire between us like a coal. "They were pulling cores off the tributary glacier back when the Reach expedition's leaders were still in graduate school. Did you know that?"

"No."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "Most people don't. It's not the kind of thing that makes it into the funding proposals." He topped off my coffee without asking, which is a thing hosts do, and also a thing handlers do—you keep the subject's hands full. "The Club keeps old files, Marguerite. We're investors. Investors read."

I drank my coffee and thought about Ines Okonkwo's face in my imagination, which was all I had of her—a voice on channel nine three weeks ago, saying *routine check complete*, and then nothing. I thought about the gear the expedition had abandoned at the drowned station, staged too neatly to be lost. Deliberate. A message, or a misdirection, or both.

"You're a long way from a supply run," I said. "Support contractors don't usually come find their contractors."

"We prefer proactive." Latch spread his hands, all apology. "Three weeks of silence from a team we're heavily invested in—it makes our members nervous. Nervous members make phone calls. Phone calls are how clubs like ours lose their charm." He leaned forward, and the firelight did something unkind to his face, hollowing the warmth out of it and leaving the architecture. "So we came to help. And to ask, plainly, since plainly seems to be the dialect up here: if you find them, Marguerite, what do you imagine they'll tell you?"

"That they're fine."

"They won't be fine. They'll be *situated*. There's a difference, and the difference is worth more than your boat." He said it lightly, a joke almost, and then let it stop being one. "Whatever they've found up there—and they've found something, silence like that is never empty—they will want to control it. Control is expensive. You know about expensive."

"I know about debt."

"Debt!" He beamed, delighted, as if I'd named his favorite grandchild. "Yes. Debt is just information about the future, priced honestly. Most people find that obscene. I find it comforting." He stood, stretched, tossed another piece of birch on the fire, and for a moment he was just a middle-aged man camping badly, and I almost pitied him, and then he said, without turning around, in the same cozy register:

"Your sister's garden looks good this year. The tomatoes came in early."

The river kept applauding. My cup was very steady in my hand, because I made it steady, the way you make anything steady when the alternative is showing a man like Latch the exact shape of your fear.

"She mentioned you might pass through," he continued, sitting back down, warming his hands. "Small world. She's collateral, of course—that's such an ugly word, we say *co-signatory*—but she's also just a person, and persons are resilient. It would take a great deal for anything unfortunate to reach her. A great deal going wrong, repeatedly, over time." He looked up at me with those reading glasses still parked on his forehead, absurd, domestic. "You understand I'm not threatening you. Threats are for adversaries. You're an asset. I'm describing weather."

"I understand weather."

"I know you do. It's why you're here." He yawned, checked a watch that cost more than my truck, and announced bedtime like a man concluding a church social. "Tomorrow we'll travel together as far as the confluence. Safety in numbers, and all that. You lead—we wouldn't want to spook anyone you might happen to find."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I lay in my boat with the tarp half over me and listened to the filing cabinet snore and the other one not snore, because the other one was awake, watching, the way I'd watch a storm cell at two in the morning—professionally, patiently, waiting for it to decide what it was.

Here's what I worked out in the dark, item by item, the way Latch would have liked:

One. They knew the expedition had found something before I did. The questions weren't fishing; they were verification. Latch wasn't asking *what did they find*. He was asking *have you seen it yet*, dressed up as small talk.

Two. The drowned station mattered to them. Old cores, ninety-four, ninety-five. Somebody had been reading that ice a generation ago, and the Club kept the file, and the file was interesting enough to send three men and a generator four hundred kilometers up a dying river.

Three. Tomas Reyes had put a gun in my face two days ago and told me to go home, and behind him, in the treeline, there had been a second set of footprints that didn't match his boots. Someone else knew. Someone outside the crew. I'd filed that away as paranoia. Now it filed itself under *corroborated*.

Four. My sister grew tomatoes, and a stranger knew it, and the distance between *knowing* and *watering* was a distance the Vantablack Club could close any afternoon it felt like it.

And five—the one I kept circling back to, the ugly one—if they already suspected what the Reach had found, then my salvage contract was never a rescue contract. It was a delivery confirmation. Find the package. Confirm the contents. And if the contents were inconvenient—

I stopped there. Not because the thought ran out, but because I heard the watcher shift his weight in the dark, and some thoughts you finish only when no one can see your face.

* * *

Morning came up gray and thin, mist standing on the water in columns like the river was exhaling after holding its breath all night. Latch made eggs. Actual eggs, carried upriver in a padded cooler, cooked in butter. The extravagance of it was the message: we can be anywhere, with anything, whenever we decide to.

"Sleep well?" he asked, handing me a plate.

"Like a stone."

"Liar," he said cheerfully. "Nobody sleeps like a stone on the Sereno anymore. The river's too nervous." He ate standing, looking upstream, and I realized he was letting me watch him not ask the question again. It was a courtesy, or a demonstration. *I could ask. I'm choosing not to. Notice the choice.*

We broke camp. The filing cabinet loaded the generator onto their boat; the watcher scanned the ridgelines with binoculars, unhurried, thorough, a man inventorying a property he already owned. I pushed off first, as instructed, and led us up through the narrows with Latch's gray hull riding fifty meters behind me like a sentence I couldn't take back.

The channels were worse than yesterday. Meltwater coming down hard off the high country, the river running brown-green and muscular, swallowing survey markers whole. At the mouth of the lateral valley—Halloran's valley, though I didn't know its name yet, only that the water coming out of it was colder than the main stem and smelled like the inside of a freezer—I throttled down and studied the entrance the way you study a door you're not sure you want knocked on.

Latch's boat idled alongside. He stood at the rail, following my gaze up the valley, and for a long moment neither of us said anything, and the mist burned off the water between us in slow rags.

"Beautiful country," he said finally. "You could hide anything in country like this."

"You could."

"Find anything in country like this?"

And there it was. The second and a half, come due with interest. I looked at him—at the reading glasses, the butter, the clean cups, the tomatoes—and I reached inside myself for the lie, and here is what I found, and what I hate, and what I'll carry: it fit my hand perfectly.

"Not yet," I said. "But the water coming out of that valley's running cold and clear. Glacial feed. If they holed up anywhere, it's up there—cold means ice, ice means a lake, a lake means flat water to camp on." I shrugged, a man reasoning aloud, a professional thinking with his hands on the wheel. "Could take me two days to work up the channel. Shallower every kilometer. Your draft's too deep for it—you'd ground out at the first bend."

Something moved behind Latch's eyes. Not suspicion—suspicion I could have handled. This was closer to appetite. He looked at the valley the way his members looked at disasters: as an opening.

"Two days," he said. "Well. We'll establish a forward camp here at the confluence. Wait for your signal." He smiled, wide and warm and terrible. "Radio checks at noon and six, yes? Standard protocol. We'd hate to lose track of you, Marguerite. Assets are precious. Especially the ones with families."

"Standard protocol," I agreed.

He clapped me on the shoulder as the boats touched, brief, avuncular, a man handling property he trusted to hold its value. Then I turned my bow into the cold water of the lateral valley, and the mist took me, and the applause of the main river faded behind me until there was only the smaller sound of the channel ahead—quieter, older, like a held breath.

Noon and six. Radio checks. Which meant that somewhere in the next forty-eight hours, on a frequency the Club monitored, I would either report finding eleven people who had voted to disappear—or I would report nothing, and become a missing asset myself, and the weather over my sister's garden would turn.

I had a transmitter, a debt, a sister, and a valley full of silence ahead of me.

I gave the throttle everything I had, and for once, speed felt less like an identity than like a countdown.
