## 7. Halcyon Reach

The lateral valley didn't announce itself. That was the first thing I noticed about it, and the last thing I understood about the whole expedition: everything that mattered out here was built to be missed.

I'd been running the Sereno for six days by then, and I'd learned its grammar. The river spoke in bends, in gravel bars, in the particular color of water over rock. It had told me where the drowned station was—told me a week too late, but rivers are like that. They only explain themselves after you've already driven into them.

So when I saw the tributary sliding in from the east, thin as a zipper in the valley wall, I almost ran past it. Almost. But there was fresh cutbank on its mouth, mud turned up in the last day or two, and above the treeline, if you squinted against the glare, a smudge of smoke that could have been cloud shadow or could have been people who wanted very badly not to be found.

I nosed the boat in.

The channel narrowed fast, faster than anything downstream, and I spent forty minutes poling through shallows that scraped the hull like fingernails on a chalkboard, apologizing under my breath to an engine that had done nothing wrong except belong to me. The walls closed in. Alders grabbed at the gunwales. And then the valley opened like a held breath released—a bowl of tundra and moraine, blue with lupine, ringed by ice that caught the afternoon sun and threw it back down like a dare.

The camp sat on a bench of gravel above the waterline. Tents. Solar arrays angled with real care. A weather mast turning lazily. A cook shelter with actual walls. Smoke from a stovepipe, rising straight up in the dead air, which was either terrible operational security or a message: *we're here, we've stopped hiding, come say hello.*

Eleven people, the contract had said. Eleven scientists, silent for three weeks, presumed scattered or dead or worse. I'd spent six days building coffins for all of them in my head, one at a time, revising the details as the river gave me evidence. Drowned here. Starved there. Turned on each other somewhere around the confluence.

They were all standing in a line at the edge of camp, watching my boat grind toward their beach.

Alive. All eleven. Standing in a row like a jury.

I killed the engine and let the current walk me in the last fifty yards, because I wanted my hands free and my face doing something neutral. Six days of rehearsing this moment—*I'm your supply pilot, I'm contracted, I'm friendly*—and what came out of my mouth when the hull touched gravel was:

"Somebody order a boat?"

Nobody laughed. Of course nobody laughed. These were people who'd voted on something, whatever it was, and voted hard enough to go dark for three weeks while the world wrote their obituaries. You don't laugh at the man who just walked into your sealed room.

A tall man with a gray beard came forward—Halloran, I'd know him from the expedition manifest even without the introduction, because he had the specific posture of someone who'd been carrying eleven lives in a canvas rucksack for a month. He looked at me the way you look at a check that might bounce.

"Marguerite Almeida," he said. Not a question. "You're late."

"River's been eating the markers," I said. "And somebody scuttled the survey station downstream."

Something moved through the line of faces behind him. Not surprise. *Confirmation.* Like I'd said a password they'd been waiting to hear.

"We know," Halloran said. "Come up. Slowly."

*Slowly.* As in: we have decided not to shoot you, provisionally, and would prefer not to revisit the decision. I climbed the bank with my hands visible, feeling like the world's most polite trespasser, and the line of them parted just enough to let me through, and that's when I saw her.

Amara Voss stood at the end of the row, arms crossed, wearing a parka two sizes too big and an expression I recognized from seven years ago and from every dream I'd had since—the expression she wore right before she told you something true enough to hurt.

Of all the calculations I'd run on this river—all the fuel math, the channel math, the debt math—I had never once run the math on her being here. The expedition roster had listed a Dr. A. Voss, medical officer. I'd read the name three times and filed it under coincidence, because the alternative was unthinkable, and I am a professional at not thinking things.

"Hey, Marguerite," she said.

Two words. Flat as the gravel under my boots. And they landed harder than any storm cell I'd ever chased, because Amara Voss did not do flat unless flat was load-bearing.

"Amara." My voice came out with a crack in it, some hairline fracture I thought I'd sealed years ago. "You're supposed to be in Nuuk. Or Tromsø. Somewhere with a hospital in it."

"I'm supposed to be exactly where I signed up to be." She uncrossed her arms. "You're supposed to be flying supply runs for a climate survey. That's what the paperwork says. So why does your boat have a salvage winch on it, Marguerite? And why did you lie on the radio every single check-in for six days?"

The camp went very quiet. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"I—" I started, and stopped, because lying to her had never once worked, not in four years together, not in seven years apart, and starting now, on her gravel, in front of her crew, felt like a special category of stupid.

"The contract changed," I said finally. Which was true. Which was also the most dishonest sentence I'd spoken on this entire river, and she heard it, I watched her hear it, watched something behind her eyes go from *ambush* to *confirmed ambush.*

"Your handlers think we're dead," she said. Not asking.

"Officially, you're overdue. Unofficially—" I looked at Halloran. At the others. At the wall of equipment behind them, the data racks, the cores racked in insulated sleeves like organ donations waiting for a body. "Unofficially, somebody's paying me to find out what happened to you. And somebody else is paying me to make sure certain things don't come back downriver."

"And you took both jobs," Amara said.

"It's complicated."

"It's always complicated with you. That's how you leave. You make leaving look like logistics."

The words hit the exact spot they were designed to hit, because she'd had seven years to calibrate and she'd always been a quick study. I stood there on the gravel with the whole camp watching, eleven scientists and one man whose entire life was a stack of lies with good tread still on them, and I did the thing I do best, which is keep moving.

"Look," I said. "I can stand here and take my medicine, or I can tell you that there's a second boat on this river. Three men, maybe four. They call themselves support contractors. Their boss is named Latch, he smiles too much, and he asked me very specific questions about your camp's location with coffee in his hand and a pistol very casually not pointed at anyone. They're a day behind me. Maybe less."

That landed. I watched it land—watched the line of scientists stop being a jury and start being a crew, watch Halloran's jaw set, watch a young woman with ice-burned cheeks—Ines, the glaciologist, I'd learn later—involuntarily glance toward the data wall like a mother checking on a sleeping child.

"You led them here?" someone asked from the back.

"No," I said. "But they'll find the tributary same as I did. Maybe tomorrow. Maybe tonight."

Halloran looked at me for a long moment. Then at Amara. Some whole conversation happened between them in the space of a breath, the kind of shorthand people develop when they've made decisions together in the cold.

"Then we don't have time for the reunion," Halloran said. "Mr. Almeida. You'd better come see what we found."

* * *

They kept it in the largest tent, the one that from outside looked like storage. Inside, it looked like a cathedral that had been built by accountants.

Ice cores lined the racks in numbered sleeves, deep blue-green, lit from below by field lamps so they glowed like something alive. Along one wall, printed charts covered every square foot—sixty thousand years of atmosphere stacked in ink, CO2 curves climbing like staircases, isotope records, dust layers, volcanic horizons marked in red. And pinned at the center, slightly crooked, like it had been put up in a hurry by someone with shaking hands: a single graph showing a trajectory that no published record on Earth contained.

"This is the Halcyon Reach?" I asked. "This is what you went quiet for?"

"We went quiet because of what it says," Amara said beside me. Close enough that I could smell woodsmoke and glacier flour on her. "Not what it is."

"Explain it to me like I chase storms for a living."

She almost smiled. Almost. "You know how a storm chaser reads a sky? Pressure falling, wind backing, and you *know*—not predict, *know*—what's coming, hours before anyone else feels it?"

"Yeah."

"That's what this is. Except the sky is sixty thousand years long, and the storm has already started, and the people betting against it own half the world's insurance portfolios." She tapped the central graph. "This record proves the collapse models are decades ahead of schedule. It proves the meltwater pulses happened faster than any published core shows. It proves—" she paused, choosing the word like a surgeon choosing a blade, "—that certain financial instruments currently valued in the billions are backed by a future that doesn't exist."

I stared at the glowing ice. Somewhere behind us, the camp murmured with the low industry of people who had already grieved their old lives and moved on to the work.

"And the Club sent you," Amara said quietly, "to make sure this never reached daylight."

"I don't know what the Club sent me to do," I said. "I know what they paid me to do. Those aren't always the same thing out here."

"No," she agreed. "They never are with you."

There it was again—that scalpel she carried, honed on seven years of absence. I turned to face her, and the tent suddenly felt very small, the way cockpits feel small when the instruments start disagreeing with each other.

"Why are you here, Amara? Really. You hate the cold. You get Raynaud's in anything under ten degrees. You once drove two hours to complain about my air conditioning."

"Because you left," she said simply. "Because I spent seven years watching the world get sicker from the sidelines and thinking, *he at least is out there looking at it straight.* And then I found out you were working for the people who look away on purpose. So I signed onto an expedition that was going to disappear, because disappearing felt more honest than what you were doing."

"That's not an answer. That's a verdict."

"Same thing, where I'm from."

Outside, the light was going long and gold across the moraine, and somewhere downriver—two valleys, maybe less—a man named Latch was making entries in a ledger, and one of those entries, I was increasingly sure, had my name on it with a small cheerful notation next to it.

"Latch will be here by tomorrow night," I said. "Maybe sooner. Whatever you're going to decide about this ice, decide it fast. And whatever you decide about me—"

"I decided about you seven years ago," Amara said. "I'm still deciding whether I was right."

She walked out of the tent into the light, and I stood alone with sixty thousand years of atmosphere glowing at my back, understanding at last that I hadn't found the expedition at all.

It had found me. And it had been waiting—not to be rescued, but to ask me a question I'd been outrunning my whole life:

Which side of the ledger was I on?

Behind me, the ice cores hummed in their lamps, patient as evidence. Ahead, through the tent flap, I could see Amara talking to Halloran, both of them glancing my way, and I knew—with the same certainty I felt reading a green sky over flat country—that whatever vote they took next, my name was on the ballot.

Downriver, the first thunder of the calving season rolled up the valley like a door closing.
