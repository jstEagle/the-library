## 8. Seven Years of Weather

The first thing you learn about Marguerite Almeida is that he never sits with his back to a door. The second thing is that he counts. Exits, fuel gauges, the seconds between lightning and thunder, the number of times your eyes flick toward someone else at a party. He counted me once, early on, and told me I blinked forty percent less than most people when I lied, which was either the most romantic thing anyone had ever said to me or the most alarming, and after seven years I still haven't settled the question.

Now he sat in Halloran's mess tent with his back to the canvas wall—no door, so no door to watch—and his eyes were doing laps anyway. Tent flap. Generator line. The ridge where Ines kept her weather instruments. The ice cores racked under blue tarps like something between cargo and hostages. He wasn't looking at me. He was looking at everything *except* me, which from Marguerite was its own kind of attention, the way a man staring hard at nothing is really staring at the shape of what he's lost.

I'd had seven years to practice not caring what he looked at. I want it noted for the record that I had gotten very good at it, right up until the moment he walked into camp smelling of river water and diesel and bad decisions, and every hour of that practice evaporated like spit on a hot manifold.

"Your coffee's gone cold," I said, because I am a medic and therefore trained to state obvious injuries.

"I noticed." He drank it anyway. Cold coffee, grimaced at like an old friend who'd let him down again. "Good coffee."

"It's terrible coffee. It's the same terrible coffee everyone's been drinking for three weeks. You're being polite."

"That's new," he said, and almost smiled, and I hated how much room that almost-smile took up inside my chest, how it just moved in without paying rent.

* * *

Let me tell you about the breakup, since apparently we're doing inventory.

Seven years ago, Tulsa, or maybe Oklahoma City—the cities blur together when your whole relationship lives in a car. We had been together three years by then, which in storm-chaser time is roughly a geological epoch. I was finishing my residency rotations; he was running chase tours and burning through money like it was a consumable. And one night in a diner outside Enid, with rain hammering the windows and the radar app open between us like a third party neither of us would acknowledge, I asked him a simple question: where is this going?

Not marriage. Not even cohabitation, God forbid. Just: direction. Vector. The thing he of all people should have understood, a man whose entire profession was reading the sky for intent.

And Marguerite—who could read a supercell's rotation from forty miles out, who could feel a mesocyclone tighten before the instruments confirmed it—looked at me with genuine bafflement and said, "Going? It's going fine."

Fine. The word landed on the table between our plates like a dropped wrench.

Here's the thing about loving a man built for velocity: he experiences every stationary object as either scenery or obstacle, and I had begun to suspect I was scenery. Beautiful, well-maintained scenery that he was always happy to return to, but scenery. He loved me the way he loved the plains—at speed, in passing, the whole landscape gorgeous precisely because he never had to stop in any one part of it. Ask him to commit to a single horizon and something in him started to die visibly, like a cut flower.

I said something cruel. He said something true, which is worse. What he said was: "You knew what I was when you got in the truck." And what I said was: "I thought I could be the destination instead of the drive." And then there was the long silence, and the rain, and the waitress refilling coffees neither of us touched, and then I took the residency in Denver and he took a contract in the Panhandle and that was that. No shouting. Storm chasers don't shout; we've seen what the sky does when pressure differentials get out of hand. We just quietly evacuated the structure before it failed.

He sent a postcard eleven months later. A tornado, naturally. On the back: *Thought of you.* No return address, because a return address would have been a location, and locations are commitments. I kept the postcard. I'm not proud of that. I'm also not going to pretend I didn't.

* * *

People ask why I joined Halcyon Reach—an expedition designed to go dark, off-grid, unfindable, for a year or more—and I give them the professional answer: the medical challenge, the isolation protocols, the chance to study human physiology under extreme conditions. All true. None of it the truth.

The truth is that I wanted to disappear from a life that kept reminding me of him. Every highway in the American West had his tire tracks on it somewhere. Every severe weather alert on my phone arrived with his voice attached, some old radio clip my memory played unbidden: *cell's tightening, we should reposition.* I dated other men. Good men. Men who sat facing doors and thought nothing of it, men who made five-year plans with the ease of people who believed they'd be alive in five years. I kept waiting to feel about them what I felt in the passenger seat of that truck at 2 a.m., chasing something enormous and dangerous across the dark—and I felt nothing, because it turns out I hadn't fallen in love with Marguerite. I'd fallen in love with the *velocity*. With being the person trusted to ride along.

So when Halloran offered me a berth on an expedition that would vanish—not die, vanish; there's a difference, though the paperwork is similar—I said yes before he finished the sentence. A woman against orders, the crew called me later, half-teasing, because technically the funding board wanted a male lead physician and Halloran told them to take their gender preferences and audit them. I came upriver knowing the radio checks would thin out and stop. Knowing my sister would worry. Knowing that somewhere behind me, a man with beautiful hands on a steering wheel was measuring his life in odometer readings and would never know I'd chosen a place where miles stopped meaning anything.

I voted for silence in Ines's tent, that first week after the discovery. I want that on the record too, because everyone assumes the medic must have been the conscience, the one arguing for disclosure. No. I voted to go dark. Sixty thousand years of atmosphere locked in blue ice, data that would gut the carbon futures market and indict half the people who fund expeditions like ours—and I looked at that and thought: yes. Bury it. Let it be verified properly, slowly, by people who won't weaponize it. Silence as principle. Silence as protection.

Three weeks later I watched a boat come around the bend carrying the one man on earth capable of making silence feel like cowardice, and I began, quietly, to reconsider my vote.

* * *

That evening in the mess tent, after the terrible coffee, I cornered him on the medical pretext. There was a gash on his forearm from the drowned station—shallow, already scabbing, the kind of wound that exists purely to give two stubborn people something to do with their hands while they talk around the actual subject.

"Hold still."

"I'm holding still."

"You're vibrating. You've been vibrating since you docked. Sit."

He sat. I cleaned the cut, and he watched my hands instead of my face, which was its own confession. Marguerite Almeida, who could hold eye contact with a rotating wall cloud, couldn't look at me over a two-inch laceration.

"You knew I was coming," he said finally. "Halloran didn't seem surprised."

"The whole camp knew. Ines spotted your boat two days ago. We had a betting pool on whether you'd actually make the second set of rapids." I tied off the last suture. "Tomas had money on no."

"Tomas." Something moved through his face—guilt, probably, arriving right on schedule. "How is he?"

"Armed and opinionated. Same as ever." I packed the wound, more carefully than the wound required. "You want to tell me why a supply pilot takes a solo salvage run up a river that's closing for the season? Because that's not a rescue, Marguerite. That's a eulogy with a schedule."

"Contractual obligation."

"Try again."

"Money."

Closer. Still not it. I put down the forceps and looked at him straight on, and he held it this time, maybe two full seconds, which for Marguerite under interrogation was practically a deposition.

"Seven years," I said. "You show up out of a fog bank looking like you slept in the boat—which you did—and you expect me to buy 'money'? I've read your tells for three years, Almeida. You blink forty percent less when you lie. You're blinking like a strobe light."

A breath. His jaw worked. And then, in a voice lower than the generator hum: "It's complicated."

"Everything with you is complicated. That's what makes it simple." I stood, stripped the gloves, and gave him the field diagnosis I'd been composing since he stepped ashore. "You're here because someone's holding something over you. A debt, a threat, a person. And you're going to spend however many days we give you calculating exits—out of this valley, out of this contract, out of whatever box they built for you—because that's the only way you know how to love anything. From the exit. Facing the door. Ready to run."

"That's not fair."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. Fairness is for people with leverage." I gathered the wrappers, the suture packet, the little theater of medicine, and paused at the tent flap. "Dinner's at nineteen hundred. Sit next to me. If you can manage it."

* * *

He managed it. Of course he managed it—that was the unbearable part. He sat next to me at dinner with his knee six inches from mine, laughing at Reyes's impression of Latch—more on Latch later, Latch deserves his own chapter and possibly his own trial—and for ninety minutes he was twenty-eight years old again, quick and bright and hungry, the man who once drove four hours through a hailstorm to bring me a replacement alternator belt because I'd mentioned mine was squeaking. Offhand. I'd mentioned it *offhand*, and he'd driven into weather that shut down two counties, because that was his language. Not words. Not staying. *Effort at speed.*

And I sat there watching him laugh and cataloguing, helplessly, all the ways he was already leaving. The way his eyes ticked to the ridge every time one of Latch's men shifted position. The way he'd positioned his chair—casually, expertly—with sightline to both the boats and the radio tent. The way he asked questions that sounded casual and weren't: how much fuel, how many boats, what's the earliest the pass closes. He was building a map of our constraints the way he used to build maps of storms, and I understood with a clarity that felt like cold water that whatever brought him here, he was already planning the way out, and the plan had a shape, and the shape did not obviously include eleven scientists and forty pounds of ice core.

Here is what seven years taught me, the lesson I paid full price for: you cannot outrun a man who loves at highway speed, but you also cannot arrive with him. He will carry you magnificently toward every horizon except the one you're standing on.

But.

But he came up a dying river alone, past survey markers and drowned stations, through rapids that killed better boats than his, because a radio went quiet and his name was on the contract. Whatever else is true—whatever debt has him leashed, whoever is squeezing him—he *came*. Tomas wouldn't board the boat and Marguerite stayed anyway. I watched him watch Halloran explain the calving models tonight, watched him do the arithmetic on the window, watched something in his face shift from *how do I get out* to *can this even work*—and that second question, that's the dangerous one. That's the one that means he's stopped calculating exits and started calculating approaches. A man who loves at highway speed, once committed to a road, does not brake.

After dinner I found him at the edge of camp, looking upriver at the glacier's blue shoulder, hands in his pockets, counting something—I'd have bet the med kit it was seconds between the ice groans, timing the mountain like it was a cell about to turn.

"Marguerite."

He turned. Full eye contact. Held it.

"There's a vote tomorrow," I said. "About whether you hear the whole truth. About what we found. Halloran wants silence. Others don't. I haven't decided which side I'm on yet." I let that sit. "Whatever they decide—you should know that if you're planning an exit that doesn't include us, I'll see it coming. I learned from the best."

"And if the exit includes you?" he said. Quiet. Not charming. Actually asking.

I looked at him for a long moment, this man made of odometer readings, and felt the tenderness and the accusation sit down together inside me like old cellmates, and heard myself say:

"Then you'd better drive slower than you've ever driven in your life."

Behind us, from Halloran's tent, came the sound of the crew gathering—chairs scraping, Ines's voice low and urgent, the vote convening an hour early.
