## 9. The Archive Vote

I have chaired a great many meetings in my life, and I can tell you the exact moment each one stopped being a meeting and became something else. Usually it's when someone says the word "respectfully" for the third time. Ines hit her third "respectfully" at roughly 9:40 in the evening, which meant we had about four minutes of collegiality left before the knives came out, metaphorically speaking. Though in Tomas's case I was no longer entirely sure the metaphor was safe.

We convened in the mess tent, which is a generous word for a structure that was originally a supply tarp and had been promoted beyond its competence. Eleven of us sat on supply crates arranged in what I insisted on calling a horseshoe and what everyone else correctly identified as a circle with a missing piece. The missing piece was where the stove stood, hissing its low blue flame, and above it hung the only decoration in camp: a printout of an atmospheric curve sixty thousand years long, taped to the canvas, curling at the edges in the damp.

I had called the vote. I want that on the record, wherever the record ends up living, if it lives anywhere at all. Nobody made me. I called it because I had spent three days watching Marguerite Almeida drink our coffee, fix our generator with the bored competence of a man defusing bombs for a living, and say almost nothing, and because I had spent those same three days watching my crew look at him the way drowning sailors look at a boat that may or may not be crewed by friends.

The problem with Almeida was not that he had arrived. The problem was that he was exactly what he appeared to be — a hired pilot with a debt, a radio, and handlers — and exactly what he appeared to be was the most dangerous thing on this river.

"Let's not romanticize him," I said, opening. "He's not a hero who wandered into our story. He's a contractor. Someone paid for his boat, his fuel, and his silence, and the someone who pays for silence is never buying it as a gift."

"He fixed the water pump," said Priya, from the geophysics end of the horseshoe. "In forty minutes. Without being asked."

"Hit men are often charming. It's practically the job description."

"Respectfully," said Ines, and there was the third one, right on schedule, "that's not an argument, Eamon. That's a mood with citations."

I allowed myself the small smile that leadership requires you to ration like insulin. "Fine. Arguments, then. One: the Vantablack Club funded the logistics chain that got us here. Permits, charters, the satellite uplink window. They have never once asked what we were looking for, which should have frightened us in month one and should frighten us now. Two: Almeida's contract is a salvage contract. You do not send a salvage pilot upriver unless someone has already declared the salvage valuable. Three—" and here I tapped the curling printout above the stove, that long blue descent of carbon and temperature, sixty millennia of the sky keeping honest books, "—three: what's on that curve doesn't just embarrass the Club. It bankrupts them. And organizations facing bankruptcy do not send one polite man in one small boat. They send the polite man first."

Silence, except the stove. Outside, the glacier did what the glacier had been doing all week — a low groan, a crack like a door opening in a house with no doors, the sound I had learned to hear as weather and was now learning to hear as a clock.

Tomas Reyes spoke from the far end, arms crossed, still wearing the expression he'd worn when Almeida found him at the forward camp: a man who had decided to be an argument and was prepared to hold the position indefinitely. "You're assuming the Club knows what we found."

"I'm assuming," I said, "that the Club doesn't need to know what we found. They only need to know that we found something we're willing to go dark for. Silence is a signature, Tomas. It's the loudest thing we've ever broadcast."

Amara Voss had not spoken yet. She was sitting where she always sat, near the medical crate, a woman who had spent seven years learning to be still in a way that I suspected was not peace but discipline. When she finally did speak, she didn't look at me.

"You're leaving out the part where he's already lied for us."

"Has he?" I asked.

"He told his handlers we're scattered, under-equipped, demoralized. He told them the river's closing early and retrieval would be, quote, 'a bad investment.' He bought us a week. He did it without being asked, and he did it badly enough that I believe he's never lied to professionals before." She paused. "He's lying for us the way amateurs do everything. Sincerely."

"That's either touching or damning," said Priya.

"It's both," Amara said. "That's the whole man."

* * *

I want to be fair to the other side of the argument, because I lost it, and because fairness is the only dignity available to a man who has been outvoted in his own mess tent.

The case for trust went roughly like this, and Ines made it better than anyone: we could not get out alone. The meltwater channels were narrowing by the day; the calving season was opening early; we had eleven people, forty pounds of ice core, and the boating skills of a faculty retreat. Almeida was not a risk we were choosing. He was a risk we had already taken, the moment his hull touched our shore. The only question left was whether we told the man steering our lives out of this valley why his life was in danger.

"You want him loyal to us," Ines said, "but you won't tell him what loyalty costs. That's not caution, Eamon. That's using him. And we've all spent three weeks telling ourselves we're the honest ones in this story."

"We are the honest ones," I said. "That's the entire point. The moment this data moves through one more pair of hands, it has one more way to die. I've watched institutions swallow discoveries for thirty years. They don't do it with malice. They do it with scheduling. A press release gets delayed. A dataset gets 'cleaned.' A finding gets reframed as preliminary. And the sky keeps its books, and nobody reads them."

"So we take it to the public ourselves," Priya said. "When we're out. Through him — he's got a transmitter, he's got contacts—"

"Through him we have a man with debts," I said, "and a sister the Club holds as collateral, and handlers who call him twice a day. You want to route the most important atmospheric record ever recovered through a man whose family is hostage to the people it ruins?"

And there it was. I'll admit it: I played the sister. It was my best card and I played it, and I felt the tent change temperature, and I hated myself with the specific, efficient self-hatred of a man who knows he's winning for the wrong reasons.

Amara looked at me then, finally, and her look said: *you know exactly what you just did.* I looked back at the stove and let the blue flame do my apologizing for me.

* * *

We broke for twenty minutes, which in expedition democracy means we went outside to be angry at the glacier instead of each other. I stood by the river with my hands in my pockets and listened to the ice work. It had been calving at the face for two days — early, weeks early, the kind of early that makes a glaciologist go quiet in a way that isn't scientific. Ines came and stood beside me. She didn't say anything for a while, which from Ines was a paragraph.

"You voted for the silence," she said at last. "Three weeks ago. You gave the speech. *Sixty thousand years can wait sixty more days.* I believed you."

"I believed me."

"And now?"

I watched a section of the far ice shelf shrug loose and go into the water with a sound like the world clearing its throat. The river took it sideways, white and slow, like something being carried out of a burning building.

"Now I think the silence was never for the data," I said. "I told myself it was — verification, chain of custody, all the clean words. But I think part of it was that as long as nobody knew, we still had a choice. The moment we speak, the choice is gone. The moment we speak, the sky's books are open, and everything anyone has ever decided about the future gets re-audited, and I don't—" I stopped. I am not a man who runs out of words often, and it alarmed me more than the calving did. "I don't know if the world thanks you for that, Ines. I've seen what happens to people who bring bad news with good evidence. The evidence gets tried instead of the news."

"Respectfully," she said, and this one was quiet, no knife in it at all, "the world doesn't get to vote. We do. That's the whole reason we're standing here instead of on a conference stage."

* * *

We reconvened at 10:15. I had prepared my closing argument on the walk back, a good one, measured and humane, the kind of argument that loses with honor. I never gave it. Amara stood up first.

"I'm going to say the thing nobody's said," she said. "The reason Eamon wants to keep him outside the truth isn't the Club. It's that the truth makes Almeida one of us, and Eamon has decided that when this ends, some of us may need to be deniable. He's rationing us. He's been rationing us since the vote to go dark — who knows what, who's load-bearing, who's expendable. It's how he kept us alive through the collapse at the upper station and I'll never stop being grateful for it. But you can't ration your way out of this one. Either the man in the boat is crew or he's cargo. And I have been cargo in that man's life before, and I can tell you exactly how it felt."

The tent went very still. I did not look at her. I looked at the curve on the wall, sixty thousand years of the atmosphere keeping its accounts, and I understood that I had lost, and that I had lost to a better argument, which is the only loss I've ever been able to stomach.

"Call it," said Priya.

"Motion on the floor," I said, and my voice was steady, I'll give it that much. "That Marguerite Almeida be told the full contents of the archive — the record, the collapse projection, the dates — and be asked, not contracted, *asked*, to carry it out. All in favor."

Six hands. Ines, Priya, Amara, Okonkwo, Reyes — Reyes, who had been arguing my side all night and switched without explanation, and I would learn later that Amara had talked to him on the ridge that afternoon, and that loyalty apparently changes hands on this river the way water changes channels, without notice and all at once — and, last, Delacroix, the youngest of us, who raised her hand like she was volunteering for something.

"Opposed."

Five hands, mine included. Five hands and the stove's blue flame and the glacier groaning its early warning outside.

"Carried," I said.

I stood, because a chair who has been overruled owes the room the sight of him standing. "Then someone tell him. Tonight. Before his handlers call at dawn and he has to choose with only half the truth in his pocket." I paused at the tent flap. "And someone should tell him the Club's man on the river has been counting our boats. Because whatever we've decided in here, the vote out there was already taken, and I don't think we were on the ballot."

Nobody laughed. Nobody argued. Outside, the ice calved again, closer this time, and the river carried the sound down toward the pass like a message we hadn't written.
