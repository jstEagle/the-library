## 10. Latch's Ledger

I keep my accounts the way other men keep their prayers: daily, privately, and with a certain tenderness for the things I'm about to lose.

The ledger itself is old-fashioned, which people find charming until they understand it isn't affectation. It's a leather-bound book with a rubber band around it, and I write in pencil, because pencil forgives. A man in my line of work should never commit to ink. Ink is for people whose numbers stay put. My numbers move. They breed. They die in the night like mayflies on a windshield. Pencil lets me erase a man without ceremony, which is, when you think about it, the entire moral architecture of the Vantablack Club rendered in graphite.

Tonight the camp is quiet. The scientists are asleep in their valley — eleven of them, breathing in shifts — and Marguerite Almeida is asleep in his boat, or pretending to be, which amounts to the same asset class. I sit by my stove with the ledger open on my knees and do what I do every evening: I count.

* * *

Let me explain the arithmetic of my profession, because everyone gets it wrong. People hear "the Club bets on disasters" and they picture monsters. Men in dark rooms rubbing their hands over flood maps. But that's not it at all. We're optimists. That is the official title — Optimist, Second Class, Optimist First Class, and above them the Partners, who are optimists the way hurricanes are breezes. We don't wish for catastrophe. We simply believe, with religious conviction, that catastrophe will arrive on schedule, and we arrange our affairs accordingly. There is no malice in it. There is no *feeling* in it at all, which is the part that upsets people, and honestly the part I find most restful.

A fire is coming? We insure the timber against surviving. A river is going to flood? We hold paper on everything the water will touch. A glacier is going to calve and drown a coastline? Somewhere in our portfolio there is a bond that pays out beautifully when it does. We are not the storm. We are the umbrella industry. And an umbrella salesman who starts praying for sunshine has betrayed his customers, his partners, and himself.

Which brings me to the ice.

* * *

Entry one hundred fourteen. *The River.*

The Sereno is having an exceptional year, melt-wise. From a hydrological standpoint this is a tragedy; from mine it is a liquidity event. Every cubic meter of water coming off that glacier is a promise being kept to someone holding our paper downstream. I have watched this river for six days now from various elevations, and I want to record, for the benefit of no one but myself, that it is beautiful. It is the color of milk ground with glass. It sounds like applause that never decides to stop. Rivers this size don't run; they *deliver*. And everything they deliver this season was paid for in advance by men who read the same reports I did and drew the same cheerful conclusions.

The channels are narrowing. I've been logging widths each morning from the ridge with a rangefinder, out of professional habit more than necessity. Four hundred meters where the survey said six-fifty. Two hundred in the narrows below the drowned station. In nine days, maybe twelve, the calving season opens in earnest and the lower corridor becomes a shooting gallery of house-sized ice. After that, nothing comes down the Sereno that doesn't come down as debris.

This is relevant to entry one hundred fifteen, so let me be precise about dates. Precision is the courtesy I extend to everyone, including the people I am erasing.

* * *

Entry one hundred fifteen. *The Crew.*

Eleven souls, per my last count through the field glasses, though I'll allow for error of plus-or-minus one, since two of them spend most of their time under tarps among the instruments. Eleven scientists who walked into this valley twenty-three days ago and then did something genuinely remarkable: they went quiet. Not lost-quiet. *Chosen* quiet. There is a difference, and I could taste it on channel nine before anyone told me anything. Lost people transmit desperately. Chosen people transmit nothing at all, and the nothing has a texture.

They found something in the ice. I don't know exactly what yet — my sources upstream were better at locating the expedition than at penetrating its discipline, which reflects well on the expedition and poorly on my budget — but I know its shape. You always know the shape. Something in that blue ice contradicts the timeline our portfolio depends on. Sixty thousand years of atmosphere, if the glaciologist's old papers are any indication, and somewhere in those sixty thousand years a fact that makes our climate positions look like a bet that the sun rises in the west.

Now, here is what a smaller man would do. A smaller man would panic. He'd radio home, demand instructions, spread the responsibility around like butter until it was too thin to taste. I have never understood that instinct. The Club pays me precisely because I don't need instructions. Instructions are what you give people whose judgment you wouldn't trust with your coat. My judgment is the coat.

So let us itemize, because itemizing is how I think:

*Item:* Dr. Halloran, expedition leader. A believer. Believers are the easiest category to manage because they can be managed *through their beliefs*. Halloran thinks silence protects the data. He is half right, which is the most dangerous fraction there is.

*Item:* Dr. Okonkwo, glaciology. She voted for the silence — I know this because I pay a very modest retainer to a very frightened man in the logistics office at Ushuaia, and frightened men talk — and she is already regretting it. Regret is a door left unlatched. I make a note to leave it alone for now. Doors are most useful just before someone remembers they exist.

*Item:* Dr. Amara Voss, medic. Formerly attached, in the romantic sense, to Mr. Marguerite Almeida, duration approximately three years, terminated seven years ago by Mr. Almeida's departure, cause: velocity. I include romantic history in my ledgers because romance is the only force on earth that reliably moves objects that money cannot. Money moved Almeida onto this river. Romance will move him somewhere money can't reach, and I intend to be standing where he lands.

*Item:* Nine others, various disciplines, various debts, various families who will want answers eventually. Answers are a product line. We sell them late and expensive.

And the cores themselves — forty pounds, give or take, of ancient air sealed in plastic and aluminum. Entry one hundred sixteen. *The Asset.* The single most dangerous object between here and the sea, and currently sitting in a snow pit under a tarp, guarded by people who believe that secrecy is a container. It isn't. Secrecy is a schedule. Everything secret comes out eventually; the only question is whether it comes out on your calendar or someone else's.

Mine, ideally. Or — and here I must be honest with the page, since the page and I have an understanding — ideally it comes out of existence entirely.

* * *

Entry one hundred seventeen. *Mr. Marguerite Almeida.*

Oh, he is my favorite line item. Let me be fair to him, because fairness costs me nothing and he has earned it the hard way.

Marguerite Almeida owes the Club one hundred and ninety thousand dollars, accrued across four years of storm chasing that was really debt chasing, each loan refinanced into the next like a river cutting a canyon. His sister holds the collateral position — not as hostage, we aren't barbarians, merely as *leverage*, which is hostage with better tailoring — and when the salvage contract came up, he took it the way a drowning man takes a rope thrown from a boat that is also sinking. He knows. Don't let the competence fool you; the man reads water like scripture, but he reads contracts like weather warnings, which is to say he notes them and drives toward them anyway.

What he doesn't know — what he cannot know yet, because knowing it would change his behavior and I prefer his behavior exactly as issued — is that his contract was never about rescue.

Let me write it plainly, since the page keeps no secrets from me: the Club sent him upstream because a lone contractor with a debt is a deniable instrument. If the expedition had been dead, he'd have confirmed it and come home. They are not dead, which is inconvenient, but not fatally so, because the second boat — my boat — was always the actual instrument. Marguerite is the reconnaissance. Marguerite is the man who finds the thing so that the thing can be handled. He believes he is a rescue pilot. He is, in the strictest accounting sense, a locator beacon with a heartbeat.

I like him anyway. That's the trouble with this work — you meet the assets personally, and some of them are genuinely good company. Almeida tells stories at the campfire with his hands. He drinks his coffee like it insulted him. Yesterday he lied to me about our route downstream — smoothly, confidently, badly — and I nodded along and thanked him for it, because a man's first lie to you is a gift. It tells you he understands the situation. It tells you he's in the game. I logged it: *Subject initiated deception, 1940 hours, re: channel selection below the moraine. Deception noted with approval.*

He'll lie better next time. They always do. And I will pretend to believe him, and he will pretend to believe that I believed him, and between those two courtesies we will travel several hundred miles of river together like old friends, right up until the moment we aren't.

* * *

Tonight I climb to the high bench above camp with the radio, because the low ground eats signal and because I like to see the glacier while I report on it. It is doing what glaciers do in the warm years — shedding, groaning, ticking like a clock that has decided to hurry. Somewhere up in that white mass sits sixty millennia of proof, and proof, unlike ice, does not melt politely. Proof has to be *managed*.

I key the handset at the scheduled window. Home answers in four seconds, crisp as ever.

"Latch. Status."

"Salvage proceeding on schedule," I say, and I smile as I say it, because I always smile when I say it, and because the phrase is doing honest work. On schedule. Our schedule. The one where the corridor closes in ten days and eleven scientists become a footnote and forty pounds of evidence becomes ballast at the bottom of a lake that didn't used to be a lake. "Contractor located the party. All hands alive. Morale fragile. Route deteriorating."

"Recovery timeline?"

"Weather-dependent," I say, which is true, and means nothing, which is also true. "I'd estimate the situation resolves itself within the fortnight."

A pause on the line. Then: "The Partners ask whether the contractor remains reliable."

I watch the glacier while I consider my answer. Down in the valley, one small light has come on — Almeida's boat, probably, or the transmitter tent, and I file the thought away for morning. The river runs silver under the last light, wide and busy and obedient, carrying somebody's payout to somebody's coast.

"The contractor," I say, "is exactly as reliable as his debts. Which is to say: completely, until further notice."

Home signs off satisfied. I close the ledger, pencil resting in the gutter of the pages, and I sit a while longer in the cold, doing the arithmetic I never write down. Ten days of open water. One river, narrowing. Eleven lives, forty pounds of ice, and one good man who still thinks he's steering.

On schedule, I write, one last time, in the margin. Then I underline it twice, and go down to make coffee for people who trust me.

It's going to be a beautiful week.
