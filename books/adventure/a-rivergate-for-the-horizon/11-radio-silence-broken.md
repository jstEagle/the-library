## 11. Radio Silence, Broken

The transmitter lived in a canvas tent at the edge of camp that Halloran called "Communications" the way a man calls a leaky rowboat "the fleet." It was a Halvorsen 4000, military surplus from some war nobody won, and it had been dead since before I arrived—dead the way things die up here, not broken so much as surrendered.

I want to be clear about something: I did not go into that tent planning anything. I went in because Ines asked me to carry a crate of sample logs off the floor where meltwater had started pooling, and once you're holding somebody's life work in your arms you might as well set it down somewhere dry, and once you've set it down you notice what else is in the tent.

You notice the transmitter.

And you notice, because you are the kind of man who has spent twenty years keeping vehicles alive with baling wire and denial, that its problem is a burned rectifier and a corroded ground strap. Two parts. Two parts and an hour of work. The kind of repair I could do in my sleep, which was exactly the problem, because sleep had become the only place where I wasn't doing arithmetic about who I owed and what they'd take if I didn't pay.

* * *

Let me lay out the ledger, since apparently I think in ledgers now. Latch had done that to me. Three days of shared coffee and cheerful menace and I'd caught his disease: everything itemized.

Item one: my contract with Vantablack. Salvage and recovery of the Halcyon Reach expedition personnel, radio check-in every forty-eight hours on their encrypted band, failure to report constituting default. Default meaning the debt reverts to full principal plus penalties. Full principal being a number I'd stopped saying out loud around the time it grew a second digit.

Item two: my sister. Renata. Who had signed nothing and owed nothing and was nonetheless listed in the Club's collateral schedule under "associated assets," which is what wealthy optimists call a woman teaching fourth grade in Tulsa when they want to remind you they know her address, her school, her Tuesday night bingo habit. Latch had never threatened her. He'd just mentioned her, once, warmly, the way you mention weather. That was worse. A threat has edges. Latch's mentions were fog.

Item three: the crew. Eleven scientists who had voted to vanish rather than let sixty thousand years of atmosphere get priced into someone's portfolio. They'd taken me in, fed me, shown me the ice archive glowing blue in its trench like a piece of sky that had fallen and decided to keep records. And every one of them knew what I was. Amara had made sure of that. "He's one of theirs," she'd told Halloran, in front of me, not cruelly—just factually, the way you'd note a storm cell on radar. Nobody had thrown me out. That was somehow harder to carry than a throwing-out would have been.

So there was the switch, sitting in a dead transmitter, waiting for me to decide which direction to throw it.

Call my handlers: betray the crew, maybe buy Renata another month of being nobody's asset. Stay dark: void the contract, convert the debt back into the monster it had been, and put my sister's address back on somebody's list of interesting places.

There was no third setting. I checked. There's always supposed to be a third setting—the clever compromise, the way to have it both ways—but I've been alive long enough to know the third setting is usually just the first one wearing a disguise.

* * *

I told myself I was fixing it for them.

That was the story I worked up while I soldered, and it was even mostly true. The expedition needed a working radio like a drowning man needs lungs. If the calving season came early—if the river closed—they'd need to raise a rescue frequency, coordinate with the survey office in Fort Sereno, do all the legitimate things a legitimate scientific expedition does when it wants to come home. A dead transmitter wasn't silence as principle. It was silence as liability.

Halloran found me elbow-deep in the chassis on the second evening. He stood in the tent flap for a while without speaking, which seemed to be his native mode, then said, "Ines says you have good hands."

"Ines is generous."

"She also says you looked at the encryption module for a long time before you touched anything else." He let that sit. Halloran was good at letting things sit; he'd had practice, three weeks of it. "We pulled the crypto boards when we went dark. You'll have noticed."

"I noticed."

"So whatever you build this thing back into, it will broadcast in the clear. Anybody listening, hears. Including anybody downstream with a better antenna and worse intentions."

"Latch," I said.

"Mr. Latch," Halloran agreed, "and whoever Mr. Latch reports to, which is a list longer and wealthier than either of us wants to contemplate after dark." He crouched next to me, old knees cracking like river ice. "Marguerite. I'm going to ask you a question and I don't want speed. Speed is your religion, I can see that, and I respect that a man should have one, but not for this."

I waited.

"If you fix this radio, whose hands are on the key?"

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about debt: it isn't the money. Money is abstract; money is numbers on a screen in a velvet room in a city I try not to think about. What debt actually is, is ownership of your future decisions. Every choice I'd made since the Club bought my paper had come pre-decided, and I'd walked upstream telling myself I was a free agent on a salvage job when really I was a tool with a heartbeat, dispatched to locate eleven people whose disappearance was worth more than their rescue.

I'd known that. Knowing it and feeling it were different weathers.

Because here was the thing I kept circling: the crew hadn't hidden from the world out of vanity. Ines had walked me through the trench, showed me the annual layers stacked like the pages of a book nobody was supposed to read yet, and explained, patiently, what it meant—that the collapse models were wrong, that the melt was running decades ahead of the published record, that half the Club's portfolio was built on betting the record was right. Sixty thousand years of sky, preserved in blue ice, saying: *you were lied to, and the liars got rich.*

"They didn't go dark to protect the data," Ines had said. "Data doesn't need protecting. It needs *time*. Time to verify, time to publish right, time so it lands as science instead of as rumor that gets litigated into mush by lawyers who bill by the hour. We stole three weeks from them. That's all it ever was. Three weeks of quiet."

Three weeks of quiet. And I was the man whose job description was loudness—check-ins, reports, position broadcasts, the whole apparatus of being watched. My handlers didn't want news from me. They wanted proof of surveillance. Silence, from me, was the one thing I couldn't give them without the whole structure coming down on Renata first and me second.

* * *

I finished the repair on the third night, or I finished everything except the decision.

The tent at midnight has a particular quality up there in the lateral valley—the light never quite leaves in summer, so it's less darkness than a long gray hesitation, and you can work without lamps and pretend the world hasn't ended anywhere. I had the Halvorsen open on the folding table, new rectifier seated, ground strap scraped bright and bolted down, power leads run to the camp's little hydro battery that chattered in the creek like a nervous animal. The set would live. I tested it into a dummy load and heard the hiss of a healthy receiver drinking current, and that hiss sounded like a held breath.

Amara came in without knocking, because Amara had never once knocked in seven years of knowing me, including the years when knocking would have been advisable.

"You've been out here three nights," she said.

"I sleep fine."

"You don't sleep at all. That's different from fine." She picked up the spare crypto board from the corner, turned it over in her hands like a tarot card. "Halloran told you what we pulled."

"He told me."

"And you're going to tell me what you're going to do, or you're going to do it and tell me after, because those are the two gears you have." She set the board down. "I've watched you for two weeks, Marguerite. You calculate exits the way other people breathe. Doorways, ridgelines, boat angles. I used to find it thrilling. Now I find it diagnostic."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It's accurate. Fairness is a different thing." She stepped closer, and the gray light made her face hard to read, which was a mercy. "I'm not asking you to be one of us. I'm asking you to decide what you are before the radio makes you choose out loud, in front of everyone, at the worst possible moment. Because that's how it will happen. It always happens at the worst possible moment. You taught me that."

She left. She was good at leaving; she'd had practice too, and I'd given her most of it.

* * *

I sat with the set until the gray light turned to actual morning, doing the arithmetic one more time, slower.

If I keyed the encrypted band—except I couldn't, the boards were gone, that door was welded shut, and maybe that was Halloran's real question answered before I could answer it. If I transmitted in the clear to Fort Sereno, position and status, salvage proceeding, crew located and alive—I'd be reporting to anyone listening, which included Latch, which included the Club, which meant handing them the coordinates of the thing they'd sent me to find. Eleven people and sixty thousand years of evidence, gift-wrapped, delivered by the man they owned.

If I stayed dark past the next check-in window, the contract defaulted. Debt reinstated at full principal. And Renata—

Renata, who was nine when our father's truck went off the Sereno bridge road and I decided, at thirteen, that velocity was the answer to grief. Renata, who lent me her tuition refund when the debt first came due and never once mentioned it again. Renata, whose name sat in a syndicate's collateral file because I'd signed a page without reading the part where they defined "associated assets," and I had read contracts for a living, that was the humiliating thing, I'd simply chosen not to read that one because hope had made me lazy.

Stay dark and I couldn't protect her. Call out and I couldn't protect them. The switch had two positions and both of them were people I loved, and the machine between them was me.

Except.

Except I kept coming back to what Ines said. *Time.* The crew hadn't asked me to lie forever. They'd asked for three weeks, and the three weeks were nearly spent, and the calving season was breathing down the river like a wolf at a door. Which meant the choice in front of me wasn't actually silence versus betrayal. It was timing. It was whether the truth went out on my schedule or Latch's.

And there was a third thing, small and ugly and honest: if I transmitted in the clear, I could say true things that served the crew. *Crew located. Alive. Medical situation stable.* All verifiable, all harmless. And I could leave out the one thing that mattered—the trench, the cores, the archive—because a clear-channel report doesn't require completeness. It requires accuracy. Latch would hear a salvage succeeding. He wouldn't hear the treasure, because I wouldn't say the word.

Was that a lie? Sort of. Was it the first lie? No. The first lie I'd told well enough to hate myself for was over campfire coffee, chapter and verse, and this would be smaller, a sin of omission, almost gentle. But sins compound up here like interest. I knew that better than anyone on the river.

* * *

At 0600 by my watch, with the camp still asleep and the glacier making its low tectonic throat-clearing somewhere above the valley, I powered the Halvorsen fully up, tuned to the Fort Sereno traffic frequency, and keyed the mic.

"This is Almeida, salvage contract seven-seven-one, aboard with the Halcyon Reach party. All hands accounted for. Condition stable. Proceeding downriver at earliest safe window. Out."

Eleven seconds of transmission. I timed it. Eleven seconds, plain voice, no coordinates, no mention of ice older than agriculture, no word that would make a certain affable man in a downstream camp reach for his ledger.

Then I sat there shaking, which surprised me. It shouldn't have. I'd just thrown the switch, and the direction I'd thrown it was sideways—not toward the crew, not toward the Club, but through some gap between them that might not exist tomorrow. I'd bought time. Time was the whole currency up here, Ines had said so, and now I was spending mine in eleven-second increments and hoping the exchange rate held.

The receiver hissed. Then, faint through the static, Fort Sereno acknowledged: *Copy, seven-seven-one. Relay noted.*

Relay noted. Somewhere south of here, a clerk would log those words, and a clerk's clerk would forward them, and eventually a man named Latch would read them over breakfast and smile his terrible smile and write *on schedule* in a book where I was an item.

But so would the survey office. So would the weather service. So would anyone with a scanner and patience, and among the patient listeners of the Sereno basin were journalists, and rival glaciologists, and at least one retired professor Ines trusted with her life, who now knew the Halcyon Reach was alive and moving. The clear channel cut both ways. That was the point. That had always been the point of clear channels: you can't control who hears the truth once it's loose.

I killed the set and sat in the gray morning with my hands flat on the table, feeling lighter and heavier in equal measure, the way you feel after putting down a weight you know you'll have to pick up again.

Outside, Ines was standing at the trench rim, looking down at the blue layers, and she turned when the tent flap moved. She didn't ask what I'd transmitted. She just looked at me for a long moment, and then at the antenna wire I'd strung through a crack in the tent wall, and then back at me.

"The window's closing faster than the models said," she said quietly. "Calving's early. We measured the serac line this morning—it's retreated forty meters in two days. Marguerite, we have days. Maybe four. Maybe fewer."

Behind her, far downriver, the air carried a sound I'd been pretending not to hear since I arrived: a deep, percussive boom, rolling up the valley like distant artillery finding its range.

The glacier was clearing its throat. And it was calling us home.
