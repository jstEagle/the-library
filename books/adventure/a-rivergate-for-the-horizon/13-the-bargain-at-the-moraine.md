## 13. The Bargain at the Moraine

*Marguerite*

Halloran gave us two hours and a radio that didn't work, which was his way of saying he trusted us and also didn't want to watch.

"Take the moraine," he said, not looking up from the core logs. "Clear your heads."

The way he said *clear your heads*, you'd have thought he was prescribing it. Maybe he was. Eleven people in a lateral valley with forty pounds of ice that could bankrupt half of Montana's old money—heads were going to need clearing, or something in camp was going to crack before the glacier did.

So Amara and I climbed.

The moraine ran along the valley's north shoulder like a spine of broken furniture—rock the glacier had chewed up and spat out over centuries, gray and sharp-edged under a sky the color of dishwater. Above us the ice wall held its own opinion of our presence. It groaned sometimes. Not often. Just enough to remind you who owned the property.

Amara walked ahead of me. She'd always been a faster hiker than she let on, back when we were together, back when she pretended to stroll so I could keep pace at my own idiotic sprint. Seven years hadn't slowed her. Seven years had, if anything, made her more efficient about everything, including silence.

We'd been walking twenty minutes when she finally said it.

"You're doing the math again."

"What math?"

"The exit math." She stopped on a boulder the size of a kitchen table and turned around. "You've been doing it since you walked into camp. Boat fuel. Rations. Radio range. Which direction Latch's men are watching from. You think nobody notices, but you have this—" she tapped her temple, "—this little twitch. Right here. You get it when you're calculating how to leave a room."

"I don't have a twitch."

"You absolutely have a twitch. I used to find it charming. Now it just makes me tired."

I sat down on the boulder next to hers because my legs suggested it and because sitting down felt like a small honest act, and honesty was apparently the theme of the evening whether I'd agreed to it or not.

The light was going blue. Up-valley, the glacier's face caught the last of it and threw it back wrong—not white but a deep bruised teal, like something lit from inside by an idea it hadn't shared yet. Ines had tried to explain to me what was in that ice. Sixty thousand years of atmosphere, pressed into bands like tree rings, each one a page of a book the Club would very much prefer stayed shut. I understood maybe a third of it. I understood all of what it meant for me, though. That part required no science.

"Ask me," I said.

Amara looked at me sideways. "Ask you what?"

"Whatever you've been not asking since I got here. You've got a list. I can hear it rattling."

She laughed—one short syllable, no warmth in it, all edge. "Fine. Why does a supply pilot with a reputation for being allergic to commitment take a solo contract three thousand miles from his sister's hospital bills? Because that's the other thing you do, Marguerite. You send money home every month like clockwork. I saw the transfer stubs in your kit bag. You weren't subtle."

"I'm never subtle about money. Money's the one thing I've never lied about."

"So lie to me now," she said. "See how it goes."

I should mention, for the record, that I'd rehearsed versions of this conversation. In the boat, coming upriver, I'd built whole architectures of half-truth: the debt as bad luck, the Club as a lender like any other, my presence as coincidence wearing a contract's clothes. I'd gotten good at them. Chapter six of my life, if you will—the first outright lie told well enough to hate myself for, delivered over coffee to Mr. Latch with a smile I'd have charged admission for.

But sitting on a moraine at dusk with Amara Voss looking at me like she could read the fine print on my soul, the architectures collapsed. All of them. At once.

"I owe the Vantablack Club four hundred and ten thousand dollars," I said.

Her expression didn't change. That was worse than gasping.

"It started as storm-chase funding. Equipment, fuel, the data contracts. They fund people like me the way casinos fund gamblers—generously, right up until they own you. The interest structure is—" I made a gesture that failed to capture it. "There isn't a word for what their interest structure is. 'Predatory' implies there's still a prey animal with rights."

"And the salvage job?"

"Wasn't a job. It was a leash. They knew the Halcyon Reach went quiet. They knew before anyone official did. And they sent me upstream alone, with a radio they controlled, to find out what happened to eleven scientists who'd stumbled onto something." I nodded up-valley, toward the ice. "Onto that."

Amara sat very still. Wind came off the glacier and moved her hair and she didn't bother fixing it, which is how I knew how carefully she was listening. Amara fixed her hair when she wasn't paying attention. When she paid attention, she went statue-still.

"They sent you to find us," she said slowly, "and report."

"They sent me to find you and confirm the asset. Latch followed two days behind me with 'support contractors.' He's been logging the river, the crew, the samples. We're line items, Amara. All of us. I heard him do it on the radio—cheerful as a man reading a grocery list. 'Salvage proceeding on schedule.'" I looked at my hands. "On schedule means you don't come back."

"And your sister?"

"Nadia. Twenty-six. Congenital heart thing they finally caught last year. The surgery's done—she's recovering, she's going to be fine—but the bills are the size of mountains and the Club holds them. Holds *her*, effectively. Collateral." I swallowed. "My contract says if I go dark, if I fail to report, the debt accelerates and they move on her. That's the trap. That's the whole architecture of it. I find you, I feed them enough to keep them patient, and somewhere downstream I figure out how to betray exactly one group of people instead of two."

The glacier groaned. Long and low, like a whale thinking.

Amara stood up. Walked three steps away. Came back.

"When you reached Tomas," she said. "At the last camp. He had you dead to rights and you talked him down. Halloran told me. You could have radioed Latch right then. One transmission: found survivors, armed, uncooperative. And instead you spent an hour convincing a paranoid man with a rifle that you were worth trusting." Her voice did something complicated. "Why?"

"Because he reminded me of Nadia," I said. "Stubborn. Armed with the truth and no plan. You can't hand people like that to the Vantablack Club. It's like feeding them to weather."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have that doesn't make me sound noble, and I can't afford noble right now. Noble gets people killed."

"No," Amara said. "What gets people killed is a man who loves at highway speed. Who commits fully to whatever's directly in front of his windshield and treats everything else as scenery. I know, Marguerite. I *was* the scenery once. Seven years ago you drove away from the best thing either of us ever had because the horizon looked more interesting than the room you were standing in."

The words landed where she aimed them, which was always her gift.

"I didn't drive away because it was boring."

"No. You drove away because staying meant slowing down, and slowing down meant feeling things at an actual human velocity, and you'd built your entire identity on outrunning that." She crouched in front of me, close enough that I could see the windburn on her cheeks and the exhaustion underneath her competence. "And now look at you. Stuck on a river that narrows every day. A destination you can't outrun. The universe has a sense of humor after all."

"I've been saying that for weeks. Nobody laughs."

"I'm laughing on the inside. It's a medical condition."

Despite everything—despite the debt and the ledger and the ice waiting to kill us—I laughed. Small, cracked, real. She waited it out with the patience of a woman who'd triaged worse.

Then she said the thing.

"I'm going to offer you something," she said, "and you're going to want to refuse it, so I want you to listen to the whole sentence before your twitch starts."

"Okay."

"If it comes to it—if Latch corners you, if the choice is the archive or Nadia—you give them the archive."

"*No.*"

"Listen to the sentence. I told you to listen to the sentence." She put a hand on my chest, flat over the sternum, the way you'd steady a spooked animal. "You give them the coordinates, the cores, all of it, if that's what saves her. And then you tell the world anyway. Because here's the thing you haven't figured out yet, Marguerite, the thing I've had three weeks on this river to figure out: the data doesn't live in the ice. It lives in us. Ines has sixty thousand years of atmospheric record in her head and her notebooks and her head again. I have the medical baselines. Halloran has the survey. You can burn every core on this river and the truth survives, because eleven people carried it out of the ice and truth is the one cargo that replicates."

"That's—" I started.

"Not finished." Her hand pressed harder. "You think your debt is money. It isn't. Your debt is the belief that you are the kind of man who has to buy his way out of every situation, that every problem has a price and you're the one holding the checkbook. The Club didn't create that. They just found it and monetized it. So here's my testimony, freely given, entered into the record of this moraine: some truths are worth bankruptcy. Some truths are worth *more* than bankruptcy—they're worth the whole portfolio, yours included. I watched a crew vote to go silent to protect this data and I voted with them and I have regretted it every single day, because silence is just a slower way of selling out. You have a chance I didn't. You have a transmitter and a conscience and a talent for lying to terrible men, and I am telling you, as the woman who loved you and the woman who still does, God help me: when the moment comes, spend yourself. Spend all of it. Bankruptcy is survivable. Being the man who kept the receipt is not."

The ice boomed. Somewhere up-valley, a serac the size of a church let go and the sound rolled down the moraine and through the soles of my boots, and neither of us flinched, because we were already shaking.

"You still love me," I said, because I have the emotional intelligence of a torque wrench and someone has to say these things.

"I love the man you're becoming against your will," she said. "It's very annoying. I had plans to be over you by now."

"The expedition's been dark three weeks. You had orders to stay dark. Joining a doomed mission to disappear—that's not a glaciology decision, Amara. That's a heartbreak decision."

"My motives are peer-reviewed, thank you."

"By whom?"

"By me. At three a.m. Repeatedly. The results are inconclusive." But she smiled when she said it, and the smile did something to the air between us that seven years and four hundred miles of river hadn't managed.

We sat there while the blue light died completely. Stars came out hard and cold, the way they only do near ice. Below us the camp glimmered—two lanterns, the transmitter tent, shapes moving. Forty pounds of the planet's memory, packed in foam and prayer.

"Two hours," I said eventually. "Halloran will send someone."

"He'll send Ines. She'll pretend it's about ice cores."

"She'll actually talk about ice cores. For an hour."

"God, I've missed being annoyed by people I love."

I helped her up off the rock and we started back along the moraine, single file, her in front, and I noticed—for the first time in seven years, maybe for the first time ever—that I wasn't calculating anything. No exits. No angles. No twitch. Just the path, the woman, the ice, and the strange weightless terror of a man who has decided, provisionally, in the dark, to stop running.

Halfway down, her radio crackled. Ines's voice, tight and fast, stripped of all pretense of ice-core small talk:

"Amara. Get down here. The glacier's calving early—and Latch's boats just cleared the confluence. Both of them. Heading upriver."

* * *

*Amara*

Later—much later, after the alarms and the maps and Halloran's face going the color of old paper—I would try to reconstruct the exact moment the moraine stopped being a confession and became a bargain. I never could. Memory doesn't timestamp things; it just files them by weight.

But I remember this: walking back down through the dark with Marguerite behind me, and for once the silence between us wasn't a wound. It was load-bearing. Two people carrying the same heavy thing, taking turns.

And I remember thinking, with the clarity that only comes at altitude and after midnight: *he's going to do it. He's going to choose wrong, or choose right, or choose both at once, and either way it's going to cost him everything he thinks he is.*

Behind us, the glacier calved again—a shot like the world clearing its throat—and ahead, down in the lantern-light, Ines was already shouting numbers.

The window was closing. Everything was.
