## 14. Loading the Blue Ice

There is a particular kind of silence that comes off a glacier at four in the morning, and it is not the absence of sound. It is the sound of something enormous deciding not to speak yet. I have spent thirty-one years listening to ice, and I can tell you the difference between a glacier that is resting and one that is rehearsing. The Sereno's child up there in the lateral valley was rehearsing. Every few minutes it cleared its throat—a crack like a rifle shot, then a long settling groan—and every time it did, Ines would look up from her core barrel and I would pretend not to see her look.

We had voted, three nights ago, to let Marguerite Almeida into the camp but not into the truth. Nine for, two against, one abstention that everyone understood to be mine. I said we should tell him nothing, and I lost, and so now we were loading forty pounds of sixty-thousand-year-old atmosphere onto boats piloted by a man who owed money to people who bet on disasters, and I was calling that a plan because the alternative was calling it what it was.

"Dr. Halloran." Ines again. "You're staring at the ridge."

"I'm admiring the view."

"The view is two men with binoculars and a radio mast."

"Then I'm admiring their equipment."

She didn't laugh. Ines Okonkwo has never once laughed at my jokes, which I respect, because most people laugh at them out of politeness and politeness is how expeditions die. She set down the core sleeve and came over to where I stood by the cook tent, and she lowered her voice the way you lower your voice in a church or a morgue.

"They moved during the night," she said. "The tall one. He walked the ridgeline at 0300. Counted our tents. Stood over the cache pit for four minutes."

"You counted his minutes?"

"I count everything. It's the job."

It was the job. That was the whole tragedy of us, if I'm honest—thirty-one years of counting things, and the thing we'd finally counted was too big to put down. Sixty thousand years of atmosphere, pressed into blue ice like flowers in a book. A record that said, in carbon and methane and dust, that what the Vantablack Club had shorted—the collapse timeline, the melt horizon, the whole actuarial gospel of managed decline—was wrong by centuries. Not wrong in the direction of comfort. Wrong in the direction of panic.

You cannot insure against a truth like that. You can only bury it or broadcast it, and the Club had sent Mr. Latch upriver to make sure we chose burial.

* * *

By seven we had the extraction table rigged between two sawhorses, and the whole crew working in pairs, and I will say this for scientists under pressure: they complain beautifully and then they work. Priya ran the bandsaw with the concentration of a surgeon and the vocabulary of a longshoreman whenever the blade chattered. Osei logged each segment—depth, year-band, condition—in a ledger he'd started waterproofing with candle wax back when we still believed we'd be evacuated properly. Amara Voss supervised the packing protocol with the calm of a woman who had already survived worse than this, though I suspected her definition of worse involved a man rather than a mountain.

The cores themselves were gorgeous. I'll admit my bias. Forty thousand years of snowfall compressed to glass, each band a page: ash from eruptions no human witnessed, lead from Rome's smelters, the sudden obscene spike of the last century reading like a scream in an otherwise quiet library. When you hold a section up to the light, the bubbles catch fire. Ancient air, burning without heat. I have watched younger scientists cry over a core, and I have never once thought them foolish.

"Segment fourteen's cracked," Priya announced. "Hairline, along the 31,000 band."

"Seal it anyway," I said. "Cracked history is still history."

"That's either wisdom or denial, boss."

"It's usually both. That's what wisdom is."

Osei snorted. Even Ines almost smiled. And above us, on the ridge, the tall man with the binoculars shifted position, and the almost died where it stood.

* * *

I want to be precise about Latch's men, because precision is all I have left. They did not threaten us. They did not carry weapons openly, though the tall one wore his jacket wrong for the temperature and the short one stood always with his weight forward, like a man perpetually about to apologize for tackling someone. They called themselves support contractors. They drank our coffee and complimented our camp and asked questions shaped like small talk: How many boats? How much cargo weight? Which channel were we planning—north fork or the moraine cut?

Every question was a ledger entry. I understood that. What I understood less well, in those early hours, was why the Club wanted the inventory at all. If they meant to let us die on the river, why learn our tonnage?

Because, I realized somewhere around the ninth core, they might not mean to let us die. They might mean to let us *arrive*—somewhere remote, somewhere deniable—and take the archive from our frozen hands. Dead men don't file insurance claims, but neither do they testify. Either way, the manifest was the murder weapon. They were weighing us.

So I lied. I told the short one—we called him Deacon behind his back, because he had the mournful face of a man who enjoyed funerals—that we were running light, twelve pounds of samples, mostly duplicates. I told him north fork. Both lies cost me nothing and bought us hours, maybe a day, and I hated myself with the mild, familiar self-hatred of a man who has discovered lying is a skill like any other and he is better at it than he'd hoped.

"You're doing great," Ines murmured, passing me with a loaded crate.

"I'm doing treason," I said. "Against whom remains to be determined."

* * *

At noon we sealed the last tube. Forty pounds became thirty-eight through sublimation and my refusal to pack the duplicate sections—let the Club chase phantom ice; there was a poetry to it I intended to enjoy later, if later arrived. We wrapped each segment in foil, then foam, then waxed canvas, and packed them into the insulated crates Amara had cannibalized from the medical stores, and Osei stenciled them, at my instruction, LABORATORY GLASSWARE — FRAGILE — NON-ESSENTIAL.

"If they open them," Priya said, "the stencil won't matter."

"If they open them," I said, "we've already lost, and the stencil will be the least of anyone's concerns."

Marguerite appeared at the edge of camp while we were lashing crates to the boat frames. He does that—appears, without announcement, the way weather appears. He'd been checking the river since dawn; I could see the river in him, in the way he stood slightly downstream of everything, always oriented toward the water. A man built like a compass needle that only points one direction.

"How's the cargo?" he asked.

"Heavy," I said. "Fragile. Older than every government on Earth."

"So, normal freight."

"Normal freight," I agreed, and we both performed the smile that men perform when they are each hiding something from the other and have agreed, silently, to keep pretending otherwise. I had voted against trusting him and lost, and I am a gracious loser in public. In private, in the ledger I keep behind my eyes, the vote was still open.

He looked at the crates a long moment. Then he said, quietly, "Whatever's in them—it's worth dying for? Because the river's going to ask."

And I gave him the answer I'd rehearsed, the professional answer, the leader's answer: "Nothing is worth dying for. Some things are worth *carrying*."

He nodded like a man who'd heard a fortune cookie and gone back to his meal, and went to check the lashings, and I stood there knowing I had just lied to him twice in one sentence, because there was absolutely something in those crates I would die for, and I had known it since the moment Ines pulled the first section and the light came through it, and every hour since, that knowledge had been hardening in me like the ice itself.

* * *

Here is the decision I made that afternoon, alone, and I record it here as plainly as I can because plainness is the only honesty available to a man who has spent the day lying:

If the mission fails—if Latch's men take the boats, if the calving closes the pass, if the river decides against us—I will burn the archive.

Not surrender it. Not scatter it. Burn it. There is a flare kit in the stern of the lead boat, ostensibly for signaling. There is white gas in the cook stove. There is me, and I am sixty-three years old, and I have published enough papers. The world does not need another cautious old man's signature on someone else's cover-up. If the truth cannot reach the light, then it will not reach the Club either. Sixty thousand years of atmosphere will go up in ninety seconds of orange, and I will go up with it, and I find—I have examined this feeling from every angle, turned it like a core section in the light—that I am not frightened of it. I am *relieved*. That is the part that should worry me. Relief is what martyrdom feels like from the inside, and I recognized the sensation the way you recognize a family resemblance in a stranger's face.

Duty, I thought, watching the ridge line glow gold toward evening, curdles. You start with obligation and end with appetite. Somewhere between the vote and the packing, my duty stopped being about the crew getting home and started being about me being the man who refuses, the man who burns it, the man whose last act means something. That's not principle anymore. That's vanity wearing principle's coat. I know this. Knowing it changed nothing. Perhaps that's the truest thing I can write: I saw the rot clearly, named it, and kept it, the way you keep a cracked core because the band inside it matters more than the flaw.

Ines found me at dusk, sitting on the cache pit lid like a dragon on the wrong kind of hoard.

"You've decided something," she said.

"I've decided several things. Most of them are classified even from me."

"Halloran." She sat beside me. Below us the river ran gray-green and fast, and far downstream, invisible but audible in imagination, the glacier was clearing its throat. "If it comes to it—"

"It won't come to it."

"If it comes to it, you don't get to decide alone. That's not how this crew works. We voted to go dark together. We vote on the rest."

"We may not have time for a vote."

"Then we'd better make time," she said, "because the alternative is you doing something noble and stupid and permanent, and I've read your papers, Tomas. Nobility is not your genre. Rigor is. Be rigorous. Rigorous means nobody gets to be a hero, including you."

I laughed—actually laughed, first time in weeks—and she allowed herself half a smile, which from Ines was a standing ovation. And I told her yes, fine, a vote, democracy to the end, and I believed about sixty percent of what I said, which is a strong majority for me these days.

* * *

Night came down cold and clear. From the ridge, the tall man's silhouette held its post against the last light, patient as bookkeeping. Down at the boats, Marguerite worked by headlamp, checking lines, testing the current with a stick, talking low with Amara in a register I pretended not to hear. The crates sat lashed and stenciled and full of burning ancient air.

Tomorrow we launch. The window is closing faster than the models said—Ines recalculated twice and got shorter answers both times—and the pass will seal like a wound, and everything we are and everything we found will be committed to moving water.

I checked the flare kit one last time. I told myself it was inventory.

I am a rigorous man. But I have learned tonight that rigor has a floor, and beneath the floor there is a man holding a match, and he is me, and he is very calm, and the glacier overhead has finally stopped rehearsing and begun, softly, to sing.
