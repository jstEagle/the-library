## 15. First Rapids

We launched at 4:47 in the morning, because Halloran wanted the light behind us and Latch wanted to be seen wanting it too.

Three boats on gray water. Eleven scientists who had spent three weeks pretending not to exist, now sitting in the open like exhibits. Forty pounds of ice core packed in insulated crates that Ines had labeled, with a straight face, *AGRICULTURAL SAMPLES — DO NOT FREEZE*, which was either a joke or a prayer. And two Vantablack men in matching drysuits, riding in my boat and Halloran's, carrying themselves like chaperones at a dance where everyone already knew how the night would end.

The one in my boat was named Pell. He was young, broad through the shoulders, and had the particular stillness of a man who has been told his job is to watch rather than act, and who resents every second of it. He sat in the bow seat facing backward—facing me—and did not once in the first hour look at the river.

"Beautiful morning," I said.

He didn't answer. Fine. I've driven with worse company. I've *been* worse company.

The Sereno was running high. That's the phrase we use when we mean the river has stopped being a river and started being an argument. Three weeks of melt off the upper glacier had swollen every channel, drowned every gravel bar I'd marked on the way up, and turned the drowned research station into a fully committed submarine. The water was the color of skim milk and moved with the low, muscular hum of something that knows its own weight.

I read it the way I read a storm wall: not all at once, but in pieces. The color of the current where it bent around the point—that told me depth. The texture of the surface downstream of the bend—smooth glass over chaos meant a hole; standing waves in a neat line meant a ledge you could ride. A V of white water pointing upstream meant rocks. A V pointing down meant a channel, and God help you if you mistook one for the other at speed.

I'd never run this stretch. Nobody had, not in this water, not this year. The survey charts in the camp were eleven years old and drawn by a student named Ines Okonkwo who had, she informed me the night before while tapping the faded contour lines, "been wrong about everything except the things that mattered."

"So which is which?" I'd asked.

She'd smiled for the first time since I'd known her. It made her look fifteen years younger and considerably more dangerous. "You'll find out."

* * *

We ran the first rapid at 6:15, and I want to be honest about what happened there, because honesty is the currency I have least of these days and I like to spend what little remains on myself.

The rapid was a quarter mile of standing waves stacked three high, with a seam of green water threading down the right side that might have been a channel or might have been a recirculating hole wearing a channel costume. From the eddy above, it looked like a coin flip. From the eddy above, everything does.

I put us on the seam. Not because I knew—I didn't—but because I've spent twenty years making decisions with sixty percent information and calling it instinct, and the seam had the right look: the foam line downstream of it was continuous, unbroken, no boil. Water that comes back around always leaves a scar on the surface. This water was moving on.

Pell grabbed the gunwales when the bow stood up on the first wave. I heard him exhale through his teeth—a small, involuntary sound, the sound of a man discovering that watching danger and being inside it are different professions entirely.

"Left," I said. "Hard left."

"Why?"

"Because the water's telling me so."

He leaned. The boat swung. We threaded between two holes that were close enough together to share a name, dropped into the trough below, and came out the bottom in a spray of glacier milk that soaked us both to the skin.

Behind us, Halloran's boat followed my line exactly—he'd made me lead for precisely this reason, though he'd framed it as courtesy. Amara was in his boat. She didn't scream on the drops. She laughed, once, a short bright bark of a laugh that carried across the water and landed somewhere under my sternum, where I have been storing things I can't afford to feel since the moraine.

Ines took her own boat down the left side, off my line entirely, through water I'd judged unrunnable. She came out clean, raised one paddle in salute or rebuke, and shouted something in Portuguese that Pell asked me to translate.

"She says your line was cowardly," I said.

"That's not what she said."

"No," I admitted. "It isn't."

* * *

By midmorning we'd run four rapids and portaged one—the river had eaten the old trail, so we lined the boats through a chute on ropes, hip-deep in current cold enough to make your bones file complaints. The ice core crates went across on a rope bridge Ines rigged between two boulders, swaying over whitewater, forty pounds of sixty-thousand-year-old atmosphere swinging like a pendulum over its own grave.

Latch watched from the bank. He hadn't come on the water. Of course he hadn't. Mr. Latch rode in the support skiff with the outboard motor and the satellite phone and the cooler of good coffee, arriving at each rapid after the danger had concluded, cheerful as a man touring his own holdings.

That was the thing about Latch I was beginning to understand. He wasn't here for the adventure. He was here for the *inventory*. Every rapid we survived was an asset depreciating. Every mile downriver was a line item moving toward resolution. When he waved at me from the bank, he wasn't waving hello. He was ticking a box.

At the lunch stop, he found me checking the boats.

"Marguerite." He handed me coffee, real coffee, the kind that costs more per cup than I used to make per hour. "Remarkable running. You read that water like a ledger."

"I read it like water."

"Same thing, ultimately." He looked downriver, squinting against the glare. "The question I have—and forgive me, it's a professional question—is about the route ahead. My maps show the canyon narrowing past the confluence. Two channels, yes? The east fork and the west."

"The maps are old."

"They usually are." He sipped. "Which do you intend?"

Here it was. The first outright lie of the day, served with coffee.

I had spent the previous evening with Ines and Halloran over the old survey charts, and the answer was simple: the east fork. The west fork was shorter, wider, and dead—it ended in a rock garden the meltwater hadn't finished drowning yet, a maze of granite teeth with nowhere to land and no way back. The east fork was longer, narrower, meaner, but it ran. Ines had surveyed it as a student. She remembered it the way you remember a house you grew up in, including the parts that have burned down since.

But if I said *east*, Latch would send his skiff ahead, or behind, or would radio whatever waited downriver, and the east fork's only advantage—its narrowness, its hostility, its refusal to accommodate a wide-beamed support boat with an outboard—would be neutralized before we reached it.

So I lied.

"West," I said, and pointed, and made my voice easy. "East forks up into dead water this time of year. West carries the volume. It's longer but it's honest."

"Honest," Latch repeated, tasting the word. "How rare, on a river."

"You get what you get."

"Mm." He looked at me for a moment longer than comfort allows, and I understood that he was not deciding whether to believe me. He was deciding how much it would cost him later if I was lying, and whether that price was worth the pleasure of letting me think I'd gotten away with it. Then he smiled his bookkeeper's smile and said, "West, then. Lead on."

I hated myself for maybe four seconds, which I considered excellent self-discipline.

* * *

The afternoon water got worse.

Not harder, exactly—worse in the way that matters, which is less predictable. The melt was peaking with the day's heat, and the river rose visibly while we watched it, swallowing the gravel bar where we'd lunched, climbing the rocks we'd tied off to. Channels that existed at noon were suggestions by three o'clock. I started reading the water not for what it was but for what it was becoming, which is a skill I learned chasing storms and never expected to use on something without a sky.

There's a moment in every bad stretch of road—every whiteout, every hail core—when you stop being a person driving and become a system responding. Hands, eyes, machine, weather, all one circuit. I used to love that moment. It was the closest thing to peace I'd ever found: total occupation, zero remainder. No debt, no sister, no woman seven years gone laughing in another man's boat. Just the next hundred yards, forever.

Somewhere in the fifth rapid, I noticed I wasn't feeling it anymore.

I was reading the water fine. Better than fine—I was reading it like scripture, every seam and eddy line annotated. But part of me was standing outside the circuit, watching, and the part outside was thinking about Amara's laugh, and about the crate of ice swinging over the gorge, and about the fact that I had just lied to a murderer with a smile and called it navigation.

Competence used to be my whole personality. Now it was just a tool. And tools, I was learning, can be pointed at things.

Pell spoke for the first time in hours. "You're going too fast."

"We're going exactly fast enough."

"The others are falling behind."

"The others have Halloran, and Halloran doesn't follow anyone he doesn't trust, and he trusts me today, which should frighten you more than the river." I glanced back. Both boats were there, riding my wake, Amara's face set forward, Ines standing in hers like a figurehead. "Besides. Speed is the only thing keeping us ahead of that."

I nodded at the horizon upstream, where the glacier shoulder hung above the valley, and where—faint, intermittent, like artillery two ridges away—the calving had begun to talk to itself. Cracks. Reports. The sound of a very large thing revising its plans.

Pell looked. For the first time since the launch, he looked at the river, really looked, and I watched him understand that he was not on a boat. He was on a schedule.

* * *

We made camp at dusk on a bench of gravel above a long pool, twelve miles down from the launch, which Ines called "adequate" in the tone other people reserve for miracles. The boats came out of the water looking like they'd been chewed. So did most of the crew. Dr. Okafor had lost a paddle and gained a bruise the size of a dinner plate. Tomas Reyes, who had refused rescue from me in Chapter Five of my life and now rowed stroke like he'd been born in the hull, quietly re-rigged everyone's foot braces without being asked.

Amara found me at the fire line, checking the crates.

"Forty pounds of ice," she said, crouching beside me, "and you check them like they're sleeping children."

"They're the only passengers I can't afford to lose."

"You lost me once."

The firelight did something unfair to her face. "I drove away," I said. "There's a difference. Losing implies I didn't know where you were."

"And now?"

"Now I know exactly where you are, and it's the middle of a river I've never run, with a syndicate's man in every boat, and I'm lying to keep us alive." I cinched a strap. "Progress of a kind."

She almost smiled. Almost. "Latch asked you about the forks."

My hands went still on the strap. "You were listening."

"I'm a medic. Listening is the whole job." She lowered her voice. "What did you tell him?"

"What did I tell him," I repeated, and looked at her, and decided—in half a second, the same half-second I'd given the green seam that morning—that she deserved the truth even when the truth was a lie. "I told him west. We're going east."

Something moved through her face, quick as an eddy line. Not surprise. Recalculation. "Marguerite Almeida," she said softly, "just lied to a Vantablack officer."

"Don't sound impressed. It gets easier every time, which is the problem."

"No." She touched the crate, once, lightly, like a benediction. "It means you're finally spending yourself on something."

She walked back toward the fire, and I sat alone with the ice and the river noise and the distant, patient percussion of the glacier coming apart upstream, and I told myself the lie had been clean, the route was good, the water would hold.

Then Ines appeared out of the dark at my elbow, silent as snowfall, and said the sentence that ended the day:

"The east fork floods first. If the calving accelerates tonight, by morning there won't be an east fork. There'll be a lake with opinions."

She looked at me, waiting, and somewhere upstream the ice boomed again—closer now, and getting closer—and I understood that tomorrow I wouldn't be choosing between routes.

I'd be choosing between speeds.
