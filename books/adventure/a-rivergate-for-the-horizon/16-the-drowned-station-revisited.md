## 16. The Drowned Station Revisited

There is a particular kind of silence that comes off a drowned building, and I had hoped never to hear it again.

The station sat where the Sereno bent north around a gravel bar, half-swallowed by the river's new appetite—three years of meltwater climbing its walls like a slow green tide. From the water you saw only the roofline and the top meter of the observation windows, dark rectangles in a concrete face, and the stub of the radio mast leaning at an angle that suggested surrender. When we'd passed it going upstream, six weeks ago now, or a lifetime, I had looked away on purpose. You don't stare at a grave when you're rowing past it. It's bad luck, and worse manners.

Now we were going downriver with forty pounds of blue ice wrapped in sleeping bags and foil, three boats riding low, and the current was doing most of our work for us, which should have felt like mercy. Instead it felt like being handed back to something.

"Station on the left," Marguerite said, unnecessarily. Everyone could see it.

"We're not stopping," Halloran said from the second boat, and it wasn't quite a question.

Latch's man Deering, wedged into the stern of my boat with his rifle across his knees like luggage he'd been forced to carry, cleared his throat. "Might be worth ten minutes," he said pleasantly. "Shelter if the weather turns. Mr. Latch likes contingencies."

Mr. Latch liked a great many things, all of them itemized. He rode in the lead boat with Marguerite, cheerful as a wedding guest, and I had spent two days watching the two of them pretend at camaraderie while the river narrowed around us like a closing hand. I had also spent two days watching Marguerite read water—really read it, the way he used to read highway radar, whole sentences at a glance—and hating how much I still found it beautiful, and hating more that I couldn't stop doing arithmetic about him. What he knew. What he'd been paid. What he'd been told.

The eddy behind the gravel bar was still there, deep enough to hold a boat. Marguerite took it without being asked, which meant he remembered it too, which meant he'd been paying attention on the way up even while pretending the station was scenery.

"We'll take twenty minutes," Halloran said finally, in the tone of a man granting permission to a thing already happening. "Ines, Amara, with me. Nobody wanders."

Nobody wandered. Everybody watched.

* * *

The water inside the station stood waist-deep and glass-still, holding the ceiling upside down in perfect miniature. We waded the entry corridor with our headlamps on in broad daylight, because the windows were silted over and the interior had gone the color of deep lakes. My boots found the floor by memory before my eyes did—the slight rise where the lab ended and the dorms began, the doorframe somebody had widened in '09 for equipment crates. I had lived here for eleven months once, young enough to think a posting this far north was an adventure rather than an exile. I had taken cores from the tributary glacier and mailed them south and believed, with the whole stupid confidence of thirty-one, that the data would speak for itself.

The data had spoken. The Club had simply been standing in the room with its hands over everyone's ears.

"Ines." Halloran's light stopped on the wall beside what had been the comms room. "Come look at this."

I expected frost damage. Mold lines. The usual graffiti of abandonment—survey crews leave initials the way dogs leave other things. What the beam found instead was a bracket. A steel equipment bracket, bolted fresh, holding a solar panel canted toward the one gap in the roofline where light still came through. Below it, a cable run in clean conduit, stapled at regulation intervals, running down into the flooded dark.

The conduit was not rusted. The staples were not rusted. The panel was maybe four years old.

"Somebody's kept power here," Ines said. She said it quietly, the way she said everything since the vote, but her hand came up and touched the panel's edge the way you touch a wound to confirm it's yours.

"Salvage crews do this sometimes," Halloran said. He didn't believe it either. Salvage crews don't run conduit underwater. Salvage crews don't staple at regulation intervals; regulation belongs to institutions, and institutions don't send people to drowned stations in valleys no map bothers to name.

We followed the cable. It ran along the corridor ceiling, dipped under the floodline, and resurfaced at the old core archive room—the room where samples used to wait for the helicopter that came twice a season. The door had been replaced. Not repaired: replaced, with a marine-grade unit, gasketed, locked, and the lock was modern, and there was a keypad on it, and the keypad had a small green light burning steadily beneath the water line like a patient eye.

Somebody had waterproofed a door to a flooded room. Somebody had decided, years ago, that whatever was in that room needed protecting from weather, animals, and scientists, in roughly ascending order of concern.

"The Club funded this station until '19," I heard myself say. My voice sounded fine, which surprised me. "Then they pulled out. Budget review. They told us the ice record here was redundant with the coastal cores."

"Maybe it was," Halloran said.

"Maybe." I put my palm flat against the cold door. Under my glove, through the steel, I could feel the faintest hum—or imagined I could, which amounted to the same confession. "Or maybe redundancy wasn't the problem. Maybe proximity was. This station sits below the same accumulation basin as Halcyon Reach. Same ice field. If there's a continuous archive in the blue ice—if there's sixty thousand years of atmosphere stacked like pages—this is the front porch of it. They didn't defund this place. They *acquired* it."

Ines turned her light on me. "You can't know that."

"No," I agreed. "But I know who has a key."

* * *

Deering was waiting at the boats when we came out, and he smiled at us with the specific warmth of a man who has been told to smile. "Find anything useful?"

"A door," I said.

"Doors are useful." He handed me a thermocouple of coffee like we were colleagues. Behind him, Latch stood at the waterline consulting a tablet that had no business having signal this far north, and Marguerite stood apart from both of them, looking at the station's leaning mast with an expression I recognized because I'd spent seven years studying it: the expression of a man doing math he doesn't want the answer to.

I went to stand next to him. Not because I wanted the answer near him. Because I wanted him to feel me wanting it.

"You've been quiet," I said.

"I'm always quiet. That's most of my personality."

"Marguerite." I let his name sit there. Downriver, the current made its long white argument against the gravel bar. "That door downstairs. That installation. It's five years old at most. Which means when Vantablack pulled their funding and told the scientific community the site was dead, they were lying, and they were lying with capital expenditure. They built infrastructure. You don't build infrastructure around a secret you hope stays buried. You build it around a secret you intend to *use*."

He didn't answer. He watched the water.

"They knew," I said. "Before Halloran. Before Ines. Before any of us ever drilled into that basin, they knew what the ice held, and they bought the land under it, and they closed the front door and kept the key. And then—" and here my voice finally cracked, which I hated, but some things deserve a crack— "and then the expedition goes silent, and suddenly there's a salvage contract, and the salvage pilot just happens to be a debt-slave who can be squeezed from both ends, and the salvage pilot just happens to have an ex aboard who knows the science, and isn't that convenient, Marguerite. Isn't that *tidy*."

"Amara—"

"Were you hired to rescue anyone?"

The question landed between us and neither of us picked it up for a moment. A boat knocked against the gravel. Somewhere upriver, faint as a headache, the glacier grumbled.

"Think about it," I said, softer now, because anger was the wrong tool and I knew it, and knowing it didn't help. "Latch shows up on the river with contractors and a budget. Latch knows your contract terms better than you do. Latch radios home that everything's on schedule. On schedule, Marguerite. Schedules imply planned outcomes. Rescue missions don't have schedules. Retrievals do. Recoveries do. Asset recoveries." I made myself look at him. His jaw was working. "They didn't send you to bring the crew home. They sent you to locate the asset. The crew was always the packaging. The ice was always the product. And you—you were the tracking collar."

"That's not—" He stopped. Started again, and his voice had gone flat and careful, the way it goes when he's driving on ice. "My contract says locate and extract personnel. That's what I signed. That's what I'm doing."

"I believe you signed that." I did, God help me. That was the worst part; I believed he believed his own paper. "But contracts aren't written by the people who sign them. Someone chose you. Someone looked at a man drowning in Vantablack debt, with a sister in Vantablack collateral, and an ex-wife on the expedition roster, and thought: *there*. There's a man with no good options and a personal stake. He'll go anywhere. He'll ask nothing. And if it goes wrong, he's deniable, and if it goes right, he owes us everything." I shook my head. "You weren't hired despite your debts, Marguerite. Your debts were the qualification."

For a long moment he said nothing at all, and I watched seven years of weather move across his face—every exit he'd ever calculated, every horizon he'd driven toward instead of through. Then, very quietly:

"When we passed this place going up," he said, "you wouldn't look at it."

"I had reasons."

"I know. I'm saying—I looked. I looked at everything, it's the job, I can't not. And there was a supply cache under the mast tarp. Sealed drums, Vantablack gray, manifest numbers. I logged it and I told myself it was standard resupply for contractors working the valley." His laugh had no humor in it, only temperature. "Standard resupply. At a station that's been dead for five years. I did the math then, Amara. I just didn't want the total."

"And now?"

"Now the total's sitting on a keypad at the bottom of a flooded room, and the man who holds the code is drinking coffee ten feet away and calling me *partner*." He turned and looked at me straight on, and I will give him this: he didn't flinch, and he didn't perform. "I didn't know. I need you to hear that, even if you can't believe it. I didn't know what they knew."

"I believe you didn't know." I reached out and took his wrist—not his hand, his wrist, where the pulse is, where the honesty lives. "What I need to know is what you'll do now that you do."

Upstream of us, the glacier answered before he could. The sound rolled down the valley like a door slamming in a house the size of a country—a deep, structural concussion, ice letting go of ice—and every bird on the gravel bar went up at once, and Latch lowered his tablet and smiled at the noise the way a landlord smiles at rent.

"There's the schedule," he called out cheerfully. "Right on time. Better get moving, folks—she won't stay open long."

Marguerite's pulse jumped under my fingers, once, hard, and then steadied into something deliberate. He looked at the station—at the drowned door, the humming lock, the gray drums under the mast—and I watched him re-read every mile of this river backward, all of it changing color, the way a negative develops.

"Twenty minutes ago you asked me if I was hired to rescue anyone," he said.

"I remember."

"Halloran needs to hear the answer," he said, and started up the bank toward the boats, fast, at the only speed he'd ever trusted. "Because I think I finally know why they really sent me. And it wasn't to find you."
