## 17. Mutiny at the Confluence

**MARGUERITE**

You can hear a confluence before you see it, the way you can hear an argument through a motel wall—two voices that used to be one river refusing to agree anymore. The Sereno's north fork came down off the glacier gray and certain of itself. The south fork came down brown and swollen with moraine melt, and where they met, the water stood up in ridges and argued in a language of standing waves I'd only ever read about in books I'd bought to impress people.

I cut the engine and let us drift, because there are moments when arriving loudly is the worst thing you can do.

Behind me, forty pounds of blue ice rode in padded cases lashed like a coffin to the deck. Ahead, on the gravel bar at the tongue of the confluence, sat two boats that hadn't been there yesterday morning. Latch's men. Latch himself, standing at the waterline with his hands in his pockets like a man admiring real estate.

"Everyone stay low," I said, which was stupid, because everyone was already low, because everyone had been low since the drowned station, since Amara had looked at me across six years of silence and asked the question I still didn't have an answer for: *Did you know what they hired you for?*

"No," I'd told her. And it was true. And it wasn't enough, because not knowing is its own kind of employment.

Latch raised one hand—not waving, exactly. More like hailing a cab he already owned.

I thought about all the lies I'd told on this river. To my handlers on Channel Nine. To Latch over campfire coffee, about routes and fuel and how many people were alive upstream. To myself, mostly, about what kind of man I was going to be when this ended. Every lie had been load-bearing. Take one out and the whole structure came down on your head.

The confluence didn't care about any of it. The water just wanted to know which way we were going.

* * *

**AMARA**

There is a specific kind of quiet that falls over scientists when they're about to do something unscientific. I've heard it in hospital break rooms before someone tells the family. I heard it now, in the bottom of Marguerite's boat, as we drifted toward Latch and his ledger.

Ines had her hand flat on the sample case. Not holding it. Resting on it, the way you rest a hand on a sleeping animal you may need to wake up screaming. Halloran stood in the second boat with his binoculars down and his jaw set in the configuration I'd learned over three weeks meant *I have made a decision and I am waiting for reality to cooperate.*

"He'll offer us something," I said quietly.

"He always offers something," Marguerite said. "That's the product. The offer."

"And if some of us take it?"

Marguerite didn't answer, which was itself an answer, and a bad one. Because that was the fracture, wasn't it? It hadn't come from outside. Latch hadn't broken us; he'd just applied pressure to a fault line we'd been pretending wasn't there since the vote. Priya wanted to deal. She had a daughter and a mortgage and a firm belief—which I could not actually call irrational—that data does no good from inside a glacier calving field or a shallow grave. Okafor sided with her. The rest held with Halloran, who held with the ice, who held with principle, which is easy to hold with until it weighs forty pounds and the water is rising.

And Marguerite—Marguerite floated between them like a man at a wedding where both families hate each other and he's related to both. I'd watched him do it for days. Keep the peace. Keep the options open. Keep himself *uncommitted*, which is the most committed thing a coward can be, and I say that with love, or whatever this is that has survived seven years and one apocalypse of a river.

The current took us sideways. Latch smiled wider.

* * *

**INES**

I want to record, for whoever eventually reads this—if anyone reads this—that I voted for silence. That was mine. When we found the archive, sixty thousand years of atmosphere stacked in blue ice like pages in a book nobody was supposed to open, I voted to go dark. Verify first, I said. Publish once, completely, or not at all. Partial truth is just noise with better funding.

I believed it. I believe it less every day, and today, watching Mr. Latch stand on a gravel bar with the patience of a man who owns the concept of patience, I believe it least.

Because here is what silence buys: it buys time for men like Latch to arrive. Silence is not neutral. Silence is a resource, and resources get extracted. We kept our secret, and our secret kept us, and meanwhile the Club sent its accountants up the river like antibodies toward an infection, and now here we were, eleven scientists and a pilot and forty pounds of the most important ice on Earth, drifting toward a negotiation we had sworn never to have.

Priya caught my eye across the boats. She didn't look like a traitor. That was the worst part. She looked like a mother doing arithmetic, and I have done that same arithmetic in the dark hours—what is a truth worth, measured against a person—and the honest answer is that the equation has never once balanced in favor of the truth. Truths don't miss you at Christmas.

"Hold," Halloran said, low. "Nobody speaks first."

But Latch had already spoken first, weeks ago, in a sense. He'd spoken when he put contractors on this river years before we ever launched. He'd spoken in the drowned station, in the survey marks from 2019 that Amara found, in the whole long sentence of a syndicate that had known what the Sereno's ice held and had bet, very publicly, very profitably, that it would never be read.

We were not the expedition. We were the error in the ledger.

* * *

**LATCH**

Item: one confluence, scenic, currently undervalued due to hostilities.

Item: eleven academics, one pilot, assorted field equipment, morale poor-to-fair.

Item: forty pounds of glacial core, provenance disputed, market value catastrophic—to certain parties—or incalculable, depending on which side of the transaction you're standing on. I have always found it restful to stand on both.

I let them drift in. You learn early in this business that the fastest way to close a distance is to stop moving toward people. They came to me the way water comes to a low place, which is to say inevitably and while insisting it had other plans.

"Dr. Halloran," I called, warm as a heating bill. "You look terrible. River life agrees with no one over fifty."

"Mr. Latch." His voice carried flat across the water. "You're a long way from a golf course."

"I'm exactly where my work is." I gestured at the boats, the river, the lowering sky—all of it, generously, as though I'd arranged the weather too. In a sense I had. Somebody had funded the permits, the satellite time, the quiet little insurance policies against discovery. "Here's my thinking. The season's closing. Calving's started early—you've heard it, I've heard it, the mountain is clearing its throat. Downstream passage closes in—I'd estimate—seventy-two hours. Maybe less. My boats go through. I know the line, I've run this river when it was younger and so was I. Your people ride with me, safe, dry, employed. The cores ride with me too. Everyone gets home. Everybody wins."

"And the data?" That was the Voss woman. Sharp. She'd been sharp since Tulsa, wherever she keeps her knives.

"Published," I said, spreading my hands, "in full, under proper institutional auspices, after a reasonable review period." A review period of, say, the heat death of the universe. "Science deserves process. Rushed science is just gossip with graphs."

I watched the crew look at each other, and I will confess—it's important to be honest in one's private records—that for a moment I thought I had them. The woman with the daughter was already nodding. The big geophysicist was looking at his boots. Fear is such a reliable currency. It never devalues.

Then I looked at the pilot. Almeida. And I saw something I had not priced in.

* * *

**MARGUERITE**

Here's the thing about being caught between two sides: eventually you notice there's no middle. There's just the moment before you pick, stretched out as long as you can afford to stretch it, and I had been stretching mine since Oklahoma. Since the velvet room. Since a man with kind eyes explained that my debt was really an investment, and investments mature.

Priya spoke first. "We accept. Conditional on safe passage for all personnel."

"Priya—" Halloran started.

"We vote again on shore," she said, and her voice cracked right down the middle, and I hated Latch for making her do that, and hated him more because he wouldn't even notice he'd done it. To him she was a line item softening. "Eleven people, Doctor. Eleven. Against forty pounds of frozen air."

"The frozen air is the point," Ines said quietly. "It's the only testimony any of this ever gave."

"It's *ice*, Ines."

"It's sixty thousand years of proof that the collapse the Club shorted is real and dated. It's the reason half their portfolio exists. You give it to him and we die somewhere convenient and the world goes on believing the nice charts."

"Or we live," Priya said, "and let someone else save the world. Someone with a boat that isn't leaking."

Both of them turned to me.

That's the trap nobody warns you about. When a group fractures, it doesn't split in two—it splits around whoever hasn't chosen, because the undecided are the only votes left worth buying. Eleven scientists and every eye came to the guy whose highest qualification was reading water and lying fluently.

I looked at Latch. He was smiling his inventory smile, and I remembered his ledger, the way he'd talked about assets, cheerful and itemized, and I remembered the drowned station and the old survey marks and Amara's question—*did you know*—and I understood, finally, in my gut where understanding doesn't help, that there had never been a salvage contract. There had been a disposal. I was the garbage route.

And I thought about my sister, back home, collateral. I thought about what Latch would do to her the moment I became inconvenient. I thought about it honestly, the way you have to, and then I thought about Priya's daughter, and Ines's sixty thousand years, and Amara standing hip-deep in cold water refusing to look away from me.

Seven years ago I left her because staying meant slowing down and slowing down meant feeling things. I'd been driving away from decisions my whole life. Miles, not years. That was the whole religion.

The confluence roared. Somewhere up-glacier, the ice boomed—a calving event, distant, like artillery from a war we were losing.

"No deal," I said.

It came out quieter than I meant. So I said it again, louder, standing up in the boat with the current shoving at us and Latch's men reaching casually for their rifles.

"Nobody's trading. Not the cores, not the crew, not one pound of ice. You want the archive, Mr. Latch, you come take it from people who are done negotiating with you."

* * *

**AMARA**

He said it the way he drives—too fast, all commitment, no exit plan—and I felt something in my chest that seven years should have killed: pride. Stupid, badly timed pride.

Latch's smile did something structural. It stayed in place but stopped being a smile. "Almeida," he said, almost gently. "Your contract. Your sister. Do the math you're famous for."

"I did," Marguerite said. "First time in my life. The numbers are terrible. I'm going with them anyway."

And the crew—the fractured, frightened, half-mutinous crew—something happened that I'll be writing about for the rest of my career if I get a rest of my career. Priya sat down. Okafor picked up a paddle. Halloran said, in the voice he used for grant committees, "Well. That's decided, then," and it was, more thoroughly than any vote we'd ever taken, because democracy under pressure isn't counting hands. It's watching who moves when the shooting starts.

Latch sighed like a man closing a tab. "Shame," he said. "Genuinely. I had us all going home dry." Then, to his men, pleasantly: "Take the boats. Leave the pilot—he and I have a private matter to settle regarding outstanding balances."

Rifles came up. The river stood up in ridges. And Marguerite did the last thing I expected, which was the first thing I should have: he gunned the engine and turned us straight into the worst water on the confluence, the standing-wave seam where the two forks fought, because it was the one place on Earth those rifles couldn't follow.

"Hold on," he shouted, to all of us, to me, maybe to himself. "This part I'm actually good at."

* * *

**HALLORAN**

Later—there would be a later, I insisted on it—we would argue about whether choosing the crew over the deal was courage or just another gamble by a gambling man. I've published enough to know history sorts that out retroactively, usually wrong.

What I know is this: the shot came from the gravel bar and took out our stern transom, and Ines screamed a heading, and Marguerite threaded three boats through standing waves that no survey chart admits exist, and behind us Latch's heavier hulls hesitated at the seam the way heavy things hesitate, and that hesitation was the whole margin between the archive and oblivion.

We came through. All three boats. Bailing, listing, laughing in the ugly hysterical way people laugh when they've stepped off a ledge and found it was a stair.

Ahead, the river narrowed into the calving corridor, walls of ice a hundred feet high on either side, groaning like a ship breaking up. Seventy hours of open water, maybe less. Behind us, Latch regrouping. Beside me, a pilot who had finally, publicly, expensively, chosen a side.

"Almeida," I said.

"Don't congratulate me yet," he said. "I owe that man everything."

"Yes," I agreed, as the first wall of the corridor rose up white and detonating ahead of us. "And I suspect he's about to come collect."
