## 18. What Marguerite Owes

They took me at the second bend past the confluence, which I should have expected, because Latch had always struck me as a man who read rivers the way accountants read ledgers—looking for where the numbers narrowed.

I'd hung back to run sweep, ostensibly because my boat had the shallowest draft and I knew how to read a braided channel, actually because after the mutiny I couldn't stand the sound of eleven people breathing in formation behind me. That was my mistake right there: sentiment dressed up as navigation. The Vantablack skiff came out of the willow shadow on the left bank with its engine idled down to a purr, and there was nothing to do but cut my motor and let them come alongside, because the alternative was a rock garden I'd already mapped and they hadn't, and I wasn't going to drown myself just to inconvenience Mr. Latch.

"Mr. Almeida." He was smiling. He was always smiling; it was less an expression than a piece of equipment he maintained. "You've been a difficult line item."

"You should see my taxes."

Two of his men took my arms—not roughly, that was the thing about Vantablack people, everything was done with the courtesy of men handling inventory—and walked me up the gravel bar while a third tied my boat off like it belonged to him now, which, in every way that mattered, it did.

* * *

The camp they'd made was obscene in its comfort. A proper tent, a propane stove, a folding table with a cloth on it. Latch poured coffee from a thermos into an enamel cup and handed it to me like we were old friends meeting at a funeral, which, in a sense, we were.

"Sit," he said.

I sat. My hands were shaking around the cup and I let him see it, because there was no percentage in pretending otherwise with this man. He appreciated accurate numbers.

"Do you know what I admire about you, Marguerite?" He sat across the table, unhurried, steam rising between us. "You're honest about your dishonesty. Most debtors lie even to themselves. You've never once pretended you were anything but for sale. It's made you very easy to work with, historically."

"Historically," I said.

"The Club is prepared to be generous." He produced a leather folio—I swear to God, a leather folio, on a gravel bar two hundred miles from the nearest notary—and opened it. "Full forgiveness. Every dollar. The principal, the interest, the penalties, the weather delay fees." He smiled at his own joke. "And your sister's obligations, which I'll remind you are held under the same instrument. Cleared. Both of you walk away owing nothing to anyone."

The number he said next was one I'd been carrying so long it had grown into my skeleton. Hearing it spoken aloud, canceled, felt like hearing a doctor say a tumor was gone. My eyes actually stung. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is supposedly the theme of this chapter: for three full seconds I would have given him anything.

"And all I have to do," I said, when I could speak, "is tell you where forty pounds of ice is floating down a river."

"All you have to do is tell me where the cores will be when my people need them to be somewhere else." He folded his hands. "Marguerite. You've seen what's coming downstream. The calving has started early. In seventy-two hours, maybe less, that corridor closes and stays closed until autumn, and by autumn the Club will have made other arrangements. I don't need to hurt anyone. I genuinely don't enjoy it. I need a location, and you need a life. This is the cleanest trade either of us will ever be offered."

He was right. That was the horror of Latch—he was always right, arithmetically. The cores were on Halloran's boat, running the main channel with Amara and four others. If I gave him the route, his men could be waiting at the last navigable eddy before the pass, and there'd be nothing heroic about what happened there. Heroes require witnesses and open ground. What there'd be instead was a quiet eddy and six tired scientists and men who'd done this kind of arithmetic before.

And if I refused? He'd take my sister's paper out of the drawer marked *incentives*, and he'd do it without raising his voice, and he'd still smile at me across whatever came next.

I looked at the coffee. Good coffee. He'd brought good coffee upriver on a salvage job, because Latch believed you should live well even during atrocities, especially during atrocities—it kept the staff loyal.

"Here's my problem, Mr. Latch," I said. "I don't know where they're going to be."

His smile didn't move but something behind it did, a small recalibration, like a man checking a receipt. "You're piloting the flotilla."

"I'm piloting the *front*. Sweep runs blind. That's the whole discipline of it—you don't follow your own wake, you don't hold the route in your head, because if you get taken, you can't give up what you don't have." I shrugged. "Halloran split the route knowledge three ways. I know my third. I don't know theirs."

It was a lie, mostly. I knew the route. I'd chosen it, alone, at three in the morning, tracing channels on a survey map by headlamp while everyone slept. But Latch couldn't verify it quickly, and speed was the only currency I had left.

Latch studied me for a long moment. Then he did something worse than threatening me. He sighed, sympathetically, and turned the folio around so I could read it.

"Then let's start with what you do know," he said. "Your third. Your section of the route. Give me that, and I'll release your sister's paper today—the sister only, the rest of your debt stands until the cores surface. Half now, half later. I'm told that's how reasonable men do business."

And there it was. Not the whole price—a partial payment, structured, installment-plan betrayal. He'd learned enough about me over the weeks to know I'd refuse the full sale on principle and then agonize over the partial one, because partial betrayals feel like prudence. They feel like protecting most of the thing while sacrificing some of it. Every collaborator in history has started with a fraction.

My sister's name was on that page. Her actual name, typed, with a balance figure beside it that made my teeth ache. She'd co-signed a loan for me once, seven years ago, back when I was fast and stupid and certain the horizon owed me a living. Everything since—the storms, the salvage contracts, the silence on Channel Nine—had been interest accruing on that single signature.

I could give Latch a third of a river and get her out. It wouldn't stop the Club. It wouldn't save the cores. But it would save *her*, and I'd spent seven years telling myself she was the one clean thing left in my ledger.

Except.

Except that Amara had stood on the moraine three nights ago and said, *some truths are worth bankruptcy,* and I'd hated her for it, and I'd believed her. Except that Halloran was running the rear boat with a man's calm certainty that he might burn for those samples and had made peace with it in a way I hadn't made peace with anything. Except that if I sold even a third of the route, I'd spend the rest of my life knowing exactly which stretch of water the blood was spilled in.

But here's what nobody tells you about refusing cleanly: it's a luxury. Refusal costs, and the cost lands on whoever you love, and you don't get to be noble in private. Whatever I did at this table was going to be priced honestly, and the bill would go to other people.

So I did neither the noble thing nor the cheap thing. I did the arithmetic thing.

"There's a cache," I said.

Latch tilted his head.

"When I came upstream, I cached supplies. Fuel, food, spare parts—standard practice for a solo run through country with no resupply. Three caches. One of them—" I pulled the map from my jacket, the real one, the one with my own route marked, because showing him the true map was the only way to sell what I was about to bury inside it "—one of them is at the mouth of the Kettle Creek cutoff. Gravel bar, left bank, cairn with a red flag. And Halloran knows about it. It's a designated fallback point. If the flotilla gets separated—which it now is—they regroup at caches. That's the protocol."

All of that was true. The cache existed. The fallback protocol existed. I'd watched Halloran brief it himself, tapping each cache site on the map with one finger while the crew memorized them.

What was also true, and what I did not say, was that the Kettle Creek cache contained fuel cans and freeze-dried stew and nothing else, and that the regroup point was forty minutes' detour off any route the flotilla would plausibly run, and that by the time Latch's men reached it, hauled it open, and found nothing but stove fuel and a note I intended to write saying *WRONG RIVER, TRY THE NEXT ONE NORTH*, the pass would be closing and the arithmetic would be over.

I was giving him something real. Real coordinates, a real cache, a real piece of my own survival plan—that was the part that stung, that I was burning my own fallback to buy hours. If the flotilla scattered badly enough to need that cache, we'd find the cairn torn apart and Latch's men camped on our supplies. I was amputating a limb to buy time for the rest of the body, and there was nothing heroic in it. Heroism would have been refusing. Sacrifice was what this was: uglier, smaller, priced honestly.

"That's the sister's paper?" I said. "That buys her out?"

Latch looked at the map for a long time. He had good instincts—I'll grant him that much—and I watched them working, sniffing the offer for trap smell. But everything I'd shown him checked out against what he already knew, and the beauty of a partly-true lie is that verification only reaches the true parts.

"It buys the sister," he said finally. "Not the debt. And Marguerite—" he tapped the map, right on the Kettle Creek junction, and I made myself not flinch "—if my men find that cache empty of anything interesting, the sister's paper goes back in the drawer and the interest keeps running. You understand that this is not forgiveness. This is a payment on account."

"I understand exactly what it is," I said. "I've understood what things were my whole life. It's the one skill the Club taught me that I'm keeping."

He laughed—actually laughed, delighted, and I understood that he liked me, in the way a man likes a well-made tool, and that liking me had never once slowed him down.

We wrote it up. He had paper, of course he had paper, and a pen, and he drafted a release for my sister's obligation in his neat, cheerful hand while his men watched me with the polite boredom of warehouse staff. I signed. The pen scratched and it sounded like ice cracking somewhere far upstream, small and preparatory.

"One more thing," Latch said as I stood. "A note for your crew, should you rejoin them. Tell Dr. Voss the Club's position hasn't changed since 2019. She'll know what that means." His smile widened by a millimeter, and I felt the sentence go into me like a fishhook. *Since 2019.* The drowned station. The years-old survey marks. The Club had known about the ice before Halcyon Reach ever launched, and here he was, handing me the confirmation wrapped in a message meant to rattle the woman I—

The woman I loved. Fine. There it is. I'd been outrunning that word for seven years too, and there wasn't enough river left to outrun it anymore.

"I'll pass it along," I said.

"They're making good time, I hear." He sipped his coffee. "The calving won't wait for any of us, Mr. Almeida. Do give my regards to the doctor."

* * *

They put me back in my boat with my rifle, my radio, and my map, because taking any of those would have converted a business transaction into a kidnapping, and Latch preferred to keep everything deniable, everything bookable. The skiff idled away into the willows and I sat in the current for a full minute, hands on the tiller, doing the thing I do when the adrenaline drains: counting.

Counting what it cost. A cache I might need. A lie that would unravel within a day, probably within hours, and when it unraveled, Latch would know I'd played him, and the sister's paper would go back in the drawer, and everything I'd bought would be foreclosed. I hadn't saved her. I'd rented her safety for approximately thirty-six hours, at the price of making the most dangerous man on the river feel like a fool.

That was the honest accounting. No heroism anywhere in the column. Just a man trading pieces of himself at a loss because the alternative was trading someone else.

I keyed the radio low-power, short range, the frequency Amara and I had agreed on back when agreeing on things was still possible. Static, then her voice, careful, coded, the way we'd practiced: *"Say position."*

"Kettle Creek confluence," I said, which was true, and then, because she deserved the truth even when it was expensive: "I sold him the cache. The real one. We can't use it again. And he gave me a message for you—something about 2019. Amara. He wanted you to hear it."

Silence on the band, long enough that I checked the transmit light. When she came back, her voice had changed, gone flat and cold in a way I'd heard only once before, seven years ago, through a door.

*"Tell you on the water,"* she said. *"Get downstream, Marguerite. The corridor's opening early—and Halloran's boat just lost its engine."*

I looked upriver. Somewhere past the bend, past the willows and the gravel bars and the man with the leather folio, the glacier was clearing its throat, and the river I'd promised to deliver eleven people through was beginning, piece by piece, to come apart.
