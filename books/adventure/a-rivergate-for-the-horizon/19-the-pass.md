## 19. The Pass

**Marguerite**

There is a particular sound a glacier makes when it decides to kill you. It isn't a roar. Roars are honest. It's a crack — a single dry snap, like God breaking a pencil — and then a pause long enough for you to do the math on where you are versus where the ice is going to be. I'd heard it twice in the last ten minutes. Both times the math had come out ugly.

"Left channel," Ines said beside me. "Trust me."

"I trust you," I said. "I don't trust the river."

"The river doesn't care what you trust. Left."

The flotilla had been three boats at dawn. We were still nominally three boats, but *nominally* was doing heroic work in that sentence. Halloran had the rear with two of his people and the heaviest sled of cores; Latch's surviving escort rode with Reyes in the middle boat, which meant my little armada was arranged like a sandwich with something hostile in the middle. The meltwater had come up four feet overnight. The Sereno wasn't running anymore. It was fleeing.

Ahead of us the valley narrowed into what the old survey charts called the Corridor — six kilometers of channel between walls of glacier ice that had spent sixty thousand years minding their own business and had recently decided to stop. Calving season had opened early, the way a door opens when someone kicks it. Every hour the route got narrower. By tomorrow there would be no route. We were running through a closing fist.

Another crack, off to the right this time, deeper in the wall. Then the groan — a long, tectonic moan, the sound of a mountain clearing its throat.

"Ines," I said. "How sure are you about this channel?"

She didn't look up from the water. Her hands rested on the gunwale the way a pianist's rest on keys before the piece starts. "I surveyed it as a student. Nine years ago."

"Nine years ago."

"It was a different river then." A pause. "It will be a different river in an hour. That's the deal we made."

*We* hadn't made any deal. But I'd learned over the last three weeks that arguing with Ines Okonkwo was like arguing with a current: technically possible, practically pointless, and you always ended up downstream of where you started.

Behind us, Halloran's engine note changed — dropped half a tone, laboring. I twisted in my seat. His boat was riding low, too low, the bow wave washing green over the foredeck. The rear wall of the corridor stood behind him like a cathedral that hated us.

"Halloran's heavy," I called back.

"He's carrying the archive," Ines said. "Of course he's heavy."

I thought about telling him to jettison weight. I thought about what forty pounds of blue ice weighed against eleven lives, and then I thought about Amara's face on the moraine two nights ago, and I kept my mouth shut and my hand on the throttle. Some cargo you don't ask a man to throw away. Some cargo *is* the man.

The left channel opened ahead of us like a wound — narrow, fast, water the color of bottle glass, braided around buried ice that showed through as pale ghosts just under the surface. Reading water is mostly reading fear: yours, and the river's. This water was afraid. That meant rocks. That meant shallow. That meant speed, counterintuitively, because slow boats get eaten by shallow water and fast ones skip across it like a lie across a dinner table.

"Throttle up," Ines said.

"You read my mind."

"Somebody has to. You've been driving like a man composing his memoirs."

I pushed the throttle forward and the world went to noise.

* * *

**Ines**

People think glaciology is patience. It is not. Glaciology is grief management conducted at geological scale, with occasional explosions. I have spent my career learning how ice dies, and I will tell you the secret nobody wants: it dies the way people do, badly, out of order, and usually while you're standing too close.

The left channel was mine. I want to be precise about that, because in the hours that followed everyone was generous enough to call it luck, and luck implies I had nothing invested. I had walked that channel at twenty-three, with a theodolite and a supervisor who smoked and a notebook I still dream about sometimes. I had measured its bends in meters and its moods in degrees. The river has changed — rivers are professional changers — but ice remembers. The bedrock underneath doesn't move. And the channel, whatever else it had become, still ran where the bedrock told it to run.

So I navigated by memory, which is a polite word for faith.

Marguerite drove the way I imagine he does everything: too fast, precisely, with an expression of mild apology. There is a type of man who treats velocity as a moral position. I used to find them exhausting. Somewhere between the drowned station and the confluence I had revised my opinion, because I had watched this one choose, repeatedly, publicly, at cost, and I had begun to understand that his speed was never escape. It was attention. He paid attention faster than the rest of us could afford to.

"Ghost rock," I said, pointing left. "Two meters down. Give it room."

He gave it room without asking how I knew. That, more than anything, is why we were going to survive the next hour: not skill, though there was skill, but the specific grace of a man who accepts information without auditing it first.

The first calving came while we were mid-channel.

I heard it the way you hear your own name in a crowd — the snap, that obscene clean snap, and then the wall on our right moved. Not slid. *Moved*, the whole face of it, a hundred meters of ice leaning out over the water like a drunk deciding whether to sit down. For one full second nothing happened. That second is the worst thing I know. Then gravity remembered its job.

The collapse sounded like the end of the argument between the earth and the sky. Ice the size of buildings went into the river and the river stood up. I mean that literally — a wall of displaced water, gray and roaring, taller than our mast, coming down the channel at us with the calm inevitability of arithmetic.

"Hard left!" I screamed. "Into the surge! Ride it!"

Every instinct says flee a wave. You cannot flee a wave in a six-meter boat. You turn *into* it, nose up, throttle open, and you pray to whatever god handles small craft, which I suspect is a junior employee with limited resources.

Marguerite turned into it. Of course he did. The boat climbed — I felt the hull go light, felt us leave the water entirely for a moment that lasted approximately one geological era — and then the crest took us and threw us down the far side like a stone from a sling. Water came aboard in tons. My hands found the sample case and held it against my chest like a child. Behind us the wave kept going, chasing itself down the channel toward Halloran.

I looked back. I shouldn't have. Everyone looks back.

Halloran's boat was climbing.

* * *

**Marguerite**

You can watch a man ride out a thirty-foot surge and live, or you can watch him fail to, and both things take about four seconds, and those are the longest four seconds the human heart is rated for. Halloran hit the wave wrong — bow quartering, too much load, the stern digging — and for a moment his boat stood on its tail like a rearing horse, and I saw two of his crew go into the water, orange suits blinking, absurdly bright against all that gray.

Then the boat came down, somehow, catastrophically, alive, and kept moving, and I breathed again, and hated myself for the relief because the wave hadn't finished. The wave was still traveling. The wave was doing what waves do, which is visit every debt in order.

"Reyes!" I shouted across the water. The middle boat was wallowing, shipping water, Latch's man — Voss's man, whatever he was now — standing frozen at the tiller. "Take the wave head-on!"

Reyes heard me. I'll give him that forever. He swung the boat, took the surge on the bow, and survived it looking like a drowned cat. Latch's other man did not look like anything, because he was already gone overboard somewhere in the white water, and nobody said his name, and everybody thought it.

Then the second calving came, from the left wall this time, and the mathematics of the morning changed completely.

Because here is what I understood, in the way you understand things during disasters — instantly, completely, uselessly late: the two collapses were converging. Ice from both sides, water from both directions, meeting in the channel exactly where the middle boat was. The river was closing like a book, and Reyes was the bookmark.

And behind all of it, last, slowest, heaviest: Halloran. Holding the rear. Holding the archive. Holding the position he'd assigned himself at dawn with a sentence I should have argued with and didn't: *"Rear boat takes the risk. That's what rear means."*

"Can we go back?" Ines asked. She was already turning, already reading the water behind us, already calculating. Scientists. They calculate at funerals.

"No," I said. "Look at it."

She looked. The channel behind us was gone — not blocked, *gone*, replaced by a churning field of brash ice grinding itself into slush, a hundred thousand pieces of the wall renegotiating their relationship with gravity. No boat goes upstream through that. No boat goes anywhere through that. The pass had closed behind us like a door, and Halloran was on the wrong side of it, and the sound of the ice grinding was the sound of the argument ending.

Ines made a noise I had never heard from her. Small. Almost polite. The noise of a woman setting down something very heavy very carefully so it doesn't break.

"He knew," she said. "Last night. He told me the rear position was — he said, *someone has to be the period at the end of the sentence.*"

"Ines—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked and reassembled itself in under a second, and I understood that this was a woman who had practiced. "Don't spend words on it. Spend them on the channel. There's a bend coming and if you take it the way you want to take it, we die."

I took the bend the way she wanted it taken.

* * *

**Ines**

Grief is a passenger you cannot put ashore. So I let it sit in the stern of the boat and hold the sample case, and I navigated.

The channel after the convergence was worse than memory and better than expectation — the flood surge had scoured it clean, actually, stripped out the shallows, left us a deep fast lane of gray-green water running at speeds I had only ever measured, never ridden. The walls here were older, bluer, compressed to glass. Sunlight came through them and turned the whole corridor the color of a hospital, of a swimming pool, of every cold thing I had ever loved. Sixty thousand years of atmosphere pressed in on both sides. Somewhere in that ice was the year Tambora erupted. Somewhere was the year my grandmother was born. The ice kept all of it, patiently, and gave it up to no one, and today it was dying loudly enough to bury us with it.

"Two hundred meters," I said. "The channel forks. Right fork is shorter. Left fork is the one I surveyed."

"Which one's alive?"

That was the question that mattered. Rivers fork and die all the time; a dead fork is a trap with scenery. I closed my eyes for half a second — half a second Marguerite gave me without complaint, holding our speed steady, trusting me with his life and the lives of everyone behind us, which is a lot to entrust to a woman navigating by a nine-year-old notebook in her head.

"Left," I said. "The right fork loses the bedrock. It'll be braided gravel. The left hugs the wall. It'll be ugly but it'll be continuous."

"Ugly and continuous," Marguerite repeated. "Story of my life. Left it is."

We took the fork. The left channel was everything I promised: ugly, continuous, a slot of water barely wider than the boat, running between walls of ice so close I could have touched them, so close the air inside was ten degrees colder, so close that when the ice groaned — and it groaned constantly now, a chorus, a congregation — the sound came through the hull as well as the air, so that we were inside the instrument, inside the experiment, inside the sound.

And then, ahead, light. Actual light, wide and flat and silver. The corridor opening onto the lower reach, the last reach, the long slide to the delta.

"We're through," Marguerite said, and his voice did something I'd never heard it do, which was shake.

"We're through the pass," I corrected. "We're not through anything else."

Because the middle boat was missing.

* * *

**Marguerite**

Reyes surfaced a kilometer later, clinging to a cooler lid, half-frozen and swearing in two languages with impressive fluency. We hauled him aboard and he lay in the bilge water gasping and said, before anything else, before his own name: "The samples. Did the samples make it?"

"They made it," Ines said.

"Then it's fine," Reyes said, and started to laugh, or cry, or some third thing that men do when the body finishes being useful and the mind presents its bill. "It's fine. It's fine."

Latch's other man never surfaced. I want to record that honestly: we searched for eleven minutes, cutting our margin to nothing, and the river kept what it took, the way it keeps everything, the way it kept the survey station and the markers and, eventually, would keep all of us, and I throttled up out of there with a silence in the boat that had a name in it that nobody said.

But the rear boat — the rear boat was the wound.

We reached the lower reach and I killed the engine and we drifted, all of us, listening. The corridor behind us was still detonating, muffled now by distance, a war heard from the next valley over. Somewhere in that noise was Dr. Elias Halloran, who had voted for silence because he believed data should arrive finished, who had carried the heaviest sled, who had said *rear means risk* and meant it the way monks mean vows.

Amara's boat came alongside ours. She looked past me, upriver, at the closed pass, and her face did not collapse. That was the terrible thing. It organized itself instead — grief becoming structure, becoming intent — and she looked at me and said one word:

"Radio."

And I understood her. Not the word. The *sentence underneath the word*. Halloran was gone, the pass was closed, the samples were through, and every hour we sat in this boat was an hour the Club's remaining assets — Latch, whoever Latch answered to, the whole velvet room of them — had to regroup and bury what we carried. Halloran had died holding the rear of a story. The story wasn't finished. Stories don't finish until somebody tells them.

The expedition's transmitter was in my boat. Broken since the camp — or broken the way I'd left it, which is a distinction I'd been avoiding for two days.

"Ines," I said. "How long to the delta?"

"At this flow? Three hours. Maybe less."

Three hours. Enough time to fix a transmitter. Enough time to decide what to say on it. Enough time, if I was honest, which I was trying lately to be, to finally stop lying to everyone — the handlers, the creditors, the woman standing next to me, myself — all at once, on an open channel, where lies can't follow.

I looked at the transmitter case. I looked upriver at the wall of grinding white that held Elias Halloran and the rear of everything.

Then I picked up the case and set it on my knees, and started unscrewing the panel, and Amara sat down beside me without a word, and Ines took the tiller, and the river carried us down toward the last open water like a sentence that had finally decided how it was going to end.
