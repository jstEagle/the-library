## 20. Broadcast

There is a particular silence that follows a glacier calving. It isn't the absence of sound—it's the presence of everything that just stopped. The detonation ends and the river keeps carrying the echo in its own way, a shudder in the hull, a tremor in your teeth, and then nothing but water moving too fast toward a place you're not sure exists anymore.

I was sitting in the bow of the second boat with forty pounds of sixty-thousand-year-old atmosphere strapped between my knees like a sleeping child, and I was listening to that silence, and I was counting heads.

Nine. We had been eleven at the camp, twelve with Marguerite, thirteen if you counted Latch's men, which I refused to. Now nine, plus Marguerite up front in the lead boat, plus Ines beside me gripping the gunwale with both hands as though the river might try to take her personally. Halloran was not among the nine. Nobody said his name yet. There would be time for names later, or there wouldn't, and either way the river didn't care.

"Channel's clear," Ines said, for the fourth time. She had the handheld radio out of its waterproof bag and she kept checking it the way you check a wound—frequently, gently, hoping for different news each time.

The transmitter was the thing. That was the whole war now, boiled down to one object the size of a bread loaf. Marguerite had spent two nights before the launch splicing wire by headlamp, cannibalizing parts from equipment that had better uses, and none of us had asked him why. We'd all known why. You don't repair a transmitter on a rescue run unless you intend to say something worth hearing.

The question—the only question—was what, and to whom, and whether saying it would kill us faster than the ice intended to.

* * *

Let me tell you what I know about Marguerite Almeida, because I've had seven years to study the subject and three weeks of fieldwork to confirm my findings.

He loves at highway speed. That was always the diagnosis. He doesn't do anything slowly—not driving, not falling, not leaving. When we were together the first time, he used to measure our relationship in miles between visits, and when the miles got expensive he solved it the way he solves everything, by going faster, until going fast became the point and the destination was just an excuse for velocity. I left because I realized I could spend my whole life being someone's horizon, always gorgeous, never arrived at.

And here he was, seven years later, running a river he'd never seen at speeds that made the boats groan, and I caught myself doing the thing I'd sworn off: watching him calculate exits. His eyes flicking to the banks, to the sky, to the radio. Running numbers. Marguerite was always running numbers—odometer readings, debt figures, fuel margins—and every number was a door.

Except this morning. This morning something had changed in the arithmetic, and I wanted to know what, and I was afraid I already knew.

We came out of the calving corridor near noon, if noon meant anything under a sky the color of old dishwater. The river widened. The walls of ice fell back from the channel and became, instead of threats, merely scenery—blue and ancient and indifferent. Ines made a sound that was half sob, half laugh, and put her forehead against the core case.

"That channel," she said. "I surveyed it as a grad student. Twenty-two years ago. It was twice that wide."

"It held," I said.

"It held," she agreed, and then, quieter: "Elias didn't."

We let the river carry that for a while.

* * *

The lead boat signaled a bend ahead—a wide gravel bar, high ground, the first place in thirty miles where a person could stand without feeling like the landscape was holding its breath. Marguerite brought us in. He beached the boats with the economy of a man who has done ten thousand harder things before breakfast, and then he stood in the shallows and looked upstream the way we all did, at the empty water where a third boat should have been, and I watched him decide not to grieve yet. Filing it. Payable later. That was Marguerite's whole accounting system: everything deferred, everything owed.

Latch's men were gone. They'd peeled off at the confluence the day before, or been peeled—I still wasn't entirely sure how much of that mutiny had been Marguerite steering rather than surviving—but "gone" was a word I didn't trust. Men like Latch didn't leave. They repositioned. Somewhere downstream, probably at the delta, probably with a satellite phone and a smile, the ledger was waiting to be balanced.

Marguerite waded back to the boats and pulled the transmitter from its dry bag.

Nobody spoke. Nine people on a gravel bar pretending to check gear, all of them listening.

"I've been thinking about this," he said, "since before the pass."

"Don't," Ines said. Not unkindly. "Or—do. But know what you're doing."

"I know exactly what I'm doing." He turned the transmitter over in his hands. "That's the problem. I've known since the drowned station."

The drowned station. Amara, I told myself, you knew. You stood in that ruined research station and read the dates on Vantablack survey equipment—equipment five years old, older than the expedition, older than the discovery—and you watched Marguerite's face while he did the same math you did, and you understood that he hadn't been hired to find anyone. He'd been hired to find the ice. The rescue was the receipt. And you'd said nothing, because there was a river to run and grief to defer, and now here it all was, payable, on a gravel bar with the last open water ticking away behind us.

"The Club sent me upriver knowing what was here," Marguerite said. "Maybe not the details. Maybe exactly the details. Doesn't matter. My contract says salvage. My contract also says I report position daily, and I haven't reported in six days, which means as far as they know I'm either dead or defecting, and either way—" he shrugged, that terrible easy shrug, "—my debt doesn't care. Debt survives death. I checked."

"So call them," Reyes said from the third boat. "Tell them where we are. Buy yourself out. It's what they'd offer."

"They already offered." Marguerite's voice stayed level. "At the moraine. Full forgiveness. My sister's safety. All I had to give them was the cores' location." He looked at the cases. "I gave them coordinates that are forty percent true. Which buys maybe a day. Maybe less."

"And now?" Ines asked.

"Now I'm done buying." He crouched, rigged the antenna, and I saw his hands were steady in the way that hands get steady right at the edge of something irreversible. "Here's the thing about owing people everything. As long as you owe them, everything you have belongs to them. Including the truth. Especially the truth." He glanced up at me, and for one second the highway-speed man went completely still. "Amara told me something on the moraine. She said some truths are worth bankruptcy. I've been running that number ever since."

"You don't have to do this for us," Ines said.

"No," he agreed. "I don't. That's what makes it mine."

* * *

I want to be honest about what I felt, because honesty is supposedly the theme of the day.

Part of me wanted to stop him. That's the part I'm least proud of. The scientist part, the part that voted for silence back in the blue-ice tunnel, the part that believed—still believes, in its marrow—that data released unverified, unreviewed, raw and screaming into the world, does damage along with good. Sixty thousand years of atmospheric record proving a collapse timeline that makes the Club's entire portfolio a bet against physics. Put that on an open band and you don't just bankrupt some wealthy optimists. You start panics. You hand the next decade to whoever shouts loudest about it. I'd lain awake at the camp rehearsing the careful version: journals, verification, a controlled release, dignity.

But I'd also watched Elias Halloran hold the rear of a flotilla through a calving corridor so that the careful version could survive to reach a journal, and the river had taken the careful version's author, and somewhere downstream men with ledgers were waiting to make sure it never reached anywhere at all.

Careful had been tried. Careful had a body count.

"Do it," I said.

Everyone looked at me. I stood up out of the boat gravel and faced them, all eight of them plus the man with the transmitter, and I heard my own voice come out steadier than I felt.

"We voted for silence to protect the data. The data is in these boats. Silence protected it straight past the point where protection matters. If we go dark another day, the only people who ever hear about the archive are the people who already know, and they've known for five years, and they built a portfolio on us never saying a word." I looked at Marguerite. "Say it badly. Say it raw. Say it to every crackling trucker and ham operator and weather station on the band. Say it so loud they can't bury it in a review cycle."

Marguerite smiled. It wasn't his highway smile—that bright, accelerating thing. It was slower. Almost unfamiliar. Like watching a man remember how to walk.

"Keying up," he said.

* * *

What he transmitted took eleven minutes. I timed it, because timing things is what I do when I can't control anything else.

He started with position and party—nine survivors, Halcyon Reach expedition, all names spoken clearly, including Elias Halloran's, given the full weight of a eulogy compressed into a roster. Then he read the summary. Ines had distilled it onto four laminated pages weeks ago, the numbers we'd die for reduced to figures a listener could scribble on a napkin: core depths, dating intervals, the greenhouse gas curves, the meltwater pulse signatures, the collapse window. He read them twice. He spelled the isotopic ratios letter by letter like a man dictating a will.

Then he went off script.

"To any listener monitoring for Vantablack interests," he said, and his voice changed, flattened into something almost gentle, which from Marguerite was more frightening than shouting. "You know who you are. Your survey team marked that research station on the Sereno five years before the Halcyon Reach expedition existed. You knew what the ice held. You funded the disaster and shorted the warning. Everything I've just read is now in the public record, timestamped, on this frequency, witnessed by every operator on the band. There is no version of tomorrow where this stays yours." A pause. Static breathing. "Also, my debt is hereby publicly worthless, since you can't collect on a man whose only asset is information you no longer control. Consider this my final statement of accounts. It balances."

He set the transmitter down.

For a long moment nobody moved. The river ran. Somewhere upstream, faint as memory, the glacier grumbled to itself.

Then Ines started laughing—a cracked, exhausted, disbelieving sound—and Reyes swore with real admiration, and one of the techs, little Okafor, who hadn't spoken above a whisper since the pass, said, "You beautiful idiot," and meant it as a benediction.

I went to Marguerite. He was coiling the antenna cable with unnecessary precision, the way he did when his hands needed a job his heart had finished with.

"They'll come for you," I said. "Not the Club. Everyone. Journalists, governments, the Club's creditors, the Club itself. You just became the most interesting man on two continents."

"I've been interesting before." He looked up. "Never free, though. Do you know what's strange? I keep waiting for the odometer feeling. The itch to be somewhere else immediately. And it's not—" he frowned, genuinely puzzled, a man discovering a new instrument reading. "It's not there."

"That's called standing still on purpose," I said. "It grows on you."

"Does it?"

"Ask me again at the delta."

Because that was the other thing, the thing under the thing. The broadcast bought the truth its freedom. It did not buy us ours. Every mile of open water remaining was a mile the Club's people could cover faster than we could, and now they had no reason whatsoever to pretend to be support contractors. We had traded our invisibility for the world's attention, and the world's attention was hours away, and the Club's boats were considerably closer.

Marguerite followed my eyes downstream and did the same math. I watched him arrive at the sum.

"How long to the delta?" I asked.

"At this water?" He squinted at the current, reading it like road. "Six hours. Seven if the channels have narrowed since the survey charts." He paused. "They'll have narrowed."

"And them?"

"Latch travels light and fast." He started pushing the first boat back into the current. "But he travels for profit, and I just made his whole position worthless on an open channel. Men like Latch don't chase lost causes." Another pause, less certain. "Men like Latch do, however, chase examples. And I just made myself a very instructive one."

We loaded the boats. Nine of us, forty pounds of ice, one transmitter still warm from telling the truth. The last open water lay ahead of us, gray-green and quickening, running toward a delta that might be a gate or a trap depending entirely on who got there first.

As we pushed off, Ines touched my arm.

"The broadcast," she said quietly. "Someone will have heard. Someone always hears."

"Yes."

"Then somewhere downriver," she said, "the world is already coming to meet us. We just have to decide whether we recognize it when it arrives."

The current took the boats. Behind us the glacier went on calving, patient as bookkeeping, and ahead the river narrowed, and narrowed, and narrowed.
