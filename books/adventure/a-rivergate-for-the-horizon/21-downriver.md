## 21. Downriver

The delta announced itself the way grief does—not all at once, but in stages you only recognize afterward.

First the water went wide and slow and brown-green, the color of something that had stopped running from anything. Then the banks softened into marsh, then the marsh into channels braiding apart like hair coming loose, and then there was no river anymore, just a hundred arguments between fresh water and salt, all of them ending in the sea.

I cut the engine and let us drift.

Behind us, maybe forty miles back through country I never want to see again, the pass was closing. We'd heard it for hours—deep concussions rolling downriver like artillery fired by a patient man—and every boat had gone quiet at each one, nobody saying what we were all thinking: *that's where Halloran is. That's where the rear guard of our whole ridiculous flotilla is now part of the geologic record.*

Ines sat in the bow of my boat with her arms around the last sample case, her chin resting on it like a pillow. She hadn't let go of it since the confluence. I don't think she could have if she'd tried; her hands had frozen into the shape of holding.

"Marguerite," she said, without lifting her head.

"Yeah."

"The water's brackish here. You can taste it."

"I know."

"That means it's over."

I didn't answer right away, because the honest answers were all bad ones. It means it's over, yes. It also means eleven people started this run and nine finished it. It also means the man who owned my life for four years is somewhere behind us under a few thousand tons of calving ice, or possibly not, because Latch struck me as exactly the kind of bookkeeper who kept himself off his own ledger. It also means my sister was free, which I wouldn't believe until I heard her voice, which I couldn't do until we reached the coast station, which was still two hours of channel-hopping away.

"It means we made it," I said finally, because Ines deserved one clean sentence after everything, and lies are only unforgivable when they're cruel.

She nodded against the case and closed her eyes.

* * *

The coast station was a shack on stilts with an antenna held up by hope and baling wire, staffed by a weatherman named Okello who looked at our flotilla—three battered boats, nine people gray with exhaustion, forty pounds of ancient atmosphere wrapped in tarps—and said, "Huh," in the tone of a man adding a line item to a very strange week.

Amara took the radio first. Professional habit. She got the delta medical net up, arranged transport for the wounded—the two crew members who'd taken shrapnel when Latch's men opened fire at the confluence, both stable, both complaining, which Amara assured me was the best possible sign—and then she handed me the handset without being asked.

She knew who I needed to call. She'd known since the moraine, since the night I told her about the debt and Dani and the trap I'd walked into with my eyes open because walking fast felt better than standing still.

Dani picked up on the second ring, which meant she'd been sitting by the phone, which meant the Club hadn't told her anything, which meant either they'd forgotten her or—

"Margo?" Her voice came through thin and crackling and eight hundred miles wide. "Margo, is that you? There were men outside the apartment yesterday and then today they were just *gone*, and someone paid three months of my rent, there's an envelope with cash in it, Margo, what did you *do*?"

I leaned against the shack's doorframe and looked out at the water doing its slow braided work of becoming the ocean.

"What did I do," I repeated. "That's a big question, Dani."

"Margo."

"I broadcast it." I said it plainly, because I'd rehearsed versions of this call for days—versions where I softened it, versions where I explained the debt first, versions where I apologized in advance for what it might cost her—and every version sounded like a man trying to keep something. "The archive. The ice data. Sixty thousand years of atmosphere, and proof that half the Vantablack Club's portfolio is built on a collapse they knew was coming. I put it out on open band. Every listener on the frequency. Then I gave copies to Okello here, and he's got a satellite uplink and, I'm guessing, very poor impulse control about news."

There was a silence long enough that I checked the handset.

"You're free," Dani said finally. Not a question.

"The debt's unenforceable. The collateral clause died with the Club's solvency, which—" I laughed, and it came out wrong, rusty, like an engine turning over after a cold winter. "Which happened about six hours ago, according to the shipping news. Half their assets are frozen. The other half are lawyers."

"And the men outside my apartment?"

"Gone, I'm hoping. If anyone comes back, you call me collect at this station and I will drive there so fast I'll leave tire marks on the horizon."

"Margo." And now her voice did the thing it used to do when we were kids and she caught me lying about where the dent in the car came from—that flat, older-sister patience that has never once been fooled. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you make it sound small so I won't worry. What did it cost?"

I looked over at Amara, who was rebandaging a crew member's forearm with the focused tenderness she brought to everything, and who was absolutely listening, because she was always listening, that was the whole problem with loving her.

"It cost me the version of me that thought speed was the same thing as freedom," I said. "Turns out he wasn't worth keeping anyway."

Another silence, but a different kind. When Dani spoke again her voice had gone soft. "Come home sometime. Slowly. I mean it. Drive the speed limit, it'll freak you out."

"I'll consider it."

"I love you, you idiot."

"Love you too. Check the envelope for tracking devices."

* * *

We buried Halloran at the top of the tide line, above the wrack line of kelp and driftwood, where the water would have to work hard to reach him.

Okello found us a plank from a derelict skiff, and Ines carved the marker herself with a dive knife, her hands steady in a way I understood completely: some kinds of shaking you stop by giving them a job. The inscription was short, because none of us had the reserves for long:

**DR. ELEANOR HALLORAN — SHE HELD THE REAR.**

"She would have hated that," Amara said quietly, standing beside me while the others filed past the grave. "She'd say holding the rear was just navigation performed at negative velocity. She'd want something insufferable. 'Dr. Eleanor Halloran, whose data will outlive everyone who ever argued with her.'"

"Then we say that instead," I said. "Out loud. At the funeral. Let the plank be the understatement."

So we did. One by one, the survivors stood at the tide line and said what Halloran had been to them—impossible, brilliant, stubborn as bedrock, generous in ways she'd deny under oath—and I went last, because I'd known her four days and it felt like theft to speak early.

"I met her four days ago," I said, "and she trusted me with the most important thing she ever found, on the strength of a salvage contract and a bad attitude. I've been thinking about what that says about her judgment, and I've decided it says she saw something in me that I hadn't gotten around to seeing yet. I intend to spend some time finding out if she was right." I looked at the plank, at the knife-carved letters going soft in the sea light. "She held the rear. The rest of us are going to hold the front. That's the deal."

Nobody clapped. Good funerals don't get applause. But Ines put her hand flat on the plank for a long moment, and Tomas Reyes—who had refused rescue in Chapter Five of my life and turned out to be the reason any of us had a Chapter Twenty-One—stood at attention like a soldier even though he'd spent the whole expedition insisting he was a civilian.

Then the tide came in and touched the grave's edge once, politely, like a handshake, and went back out.

* * *

Latch's absence was its own kind of presence.

Okello confirmed it that evening, spreading a printout of the shipping news across the shack's table like a man dealing cards he already knew were winners. "Vantablack Holdings entered voluntary receivership at oh-six-hundred. Trading suspended in fourteen related entities. There's a warrant out for a Mr. A. Latch—no first name given, which tells you how much the Club trusted even its own paperwork—in connection with, and I quote, 'the Sereno River incident.' Two bodies recovered downstream of the calving corridor. Neither identified as Latch."

"So he's dead or he's gone," Reyes said.

"He's *bookkeeping*," I said. "Somewhere there's a new ledger with a new name at the top and a line item that reads 'Sereno: total loss, write off, learn.' He'll be back in five years selling flood insurance to coastal cities. Men like him don't drown. They diversify."

"But not your debt," Amara said. It wasn't quite a question either. She'd been circling it all day, the way you circle a wound to check it's really closed.

"Not my debt. Unsecured creditors go to the back of the line in receivership, and the Club's line goes around the block twice. Whatever paper Latch held on me is worth exactly what it's printed on, which is nothing, which—" I turned my empty wallet over in my hands, an old habit, checking a balance that no longer existed in any form. "Which means I'm broke. Actually broke. Not pretend-broke with a syndicate holding the mortgage on my future. Just a man with a truck, a radio, and a reputation I haven't figured out yet."

"Salvage pilot finds lost expedition, exposes climate conspiracy," Okello offered cheerfully. "That's not a reputation, friend. That's a franchise."

"That's a target," Reyes said.

"It's both," Amara said. "Everything true is both."

* * *

Evening came down over the delta the way it does in high latitudes—slowly, reluctantly, the sun refusing to fully commit to leaving—and I walked out onto the flats while the light was still doing its golden inventory of the water.

Amara found me there. Of course she did. She always found me; the question had only ever been whether I stayed found.

We stood shoulder to shoulder, looking at the channels braiding silver and rose toward the sea, and for a while neither of us said anything, because seven years of silence doesn't get paid off in one conversation, but it can get *restructured*. We'd done that on the moraine. Tonight was just the first payment.

"My sister's free," I said eventually. "My debt's dead. My employer is a smoking crater. I have no plan, no money, and a truck with a cracked block that I drove here at speeds I'm not proud of."

"Sounds terrible."

"It's the best I've felt in four years."

She laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of her, and I catalogued it the way I used to, the way you catalogue a sound you're afraid you'll never hear again and then, impossibly, do.

"Marguerite." She turned to face me, and the low sun put a rim of fire along her shoulder, and I thought: *this is what the ice keeps. Not sixty thousand years of atmosphere. This. Moments, archived in a person.* "What happens tomorrow?"

"Honestly? Okello says there's a supply flight out in three days. The samples go on it with Ines and two escorts, straight to a lab that isn't compromised. The rest of the crew follows by boat. And I—" I looked down at my hands, which were still faintly trembling from the pass, from the confluence, from four years of running, and which I noticed, with some interest, were beginning to stop. "I genuinely don't know. First time in my life. I've measured everything in odometer readings since I was nineteen. Miles per day, miles per tank, miles between disasters. And now the meter's broken and I'm standing in a place where the road ends and the water starts, and I keep waiting for the panic and it isn't coming."

"What's coming instead?"

I considered the question honestly, because she'd earned honesty from me at rates no creditor ever had.

"A horizon," I said. "Just sitting there. Being a horizon. Not daring me, not owing me anything. I keep thinking I should chase it and then I remember I don't have to. It'll still be there tomorrow. That's the whole point of it. That's apparently what horizons are *for*."

Amara was quiet for a moment. Then she reached over and took my hand—the trembling one—and held it the way you hold something you're deciding whether to keep, except she'd already decided, years ago, and was just letting me catch up.

"There's a research station in Fairbanks," she said carefully. "Rebuilding the paleoclimate program from scratch, post-broadcast. They need field medics. They also need pilots who can read water and lie to the right people." She glanced sideways at me, one eyebrow up, the old ambush smile. "It's seasonal work. It pays badly. It involves a great deal of ice."

"You're offering me a job."

"I'm offering you a direction. The job's yours if you want it. The rest—" she squeezed my hand, "—the rest we negotiate at highway speed. Or below. Dealer's choice."

I looked out at the delta one more time. Behind us, upriver, the pass was sealed now—I could feel it the way you feel a door close in another room, a change in the pressure of the world. The route that had nearly killed us was gone, and would stay gone until next season, and next season the ice would be different, because the ice was always different now, that was the whole finding, that was the thing I'd carried out on open band while my life burned down around me like a bridge behind a speeding car.

Ahead of us, the sea. Around us, the braids of the river, each one going its own way and all of them arriving.

"Below highway speed," I said. "Considerably below. I'm told it's possible."

"It's possible," Amara said. "I've seen it done."

Far out where the channels met the tide, something surfaced and rolled—a seal, or a porpoise, or just the delta breathing—and the last light came down the water toward us like a message sent slowly, on purpose, by someone who knew we'd wait for it.

Tomorrow there would be flights to arrange and statements to give and a world rearranging itself around sixty thousand years of truth. Tomorrow the phone would ring with journalists and lawyers and, knowing my luck, at least one new syndicate. Tomorrow I'd have to become whatever person Halloran had seen in me four days and a lifetime ago.

But tonight the tide was coming in, and my sister was free, and the woman beside me had decided to stay found, and for the first time since I was nineteen years old, nowhere on earth was chasing me.

I stood on the shore with nothing owed and nothing certain, and I let the horizon stay exactly where it was.
