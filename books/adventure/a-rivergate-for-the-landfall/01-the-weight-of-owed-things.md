## 1. The Weight of Owed Things

The storm had been Isandro Coldharbour's idea, which was the first thing wrong with the day.

She'd read the pressure drop off the ridge-line at dawn — that bruised, greenish sag in the clouds she'd learned to trust before she'd learned to read — and told the mining outfit at Kettle Gap their haul road would be clear until midafternoon. She'd been wrong by four hours. The front came down the Landfall Range like something with intent, swallowing the pass whole, and now her anemometer mast was bent double, her weather balloon was a scrap of latex in a spruce top, and the Syndicate's ore convoy was stranded on a shelf of road that would be gone by morning.

Which meant the money she'd been promised for the forecast was gone too. Worse than gone. It had turned into a debt, and debts to the Vane Syndicate did not stay still. They grew. They compounded like interest on a wound.

Isandro stood in the doorway of her shack above the tree line and watched the storm eat the world. Rain came sideways, hard as gravel. Somewhere down-valley, thunder rolled along the granite shoulders of the range and kept rolling, a long animal sound, and beneath it — fainter, but she could feel it through the floorboards — the deep percussion of the calving fields beginning to wake. Two weeks early, if the old-timers were right. The glacier was shedding its winter skin ahead of schedule, and everything downstream of it, including her, was living on borrowed time.

Borrowed. The word sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Inside, the shack smelled of kerosene and wet wool and the ghost of yesterday's coffee. Her instruments crowded one wall: barometers, hygrometers, a hand-cranked radio she'd traded two good knives for. On the table, under a tin cup she used as a paperweight, lay the letter that had come up with the morning rider — heavy cream stock, sealed with black wax, the Vane Syndicate's sigil pressed into it like a thumbprint on a throat.

*Balance outstanding: 4,120 crowns. Terms revised per Clause Nine. A representative will call.*

Clause Nine. She'd signed the original note three years ago, when her mother's lungs were failing and the doctors in Rivergate wanted payment upfront, and she'd signed it the way drowning people sign things: fast, grateful, without reading. She'd read every word since. Clause Nine gave the Syndicate the right to *restructure* her debt whenever they judged her earnings insufficient, and they had judged so four times now, each revision adding zeros like rings added to a tree.

She poured the last of the coffee, cold, and drank it anyway. Outside, lightning stitched the valley white, and in that instant of light she saw the shape coming up the switchback trail — a single rider, no lantern, moving steady through weather that had sent everyone else indoors hours ago.

Her stomach dropped before her mind caught up. Nobody rode into this but collectors and fools, and the Vane Syndicate never sent fools.

* * *

He arrived at her door dripping like something hauled out of a river, and he smiled the way men smile when they already know how the conversation ends. He was broad, gray-templed, dressed in oilcloth too fine for the mountains, and he introduced himself as Master Corvane, *of the Syndicate's Reconciliation Office*, which was a polite way of saying he collected what people owed and reconciled them to the fact of losing it.

"Miss Coldharbour." He didn't wait to be invited in; he simply filled the doorway, then the room, his presence rearranging her furniture around him. "Miserable night."

"It's the Landfall," Isandro said. "It doesn't do pleasant."

"No." He set a leather case on her table, directly over the letter, and clicked it open. "It does profitable, though. For those who understand it. You understand it better than anyone on this side of the range — that's why we've tolerated your... irregularities." He said *irregularities* the way a priest might say *sins*, gently, with the expectation of confession. "Four thousand one hundred and twenty crowns. And tonight you cost us a convoy's delivery window. Do you know what a missed delivery window costs, Miss Coldharbour?"

"I imagine you'll tell me."

"More than you'll earn in ten years of chasing storms for mining outfits that pay in scrip and gratitude." He drew from the case a folded document, and laid it flat, and turned it so she could read it right-side up, a small courtesy performed with great care. "So. An alternative."

She didn't touch the paper. "I'm listening."

"A government survey team went into the high Landfall eight months ago. Eleven souls. Cartographers, geologists, a glaciologist or two. Officially, they were mapping the headwaters corridor." Corvane's eyes stayed on hers, mild and bottomless. "Officially, they were lost. An avalanche, they said. In summer. On a slope with no snow."

"And unofficially?"

"Unofficially, they stopped answering their relay. Their basecamp sits up there still, stocked, intact. No bodies recovered. No search party ever mounted worth the name." He tapped the document once. "The Ministry closed the file. The Ministry does not close files on eleven missing people unless someone very highly placed wants them closed. We find that interesting. We find it *valuable*. And we want you to go up there, Miss Coldharbour, and find out what happened to them."

"Why me?" She kept her voice level, though her pulse had picked up its feet. "You've got enforcers. Trackers. Men who shoot first and reconcile later."

"Because you know that range better than any living soul, because you're not government, and because—" the smile again, thin as a blade's edge, "—you owe us exactly enough to say yes. Find the team, or find what happened to them, and bring back proof. Do that, and Clause Nine is voided. Every crown. Clean slate. Walk away owing nothing to anyone."

Nothing to anyone. The words went through her like liquor, warm and treacherous. Three years of hauling herself uphill under that weight — the doubled shifts, the gear sold, the winters where she ate what she could trap because every spare crown went down the Syndicate's throat. To be free of it. To own her own mornings again.

And underneath the warmth, cold water: the Vane Syndicate did not give gifts. Whatever they wanted from that mountain, it was worth more than her debt, and they intended to use her hands to dig it out while keeping theirs clean.

"What aren't you telling me?" she asked.

Corvane closed the case. "Everything I don't have to," he said pleasantly. "That's what makes it a bargain."

* * *

They left before first light, because Corvane said the offer expired with the sunrise and she believed him about that the way she believed her barometers. He rode with her as far as the old survey trailhead below Mount Ceren, where a Syndicate sledge waited with the gear they'd recovered from the team's forward depot — and it was there, standing in the blue half-dark with her breath smoking, that Isandro understood how strange the whole thing truly was.

The gear was *good*. Too good. Expedition-grade tents, still zipped shut. Cook stoves packed in their crates. Rope coils stiff with disuse but uncut, harnesses unbuckled, crampons oiled. Eleven people did not walk away from equipment like this. Eleven people did not leave their tents zipped.

"They left in a hurry," she said, turning a tent pole in her gloved hands.

"They left," Corvane agreed, "or were made to look like it. That distinction, Miss Coldharbour, is precisely what we're paying you to resolve." He lifted the last item from the sledge himself, holding it out across both palms with something almost like ceremony. "This was found at the depot. It belonged to the survey leader. We'd like it back when you return — with answers."

A compass. Brass-cased, heavy for its size, the lid worn smooth by years of a thumb worrying it open and shut. Isandro took it and flipped the cover, and the needle swung, steadied, pointed north with a soft sureness that felt obscene given everything else pointing nowhere.

Engraved inside the lid, in a careful hand, were two words:

*E. ASHVALE.*

She frowned. The survey leader's name, according to the file Corvane had let her glimpse, had been Halvard Maren. She said so.

Corvane's face did something very small and very quick — a flicker, there and gone, like a fish turning under ice. "Then perhaps the compass belonged to another member of the team," he said. "People trade things on long expeditions. It hardly matters."

But it mattered. She knew it mattered the way she knew a pressure drop before the instruments confirmed it — some older, truer instrument in her gut swinging hard off true north. A compass engraved with a name that appeared on no manifest, carried by a team that officially never existed, handed to her by a man whose syndicate wanted the mountain searched by someone deniable.

"Eight months," she said. "If anyone survived up there—"

"Then you'll be doing them a kindness, won't you." Corvane swung up onto his horse. The sky behind him was going the color of pewter, and far up-range, faint as a heartbeat, the glacier spoke again. "Two weeks, Miss Coldharbour. After the calving starts in earnest, nobody goes up and nobody comes down. Not even you." He gathered the reins, and paused, and looked at her with something that in a better man might have been pity. "One more thing. You'll have company on the trail. A man named Rennick. He's... experienced in the high country. Consider him a courtesy of the Reconciliation Office."

*A watcher,* she thought. *A leash with legs.* But she only nodded, and watched him ride down into the treeline, and stood alone in the gray morning with eleven strangers' gear at her feet and a dead woman's — or a living stranger's — compass burning a cold circle in her palm.

*E. Ashvale.* She said it aloud, once, just to hear how it sounded against the wind. The needle trembled, as if answering.

Above her, the Landfall Range stood white and silent under its new snow, hiding whatever it hid, patient as a creditor. Isandro Coldharbour shouldered the pack, turned her face toward the pass, and began to climb — owing everything, owning nothing, carrying the one thing on that whole mountain that had a name.
