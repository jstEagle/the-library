## 2. Gear With No Tracks

The storm broke at dawn, and Isandro Coldharbour came down out of it like something the mountain had coughed up — frost in her lashes, blood in one boot, and a debt in her chest heavier than the pack on her back.

The survey camp sat in a bowl below the ridge line, tucked against the shoulder of the glacier where the wind couldn't reach it. From two hundred yards up-slope she counted four tents, a cook shelter, a cache platform, a flagpole with nothing on it. All of it wearing a skin of fresh snow, maybe a hand's depth, maybe less. Two days of snow, three at most. The storm had come in behind her the whole way up the valley, and it had come in behind whoever had left this place too — or whoever had been made to leave.

She crouched at the edge of the bowl and did what she always did before walking into a place she didn't trust: she watched it the way she watched a storm front, reading its edges, its silences, the things it was trying not to tell her.

No smoke. No movement. No birds working the cook scraps, and there should have been birds — the ravens up here were thieves and gossips, and an unguarded camp was a feast. The absence of them itched worse than the cold.

"Alright," she said to nobody, because talking to nobody was a habit the mountains had taught her and she'd never bothered to unlearn it. "Let's see what you're not saying."

She came down into the bowl at an angle, boots deliberate, and stopped at the perimeter of the nearest tent. The snow told its story in the flat, uninflected voice snow always used, and the story was this: no footprints led out. None led in, either, older than the last snowfall — but that meant nothing by itself. What meant something was the *absence* on the far side. She circled the camp once, slow, and the circle held. Four tents, and around all four of them, the snow lay smooth as a bed made by a careful hand. No trail to the latrine trench. No trail to the cache. No trail down-valley toward the government road or up-valley toward the passes.

People did not walk out of a camp without leaving tracks. People did not even *leave a tent* without leaving tracks — not to piss, not to fetch water, not to run. Which meant either the snow had fallen after the last person stepped outside and before the next step, a window of hours at most — or the people had gone up. Taken. Lifted out by helicopter, sledged out on machines that had since been snowed over, erased the way a chalk line is erased.

Isandro stood in the middle of that terrible tidiness and felt the hair on her forearms rise under her layers.

"Alright," she said again, quieter.

The nearest tent was zipped shut from the inside.

She stood in front of it for a long moment, breath smoking, one hand hovering near the zipper pull like it might bite. Tents zipped from the inside meant people had been in them. Tents zipped from the inside with no one in them and no tracks out meant the people had gone through the fabric — or hadn't gone anywhere at all, and the fabric was all that remained of a decision made by other hands.

She pulled the zipper. It ran smooth, oiled, cared for. The tent exhaled a breath of air that had been shut in with it — cold, dry, and faintly, terribly ordinary. Coffee and canvas and kerosene. The smell of a place people had meant to come back to.

Inside: two sleeping bags rolled and lashed. Boots paired at the door. A jacket on its hook. A paperback with a stone on it to hold the page. A tin cup on the crate that served as a table, half-full of something long since frozen, a spoon still standing in it like a flag planted by a man who had stepped away for a moment and intended to step back.

Nobody walked out of a camp and left their boots. Nobody left their coffee. Nobody left a book mid-page unless they expected the page to still be there tomorrow.

Isandro backed out of the tent into the white glare and stood with her hands on her hips, looking at the other three tents, and now she made herself walk to each one, because the job required it and because dread, she had learned, was a debt like any other — you paid it in full or it collected interest.

The second tent: same story. The third: same, except a pair of reading glasses folded on the pillow, which was somehow worse than the boots. The fourth tent was larger, a command tent, and it was here the camp stopped pretending to be ordinary.

A map table stood in the center, and every chart on it had been stripped. Not lost — *stripped*. She could see the ghost-edges where sheets had been pinned, the pinholes in the corkboard, the clean rectangles of unfaded canvas where maps had lain so long the sun had tanned the table around them. Someone had come through and taken every piece of paper in this tent with a line drawn on it.

On the tent's rear wall, where a government survey insignia had been stenciled — the crossed pick and compass rose of the Landfall Range Office — someone had painted over it. Not weathered. Not faded. *Painted*, in a gray that still had a wet-looking sheen under the frost, the kind of gray that comes out of a can issued by an institution that wants a thing gone *now*. She dragged a mittened thumb across it and the paint came away on the fabric in a faint smear. Fresh. Days old, at most. Painted *after* the team vanished, which meant someone had come back to this camp specifically to un-own it. You don't repaint a seal on a camp where a team got lost. You repaint a seal on a camp that never existed.

Isandro stepped out into the light and let out a long breath that shook a little at the end of it, and she was honest enough with herself, alone as she was, to notice the shake and name it.

The Vane Syndicate wanted her to find six government surveyors who had walked into the Landfall Range in high summer and not walked out. The collector — Veyra, all silk and patience, a woman who spoke about debt the way priests spoke about sin — had made it sound like a search. *Recovery of assets*, she'd said, and smiled like the phrase was a kindness. Find the team, find whatever they'd found, and Isandro's ledger would shrink by a figure that had been sitting on her chest for three years like a rockslide.

Isandro had told herself it was a rescue job. Missing persons, that was all. People got lost in the Landfall; the range was a hundred miles of ice and teeth, and it ate surveyors the way rivers ate bridges.

But lost people did not leave their boots.

She went to the cache platform next, because if the team had been taken, the takers had been thorough — and thorough people, in her experience, were thorough until they got bored or until they got everything. The cache was a raised larder on posts, bear-proofed, standard government build. The padlock hung open and empty. Inside: nothing. Not stripped of food — stripped of *paper*. The food was there, sealed rations stacked in neat bricks, fuel cans, a medical kit with its seal unbroken. Everything a team needed to survive months. Everything except documents, charts, journals, and the six people the documents belonged to.

They'd left the calories and taken the truth. That told Isandro more than the empty tents did. Whoever had come through here hadn't been looters and hadn't been in a hurry. They had known exactly what mattered, and it wasn't supplies, and it wasn't even, strictly speaking, the team.

She stood there with the wind combing down off the ridge, and she thought about the compass in her inner pocket, the one Veyra had pressed into her hand with the camp's coordinates like a tip to a fortune-teller. Brass case, government issue, and engraved on the back in a fine clerk's hand: *E. Ashvale*. A name she didn't recognize. A name Veyra had pretended not to care whether she recognized.

She took it out now. The needle swung lazily, settling, pointing north the way needles do, and she watched it the way she'd watched the camp — reading its edges.

Then she turned toward the ridge, and the needle went mad.

It didn't waver. It *spun* — a fast, jittering rotation, like an insect trapped under glass, like a thing in pain. Isandro frowned and turned back toward the valley. The needle settled. She turned toward the ridge again. It spun.

She'd seen this before. Iron in the rock, a buried sled runner, a crashed machine's engine block sleeping under the snow. But she'd never seen it so *clean*, so total, so deliberate — the needle didn't fight toward north and lose; it simply abandoned north altogether, whirling like it was being paid to.

Isandro climbed.

She told herself it was the job. The ridge was the obvious high point; a survey team would have instrument stations on it; whatever was up there turning her compass into a frightened animal might be a marker, a cache, a body. She told herself a lot of things on the way up, and the snow deepened, and the wind came off the crest in long white breaths, and the needle spun faster the higher she went until it was a blur in the brass window and she stopped looking at it and just climbed.

Near the crest, in the lee of a rock rib, she found the first thing that wasn't snow.

A cairn. Small, knee-high, built by hands that knew cairns — stones fitted, not stacked. On top, weighted under a flat rock, a strip of metal flashing, the kind survey crews carried for markers, and scratched into it with a knife point in a hurried, disciplined hand:

*WE WENT TO GROUND. NOT LOST. TELL NO ONE WHERE. — E.A.*

Isandro read it three times. The wind read it over her shoulder.

*Not lost.* Six people who had walked out of their own camp — no, who had been *taken* from their own camp, or had fled it — and had chosen, deliberately, to vanish. To erase themselves harder than the government had erased their seal. *Went to ground* like foxes going to earth, like people who knew they were hunted and knew the hunt would come with paint cans and stripped charts and a syndicate's money behind it.

And *tell no one where* — written by a man whose compass had been handed to a debt collector and passed to a stranger as a hunting license.

Isandro stood in the wind with the cairn at her boots and felt the shape of the thing finally come clear, the way a storm front comes clear when it's still fifty miles out but you can already smell it. The team hadn't wandered. The team hadn't died. The team had *hidden* — from the government that sent them, from whoever had painted over their seal, and now, God help them, from her. Because the only people who sent a broke storm chaser after a vanished survey team were people who wanted the survey team found, and the only people who wanted that were people who wanted what the team had seen.

She looked down-valley, toward the camp with its zipped tents and its half-frozen coffee, and then up, toward the high country where the needle still spun behind her like a struck bell.

E. Ashvale had gone to ground. E. Ashvale had left a message for anyone clever enough to climb a ridge and honest enough to read it. And Isandro Coldharbour, who owed the Vane Syndicate a sum she could not count and who had spent three years telling herself she was still a good person underneath the arrears, had just been asked by a dead-or-alive stranger to keep a secret from the very people holding her leash.

She crouched, and she did not scratch out the message. She did not photograph it for Veyra. She memorized it, every scratch of the knife, and then she sat down in the snow beside the cairn like a woman visiting a grave, and she laughed — one short, hard sound the wind took away.

"Tell no one where," she said. "Ashvale, you absolute bastard. You've got no idea what you just asked me to do."

But her hands were steady when she took out the compass, and the needle spun and spun, and Isandro Coldharbour — debtor, orphan of the lowlands, storm chaser with nothing left to lose but the last scrap of her name — made a decision there on the ridge that she would spend the rest of this story keeping.

She would find E. Ashvale first. Before the Syndicate did. Before the government did. And she would decide, with the evidence in her own two hands, what a debt was actually worth.

She stood, turned her back on the spinning needle, and started down toward the valley — where, three days' hard travel south, a jail cell in Rivergate held a disgraced cartographer whose maps of this very range had been declared false, and whose confession, Isandro would soon learn, was the other half of the message scratched into that strip of flashing.

The mountain kept its secret. Isandro carried hers down out of the snow, and the storm came in behind her, and covered the ridge, and the cairn, and every track she'd made, as if the Landfall itself had agreed to the bargain.
