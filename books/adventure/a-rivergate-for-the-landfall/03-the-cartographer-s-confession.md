## 3. The Cartographer's Confession

The jail at Rivergate was a converted customs house, and it smelled like every government building Isandro had ever hated: wet stone, old paper, and the particular staleness of men who signed things in other men's absence. She'd walked three days down from the survey camp with the compass in her pocket and a warrant's worth of questions in her head, and the magistrate had answered exactly none of them before sliding a visitor's slip across his desk like it was something contagious.

"You want to see the forger," he'd said. Not a question. "She's been asking for you by name. That alone should tell you something."

It told Isandro plenty. It told her that whatever Odile Ferrant knew, she'd known Isandro was coming before Isandro had decided to come herself. That was either a trap or a lifeline, and in her experience the two were often issued from the same office.

The cell block was down a flight of stairs that smelled of tallow and river damp. A single lamp burned at the end of the corridor, and behind the third door, a woman sat at a table bolted to the floor, hands folded, posture straight as a plumb line, as though she'd been expecting company for years and had simply gotten tired of waiting.

"Isandro Coldharbour." Odile Ferrant's voice was dry and precise, the voice of someone who labeled things for a living. "You're shorter than your reputation."

"You're blonder than your mugshot." Isandro leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed. "You've got five minutes before the magistrate remembers he hates visitors."

"Then I'll spend them well." Odile unfolded her hands and laid them flat on the table, palms up, like a dealer showing an empty deck. "You found the camp. You found the compass. And you've spent the last three days asking yourself why a government survey team would vanish with their tents zipped and their tea still in the pot. I can answer that. But not for free."

"Nothing in this range is free."

"No," Odile agreed. "But some things are worth the price. Sit down, Coldharbour. You're going to want to be sitting."

* * *

They had taken everything from Odile Ferrant when they arrested her — her instruments, her commissions, her name — but they had not taken her hands. She spoke with them even now, ten days into a cell, drawing lines in the air above the bolted table as though the air were vellum and she still had the right to mark it.

They had come for her in the spring, she said. Not the constables — the constables were always last, the way gulls come after the gutting. First there had been a man from the Land Office, courteous, well-fed, carrying a folio of her own finished charts for the Landfall survey. He'd laid them out on her worktable one by one, and he'd asked her, very reasonably, to change a few things.

"Change what?" Isandro had asked.

"A valley." Odile's hands stilled. "A river valley, high on the eastern flank, fed by a hanging glacier. My field men charted it in the autumn survey. Good grazing land. Timber. A rivergate — a narrow pass where the meltwater cuts through the ridge — that could be dammed, bridged, settled. It was the single most valuable piece of ground in the entire range, and it was sitting there on my map like an open purse." She paused. "The man from the Land Office asked me to close it."

"Close it how?"

"You can't just erase a valley, Coldharbour. The terrain has to go *somewhere*. So we made it a glacier. I redrew the eastern flank as unbroken ice — crevassed, unstable, worthless. I moved the rivergate two miles north into a box canyon that doesn't exist. I labeled the valley itself as a meltwater basin, seasonally flooded, unsuitable for grant." Her mouth twisted. "It was the finest false map I have ever drawn. Every contour plausible. Every elevation consistent. I made the lie *walkable*, which is the only kind of lie that survives a survey. And I signed it."

"Why?"

"Because he showed me the alternative." For the first time, Odile's composure cracked — not into fear, but into something older and more tired. "The man had a list of my debts. Not the ones I'd declared. The ones I'd inherited from my father's firm when it failed. He had amounts, dates, holders. And he had a price for making all of it go away, which was my signature on the doctored plates, and my silence afterward." She looked up. "You of all people should understand, Coldharbour. They don't buy you with money. They buy you with the *absence* of money. With the thing you owe."

Isandro said nothing. The compass sat in her pocket like a stone she'd swallowed.

"I signed," Odile went on. "And I told myself it was paper. Ink on vellum. No one dies of a redrawn contour line." Her hands flattened against the table. "Then in the spring, the survey team went up to verify my charts before the land grants were sealed. Elias Ashvale's team. Eleven people. And Ashvale — who had walked that range for twenty years, who had *felt* the lie in my contours the way a man feels a wrong step in the dark — Ashvale went looking for the valley I had buried."

* * *

Isandro pulled the compass from her pocket and set it on the bolted table between them.

Odile went very still. Then she reached out — slowly, the way you reach toward something that might bolt — and turned it over. Her thumb found the engraving on the case and stopped there, tracing the letters.

"E.M.A.," she read. "Elias Maren Ashvale." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious at once, which Isandro thought was probably the most dangerous combination a person could wear. "Where did you get this?"

"It was in his kit at the basecamp. Along with everything else they left behind. Zipped tents. Full stores. No tracks out." Isandro watched her face. "The magistrate says they scattered. Says the glacier took them. Says it's a tragedy and the grants proceed next month."

"The magistrate," Odile said, "signed the warrant for my arrest the same week the Land Office man visited me. Ask yourself who tells the magistrate what a tragedy is." She set the compass down with exaggerated care, as though it might detonate. "Ashvale found the valley, Coldharbour. He found it, and he found out what I'd done to the maps, and he did what an honest man does — he packed his evidence and started down the mountain to report it. And somewhere between the valley and the rivergate, eleven people and their evidence stopped existing."

"You don't know that."

"I know that my cell has a new lock. I know that my meals come on schedule and no one has questioned me in ten days, because a dead survey team doesn't need a forger tried — it needs a forger *forgotten*, quietly, before the grants seal." She leaned forward. "I also know that you're going back up that mountain, because you have debts of your own, and I know whose ledger they live in, because the Vane Syndicate is the buyer on the other end of every erased contour line I ever drew."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere above them, a door slammed, and both women flinched in the same instant — a small, involuntary synchrony that Isandro would think about later, more than she wanted to.

"Here is my bargain," Odile said. "I can read terrain the way you read weather, and I can tell you where the official charts lie, because *I* am the one who lied in them. I know where the valley is. I know where Ashvale would have cached his evidence, because I know how survey men think — I drew their maps for six years. Take me out of this cell and put me on your crossing, and when we come down with the truth, my name comes down with it. Cleared. In writing. Publicly."

"And if you're wrong? If the team really did scatter?"

"Then you've lost nothing but a few days and the pleasure of my company." Odile's chin lifted. "But I'm not wrong, Coldharbour. The needle of that compass has been driving you mad since the camp, hasn't it? Spinning near the ridge. Refusing north."

Isandro didn't answer, which was answer enough.

"That's not a broken needle," Odile said softly. "That's a surveyor's compass with a second card under the first. Ashvale had it built that way — a hidden bearing, for hidden things. It's not pointing north. It's pointing at what he buried." She pushed the compass back across the table. "He's telling someone where the truth is. He's been waiting for someone to read it."

* * *

The magistrate refused, of course. Isandro had expected that, so she hadn't asked the magistrate — she'd asked the man from the Land Office, whose name she had lifted from Odile's description and whose signature she had found, three days later, on the bottom of a grant document posted in the customs house window. A little forgery of her own: a release order, Odile Ferrant, remanded to the custody of a licensed guide for recovery of government property in the Landfall Range. The ink was good. The desperation was better.

They walked out of Rivergate at dawn, Odile blinking in the light like a creature pulled from a cellar, a borrowed pack on her back and the compass in Isandro's pocket pointing, when it bothered to point at all, toward a ridge that no official map said was worth crossing.

"You forged a magistrate's seal," Odile said as the road bent toward the mountains. There was something in her voice that might have been admiration, or might have been alarm.

"I forged a *clerk's* seal. Magistrates have better hands." Isandro adjusted her pack straps and didn't look at her. "You lied a valley off a map. I lied a mapmaker out of a jail. We'll call it even until you give me a reason not to."

"And when the Syndicate learns you've pulled me out?"

"They'll learn when it costs them something." Isandro squinted up at the Landfall Range, where the morning clouds were stacking themselves into something with intent. "Which is the only kind of news worth carrying, in my experience."

Odile was quiet for half a mile. Then: "There's a supply post at the tree line. If you want a crossing crew, that's where the desperate ones gather." A pause. "And Coldharbour — if we find what Ashvale buried, understand what we'll be carrying down. Not documents. A land-grant fraud with the Syndicate's name on the buyer's line and the government's name on the seller's. Men have been erased for less than the *rumor* of that."

"Then we'd better not be rumored," Isandro said. "We'd better be loud."

Ahead of them, the first peaks of the Landfall Range caught the sun, and somewhere above the tree line, under ice that was already beginning its slow seasonal retreat, a valley that did not exist waited to be found.
