## 4. Snowmelt and Small Treasons

The trail out of Rivergate was a scar of mud and meltwater, and Odile Ferrant hated every step of it.

She had spent eleven years making maps, and she could not remember a single one that had prepared her for how it felt to walk inside the blank space of her own work. The Landfall Range rose ahead of them in tiers of white and grey, its shoulders wrapped in cloud, and somewhere on those slopes — if her confession held any truth at all — lay a valley she had erased with a ruler and a steady hand. She had told herself for three years that she'd had no choice. The men from the Ministry had come to her shop with her brother's debts in one fist and her reputation in the other, and they had said *adjust the survey*, as though they were asking her to correct an erratum. She had adjusted. She had gone home and been sick into the canal behind her lodging house, and then she had gotten up the next morning and done it again, because a woman who falsifies one map is already guilty, and guilt does not compound the way debt does.

Now she walked at the back of a four-person column led by a storm chaser who smelled of ozone and old rope, and she watched Isandro Coldharbour's broad back and tried to decide whether the woman was going to save her or sell her.

"Your line's drifting," Isandro called over her shoulder, without turning. "You're cutting the switchbacks."

"I'm walking efficiently."

"You're walking like someone who wants to be off this mountain before anyone asks her a question." Isandro stopped and let Odile catch up, and up close her face was all weathered planes and a mouth that looked built for skepticism. "I read your charts. The official ones. They're beautiful. That's what bothers me — nobody draws contour lines that honest by accident. You knew exactly what you were smoothing over."

Odile kept her eyes on the trail. "A cartographer knows every lie on her own map," she said. "That is the curse of the profession. I can show you where the valley isn't. Whether it *is* anywhere—" She shrugged, and the gesture cost her more than she let it. "You'll have to trust me past the tree line. After that, my maps are the only ones worth carrying."

"That's what worries me," Isandro said, but she started walking again, and Odile understood that for this woman, suspicion and acceptance were not opposites. They were just two ways of moving forward.

Behind them both, bringing up the rear with the easy lope of a man who had never once in his life been cold, Rennick Voss counted his steps and memorized his bearings.

* * *

Rennick had left the Syndicate's service twice before, in the small ways people do: he'd skimmed a cut from a cargo run, he'd lied about a debtor's whereabouts to buy the man a night's head start. Both times, the ledger had caught up with him, and both times he'd paid it back and told himself the account was closed. This third time felt different, because this time he wasn't stealing from the Syndicate. He was standing inside its patience, wearing its colors under his coat, and every evening when the camp went quiet he took out the little brass transmitter disguised as a pocket watch and tapped their position into it in the collector's cipher.

*Day two. Coldharbour's party of four. Route via the Kestrel Shelf. The girl is Ashvale's daughter.*

He always hesitated before that last line. Wren Ashvale was seventeen years old and walked like the mountain owed her something, which, Rennick supposed, it did. She had appeared at the Rivergate stables the morning they departed, standing in front of Isandro's packhorse with her arms crossed and her father's spare boots on her feet, and she had said, "Either I come with you or I follow you, and if I follow you I'll die stupid, so you may as well have me where you can see me." Isandro had stared at her for a long moment — Rennick had watched her do the arithmetic, the cost of a minor versus the cost of a corpse — and then said, "You carry your own weight and you obey me on the ice," and that had been that. No ceremony. Families, Rennick was learning, formed faster than trusts.

He told himself the reports were harmless. Information wasn't betrayal; information was just weather, passing through him to someone else. The collector, a soft-spoken man named Halloway with ink-stained fingers and a voice like a bank vault closing, had made it very clear what happened to Isandro's debt if the reports stopped. And what happened to Rennick, which was a shorter list.

But tonight, crouched behind the horse lines with the brass watch open in his palm, tapping out the day's coordinates, Rennick heard footsteps in the snow and closed the case too slowly.

Wren Ashvale stood ten feet away, half in shadow, her breath steaming.

"You walk quiet for someone so young," he said.

"My father taught me. He said you can tell everything about a person by how they move through snow." She didn't come closer. Her eyes went to his hand, to the watch, back to his face. "What are you doing?"

"Checking the time."

"At midnight. Behind the horses."

"The time matters most at midnight."

She held his gaze, and Rennick felt the particular discomfort of being measured by someone with nothing left to lose. Then she turned away toward her tent, and paused. "My father's compass points wrong near the ridge," she said quietly. "Isandro thinks it's broken. I think it's pointing at something. Goodnight, Mr. Voss."

He stood there a long while after she was gone, the watch warm against his ribs, feeling like a man who has sold a ladder and then noticed the fire.

* * *

Wren did not sleep. She lay in her father's borrowed sleeping bag — it still smelled of him, of pipe smoke and pine pitch — and listened to the glacier talk to itself down-valley, a sound like a giant grinding its teeth in its sleep.

Three months ago she had been a schoolgirl in the lowlands, good at mathematics and bad at sitting still, waiting for letters that came thinner each season. Her father wrote about the survey the way other men wrote about love affairs: the light on the icefall at dawn, the blue of deep crevasses, a valley he couldn't name yet but described anyway, in words he must have known were dangerous even as he chose them. *There is grass here, Wren. Grass, at nine thousand feet. Soil you could plant in. They will want to take it from us, and I don't know how to stop them except to write it all down.* His last letter had ended mid-thought, the way letters do when the writer doesn't know a letter is last.

Then the government notice: *survey team overdue, presumed lost to weather.* Then the sealed tents, the intact supplies, the absence of footprints. Then nothing. The world had simply closed over her father like water.

She got up, pulled on her boots, and went to sit by the banked cookfire, where Isandro sat sharpening a knife with the metronomic patience of someone who used sleep as a tool rather than a need.

"He's reporting us," Wren said.

Isandro didn't look up. "I know."

"You *know*?"

"I've known since the stables. He checks his watch at odd hours and he never winds it. A man who checks an unwound watch isn't telling time; he's sending it." The knife whispered along the stone. "Halloway sent him to keep a leash on me. It's fine. As long as I know where the leash is, it's just rope."

"And when the rope pulls?"

Isandro finally looked at her, and in the firelight her eyes were the flat grey of the icefields above them. "Then we'll find out what kind of man is holding the other end." She nodded at Wren's hands, which were white-knuckled around her knees. "Show me the journal page again."

Wren passed it over — the one scrap of her father's writing she'd brought, copied out in her own hand so the original stayed hidden in her boot lining. *Grass at nine thousand feet.* Isandro read it the way she seemed to read everything, once, completely, filing it somewhere behind her eyes.

"Odile says the valley would sit past the calving fields," Wren said. "Under the ridge where the compass spins. My father found it. The others found it. And instead of coming home, they—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, hated being seventeen, hated the way grief made her small. "They hid. Or they were taken. Either way, they knew enough to be afraid of their own government, and nobody told me. Nobody told *anyone*."

Isandro set the knife down. For a moment she looked almost gentle, which on her face was as disorienting as a warm current in a frozen sea. "My mother used to say the mountains keep what people can't carry," she said. "Maybe your father's up there keeping something too heavy to bring down alone. So we go get it." She picked the knife back up. "Sleep, Ashvale. Tomorrow we cross the tree line, and after that I need everyone sharp."

* * *

Dawn came up pink and merciless, and the crew broke camp in the wordless choreography of people who were not yet friends but were learning each other's rhythms. Odile unrolled her true charts on a flat rock — the ones she'd drawn from memory, the ones that had nearly hanged her — and weighted the corners with stones.

"Past here, the official survey is fiction," she said, tracing a line above the tree line where the printed maps showed clean, gradual slopes. "In reality there's a serac field, a hidden cirque, and a crevasse system the size of a cathedral. If we follow the government chart, we die politely and on schedule."

"And yours?" Isandro asked.

"Mine are drawn by a liar." Odile met her eyes steadily. "But a liar who wants to confess."

Something passed between them then — not forgiveness, nothing so generous, but a recognition, two women who had both sold pieces of themselves to survive and were now trying to buy them back at altitude. Isandro rolled up the chart and handed it back with a nod that contained, for her, a speech.

They climbed. The tree line fell away below them in a dark tide of wind-bent pines, and the world opened into white. Wren walked second, counting her father's boot prints in her memory instead of the snow. Rennick walked last, and at the noon halt, while the others ate, he drifted to the edge of camp and opened the brass watch one more time.

His thumb hovered over the cipher keys.

*Day three,* he began, and then stopped. Below them, far down the valley, he could see a thread of smoke rising from the tree line where no licensed party should be. Two threads. Three. Climbing parties, moving with the unhurried discipline of professionals. Of collectors.

His thumb moved. But what he tapped out was not the coordinates Halloway wanted.

*Party of four ascending Kestrel Shelf. Do not approach the ridge. The compass is right. There is grass up there.*

He snapped the watch shut, heart hammering, and told himself he didn't know why he'd done it.

Above them, faint as a struck bell, the mountain made a sound none of them had a name for yet — a deep, tectonic groan rolling down from the calving fields, the glacier clearing its throat. Odile looked up sharply, checking the sun, counting days in her head.

"We have less time than I thought," she said. "The season's turning early. We cross the ice fields tomorrow — or we don't cross at all."
