## 5. What the Glacier Keeps

The ice gave up its dead slowly, the way it gave up everything — grudgingly, in its own time, on terms no one else had set.

Wren Ashvale had been watching the moraine wall for three days before she understood what she was looking at. At first she'd taken it for a rockfall scar, a smear of darker stone exposed where the glacier's shoulder had slumped in the summer melt. But stone didn't glint like that. Stone didn't have corners.

"Odile," she said, and her voice came out strange to her own ears, flattened, as if the altitude had pressed it thin. "Come look at this."

Below her, at the edge of camp, Odile Ferrant looked up from her drafting board — an actual board, lashed to a sled runner, because the woman refused to work from memory even when memory would have been faster. Her spectacles caught the white glare and turned it silver. "If it's another rock shaped like something, I've already seen the one that looks like your father's profile, and I told you, that's pareidolia, not providence—"

"It has corners."

That stopped her. Everything about Odile stopped when data entered the room; it was the closest thing to a pause button the woman possessed. She climbed the moraine with surprising care for someone who claimed to have no head for heights, and when she reached Wren's side and followed the line of her gloved finger, she went very quiet.

"That's not natural," Odile said.

"No."

"That's *manufactured*."

"Yes."

They dug for two hours. Isandro joined them after the first forty minutes without being asked, which was its own kind of language from a woman who asked permission of no one. Rennick hung back with his arms crossed, pretending disinterest so thoroughly that Wren knew he was memorizing every detail for whatever report he still believed he was going to send. She had stopped pretending not to know about Rennick's reports. It made him careful, and careful men made mistakes slower.

The ice came away in plates, blue-white and glassy, and beneath it lay a cache — a proper survey cache, aluminum-bound and government-stamped, the kind her father had drilled into his teams the way other men drilled scripture into their children: *the mountain takes everything you don't secure, so secure everything.* The seal was intact. The stencil on the lid read **LANDFALL COMPREHENSIVE SURVEY — TEAM ONE**, and beneath it, smaller, hand-painted over the official designation in weatherproof enamel, a single word:

**KEPT.**

Not lost. Not abandoned. *Kept.* Someone had stood here in the wind with a brush and made a promise to whoever came looking.

Wren's hands were shaking too badly to work the latches. Isandro took over without comment, and there was something almost gentle in how the storm chaser handled the clasps, as if she understood — as Wren could not yet make herself understand — what might be inside.

Inside were instruments wrapped in oiled cloth. A theodolite. Barometers. Rations gone to tallow and dust. And at the bottom, wrapped in a shirt she recognized instantly, violently, the way you recognize a face across a crowded station platform — a canvas-bound journal, its cover stained with mold and altitude, tied shut with bootlace.

Her father's handwriting was on the flyleaf. *Elias Ashvale, Survey Leader, Team One. If found, deliver to Wren.*

"To me," Wren said. "He addressed it to me."

No one said anything. The glacier creaked somewhere below them, that vast slow groan it made when it settled, like a house the size of a country shifting in its sleep. Isandro put the journal in Wren's hands and stepped back, and even Rennick looked away, giving her the small mercy of privacy in a place that offered none.

She didn't open it at the cache. She carried it down to her tent, and sat with it on her knees through the long gold hour of alpenglow, and did not open it, because once she opened it he would be dead again — properly dead, finally dead, dead in a way that couldn't be undone by hope or stubbornness or any of the machinery of denial she'd been running since the government had sent her a form letter with the word *presumed* in it.

Then she opened it.

* * *

*Day 41 above the tree line. The charts are wrong. Not mistaken — wrong. I've surveyed enough bad maps to know the difference between error and intention, and this is intention. There is a valley west of the Serpent's Spine that appears on none of our official sheets. Odile Ferrant's originals show it. The versions we were issued do not. Someone between her desk and our packs removed eleven square miles of habitable land from the record.*

*It is the greenest country I have seen above four thousand feet. Hot springs feeding meadow grass in the dead of winter. Timber stands old enough to be cathedral wood. Water in quantities the eastern provinces would go to war over. We named it Rivergate, unofficially, because the meltwater runs through it like a gate swinging open.*

*Day 44. Merrin thinks we should log it and move on. I keep thinking about who paid for this survey. I keep thinking about who will buy what we find.*

*Day 47. Today a government skiff overflew us without flight clearance. They photographed the valley for six minutes. Then they photographed us.*

*Day 52. I have made copies of everything. The land-grant drafts, the correspondence, the valuation assessments — they are already pricing this valley, Wren, they are pricing it while we stand on it, and the price assumes it is empty, and it is not empty, there are families living in the high meadows who have been here longer than the Republic has been a republic, people the maps were drawn around, not over. If anything happens to us, the second cache holds the sealed documents. The compass knows the way. I'm sorry I never told you about the compass. I'm sorry for most things lately.*

*Day 55. Men came in the night. Not survey men. They wore no colors but they moved like the Syndicate moves, and they had our route memorized better than we did. We are splitting the evidence. If you are reading this, then the ice kept my half, and I am glad, and I am sorry, and I love you. Go west. Follow the needle past the calving fields. Trust the mapmaker. She drew the truth first.*

Wren read it three times. By the third reading the words had stopped being words and become simply the sound of her father's voice, which she had not heard in fourteen months, and which she now understood she would never hear again except like this — ink and pressure and the ghost of a pen driven hard enough to emboss the next page.

They hadn't wandered. They hadn't been lost. They had been *hunted*, on their own survey, by their own government's money, and the ice had kept the proof the way ice keeps everything: faithfully, patiently, until someone came willing to dig.

When she came out of the tent her eyes were dry and her hands were steady, and both of those facts felt like someone else's. Isandro was sitting on a supply crate sharpening a knife she didn't need to sharpen, and she looked up, and whatever she saw in Wren's face made her put the knife down.

"He left it for me," Wren said. "The whole thing. He knew they'd come for them, and he built a trail out of caches and compass bearings so that whoever survived would find the rest." She held up the journal. "There's a second cache. West. Across the calving fields. Sealed documents — land grants, valuations, names. Names inside the Ministry, names inside the Syndicate. He says trust the mapmaker."

Every head in camp turned toward Odile Ferrant.

The cartographer had gone the color of old candle wax. "I drew the valley," she said quietly. "I drew it true. I signed affidavits saying my charts were altered after they left my office, and no one — *no one* — would hear it. They took my license instead of my story because a story can still be dangerous, and they knew it." She pushed her spectacles up with one trembling finger. "Elias Ashvale wrote that?"

"He wrote that."

"Then I'm coming to the calving fields," Odile said, and for the first time since Wren had met her, the woman sounded less like a disgraced clerk begging for redemption and more like exactly what she was: the only person alive who knew what the Landfall Range actually looked like underneath its lies.

* * *

They broke camp at first light, because the season would not wait for their grief. The calving fields began beyond the Serpent's Spine — twelve miles of glacier terminus stacked against a hanging valley, where the ice front leaned over the melt-lake like a rotten wall waiting for a reason. Every guide Wren had ever heard of crossed it early in the season or not at all. The season had already begun. Two nights ago they'd heard the first calving, a report like artillery rolling up the valley, and Isandro had stood outside her tent listening to it with an expression Wren was beginning to learn how to read: the look of a woman doing arithmetic with thunder.

"The lake is rising," Isandro said at the morning council, tapping the ice field on Odile's chart. "Warm nights, heavy melt. Every day we wait, the crossing gets shorter and the seracs get hungrier. Every day we wait, whoever the Syndicate sends gets closer." She glanced at Rennick when she said it, briefly, deliberately, and Rennick studied his boots with the devotion of a man praying. "So we cross tomorrow. Today we rest, rope up, and mark the route."

It was Wren who took the last watch before dawn, and Wren who stood alone at the edge of camp with her father's compass open in her palm.

It was a beautiful instrument, brass-cased, older than the survey — older than her, maybe. She'd assumed it was standard issue until tonight, turning it under the starlight, tracing the engraving on the caseback that she had somehow never looked at closely before, or had looked at and refused to see. Small letters, worn nearly smooth:

*For I.C. — the needle always finds its way home. — E.A.*

I.C. Not W.A. Not Elias. Some other initial entirely, some name her father had loved enough to give away his compass to — and the needle, which had spun madly near the ridge at the abandoned camp, now pulled steady and insistent toward the west, toward the calving fields, toward the second cache, as if it had known the way all along and had only been waiting for her to ask.

Behind her, a sound: boots on frozen scree, unhurried. Rennick, walking out of the dark with his hands visible and his face unreadable, stopping just beyond arm's reach.

"You should sleep," he said. "Big day tomorrow."

"Who is I.C.?" Wren asked, and held the compass up between them, and watched the defector's expression do something complicated and terrible in the starlight.
