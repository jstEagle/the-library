## 6. The Collector's Patience

Rennick had written the report at dawn, kneeling behind a boulder with his back to the camp, the transmitter cupped in both hands like something warm he was trying not to spill. *Party of four. Two days ahead of schedule. Crossing the lower moraine toward the tree line by midday. Ashvale's daughter is with them — Coldharbour didn't turn her away.* He had hesitated over that last line longer than he should have, thumb hovering above the send stud, because there was no tactical value in it and yet he'd typed it anyway, the way a man confesses to a priest things the priest already knows.

He sent it. Of course he sent it. A man did not survive eight years inside the Vane Syndicate by developing scruples at altitude.

And still his hands shook afterward, and he told himself it was the cold.

The truth was uglier: he had begun to like them. That was the rot in him, the flaw the Syndicate had never managed to burn out despite everything they'd tried. He liked Odile Ferrant's dry muttering over her contraband maps, the way she treated the mountain like an old colleague who'd lied to her once and could be forgiven but never forgotten. He liked Wren Ashvale's ferocity, the girl carrying her dead father like a lantern she refused to set down. He even liked Isandro Coldharbour — and that was the most dangerous liking of all, because Rennick knew what the Syndicate intended for her, and every mile north made the knowing heavier.

He had told himself the job was simple. Watch the storm chaser. Keep her moving toward the pass. Let the collectors do the rest. Simple work, if you kept your heart in a lockbox and threw away the key.

The tree line came up on them in the early afternoon, a ragged dark hem of blackpine stitched across the slope, bent all one direction by forty years of wind like grass before a comb. Beyond it the ground rose into open scree, and beyond that, faint as a scar, the white shoulder of the Landfall ice fields. Wren spotted the shelter first — a survey hut, government-stamped, half-swallowed by a drift — and Isandro called a halt, and Rennick felt the moment arrive the way you feel a floorboard give beneath your boot.

Because the collectors were already there.

He saw them before anyone else did, which was its own small damnation: two figures standing among the pines up-slope, patient as fence posts, dressed in the grey weather-gear that cost more than a dockworker earned in a year. One of them raised a hand — not a threat, a greeting, courteous as a banker — and Rennick's stomach dropped through the mountain.

"Isandro," he said, and his voice came out wrong, too flat. "Don't go up there."

She turned. She was quick, always quick; he'd counted on that quality when he recommended her to the Syndicate three years ago, and now it turned against him like a blade brought home. Her eyes went from his face to the treeline to his face again, and he watched the arithmetic complete itself behind her eyes, fast and merciless.

"You've been reporting us." Not a question. She said it quietly, which was worse than shouting. "Since the trailhead."

"Since before the trailhead," Rennick said, because lying now would be a second betrayal stacked on the first, and he found he hadn't the stomach for it. "Every camp. Every mile. I'm sorry."

Wren's hand went to her knife. Odile simply stepped back, one pace, the way a person steps away from a structure they've decided is about to collapse. And Isandro looked at him for a long, cold moment and said nothing at all, and that silence was the worst thing he had ever been given.

Then the collectors came down through the pines, unhurried, boots crunching, and the moment for anything except survival was gone.

* * *

The tall one was called Meric Voss, and Rennick knew him from the old days in Rivergate — knew the way he tilted his head when he was deciding how much of a person to leave functional. Voss stopped ten feet off, hands loose at his sides, and smiled at Isandro Coldharbour with genuine warmth, which was the most frightening thing about him.

"Coldharbour. You've made excellent time." He glanced at the others the way a man glances at luggage. "Ferrant. And this must be the Ashvale girl. The Director will be pleased you're all together. Saves everyone a great deal of travel."

"We're not going anywhere with you," Wren said.

"No," Voss agreed pleasantly. "You're going with us, or you're staying here. Those are the options. The mountain doesn't offer a third." He turned back to Isandro, and his tone shifted into something almost kind, which was how the Syndicate delivered its cruelties — wrapped in consideration, like poison in sugar. "Let me simplify your situation. Your debt stands at eleven thousand crowns plus interest, plus recovery costs, plus — new this morning — the fee for hiring falsified charts without disclosure. You cannot pay it. You were never going to pay it. The debt was never meant to be paid, Coldharbour. It was meant to keep you walking where we pointed. It's a leash, not a ledger. Surely you'd worked that out."

Rennick watched her take it. Watched it land — the whole architecture of her last three years reassembling itself in her face, every desperate job, every scraped-together payment, every night she'd spent doing arithmetic that was designed never to come out even. He watched her understand that the storm-chasing contracts, the "opportunities," the collector who'd offered her this job as an *out* — all of it had been a hand on a collar.

"Yes," Isandro said finally. Her voice was steady. "I'd started to wonder."

"The survey team found something the government sold before it ever surveyed it," Voss went on, conversational, terrible. "A valley. Fertile ground, water, timber — worth more than every mine in the range. The grant is signed. The buyers are named. And four people and a disgraced mapmaker are not going to undo a transaction of that size. So here is my generous offer. Give me the documents' location — or the compass, since we know it points to their caches — and walk away owing nothing. Truly nothing. Clean slate. Or refuse, and we take everything, and the mountain keeps whatever's left."

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere above, ice cracked and settled, a sound like a door closing in an empty house.

And Rennick discovered that his body had made a decision his mind had been avoiding for six days.

His sidearm was out of its holster before Voss finished turning. Eight years of Syndicate discipline lived in his hands — the draw was clean, the stance was correct, and the muzzle found the center of Meric Voss's chest with the intimacy of long acquaintance. The second collector's hand flashed toward his own weapon and froze there, weighing odds.

"Rennick." Voss didn't sound surprised. That was the horror of it; he sounded *disappointed*, a tutor watching a promising student fail his final examination. "Put it down. You know how this ends. You know what happens to defectors. There isn't a hole deep enough in the Landfalls—"

"I know," Rennick said. His voice was calm. His hands were not, and he let them shake; let them see it. "I've buried enough of them to know exactly how it ends. But here's what you haven't priced in, Voss. I've spent a week watching these people. The mapmaker gives away her rations when she thinks no one sees. The girl hasn't slept properly since her father died and still takes second watch every night. And Coldharbour—" he glanced at her, and couldn't hold the glance, and looked back at the gun sight instead, "—Coldharbour owes you nothing, because you never lent her anything. You can't collect on a leash by calling it a loan."

"Your supplies," Voss said softly. "Your transmitter. Your route knowledge. You'll die up here with them, and it won't be quick."

"Probably." Rennick's finger tightened. "But not today, and not by you. Drop your weapons. Kick them downhill. Then walk back the way you came, and tell the Director the Ashvale survey is closed."

For three full seconds, Meric Voss considered him. Rennick had seen that look before, on men deciding whether a problem was worth the mess. Then the second collector moved — a feint, half a heartbeat, hand dipping — and Rennick fired past Voss's shoulder into the pine trunk beside his head, close enough to snow dust his collar, and whatever arithmetic Voss had been running came out against him. Weapons hit the scree. Boots crunched away upslope, unhurried even in retreat, because men like Voss never hurried; patience was the whole of their religion.

At the tree line Voss paused, and turned, and called down: "There are more of us than there are of you, Rennick. There always have been."

"Then you'd best hope," Rennick said, "that we're better."

* * *

Isandro made them strip the collectors' abandoned packs and redistribute everything by lamplight in the survey hut, because sentiment was a luxury and gear was not, and only after the last carabiner was racked did she turn to Rennick, who sat apart against the far wall with his hands empty in his lap like a man at his own trial.

"Say why," she said. "And say it true, because I'll know."

He looked at his hands. "Three years ago I read your file. Debt, talent, no family, no one to miss you if the mountains kept you. I flagged you as an asset. That's how I thought of people — assets, liabilities, exposure. It was easier than thinking of them as people." He drew a breath. "Then I watched you take Ashvale's daughter aboard when turning her away was the smart move. I watched Ferrant navigate us through that crevasse field off charts that could hang her. And I realized I'd spent eight years working for men who would have erased every one of you the way they erased the survey team, and called it business, and slept fine." His jaw worked. "I don't sleep fine anymore. That's the whole of it. No nobility. Just insomnia."

Odile snorted softly from her corner. Wren said nothing, but she moved her bedroll six inches closer to his, which from Wren Ashvale was a pardon signed and sealed.

Isandro crouched in front of him. Up close he looked older than his file claimed, grey coming in early at the temples, a man worn smooth by years of being handled.

"A leash," she said. "Not a ledger."

"Yes."

"Then here's what happens to leashes." She held out her hand. "We cut them. All of ours. Yours to the Syndicate, mine to the debt, Odile's to whatever they're holding over her, Wren's to her ghosts. We cut them at the top of that glacier or we die trying, but nobody on this mountain owes anybody anything after tomorrow — except each other. Can you live under those terms?"

Rennick took her hand. His grip was cold and strong and shaking, and he did not try to hide it.

Outside, far up the valley, the ice fields spoke — a long, rolling thunder, the season's first calving, the mountain clearing its throat. Isandro stood in the doorway and listened, and understood that the sound was a clock, and that somewhere behind them Voss was already reporting to men with more guns and less patience, and that the pass ahead would not stay open for grief or doubt or anything at all.

"Dawn," she said. "We cross at dawn."
