## 7. Crossing Before the Calving

Odile Ferrant had spent eleven years drawing maps that lied, and she had told herself a hundred comfortable stories about why. The government wanted clean lines. The public wanted simple country. A map was a promise, and promises were always simplified. She had believed it, mostly, right up until the night they took her drafting table away in handcuffs and she understood that every line she'd smoothed had been a door closing on someone.

Now she knelt on the ice at the edge of the Landfall calving fields with her true charts spread beneath a rock and a wind-chewed thumb, and the land itself was correcting her work in real time.

The first calving came an hour after dawn.

She felt it before she heard it — a tremor through her knees, through the ice, through the small bones of her hands — and then the sound arrived, rolling up the valley like the sky splitting along a seam. Somewhere behind them, miles back along their own trail, a face of the glacier let go. Odile had heard avalanches. This was not that. This was older, vaster, the sound of a mountain deciding to become water.

"Time's done being polite," Isandro said. She stood at the field's edge with the compass open in her palm, its needle steady for once, pointing into the white. "Odile. Tell me you can get us across."

Odile looked at her chart — the real one, inked in her own hand in a prison cell by candlelight, smuggled out sewn into the lining of a coat she no longer owned. It showed the crevasse maze as a spiderweb of fine blue threads, each one a crack deep enough to swallow a team and their rope and their names. No official chart showed any of it. The official chart showed this whole plateau as blank, pleasant white, suitable for travel, suitable for nothing to happen here at all.

"I can get us across," she said. "If you walk exactly where I say, exactly when I say it."

"And if we don't?"

"Then the glacier keeps us," Odile said. "It's very good at keeping things."

Isandro held her gaze a moment longer. There was something in the storm chaser's face Odile hadn't seen there before — not trust, not yet, but the shape trust makes when it's deciding whether to arrive. Then Isandro turned to the others. "Rope spacing, ten meters. Nobody steps off my heels except when Odile says otherwise. Wren — you're on probe. Rennick, rear guard, and if I hear one word about Syndicate routes being faster, I'll feed you to the first crevasse myself."

Rennick raised both hands, half surrender, half apology. He'd been quieter since the tree line. Quieter, and carrying less, and watching Isandro with an expression Odile recognized from mirrors: a man recalculating what he owed and to whom.

They went onto the ice.

* * *

The maze began gently, which Odile knew was its cruelest trick. The first hour was firm snowbridge over hidden dark, the surface so smooth it seemed almost courteous. Wren walked point with the probe pole, driving it down every two paces — thock, thock, thock — a heartbeat made audible. Each time it sank through snow into nothing, Wren marked the spot with a wand of red cloth and Odile bent over her chart and adjusted.

Her true map was good. She allowed herself that much pride, hunched against the wind with numb fingers tracing the blue threads. She had drawn this maze from memory, from fragments, from the testimony of a trapper who'd crossed it thirty years ago and from her own reading of the old survey photographs the government had never deigned to examine closely. And here were her threads, made flesh: the long lateral crevasse at the plateau's throat, the braided field beyond it, the pressure ridge shaped like a sleeping animal that the trapper had sworn looked like his dead wife.

But good was not the same as true. Ice moved. Her map was three years old, and the glacier had been thinking the whole time.

"It's shifted," she said, an hour in, kneeling at the edge of a crevasse that should have been forty meters east. "Everything's drifted. The whole field is rotating — slowly, but—" She pressed her palm flat to the chart. "I drew you a map of a river. We're standing in the river."

"So redraw it," Isandro said.

It wasn't cruel. That was the thing about Isandro Coldharbour that kept surprising Odile: the bluntness without contempt, the demand without dismissal. *Redraw it.* As though a woman who had spent eleven years falsifying maps could simply — do the honest thing, now, at speed, with the sky cracking behind them.

"I need height," Odile said. "I need to see the pattern. Crevasses aren't random — they follow the flow of the ice, and the flow follows the bedrock underneath. Give me ten minutes on that ridge and I can read the next two kilometers like handwriting."

"The ridge is wind-scoured serac," Rennick said. "You fall off it, you won't even make a hole in the snow. You'll just be gone."

"Then don't watch," Odile said, and started climbing.

She didn't tell them the other reason she needed the ridge. She didn't tell anyone, ever, the real reason she'd memorized this valley, this range, this whole erased country: because somewhere under the ice was the place her brother had been mapping when the government recalled him, and she had never learned what he found. Every honest line she drew now was a line drawn late, an apology in ink, addressed to a man who might be alive in a valley that officially did not exist.

From the ridge, the maze opened beneath her like a sentence finally legible.

There. The master crevasse, running northwest, feeding its children southward in families — she could see the whole grammar of it, the way the ice stretched over the buried bedrock ridge and tore along its flanks. And there, threading between the families, a corridor of unbroken bridge where the compression had healed the cracks shut. It wasn't on her chart. It couldn't be; three years ago it hadn't existed. But it was there now, a pale road through the web, and the glacier had drawn it for her.

She scrambled down, breathless, and put her finger on the chart. "Here. Not my line — a new one. Compression zone. The ice has been squeezing itself shut for three years and it's left us a road." She looked up at Isandro. Snow was in her eyelashes and her hands wouldn't stop shaking and she had never in her life meant words more. "Trust me. Not the map. Me. I'll read it as we go."

Isandro studied her for a long moment. Behind them, another calving boomed, closer now, a drumbeat counting down.

"You drew lies for eleven years," Isandro said. "Why should I believe the truth comes easier?"

"Because lying is work," Odile said. "Every false line has to agree with every other false line, forever. The truth only has to be itself." She swallowed. "And because I'm tired, Ms. Coldharbour. I am so tired of doors closing."

Something shifted in Isandro's face. She clipped Odile's rope to her own harness — front position, beside her, not behind. "Then read," she said. "We walk together."

* * *

They crossed the throat of the maze as the light went strange and low, the sun drowning in cloud to the west, and the glacier behind them began to speak in earnest.

It came in waves now — calving after calving, each report closer than the last, each one followed by that long subterranean groan, as though something enormous were turning over in its sleep and finding them crawling on its chest. Snow dust drifted past them in horizontal rivers. Wren stopped talking entirely, which frightened Odile more than the thunder; the girl had talked constantly since the cache, since her father's journal, since grief had found a job to do. Now she drove the probe in silence, thock, thock, thock, and Odile walked beside Isandro and called the turns.

"Left of the blue thread. Wide step here — the bridge is thin, feel for the hollow sound. Now hard right, along the pressure ridge, don't look down the far side—"

Once, the ice spoke back. They were mid-corridor when the snowbridge beneath Wren exhaled — a soft whump, a settling, a sigh of air escaping some buried chamber — and the surface sagged a handspan under her boots. Wren froze. Odile saw her go still in the particular way of someone standing on a held breath.

"Don't move," Odile said. She crouched, laid her palm flat, listened with her whole body the way she'd once listened to paper. The sag was local. The compression around it was sound. "Two steps forward, fast, on my mark. One — two—"

Wren moved, and the bridge held, and nobody breathed until they were all twenty meters on.

"That was your map?" Isandro asked quietly.

"No," Odile said. "That was me."

Isandro nodded slowly. And then, in the flat tone of someone paying a debt she hated owing: "Ferrant. If we live through this — I misjudged you. In the cell, and after. I thought you were a woman who sold the valley."

"I did sell the valley," Odile said. "Line by line. That's why I know the price." She met Isandro's eyes through the streaming snow. "The question isn't whether I betrayed it once. It's whether you'll let me buy it back."

Isandro didn't answer with words. She reached over and adjusted Odile's rope knot — a small, unnecessary tenderness, a cartographer's correction, making a true line truer — and walked on.

* * *

They came off the last snowbridge as full dark fell, onto the shoulder of rock and old moraine where the maze ended, and behind them the calving fields lit themselves from within — no, Odile realized, that was lightning, or something like it, the glacier flashing green-white as vast masses sheared away, each flash followed seconds later by the roar rolling up their backs like a hand pushing them onward.

Rennick counted under his breath. "That one was inside two miles."

"Then we camp cold and move at first grey," Isandro said. "No stove. No light. We eat standing up."

They ate standing up, four figures on a black shoulder of stone while the world behind them tore itself apart and rebuilt itself as water. Odile sat apart with her chart on her knees, revising by memory and starlight, adding the new corridor in fresh ink, striking through her old confident lines. It felt like penance. It felt like prayer. The landscape had watched them cross it — she was certain of that, in the way mapmakers are certain of things they cannot prove — and it had permitted them, barely, grudgingly, the way a witness permits a stranger to pass who has finally begun telling the truth.

Wren came and sat beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. "My father had one of your maps," the girl said. "The official one. He used to laugh at it. He'd say, *whoever drew this has never been cold in their life.*" She paused. "He'd have liked knowing it was you. That you came back for it."

Odile's throat closed. She looked at the compass hanging at Isandro's neck, catching the green flicker of the dying glacier, its needle trembling toward the high pass ahead — toward the second cache, toward whatever survived there, toward the truth they carried like a lit fuse.

"He's going to know soon," Odile managed. "All of it. Everyone will."

The words had barely left her when Rennick rose from the shadows at the camp's edge, binoculars fixed on the pass above them, and his voice came out stripped of everything but fact.

"Lanterns," he said. "On the switchbacks. Six of them. Climbing."
