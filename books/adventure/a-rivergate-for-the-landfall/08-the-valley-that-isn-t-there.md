## 8. The Valley That Isn't There

The compass stopped spinning at dawn, and Isandro felt it like a hand closing around her wrist.

For six days the needle had been a mad thing — whirling, sulking, pointing at nothing and everything as they climbed out of the crevasse fields Odile had navigated by memory and stubbornness. Now, on a shelf of grey stone above the last icefall, it settled. It pointed not north but east, into a wall of rock that the official charts insisted did not exist.

"Odile," Isandro said quietly. "Your valley."

The cartographer came up beside her, breath steaming, fingers blue at the tips despite the good gloves Rennick had traded for in Coldharbour's last outpost. She looked at the compass, then at the rock wall, then at a fold in the mountain that no one who hadn't spent twenty years drawing lies for the government would ever have noticed — a seam of shadow too regular to be accident.

"It should be there," Odile said. "I drew it out myself. They made me erase it twice." Her voice had the flatness she got when the old shame rose in her. "Third time I drew it small enough to hide inside an error margin. If they found this cache, it's because someone remembered my mistake."

Wren was already moving toward the seam, ice axe hooked through her belt, her father's journal strapped against her chest under her coat like a second heartbeat. Isandro let her go. There was no stopping Wren Ashvale when she walked toward her dead; there was only walking beside her.

* * *

The passage was a chimney of broken stone, half-choked with avalanche debris, and it opened the way a held breath opens.

Isandro had imagined the valley a hundred times since Odile first confessed to its erasure — imagined green, imagined water, imagined something soft in all this iron and ice. What she found was more terrible than any of it: a river running silver through a floor of scree and stunted pine, a hanging meadow gone gold with autumn, and above it all the Landfall peaks rising like witnesses. It was beautiful. It was habitable. It was everything the charts said could not be here, which meant every acre of it had been sold on paper as worthless while some clerk in the capital signed away a living country.

"There." Rennick's voice, low and rough. He pointed down the slope to where a cairn of stones marked a hollow beneath an overhang — and where, beside the cairn, a figure sat motionless in the frost.

They went down carefully, weapons loose, because for eleven days everything that had looked like rescue had looked like a trap instead. But the figure didn't move until Wren broke from them at a run, and then it moved all at once — scrambling back against the stone, snatching up a rusted signal flare gun and levelling it with hands that shook so badly the aim described small circles in the air.

"Stay off the grant land," the man rasped. "It's sold. You're trespassing. It's all been sold."

"Corwin Ashvale's team," Isandro said, keeping her voice level, the way you talked to spooked animals and armed creditors. "We're looking for what he left."

The flare gun wavered. The man's eyes — sunken, cracked-lipped, wild at the edges but with something underneath still burning — fixed on Wren, on the set of her jaw, on the shape of her face.

"You have her chin," he whispered. "You have Elise's chin. Wren?"

"Uncle Teodor," Wren said, and her voice cracked clean through, and the flare gun dropped into the snow.

* * *

He was Teodor Vasch, survey geologist, and he had been alone in the valley for eight months.

They got a fire going in the overhang while Wren sat pressed against his shoulder as if afraid he'd dissolve. He ate the way starving men eat, badly and desperately, and talked the way men talk who have not spoken aloud in seasons — in bursts, in loops, circling back to the same terrible center.

"They came at night," he said. "Not Syndicate. Government. Black jackets, no insignia, but the paperwork was official — I saw the seals. They took Corwin and the others up the pass. I'd gone down to check the seismographs in the lower cut. When I came back, camp was empty. Tents zipped. Kettle still warm." His hands trembled around the tin cup Isandro pressed into them. "So I hid. I hid and I watched, and after two weeks a supply flight came in — not to us, to men in black jackets — and they started surveying. Staking. Measuring my colleagues' graves, maybe, or wherever they were taken. And I understood: we weren't lost. We were *in the way*."

"The documents," Isandro said gently. "Corwin's journal mentioned a second cache."

Teodor laughed, and it was a horrible sound, dry as calving ice. "Cache. Yes. He called it that so the radios would sound innocent." He crawled to the back of the overhang, hauled aside a slab of stone, and lifted out a waterproof case scarred with eight months of hiding. He set it in Isandro's hands like a man handing over a live grenade.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth: sealed land-grant documents, countersigned, stamped, witnessed. The Landfall Valley holdings, transferred at a fraction of assessed value to holding companies whose names Isandro knew the way a drowning woman knows the shape of the water — Vane proxies, every one. And beneath the grants, letters of instruction bearing signatures from three deputy ministers and, on the final page, a name that made Isandro's breath stop: the Vane Syndicate's own charter counsel, co-signing with the Office of Interior Lands.

They hadn't just erased a valley. They'd written themselves into it, legally, with the government's own hand.

"That's why they can't let us come down," Odile said softly, reading over Isandro's shoulder. "It's not the fraud. Fraud gets buried all the time. It's that these signatures are *current*. These men are in office now. This is a confession with their names on it."

Isandro looked at the papers, and she thought about the number. The number the collector had recited to her in a warm room in Coldharbour while snow fell past the window — the debt, principal and interest, that owned the next ten years of her life. All she had to do was bring this case to the Syndicate's own lawyers, unopened, and walk free. That had always been the shape of the job. Find the team, find the proof, deliver it, be forgiven.

She had told herself for eleven days that she hadn't decided. She decided now, standing in a stolen valley with a dead friend's brother-in-law shivering beside her and his niece watching her face.

"Wren," she said. "When we get over the pass, this goes to every relay station in the range. Wide-band, unencrypted, before anyone can buy it back or bury it. Every operator, every settlement, every ship in the sound."

Wren looked at her, and there was grief in her face and fury and something that might, in another life, have been gratitude. "My father would have wanted—"

"Your father wanted it *known*," Isandro said. "That's why he hid it instead of selling it. He could have bought his way home with this. So could I." She closed the case. "That's the whole trick of it. They make the truth worth more than the telling."

Rennick shifted at the edge of the firelight. He'd been quiet too long, and Isandro had learned what his quiet meant. "There's a problem with the telling," he said. "Look at the pass."

* * *

She climbed the moraine at the valley's mouth and glassed the eastern saddle, and her stomach dropped through the mountain.

The pass was closing. Not with weather — with people. A column was coming up the far side, roped, disciplined, carrying loads that caught the low sun with a glint she knew from bad years and colder nights: Syndicate field gear, Syndicate rifles. Eight, maybe nine climbers, moving well, moving fast, and behind them on the glacier below, the white scar of a landing strip already stamped into the snow.

And the sky over the saddle was doing what the sky did in late season — bruising violet along the west, wind streaming off the summit ridge in long banners. Calving weather. The icefall below the pass would let go with the next pressure change, and everyone on the wrong side of it when it did would be on the wrong side forever.

"They knew where the cache was," Rennick said beside her. "They always knew. They let us do the climbing." He paused, and Isandro heard him decide to say the rest. "I reported our position. Twice. Before the icefall. I—" His jaw worked. "I thought I was reporting to handlers who wanted the team found alive. I didn't know they were the ones who buried it."

Isandro lowered the glasses. She thought about leaving him on the moraine. She thought about how he'd turned his gun on his own handlers at the tree line, how he'd given away his supplies, how he'd carried Wren's pack for two days without being asked. She thought about debts, and leashes, and the difference.

"Then you'll carry the case," she said. "Over the pass. On the rope with the rest of us. That's how you report now."

Something went out of his shoulders that he'd probably been carrying since Coldharbour.

"We can't outrun them to the saddle," Odile said, studying the pass with a cartographer's eye. "But we don't have to. There's a route — mine, off the erased maps. It cuts under the icefall through the medial moraine and comes out above their column. If we move now, we reach the narrows first. The narrows is a defensible hour, maybe two. After that—" she glanced west, at the bruising sky, "—after that the mountain decides."

"And if the mountain decides wrong?" Wren asked.

Teodor Vasch pushed himself upright against the overhang, gaunt and shaking and absolutely steady-eyed. "Then the mountain buries the proof with us," he said, "and the valley stays a lie on paper forever. Get me up that pass. I've been dead for eight months. I'd like the dying to mean something."

Isandro slung the case across her chest, cinched it tight, and looked once more at the column crawling toward the saddle — at the old life coming up the mountain to collect her.

"All right," she said. "Rope up. We're going through the valley that isn't there, over a pass that won't exist by nightfall, carrying the truth that ends the Syndicate. Move like your lives depend on it."

She turned west into the rising wind.

"Because they do."
