## 9. A Rivergate for the Landfall

The pass had a name on no map, and Odile Ferrant had given it one anyway, in pencil, on a chart no government would ever certify: *the rivergate*. A slot of gray water between walls of blue ice, narrow enough that a strong arm could throw a stone across it, and the only way down from the high country before the calving season sealed everything like a fist closing.

Isandro Coldharbour stood at its throat and listened to the glacier breathe.

It was not a sound so much as a pressure — a deep, tectonic groan rolling up through her boot soles, through the bones of her shins, into the teeth she'd been clenching since dawn. Somewhere behind them, miles back across the ice fields, the first calvings had begun. She had felt them as tremors in the snow, seen the gulls lift off the seracs in startled sheets. The season was turning. The land was keeping its own appointments.

And behind them, closer than the calving, came the Syndicate.

"Smoke," Wren Ashvale said. She lay flat on the moraine ridge above the pass, her father's journal strapped tight against her chest, her binoculars steady in hands that had not stopped shaking for two days and would not stop now. "Two columns. They've stopped moving. They're waiting for something."

"They're waiting for us to panic," Rennick said. He was crouched over the radio case, feeding wire with the unhurried competence of a man who had once done this for the other side. His supplies were gone — burned, traded, spent buying them their escape at the tree line — and he looked thinner for it, hungrier, and somehow cleaner. "They want us cornered. Cornered people make deals. That's the whole religion of the Vane Syndicate, Isandro. Debt and desperation. They'll offer you your ledger back for the case."

Isandro looked down at the case. It sat between her boots like a sleeping animal: oilskin-wrapped, sealed with survey tape, heavy with the land-grant documents that named the buyers inside the government — the men who had erased a valley, erased a survey team, and signed the empty land over to the Syndicate with ink the color of dried blood. Half-mad Teodor Vasko had guarded it in his ice cave for a year, talking to his dead colleagues, and had pressed it into Isandro's hands with the gravity of a man handing over a coffin.

"They can offer," Isandro said. "I've stopped listening."

* * *

Odile Ferrant had spent eleven years learning that maps were arguments, and the government had won every argument by simply deleting what it didn't like. She knelt now at the rivergate's edge with her true chart spread on a flat stone, weighted at the corners with glacier-polished pebbles, and traced the water's course with a finger gone brown at the nail from cold.

"It floods here first," she said. "The meltwater backs up behind the icefall every spring — a lake that isn't on any official chart, because officially there is no valley to feed it. When the glacier calves, the dam fails. The whole rivergate becomes a river." She looked up at Isandro, and her voice, usually so careful, so hedged with a disgraced woman's qualifiers, came out flat and certain. "We have maybe two hours after the first big calving. Less, if the wind shifts the melt."

"So we don't be here in two hours," Wren said.

"We're not leaving," Isandro said.

The silence that followed had weight. Rennick's wire-feeding hands went still. Wren turned, and even Odile rose from her chart.

Isandro crouched and unwrapped the radio. It was a relay unit, government issue, scavenged from the survey camp — the kind bolted to every weather station in the Landfall Range, the kind that could, with the right codes, which Rennick had carried in his head for six years, broadcast to every station at once. Every ranger post. Every mining claim. Every listening ear from the tree line to the coast.

"You're going to read it," Wren said slowly. "All of it. Out loud."

"I'm going to read it until someone makes me stop." Isandro met her eyes. Wren had her father's stubbornness and none of his patience, and Isandro had come to love her for it the way you come to love weather — helplessly, and with a certain respect for the damage. "Your father's team didn't die for a valley. They died because they refused to say it wasn't there. The least I can do is say it is. Loudly. On their frequency. In their names."

"And the Syndicate party climbing toward us?" Rennick asked.

"Isandro," Odile said gently, "they will kill you."

"They'll try." She stood, and the wind off the ice took her hair and whipped it sideways, and she felt — for the first time since a bad season and a bad bet had put her name in a Vane Syndicate ledger — light. "I've spent three years paying a debt I never understood. Every job, every storm I chased for their money, every favor — I thought I was buying my way out. I was buying my way *deeper*. This—" she put her boot on the radio case, "—this is the first payment I've ever wanted to make."

* * *

Rennick had defected from the Syndicate once before, and it had cost him everything except his life, which he had privately considered a bureaucratic oversight. Now he sat at the radio with the transmission codes written on his forearm in grease pencil and the Syndicate's climbing party three quarters of an hour out, and he felt something he had not felt since he'd first signed their papers: *clean*.

He worked the dials. Found the relay band. The first station answered with a bored operator's click and a request for identification.

"Rennick," he said. "Formerly of the Vane Syndicate, currently of nowhere in particular." He glanced at Isandro. "Say the word."

"Say everything," Isandro said. "Say it twice."

He said everything. He gave the codes, opened the network, and Isandro Coldharbour — storm chaser, debtor, dead woman walking — unwrapped the land-grant documents and began to read.

She read the grant numbers. She read the names: undersecretaries, survey commissioners, a magistrate whose signature she recognized from her own debt papers. She read the purchase price the Syndicate had paid for land that officially did not exist, and the date the survey team had been declared lost, and the date — eleven days *earlier* — that the grant had been signed. She read Teodor Vasko's sworn statement, dictated in a shaking hand by lamplight in a cave of ice, naming the men who had come for his team in the night.

The relays lit up one by one. Station Four. Station Nine. The ranger post at Cold Larch. The coastal office. Operators who had been half-asleep at their dials sat up. Some cut in with questions. One, voice cracking, said: *"My brother was on that survey team."*

"I know," Isandro said into the microphone. "So was my compass."

* * *

Wren Ashvale heard the Syndicate party before she saw them — boots on scree, the metallic whisper of a rifle sling. She left the radio's glow and took the ridge, moving low, and found them coming up the moraine in a loose file: six men, roped, professional, their breath pluming in the blue afternoon light.

The leader raised a hand and they stopped. He looked up at her — a girl on a ridge above a drowned valley's only door — and called out, courteous as a banker.

"Miss Ashvale. Your father was a colleague of the Syndicate's, once upon a time. We only want the case. Walk down, and no one is charged with anything. The debt of your passage is forgiven."

Wren thought of her father's journal against her chest. She thought of the last entry: *If I am not believed, let the ice believe me. Let it keep what they erased until someone comes to carry it out.*

"You can't forgive a debt," she called back, "that was never mine. That's the whole problem with you people. You lend what was never yours and collect what was never owed." She lifted the flare pistol Odile had pressed on her that morning. "The valley is there. Say it isn't. Go on. Say it to my face with every relay in the range listening."

Below them, faint but unmistakable, Isandro's voice was still reading names.

The Syndicate leader's courtesy cracked, just at the edges. "Last chance."

"Glaciers don't give last chances," Wren said, and fired the flare straight up, the signal — *they're here, they're committed* — and the ridge erupted into the ugly, brief business of holding a pass: Rennick's rifle cracking from the rocks, Odile's voice calling ranges off her own map like a gunner's litany, stones loosed to slide and scatter the roped line. Nobody died. That mattered to all of them, though none of them said so. They were not the Syndicate. They only needed minutes.

* * *

Isandro read the final page as the glacier answered.

The calving came not as a sound but as a verdict — a serac the size of a courthouse shearing off the icefall and falling into the meltwater lake behind the dam, and the whole world lurched, and Odile screamed *the gate, the gate, GO*, and the rivergate pass went from a slot of gray water to a brown, boiling, thundering flood in the space of ninety seconds. The route behind them — the route the Syndicate party needed, the route Isandro had used three years ago fleeing a storm she should never have chased — vanished under ten feet of churning melt, icebergs grinding in the current like teeth.

The Syndicate stood on the far moraine, stranded, their courtesy gone, their rifles useless across a river that had not existed an hour ago. The land they had bought with erased names had declined to be sold.

Isandro keyed the radio one last time.

"This is Isandro Coldharbour, transmitting from the Landfall Range. The documents are read. The valley exists. The survey team of Aldous Ashvale was murdered for finding it. Every station hearing this: copy it, keep it, tell it." She looked at her crew — Wren with the flare pistol still raised and tears freezing on her face, Odile holding her true map against the wind, Rennick with his rifle across his knees and his eyes closed like a man at prayer. "My debt to the Vane Syndicate is settled. Not paid. *Settled.* There's a difference. Ask the glacier."

She switched off the transmitter, and the silence that followed was not empty. It was the silence after a verdict.

* * *

They made camp that night on the high shelf above the flood, and the rivergate roared below them like something being born. Rennick brewed tea from snow and the last of everything. Odile began, in pencil, on fresh paper, the first honest map of the valley that wasn't there — and now, by morning, would be everywhere.

Isandro sat apart with the compass open in her palm. The needle no longer spun. It pointed, steady and true, down into the flooded dark, toward the cache, toward the journal, toward the name engraved on the case that she had finally, on the ice, let herself read aloud: *E. Coldharbour*.

Her father's compass. Her father's valley. Her father's debt, paid.

Wren came and sat beside her without a word, shoulder against shoulder, and passed her the tea.

"Tomorrow," Wren said, "we walk down and tell everyone what we did."

"No," Isandro said, watching the flood carry the old world away. "Tomorrow we walk down and find out what they did with what we gave them. That's the part I'm afraid of."
