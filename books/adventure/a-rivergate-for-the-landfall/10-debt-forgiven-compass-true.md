## 10. Debt Forgiven, Compass True

The news reached the valley the way spring did — slowly, then all at once.

Isandro heard it first as a rumor carried up the rivergate trail by a trapper with frostbitten ears and a sack of letters no one had expected: the Vane Syndicate's charter was dead. Suspended, dissolved, its officers summoned to the capital in irons or in flight, depending on which account you believed. The land-grant documents they'd carried over the pass — the ones Isandro had broadcast from the relay station with her hands still bleeding and the floodwater thundering at her back — had done what no storm chaser's courage ever could. They'd unmade an empire of paper.

She read the official proclamation twice, standing in the doorway of the survey cabin that Wren's father had built with his own hands, its timber still smelling of the resin he'd sealed the joints with. *The existence of the Rivergate Valley is hereby acknowledged.* As if a valley could be unacknowledged. As if the river had ever cared what the maps said.

"They got the wording wrong," Odile said over her shoulder. She was bent over the great table she'd claimed as her own, drafting the new charts — the true ones, the ones with the valley drawn in confident ink, its contours taken from her own instruments and her father's journal and the half-mad testimony of Corvin Hale, last of the survey team, who was slowly coming back to himself in the cabin at the valley's heart. "It's not *acknowledged*. It's *restored*. There's a difference. One is a gift. The other is an apology."

"Which do you want?" Isandro asked.

Odile's pen paused. She was thinner than when they'd met, harder too, the disgrace burned off her like tarnish. "Neither," she said. "I want the maps to be right. The rest is weather."

Isandro left her to it. Some people made their peace with paper. Odile had made her peace with ink.

* * *

The valley in full spring was not the place the government's charts had promised — no wasteland, no worthless ice-shadow. It was green. Absurdly, stubbornly green, a long trough of meadow and birch and slow braided water cupped between ridges that the Landfall Range had kept secret the way a fist keeps a coin. The survey team had found it and understood at once what it was worth, and what it would cost them. Nine of them had died for that understanding. Corvin had survived by hiding so deep in it that the searchers' own maps led them astray.

Now the families were coming down from the high camps where Corvin had sheltered them — not just the survey team's kin but others too, prospectors and homesteaders the Syndicate had displaced, people who'd been living off every ledger and land roll for years. They came with goats and seed potatoes and children who'd never seen a valley that was allowed to exist. Wren stood at the meadow's edge with her father's journal under her arm, directing the first wagon down the switchback like a general, though her voice cracked on every third word.

"They're going to name something after him," she told Isandro that evening, by the fire. "A peak, maybe. The survey peak. It's what he'd have hated most."

"He'd have hated it politely," Isandro said, and Wren laughed — the first real laugh Isandro had heard from her since the ice fields, since the cache, since the morning she'd read her father's last entry aloud with her voice steady as a level and her hands shaking like a storm flag.

There was talk of hearings, of tribunals, of Isandro giving testimony in the capital. A magistrate's letter sat in her pack, requesting her presence, offering her protection, offering her — the word made her snort — *standing*. She hadn't answered it. The truth was already out, broadcast to every relay in the range; there was nothing her voice could add that her voice hadn't already added. Let the lawyers have the courtroom. She'd had the pass.

* * *

Rennick came back on the fourth day of the thaw, walking up the valley trail with an empty pack and a face like a man who'd argued with a glacier and won on points.

He'd spent the winter in the lowlands, testifying, he said, in exchange for his freedom — turning over everything he knew about the Syndicate's collectors, their routes, their ledgers, the leash they'd kept on Isandro's debt. He'd watched the charter dissolve from the gallery of a hearing room. "I kept waiting to feel something," he told her, sitting on the cabin's woodpile, turning his ruined gloves over in his hands. "Triumph, maybe. Or fear. All I felt was cold. I think I left the cold up on the ice."

"Your supplies didn't," Isandro said. "You gave us every ration you had to buy us that head start."

"I gave them what they were going to take back anyway." He shrugged, but his eyes didn't. "I spent eleven years collecting debts for men who never once asked what a person owed themselves. I'd like to find out. If that's not too sentimental for a defector."

"It's sentimental," Isandro said. "Stay anyway."

He stayed.

* * *

The debt itself died quietly, which Isandro had not expected. She'd imagined it as a thing with teeth, something that would have to be killed. Instead a clerk in the capital signed a form, and the number — the enormous, crushing, eleven-years-of-her-life number that had driven her into storm cells and debt cells and finally up a mountain — ceased to exist. The Syndicate's claims were voided with its charter. Every debt. Every lien. Every name in every ledger.

She found out from Rennick, who read it off the bottom of a legal notice and then looked up at her carefully, the way you look at someone standing on thin ice.

"That's it," he said. "You're nobody's."

Isandro walked out of the cabin and down to the river and stood there a long time. The water ran loud and clean over stones that had never appeared on any official chart. Eleven years she'd been running — from collectors, from creditors, from the memory of a father who'd walked into the Landfall Range one autumn and never walked out. She'd told herself the debt was the chain. But the chain had been the running. And here, in a valley that officially did not exist until three weeks ago, she had stopped.

* * *

The compass told her the rest.

She'd carried it since the collector had pressed it into her hands that first day, along with the survey team's abandoned gear — a brass instrument, dented, its needle spinning wild near the ridge where the caches lay. She'd assumed it belonged to one of the vanished surveyors. She'd carried it as a debt of a different kind, a thing owed to the dead.

It was Wren who noticed, on an evening in late summer when the whole crew had crowded into the cabin against the rain. Wren, who'd grown up watching her father's hands, who knew his tools the way other children knew lullabies.

"Where did you get this?" she said suddenly, very still, the compass flat in her palm.

"A collector. Syndicate. They said it came out of the survey camp."

"It didn't." Wren turned it over. There, on the case's underside, worn nearly smooth, was a maker's mark and a serial number, and beneath them a repair stamp — a tiny stamped bird, the mark of a shop in Coldharbour that had closed twenty years ago. "My father sent instruments here for servicing. He told me once about a compass he'd repaired for another surveyor — a storm chaser who worked the lowland passes. A man who went missing in the Landfall before the survey ever started." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and fierce. "Isandro. What was your father's name?"

Isandro's mouth had gone dry. "Elian Coldharbour."

The compass sat between them on the table, its needle swinging, settling, pointing — not north, not toward any cache, but out the window, up the valley, toward the high country where the survey peak stood and where, eleven years ago, a man had walked with his instruments and his maps and his stubborn, doomed honesty.

"He wasn't erased with the team," Wren said softly. "He was erased before them. He found something, or he was looking for it, and the Syndicate buried him first — and then buried the valley, and then buried the survey team that found it. Your debt, Isandro. It was never money."

Isandro picked up the compass. It was lighter than she remembered. Or her hands were stronger. The needle trembled and held, steady as a heartbeat, pointing at the place where the land kept its dead and its truth in the same green silence.

"They gave it to me on purpose," she said. "The collectors. They knew whose it was. They handed me my father's compass and sent me up the mountain to erase the last of him." She laughed, and it came out ragged. "And I carried him home instead."

* * *

They stayed. That was the whole of it, and it needed no proclamation.

Odile stayed because the valley was unmapped in every way that mattered and she intended, at last, to map something honestly, beautifully, completely — the great table by the window, the new charts spreading across it like wings. Rennick stayed because he was learning what a man owed himself, and the answer so far seemed to involve firewood, goats, and Wren's laughter, which he pretended not to court and courted shamelessly. Wren stayed because the valley was her father's grave and her father's gift, and because someone had to teach Corvin's sheltered families how to read the weather, and because — though she'd have denied it — she could not have left the three of them any more than she could have left the mountain.

And Isandro stayed because for the first time in eleven years, no one owned her morning. She stood on the cabin step at first light with the compass open in her hand, and the needle pointed up the valley, and she understood at last that it had never been pointing at caches or passes or debts. It had been pointing home the whole time. She just hadn't had one.

The trapper came again at the end of summer, with letters and a newspaper and a rumor that the capital was commissioning a new survey of the Landfall — a true one — and that they wanted it led by someone who knew the range, who'd crossed it against the calving, who'd held the rivergate pass when it mattered. The letter named her. There was money in it, and men, and glory, all the things she'd chased through storms for a decade.

Isandro read it by the river, then folded it into her pocket and looked up at the peaks going gold with the evening, at the smoke rising thin from the valley's first honest chimneys, at Odile's window glowing and Wren's voice carrying down the meadow and Rennick's axe keeping time somewhere in the birches.

"Storm season's coming," Rennick said, appearing beside her, hands in his pockets.

"Is," she agreed.

"Going to chase it?"

Isandro closed the compass and smiled, and the needle inside, she was sure, did not move an inch.

"No," she said. "I'm going to be home when it hits."
