## 1. The Tide That Owed Nothing

The bus dropped me at the bottom of Rivergate's one hill at four in the afternoon, in weather that couldn't decide between drizzle and apology. I had eleven dollars and forty cents, a duffel bag with a broken zipper, and a knife I wasn't supposed to have crossed two borders with. That was the full inventory. I've traveled lighter and I've traveled poorer, but rarely with such a clean slate—no contacts, no reputation, no one in three hundred miles who knew what my name used to mean.

That was the point. Names are heavy. I'd left mine in a courthouse in Kathmandu and a newspaper in Santiago, and what was left over—Petra Voss, thirty-four, no fixed trade, references available upon request from absolutely no one—felt like a coat that fit a woman I'd stopped being.

Rivergate sits where a river the color of old pewter gives up and becomes the sea. The town is built on the slope above its own harbor like a man leaning back from a table he suspects is about to be flipped. There's a cannery, a church with a leaning spire, a harbor trust office with brass on its door so polished it looked like a threat, and a lighthouse on the northern headland that had been decommissioned so long it had the resigned posture of a retired animal. The whole place had the smell of fish and diesel and rain that had been falling, in one form or another, since the Pleistocene.

I went into the cannery because the cannery was hiring. The sign in the window said NIGHT SHIFT, GUTTERS, NO EXPERIENCE NECESSARY, which was a lie in the sense that experience was necessary, just not the kind they meant. I'd gutted fish before, in Alaska, in another life with another name, and my hands remembered the work even when the rest of me preferred to forget everything.

The floor manager was a woman named Brann with forearms like dock rope and the eyes of someone who had fired better liars than me. She looked at my hands, then my face, then my hands again.

"You've done this," she said.

"I've done things."

"Name?"

"Voss. Petra Voss."

She wrote it down without interest. That was the first gift Rivergate gave me: my name, written down by a stranger, meaning nothing. I could have wept.

"Night shift starts at ten. Don't be late, don't drink on the floor, don't talk about the Trust where the Trust can hear you."

"Is the Trust in the habit of listening?"

Brann looked at me the way you look at a tourist asking about the weather in an avalanche zone. "Everything here belongs to somebody," she said. "Best to remember whose."

* * *

Gutting fish is honest work, which is to say it is repetitive, cold, and smells like something died and meant it. You stand at a steel trough for eight hours with a blade and a conveyor of silver bodies, and you empty them, and you don't think, because thinking is for people whose hands aren't full of herring. The woman next to me, older, missing two fingers on her left hand and unbothered about it, taught me the local rhythm without a word—two cuts, a twist, a scoop, flick. By two in the morning we had a conversation going entirely in elbows.

Her name was Sefa. Around three, during the dead hour when the conveyor slows and the floor goes quiet enough to hear the harbor, she nodded toward the high windows.

"You'll want to look at that," she said. "If you've never seen it."

"Seen what?"

"The tide going out."

I glanced up. Beyond the smeared glass, the harbor lights were reflected in water that looked—wrong. I couldn't have said wrong how, not then. Water reflects light the way it reflects light; you stop noticing the grammar of it after a while. But something in the sentence of it had lost a verb.

"Low tide," I said. "Happens twice a day everywhere."

"Not like this." Sefa didn't stop working. Two cuts, a twist, a scoop, flick. "Tide tables say low's at six-forty. It's three now. And it's not supposed to go out past the channel markers." She said it the way you'd mention that the sun had come up in the west—annoyed, mostly, at the inconvenience of physics.

I kept gutting. I want that on the record, whoever's keeping it: I kept gutting for a full four minutes, because I had decided, somewhere in the last two years, that I was a woman to whom strange things did not happen anymore. Strange things happened to guides. Guides stood on ridgelines and read the sky and made decisions that got printed in newspapers with the word *tragedy* in the headline. I gutted fish. Fish did not present mysteries. Fish presented roe.

Then the foreman killed the conveyor for the changeover, and the silence came in like a physical thing, and through it I heard the water.

Not waves. Water *leaving*. A long, low exhalation, the sound of the bay draining through some throat too wide to see, and under it a hiss, and under that—this is the part I still don't say easily—a kind of groan, deep and structural, the sound a building makes when it settles, except the building was the seabed.

I took off my apron. Sefa watched me do it and didn't ask where I was going, which meant she already knew.

* * *

The whole town, it turned out, had come out to watch. Not the whole whole—there were always the ones who wouldn't look, and I learned later those were the smart ones—but a good hundred people stood along the harbor rail and down on the exposed cobbles of the old boat ramp, in coats and blankets, in the particular silence of a crowd watching something it has decided not to comment on.

The bay was emptying.

I don't mean the tide was low. I've seen low tides that exposed mudflats and stranded boats at drunken angles. This was different. This was the sea packing up and going. The water's edge had retreated past the channel markers, past the sandbar, past the kelp line, out and out toward the horizon, leaving behind a vast gleaming plain of seabed—sand and stone and the ribbed patterns the water leaves when it's been somewhere a long time and is leaving in a hurry. Pools sat in the hollows, silver under the moon, full of stranded fish turning in slow circles like people at a party where they no longer know anyone. A crab walked the flats with the brisk confidence of a commuter. The whole bay, a mile of it at least, lay open to the air like a wound that had decided to show itself.

"Third time this season," said a man near me, to no one. "Third time."

"Tables are wrong," said his companion. "Trust says the tables are right and the sea's wrong."

"Trust would."

I stood at the rail with my gutting knife still in my pocket and my hands smelling of herring, and I did what I always do when the world misbehaves: I read it. Old habit. You spend enough years responsible for other people's lives on ice and rock and moving water, you develop a reflex for terrain, and what I was looking at was terrain—new terrain, exposed terrain, a landscape that had been underwater since before the lighthouse was built and was now, temporarily, a place a person could walk.

And beneath the lighthouse, where the headland's black rock went down in old stairs of basalt to a tideline that wasn't there anymore, the sea had opened a door.

I saw it first as a shadow, then as a shape, then as a mouth. A cave, a real one, tall as a church door and going back into the headland's dark like a held breath. The charts of this coast—the ones I'd read on the bus, because I read charts the way other people read horoscopes—showed nothing there. Solid rock, the charts said. And yet the rock had a doorway in it, and the doorway was weeping seawater in thin bright threads, and the sand in front of it was rippled and fresh, printed with the retreating water's last fingerprints.

Nobody else seemed to be looking at it. Or rather—this was the detail that raised the hair on my arms—everyone was very carefully not looking at it. The crowd watched the flats, the stranded boats, the moon. Their eyes slid off the cave mouth the way eyes slide off a scar on a face you're being polite about.

"Hey." I said it to Sefa, who had followed me down. "What's under the lighthouse?"

"Rock."

"There's a cave."

"There's rock." She said it mildly, but she said it the way Brann had said *whose*—with a weight on the word, a door closing. Then, quieter, watching the flats: "My grandmother said the water used to go out like this, before the seawall. Before the Trust. She said the bay was breathing then. In and out. Like something alive." She shrugged. "Old women's stories."

The wind came off the empty seabed carrying a smell I knew from high places, from glacier caves and volcanic vents—the smell of deep cold air coming up out of the earth, air that had been underground long enough to forget the sky. On a coast. Under a lighthouse. In a bay the charts called solid rock.

Here is the thing about me, and I want to be honest about it because the rest of this story will try to make me sound better than I am: I have a defect. When a normal person sees an impossible door in a cliff, they feel a shiver and go home. I feel the shiver and then I feel the pull, and the pull is a rope, and I have never in my life not tied in. Mountains, rivers, caves, bad marriages, doomed expeditions—it's all the same rope. You clip in because the not-knowing is worse than the falling.

But I'd been trying, these last two years, to be a person who walks away. I'd gotten good at it. I had a whole technique: you don't ask the second question, you don't read the second chart, you don't learn the second name. You gut your fish, you take your pay, you get on the bus, and the mystery stays behind you on the platform, getting smaller.

So I stood at the rail and I made the argument. I made it thoroughly, the way you'd argue down a client who wants to summit in weather. Eleven dollars and forty cents. No gear. No rope, no headlamp, no harness—the knife didn't count, a knife is not equipment, a knife is a personality trait. A tide that had gone out impossibly far and could, by any law worth respecting, come back in impossibly fast, and me standing on the menu instead of the balcony. A town that looked at a cave and practiced not seeing it. A harbor trust with brass on its door and, I'd have bet the eleven-forty, something to hide. Every single line of the risk assessment said: *bus, sleep, morning, gone.*

And underneath the argument, quiet as water under a floor, the other voice. The one that said: *the charts are wrong. The charts are wrong and you're the only one in this crowd who can read them well enough to know it. The sea just opened a door that isn't supposed to exist, and it opened it in front of you, specifically, at three in the morning, in the one town you stopped in because you'd never heard of it.*

The tide owed nothing. That was the thought that finally did it, standing there with the smell of the deep earth in my nose. The tide had come in and gone out for ten thousand years without asking Rivergate's permission, and tonight it had gone out further than the tables said it should, and it had shown a door, and it didn't owe the Trust an explanation, and it didn't owe me one either. But it had shown me. Of everyone on that rail, it had shown *me*.

I've been punished for reading terrain before. I know how that story goes. I wrote the last one.

"Going to look?" Sefa asked. She wasn't looking at me. Two cuts, a twist, a scoop, flick, on a fish that wasn't there.

"No," I said.

We stood there another ten minutes while the pools on the flats went silver and still, and somewhere out in the dark the water paused at the far edge of its retreat—the whole sea, held, like a breath—and I swear on the last honest thing in me that in that pause, the whole bay seemed to *wait*. Not the town. The bay. The way a room waits when someone is about to say the thing.

Then the water turned. I felt it before I saw it, a change in the air pressure, a sound under sound, and the first thin tongue of it came sliding back across the flats, quick and businesslike, erasing the crab's commute, erasing the ripples, erasing the evidence, and the crowd made a low sound like a congregation sitting down.

The cave mouth began to fill. The bright threads of weeping water thickened, merged, became a sheet, became a curtain, and the door that wasn't on any chart closed itself with the patience of something that had done this ten thousand times before and would do it ten thousand more, whether anyone watched or not.

"Shift's back on in five," Sefa said, and walked inside.

I didn't go in. I stood at the rail until the bay was a bay again, lying to me with its smooth moonlit surface, and I thought about the eleven-forty and the bus at seven-fifteen and the technique, my good technique, the not-asking of second questions.

Then I thought about the way the whole town had practiced not looking, and how a town only learns not to look at something if someone taught it, and who teaches a whole town not to look, and what does it cost, and who pays.

I took out the knife and looked at it. It wasn't equipment. But it was a start.

The shift bell rang. I went in and gutted fish until dawn with the taste of deep cold air still in my throat, and I told myself the whole way through that in the morning I would be on that bus, because I am, historically, a woman who leaves.

The bus left at seven-fifteen.

I was not on it.

At first light, with the harbor gray and innocent and the lighthouse standing over its secret like a hired witness, I walked down the boat ramp with my knife in my pocket and the tide tables in my head, and I did the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do all night.

Low tide tomorrow: six-forty-one in the morning.

I had until then to decide what kind of woman I was going to be.

The sea, patient as a debt, waited to find out.
