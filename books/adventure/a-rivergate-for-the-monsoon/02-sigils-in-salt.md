## 2. Sigils in Salt

I told myself I wasn't going back.

This is a thing I tell myself often, and it has roughly the same success rate as telling myself I'll quit smoking, learn Portuguese, or stop taking jobs from men with soft hands and hard ledgers. The self is a client who never listens to her guide.

I'd gone back to the fish house at four, worked the shift, and walked out at nine with salt crusted into every crease of my knuckles and eleven dollars folded into my boot. The bay was still empty. That was the thing. Tides come back. This one hadn't. Nine hours after the water had walked out of Rivergate like a tenant skipping rent, the harbor floor lay there under the gray morning light, gleaming, exposed, patient. Boats sat heeled over on their mooring lines like drunks leaning on each other outside a chapel. Gulls walked where they should have swum, offended by the arrangement.

And beneath the old lighthouse, the cave mouth stood open.

In daylight it looked less like a wound than a doorway, which was somehow worse. Wounds close. Doorways invite. It was maybe four meters across, framed in black stone that the tide had spent—what, centuries?—polishing without ever being allowed inside. The rock around it was dry in a way rock under a lighthouse has no business being dry. Water had touched everything on that shore except the two meters directly around the entrance, as if the sea had signed some agreement it intended to honor.

I stood at the top of the harbor stairs with my hands in my pockets and conducted the argument I always conduct.

*You're nobody here. Nobody knows your face. In three days you can be on a bus south, and in three weeks you can be somewhere the word "Voss" means nothing, which is everywhere except about forty people in Kathmandu and Santiago and one very persistent journalist in Denver.*

*It's a cave,* said the other voice, the one that got me blacklisted in the first place. *Caves are just mountains with their mouths open.*

*I know what happened last time you listened to that voice.*

*Yes. Don't you want to know why?*

That's the dirty trick grief plays. It doesn't argue. It just asks questions it knows you can't answer.

I went down the stairs.

* * *

The harbor floor was a lesson in what a town chooses not to see. Bicycle frames furred with weed. A shopping trolley standing upright, dignified, like a monument to something. Anchor chains, crab pots, a child's boot, an engine block, and everywhere the pale litter of a century: bottles, buttons, coins green as pond scum. Towns pour their secrets into water because water keeps them. Somebody in Rivergate's history had counted on this floor staying drowned.

The cave swallowed the light about ten meters in. I had a headlamp in my pack—the one piece of professional equipment I hadn't sold—and I clicked it on and stepped through the doorway, and the temperature dropped ten degrees in a single stride, the way it does when you cross from weather into geology.

Inside was a throat of black basalt, descending in a series of shallow steps worn smooth. Not smooth by water. Smooth by feet. I crouched and put my palm on the stone and felt the polish, that glassy give under the skin that only comes from thousands of years of soles. Caves carved by tide don't get foot-polished floors. Tide caves get chiseled walls, barnacled ceilings, chaos. This was architecture. Someone had been coming here, regularly, for longer than Rivergate had been a town.

"Okay," I said aloud, because saying things aloud in empty places is how I keep the other voice company. "Okay."

The passage turned twice and opened into a chamber the size of a chapel, and my headlamp found the ceiling first—a dome ribbed with flowstone, dry, hung with mineral curtains the color of old ivory—and then the walls, and then I stopped walking entirely.

Marks. Cut into the rock, chest height, in lines and clusters, dozens of them, receding into the dark beyond my beam.

I know survey marks the way a priest knows scripture. For six years they were my alphabet. Cut marks, paint marks, scratched codes on boulders and bridge abutments and the flanks of unnamed peaks: route confirmed, load station, anchor tested, water here, danger past this point, camp, cache, *turn back*. Every expedition leaves them, and every serious expedition encodes them, because routes are money and money attracts idiots. My old team used a cipher we built ourselves over one long drunk winter in a Pokhara guesthouse—hash marks keyed to rope lengths, chevrons keyed to dates, a little hooked symbol we called the fishhook that meant *safe water, drinkable, tested*.

There was a fishhook on the wall of a sea cave under a lighthouse in a town whose name I'd learned four days ago.

I stood there while the cold came up through my boots and did the arithmetic I didn't want to do. The cipher wasn't published. It wasn't taught. It existed in the heads of five people, and two of them were dead—one in the disaster, one after it, though the coroner called that one an accident too and I have learned to stop arguing with coroners. Which left three. Me, and two others who had no reason to be on this coast, unless one of them had been, years ago, cutting marks into rock that shouldn't have known their language.

My hand rose toward the nearest cluster before I'd decided anything. Old habit. Read the wall like a page.

A vertical line with three cross-ticks: *surveyed, third pass.* A chevron pointing down-slope: *descent route, stable.* The fishhook beside a seep line in the rock where, when I held my fingers to it, actual cold moisture beaded—fresh water, in a cave below sea level, which is a sentence that should not parse. And above all of it, larger, deeper-cut than the rest, worn nearly smooth with age:

A river.

Not a picture of one. A sigil. A single continuous line that doubled back on itself twice and then ran straight, the way cartographers draw living water on old charts—as opposed to dead water, which gets drawn straight all the way through. I'd seen that convention once, in a folio of seventeenth-century hydrography a collector in La Paz had shown me, back when I still took rich people's money to look at beautiful things. Living rivers were drawn with returns. The doubling-back meant the water moved, decided, breathed.

Someone had cut a living river into this wall and then, below it, a string of marks I read slowly, twice, because the first reading made no sense:

*Source upstream. Source sealed. Do not open.*

Sealed by whom? Sealed how? You don't seal a source. A source isn't a door. Unless—

Unless someone had made it one.

I took off my pack and sat down on the foot-polished floor with my back against the basalt, which is not what a careful person does in an unknown cave, and I looked at the sigil until my lamp started to dim, and I thought about nothing, which is to say I thought about everything, which is to say I thought about a rope in a storm and a man named Anselm Vey shouting into wind that ate his voice, and I made myself stop.

Here is what I did not do. I did not take photographs. Some instinct older than sense told me that images travel—that a phone picture becomes a message becomes a post becomes a question asked by the wrong person in a bar—and whatever these marks were, whoever had cut them had wanted them secret for a long time, and I had spent enough of my life being the wrong person in bars. I photographed nothing. I memorized instead, tracing each cluster with my eyes the way I used to trace routes onto mental maps before dawn starts, and if memory is less reliable than film, it is also less findable.

Then I sat in the dark for a while with the lamp off, because turning it off felt honest. The dark in that chamber was total in a way surface dark never is—no ambient glow, no horizon, just pressure—and underneath the silence I could hear it. Faint. Rhythmic. Not dripping. Something slower. A swell and release, swell and release, like breath drawn through stone.

The elders of nowhere in particular would call that imagination. I've stood inside glaciers and heard them breathe. Rock does that, when water moves through it. But water moving through rock under a drained bay, in time with nothing—

"Get out," I told myself quietly. "Get out, get paid, get gone."

I turned the lamp back on and stood up, and that is when the pebbles skittered behind me.

* * *

Small sound. Scuff and rattle, ten meters back toward the entrance passage, the unmistakable noise of a body trying very hard to be silent and failing at the exact speed of guilt. I killed the lamp and stood still and let the dark have me, and counted.

Five seconds. Six. Then a whisper, thin and young, addressed to himself with the desperate sincerity of a boy rehearsing an alibi: "It's fine. It's fine. She can't see in the dark either."

"I can, actually," I said, and clicked the lamp on.

He froze mid-crouch like a cat caught on a kitchen counter—a kid, twelve maybe thirteen, all elbows and hollows, dark hair cut badly by someone who loved him but couldn't see, wearing rubber boots two sizes too big and a fisherman's oilskin that had been cut down from an adult's and still fit him like a tent. His eyes were enormous and unrepentant. That was the detail I noted professionally: most caught trespassers look guilty. This one looked annoyed at being caught, as if the fault lay in my hearing rather than his stealth.

"You followed me," I said.

"No." He considered. "I followed the tide. You happened to be going the same direction."

"And then into the cave."

"The cave was also the same direction."

I looked at him. He looked at me with the flat frankness of children and gulls, and I recognized the type immediately, because I had been the type: the kid who reads maps for fun, who knows the town better than the town knows itself, who treats locked doors as personal invitations. Half the best guides I ever worked with started exactly there, and half the corpses I've had to write reports about started there too.

"What's your name?"

"Ilya." No hesitation, no fear. Then, because honesty apparently had a budget and he'd overspent: "Everyone calls me Ilya."

"Who's everyone?"

"The pier. The fish house. I gut sometimes, mornings." He nodded at my hands, at the salt still in the creases. "You're new. You hold the knife wrong but fast. Marta says you'll lose a thumb by Thursday."

"Marta may be right." I kept the lamp steady on him and watched his gaze slide past me, up the wall, to the marks. Watched him see them. Here was the strange part: he didn't gasp, didn't go wide-eyed with discovery. He looked at the sigils the way you look at a relative at a funeral—recognition mixed with dread, the face of someone confirming a thing he'd hoped was rumor.

"You knew they were here," I said.

"I knew they were *somewhere*." He came closer, boots whispering on the polished floor, and stopped a respectful distance from the wall like it might wake. "Odell's papers. The cartographer? His daughter copies them. I've seen her book." He said it lightly, tossed off, but I caught the weight underneath: *his daughter*, said with the particular care adolescents use for girls they are not ready to say anything else about. "She thinks they're a map. She thinks—" He stopped himself, jaw working, suddenly aware he'd said more than the budget allowed. "Anyway. Everyone says the cave's dangerous. Corvane Dall said on the radio it's unstable and going to be sealed. So."

"So you broke into the cave that's about to be sealed."

"Somebody should see it before they do." He shrugged, and the shrug was the whole boy: defiance stretched thin over something that was almost grief. "They seal everything."

I should have sent him out. That was the correct move—walk him to the entrance, deliver him to the harbor stairs, return to anonymity, catch the bus south, add one more town to the list of places where I was nobody. Instead I heard myself ask, "How long have the marks been here? Do the old ones talk about the cave?"

And Ilya looked at me with those enormous unrepentant eyes and said, "What marks? There aren't any marks. Odell's papers are sealed, the cave's getting sealed, and if anyone asks, I followed the tide and you happened to be going the same direction."

Which is when I understood that the boy hadn't followed me at all. He'd been watching the cave, waiting for someone foolish enough to enter it, and I had simply been the first fool the tide produced. He wanted a witness. He wanted an adult—any adult—who would look at the wall and confirm that the thing his friend believed in her notebook was real.

I had come here to remain invisible. Standing in a smuggled lamplight in a forbidden cave, holding a cipher only three living people could read, having already been seen by a boy who now knew my face, my knife habits, and my name-adjacent presence at Marta's fish house—invisibility was going about as well as my other resolutions.

"There's water seeping through that wall," I said, pointing. "Fresh. Below sea level. That shouldn't be possible."

"It's possible," Ilya said simply. "Mara says the whole valley sits on it. Like a lung." He glanced at me sideways, testing. "She says it breathes."

I said nothing. Behind us, faint and rhythmic, the stone swelled and released. Swelled and released. The boy heard it too—I saw it land in him, saw his shoulders drop half an inch, the way a person relaxes when a stranger confirms the ghost they've been pretending not to believe in.

"We should go," I said. "Before the Trust seals it with us inside."

We climbed back through the throat of the cave and out into the flat gray morning, and the bay was still empty, still gleaming, still owed. On the harbor stairs Ilya peeled away without goodbye, rubber boots slapping, and paused once to look back—not at me, at the cave mouth, the way you check on a sleeping animal you're not sure is friendly.

I stood there a long moment, doing final arithmetic. Eleven dollars in my boot. A fee waiting from a magistrate I didn't trust. Marks on a wall in a dead man's cipher. A girl with a notebook copying sealed papers. A source, sealed, upstream of every map.

I had photographed nothing and told no one. Both resolutions intact, technically.

But the boy had seen my face, and the tide still hadn't come home, and when I climbed the stairs toward the fish house and the smell of brine and money I didn't yet have, a black car was idling at the top with its engine running quiet and its passenger window already rolling down.
