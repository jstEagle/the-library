## 3. The Harbor Trust

The summons came on the third morning, delivered by a boy with a fish scale stuck to his cheekbone and an envelope so heavy with good paper it felt like a bribe before I'd even opened it.

*The Harbor Trust requests the pleasure of your attendance at eleven o'clock. Corvane Dall, Magistrate.*

*Requests the pleasure.* Nobody in Rivergate had requested anything of me yet except more speed with the gutting knife and less opinion about the smell. I turned the envelope over. Wax seal, harbor wheel pressed into green wax, the kind of formality that costs money and means someone has been asking questions about you.

I knew what that meant. I'd spent six years being the answer to other people's questions.

"You're the one who went into the cave," the boy said. Not a question either.

"I walked on a beach," I said. "The cave was already there."

"Rosa saw you come out at dawn." He said it the way people say *the priest saw you at confession* — flat, factual, already filed somewhere permanent. Rivergate was a small town pretending to be a port. Everything traveled by mouth faster than any boat could carry it.

"Then Rosa should apply for a job with the Trust," I said. "They like witnesses."

He didn't laugh. He took a coin from his pocket, looked at it, and put it back, which told me he'd been paid to deliver the message and possibly to observe my reaction, and that he was honest enough to be bad at both. Then he left, and I stood in the doorway of my rented room — four walls, a bed that complained, a window with a view of a wall — and thought about how fast invisibility dies in a place like this.

Three days. That's all it had taken. Three days of keeping my head down, gutting hake for Harl at the cannery dock, sleeping like a stone because exhaustion is the only sleep I trust, and already the town had decided I was a story worth telling. *The stranger who went into the cave under the lighthouse.*

Here's what nobody tells you about disgrace: it doesn't make you careful. It makes you hungry in ways you can't admit, and hunger reads faces. I'd seen the tide go out that night like the sea changing its mind, seen the cave mouth open beneath the lighthouse like something exhaling after a long held breath, and every instinct I owned — the ones that kept me alive through two decades of mountains and the ones that got me blacklisted from two countries — had said the same thing: *walk away, Voss. You're good at walking away.*

I hadn't walked away. I'd gone in at dawn with a headlamp and no witness, which was either curiosity or self-destruction wearing curiosity's coat, and inside I'd found survey marks cut into the salt-crusted rock in a cipher I knew from another life. A cipher from an expedition that ended with a rope, a storm, and a name that people now said the way you say a disease.

So when the Harbor Trust requested the pleasure of my company, I understood exactly what was happening. The only question was whether I'd pretend otherwise.

* * *

The Trust house sat at the head of the harbor like a fat man at the head of a table: granite, three stories, windows tall enough to be arrogant. Inside smelled of lamp oil and old money and, underneath it, faintly, of brine — the whole building seemed to be quietly rotting from the water side up, which struck me as honest in a way nothing else about it would turn out to be.

Corvane Dall received me in a room with charts on the walls. Real ones, hand-drawn, some of them older than the town's church. He was perhaps sixty, built like a mooring post, with the kind of stillness that comes from never having needed to hurry because the world hurries toward men like him. His hands were folded on the desk. His eyes did the work of a full crew.

"Petra Voss," he said, and let the name sit there between us like a fish on a slab.

"Magistrate."

"You know who I am."

"Everyone knows who everyone is here. It's the town's only industry besides fish."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not a smile. An acknowledgment that I'd scored, filed away for later. "You were seen entering the cave beneath the old light three nights ago. You emerged at dawn. You have not spoken of it to anyone."

"Have you checked with Rosa? She seems to run a better intelligence service than you do."

This time the stillness changed quality. I'd been around powerful men my whole working life — expedition funders, ministry officials, the kind of people who sign things that other people die under — and they all share one trait: they do not enjoy being reminded that the world contains anyone they don't own. Dall recovered fast, though. Credit where due.

"The cave," he said, "is a hazard. The tide event that exposed it was anomalous. The structure beneath the lighthouse foundation is compromised. The Trust intends to seal it permanently, for public safety."

"Then seal it. You don't need me. You've got dynamite and a harbor full of masons."

"We need verification." He slid a paper across the desk. "A professional assessment. Structural instability, documented, signed, witnessed. Something we can show the inquiry board if anyone asks why a landmark was destroyed."

"And why would anyone ask?"

"Because there are people in this valley who believe the cave is something other than a hole in the rock." He said it lightly, but his eyes didn't. "Superstition. Old families, old stories. The Trust has tolerated them for years because tolerance is cheaper than conflict. But now the sea itself has made a spectacle of the thing, and spectacle breeds belief, and belief breeds obstruction. I want this handled cleanly. Verified. Closed."

I looked at the paper. The figure at the bottom had a number of zeros that made my stomach clench with a shame I recognized immediately and hated anyway. Six months of wages. A year, maybe, at cannery rates. Enough to leave Rivergate. Enough to eat somewhere better than sardines and pride.

"And my qualifications?" I asked. "You know who I am. You said my name like you were reading a charge sheet. What exactly does the Trust think it's buying?"

"A mountain guide," Dall said, "who understands unstable ground. Who has, I'm told, extensive experience assessing terrain that wants to kill people." A pause, precise as a chisel tap. "And who is currently unaffiliated with any institution, guild, or country that might complicate her testimony."

There it was. He'd done his research. He knew about Nepal. He knew about Chile. He knew that a woman with my record would sign almost anything, because a signature from me was worthless everywhere else and therefore cheap here, and because I was broke, and because broke people with ruined names make ideal instruments: deniable, disposable, grateful.

I want to tell you I stood up and walked out. I want to tell you I told him what I'd found in that cave — the cipher marks, the ones that shouldn't exist on this coast, the ones that pointed upstream like a sentence pointing at its own beginning — and watched his face do something human.

What I did was ask, "Half now?"

Dall inclined his head, and I swear there was something like respect in it, or its taxidermied equivalent. "Half now. Half on delivery of the report."

* * *

I walked back along the harbor wall with the advance in my pocket — actual notes, not a transfer, which meant he wanted it untraceable too, which meant *he* was being careful in ways that confirmed everything my stomach was telling me — and I conducted the argument with myself that I'd been conducting, in one form or another, since the rope came down in the storm six years ago.

*It's just a signature,* said the pragmatist, who sounds a lot like me and lives rent-free in my skull. *The cave probably is unstable. Half the coast is unstable. You'll write an honest assessment, and if it happens to conclude what he wants, well — geology doesn't take sides.*

*You haven't been inside long enough to assess anything,* said the other voice, quieter, the one that had kept me alive on ice at four in the morning when the pragmatist was asleep. *You signed off on a route once because the funder wanted it signed. You know how this ends. You've lived how this ends.*

That was the thing I couldn't say out loud, then or now, the thing at the center of the disaster like a stone at the center of a fruit: expeditions don't fail because mountains are hard. Mountains are always hard; that's their whole personality. Expeditions fail because somewhere above the last honest camp, someone with clean boots and a ledger decides that the margin for error is a cost line. And the person on the rope — the guide, the one whose judgment gets written into the report afterward — learns that her name is cheaper than the equipment that failed.

I had learned that lesson at the price of two lives and my entire world, and here I was, six years later, about to sell my judgment again to a man with a ledger, for half up front.

The difference, I told myself, was that this time nobody would die. This time it was just a cave and a piece of paper. This time I'd look at the rock, write what the rock actually said, and if the rock happened to agree with Corvane Dall, that would be the rock's business, not mine.

The pragmatist nodded approvingly. The quiet voice said nothing at all, which was worse.

* * *

I spent the afternoon doing what I'd been paid to do, or the honest version of it, which is more than Dall bargained for. I went back to the cave at low tide — the tide had been behaving normally since that first night, retreating a few meters instead of a few hundred, sulking, as if embarrassed by its earlier honesty — and I climbed down into the mouth with a hammer, a chalk line, and the professional detachment I was renting out.

The rock was real. Basalt and old sedimentary layering, wet-slick near the entrance, drier deeper in where the salt had crusted white. I tested it the way I'd test a serac face: soundings with the hammer, listening for the hollow note of delamination, watching for seep lines. And the truth — the inconvenient, complicated truth — was that the cave was *mostly* sound. Solid arch overhead, good drainage channels, no fresh collapse debris. There was one section, maybe thirty meters in, where a fracture ran diagonal through the ceiling and grit trickled down it when the swell pushed air through the chamber, and that section was genuinely dangerous, and I marked it honestly with chalk.

But the rest of it was fine. Better than fine. And the rest of it held the marks.

I'd photographed nothing that first dawn — an old habit; cameras create evidence, and evidence has a way of testifying against the wrong people — but I'd memorized them, because memory is the only vault I've ever trusted. Survey marks, cut clean and deliberate, in a notation system used by high-altitude mapping expeditions in the Karakoram twenty years ago. A system I knew because I'd been trained in it, because I'd *used* it, because the man who taught it to me had died holding a rope I'd tied.

Those marks were not decoration. They were a sequence. Waypoints. And they ran deeper into the cave, and the last one I could read pointed not seaward but inland — upstream, if you thought of the seep water running past your boots as a river thinking its way home.

A river that appears on no map, marked in a mountaineer's cipher, on a coast where no such expedition had ever officially been.

I sat on a dry ledge in the dark with my hammer across my knees and did the arithmetic that guides do. Option one: write the report Dall wanted. Seal the cave. Take the money. Leave town before the ink dried, and spend the rest of my life adding this to the list of small betrayals I don't talk about, a list that already had weight enough to bend a spine.

Option two: write the honest report. Lose the second half of the fee. Stay broke. And put myself directly in the path of a magistrate who had chosen me precisely because I was disposable, and who would not enjoy discovering I wasn't for sale after all — or worse, that I was for sale and had sold myself to him already, and could therefore be destroyed with my own signature.

Option three, which I didn't let myself think about, involved the marks, and upstream, and whatever it was that a river does when it holds its breath.

I finished the sounding survey. I noted the fracture. I wrote, in my notebook, in pencil, in shorthand only I could read: *Cave stable except NE section. Marks genuine. Sequence deliberate. Someone surveyed this who knew what they were looking for.*

Then I closed the notebook, and I thought about the zeros on Dall's paper, and about sardines, and about how a person can know she's walking into a trap and walk in anyway, and call it pragmatism, and almost believe it.

* * *

Harl paid me off at the cannery that evening — not angrily; he was the rare employer who considered a resignation a weather event, regrettable and impersonal — and I ate my last free meal at the dock canteen among men who talked about the tide. The impossible tide. It had become a story overnight, growing in the telling: the sea had pulled back to the horizon, some said; the sea had shown the bones of a drowned village, said others; the sea had opened a door, said the ones who'd seen me come out of it, and they said it while looking at me, and I ate my stew and gave them nothing, because giving them something was how you became a story permanently.

Afterward I walked the harbor wall in the dark. The lighthouse stood dead above me — decommissioned forty years, Dall's paperwork had mentioned in passing, though the Trust maintained the foundation — and below it, behind the normal modest tide line, the cave mouth waited, patient as a held breath.

Somewhere upstream, in the dark hills I couldn't see, water was moving. Water was always moving. That's the first thing they teach you and the last thing you forget: the river doesn't care about your ledgers. It goes where it goes, and it remembers every channel it's ever cut, and if you dam it or drain it or sell it, it doesn't argue. It waits. And then one night the tide goes out farther than it has any right to, and a door opens, and the waiting is over.

I had taken Dall's money. That was done, and I wouldn't insult us both by pretending I'd taken it to investigate from the inside. I'd taken it because I was broke and tired and my name was worth nothing anywhere else, and because half a year of wages buys a lot of silence from your own conscience.

But I had also written *marks genuine* in my notebook, and I knew myself well enough to know that the report I'd eventually hand over depended entirely on what I found upstream of that last waypoint — and that Corvane Dall had made a serious error, the same error funders always make.

He'd assumed the person on the rope was broken.

Broken, yes. Sold, temporarily. Finished — no. Because standing there under the dead lighthouse with stolen salt wind coming out of the dark, I felt something I hadn't felt in six years, and it wasn't conscience and it wasn't courage and it certainly wasn't gratitude.

It was the old, cold, waking joy of a route opening ahead of me.

In the morning I would start drafting his report. In the meantime, I had a cave to go back to, and marks to copy from memory onto paper, and a sequence to try to finish reading.

I didn't notice the boy following me until the third night. By then it didn't matter.
