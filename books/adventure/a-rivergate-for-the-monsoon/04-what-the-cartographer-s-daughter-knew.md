## 4. What the Cartographer's Daughter Knew

My father keeps his secrets in a locked drawer the way other people keep their sins in locked drawers—badly, and with a key he thinks nobody knows about.

The key lives in the third teacup from the left on the shelf above the stove. It has lived there for eleven years, which is how long I've known about it, which is how long I've understood that adults are mostly just children who have learned to file things. I was five when I found it. I didn't take it then. I waited until I was fourteen, because I wanted to be old enough to understand whatever I found, and because at fourteen I had already learned that waiting is its own kind of theft—the slow kind, the kind that leaves no broken locks behind.

The drawer held papers. Of course it did; my father is Odell Vane, cartographer, the man who has drawn every chart of Rivergate Bay for thirty years, the man whose maps hang in the harbor trust's offices with little brass plaques under them saying *ODDELL VANE* in letters so confident you'd think he'd never once been wrong about anything. The papers were older than the maps, though. Survey sheets. Field notes in a cramped hand that wasn't quite his. And across them, over them, threaded through them like veins through a wrist: symbols.

Not letters. Not numbers. Marks—a spiral with a gap in it, a chevron stacked three high, a line that bent twice before it committed to going anywhere. They repeated. They *meant* something. You could tell they meant something the way you can tell a stranger is crying in another room—not by seeing it, but by the shape of the silence around it.

I copied them. All of them. For months. Into a notebook I kept inside the false spine of a book about lichen, because nobody has ever opened a book about lichen voluntarily, and if they did, they deserved to find what they found.

Here is what I believed, and believe still, though belief has gotten heavier lately, like a coat soaked through: those marks were a map's confession. My father draws coastlines for a living, but every coastline is a lie of convenience—it says *here the land ends*, when really the land just changes its mind about being land. The sigils said something underneath all that. They said there was water feeding this bay from somewhere no chart admitted to. A source. A true one. The kind of thing you'd seal up in a drawer and lock with a key in a teacup, if you'd spent thirty years drawing maps that left it out.

I know how this sounds. Sixteen years old, girl with a notebook full of squiggles, certain she's found the secret of the valley while the grown men argue about pilings and dredging permits. Ilya told me I sounded like a prophet or a lunatic, and I said those were historically the same job description, and he laughed, and then he helped me copy the chevrons anyway, because Ilya is loyal in the way of boys who haven't yet learned that loyalty is expensive.

Then the tide went out and didn't come back on schedule, and the bay lay there like a gutted fish, and beneath the old lighthouse there was a cave mouth where no cave mouth should be—and I stopped sounding like anything, because I was too busy standing on the seawall with my mouth open like everyone else.

* * *

I want to be fair to myself here, since fairness is in short supply elsewhere in this story: I did not go into the cave first. I wanted to. God, I wanted to. But the tide was still out and the mudflats were sucking at everything, and my father had that look he gets—the one that means *if you die doing something stupid I will have to feel things in public*—so I stayed on the wall with the whole town and watched a stranger pick her way down instead.

She wasn't from Rivergate. That much was obvious from fifty yards. She walked like the ground owed her money. She had the look of someone who'd slept badly in places that charge by the hour, and her hands—when she pushed her hair back—I noticed her hands, rope-scarred across both palms, the calluses sitting wrong for a fish-gutter, right for someone who'd spent years holding lines in weather.

She stood at the cave mouth a long time. Not gawking like the crowd. Reading it. Her head moved in small increments, left to right, the way mine moves when I'm working through my father's field notes.

And then she went in, and the crowd lost interest the way crowds do, and I did not lose interest, because I am constitutionally incapable of it.

* * *

I followed her at dawn. This was not brave. I want that on the record. Bravery would have been knocking on her door and asking questions like a person. What I did was sneak down onto the exposed flats at first light, cold enough that my breath came out in flags, and slip into a sea cave alone, which is the kind of thing my father would call "a decision" in that flat voice he uses, and my mother would have called something shorter and louder.

Inside, the sigils were everywhere.

That's the part I couldn't have prepared for. In the drawer, they were ink, arguable, deniable. Here they were cut into stone, salt-rimed, old—older than the trust, older than the lighthouse, maybe older than the town pretending not to be a town. The spiral-with-the-gap sat over the entrance like a signature. The triple chevron ran along the wall in a descending sequence, and beside each one, smaller marks I hadn't seen in the papers: tally strokes, corrections, the handwriting of people who came back and checked their own work.

I had my notebook out. I was copying. I was so absorbed that I made the classic error of the obsessed, which is forgetting that caves have two mouths' worth of acoustics, and that's why I heard her before I saw her.

"You're copying them."

She was ten feet away, backlit by the grey mouth of the cave, and I nearly put my pencil through my own palm. Up close she looked worse and better than she had from the seawall—worse around the eyes, better in the posture. She stood like the floor might try something.

"They're on my father's papers," I said, which was true, and also the fastest way to establish I wasn't just a vandal with good taste.

"Whose father?"

"Odell Vane."

Something crossed her face. I've thought about that something a lot since. At the time I read it as nothing—strangers don't react to local names—but I've since learned to read her face the way I read charts, which is to say assuming the interesting information is in what's been deliberately left blank.

"He's the harbor cartographer," she said.

"The harbor cartographer," I agreed. "Who owns survey marks in a cipher that doesn't appear on any map of this coast, cut into rock that's been underwater since before the trust existed." I held up my notebook. "I've been transcribing these for six months. Do you know what they are?"

"No," she said.

And here is the thing—I believed her, in that moment. That's the detail that humiliates me most. She said *no* with such flat finality, such practiced boredom, that I actually believed her, and I pride myself on being able to smell a lie the way Ilya smells herring. But she was good. She was very, very good. She looked at the sigils the way you look at graffiti in a language you don't speak, and she turned away from them, and she asked me how deep the tide came into this cave, and whether anyone knew I was here, and she walked me out with a hand hovering near my elbow like she was escorting me away from evidence rather than toward safety.

At the cave mouth she said, "Don't come back here alone," and it sounded almost like concern, and I went home glowing, because a stranger with rope-scarred hands had worried about me, and I was sixteen, and praise from strangers weighed more than sense.

It took me two days to work out what had bothered me about the whole exchange. Two days, and then I was lying awake at one in the morning when it arrived whole, the way the important things do, uninvited:

She had said *no*, she didn't know what the marks were. But when I'd first spoken—before she answered—her eyes had already gone to the spiral over the entrance. Not searching for it. Going to it. The way your eyes go to your own name on a list.

You only find a symbol that fast if you already know where it lives.

* * *

Two days after that, the stranger reported the cave as dangerous.

I heard it secondhand, then thirdhand, then firsthand from my father, who came home with his jaw set in the particular configuration it takes when he's been in a room with Corvane Dall. The Trust had hired her—paid her, paid her well—to verify the cave was unstable. Unstable. Structurally unsound. A hazard to public safety, best sealed forthwith, concrete and rebar and a plaque maybe, *BY ORDER OF THE RIVERGATE HARBOR TRUST*, brass letters, very confident.

"She gave a professional assessment," my father said, not looking at me. He never looks at me when he lies; he looks at the middle distance, where his maps live. "Rockfall risk. She'd know. She's a mountain guide."

"Was," I said. "What's her name?"

"Voss. Petra Voss." He said it carefully, the way you set down something that might be hot.

The name meant nothing to me then. It means a great deal to me now, but that's later, and later is not where I'm telling this from. Then, it was just a name attached to a face attached to a lie, and I sat with my lichen book open on my knees and felt something in my chest go hard and bright and stupid.

Because here's what I knew that the Trust didn't know I knew, that my father pretended not to know, that maybe even Petra Voss didn't know I knew: I had watched her read that cave. I had stood four feet behind her in the dark and watched her head move through those marks like a finger down a familiar page. Nobody walks a wall of unknown symbols in sequence. Nobody finds the spiral without hunting for it. She knew them. She knew them the way I knew them—from somewhere, from some other place, some other life—and she had stood in front of the only physical proof that my father's locked papers pointed at something real, and she had said *unstable*, and taken the money, and now the machines were coming.

People lie for lots of reasons. I'm sixteen, not naive; I've lied about where I was and who with and what happened to the good scissors. Most lies are small and load-bearing, holding up ordinary lives. But there's a category of lie that isn't about hiding something small—it's about burying something enormous, and you can always tell, because the liar doesn't just deny the thing, they build over it. Concrete. Rebar. A plaque.

Corvane Dall builds over things professionally. I've watched him do it my whole life—the way the trust's maps stop at certain inlets, the way certain questions about water levels get answered with other questions. I used to think my father's sealed drawer was shame. Now I wondered if it was instructions.

And Petra Voss—Petra Voss was either Dall's newest tool or the most interesting person ever to walk into this town, and either way, she had looked at the truth of the river and sold it for a fee.

I copied the last of the chevron sequences that night. Then I wrote, at the top of a fresh page, in letters as confident as any brass plaque: *SHE'S LYING.*

Underneath it, because I am my father's daughter and precision is the family disease: *But she read them left to right. Whoever taught her is upstream.*

Ilya says I should leave it alone. Ilya says the Trust has lawyers and boats and a magistrate with a voice like wet gravel, and I have a notebook and a book about lichen. Ilya is probably right. Ilya is usually right, which is why I don't tell him things anymore until after I've done them.

Because here's what I keep coming back to, turning over like a stone on the flats: the sigils descend. The chevrons step downward, westward, inland—upstream, if you follow the water backward instead of forward. Every mark in that cave is an arrow pointing away from the sea, toward the gorge, toward the country above every map my father ever drew. The cave isn't the secret. The cave is the *doorway* to the secret, and they're going to pour concrete in the doorway, and whatever's upstream will stay buried, breathing, unheard, forever—or until somebody follows the marks the direction they're pointing.

My father locks his drawer. The Trust locks its ledgers. Petra Voss locks her face.

I have a notebook, a pencil, a pair of boots, and the entire summer ahead of me.

I started packing the night I wrote *upstream* in my book about lichen. Just quietly. A knife, matches, the notebook, my father's oldest field notes copied out in my own hand in case the originals grew legs. I told myself I'd decide later whether I was actually going. I told myself a lot of things that week. That's the trouble with being sixteen and certain—you have to keep talking yourself into your own courage, and some mornings the talk doesn't take.

It took on Thursday. On Thursday I watched, from the seawall, a supply boat deliver crates marked TRUST WORKS to the lighthouse path, and I watched Petra Voss stand on the flats below the cave with a clipboard she never wrote on, looking at the cave mouth with an expression I recognized because I see it in my mirror: the expression of someone measuring the distance between what they said and what they know, and finding it wider than they'd budgeted for.

She was lying. I was sure of it the way I'm sure of north.

So on Friday morning, before the tide turned, I left a note on my father's drafting table—*Gone to Senn, back Sunday, don't worry, I have the good boots*—which was half a lie, because I wasn't going to Senn, and I wasn't coming back Sunday, and the good boots were the only true sentence in the whole arrangement.

I went upstream, following the chevrons, following the water backward.

I did not know, then, that I was following a woman's ruin upstream too—that the marks on the walls and the disaster in her past were the same river. I didn't know about the expedition, or the rope, or the storm, or the funder who cut corners and called it weather. I didn't know my father's name was written anywhere in that story.

I knew exactly one thing, standing at the head of the gorge trail with the fog coming down like a held breath: somebody had to follow the marks before the concrete dried.

It might as well be the girl nobody bothers to lock the drawer against.
