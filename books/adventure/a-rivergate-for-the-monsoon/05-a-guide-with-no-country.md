## 5. A Guide with No Country

The knock came at six in the morning, which in Rivergate is a civilized hour only if you haven't been up since four gutting herring. My hands smelled like the inside of a fish's bad decisions, and I was on my second cup of the harbor trust's cheapest coffee when the door of my rented room—rented by the week, paid in coins counted twice—opened without so much as a courtesy creak.

I hadn't locked it. Old habit from places where locks were either useless or an invitation. Also, I owned nothing worth stealing except a knife and a pair of boots, and anyone desperate enough to take those would need them more than me.

The man in the doorway was sixty-odd, built like a piece of driftwood that had decided to keep standing out of spite. Salt-and-pepper beard trimmed short, ink stains on his fingers in the way that never washes out of people who draw for a living. He held his hat against his chest like he'd rehearsed the gesture.

"Petra Voss," he said.

Not a question. That was the first wrong thing. The second wrong thing was that he didn't look afraid of me, and everyone who recognized my name eventually looked afraid of me—the way you're afraid of a dog that bit someone, even if you weren't the one bitten.

"You've got the advantage," I said.

"Odell. The cartographer." He said it like it should mean something, and to be fair, in Rivergate it did. Half the charts nailed to the fish market wall had his hand in them. "We need to talk about your report."

"My report is filed."

"Your report is *wrong*."

I set down the coffee. There are conversations where you want your hands free, and this had the shape of one. "Magistrate Dall seemed satisfied."

"Dall signs what's put in front of him if the number at the bottom is small enough." Odell stepped inside uninvited, which I respected, and closed the door behind him, which I didn't. "You wrote that the cave is structurally unsound. Limestone delamination, tidal scour, risk of collapse. You recommended sealing it with graded fill before the spring tides."

"Accurate enough."

"It's accurate if you never went past the entrance chamber." He looked at my hands, then at my face, doing the arithmetic men like him do—measuring a person the way they measure coastline, looking for where the smooth line breaks. "I've spent forty years reading terrain off paper. I know what a real survey looks like. Yours has the measurements of someone who walked thirty meters in and walked back out. Fast."

"The cave is dangerous."

"The cave is *labeled*," he said. "In a cipher I have spent eleven years trying to read. And yesterday morning a woman stood in front of it for two hours and came out knowing things she claims not to know."

I kept my face still. It's a skill. You learn it after a disaster, when every journalist and every inquiry board and every drunk in every port town wants to see the guilt move across your features like weather. You learn to be a flat horizon.

"I don't know what you want from me, Mr. Odell."

"Mara," he said, and the flatness broke—not mine, his. His voice cracked down the middle like ice under a boot. "My daughter. She's gone."

* * *

He told it badly, the way people tell things that have been eating them alive all night—out of order, with gaps, doubling back. Mara had left sometime before dawn. Her bed unslept-in, or slept in so briefly it hardly counted. On her desk, weighted under a brass compass, a note. He'd brought it. Of course he'd brought it; cartographers carry documents the way priests carry scripture.

He unfolded it on my table with hands that were not steady.

It wasn't a letter. It was three lines of symbols, drawn in pencil, precise as engraving—a spiral broken by a vertical stroke, a chevron over two parallel bars, and beneath them a mark I knew better than my own signature: the hooked S-shape with the crossbar, the one that means, in a cipher used by exactly one expedition in the history of expeditions, *the water rises here.*

Below the symbols, in a sixteen-year-old's handwriting: *Gone to find where it breathes. Don't follow until the third day. —M.*

"She copies them," Odell said. "She's been copying them for months. From my sealed papers. I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought she was making patterns. Girls her age make patterns. I told myself it was harmless."

"And it isn't."

"It isn't," he agreed, "because those aren't patterns. They're instructions. And because whoever drew the originals drew them for people who could survive following them." He sat down heavily on the room's one chair, which groaned but held, like most things in Rivergate. "I need you to tell me what they say."

"Why me?"

"Because I watched you stand in that cave mouth and I saw recognition, Ms. Voss. I'm old, not blind. And because—" he exhaled through his nose, long and controlled, a man managing himself the way you manage a rope under load— "because I know where those marks come from. I've known for eleven years. I just never had the courage to say it to the person who'd understand."

The coffee had gone cold. I drank it anyway. There's a version of this moment I'd rehearsed for a decade, usually at three in the morning in rooms like this one, in ports whose names I made a point of forgetting: the moment someone finally connected the survey marks to the survey, the survey to the expedition, the expedition to the disaster, and the disaster to me. In the rehearsal, I always had a good answer ready. A deflection. A lie with clean edges.

What I actually said was: "How long have you known?"

"Since the papers came to me." He reached into his coat and produced a folio—oilcloth-wrapped, tied with cord, the corners soft with handling. "Eleven years ago a man came to my shop with a commission. Copy these marks onto a coastal chart, in a location I'll specify. Don't ask what they mean. The pay was obscene. I was young and my wife was sick and I told myself it was just ink." His jaw worked. "The man's name was Corvane Dall. He was nobody then. A junior man at the Trust, ambitious, with money behind him that nobody could trace. He wanted the sigils placed at the sea cave below the lighthouse. Precisely there. He wanted them to look old."

"Forged?"

"Transposed. They came from somewhere else originally." He untied the cord with fingers that shook, and spread the folio open on my table, and there it was.

I don't know what I expected. Some part of me, apparently, expected nothing—that the whole thing would dissolve, that the cipher would turn out to be a coincidence, that the past would continue to be the past, i.e., elsewhere. Instead I was looking at a copy of the original field sheets from the Kestrel-Aquifer Survey. Expedition 9. *My* expedition. My own notation was on the margins—my younger hand, tighter, more certain, a hand that hadn't yet learned what its decisions cost. The hooked S. The spiral. The chevron over the bars. Eleven symbols, maybe twelve, mapping a hydrology that no chart of this coast had ever shown, because we'd found it nowhere near this coast.

Except we had found it near water. That was the whole point of the survey. We'd been funded to trace a living aquifer—an aquifer that moved, that migrated through limestone the way a rumor migrates through a town—and we'd found it, and then the mountain had come down, and three people had died, and the funding had evaporated along with everything else, and the data had supposedly burned with the base camp.

Supposedly.

"These were destroyed," I said. My voice came out level, which surprised me. "In the fire."

"Some of them, probably." Odell's eyes were on me now, steady and terrible. "Enough survived. Enough that Dall had them copied here, onto this coast, eleven years ago. Marking a cave that didn't exist until last week's tide pulled the sea away from it." He paused. "Or that existed the whole time, and the tide finally owed us the truth."

I turned the pages. My hands were steady too, and I hated them for it. Somewhere under the fish smell and the cheap coffee and the weekly rent, the girl who'd led Expedition 9 was sitting very straight, reading her own dead handwriting, and the room had gotten cold in the specific way rooms get cold when you realize the door you've been running from has been open behind you the entire time.

"Dall commissioned you to forge these," I said slowly, working it forward, "eleven years ago. Before the cave was exposed. Which means he knew it was there. Which means—"

"Which means he knew what the marks meant, and where they'd surface. Or he knew someone who knew." Odell's voice dropped. "And now the cave is open, and the Trust wants it sealed within the month, and my daughter has taken those marks upstream like they're a treasure map, and the third day starts tomorrow."

* * *

Here is what I should have done. Any sensible person would have done it. I should have given him back the note, told him to hire the coast guard, told him to lean on Dall, told him to drag the harbor trust's whole rotten apparatus into the light by its collar. I should have finished my cold coffee, packed my knife and boots, and been on the north road by noon, because that is what I do. That is what I have done for eleven years. I am a guide with no country, and the whole trick of having no country is that you never stay anywhere long enough for it to ask anything of you.

Instead I heard myself say: "Show me the rest of the note. The paper. What kind?"

"What?"

"The paper she wrote on. Was it chart stock? Laid paper? Was it torn from something?"

He blinked at me, thrown. "Chart stock. From my own drawer. Why does that—"

"Because she wanted you to know it was deliberate. She took materials from your desk so you'd understand she planned it, that it wasn't a whim." I stood up. My legs had opinions about this—they'd been on their feet since four—but they obeyed, the way legs do when the brain goes somewhere worse than tired. "A sixteen-year-old doesn't quote a dead expedition's cipher unless she can read it. Which means your sealed papers weren't just copied. She learned the system. Which means she knows the third-day rule isn't a suggestion."

"The third-day rule," Odell repeated. "You know what it means."

"It means the marks describe a route that floods." I was already pulling my boots on. "The original survey team used it for aquifer channels. You wait three days after heavy rain before entering, because the water that fills these systems doesn't drain—it *breathes*. In and out, on a cycle. Enter on the wrong breath and the channel inhales you." I laced the boots tight. "She left yesterday. If she went in yesterday, on a rising cycle—"

"Oh God."

"—then the note isn't telling you to wait. It's telling you how much time she thinks she has." I grabbed my jacket. Then stopped, because there was one more thing, and it had been sitting in my chest since he'd unfolded the folio, and cowardice said leave it, and eleven years of cowardice said *leave it*, and I was so tired of that voice I could have drowned it in the bay.

"Odell. The fire at base camp. The one that destroyed the survey data." I looked at him. "Did Dall ever tell you where he got the originals?"

His face did something complicated. Grief and guilt fighting for the same ground. "He never said. But the pages were water-stained, Ms. Voss. Badly. Smoke-damaged at the edges. Whoever carried them out carried them a long way, and hid them a long time, and sold them late." He met my eyes. "I assumed one of your people survived with them. I assumed—" he faltered— "I assumed you'd sold them yourself, honestly. After. Everyone needs to eat."

Everyone needs to eat. There it was—the ordinary, forgivable sin, offered to me like a coat. Take it. Say yes, I sold the data, it was ugly but human, and we can move on to the girl.

"I didn't sell them," I said. "I didn't take them. I've spent eleven years assuming they burned, and building my life on top of that assumption like it was bedrock." I slung the satchel over my shoulder. "Which means somebody else walked out of that camp carrying them. Somebody who was there. And I think I might know who."

"You do?" 

"I do," I said, and didn't elaborate, because the name in my head belonged to a man listed among the dead, and saying it aloud in this rented room felt like summoning weather. "Get your charts. The ones with the gorge above the lighthouse. Real ones, not the Trust's. And Mr. Odell—if anyone asks, you came to me about fish prices."

"Where are we going?"

"Upstream," I said, "of every map. And we're going fast, because your daughter is smarter than both of us, and smart people in flooded country die of exactly that."

At the door I paused, one hand on the frame, and let myself feel it—really feel it, for the first time instead of around it. The dread, yes. But underneath the dread, something worse and warmer: the past hadn't found me by accident. It had been waiting here, inked onto a forged chart, sleeping under a lighthouse, patient as water. I had run eleven years in a circle, and the circle had closed, and the only direction left was the one the river already knew.

I locked the door behind us. First time in years. It felt like a promise I couldn't afford to make, so I made it anyway.
