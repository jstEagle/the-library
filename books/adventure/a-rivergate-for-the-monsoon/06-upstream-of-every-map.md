## 6. Upstream of Every Map

The gorge above Rivergate doesn't announce itself. It just starts—one moment you're walking through gorse and sheep-cropped grass with the sea still audible behind you like a conversation you've left too early, and the next the ground drops away on both sides and you're inside the earth's argument with itself.

Odell walked behind me. He'd insisted on that arrangement—*you lead, I'll follow*—and I understood it wasn't deference. It was a test he didn't know he was administering. He wanted to see how a disgraced guide moved over broken ground. Whether her feet had forgotten anything.

They hadn't. That was the cruel joke of my whole life: everything else about me could be ruined, blacklisted, erased from two countries' worth of expedition registries, but my feet remained loyal. They read stone the way Odell read maps, without effort, without mercy.

"Those," Odell said, an hour in, when we passed the first cairn. "You stopped at it. You looked at it like it owed you money."

It was a stack of five stones, river-smoothed, wedged into a crack above the waterline. To anyone else it was geology having a lazy afternoon. To me it said *safe line, left bank, water rises fast here.* And beneath that, in the grammar of stacked stones rather than words, it said something no cairn should ever say to me again, because only one expedition in history used this particular dialect—three stones crossed by two, the signature of a survey team I last saw scattered across a glacier's moraine like dropped cutlery.

"It's a trail marker," I said.

"From your expedition."

I didn't answer. The gorge answered for me, funneled wind down its throat so it whistled past us like something inhaling.

"Petra." He used my name the way people use a crowbar. "These marks are twenty years old. Maybe more. You were what, nineteen when you led that climb?"

"Twenty-two." I stepped over a slab of fallen shale, testing it with my heel before committing weight. "And I didn't lead it. That's the official version everyone loved. A twenty-two-year-old leading six climbers into a storm. It made such a tidy story. Young woman, big ambitions, poor judgment. The registries ate it with a spoon."

"And the unofficial version?"

"Is upstream," I said. "Everything is upstream. Keep walking."

* * *

Here is what I remember about the rope, and I have tried very hard over the years to remember less.

It was a dynamic line, sixty meters, rated for falls well beyond anything we planned. Except that it wasn't. The funder—the man whose name sat at the top of the expedition letterhead like a crown, whose money bought the permits and the porters and the weather window we couldn't afford—had supplied gear from a broker nobody had vetted. I found the fraying three days before the storm. I held it up to the light at base camp and watched the sheath's core glint through a worn patch the size of a thumbnail, and I made a decision.

That's the sentence I have never said aloud. Not to the inquiry, not to the blacklisting boards in Kathmandu or Santiago, not to myself at three in the morning. *I made a decision.*

I decided not to turn back.

Not because I was ambitious—I was, God help me, but ambition is a slow poison, not a sudden one. I turned back-adjacent. I rerouted. I moved the traverse off the suspect rope onto fixed lines we'd placed ourselves, and I told myself this was leadership, this was adaptation, this was exactly what a good guide does when the equipment fails: you don't quit, you improvise. And then the storm came twelve hours early, because storms don't read itineraries, and improvisation in a whiteout is just falling with extra steps.

Two of my six came home. I was one of them.

The inquiry asked why I hadn't aborted at the equipment discovery. I said, truthfully, that I'd rerouted around the hazard. What I did not say—what I have carried like a stone in my boot for years—is that the real reason I didn't abort was that aborting meant admitting to the funder, in front of everyone, that his gear was garbage, and I was twenty-two and he had promised me the second ascent of a face nobody had touched, and I wanted it the way the drowning want air. So I built a workaround instead of building an exit, and the mountain, which is honest in the way nothing else is, billed us for the difference.

Odell didn't know any of this yet. He knew the registry version. But he kept asking questions in that cartographer's way—not *why did they die* but *where*, always where, as if geography might hold the answer that testimony wouldn't.

"The cairns," he said, as the light went bronze and the gorge walls closed to a stone's throw apart. "Who taught you to read them?"

"A woman named Sefa. She ran resupply for our approach. Local guide, third generation. She stacked them as we went in so we could find our way out blind if we had to." I paused at a fork where the river split around a boulder the size of a chapel. "She told me: a cairn isn't a signpost. It's a promise somebody made with their hands. You can lie with your mouth all day, but stacking stones true takes time, and liars are usually in a hurry."

"Was she on the mountain? During—"

"No. She refused to go above the snowline. Said high places belonged to whatever lived there, and she wasn't going to interrupt." I took the left channel, because the cairn at the fork—a weathered thing, older than the others, two stones crossed by one—said *left, even when right looks easier.* "She was the smartest person on that expedition and I ignored half of what she told me. That's also in the unofficial version."

* * *

We camped that night on a gravel shelf above the water, under an overhang that some previous traveler—Sefa's people, maybe, or the survey team whose marks we were following—had walled with a low crescent of fitted stone. Someone had left a fire ring. Someone had left, tucked into a crevice, a tin that once held tea and now held nothing but the smell of decades.

Odell boiled water over a small flame while I checked the route ahead by headlamp, reading the gorge walls for tomorrow's problems. High water marks, old ones, sat four meters above the current flow. Whatever fed this river, it had moods.

"You're going to ask me," I said, without turning around, "about the storm."

"I wasn't going to ask tonight."

"Everyone asks eventually. Better on a full stomach." I clicked off the lamp and sat. The dark in a gorge is total in a way open country never manages; the walls hold it like cupped hands. "There was a rope. It was bad. I knew. I routed around it instead of turning back, because turning back meant telling a powerful man he'd cheated us, and I was young, and I wanted the summit more than I wanted to be safe. The storm arrived early. Dev Chand and Marisol died in the whiteout on terrain I would never have chosen in daylight. I survived because I happened to be clipped to the one anchor I trusted."

The fire popped. Somewhere downstream, water worked at stone with the patience of the employed.

"That's it?" Odell said. "That's the confession that ended your career?"

"That's the confession I can prove. The rest—" I fed a stick to the flames. "Afterward, I found purchase records. The funder's broker sold us retired gym rope. I brought it to the inquiry. And then the funder's lawyers brought photographs of my route plan, marked up to show my 'deviation,' and suddenly the story was that a reckless young guide abandoned the approved line in deteriorating conditions. Which was technically true. Every word of it technically true. That's the thing about being framed—it works best when the frame is hung on real nails."

"Who was the funder?"

"I never learned his face. Letters, wire transfers, a lawyer who smelled like cough drops. The expedition was called the Meridian Source Survey." I laughed, and it came out wrong, dry and short. "Source. We were looking for the headwaters of a river that appeared on no map. Sefa said her grandmother sang about it. *The source that breathes,* she called it. I thought it was folklore. I thought a lot of things."

Odell had gone very still across the fire. In the light I watched him do cartographer's arithmetic—Meridian, source, unmapped river, sigils in a sea cave—and arrive at a sum he clearly didn't like.

"Your expedition," he said slowly, "was here. Wasn't it. On this coast. These cairns—"

"Are Meridian cairns. Yes." I pulled my blanket up. "Which means either my doomed survey ranged a lot farther than its paperwork admits, or someone has been walking this gorge using their system ever since. Both possibilities keep me awake, so let's both enjoy that."

* * *

Sleep came in pieces, the way it does in country that remembers bad weather. I dreamed in fragments: a rope's core glinting through its sheath; Dev Chand laughing at base camp, teaching Marisol a card game with half a deck; the specific sound a storm makes arriving over a ridge—not loud at first, just a pressure change, the air itself flinching. In the dream I stood at the fork in the gorge and both channels were full of white water and both cairns said *left* and there was no left.

I woke before dawn with my heart going like a trapped bird and lay listening to the river until it slowed.

We walked. That was the whole cure, if there was one—grief moving at walking pace, which is the only speed it tolerates. By midmorning the gorge narrowed into a slot where the walls leaned close enough to touch both sides, and the river ran green and glassy over stone worn into long flutes. And there, on a ledge ten meters up, half-swallowed by lichen: a cairn. Not stacked—*built*. Mortared. With a flat capstone carved in a spiral that wound inward like water finding a drain.

I climbed to it because my feet decided before I did.

Under the capstone, in a cavity the size of a fist, was a lead sleeve, and in the sleeve, a rolled strip of oilcloth. Inside the oilcloth, writing. Survey figures. Elevations. Flow rates. And a date from the year my expedition died, in handwriting I recognized with a lurch that started in my stomach and ended somewhere behind my eyes, because I had seen it on route plans and equipment manifests and one final, furious letter.

Dev Chand's handwriting. Our own surveyor, recording measurements in a gorge none of our maps covered, months after the mountain should have finished with him.

Except Dev Chand died on the mountain. I carried the inventory. I signed the list.

"Petra?" Odell called up from below. "What is it?"

I looked at the figures in my hand—flow rates that matched nothing, elevations that descended *below* the level of the sea cave we'd come from, as if the water ran uphill to get there—and then at the spiral carved into the capstone, winding inward, the same mark I'd seen scratched in salt beside the sigil of the river with no name.

"Someone came back," I said. "Someone who was supposed to be dead came back here, and measured the water, and hid what they found." I folded the oilcloth along its old creases, careful as handling a wound. "And Odell—these readings say the source isn't uphill of us. It's underneath. All of it. We've been walking on top of it since Rivergate."

Downstream, faint but unmistakable, carried up the gorge's throat on the wind: machinery. Pumps, working in rhythm, drinking.
