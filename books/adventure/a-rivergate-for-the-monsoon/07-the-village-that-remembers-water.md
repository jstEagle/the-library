## 7. The Village That Remembers Water

The gorge narrowed all afternoon until it felt less like walking through a landscape and more like being swallowed by one, slowly, with good manners. Odell hadn't spoken in an hour. He walked the way men walk when they're carrying a note from their daughter in their chest pocket and reading it with every step.

I let him. I had my own cargo.

The cairns kept coming—small stacks of river stone, unremarkable to anyone who hadn't spent six years of her life learning a survey cipher that officially didn't exist. Two stones crossed at the base meant *water route*. A third laid flat on top meant *safe passage, seasonal*. And when someone had wedged a fourth stone into the stack sideways, like a splinter, it meant something my old expedition leader used to translate as *don't trust the bank*.

There were a lot of sideways stones today.

"You're smiling," Odell said, without looking at me.

"I'm not."

"Your mouth is doing something."

"It's doing arithmetic." I nodded at the next cairn, perched on a shelf of black slate above a chute of white water. "Someone's been maintaining these. Recently. Moss hasn't colonized the fresh placements. That means hands, and hands mean people, and people this far up a gorge that isn't on any map means either smugglers or believers."

"Which worries you more?"

"Smugglers have prices. Believers have opinions."

He almost laughed. It came out wrong, half-strangled by worry, but it was the first sound he'd made that wasn't my daughter's name repeated under his breath like a prayer he'd lost faith in. I counted it as progress.

We rounded a shoulder of rock where the gorge opened its throat, and there it was: Senn.

I'd seen drowned valleys before—in Chile, mostly, reservoirs built over villages that had been paid off and forgotten. This wasn't that. Senn sat *above* the waterline, but barely, a scatter of stone houses on terraces that stepped down toward a river gone strangely quiet. The houses were old in the way that things are old when nobody could afford to replace them and nobody wanted to leave. Smoke rose from three chimneys. Laundry hung between two of them, drying in air that smelled of woodsmoke and wet stone and, underneath it, something mineral and cold, like the inside of a bell.

And the river—the same river we'd been following since dawn, loud and argumentative for hours—had gone silent. Not frozen. Not dammed. Just *low*, sliding over its bed with the embarrassed hush of a stream running through its own footprint.

"That's wrong," I said.

"What?"

"A river this size, this time of year, should be shouting." I crouched at the bank, touched the water. Cold enough to hurt, clear enough to count pebbles. "It's not flood season and it's not drought. This is a river that's been told to be quiet."

Odell looked at me the way clients look at guides when they realize the guide has stopped performing competence and started exhibiting it. "You can tell that from touching it?"

"I can tell that from twenty years of being lied to by water. Rivers don't whisper. They get made to."

* * *

They saw us coming long before we reached the terraces. Of course they did—a gorge has fewer sightlines than a cathedral nave, and two strangers walking upstream past maintained cairns would have been spotted, discussed, and probably bet upon before we'd covered the last kilometer.

An old woman met us at the lowest terrace, standing in front of a gate she hadn't closed, which was its own kind of message. She was small and upright as a survey stake, with hands that had clearly done decades of work and intended to do decades more out of spite. Behind her, doorways had filled with watchers: children, a man mending a net, another woman holding a ladle like a scepter.

"You followed the marks," the old woman said. Not a question.

"Some of them," I said. "The ones I could read."

"Then you read more than the Trust thinks anyone alive can read." Her eyes went to Odell, took in his city boots, his cartographer's stoop, his face doing open-heart grief in public, and dismissed him as harmless. Back to me. "You'll eat. You'll sleep. In the morning you'll tell us why you're really here, because nobody walks the gorge in fog season for exercise."

"We're looking for a girl," Odell said. "Sixteen. Dark hair. She'd have been asking about the source."

Something moved through the watchers behind her—not surprise. Recognition, passed along like a plate.

"The Vane girl," said the man with the net. "She came through four days back."

Odell made a sound I've only heard from people at altitudes where the air runs out.

"She's well?" he managed.

"She was," the old woman said, with a precision that told me exactly how much she wasn't going to say. "Ask again in the morning. I'm Maren Ashka. You'll call me nothing, because I won't answer to it anyway. Come."

* * *

Senn fed us barley soup with river fish in it and bread dense enough to use as ballast, and I ate like a person who'd been gutting fish for coin instead of glory, which is to say without dignity. The house was Maren's, low-ceilinged, warm, its walls hung with things that took me a while to identify because I kept misreading them as decoration.

They weren't decoration. They were records.

Rope knotted into patterns. Slate tablets scratched with lines and levels. Driftwood lengths notched at intervals. And on the largest wall, taking up the whole space above the hearth, a carved relief of the valley itself—but not this valley. A fuller one. The river ran through it thick and proud, and the terraces of Senn stood higher up the slopes, and at the head of the carving, where the gorge narrowed to a throat, someone had cut a spiral.

A spiral with a gap in it. Open, like a mouth mid-word.

I knew that mark. I'd been following its cousins for two days in salt and stone.

"That's the sigil," I said. "From the cave."

Maren didn't turn from the fire. "That's the source."

"The cave under the lighthouse—"

"Is its front door." She said it the way you'd say *that's the pantry*. "When the tide lies down far enough, the sea gets a look inside. The sea tells no one. The Trust tells everyone the wrong thing."

Odell set down his bowl. "My daughter believes the sigils mark a river's true source. A river that appears on no map."

"Because maps are how the Trust owns things," Maren said. "You cannot tax what you cannot find. You cannot pump dry what officialdom agrees does not exist. So the river was left off the surveys. Left off the charts. Left off the ledgers—except the private ones." She finally turned, and her eyes were the flat gray of the water outside. "How much do you know about where your fees go, cartographer?"

"I draw coastlines," Odell said quietly. "I've recently learned I may have helped bury a mountain."

"Then you're ahead of most of them."

* * *

The elders gathered after dark, in the hearth room, and I want to be honest about what that gathering was, because I've sat through enough expedition briefings and inquiry hearings to know theater when it's aimed at me. Seven elders, arranged with the casual precision of people who'd rehearsed this seating for years. Maren at the center. A man called Toma who'd been the net-mender and turned out to be the hydrologist, insofar as a hamlet can have one. Two women whose names I caught and forgot and would spend the rest of this story relearning.

They questioned me for two hours. Where I'd learned the cipher. Who I'd worked for. What happened on the mountain—I watched their faces when I gave them the official version, the one from the inquiries, and every one of them heard the seams in it. Mountain people always do. We are a worldwide guild of people who know what a lie about altitude sounds like.

Then Toma asked the question that mattered.

"When you touched our river today. What did it feel like?"

"Quiet," I said. "Held. Like a lung that's been told to breathe shallow."

The room changed temperature. Not literally. But the way a room changes when a stranger says the true thing without knowing it's the sacred thing.

"Hold your breath," Maren said.

I did. Ten seconds. Fifteen. At twenty my vision started to sparkle at the edges, and she put up a hand and I breathed.

"Now imagine holding it for eleven years."

Toma spread his hands over the hearth. "The source breathes. That is not poetry, though we say it poetically because poetry survives and instruments get confiscated. The aquifer rises and falls. Slowly—over days, seasons. When it rises, the river swells even in drought. When it falls, the river rests. The old gauge at the broken weir measured the breathing. Our grandparents timed plantings by it. My father swore the water tasted different on the exhale." He shrugged, defying me to laugh. "Mineral load shifts with pressure. So yes. Different taste. The legend and the chemistry agree, which is more than most marriages can claim."

"And now?" I asked, though I already knew. I'd known since the bank that morning. I just needed to hear it said by someone whose livelihood depended on getting it right.

"Now the river holds its breath," Toma said. "Constantly. No rise, no fall. Flat. Eleven years flat." He pulled a slate from beneath the bench and set it on my knees. Lines scratched into it, years of lines, a jagged waveform that climbed and fell like a mountain range—and then, eleven years ago, chopped flat as if someone had planed the mountain off.

Eleven years. I did the arithmetic I'm apparently always doing. Eleven years ago I was leading an expedition into mountains that didn't exist on maps either, funded by money whose source I never thought to question, carrying survey equipment I'd been told was standard issue.

Standard issue. For a coastal reconnaissance. Inland. Upstream.

"Oh," I said. Out loud. Stupidly. The kind of syllable that changes rooms.

Maren watched me arrive at it. "Say it."

"The pumps." My voice came out level, which surprised me. "Somebody found the source. Somebody's been pumping it. Constant draw—that's why the waveform's flat. The river's not resting. It's being held." I looked down at the slate, at eleven years of a heartbeat erased. "And whatever operation can run pumps at that scale, continuously, in a gorge that isn't on any map—it needs funding. Serious funding. The kind that buys silence."

The kind that funds expeditions, I didn't say. The kind that cuts corners on rope and calls it budget discipline, and when the rope fails and three people die, buys an inquiry report that blames the guide's judgment.

I had spent nine years believing my ruin was a private weather system. Mine alone. Rained on me, pooled around me, followed me across borders. Sitting in Maren's hearth room with a dead heartbeat scratched on slate, I understood for the first time that my disaster might have been not a storm but a *drain*—one outlet of a machine so large it flattened rivers and ruined guides as a matter of routine accounting.

"You've gone somewhere," Maren observed.

"I've gone eleven years back," I said. "Give me a minute. It's a long walk."

* * *

Later, when the elders had gone and Odell slept badly on a pallet near the fire, Maren sat with me by the door where the river sound—or its absence—came through the walls.

"You recognized the flat line," she said. "Not just what caused it. What it costs."

"Everyone in this valley pays it. Your plantings. Your fish."

"Yes. But you—" she tapped the slate, the place where the waveform died, "—you flinched at a specific year. Grief has a calendar. Yours is marked."

I could have given her the official version again. She'd have filed it with the others.

"There was an expedition," I said. "Eleven years ago. Funded by a consortium I never met, through lawyers I never liked. We were mapping headwaters. Officially. The funder pushed us faster than the terrain allowed, swapped our specified gear for cheaper kit, and when the anchor failed in a storm and took three of my people, the inquiry found my judgment wanting. I've carried that. Partly because some of it was true—I led them there. Mostly because it wasn't."

"And the funder?"

"Anonymous. Layers deep. I stopped digging when the digging started costing me work."

Maren nodded slowly, the way you nod at a patient whose symptoms finally match the disease you suspected. "Corvane Dall was a young man eleven years ago. Hungry. The Trust's syndicate bought its first mills then. Nobody asked where the power came from, because the mills ran cheap, and cheap is a wonderful anesthetic." She looked out at the silent river. "You think you wandered here, guide. Broke, anonymous, drifting. But the marks in that cave have been waiting eleven years for someone who could read them, and you walked into Rivergate the same week the tide owed nothing."

"That's coincidence."

"I'm eighty-one. I've met maybe four coincidences in my life. The rest were schedules."

Inside, Odell turned over in his sleep and murmured a name. Mara. Four days ahead of us, alive as of Senn, moving upstream toward a source that breathed and a pumping station that had strangled it—and somewhere between here and there, a magistrate who closed roads when girls got curious.

I sat with the slate on my knees a while longer, tracing the flat line with one finger. My ruin, it turned out, had a watershed. It drained into everything.

In the morning, Maren said, we'd take the high path past the broken weir, where the old gauge still stood shattered, and where, four days ago, a girl with dark hair had asked the elders the same questions I was asking—and then, being sixteen and unblessed with patience, had gone on ahead.

Upstream. Toward the breath.

Toward whatever was holding it shut.
