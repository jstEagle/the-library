## 8. Corvane's Ledger

There is a particular pleasure in a ledger that balances, and I have spent thirty years cultivating it the way other men cultivate orchards. Most people imagine corruption as something dramatic—sacks of coin in darkened rooms, daggers, midnight oaths. Amateurs. Corruption, done properly, is arithmetic. It is columns that reconcile. It is a discrepancy of four percent, year over year, buried in a line item called *Hydraulic Maintenance, Upper Reaches*, which no one has ever audited because no one has ever been given a reason to look, and because I sign the auditor's stipend myself.

I was at my desk before dawn, as always. The harbor trust offices occupy the old customs house on Quay Street, granite and salt-stained glass, and from my window I can watch the bay perform its morning theater. This week the theater had gone strange. The tide had drained out past every mark on Odell's charts and left the flats gleaming like the inside of an oyster shell, and the town had come down to stare at it the way townspeople stare at anything they don't understand—which is to say, hungrily, and in groups.

The cave beneath the lighthouse was now a matter of public conversation. That was unfortunate. Caves are only dangerous when people know about them.

I poured my coffee—it came up from the south, roasted dark, paid for out of the same discretionary fund that paid for the pumps' lubricant—and opened the ledger.

* * *

Let me describe the ledger, because it deserves description. It is not a book, properly speaking; it is three books. The first is the public ledger, which is honest in the way a shop window is honest: everything visible, nothing real behind it. The second is the Trust's internal ledger, which is honest in the way a confession extracted under duress is honest: technically accurate, carefully incomplete. The third book has no name. I think of it as the true ledger, though "true" flatters it. It is simply the one where the water goes.

The aquifer feeding Rivergate had been producing, by my most conservative estimate, eleven million gallons a day when I first found it, twenty-two years ago, a young magistrate with a head for figures and a father-in-law deep in the milling syndicate. The river's surface flow—the pretty part, the part with the fish and the ferry and the postcard views—was perhaps a tenth of what moved beneath the valley floor, through gravel beds older than the town, pressurized by the weight of the gorge walls. The old surveyors had known. That was the trouble with the old surveyors. They'd known, and they'd written it down, and they'd drawn their little sigils on the cave wall like children marking a treasure map, and then they'd had the poor judgment to die or scatter or take Trust money, which amounts to the same thing.

Odell took the money. I want that understood. People will say, later, when this all comes apart the way things do, that Corvane Dall corrupted an innocent man. Nonsense. I purchased a service from a willing seller at a fair price. He drew the sealed survey that declared the upper reaches geologically barren, and he signed it, and he went home to his daughter and his charts and slept soundly for sixteen years. If his conscience has only now begun to invoice him, that is between him and it. My accounts with him were settled long ago.

The mills, of course, were the point. A mill runs on fall—water dropping through a wheel—and the syndicate's mills had been starving for decades as the surface river thinned. But a pressurized aquifer is better than any waterfall. It never freezes, never droughts, never argues with you about seasonal flows. We drove the intake shafts up into the gorge under cover of the road works in '09, and we bled the source through a private penstock to the syndicate's turbines, and the output figures climbed, and everyone who mattered grew comfortable, and the river above Rivergate began, very slowly, to hold its breath.

That phrase is not mine. It comes from the hamlet up-valley, Senn, whose elders have been saying it for years in that maddening way of theirs—not accusing, just observing, the way you'd note weather. *The river holds its breath.* I have read their petitions. All nine of them. I file them under *Folklore, Hydrological*, because filing is a form of mercy and because the alternative is reading them as testimony, and testimony requires response, and response requires either lying under oath or telling the truth, and I have built thirty years on never being forced to choose.

* * *

At half past seven, Pellis knocked—the double knock, two short, which means information I won't enjoy but need anyway. Pellis is my clerk, a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and the moral flexibility of a well-made hinge. He swings both ways depending on the door.

"The girl," he said. "Mara Odell."

"Ah." I set down my pen. When a clerk opens with a name instead of a report, the report is bad. "Tell me."

"She was seen Tuesday. Upper gorge road, past the third cairn. Brannick's boy was hauling lime up to the kiln and passed her walking. Alone. Carrying a satchel." Pellis paused, savoring it, the way he savors everything unpleasant. "She was headed toward the pumping station access."

Tuesday. The day after the tide went out and the cave opened its mouth to the whole town. I looked at the window, at the bay lying there shamelessly empty, and did some arithmetic. Not the pleasant kind.

"Where is she now?"

"That's the thing. She didn't come back down. Brannick assumed she'd cut over to Senn. Her father reported her missing yesterday evening."

"Yesterday evening." I let the words sit. A cartographer's daughter, raised on contour lines and compass bearings, walks alone toward the one structure in the valley she should never see, and her father waits a full day before raising alarm. Either Odell is a negligent parent—and I have met the man; he is many things, but he counts everything—or he knew where she'd gone and hoped she'd come back before he had to admit it. Or, a third possibility, colder: he knew exactly what she'd find up there, because he helped build the lie she was unbuilding.

"Anything else?"

"One more thing." Pellis produced a folded paper, and I confess my stomach did something it rarely does, which is move. "The stranger. The woman who verified the cave for us. Voss. Brannick's boy says she was asking at the fish house about the gorge trail. Which cairns still stand. Where the old survey camps are."

I sat with that for a moment. Petra Voss. I had bought her report for eighty crowns and a signature, expecting a broken drifter grateful for the coin, and I had received exactly what I paid for—a document declaring the sea cave structurally unsound, recommending immediate sealing. Clean work, actually. Better than clean. It had the specific texture of a lie told by someone experienced in telling them, which is why I hadn't questioned it. You can always tell a professional liar's paperwork; amateurs over-explain.

But now the professional liar was asking after the gorge. And the cartographer's daughter was already up there. And the tide had shown the whole town a door I'd spent two decades keeping shut.

"Pellis," I said, "pull the file on the '04 expedition."

He blinked. "The mountain one? The Nepali—"

"The one Voss led. Before Nepal. There's a preliminary survey in there—I funded it. Quietly, through the syndicate's development arm." I had almost forgotten it myself, which is a dangerous admission even in the privacy of my own skull. Twenty years ago, Corvane Dall, ambitious and undercapitalized, had put money into a young guide's expedition to chart high-country watersheds, looking for exactly the kind of pressurized source I would later find in my own valley. The expedition ended badly. Rope failure, storm, two dead, and the funder's corner-cutting gear buried so thoroughly under the official findings that even I had nearly forgotten my own signature on the purchase order. Nearly. I don't forget signatures. Signatures are the whole religion.

"Why?" Pellis asked.

Because if Voss recognized the sigils in that cave—if she read them the way I suspect she can, having learned cipher-notation from the same survey tradition—then she knows the marks' lineage, and the lineage leads back through Odell's sealed papers to the original watershed surveys, and somewhere in that chain of paper is a thread that, pulled hard enough, unravels the entire garment. And because a woman who takes eighty crowns to lie about a cave is a woman who understands what money is for, and might be persuaded to understand what silence costs.

Or she might be the kind who takes the money and then goes looking anyway, out of spite or conscience or whatever broken mechanism drives people like her up mountains and into gorges. I've employed both kinds. The second kind always ends up in the ledger eventually—one way or another.

"Just pull it," I said.

* * *

By ten I had the file open on my desk, and by noon I had read it twice, and by one I had made three decisions, which for me constitutes a crisis.

The file confirmed what memory had softened: I had supplied the expedition's rope through a syndicate subsidiary at a price that should have told young Dall everything about its quality. Two climbers dead on a face in the Khumbu region—no wait, that was the later disaster, the famous one, the one that blacklisted her. The '04 survey was domestic, upstream country, and it failed differently: funding withdrawn mid-season, the guide left holding debts and a half-finished map, and a notation in my own hand authorizing the withdrawal because the preliminary readings suggested the sources were *not commercially significant*.

They were commercially significant. I had simply been looking in the wrong valley. The irony is available to me now, free of charge.

Decision one: the gorge road closes. Officially, a slip hazard after the anomalous tides—perfectly plausible, the bay had behaved like a lunatic all week, and engineering reports are cheap. Unofficially, a closed road means no witnesses going up, and the ones already up there become trespassers, and trespassers can be managed with a constable's patience rather than a magistrate's risk.

Decision two: Mara Odell becomes a runaway, not a missing person. There is a difference, administratively, and the difference is search parties. A missing child summons the valley; a runaway summons a form. Her father's grief will be genuine, which is useful—genuine grief makes excellent camouflage. I drafted the notice myself. *Sixteen years of age, last seen wearing a grey coat.* I am very good at notices. They are the poetry of bureaucracy: small, sad, and load-bearing.

Decision three concerned Voss, and here I will be honest, since honesty in the privacy of one's own accounting is the only kind that costs nothing: decision three frightened me, because it required spending rather than withholding, and men like me are defined by what we refuse to spend. I would offer her more. Enough to make the sealing permanent, enough to send her over the border with a clean purse and a clean slate—perhaps even a letter to certain contacts in Chile, where her blacklist apparently has roots. Buy the whole woman, not just the report. A partial purchase is how you end up reading about yourself in someone else's testimony.

Pellis returned at two with the file request logged and a question he'd clearly been carrying all afternoon.

"And if the girl's found something up there? At the station?"

"Then she'll have found a pumping facility operating under Trust authority," I said, "with permits."

"With forged permits."

"With permits," I repeated, and looked at him until he looked away. This is the entire art. You do not deny the thing. You deny the adjective. Forged becomes issued, secret becomes proprietary, stolen becomes allocated. The words do the work; the water does the rest.

* * *

I walked down to the quay at dusk, as I do most evenings, because a magistrate should be seen, and because I like to look at the bay when it's behaving. Tonight it wasn't. The water had come back in, but wrong—too fast, too far, slopping over the low steps of the customs stairs, and the fishermen stood around in that grim silent way that precedes deputations. Old Maren caught my eye across the shingle and didn't nod. Maren has not nodded at me in six years, since the third petition, and I respect him for it. Hatred with consistency is practically integrity.

The river, coming down from the gorge, ran thin and greenish over the shallows, and where it met the returning tide there was a smell I hadn't noticed before, or had chosen not to—a mineral staleness, like water kept too long in a cellar. The breath-holding smell. The Senn elders would have known its name. They have names for everything up there; it's how people without power keep records.

I stood there doing the arithmetic I do every evening, the only arithmetic that has never once balanced: the syndicate's output against the river's decline, my position against my exposure, the years remaining against the years required. Twenty-two years of clean columns. Eleven million gallons a day, give or take what the source could spare, and lately—this season, since spring—give or take considerably more. The pumps had been working harder for less. I had told myself it was sediment, wear, the ordinary tax on machinery. Standing at the rail, watching the thin green thread of the river fail to push back the sea, I permitted myself the thought I had been declining for months:

The source was not tired. The source was dying. And things that die while you're drinking from them have a way of taking the drinker's hand on the way down.

A girl was up there with a satchel full of her father's sealed papers. A disgraced guide was asking after the trail. The tide had shown the town a cave with twenty years of my secrets written on its walls in somebody else's alphabet.

Back in the office, I wrote the order closing the gorge road, signed it, dated it, and filed a copy under *Public Safety*. Then I wrote a second order, unsigned, and gave it to Pellis verbally, because some instructions should exist only as breath: Brannick and his brothers were to go up at first light, walk the road to the station, and bring the girl down—gently, if possible. And if they encountered anyone else on that road, anyone at all—

"Gently," I said again, and heard how the word sounded, and decided not to hear it twice.

Pellis paused at the door. "And the Voss woman?"

I looked at the ledger, all three of it, and at the column where, twenty-two years ago, a younger man had written *not commercially significant* in a hand steadier than mine is now.

"Find out what she knows," I said. "Then we'll discuss her price."

Outside, the river ran thin toward a sea that owed nothing to anyone, and up in the dark gorge, I had no doubt whatsoever, the water was holding its breath—waiting, patient as paperwork, for someone to finally open the file.
