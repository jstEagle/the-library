## 9. Rope and Reckoning

The gorge above Senn does not echo so much as it swallows sound and returns it late, like a creditor who knows you can't pay. Odell walked ahead of me for most of the morning, map case slung across his back, and every time he spoke his voice came back to us a half-second wrong, as if the rock were considering whether to repeat it.

"Petra," he said, and then the gorge said it too, *Petra*, and I flinched both times. Not at him. At the name. Six days in Rivergate and I'd been "the fish-gutter," "the stranger," "that woman from the night shift." Now I was Petra again, and being Petra again meant being the woman whose name ended three careers that weren't hers.

We'd left Senn before dawn against the elders' advice, which is to say we left Senn with their blessing wrapped around a warning like a knife in a cloth. Mother Anselm had pressed a twist of waxed paper into my hand — salt and something bitter — and said, "The river holds its breath up there. Don't make it exhale on you." I'd thanked her. I hadn't understood her. That's usually how prophecy works.

The cairns were still there.

That was the thing I couldn't get past, walking. The cairns were *still there*. Eleven years of monsoons and rockfall and nobody's boots, and the little stacked stones my expedition had raised marked the route as cleanly as if we'd passed last week. Cairns are supposed to be temporary. They're supposed to be arguments the mountain wins eventually. These had won nothing; they'd simply waited, patient as unpaid debts, for the one person who could read them to come back and be ruined by them.

"You built these," Odell said at the third one. It wasn't a question.

"I built those."

"And the marks on them?"

I turned the stone so he could see the chiseled glyph beneath the lichen — a vertical stroke crossed by two horizontals, offset, like a river refusing to run straight. "That's ours. Route confirmation. Third variant means the path shifts after rain." I heard my own voice go flat and instructional, the voice I used to use with clients who asked too many questions on exposed ground. "Your daughter read them right, by the way. Whatever she copied out of your papers. She read them right."

Odell was quiet for ten steps. Then: "You're not what I expected."

"What did you expect?"

"Somebody harder." He said it without apology, which I appreciated. Hardness is a costume most people wear badly. "The reports made you sound like a machine that had eaten a person."

"The reports were written by people who needed me to be a machine that had eaten a person. Machines can be blamed. People have to be listened to."

He looked at me sideways at that, and I knew he'd heard the shape of what was coming, even if neither of us said it yet. There's a moment on every rope team when you realize your partner knows something you haven't told them, and the mountain between you gets very small and very crowded all at once.

* * *

The rockfall caught us at midafternoon, in a narrows where the gorge pinched down to a throat of black slate and the river below ran green and silent, holding its breath exactly as Anselm had promised.

I heard it before I saw it. That's the thing nobody tells you about rockfall — it isn't sudden. It announces itself. A tick of stone somewhere above, then another, then the particular dry rattle like dice in a cup that means the slope has decided something. Eleven years and my body still knew it before my mind did.

"Down!" I grabbed Odell's pack strap and hauled him toward the wall, into the shallow overhang where the old survey camp had once anchored its latrine trench — glamorous, but solid rock, and solid rock is the only glamour that matters when the sky starts shedding itself.

The slide lasted maybe forty seconds. It sounded like the end of the world being rehearsed by an amateur company. When the dust cleared, the path behind us was gone — buried under a fan of blocks the size of coffins — and the path ahead was worse than gone, because the ledge we needed had sheared away entirely, leaving a gap of perhaps eight meters of blank vertical wall with the river churning forty feet below.

And Odell wasn't moving.

"Odell." I rolled him. His face was gray under the dust, and his left leg lay at an angle legs don't negotiate. A glancing block, caught him across the shin on its way through. He hissed through his teeth and did not scream, which told me more about his character than any amount of conversation.

"My fault," he managed. "Should've watched—"

"It's nobody's fault. Rockfall doesn't take requests." I got water into him, splinted the leg with a trekking pole and the tape from my kit — the kit I'd rebuilt piece by piece over eleven years, buying back the gear of my old life one item at a time like a widow repurchasing her wedding silver. "Can you hop? One shoulder?"

"On that ankle? No." He almost smiled. "Cartographer. We draw the mountains. We don't expect them to fight back."

"Well. Welcome to the other department."

Here is what I want to be honest about, because honesty is going to become a theme whether I like it or not: for about ninety seconds, standing there with a broken man and a dead-end ledge, I thought about leaving him.

Not seriously. Not in the way you actually leave someone. But the thought arrived, uninvited, wearing my own face: *you could go back, you could reach Senn by dark, you could tell them the gorge took him, and no one would ever know, because no one ever believes the disgraced guide anyway.* That's what ruin does. It doesn't just close doors. It leaves them open, tauntingly, onto the ugliest rooms in you.

Then Odell said, "Mara would have liked this route," and the door shut.

* * *

The traverse was the problem. The traverse was always the problem.

Eight meters of blank wall, a ledge on the far side barely wide enough to stand on, and between us and it: nothing. Below: the river, running high with snowmelt despite the drought the Trust pretended didn't exist. My rack held two ropes — one good, one suspect, its sheath glazed and stiff in a way that makes guides touch it the way you'd touch a wound. Four cams, two of them with soft lobes. A handful of slings. One harness, mine. Odell had a daypack and a broken leg.

There is a maneuver for this. You anchor high, you rig a tensioned handline across the gap, you shuttle the injured party across on a improvised litter or a piggyback carry, and then you cross yourself. It's called a Tyrolean when it's done properly and a prayer when it's done with failing gear, and I had done it exactly once before, on a mountain whose name I still couldn't say without tasting copper.

Khangsar Ri. Day six. The storm that came in nine hours early. And the anchor — I can see it now, I will see it until I die — the anchor that our funder's logistics man had insisted on, because the proper anchors were heavy and heavy gear costs helicopter time and helicopter time costs money. A single marginal piton where the plan called for three. I had argued. I had argued and then I had *complied*, because I was thirty-one years old and the man signing the checks had a reputation for ruining reputations, and I told myself the piton would hold, because telling yourself things is the oldest technology humans have.

It held for eleven of the fourteen crossings. On the twelfth, with Devi Rana on the line, it didn't.

They pulled her body out of the serac field four days later. The inquiry asked why the anchor was single-point. I said I didn't know. That was the lie. The truth was I knew perfectly well, and saying it aloud would have meant naming the funder, and the funder's lawyers were in the room, and I was young, and I chose the version of silence that felt like survival and turned out to be the slowest form of drowning.

Eleven years. Every night shift gutting fish in some port town, every blacklist, every border guard squinting at my passport, all of it downstream of one piton and one cowardice.

And now here was the same maneuver, laid out in front of me like an exam I'd failed, retake mandatory.

"Talk to me," Odell said. He'd watched my face do whatever it was doing. "Guides talk when they work. I've read that."

"Guides talk when they're calm. Right now I'm performing calm, and the performance requires concentration."

I built the anchor first, and I built it the way I should have built it at Khangsar. Three pieces, equalized, load-tested with my whole weight plus a bounce, then again, then a third time while Odell watched me test it a third time and wisely said nothing. The slate took good cams — cracks from some ancient freeze-shatter, parallel, generous. For once the mountain was cooperating, or at least not actively lying.

The suspect rope became the haul line, never the lifeline. The good rope carried everything that mattered. I rigged the tensioned span, hauled it drum-tight, and hung a loop of sling at midspan for the carry.

"Listen carefully," I said, kneeling by Odell. "This is going to hurt in ways you'll want to describe to me later. Don't. Save it. Breathe when I say breathe, hold when I say hold, and if you feel yourself falling, do absolutely nothing clever. Cleverness is how people die on ropes."

"That's oddly comforting."

"It's the only honest sentence in guiding."

Getting him across took twenty minutes and cost him a scream he strangled down to a groan, and cost me a shoulder I'd be invoicing myself for later. But he came across, and I lowered him onto the far ledge, and he lay there breathing in the dust-light and looking up at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the look of a person recalculating everything they believed about someone.

Then it was my turn.

I clipped into the line. And halfway across — eight meters of air, green river turning below, the suspect rope humming its one bad note — the middle of my body simply stopped working. Not fear exactly. Memory. The serac, the crack, Devi's voice on the radio saying *Petra, the line, the LINE*, and my hands knowing even as I answered that there was nothing in the world I could do, and the specific silence afterward that was worse than any sound.

The sling loop was there. I'd placed it for Odell. I put my weight into it and hung there, mid-river, mid-air, mid-life, and let myself hang.

Then I finished the crossing. Hand over hand, the way it's taught, the way it's done, boring and perfect and alive.

* * *

We sat on the far ledge while the light went long and amber, sharing water and the last of the Senn bread. Odell's leg was propped on a stone. He hadn't asked me questions, which is a discipline, and eventually the discipline broke in the kindest possible way.

"You've done that before," he said. "The traverse. Before."

"Yes."

"And it went badly."

I looked at the river. Held breath, green glass, patient. Somewhere upstream, pumps were drinking it. Somewhere upstream, my past and this valley's future were tangled together like wet rope, and I was done pretending I could walk between them.

"It went worse than badly," I said. "A woman died. Her name was Devi Rana. She was the strongest climber on the team and she trusted me, and the anchor failed because it was built cheap, and I knew it was built cheap, and I let it happen because the man paying for the expedition — Corvane Dall's syndicate, though I didn't know his name then, just his money — the man paying had cut corners, and I complied instead of refusing." My voice came out steady, which surprised me. Steady like the river. Holding its breath. "I spent eleven years letting everyone believe it was my judgment that failed. Because my judgment did fail — just not the way they said. My judgment failed when I kept quiet."

Odell was very still. Cartographers, I was learning, know how to hold a contour line without smoothing it.

"Why tell me now?" he asked.

"Because if I say it out loud on this ledge, it becomes true in front of a witness, and I need it to be true somewhere besides inside me." I handed him the water. "Also because I think your daughter followed the same river I did, and I think Dall's name is going to be at the end of it, and when that happens I intend to already be someone who tells the truth. Practice now. Perform later."

He drank. Then he said, quietly, "The sealed survey. The one I buried. Dall paid for that too."

I closed my eyes. Of course. Of course the threads ran to the same spool. "We're going to need more bread," I said.

It was Odell who spotted it — cartographers see edges before they see anything else. Across the gap, on the shattered ledge where the rockfall had sheared the path, someone had stacked stones. Small ones. Recent ones — the lichen scraped clean, the stacking confident, a little showy.

Three stones, topped with a fourth set off-center. A vertical stroke implied in the lean.

A sigil. Fresh. Pointing upstream.

"She came through here," Odell breathed. "After the fall. She climbed past this."

Mara had come through a fresh rockfall alone, sixteen years old, and left a mark only two people in the world could read — and one of them was bleeding on a ledge, and the other had just finished confessing to a murder that wasn't hers.

I stood up. My shoulder screamed. I ignored it.

"Then we move," I said. "Before whoever dropped that rock decides to drop another."
