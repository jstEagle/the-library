## 10. The Girl on the Weir

The thing nobody tells you about running away is that it's mostly sitting.

I'd imagined the dramatic parts—slipping out the window, the note on the kitchen table, the walk into the gorge with my father's sealed papers wrapped in oilcloth against my chest. I'd imagined the town waking up and gasping. What I had not imagined was day four, when the drama ran out and I was sitting on an upturned crate in an abandoned survey camp, eating cold rice with my fingers, arguing with a dead man's arithmetic.

The dead man was not actually dead. His name was Halloway, and he'd been a hydrographer on the expedition that came through here forty years ago, and he had died of old age in a town I'd never heard of, but his field notebooks were very much alive in my hands, and they were calling me a liar.

Not me specifically. My father.

"Go on, then," I said to Halloway's cramped handwriting, "tell me I'm wrong."

He didn't, because I wasn't. That was the problem. I'd spent months copying sigils out of my father's sealed folios, months building a theory that the marks in the sea cave weren't decoration, weren't superstition, weren't the sort of thing cartographers put on maps to make tourists feel mystical. They were a record. A survey. And Halloway's notebooks confirmed it in the least dramatic language imaginable, which somehow made it worse.

*The source is not a spring,* he'd written, in the flat voice of a man who did not know he was writing a heresy. *The source is a body. It rises and falls on a rhythm we could not correlate with rainfall or season. Recommend the aquifer be classified as a living system and left ungauged.*

Left ungauged. I looked up at the shattered river gauge down the slope, its brass dial split like a dropped plate, and I thought: someone disagreed with you, Halloway. Someone disagreed so hard they broke your recommendation over a rock and built a pump station up the valley instead.

* * *

The survey camp had been a real place once. Three canvas-roofed huts gone to rot, a stone hearth with forty years of rain in it, a drying rack for maps that now held only the skeleton of somebody's shirt. I'd found it by following the sigils upstream, which sounds braver than it was. Mostly I followed them because going back was unthinkable and going forward was at least a direction.

I'd been decoding since dawn, by the light through the hut's torn roof, and the picture that was assembling itself out of Halloway's numbers and my father's cipher was not the one I'd come for.

I'd come for glory, if I'm honest. I'd come for the thing a sixteen-year-old wants, which is to be the one who was right, spectacularly and publicly right, while her careful, cautious, sealed-drawer father turned out to have been sitting on the discovery of the century. I'd imagined the reveal. I'd imagined the town square. I'd imagined, God help me, applause.

What Halloway gave me instead was a dying thing.

The numbers didn't lie, and I'd checked them three ways because I wanted them to. The aquifer breathed—that was Halloway's word, *breathes*, underlined twice, the only underlining in four hundred pages of restraint. It filled and emptied on a cycle of roughly eleven days, and every measurement in the old survey sat comfortably inside that rhythm like a sleeper in a bed.

Then I cross-checked against the river levels I'd been recording myself, secretly, for eight months, from the weir below town. The breathing was still there. But the amplitude had shrunk. When I plotted the last two years against Halloway's forty-year-old baseline, the breath had been cut by more than half. The source wasn't sleeping. It was suffocating, slowly, the way you suffocate when someone puts a hand over your mouth and doesn't mean to kill you, only to keep you quiet.

And there was only one thing upstream that could put a hand over a river's mouth.

I'd seen the pump station from the ridge on my second day out. I'd told myself it was a water works, a municipal thing, boring. But municipal things don't have fences with fresh wire, and they don't have a road kept graded in a valley where the gorge road has been allowed to wash out everywhere else, and they don't run at night. I'd watched its lights through the fog and felt, for the first time in my life, the specific cold that comes from realizing a thing is not an oversight. It's a policy.

* * *

Here is what I knew by the end of day four, sitting on my crate with rice on my fingers:

One. The source of the river—the true source, the one on no map, the one my father's sigils pointed to like a compass needle—is a living aquifer in the high gorge, and it breathes.

Two. Something upstream is draining it faster than it can breathe, and has been for years.

Three. The Harbor Trust controls everything upstream, and Corvane Dall controls the Harbor Trust, and Corvane Dall had personally come down from his office to offer a stranger money to call the sea cave unstable.

Four. My father had sealed papers describing the source in detail, and had never said a word about it to anyone, including me, including the town whose water it was.

Five. I was alone, sixteen, four days from home, and the only person who knew where I was was me.

I want to be able to say that the fear came later. It didn't. It was there the whole time, a cold stone in my stomach I ate around, slept around, did arithmetic around. Courage, I was learning, isn't the absence of the stone. It's the discovery that you can carry it and keep walking anyway, and that walking is the only thing that keeps the stone from getting heavier.

* * *

On the fifth day I made the decision that I will defend for the rest of my life and that I also know, in the honest room at the back of my head, was half madness.

I decided to leave a trail.

Because here was the thing about the stranger—the guide, Voss. I'd watched her stand in front of the Trust and call the cave dangerous. I'd watched her do it well, too, that was the awful part. She hadn't fumbled it. She'd given them the flat professional voice, the measured concerns, the recommendation to seal, and Corvane Dall had nodded along like a man hearing exactly what he'd paid for.

And I'd watched her eyes.

I've grown up around maps. You learn to read faces the way you read terrain—the official route and the actual route are rarely the same line. When Voss said *unstable*, her eyes went to the cave mouth behind her, just once, the way your eyes go to a door you've closed on something you love. Nobody lying about a place looks at it like that. People lying about a place look anywhere else.

So: she was lying for them, which made her theirs, or she was lying to them, which made her something else. And either way, she was the only other person in Rivergate who had stood inside that cave and read the sigils without needing them explained.

If she was theirs, my trail would tell me nothing. If she was something else—

Well. If she was something else, she might be the only help in the world I was going to get.

* * *

The sigil system, once you understood it, was beautiful in a way that made me furious at my father for hiding it. It wasn't a language, exactly. It was closer to the way a river itself writes—direction, character, warning, all compressed into marks a surveyor could cut with a chisel in ten seconds. *Water here runs true.* *Water here is held.* *Water here remembers.*

Halloway's notebooks had a key page, half-finished, and my father's folios had the rest, and between them I could write it. Not well. My early attempts looked like a drunk man's handwriting, all wobble and second thoughts. But by the fifth day I could cut a legible mark into wood with my father's survey knife in under a minute.

I cut them going down.

Not toward the pump station—I wasn't ready for that, and I knew it, and knowing it felt like the first adult decision of my life. Down, toward the gorge, along the route I'd come up, at the places where a follower would have to pause anyway: the fork in the trail, the leaning pine, the broken weir where the old gauge lay in pieces. At each one I cut a mark that said, in the old surveyors' grammar: *A reader passed here, going down. The water is held. Follow.*

It was, I recognized, a message in a code that maybe three people alive could read, left for a woman who might be working for the man I was hiding from, by a girl who had told no one where she was going. My father would have called it reckless. He would have been right. I carved the next mark anyway.

Because here's what the notebooks had that I couldn't un-know: Halloway's final entry, dated six weeks before the survey was abandoned. *Dall's people were here again today. They asked for our amplitude figures. I gave them the falsified set. I don't know what they intend to build, but a man who asks how deeply a thing breathes is not asking in order to let it breathe.*

Forty years. Corvane Dall had been asking that question for forty years, and the whole valley had gone on drinking its tea and mending its nets on top of the answer.

* * *

That night the fog came up the gorge like something with intent, and I sat in the ruined hut with my back against Halloway's crate of notebooks and listened to the river below, and for the first time I heard it the way the elders in the old stories must have heard it. Not as scenery. As a chest, rising and falling, eleven days to a breath, and rising too shallow.

I thought about my father, and I was surprised to find that the anger had gone soft. He'd sealed those papers for a reason, and the reason was probably exactly this: that a girl with a knife and a theory would come up here alone. He'd looked at the truth and decided it was too heavy for the town to carry, and maybe he'd been right, and maybe carrying it anyway was the only decent thing left to do, and maybe both of those could be true at once. That was another adult discovery, and I resented every one of them. Adulthood, so far, was just learning that all your choices cost.

I thought about the stranger again. Voss. I'd asked around before I left—of course I had—and the name had gotten me a strange look from the fish-crier and a longer one from Ilya, who was fourteen and thought he knew everything and was, annoyingly, sometimes right. *Blacklisted,* the fish-crier had said. *Nepal. Chile. Don't ask her about it.* A guide with no country. A woman who read dead surveyors' marks on a coast where they had no business being, and lied about the cave with her eyes on the door.

There was a story there. I was sixteen and made of appetite; of course there was a story there. But the story could wait. The river's breath couldn't. Halloway's curve didn't flatten gently—it fell off a cliff somewhere in the next two years, if the trend held. Whatever the pumps were doing, they were close to done, and when the source stopped breathing, the river didn't just dry up. Halloway's notes were explicit about that too: a living aquifer, killed, collapses. Takes the water table with it. Takes the valley with it.

So the trail. Down through the fog, mark by mark, my father's knife in my hand, courage out ahead of judgment like a dog that had slipped its lead and didn't care.

* * *

On the sixth morning, I heard them coming.

Two sets of boots on the trail below the camp, moving carefully, which meant not hunters and not hikers. Hunters crash. Hikers chatter. These moved the way my father moved in the field—reading the ground, choosing each step. I put out the tiny flame I'd allowed myself and went flat behind the collapsed wall of the second hut, heart going like the pump station's pistons, and watched the fog below the camp for shapes.

They came up into view by the leaning pine, and I stopped breathing altogether.

The first was my father. Mud to the knees, a rope coil over one shoulder, a face I'd known for sixteen years wearing an expression I'd never seen on it—open fear, unhidden, the face of a man who had stopped pretending the situation was manageable. And behind him, sure-footed on the wet rock, moving like the mountain was a language she'd been fluent in once and hadn't forgotten:

The stranger. Voss.

They stopped at the pine, at my mark, and I watched Voss crouch and touch the cut sigil with two fingers, and I watched her go very still.

Then she laughed. It was a short, cracked sound, half disbelief, and she said something to my father I couldn't catch, and he said something back, and then she looked up—not around, not searching, but *directly at the ruins of the hut where I was hiding*, the way you look at a door you've closed on something you love—and she said, clearly, across the whole fogged clearing:

"She's reading Halloway. She's got the amplitude figures. And she wants us to follow."

My stone of fear turned over in my stomach and became, for one impossible second, something almost like pride.

I stood up out of the ruins before I could think better of it, knife still in my hand, four days of cold rice and dead men's arithmetic at my back, and said the first thing that came into my head:

"You lied to the Trust."

Voss looked at me—this girl with a knife, this runaway, this stranger's daughter—and didn't flinch, and didn't apologize, and didn't lie.

"Yes," she said. "And you're going to show me why I was right to."
