## 11. Reunion at the Broken Gauge

The gauge had been dead longer than the girl had been missing. That was my first thought when we came around the bend of the gorge and saw it: a rusted iron ladder bolted to the cliff face, a staff plate gone green with algae, and the whole assembly sheared off at the third rung like something enormous had leaned on it and gotten bored. Below it, the river ran shallow over pale stone, running a finger along its own old scars.

"Broken gauge," Odell said, unnecessarily. He'd been saying things all morning—naming rocks, naming bends, naming his own exhaustion—and I understood it was how he kept from screaming. A cartographer names things. It's a form of prayer.

The Senn elders had come this far with us and no farther. Old Vess, who walked like her knees were negotiating separately with gravity, stopped at the lip of the narrows and put one hand flat against the rock.

"She's here," Vess said. "The water's been holding its breath since dawn, but past that bend it lets a little out. Like someone talking under a door."

I didn't ask how she knew. I'd spent two days in Senn learning not to ask. The village remembered water the way other places remembered wars—with shrines, with prohibitions, with an oral archive of floods so detailed you could date a bad year by the height marks on the chapel door. They had fed us eels and suspicion in equal measure, and then, when Odell showed them Mara's note with its copied sigils, they had given us something better: a guide of their own choosing, which turned out to be Vess, and a warning, which turned out to be about me.

"You lie like a paid woman," she'd told me at the dock, cheerfully. "Because you are one."

She hadn't been wrong. I was carrying Dall's money in an oilskin pouch against my ribs, three hundred in trust scrip, and every step upstream it weighed more. By the time we reached the broken gauge it weighed as much as the pack.

* * *

We found her above the gauge, in the wreckage of the old survey camp, and I want to say it was dramatic—that she stood up silhouetted against the sky, that there was a moment of recognition, of relief. There wasn't. What happened was this: Odell shouted her name and his voice cracked in the middle of it like a dropped plate, and Mara poked her head out of a collapsed survey tent looking annoyed, the way teenagers do when reality interrupts them mid-thought, and said:

"Dad. You brought *her*?"

Then she saw Vess behind us and revised her tone slightly downward, from outrage to caution, which told me she'd been raised right even if she'd been raised stubborn.

She was filthy. Her hair was full of pine needles and her hands were stained blue-black with ink, and around her neck, on a cord, hung a wooden tablet covered in characters I recognized before I wanted to: the river-sigil, the one from the cave, cut deep and worn smooth, the kind of thing you make when you've been copying something for months and your hands start doing it without permission.

"Mara," Odell started, and then apparently had prepared nothing after the name, because he just stood there doing the thing fathers do, which is being simultaneously furious and grateful and unable to sequence the two.

"I left a note," Mara said.

"A note quoting a cipher," Odell said. "In a language nobody reads."

"*Somebody* reads it." She looked at me when she said it. Not accusingly. Worse—expectantly. Like I was a locked box she'd already picked and was waiting for me to notice.

Vess lowered herself onto a fallen beam with the sigh of a woman settling in for entertainment. "Go on, then," she said to Mara. "Say what you dragged us up a gorge to say."

Mara took a breath. She was sixteen and she stood like she was forty and certain, which is a dangerous age to be either.

"You went into the cave," she said. "Three days ago. You came out and you told the harbor office it was unstable. Cracked vault, risk of collapse, recommend sealing. I read the report. My father keeps copies of everything filed against Trust property—it's a habit, he thinks I don't know." She glanced at Odell, who had developed a sudden interest in his boots. "And it's a lie. Because the marks inside aren't collapse-marks. They're route-marks. And whoever cut them knew exactly what they were doing, because they're expedition-grade, and there hasn't been an expedition on this coast in twenty years."

The wind came down the gorge and moved the river's surface, and for a second the water seemed to hesitate, the way it had been hesitating for days, breathing shallow.

"So," Mara said, folding her arms. "Either you're the worst liar on this coast, or you lied anyway. Which is it?"

I could have managed it. That's the ugly truth I keep having to look at. I could have said the report was preliminary, that the cave showed surface cracking consistent with deeper instability, that sealing was precautionary. I have talked my way through avalanches, border checkpoints, and one memorable tribunal in Santiago where the truth would have cost me everything and a well-shaped sentence cost me only my self-respect, which was already spent. Lying is a skill. It doesn't atrophy.

But Vess was watching me with those river-colored eyes, and Odell was watching me like a man watching a scale tip, and Mara was sixteen and had walked away from everything safe to follow marks her father helped bury, and somewhere in the last two days I had gotten tired in a way that wasn't physical.

"The cave's sound," I said. "The vault's intact. The marks are expedition-grade because they're mine. I cut some of them nineteen years ago, and I recognized the rest, and I reported the cave as dangerous because Corvane Dall paid me three hundred scrip to say it was dying, and I took the money because I'm broke and blacklisted and I told myself a sealed cave hurts no one."

Silence. The good kind and the bad kind, both at once.

"That's it?" Mara said. "That's the whole confession?"

"You want the part where I hate myself? There's a lot of it. It gets repetitive."

Something happened in her face then—not forgiveness, nothing so finished. Recalculation. She'd built a story about me in that tent, months of decoding her father's papers with a stranger-shaped hole in the middle of them, and now the stranger had stepped into the shape and she had to decide whether the fit was real.

"You led the Quintana expedition," she said. "Upstream source survey, nineteen years ago. Funded through a shell company. My father has the correspondence."

"Mara," Odell said quietly.

"No. She should know I know." She turned back to me, chin up. "That expedition ended with two people dead and your license burned, and everyone says it was your rope, your call, your storm. But the funding trail goes through Dall's syndicate, and my father's letters say the gear manifests were falsified before you ever left base camp. So either you killed two people, or you were handed a rope that was already cut and took the blame for nineteen years. I came up here to find out which."

"And?"

"And you just told me you take money to lie." Her voice wobbled, once, and steadied. "So why should I believe you now?"

It was, I had to admit, an excellent question. The kind I'd have asked at sixteen, if I'd been braver than I was, which I wasn't.

"Because it costs me," I said. "Everything I've said to you today makes my life worse. The report I filed can be used to charge me with fraud. The name I gave you puts me back in front of the people who ruined me. If I were managing my survival, I'd still be lying. I'm tired of surviving."

Vess made a small sound that might have been a laugh or a cough. "There it is," she said. "Honest wreckage. Now you can build on it."

* * *

We sat in the wreck of the survey camp while Mara showed us what she'd found, and I'll tell you the strange thing: it felt like a briefing I'd once given, nineteen years ago, to a team that didn't come home. Same gestures. Same charcoal on flattened paper. She'd reconstructed the aquifer from her father's sealed surveys—the living source, the breathing chamber, the network of sluices cut into the gorge by engineers who understood that the river was a creature and not a resource. And laid over it, in red ink in a child's careful hand, the pumping lines. Eleven years of extraction. The river's shallow breath made visible.

"They're bleeding it," she said. "Dall's mills, the syndicate's power contracts—the whole harbor runs on water that was never his. And when the aquifer fails, it won't drain politely. It'll collapse. Take the valley floor with it. Senn first."

"Senn knows," Vess said. "Senn has always known. We remember what the water did before the pumps. We remember what it promised to do after."

Odell hadn't spoken since the confession. He sat with the wooden tablet in his hands, turning it over, reading his own buried work in his daughter's hand-carved copy. When he finally looked up at me, he looked older than he had at the dock, and lighter, somehow, too.

"I can't undo the report," he said. "What I filed, what I buried. Nineteen years of silence doesn't come back clean."

"No," I said. "It comes back wet."

He almost smiled. "Then we go to the source. All of us. And we open whatever needs opening, and I testify to whatever needs testifying, and if the valley drowns my maps, then the maps were lies anyway."

Mara was watching us the way you watch weather change—wary, ready to be disappointed, unwilling to say hope out loud in case it heard.

"It's four days' walk to the upper station," she said. "Five, with him." A jerk of her chin at Odell, affectionate and merciless. "And Dall's closed the gorge road as of yesterday. I watched the crew nail the notices up. Trespass, hazard, Trust authority." She paused. "They also posted a description of a woman answering to Petra Voss. Reward attached."

"How much?" Vess asked.

"Fifty."

Vess snorted. "Insulting."

"He knows what I cost," I said. "Cheap is his way of saying he expects me to come cheap."

Mara slung her pack—too big for her, packed by someone who'd planned to live rough for weeks—and stood. For a moment she looked at me across the broken gauge, across the shallow breathing river, across nineteen years of other people's lies, and held out the wooden tablet on its cord.

"You read these," she said. "Better than I do. So lead."

I took it. The wood was warm from her skin, and heavier than wood should be, the way true things get heavy when you finally hold them.

Behind us, downstream, the river exhaled—a long, low rush, as if something far below had turned over in its sleep. Vess cocked her head, listening, and her face went carefully blank in the way of people who have lived their whole lives downstream of decisions made by others.

"That's not the tide," she said. "That's the pumps. They've changed their rhythm. Someone upstream is pulling harder than they ever have."

Mara met my eyes. "They know we're coming," she said. "Or they know we're close enough. Either way—we should move tonight."

I looked up the gorge, where the fog was already gathering in the narrows like a held breath, and thought about ropes, and storms, and the exact weight of a decision made in bad light. Then I tightened the straps on my pack, and led them upstream into the dark.
