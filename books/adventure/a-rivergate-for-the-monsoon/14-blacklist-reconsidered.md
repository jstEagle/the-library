## 14. Blacklist, Reconsidered

The satchel sat between us on the floor of the Senn granary like a sleeping animal we were all pretending not to look at.

I'd seen that kind of quiet before. On expedition tents the night before a summit push, when everyone knows the weather window is closing and nobody wants to be the first to say it. The silence isn't peace. It's arithmetic being done in the dark.

Mara had spread her father's papers across three flour sacks and was copying figures by lamplight with the fierce neatness of someone who believes handwriting is a moral act. Odell sat with his splinted arm cradled against his chest — the rockfall had cost him a collarbone and most of his composure — watching her the way you watch water rise around your boots. The elders had gone to their signal posts. Ilya was asleep against a grain bin, still smelling faintly of the river he'd nearly given himself to, one hand curled around the strap of the satchel even in sleep, as if the evidence might bolt like a horse.

And me, I was doing my own arithmetic.

Here is what was in the satchel, for the record I would eventually give, under oath, to people who did not want to hear it:

Expedition ledgers from the Voss ascent, seven years old, bearing Corvane Dall's signature as funding principal. Correspondence showing he had ordered the rope replaced with cheaper line two days before the storm. A survey report — Odell's own hand, though he hadn't known then what it would feed — mapping an aquifer beneath the gorge, living, breathing, vast enough to drink a valley or drown one. And pumping manifests, years of them, documenting every liter Dall's syndicate had bled out of it while Rivergate's wells went salt and its tide went strange.

Enough to clear my name. Enough, if I walked into any inquiry office on the coast and laid it on the desk, to end the blacklist, restore my certifications, put me back on mountains where the air is honest and nothing remembers your face.

That was option one. It had a shape I knew. It had always had a shape I knew: get out, get clear, get gone.

Option two had no shape at all yet. Option two was the sluices.

* * *

"You're doing it again," Mara said, without looking up from her copying.

"Doing what."

"The thing where you stare at the wall like it owes you money." She dipped her pen. "You've been doing it since the gauge. Since before the gauge, probably. My father does it too. It's a guide thing, I think. You all believe that if you look at a problem long enough and hard enough, it'll solve itself out of embarrassment."

"Sometimes it works," I said.

"Has it ever worked?"

"Not once," I admitted.

She set down her pen and turned to face me fully, and I braced myself, because Mara at full attention was worse than any storm I'd ever been paid to walk into. Sixteen years old and she looked at people the way her maps looked at terrain: completely, unsparingly, with an assumption that everything worth knowing was knowable if you just refused to flinch.

"You could leave tonight," she said. "Take the coast road. Be in Harrow's Ford by morning, inquiry office by noon, cleared by winter. We both read the same papers. Don't tell me you haven't done the math."

"I've done the math."

"And?"

"And the math says the pumps run another four days before they crack the aquifer's spine. After that it doesn't matter what anyone testifies, because there won't be a valley left to belong to." I turned my palms up. "That's the math."

"So you stay." She said it simply, the way she'd say *the river bends north here*, and something in my chest twisted sideways, because no one had ever assumed I'd stay before. Not once in seven years. People assumed I'd leave the way water assumes downhill. It was the one law everyone trusted about me.

"It's not decided," I said.

"It's decided. You just haven't told yourself yet." She picked her pen back up. "For what it's worth, I think you should clear your name. I think you deserve it. I also think you won't do it while those pumps are running, because you're the kind of person who can't." A pause. "My father's the other kind. He took the money. So don't ask him which way to go. Ask him why he didn't go the right way when it was cheap, and then do the opposite of whatever excuse he gives you."

"Mara—"

"He's my father and I love him," she said, in a voice like a slammed door opening onto a lit room, "and he helped ruin you, and I'm allowed to say both things in the same breath. That's what this valley taught me. The water holds everything. It doesn't sort it first."

She went back to her copying. I sat with what she'd handed me, which was heavier than the satchel and fit in no pack.

* * *

Odell found me at the granary door near midnight, where I was watching the fog come up the gorge like something being poured.

"She's harder on me than I deserve," he said.

"She's easier on you than you deserve."

"Yes." He settled beside me against the doorframe, careful of the arm. For a while neither of us spoke. The fog moved. Somewhere below, the river made a sound I had learned in the last week to hear properly — not rushing but *breathing*, slow and labored, a lung working against weight.

"When I took Dall's money," Odell said, "it was for the maps. Not vanity — need. Mara's mother was sick, and ink and vellum don't buy themselves, and Dall came to me with a commission so simple it felt like mercy. Survey the upper gorge. Report the water table as static. One sentence of falsehood, he said, and your daughter never wants for anything." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as old paper. "One sentence. That's all complicity ever costs at the start. They quote you a single word and let you discover the rest of the sentence yourself, over years, at interest."

"I know how it works," I said. "I signed contracts too."

"Yours had ropes in it."

"Mine had ropes in it," I agreed.

He was quiet a moment. Then: "The inquiry. If you take the records to the coast — you understand what happens? Dall's lawyers will bury the pumping manifests under motion after motion. The aquifer dies on schedule. Your name gets cleared posthumously, in a sense — cleared of a crime, attached to a corpse. And you'll be free to guide anywhere in the world except anywhere that matters."

"I've thought about it."

"Have you thought about the other thing? What happens if you use it as leverage instead? Because leverage cuts both ways, Petra. Dall reads a threat to expose him and he stops negotiating and starts hunting. Properly hunting. He's already closed the road. By dawn there will be men in the narrows with lamps and rifles, and they won't be carrying warrants."

"I know."

"And you'd trade your vindication for that? For a fight you might lose, in a valley that isn't yours, for people who watched you arrive broke and anonymous and offered you fish guts and suspicion?"

I looked at him. Seven years of rehearsing this exact conversation with every ghost I owned, and now it was here, wearing a cartographer's tired face, and the answer came out of me before the rehearsal could stop it:

"They fed me," I said. "Badly, and with suspicion, but they fed me. The boy followed me into a cave instead of reporting me. The elders gave us beds and signal fires. Your daughter decided I was worth the truth inside of a day." I shook my head. "Nobody's ever assumed I'd stay, Odell. Do you know what that does to a person? Being the one who's expected to leave? You start believing leaving is your nature instead of your wound."

The fog poured past us. Down in the dark, the river breathed.

"If I take this to the coast," I said, "I win. Cleanly. Name restored, record wiped, and every year afterward I'll stand on some honest mountain knowing I traded a living valley for a clean ledger, and I'll deserve the knowing. If I stay—" I laughed, short and unamused. "If I stay, maybe I lose everything including the name anyway. Maybe Dall's hunters find us in the fog and this ends badly and the last thing written about Petra Voss is that she died upstream of every map, over some water."

"But?"

"But the water's real." I heard my own voice go strange. "I stood in the cavern, Odell. I felt it breathe. Everything else in my life has been stone and paperwork and men with signatures. This is the first true thing I've been offered in seven years, and it's asking me to choose it over the only vindication I've ever wanted." I looked at him. "That's not a choice. That's a trap with two doors, and both doors are me."

"No," Odell said quietly. "Both doors are the valley. You just live in only one of them."

* * *

Dawn came gray and wrong, the fog so thick the granary seemed to float. Ilya woke, ate half a loaf without speaking, and then looked at me with those eyes that had followed me into the cave and said, "They're in the narrows already. I heard the dogs before light."

"How many?"

"More than the fires we have."

Mara finished her last copy, blotted it, and divided the papers into three bundles with a cartographer's economy: originals, copies, and the decoding key, each going to a different pocket on different bodies. "So no one death loses everything," she said, matter-of-fact, and none of us corrected her phrasing, because correcting it would have meant looking at it.

Then she brought me the original expedition ledger, Dall's signature on the rope order, and held it out.

"This is the one that clears you," she said. "The single page. You could carry just this and run."

I took it. The paper was soft with age, the ink browned, the signature confident as a man signing someone else's death. I had spent seven years imagining this document without knowing it existed — building my freedom out of a page I couldn't produce, the way prisoners build windows out of habit.

I could feel the whole coast waiting behind it. Inquiry rooms. Restored certifications. My name spoken without the flinch. All of it, one page wide, one night's walk away.

And behind me, downstream, Rivergate waking to salted wells and a tide that owed nothing to the moon. And above me, the source, holding its breath against the pumps. And around me, a girl who'd decided I was worth the truth, a boy who'd stowed away into a flood, an old man holding a gate with one good arm, elders lighting fires for strangers.

I put the page back in the bundle.

"Not yet," I said. "First the sluices. Then the testimony. In that order, or not at all."

Mara nodded once, like a colleague. Odell closed his eyes briefly, the relief of a man watching someone else refuse the bribe he once accepted.

It was Ilya who said what we were all not saying, standing at the granary door with the fog pressing gray against the gray world outside:

"The dogs are closer than the source."

He was right. Through the fog, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of boots on the gorge road — many boots, moving fast, and a whistle cutting through the mist in three sharp notes that meant *close the net.*

I slung the satchel, checked the knife at my belt, and looked at the three faces that had somehow become, without my permission or my notice, the first pack I'd had in seven years.

"Then we move now," I said, "and we pray the elders' fires burn brighter than Dall's lamps. Everyone up. We go for the source — and if the fog wants us, the fog can learn what I learned on the ice: nothing catches a guide who finally has somewhere to be."

We stepped out into the white, and somewhere ahead of us, three notes whistled again — closer now, and answered, this time, by firelight flaring high on the gorge walls.
