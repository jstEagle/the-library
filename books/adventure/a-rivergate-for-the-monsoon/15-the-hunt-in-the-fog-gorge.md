## 15. The Hunt in the Fog Gorge

The fog came up the gorge at dusk like something with an appointment to keep.

I'd seen fog on six continents. I'd been pinned by whiteout at nineteen thousand feet, had walked through cloud so thick on the Cordillera Sarmiento that I navigated by the sound of my own boots. But this was different. This fog rose *from* the river, breathed out of the water itself, as if the aquifer we were running toward was exhaling through every crack in the valley's throat. It filled the narrows between the cliffs in minutes, erasing the trail, the cairns, the far bank, everything except the sound of the river below us—low and strained, still holding its breath, still waiting for someone brave enough or stupid enough to open its lungs.

"Petra." Odell's voice came out of the gray a hand's width from my shoulder. He was breathing hard. His arm was still strapped from the rockfall, three days of walking on it doing him no favors. "Lanterns. Behind us. Two, maybe three."

I turned. Far down the narrows, where the old survey road clung to the cliff above the water, points of light swung and bobbed. Not lanterns carried steady, the way you carry a light when you're walking home. Swung wide, the way you carry one when you're searching.

Dall's hunters. They'd closed the gorge road behind us that morning—officially for "slip damage"—and I'd known then that closing a road is what a man does when he wants the people on it to stop being a problem and start being a target.

"How far?" Odell asked.

"An hour. Less if they know the ground." I looked at the fog, then at the black shape of the cliffs, then at the satchel strap across my chest, where the expedition records rode against my ribs—the ones that named Corvane Dall as the funder of the climb that killed two of my team and my career. "They don't need to catch us, Odell. They just need the satchel."

"Then we don't get caught."

"We don't get caught," I agreed, and hated how easy the lie sounded. Because here's the arithmetic of a chase: the hunted carries everything, the hunters carry nothing, and the ground decides who tires first. The ground tonight belonged to the river.

* * *

We moved fast through the fog, which is to say we moved blind and called it fast. Mara set the pace—she knew this stretch from her father's maps better than any of Dall's men could know it from a lifetime of walking it—and she read the sigils Odell had chalked on stone at each bend, the ones she'd left on our way upstream, now our only thread back toward the pumping station. Her sixteen-year-old certainty was a lamp of its own. I remembered being that sure of anything and felt the loss like a missing tooth.

The Senn elders had split off at dusk, which I'd thought was them going home. It wasn't.

The first signal fire bloomed maybe twenty minutes later—a sudden orange stain in the fog to our left, high on the opposite slope, so bright it threw our shadows across the trail. Then another, downstream, closer to the hunters' lights. Then a third, upstream, behind *us*, and I understood.

"They're lighting fires along both banks," I said. "Every fire looks like us stopping to camp. Every fire looks like a silhouette against flame."

Old Maren's voice drifted down through the fog, cracked and unhurried, singing something in the valley dialect—the water-memory songs, the ones the elders used to sing to a river that could hear. From the hunters' side of the narrows, it must have sounded like voices everywhere. Like the whole gorge had grown people overnight.

One of the lanterns stopped. Hung in the fog, uncertain. A second joined it. Men calling to each other, too far to make out words, only the tone—men discovering that the dark they'd entered was not empty.

"It won't hold them long," Odell said. "Dall pays too well for doubt to last."

"Then we spend the time buying," I said. "There's a crossing ahead. The weir below the broken gauge. If we take the near bank past it, the trail dies in scree. No footprints, no chalk marks, no road."

"And if they guess?"

"Then they guess wrong in the dark, same as us."

* * *

The weir announced itself before we saw it—a long, low seam of white noise in the black, the river spilling over the old stones with a sound like sustained applause. The fog was thicker here, fed by the spray, and the footing was wet granite worn glass-smooth by a century of floods. I went first, testing each step, the satchel heavy against my chest, and I want to be honest about what I was thinking, because what I was thinking was: *this is exactly the kind of crossing where things go wrong.* Wet rock, bad light, tired people, a river underneath with opinions. I have buried people on crossings gentler than this one.

Mara came second. Odell third, favoring his arm, swearing under his breath in cartographer's Latin, which is apparently a thing.

And behind us, small and quick and absolutely not supposed to exist, came a fourth set of footsteps.

I heard him before I saw him—the rhythm wrong, too light, too eager. I turned in the fog and there was Ilya, the boy from the cave, twelve years old and soaked to the waist, grinning at me like the grin alone would keep him alive.

"You—" I said, and found I had no sentence prepared. "You stowed away. Since when?"

"Since Senn." He shrugged, water dripping off his sleeves. "You needed someone fast. And I wanted to see the source."

"The source can kill you."

"So can staying in Rivergate, if the Trust has its way." He said it simply, without drama, the way kids say true things, and I felt the truth of it land somewhere under my ribs. This was a boy whose town was being drained dry by men with ledgers, and he'd done the math himself.

I should have sent him back. There was no sending him back. The hunters' lanterns were already moving again below us, and the fog was full of Maren's songs, and the only way out of the narrows was forward.

"Stay behind me," I told him. "Touch nothing. Argue with nothing. If I say run, you run."

"Yes, ma'am." He saluted. I decided not to kill him.

* * *

It went wrong, of course. Things go wrong; that's the one law of mountains and rivers I've never seen repealed.

It happened at the tail of the weir, where the old gauge station leaned out over the water on rotten pilings—a shattered wooden skeleton, the gauge itself long since snapped, the numbers on its face eaten blank. The crossing narrowed there to a ledge barely wider than my boot, slick with spray, and beyond it the scree slope began. We were halfway across the ledge when the lanterns rounded the bend below—not scattered anymore, not fooled, but gathered and climbing, four or five lights moving with purpose.

"They've found the trail," Odell breathed.

"Then we stop being quiet." I pushed past him, hauling Mara toward the scree. "Run. Now. Scree, then the high shelf, then—"

Ilya stumbled.

It was nothing—a wet boot on wet stone, a boy's weight gone sideways—but the ledge gave him nowhere to go, and the river took the rest. He slid off the edge with a sound too small for what it meant, and the white water folded over him like a door closing.

I don't remember deciding. I remember the satchel strap coming over my head, and I remember shouting—"*Take it! Take it and run!*"—thrusting the records into Mara's arms, and then I was flat on the ledge with my arm in the river to the shoulder, and the river had Ilya.

Cold doesn't describe it. The water coming off the aquifer's throat was meltwater cold, glacier cold, the kind of cold that stops being temperature and becomes a hand around your bones. My fingers found fabric. Found an arm. Found a grip. And the river pulled, patient and enormous, the way it must have pulled for ten thousand years, and I dug my knees into granite and held on and understood, with perfect clarity, that I had once made this exact choice differently—that on a mountain seven years ago, with a rope and a storm and a man falling, I had let the arithmetic win, and I had never once forgiven myself, and here was the universe handing me the same exam.

"Hold!" Odell was on my legs, anchoring me, screaming over the water. "MARA—THE STRAP—GIVE HER THE STRAP!"

A rope of some kind—my spare coil, thrown, caught—came across my back. I got it under my arms. And Ilya, in the white water, did the thing that saved us both: he stopped fighting. I felt it happen through my grip—the thrashing dying, the boy going limp and smart, letting the water push him toward me instead of away, using the river's own strength. Some instinct older than instruction. The village children grew up on this water. Maybe the water remembered him.

I hauled. Odell hauled. Between the three of us and one merciful god of wet granite, we dragged him onto the ledge, blue-lipped and coughing, alive.

But the satchel—

"Mara," I said. "Where's—"

She was standing at the lip of the scree, and she was looking at the water, and my stomach dropped through the world, because the satchel was gone. Torn from my shoulder in the slide, or dropped in the reach—I didn't know. The records. Dall's name. The proof of everything. Somewhere in that black current, riding toward the sea and the Trust's lawyers and oblivion.

"No," Odell whispered.

And then Ilya, still coughing, still shaking, lifted one hand and pointed downstream—to the shallows below the weir, where the current slackened against a gravel bar.

Something pale was snagged there. Turning slowly. The satchel's strap, hooked on a dead branch, the bag half-submerged, filling, sinking by degrees.

Nobody else could reach it. The bar sat midstream, cut off by channels too deep to wade and too fast to swim. Except—

"I can," Ilya said. His teeth were chattering. His eyes were enormous. "The eddy comes around the bar. I've swum worse in the harbor. Give me a line."

"Absolutely not," I said.

"They'll be here in a minute." He nodded downstream at the lanterns, closer now, their light smearing yellow through the fog. "Everything's in that bag. You said. Everything."

He was right, and I hated him for it, and I tied the line around his chest with hands that wouldn't quite obey me, knotting it the way I'd knotted ropes for climbers I loved, checking it twice, three times, because this time—*this time*—the gear would not fail and the judgment would not fail and nobody was going into that water twice.

"In on the eddy, out on my pull," I told him. "Ten seconds. If the bag fights you, leave it."

"It won't fight me," he said, and dove.

I have stood at the top of unclimbed faces with less fear in my mouth. Ten seconds. The fog swallowed him whole—one pale flash in the churning water, gone. Odell counted aloud, his voice cracking on eight, and on nine the line went taut and I pulled, and Ilya surfaced gasping with one arm locked through the satchel strap, and we brought him in together, and he lay on the granite spitting river water and holding the bag above his head like a trophy, laughing his half-drowned laugh.

Above us, on the far bank, the first signal fire flared again—closer now, deliberately close, Maren's silhouette bent over the flames like a woman tending a kitchen stove rather than staging a rescue. The hunters' lanterns stopped. Wavered. Turned aside, drawn toward the fire, toward the song, toward the dozen phantom camps the elders had lit along both banks of the gorge.

By the time the fog closed behind us and the scree took our footprints and gave nothing back, the lights below were hunting shadows.

* * *

We made the high shelf an hour before dawn and stopped because we had to, not because it was safe. Ilya shook under every blanket we owned. Mara sat with the satchel in her lap, drying the records page by page over a candle stub, her hands steadier than mine. Odell slept sitting up against a boulder, arm cradled, exhausted past dignity.

I sat at the edge of the fireless dark and did the accounting I always do after a river tries to keep someone. Two nearly drowned tonight: a boy and a truth. Both saved by a child's stubbornness and a stranger's songs. I had come to Rivergate seven weeks ago with a duffel bag and a rule about traveling alone, and the rule had seemed so reasonable at the time. Rules usually do, right up until a twelve-year-old dives into a flood channel for your evidence and an old woman burns signal fires all night for a woman she's known a fortnight.

"You're staring at him," Mara said quietly, beside me. "Like you're trying to figure out what he cost you."

"Something like that."

"He'd do it again."

"I know. That's what scares me." I watched the boy sleep. "I spent seven years making sure I owed nobody and nobody owed me. It's very clean. Very portable. You should try it sometime."

"It sounds lonely."

"It is. That's the feature."

She almost smiled. In the candlelight she looked suddenly, unbearably like her father—the same stubborn jaw, the same way of looking at a lie until it confessed. "The elders," she said. "Maren. The others. They didn't ask you if you wanted help tonight. They just... came."

"That's what a pack does."

"I thought you didn't run with packs."

"I didn't." I looked down the gorge, toward the pumping station waiting for us in the dark, toward the source that breathed beneath it, toward whatever Dall had left between us and the sluices. "Turns out I wasn't asked."

Below us, far off through the fog, one last signal fire bloomed and died—Maren's goodnight, or her warning. Either way, it meant the same thing: *we see you. Keep moving.*

I woke the boy gently, shouldered the satchel, and led my pack down toward the water.
