## 18. Sluice One

The aquifer chamber had a way of making you whisper even when there was no one to disturb. The water rose and fell in the great dark like something sleeping on its side, breathing, and every breath moved the air against your face with a warmth that felt almost deliberate. Mara stood at the first sluice wheel with her decoded pages flattened against her chest, and I said the words I'd been rehearsing since we left the broken gauge.

"Sequence starts now. Once it starts, it doesn't stop."

"I know," she said. "I've known for three days."

"You've known for three days at sixteen. That's not the same as knowing."

She gave me a look that Odell must have patented, the one that says *I have read more of your maps than you have*, and put both hands on the wheel.

Behind us, down the long throat of the approach tunnel, came the sound I'd been listening for all night and pretending I wasn't: boots. More than two pairs. Metal on wet stone, unhurried. Dall's hunters didn't hurry. Hurrying was for people who thought they might lose.

* * *

Let me back up an hour, because arithmetic is easier if you show your work.

The sequence, per Mara's decoding, was four gates. One, three, two, four — the river's own order, the order the old village ritual used, the order that let the aquifer swell into its channels gradually instead of detonating through them like a shell through wet paper. Each gate opened by a wheel-and-chain mechanism built by people who'd been dead so long their language survived only as sigils. Each gate, once opened, stayed open; there were no closing wheels, only opening ones, because the people who built this place had never imagined anyone would want to shut it again.

The catch — there is always a catch, and I say that with professional affection — was the fourth gate. Its mechanism sat inside a chamber that flooded as the third gate opened. You turned the fourth wheel from inside, and then you got out before the water finished arriving, through a passage that the survey marks suggested existed and did not guarantee.

*Suggested.* *Did not guarantee.* If I'd written expedition reports in that voice my whole career, I'd still be employed.

So the plan, such as it was: Mara opened one and three from the gallery ledge where the wheels sat above high water. I took two, which required a traverse across the chamber's east wall — yes, that traverse, the same geometry, the same rotten iron stanchions, because apparently the universe keeps one joke and tells it forever. Odell held the fourth wheel's position, keeping its chain tensioned from inside the chamber so the gate wouldn't jam when its turn came, and then climbed out through the suggested passage.

That was the plan. I want it on the record that the plan had everyone leaving the water. I have been accused of many things, most of them accurate, but I do not write plans that spend people. Not anymore.

The hunters arrived before we could test any of it.

* * *

They came up the approach tunnel with lanterns, six of them, and Corvane Dall at the front, which surprised me until it didn't. A man who has spent years draining a living river does not send clerks to watch it die. He came himself, coat buttoned, walking like a man entering his own counting house.

"Voss." His voice carried across the water without effort. "You're standing on the only thing in this valley worth anything, and you're about to break it."

"It's not a thing," Mara called back. "It's alive."

"Mara." He softened his voice for her, and I'll grant him this: he was good at it. It sounded almost paternal. "Your father understands what happens if that gate opens. Ask him."

Odell, crouched at the fourth wheel with a chain wrench in his hands, went very still. I watched him decide not to answer, and I understood the cost of that silence better than he knew.

"Dall," I said, "your pumps are killing the valley. You know it. Your ledgers know it. Even your hunters know it — they just don't know what else to do for wages."

"My mills employ four hundred people." He stepped closer to the waterline, lantern raised. The light caught the rising-falling surface, and for one unguarded second something like reverence crossed his face, and then was filed away. "You want to talk about killing? Open those gates wrong and you drown Senn. You drown the low fields. You drown Rivergate itself. I am the only man here trying to prevent a flood."

He believed it. That was the horror of Corvane Dall, the thing I'd finally understood somewhere in the fog gorge: he had spent so long telling himself the theft was stewardship that the story had become load-bearing. You couldn't argue him out of it. You could only out-argue the water, and the water doesn't negotiate.

"Mara," I said quietly. "Gate one."

She turned the wheel.

The sound was enormous and ancient, stone grinding against stone under centuries of mineral patience, and beneath it the aquifer answered — a deep subsonic shift, the whole chamber's breath catching. Water began to move in channels none of us could see, following paths older than the town, older than the Trust, older than any map Odell had ever falsified or preserved.

"Stop her!" Dall's voice cracked open. The hunters surged forward along the ledge.

And here is what I learned about arithmetic under pressure: it gets very simple very fast. Six hunters, one narrow ledge, one girl at a wheel who could not stop turning without jamming the mechanism, one injured cartographer at a chain, one disgraced guide with a piton hammer and no reputation left to lose.

I met them at the choke point where the ledge narrowed to a body's width. I didn't fight, exactly. Fighting implies exchange. I made the ledge expensive. A shoulder into the first man's chest, over the water he'd rather not fall into. A hammer swung flat to keep the second honest. They weren't killers — Dall's payroll ran to enforcers, not murderers, and men who enforce paperwork hesitate when the paperwork starts bleeding. The third one grabbed my wrist and I looked at him and said, "Son, I have drowned in worse places than this in my head four hundred times. You're new to me." He let go.

Behind me, gate one groaned fully open, and the aquifer exhaled.

* * *

Gate three was supposed to follow immediately. Mara got halfway through the turn when a thrown lantern — I never saw who threw it, and to this day I choose not to know — burst against the wheel housing and scattered burning oil across the gallery. She jumped clear. The wheel spun free, then seized, half-turned, jammed on a bent pawl.

The chamber changed. I felt it in my teeth before I heard it: the breathing rhythm stuttering, the water level lurching upward with a slop of displaced volume, the whole cavern recalculating around a gate stuck between states. Mara's pages said a half-open third gate meant pressure backing up into the second channel. Which meant my traverse route was about to be underwater. Which meant—

"Two!" I shouted. "I'm going for two now!"

"Petra—" Odell started.

"No time for the speech. Hold four!"

I clipped into the east wall anchors and went across the traverse with the water climbing beneath me, each stanchion announcing its opinion in rust-flakes and complaint. This was the maneuver. This exact maneuver, this angle of commitment, this moment where you pass the point of retreat and the rope is the only conversation left. In Karakoram-haze and storm-dark, eight years ago, this was where the anchor failed — not because of my knot, not because of my call, but because the funder had bought rated-for-show line and someone upstream had signed off on it, and I had carried that failure in my chest so long I'd mistaken it for an organ.

The stanchion held. My gear held. My hands, shaking, held. I reached the second wheel with the water licking the underside of the ledge and turned it, and gate two ground open, and the pressure in the chamber dropped like a held grudge released.

Which left four. And four needed its chain tensioned from inside the chamber, right now, or the whole sequence stalled with the valley's fate balanced on a bent pawl.

Odell was already moving.

* * *

I want to be precise about what happened next, because precision is the only apology I have left in me.

Odell climbed down into the fourth chamber with his chain wrench and his bad knee and thirty years of guilt, and he set the wrench on the wheel, and he kept the tension while the water rose around him — because the third gate, jammed half-open, was feeding that chamber faster than the schedule allowed. The suggested-existence passage out was already taking water at its lip.

"Odell! Out! Now!"

"In a minute!" He had that cartographer's calm, the one that treats catastrophe as a drafting error. "Chain slips if I let go. Give me—"

A hunter — Dall's own man, I saw it happen — tried to help. Reached down from the gallery rim to take the wrench. And the rockfall came, whether shaken loose by the opening gates or by our six sets of boots on a ledge never built for argument, I couldn't tell you. Stone came down the chamber's throat like a verdict. The hunter scrambled back. Odell did not.

The rock caught his leg below the knee and pinned him against the wheel housing, and the water kept rising, patient as arithmetic.

* * *

Here is the equation, laid out flat, because I promised you work shown:

Someone must hold the fourth chain until gate three can be cleared and the sequence restored — Mara at the jammed wheel, prying the pawl with a piton, minutes away, maybe. Someone must then turn gate four from inside a chamber that floods when it opens. And the exit passage, the *suggested* one, was now drowning.

One person inside. One job that cannot be handed off mid-task. One exit that may or may not exist.

Odell looked up at me through the spray, pinned, water at his waist, and said, "Petra. The passage."

"I see it."

"Do you?"

"I see it," I said, and hated how much I sounded like a survey report.

Dall stood at the far edge of the gallery, lantern dead, watching his empire's machinery fill with the thing he'd sworn he was preventing. For a moment our eyes met across the water, and I saw the last disguise burn off him — not the steward, not the magistrate, just a man watching numbers come due. He mouthed something. I think it was *stop*. I think he meant everything.

Mara screamed from the wheel housing — "The pawl's giving! Thirty seconds!" — and the aquifer breathed in around us, vast and waiting, and the water in the fourth chamber climbed another handspan up Odell's chest.

He met my eyes. He didn't ask me to save him. He asked me, with a small terrible nod toward the fourth wheel, to finish it.

And I understood, standing on the ledge with my hammer loose in my hand, that sacrifice had stopped being a question of courage and become a question of subtraction. Someone pays. The river doesn't care who. The only variable left was which name I could live inside afterward — and I had already lived inside the worst one for eight years, so I knew exactly what I was choosing between.

I drove a piton into the gallery rim, clipped my rope, and went down into the water toward Odell and the wheel, shouting the only order that mattered:

"Mara — when that pawl clears, you turn three, and you don't look down. Whatever happens next, you don't look down."

The water closed over my boots, warm as breath, and began to rise.
