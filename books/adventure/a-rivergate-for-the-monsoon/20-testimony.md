## 20. Testimony

They gave me a room with a window that faced the harbor, which I took as either a courtesy or a threat. From the third floor of the Rivergate customs house, you could see the old lighthouse, the cave mouth beneath it now gated with new timber, and the bay—full again, restless, the color of hammered tin. Three weeks since the sluices. The water had come back like a debt collector, and it had brought the monsoon season's first clouds with it, stacked on the horizon like furniture under a sheet.

I had not slept well. This was not unusual. What was unusual was that I had a clean shirt, borrowed from Odell, that almost fit, and a hearing at nine o'clock where the whole question of my life would be asked out loud by strangers with pens.

"You could still run," Ilya said. He'd appointed himself my escort, which meant he'd shown up at dawn with two rolls and an opinion.

"I'm done running."

"You say that like it's a skill you mastered."

"It's more of a practice. Like prayer, if prayer made you sweat."

He grinned and handed me a roll. We ate standing up, which is how I'd eaten for six years, and I thought, not for the first time, that I didn't know how to eat any other way. That was the thing nobody tells you about ruin. It teaches you habits that outlive it.

* * *

The inquiry was held in the customs house's long room, under a ceiling fan that turned with the conviction of something that had never once been in a hurry. Three commissioners sat at a table draped in green cloth. A stenographer. A clerk with the Trust's seized ledgers stacked beside him in boxes, the way you'd stack evidence of anything—grain theft, murder, a river.

Corvane Dall was not present. Dall was present nowhere anymore, except in the water, and in the ledgers, and in the particular silence that fell whenever his name was said in this town, a silence I'd learned to read as the sound of an empire being carefully not discussed.

The middle commissioner, a woman named Halvers with spectacles and the patience of a geologist, opened the proceedings by reading my name into the record. "Petra Voss. Formerly licensed in Nepal and Chile. Currently unlicensed anywhere." A pause. "Is that accurate?"

"It's accurate."

"Let the record show the witness smiled when she said it."

I hadn't realized I had. Old habit—smiling at my own indictment, like a dog that's been kicked often enough to wag preemptively.

They started with the original disaster. Of course they did. That was the door everyone wanted opened, the one I'd kept my shoulder against for six years, in Kathmandu and Santiago and a dozen towns between, until Rivergate had kicked it off its hinges for me.

Halvers laid it out with the flatness of someone reading a weather report. An expedition on the Khorpa massif, funded by the Meridian Survey Consortium. Four climbers. A storm. A rope. One death—Tomas Reyes, twenty-six years old, a strong climber and a better singer, who had taught me a song about a mule that I still could not hear without flinching. My license revoked on findings of "judgment failure under pressure." My name passed from agency to agency like a coin everyone suspected of being shaved.

"Ms. Voss," Halvers said. "For six years you have declined to give a full account. The Consortium's report attributes the accident to your decision to continue the ascent past the second col in deteriorating weather. Is that account true?"

Here it was, then. The room went quiet in the specific way rooms do when they're waiting to find out what kind of person you are.

I looked at my hands. They were steadier than I expected. Six years of gutting fish will give you hands like that—steady, scarred, smelling faintly of things you can't wash out.

"Parts of it," I said. "That's what made it such a good lie. Good lies are mostly true."

"Then tell us the whole truth. In your own order. We have the day."

* * *

So I told it. Not the version from the reports, not the version I'd rehearsed in a hundred cheap rooms, but the thing itself, walking pace, the way I'd finally told Odell in the gorge with the rockfall dust still settling.

The truth was that the weather window was real when we committed. That's the part people don't understand about mountains—they don't punish ambition, they punish arithmetic, and the arithmetic had said we had eleven hours and we needed nine. The truth was that the Consortium's funder, a man named Aldous Renk whose name I had not said aloud in six years and whose name now sits in the Trust's ledgers beside Corvane Dall's, had insisted on the summit push because a rival team was two weeks behind us and sponsorship renewals were due in March. The truth was that Renk had cut the rope budget—had specified lighter line, cheaper anchors, a smaller margin—and I had signed off on the equipment manifest because I wanted the summit too, and wanting things makes you a bad auditor.

"The gear failed at the traverse below the col," I said. "An anchor I placed held. The one Tomas was on did not. It was rated for the load. It should have held. It didn't, because the steel was soft, because the order was filled by the lowest bidder, because a man in an office decided that a margin of safety was a margin of profit."

"But you continued past the second col," Halvers said. "Against the forecast update. That was your call."

I had known this was coming. I had known it for six years, the way you know a tooth is going to have to come out.

"Yes. That was my call. And it was wrong." I made myself say it slowly, so I couldn't dress it up. "I had a forecast update at midnight that I chose to read optimistically. I wanted the line. I'd been chasing that line for three seasons. When the storm came in early, I was already past the point where turning around cost me the summit, and I told myself the col would give us shelter, and I made the arithmetic flatter itself. I sent Tomas onto that traverse in a building storm because I didn't want to come down with nothing. His rope was fine. His anchor was not. Both things are true. The bad steel killed him, and my judgment put him under it. I have spent six years telling only the first half, because the first half made me innocent, and I would rather be guilty of my actual share than innocent of a fiction."

The stenographer's keys had stopped. Then started again.

Halvers removed her spectacles, which I gathered was significant. "Why now? You had every opportunity to say this in Kathmandu. In Santiago. Why did the record stay half-told until Rivergate?"

Because I was a coward with a passport, I thought. Because innocence, even false innocence, is warm, and I'd wrapped myself in it like a coat I couldn't take off without freezing.

"Because for six years the truth cost everything and bought nothing," I said. "And then I came to a town where the truth was going to cost everything and buy a valley. It turns out that was the exchange rate I needed. I'm not proud of the arithmetic. But I'm done lying about it, including to this room, including the parts that make me look worse than the reports did."

"Your testimony makes you look considerably worse than the reports did," Halvers observed.

"Good," I said. "Then it's probably true."

* * *

The afternoon was the aquifer. The clerk brought out the Trust's ledgers, and I watched a room full of officials understand, page by page, what the water had already told them: eleven years of siphoning, the "flood control" assessments that were invoices in disguise, the mills in two provinces running on a river that appeared on no map because it had been purchased off every map. Odell testified after me—his voice steady, his hands not—about the survey he'd buried and the money he'd taken and the report he'd rewritten to put the disaster on my judgment, because Dall had asked and because Odell had a daughter and debts and the ordinary human genius for telling himself a smaller lie. He said my name when he apologized. He said it like it weighed something. I nodded, and that was all either of us needed, though the record will show I did not say *it's all right*, because it wasn't, and we're both too honest now to pretend otherwise.

Mara testified last. Sixteen years old, and she read her decoded sigils into the record with the flat authority of someone twice her age, and when a commissioner asked how she'd known to follow the marks upstream, she said, "Because they were true and nothing else around them was," and I thought: there it is. The disease. I'd had it at her age. I hoped hers would cost less than mine did.

The commissioners deliberated for two days, which everyone agreed was fast for an inquiry and slow for a town that had already made up its mind at the tavern.

The finding, when Halvers read it aloud, was not the absolution I'd once dreamed of in the bad years. That's the thing about the cleaner version of yourself—it's cleaner because it's been edited, and I had finally stopped editing. The record now read that the Khorpa disaster resulted from "a convergence of commercial negligence, falsified equipment certification, and errors of judgment by the expedition leader, for which the leader bears direct and acknowledged responsibility." My license was to be reinstated. My name was to be cleared. Neither word appeared with the word *blameless*, and I had asked, in writing, that they not.

Ilya was waiting on the customs house steps when I came out, with Mara beside him and the whole ridiculous pack of them—Odell, two of the Senn elders who had traveled downriver for the reading, even the harbormaster who'd once shorted my pay by a fish.

"Well?" Ilya said.

"Cleared. Complicated. Both at once."

"That sounds like you."

"It's the most accurate thing anyone's said to me all week."

Mara was holding something behind her back. She brought it around: a sheet of drafting paper, Odell's good ink, and drawn on it in the old expedition cipher—the one that had started all of this, the one that shouldn't have existed on this coast—was a new sigil. A river line, a source mark, and beneath it a small figure standing at the head of the water, arms open. Not triumphant. Just there. Holding the door.

"What's this?" I asked, though my throat had already told me.

"The gorge crossing," Mara said. "The town voted last night. First guided crossing of the reopened gorge, when the monsoon fills it. They want a guide." She looked at me with that dangerous idealism of hers, the kind that walks upstream into gorges without telling anyone. "They want you. I'm apprenticing. That part wasn't voted on. That part I decided."

I stood on the steps of the customs house with my name cleared and my record complicated and a piece of paper in my hand that was worth more than the license, and out over the bay the first real rain of the season was coming in off the water, fat and warm and unhurried, falling on Rivergate like something that had finally been allowed to come home.

"When do we leave?" I said.
