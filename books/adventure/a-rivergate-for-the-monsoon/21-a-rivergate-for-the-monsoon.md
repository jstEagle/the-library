## 21. A Rivergate for the Monsoon

The rain came on a Tuesday, which felt right. Rivergate had always struck me as a Tuesday kind of town—unremarkable on the surface, quietly holding everything together underneath.

I was in my father's shop when it started, re-inking a chart of the gorge that had been wrong for eleven years and was finally, blessedly, about to be wrong no longer. The first drops hit the skylight like fingers testing a lock. Then more fingers. Then fists. Then the whole sky leaning its weight against the glass, and I put down my pen and went outside and stood in the street like an idiot with my face turned up, along with half the town, all of us getting soaked and none of us caring.

You have to understand what the rain meant here. Rivergate had spent years pretending it wasn't drying up. Wells dropping, gardens going to salt, the river running thin as an apology through its own bed. We'd told ourselves stories—drought cycles, shifting currents, the sea keeping what it took. And then Petra Voss opened the sluices, drowned Corvane Dall's empire in its own stolen water, and stood in front of the inquiry and told the truth so completely, including the parts that made her look bad, that the truth had nowhere left to hide.

Three weeks after her testimony, the aquifer finished remembering itself. And now the sky was doing its part.

Old Senn elders came down from the valley two days later, walking behind a cart of seedlings, singing something low and repetitive that my father said was older than the charts. The river rose to meet them. By Friday you could hear it from the harbor trust steps—a sound the whole town had forgotten how to sleep without, that deep continuous throat-clearing of moving water.

And by Saturday, there was a notice nailed to the trust house door, in handwriting I recognized because I'd been studying it for months:

*Gorge crossing, first guided party, dawn Sunday. Twelve places. Bring boots you don't love.*

My father read it twice. "She's not charging," he said.

"She's never charging again," I said. "That's the point."

He looked at me over his spectacles with the expression he used when I'd said something true and inconvenient. "You've already asked her, haven't you."

"I asked her three weeks ago."

"And?"

"And she said no."

* * *

Here is what asking Petra Voss for anything is like. You go to her workroom above the fish shed—the same one where she gutted herring her first winter in town, back when she was nobody, which was a lie she maintained for almost a full week—and you find her mending rope, because she is always mending rope, and you say your piece while she keeps working, because stopping would mean the conversation mattered.

I said: "I want to apprentice. Properly. Not just decoding your sigils, not just carrying the satchel. The actual work."

She pulled a stitch tight. "The actual work got Odell trapped under a rockfall."

"The actual work also got him out."

Another stitch. Rain drummed on the roof like applause from a very large, very wet audience. "Mara. You're sixteen."

"I'm sixteen and I read your cipher before you knew I existed. I survived alone at a survey camp for nine days. I found the sluice sequence in a dead language's grammar. What exactly am I waiting to be old enough for?"

"Judgment," she said. "Which is the only thing this job actually requires. Everything else is knots."

"You didn't have judgment at sixteen either. You had a funder who cut corners and a rope team that trusted you anyway, and you learned judgment the way everyone learns it—which is badly, once, and forever after."

That landed. I watched it land. Her hands went still on the rope for the first time in the whole conversation, and I thought: good, and immediately after: oh no.

"Odell told you that story," she said quietly.

"Odell tells everyone that story now. He says it's penance." I made myself hold her eyes. "I'm not asking you to pretend the work is safe. I'm asking you to teach me anyway. That's the whole difference between you and Dall, isn't it? He hid the danger in paperwork. You'd just tell me where it is."

A long silence. Outside, the river said something in its new voice, deep and unhurried.

"Twelve places," she said finally. "One's yours. If you're late, I give it away."

"That's it? No speech?"

"Mara." She went back to her rope. "Speeches are how people avoid knots."

* * *

Sunday dawn came gray-green and breathing. Twelve of us at the gorge mouth: me; Ilya, who had stowed away on an expedition once and nearly drowned for a satchel and now treated both facts as credentials; two Senn elders who claimed they were coming to supervise the river, not us; a millwright, a midwife, a boy of maybe fourteen whose family had lost their orchard to the dry years; my father, who pretended he was there to survey but had brought a crossing pack like a man surrendering to a hobby; and six others whose faces I catalogued out of habit, because that's what four months around Petra does to you.

Petra stood on the wet rock at the gorge entrance with the rain coming off her hood in ropes, and gave the shortest safety briefing in the history of guiding.

"The water is high, the rock is new, and every handhold you see was placed last week by someone who loves this valley and is not a professional. Test everything. Trust nothing on the first touch. If I say a word you don't know, ask. There are no stupid questions in moving water, only expensive ones." A pause. "Questions?"

The millwright raised a hand. "Is it true you were blacklisted in two countries?"

"It's true I was blamed in two countries," Petra said. "Whether I did it is a longer story, and we have a gorge to cross. Move out."

And that—that—was the moment I understood she'd changed, or rather that she'd stopped changing herself to fit the shape other people needed. The old Petra, the one from the cave report, would have deflected or lied or gone cold. This one just let the question sit there in the rain, unanswered and unhidden, and walked into the gorge with it trailing behind her like a scarf.

* * *

The gorge had been closed eleven years. The Trust had locked the road, posted signs about instability, and let the place go feral—and feral, it turned out, meant alive. Ferns the size of cart wheels. Water threading down every wall. Our new cairns and bolts sitting alongside the old sigils like different generations of the same family arguing politely.

Petra led from the front but taught from everywhere. She'd stop the column at a slick slab and make each of us find our own line before she showed hers. She'd send me ahead on the easy stretches—not as a test, I realized later, but because being responsible for other people's eyes is the fastest way to learn what a guide actually sees. At the second traverse, over a chute of white water where the rock hummed under your palms, she clipped my harness herself and checked it twice and said nothing, and the nothing was louder than any lecture.

Halfway across that traverse, my boot skated.

It was nothing—one wet grain of granite, a half-second slide, my weight catching on the line before it became anything. But I heard the whole column inhale, and I heard Petra's voice come back along the rope, level as a spirit gauge: "Weight on. Reset your feet. Look at me, not the water."

I looked at her. She was braced on the far side, and her face wasn't fear. It was recognition—she'd been exactly here, in exactly this position, except the person sliding had been someone else and the gear had been bought cheap by a man counting profits, and it had gone differently, and she had carried it across two countries and a decade to this gorge, to this morning, to me.

"Good," she said when I reset. Just that. But the way she said it, I understood that "good" was covering an entire archive of things she'd decided not to say, and that keeping them unsaid was itself a skill, and that I was apprenticing whether the paperwork existed or not.

* * *

We lunched at the broken gauge—the one where she'd found me, where she'd owned the lie about the cave report with the whole valley listening. Someone had bolted a small brass plate to the shattered pillar. I read it upside down from across the pool:

*Here the record was corrected.*

No names. Petra's idea, obviously. My father touched the plate longer than he touched anything else that day, and I didn't ask, because some penance you don't make people narrate.

It was there, eating hard bread over white water, that the elders told the story I'd only heard in pieces. How the source breathed—rose and fell, rose and fell—for centuries, and the valley lived inside that rhythm like a child inside a mother's sleeping breath. How the pumps had made it hold its breath. How a stranger with a ruined name had come up the gorge reading marks in the stone that no map admitted to, and had listened instead of filing it away.

"We didn't choose her," the elder Neshka said, catching me watching Petra downstream, checking a knot with her teeth. "The water chose her. We just agreed not to argue."

"She argued," I said.

"For a while," Neshka agreed, pleased. "Then she stopped. That's what staying looks like, girl. Arguing until you lose to the right thing."

* * *

The crossing took six hours. When we came out the lower end, into the light where the gorge opens onto the flooded valley—the terraces green for the first time in years, the village of Senn's roofs shining, the whole basin holding water like a cup finally allowed to be full—the twelve of us stood there getting rained on and nobody moved for a long time.

Then the midwife started crying, and then the orchard boy, and then, briefly and furiously, Ilya, who wiped his face and said, "Spray. From the river," and nobody dignified that.

Petra stood apart, the way she always did, and I watched the town do something I hadn't seen before: they didn't leave her apart. The millwright drifted over. Then my father. Then Neshka, talking at her about terrace drainage with the relentless intimacy of someone who has decided you're hers now. Petra answered in short sentences and kept checking the sky and the water line, but she didn't step back. She stood there and let it happen to her, the belonging, like weather she'd finally stopped forecasting her way out of.

Later, on the walk home, she fell into step beside me.

"You hesitated on the traverse," she said.

"A half-second."

"A half-second is a curriculum. We'll drill it until it's zero." She glanced sideways at me. "Also, you talked the orchard boy out of panic at the third pitch better than I could have. I heard you. You used the counting trick."

"You taught me the counting trick."

"I taught you the trick. Using it on someone else is a different skill. Most guides never learn it." She faced forward again. "Dawn Tuesdays and Saturdays. Rope work before breakfast. You'll hate me by midsummer."

"Probably by spring," I said, and she made a sound that in anyone else would have been a laugh.

At the split where the lane forked toward her workroom, she stopped. The rain had thinned to a fine silver drift, and the river below was loud and glad, and somewhere upstream the source was breathing—really breathing again, the elders confirmed it daily, rising and falling in its ancient rhythm like something enormous asleep and dreaming of us.

"Mara," she said. "Why this? Honestly. Not the adventure answer."

I thought about lying, because I was good at it once, and then thought about what she'd done at the inquiry, and told the truth instead.

"Because you came here with nothing and no country and a name that was a crime scene, and you stayed anyway, and now the valley drinks because of you. Because I watched you choose the valley over your own clean name, and then testify anyway, and get neither the glory nor the escape—and you're still here, mending rope above a fish shed." I shrugged, wet and seventeen soon and certain. "I want to be the kind of person who stays. Nobody in this town teaches that. Except you."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then: "Staying costs more than leaving. Every year it costs more than the year before."

"I know. I've read the ledger."

Something moved through her face—there and gone, like a fish under the surface. "Tuesdays," she said. "Don't be late."

She walked on toward the fish shed, and I stood in the rain watching her go, and I understood that whatever had just passed between us, neither of us would ever call it what it was. Guides don't. They just keep checking each other's knots.

* * *

That night I sat up in the shop copying the day's route into the new master chart—the first honest chart of the gorge in eleven years, sigils and bolts and brass plates and all—and my father came down to check on me and found me grinning at the page.

"What?" he said.

"She asked why I wanted this. And I answered honestly. And she didn't run."

He put his hand on my head, briefly, the way he did when words would ruin the moment. "Her record's cleared, you know. Officially. Came through today—the inquiry's final finding. Full reinstatement. She could guide anywhere in the world now. Nepal. Chile. Anywhere at all."

I set down my pen. "Did you tell her?"

"Tried. She said she'd read it when she finished the rope she was mending." He smiled, tired and wondering. "Mara, that rope has been 'almost finished' for four days."

We looked at each other, and neither of us said the obvious thing, because the obvious thing was too large and too fragile to say out loud in a shop full of paper: that the world had just handed Petra Voss every exit she'd ever wanted, stamped and signed, and she was mending rope above a fish shed in a town that used to be a hiding place and was slowly becoming a home—and that none of us, least of all her, quite believed she'd still be here when the season turned.

Outside, the rain kept falling on the valley like an answer to a question asked years ago. And in the morning, I would be at the fish shed at dawn, early, with the kettle already boiling—because I had noticed, the way you notice everything in your first month of apprenticeship, that Petra checks the eastern horizon every single morning now, toward the sea cave, toward the old sigils, as if she's waiting for something to arrive.

Or, I thought, blowing out the lamp, as if she's deciding whether to go and meet it.
