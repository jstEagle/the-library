## 1. The Debt in the Sand

The salt flats at dawn looked like someone had spilled the sky on the ground and forgotten to clean it up. Ivo Marchetti had thought that exact thought every morning for eleven days now, and it had not gotten less true, only less comforting.

She crouched at the edge of a crust of white and pressed two fingers into it. Soft. Yesterday's crust would have held her weight like a plank; today's gave with a wet little sigh, the kind of ground that swallowed boots and grudges equally. Rain somewhere inland, then. The flats were waking up.

"Good," she said to nobody, because talking to nobody was one of the few luxuries of tracking alone. "Mud slows her down too."

Sefa Vane had a nine-day lead and the endurance of a woman who had clearly spent her life running from things, which Ivo respected professionally and resented personally. The trail was old — a scuffed boot heel here, a fire ring there, built small and hot and doused with sand like a woman who'd read a ranger's field manual and taken notes. But trails on the salt didn't lie for long. Wind erased everything eventually, but it erased slowly, and Sefa Vane was not slow.

Ivo stood, brushed white dust from her knees, and took out the compass.

It sat in her palm like a sleeping bird: brass gone green at the seams, glass scratched into a fine web by six decades of pockets and weather, the needle trembling under its little dome of sky. Her mentor's compass. Ranger-Captain Odile Ferran's compass, worn smooth where Odile's thumb had worried it through thirty years of patrols, and now warm from *Ivo's* thumb, which still felt like wearing another woman's coat.

*Take care of it,* Odile had said, or rather had written, in the letter that came with the box of effects, because Odile had died the way she lived — efficiently, in her sleep, without giving anyone time to say anything inconvenient back.

And then some light-fingered drifter had walked out of the Waystation inn at Redrock with it tucked under her cloak, along with forty crowns from the till and, as it turned out, Ivo's entire ability to sleep soundly.

It wasn't about the money. Well. It was partly about the money — Ivo owed the Waystation's owner, Marta, forty crowns she did not have, because the debt had transferred to her the moment the theft landed on her watch, and Marta was a fair woman but fairness has interest rates. Six years ago Ivo had come to Odile flat broke with frostbite on both ears, and Odile had co-signed the loan that bought her gear, her horse, her start. *Debts are how we know we belong to each other,* Odile used to say, which sounded wise until you were the one doing the belonging.

So: forty crowns, one compass, one thief, and eleven days of salt.

Ivo opened the compass lid and checked her heading the way she'd checked it a thousand times since Redrock — north-northwest, toward the coast road, because everyone knew thieves ran for coasts. Coasts meant ships, ships meant disappearances, and Sefa Vane struck Ivo as a woman who appreciated a good disappearance.

Except.

The needle wasn't pointing north-northwest.

Ivo frowned at it. Turned a slow circle. The needle swung lazily, settled, and pointed — there. Not north. Not anywhere near north. It pointed east-southeast, dead steady, off across the flats toward a horizon that contained, as far as Ivo knew, nothing at all except more salt and, eventually, the sea.

"That's wrong," she told the compass.

The compass declined to apologize.

She checked it against the sun, low and orange on her left shoulder. East-southeast confirmed. She checked it again an hour later, walking, and again at noon when she stopped to share dried figs with her mule, Biscuit, who ate his portion with the untroubled dignity of an animal who had never owed anyone anything. East-southeast. Steady as a sermon.

Compasses didn't do that. Compasses pointed north the way rivers ran downhill — it was practically their whole personality. A compass that pointed elsewhere was either broken, magnetized by something enormous and strange, or lying, and in Ivo's experience objects only lied when people had made them lie, and Odile Ferran had been many things but never a liar.

Odile had been, however, secretive in the way of old rangers who'd survived by knowing more than they said. Ivo turned the brass case over in her fingers, looking at it properly for maybe the hundredth time and actually *seeing* it for the first. There, on the underside, so worn it was nearly invisible: a stamp. Not the ranger corps mark. Something else — a circle crossed by three wavy lines, like a sun setting into water.

She'd assumed it was a maker's mark. Makers stamped compasses. That was the natural order.

But makers' marks didn't make needles point at the ocean.

* * *

By late afternoon the flats began to change. The salt thinned, cracked into plates tilted up like dropped crockery, and between the plates came color — first a greenish scum of algae, then actual grass, sparse and gray and stubborn, then pools of brine that reflected the clouds so perfectly that Biscuit refused to walk near them, convinced the sky had fallen and he might step through it.

"You're a coward," Ivo told him fondly. He flicked an ear, accepting the charge.

And there, ahead — tracks. Fresh ones. Boot prints in the soft margin where salt gave way to mud, going fast, going straight, and beside them the unmistakable stagger-line of someone favoring a blistered left foot. Nine days of hard walking will do that. Ivo felt a surge of hunter's satisfaction so sharp it surprised her, followed immediately by a smaller, quieter feeling she chose not to examine: relief. Eleven days alone with her thoughts and a compass that misbehaved, and here at last was a flesh-and-blood person she could simply *ask.*

She found the campfire an hour before dusk, cold, doused, textbook. Beside it, weighted under a stone, a note.

Ivo stared at it for a long moment. Thieves did not leave notes. Thieves especially did not leave notes for the ranger chasing them, addressed — she crouched, brushed off the salt — *To the ranger with my grandmother's compass.*

My grandmother's.

She picked it up. The handwriting was quick and slanted, written by someone sitting down for the first time in days:

*Ranger — I know you're behind me. I'm sorry about the forty crowns; I'll repay it, I swear it on the water. But the compass isn't mine to return and it isn't yours either, though I think you've guessed that by now, or you wouldn't be following this heading instead of the coast road. If it points seaward, follow it. Please. There's a thing under the tide at Rivergate that needs it, and needs someone who loved the woman who carried it. You have until the monsoon. After that the sea takes its doors back.*
*— S. Vane*

Ivo read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way you re-read a sentence that has quietly rearranged the furniture of your life.

*My grandmother's compass.*

She looked at the brass case in her hand. At the worn stamp — the sun sinking into three waves. At the needle, even now, tugging gently east-southeast, toward a horizon that smelled, faintly, unmistakably, of salt water and old stories.

Odile had grown up somewhere coastal. Ivo had always assumed the interior — Odile was desert to her bones, could read a dune like a ledger. But there had been gaps in the stories, whole years with no anecdotes, the way old soldiers skip the parts that cost them. And Odile had never once let anyone else hold the compass. Not once in six years. Ivo had teased her about it — *what, you don't trust me?* — and Odile had smiled that dry smile and said, *I trust you with everything except this,* and had never explained, and Ivo had let it go, because mentors were allowed their mysteries and there had seemed to be time.

There was no time now. There was a monsoon, apparently, and a drowned door, and a thief who apologized for stealing and swore repayment *on the water*, which was a phrasing Ivo had never heard and which sounded, uncomfortably, like an oath from somewhere specific.

Biscuit nudged her shoulder, smelling indecision and hoping it was figs.

"I know," Ivo said.

Here was the arithmetic, laid out plain. Behind her: Marta, forty crowns, a debt, a duty, a contract signed in the Waystation's ledger that said *recover goods or remit value.* Ahead of her: a stranger's wild story, a compass behaving like a divining rod, and a coastline she'd never seen, promising nothing she could put in a report. Every sensible instinct she owned, every rule Odile herself had drilled into her — *follow the contract, finish the job, sentiment is what gets rangers killed* — said turn around. Go home. File the loss. Eat something that wasn't figs.

But the compass pointed seaward. Odile's compass. Odile, who had trusted Ivo with everything except this one thing, and had then died and left it to her anyway, which meant — didn't it? — that somewhere in that careful, closed heart, she'd finally decided Ivo should know. Should be *sent.* This was a message. It had just taken a thief and eleven days of salt to deliver it.

"Sentiment is what gets rangers killed," Ivo murmured, and heard how hollow it sounded over the wind coming off the flats — a wind that carried, for the first time, the smell of the sea.

She fed Biscuit the last of the figs, all of them, because tomorrow they'd be walking into whatever waited at the edge of the map and she was not going to do it owing her mule an apology too.

Then she stood, squared her shoulders toward the east-southeast, toward the water, toward the wreck of whatever Odile had spent thirty years not telling her —

and followed the needle.

* * *

The sea announced itself long before it appeared: first the smell, thick and green and alive, then the changed sound of the wind, then gulls — actual gulls, wheeling and screaming insults at each other overhead, the first living creatures besides Biscuit Ivo had seen in a week. She crested the last low rise of salt-crusted land at dusk and stopped so suddenly that Biscuit walked into her.

Below, the world ended in mudflats that ran out farther than she could see, gleaming bronze in the dying light, ribbed with channels of retreating water. The tide was going out — going out *enormously*, pulled back like a curtain, exposing a seabed no one was meant to see. And far out, black against the sunset, sticking up from the exposed mud like the fingers of a drowned giant —

ribs. Dozens of them. The skeleton of a ship so vast it had to be a story, and yet there it was, real as the mud, real as the compass in her hand whose needle now strained toward it like a dog toward home.

And standing at the edge of the flats, small and still and watching the wreck with her back half-turned, was a woman with a blistered left foot who had stopped running.

Ivo's hand went to her warrant. Then, slowly, it went instead to the compass, and held it up, and let the needle point.

"Well," she said, to the sea, to the thief, to the ghost of Odile Ferran. "You'd better explain."
