## 2. What the Tide Uncovered

The sea announced itself before Ivo saw it — a smell like wet iron and old fish that rolled up the cliff road and wrapped around her like a damp blanket. She had not smelled open water in eleven years. The desert had scrubbed it out of her, replaced salt with dust, gulls with vultures. Her body remembered anyway. Her stride lengthened without her permission.

*You're chasing a thief, Marchetti,* she reminded herself. *Not coming home.*

The compass sat in her palm where it had sat for six days, its brass case warm from her hand, its needle doing something no honest needle should do. It didn't point north. It hadn't pointed north since she'd crossed the last line of dunes. It swung seaward now, steady as a plumb line, tugging at its pivot like a dog at the end of a rope.

Maren Ashk would have had an explanation. Maren always had an explanation, delivered in that dry voice that made you feel clever for asking and slightly foolish for not already knowing. *"A compass is just a question asked of metal, Ivo. Sometimes the metal answers a different question than the one you meant."*

Ivo had thought, for the first three days of the chase, that the needle was broken. On the fourth day she'd caught herself talking to it. On the fifth day she'd stopped pretending she wasn't.

The cliff road dropped toward a bay so wide its far edge was a rumor. Below, the tide was going out — going out *wrong*, if the terrified chatter of the fishing village she'd passed through that morning was anything to go by. An old woman selling dried kelp had grabbed Ivo's sleeve with surprising strength and said, "It's pulling back like it's ashamed of something. Third time this season. You mark me, girl, when the sea takes off its clothes, somebody's about to get dressed down."

Ivo had thanked her, bought two strips of the kelp because refusing felt rude, and eaten them on the walk down. They tasted like the inside of a boot that had been left in the rain. She'd chewed them anyway. Desert habits died hard; you ate what was offered, because tomorrow the offer might be sand.

She crested the last rise and there — there — was Sefa Vane.

The thief stood at the tideline, small against the enormous gray sweep of exposed seabed, and she wasn't running. That was the first strange thing. Ivo had chased Sefa across four hundred miles of salt flat and scrubland, and the woman ran like water finding a crack — everywhere at once, impossible to corner, gone before your shadow finished arriving. But now she stood motionless, hood back, dark hair whipping in the wind, staring out at the bay as if the whole world had narrowed to whatever she was seeing.

Ivo did what any sensible ranger would do. She used the cover of the rocks, closed the distance in a low crouch, and came out onto the wet sand with her hand on her knife and her boots sinking into mud that sucked at each step like it wanted to keep a souvenir.

"Sefa Vane," she called, pitching her voice over the wind. "End of the road."

Sefa didn't turn around. "You're standing on someone's house," she said.

"I—what?"

"Look down." Sefa pointed at Ivo's feet. "Carefully."

Ivo looked. Half-buried in the silt beside her boot was a curved shape, green-black with age, furred with barnacles. A roof tile. And beyond it another, and another, a line of them marching off into the exposed flats, following the ghost of a street. She turned in a slow circle. There were walls under the mud everywhere — low shoulders of stone breaking the surface like the backs of sleeping animals. A chimney. A doorway with nothing behind it but more doorway, room after drowned room receding into the gray.

"The village," she breathed. "The one from the songs. *Kellwater.*"

"The one from the songs." Sefa finally turned, and Ivo got her first proper look at the thief's face: sharp-boned, sunburnt, younger than her reputation and older than her years, all at once. There was sand in the creases around her eyes and something wilder than exhaustion in them. "My grandmother sang it to me. *Kellwater the proud, Kellwater the drowned, who traded her bells for the silence of the sound.* I thought it was a lullaby. Turns out it's directions."

"You led me here." It wasn't a question. Six days of pursuit rearranged themselves in Ivo's head — every impossible escape, every trail that vanished on bare rock, every time Sefa could have lost her and somehow, *somehow*, never quite did. "Every campfire. Every shortcut. You were herding me."

"I was *outwalking* you," Sefa said. "Herding implies I wanted company. But yes. You were useful. You're very hard to lose, Ranger Marchetti. It's honestly impressive. Do you sleep?"

"Rarely. It's part of the charm." Ivo drew her knife — mostly because a ranger who'd said *end of the road* ought to follow through, and partly because her hand needed something to hold while the rest of her reeled. "Give me the compass, Sefa. It belonged to Maren Ashk. She's dead, and it's all that's left of her, and I have watched you carry it across half a desert like it was a pebble you picked up."

Something flickered across Sefa's face — not guilt. Grief, maybe, or its stubborn cousin. "And I've watched you chase me across half a desert," she said quietly, "and I need you to wait about ten minutes before you decide what I am. Because the tide's still going out, and in ten minutes you're going to see why I stole it."

"The tide doesn't care about your reasons."

"No," Sefa agreed, turning back toward the water. "But my grandmother's compass does. Watch the needle."

Ivo watched. As the sea withdrew — further now, absurdly further, the fishing boats in the distance sitting tilted on naked sand like toys forgotten by a giant child — the needle swung. Not seaward anymore. It rotated slowly, deliberately, until it pointed straight out across the flats at a black shape rising from the retreating water.

At first Ivo took it for a reef. Then the shape resolved into curves, into arches, into ribs — dozens of them, vast as cathedral arches, green and gold with age, rising out of the seabed in a long graceful spine. A ship. A ship the size of a hill, beached by the falling tide, its hull split open along one side like a fruit that had waited a hundred years for someone to come pick it.

The *Rivergate*. The legendary wreck. The ship that had carried the exiled heir of Kellwater out to sea and then, according to every version of every song, simply *stopped existing* — swallowed by the deep, cargo and manifest and all, taking the truth of some ancient betrayal down with it. Ivo had heard the ballads in taverns from here to the Salt Reach. She'd always assumed they were the usual tavern mixture: one part history, nine parts beer.

The beer, apparently, had been understating things.

"Oh," she said. It was not a rangerly word. It was not even a word, really. It was the sound a person makes when awe walks up and knocks the anger right out of their hands.

"Told you," Sefa said softly. "Ten minutes."

The wind shifted, carrying the smell of the exposed wreck — tar and salt and something older underneath, something like a library that had learned to swim. Down the beach, tide pools gleamed among the drowned streets, full of stranded fish flashing silver, and crabs marched in confused processions across what had been, until this morning, the floor of the sea. The whole bay felt like a held breath. Like the world had pulled back a curtain and was waiting to see what Ivo Marchetti would do about it.

She realized her knife was pointing at the ground. She hadn't decided to lower it. Her arm had simply concluded, somewhere between the roof tiles and the ribs, that the argument was over.

"Maren knew," Ivo said slowly. "That's why the needle — it's not broken. It's been pointing *here*. The whole time. She tuned it to this place before she died, and she never told me, and then someone stole it, and I—" She stopped. Started again. "Why did you steal it? If you already knew where you were going?"

Sefa hugged her elbows, watching the wreck as if it might notice her. When she spoke, her voice had changed — less thief, more granddaughter. "Because the compass isn't a compass. It's a key. Or it's part of one. My grandmother gave it to Maren Ashk thirty years ago, when they were young and the salvage fleet hadn't yet decided our family's history was theirs to sell. Maren promised to bring it back when it was safe. Then Corvane's people started erasing records, burning manifests, disappearing anyone who claimed descent from Kellwater. Maren hid it instead. And then she died, and it ended up in a ranger's evidence locker in Saltreach, tagged as unclaimed property, and I—" She spread her hands. "I reclaimed it. Aggressively."

"That's called theft."

"That's called *inheritance with extra steps*." Sefa glanced at her, and there — for the first time — was a flash of humor, crooked and tired. "Look. In ten minutes the tide turns. In six tides the monsoon hits this coast and puts the whole bay underwater until next spring, and Corvane's fleet will be here before then with divers and contracts and lawyers, and everything in that ship will be signed, stamped, and stolen, legally, forever. Unless someone gets to the manifest first."

"Manifest."

"Proof. Of who owned what. Of what Corvane's family took, and how, and from whom." Sefa stepped closer, mud sucking at her boots, and held Ivo's gaze with the steadiness of someone who had rehearsed this speech across four hundred miles of desert and never expected to deliver it. "I know you have a contract on me, Ranger. I know what bounty hunters do to people like me. But I'm asking you — not as your quarry, just as a person standing on her ancestors' street — to wait until the tide turns. Look at it. Then arrest me if you still want to."

Ivo looked at the wreck. At the ribs of the *Rivergate* catching the low light like the skeleton of something that had once been magnificent. At the drowned chimneys of Kellwater poking through the mud. At the compass in her own pocket, which had never once pointed home, and which she was beginning to suspect had been pointing at this exact moment since before either of them was born.

Maren would have had an explanation. Maren would also, Ivo suspected, have already taken off her boots and started walking toward the wreck.

"The tide turns in ten minutes," Ivo said at last, and sheathed her knife. "Then you're going to tell me everything. Starting with why a legendary wreck needs a thief, a ranger, and a compass to open it."

Sefa's grin was sudden and entirely unrepentant. "Because," she said, nodding past Ivo's shoulder toward the headland, "it's going to need a fourth. And he's late."

Down the beach, a lone figure was picking his way across the exposed flats — broad-shouldered, limping, wearing the patched oilskins of a salvage diver, and moving with the unmistakable urgency of a man being followed.

* * *

He reached them at a stumbling jog, breathing hard, and Ivo had her knife out again before she'd consciously decided anything. Old habits. The man raised both hands, palms out, and looked from Sefa to Ivo with the weary expression of someone whose week had gone badly and was negotiating for worse.

"Sefa," he rasped. "We have a problem. Fleet scouts burned my boat at anchor this morning. They know I'm gone. And they found my campfire smoke on the headland an hour ago." He jerked his chin at the distant horizon, where — Ivo saw it now, a smudge too straight to be cloud — sails were gathering.

"How long?" Sefa asked.

Brannoc — for Ivo would learn his name later, though the sea seemed to already know it — spat into the mud.

"Two tides," he said. "Maybe less. The monsoon's coming early, and Corvane's coming with it."
