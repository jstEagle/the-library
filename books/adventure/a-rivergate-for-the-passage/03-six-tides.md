## 3. Six Tides

The compass needle swung seaward and stayed there, trembling like a dog that had caught a scent it couldn't name.

Ivo Marchetti crouched at the edge of the wet sand, the stolen compass open in her palm, and let herself feel — for exactly three breaths — the full weight of her own stupidity. Six years she'd carried this debt. Four days she'd chased the thief who took it. And now, forty paces ahead, standing on the exposed seabed like a woman waiting for a coach that was never coming, Sefa Vane wasn't running anymore.

"You knew," Ivo said, closing the distance. Her boots left prints in sand that should have been fathoms underwater. "The whole time across the flats. You weren't fleeing. You were leading."

Sefa didn't turn. She was watching the horizon, where the sea had pulled back so far it looked like the world's largest door had been left open by accident. "I was hoping you'd give up," she said. "You're very stubborn. He mentioned that."

"He."

"Your mentor. Aldous Wren." Sefa finally looked at her, and up close her eyes were the color of shallow water over green glass. "He wrote to my grandmother. Twice. Did you know that?"

Ivo's hand went to the compass without meaning to. "Aldous didn't write letters. He said ink was just gossip that wouldn't die."

"He made an exception." Sefa nodded toward the dark shape rising from the seabed ahead — ribs, black and vast as a cathedral's skeleton, draped in weed and barnacle and six decades of forgetting. "That's the *Rivergate*. My family's ship. Seized by Admiral Corvane before I was born, along with everything my grandmother owned, including—" she held out her hand, palm up, patient as tide, "—that compass. Which is mine. Which he stole back for me, the year before he died, and asked you to deliver. You just never opened the package, did you?"

Ivo stared at her. The wind came off the retreating water smelling of salt and old rope and something older still.

"I was told to recover it," she said finally.

"You were told to *return* it. There's a difference. Aldous Wren understood the difference." Sefa's voice cracked, just slightly, on the name. "He was the only man in the fleet who ever did."

* * *

They sat in the wreck's shadow because there was nowhere else to sit, and because the sheer size of the thing demanded company. Up close the ribs were taller than the watchtower at Saltmere Station, and Ivo, who had crossed the Ashen Reach alone twice, felt small in a way she hadn't since childhood.

"Tell me about the manifest," she said. "And don't lie to me. I've been lied to by professionals. I can tell."

"That's fair. I've been lying to you for four days." Sefa pulled a strip of dried fish from her pack and offered half, which Ivo took, which surprised them both. "The manifest lists every cargo hold of the *Rivergate* on its final voyage. Every deed, every title, every bill of lading signed under the old coastal law. Including the original grant to my family — the Vane charter, signed by a magistrate whose signature Corvane later forged over. The manifest proves the seizure was theft. Not salvage. Theft."

"And Corvane's fleet has spent sixty years calling itself the rightful heir of every wreck on this coast."

"Every wreck, every claim, every drowned family's savings." Sefa chewed thoughtfully. "He didn't just take our ship. He took the proof that we'd ever had one. That's the elegant part. You can't contest a theft if the paperwork saying you owned anything has been locked in a strongroom at the bottom of the sea."

"So why hasn't anyone dived for it?"

"Because the strongroom is tide-locked, and because Corvane's divers patrol these waters like gulls on a fishing boat, and because everyone who remembered the way in was either bought, frightened, or dead." Sefa smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile. "My grandmother died last winter. She was the last of the first kind and the second kind both."

Ivo turned the compass over in her hands. On the back, worn nearly smooth, was a tiny engraving she'd never bothered to read: *For the passage.* Aldous's handwriting. Of course it was.

"The monsoon," she said.

"Six tides." Sefa held up fingers, folding them down one by one. "When it lands, the cave floods past diving depth, the wreck shifts, and whatever's in that strongroom stays there until the next century decides otherwise. Corvane knows it too. His scouts have been sniffing along this coast for a month. He means to burn the manifest before the storm does it for him."

Ivo looked at the wreck. At the thief. At the compass pointing seaward, faithful to a promise she'd never known she was keeping.

"I'm a ranger," she said. "My job is the law."

"Your job," Sefa said gently, "was a dying man's wish, and you've been doing it badly for six years because nobody told you what it actually was." She stood, brushing sand from her knees. "Come on. If we're going to rob an admiral, we'll need help, and I know exactly which disgruntled diver owes me a favor."

* * *

Sefa counted the tides the way other people counted money: obsessively, and with a running total always in mind.

One tide gone already, spent talking. Two, walking the drowned flats to the fisher-camps where Brannoc kept his boat. Three would be spent convincing him. Four and five, diving. Six—

Six was the problem. Six was when the sky turned the color of a bruise and the sea came home angry.

She sat on the *Rivergate*'s lowest rib while Ivo slept fitfully against the hull below, one hand on the compass even in sleep, and let herself do the arithmetic of hope. It was not encouraging arithmetic. But then, her grandmother had taught her sums using ship's stores, and the answer to *not enough* had always been the same: find more, or want less.

"You're staring at me," Ivo said, without opening her eyes.

"You snore like a landslide."

"I do not. Rangers are trained to breathe quietly. It's in the manual."

"There's a manual?" For the first time in four days, Sefa laughed — a real one, startled out of her, and she saw Ivo's mouth twitch in the dark with the effort of not laughing back. "Gods. They gave the desert rangers a *manual*. What's in it, how to be stoic at people?"

"Chapter one is mostly about hats."

And there it was — the laugh again, both of them this time, echoing off the black ribs of a dead ship, absurd and small and alive. Sefa wiped her eyes and felt something shift in her chest that she refused to examine until morning.

"Why did you chase me?" she asked. "Really. Four days across the flats, alone, for a debt that isn't even yours."

Ivo was quiet long enough that Sefa thought she'd fallen back asleep.

"Aldous raised me," she said at last. "Found me half-dead in a dry wash when I was nine, fed me, taught me stars and law and how to swear properly in three languages. When he was dying he made me promise to see the compass delivered. I thought he meant recovered. I think—" her voice did something complicated, "—I think he knew I'd need to come here. To the coast. To something bigger than my grudge against you. He was always arranging things like that. Sneaky old man."

"He sounds like he'd have liked my grandmother."

"He'd have married your grandmother."

"Oh, absolutely. She had terrible taste in stubborn men."

Below them the sea breathed in the dark, patient, filling its lungs. Somewhere out beyond the sandbars, Sefa knew, Corvane's lanterns were already moving along the coast like a slow, greedy constellation.

Two tides to convince a diver. Two to dive. One spare, if the sea was merciful, and the sea had never once been merciful to a Vane.

But sitting in the ribs of her family's ship with a laughing lawwoman and a compass that pointed true, Sefa Vane found — to her considerable irritation — that she believed they might manage it anyway.

Then Ivo went stiff beside her, hand rising for silence.

Down the beach, where the tide had gone out furthest, a lantern flickered. Then a second. Moving toward the wreck in the careful, spaced pattern of men who had done this before.

"Fleet scouts," Sefa breathed.

"No," Ivo said quietly, watching the lights, and her voice had gone flat and cold as a drawn blade. "Scouts walk like that only when they've already found what they're looking for. Those aren't scouts. That's a salvage crew — and they're heading straight for the strongroom entrance."
