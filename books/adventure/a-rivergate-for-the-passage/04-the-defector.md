## 4. The Defector

Brannoc Hale had been holding his breath for eleven years.

Not literally — a diver who did that would be dead by the second season — but in the way that mattered. Every time Admiral Corvane's clerks came down to the drying sheds with their ledgers and their ink-stained fingers, every time a name vanished from a salvage roll or a wreck was reclassified as *unclaimed* or a diver's share shrank to a rounding error, Brannoc swallowed the words rising in his throat and breathed out slow through his nose. Eleven years of that. His lungs had gotten very good at it. His patience had not.

So when the girl with the salt-cracked boots walked into the Gull's Anchor and asked, too loudly, whether anyone knew the flooded passages beneath the Widow's Teeth, Brannoc did what he always did with trouble.

He ignored it thoroughly and obviously.

"I said," the girl repeated, planting herself at his table like she owned the wood grain, "the flooded passages. Under the Teeth. The ones the fleet pretends don't exist."

"Never heard of them," Brannoc said into his stew.

"Your ears are turning red."

"They do that when the stew's hot."

The girl — Sefa, though he wouldn't learn the name for another hour — pulled out the chair opposite him and sat, uninvited, with the particular confidence of someone who had recently stopped caring what happened to her. Beside her, a tall woman in a ranger's dust-cape stood with her arms crossed, radiating the specific patience of someone who had been dragged into this and had decided, for reasons of her own, not to drag back.

"I know who you are," Sefa said, quieter now. "Brannoc Hale. Third dive crew, *Kestrel's Mercy*. You pulled forty-one bodies out of the *Loranne* wreck two years ago, and Corvane's clerks logged it as a cargo recovery. No names. No families notified. You wrote to the harbor office four times. They burned your letters."

The stew had gone cold in Brannoc's mouth. "That's not public record."

"No," Sefa agreed. "It's my family's record. My grandmother kept the fleet's true ledgers before Corvane seized them. She wrote everything down. Including the divers who did honest work and got erased for it." She leaned forward. "You're in there, Brannoc Hale. Page after page. She thought you deserved to be remembered."

Brannoc set down his spoon. It clinked against the bowl, and in the sudden quiet of the Gull's Anchor, it sounded like a bell.

"Who's your grandmother?"

"Dead," Sefa said. "Three years. Corvane's order, though the paper says fever." She slid a folded scrap of paper across the table. On it, in a spidery, careful hand: *B. Hale — 41 souls — the Loranne — tell them he tried.*

Brannoc looked at it for a long time. Outside, the harbor bells rang the third tide, and somewhere a fleet patrol boat coughed its way past the breakwater, and eleven years of swallowed breath sat in his chest like a stone he'd mistaken for his own heart.

"The flooded passages under the Teeth," he said at last. "Why do you want them?"

"Because my family's ship is in there. And the proof of what Corvane did is in the strongroom. And I can't swim."

Brannoc blinked. "You walked into a harbor tavern and asked a stranger to dive for you, and you can't swim."

"I'm told it's a character flaw."

"It's a *fatal* flaw," Brannoc said, but he was already folding the paper and tucking it inside his shirt, against the skin, where he kept everything that mattered. He looked up at the ranger. "And her?"

"Ivo," the ranger said. "I'm mostly here to make sure she doesn't get herself drowned."

"She's your prisoner?"

"Complicated," Ivo said.

Brannoc nodded, because in his experience *complicated* was the only honest word anyone ever used. He pushed his stew to the middle of the table, an old dockhand's signal that the seat across from him was open to talk.

"Sit," he said. "Both of you. And order something, because Marla watches me like a hawk when strangers sit down and nobody buys food."

* * *

They talked until the fourth tide.

Brannoc told them what he knew, which was more than he'd ever told anyone, all of it pouring out now that the dam had a crack in it. The Teeth — three sea-stacks off the drowned headland — were hollow. The fleet knew it; Corvane had sealed the charts a decade ago, after the first survey divers came back babbling about a wreck inside a cave, ribs like a cathedral, a hull older than the kingdom. Two of those divers had drowned on the follow-up dive. Officially, they'd been drunk. Brannoc had been friends with one of them. Tam Voss hadn't touched a drop in six years.

"There's a way in that doesn't need the main mouth," Brannoc said, drawing on the table with a wet fingertip. "The old fisherfolk called it the rivergate. Freshwater spring runs under the headland and out through the cave — the salt and the fresh don't mix right, so there's a layer, a kind of door where the water changes. You dive through the salt, and if you hit the cold layer, you've gone too deep and you'll never come back up. But thread between them—" he drew a wavering line, "—and it carries you. The current does the work. That's why they named it that. The rivergate for the passage."

"And Corvane doesn't know about it?"

"Corvane knows *of* it. He thinks it's a fisherwives' story. He also thinks Tam Voss died drunk." Brannoc's jaw tightened. "He's wrong about a lot of things. He's wrong about the tide window, too. He'll wait for the monsoon to pass before he dives — big salvage needs calm water, that's fleet doctrine, it's in his bones. But the cave floods early in a storm. If you want what's in that strongroom, you don't have six tides and a calm week after. You have six tides. Full stop."

Sefa and Ivo exchanged a look that had a whole conversation in it, and Brannoc, who had spent eleven years reading people the way he read water — for the undertow beneath the surface — caught most of it. *Can we trust him? Do we have a choice?*

"You'll want payment," Sefa said carefully.

"I want my name back," Brannoc said. "Forty-one names back. When you open that strongroom, when the truth comes out — I want it read aloud. All of it. At the harbor, where the clerks have to stand and listen." He shrugged, embarrassed by his own voice. "My mother always said I was a vain man. She was right. I just want to be vain about the right thing."

Sefa put her hand over his on the table. Her fingers were rough as rope. "You'll have it," she said. "Every name. I swear it by the wreck and the water."

Brannoc believed her. That frightened him more than the dive did.

* * *

They made camp that night in the dunes behind the drowned headland, a hollow between two hills where the firelight wouldn't carry to the fleet watchtowers. Brannoc built the fire small, the way he'd been taught — driftwood and dried kelp-stalk, low and blue-flamed, more warmth than light.

They were passing around a bag of dried figs and arguing — gently, the way people argue when they're becoming friends and haven't admitted it yet — about whether the *Kestrel's Mercy* was named for a bird or a woman, when Sefa started to sing.

It was low, half under her breath, a walking-song with a rolling rhythm like oars. Brannoc went still.

"What is that?"

"Grandmother's," Sefa said. "She sang it when she was measuring rope. It's a counting song — the verses count fathoms. Eight fathoms to the gate, nine to the hall, ten to the strongroom door. I didn't know that's what it was until Ivo made me say the words out loud instead of just humming them."

Brannoc took a fig and didn't eat it. "Sing it again."

She did. And Brannoc, who had spent eleven years underwater and eleven years silent, felt something in his chest unlock — because he'd *heard* that song before. Half-remembered, drifting up through a hatch on a survey dive, faint through the hull, and Tam Voss had laughed and said *the wreck's singing, Brannoc, the old ones say the wreck sings* — and they'd both laughed it off, and three months later Tam was dead and the song had gone with him.

"It's a map," Brannoc said quietly. "Your grandmother hid the route in a song. That's why Corvane never found the strongroom. He was looking for charts, and she wrote it in music."

Sefa's eyes were bright in the firelight. "She always said paper burns but a song lives in people."

Ivo, stretched out on the sand with her boots off for the first time in what looked like weeks, said drowsily, "My mentor used to say the desert keeps its maps in stars and the sea keeps hers in songs. I thought he was being poetic. He was being *literal*. That man was insufferable."

"Was?" Brannoc asked, and immediately wished he hadn't, because Ivo's face did something complicated and closed, and Sefa reached over and squeezed the ranger's shoulder, and nobody said anything for a while, and the fire crackled its low blue crackle, and the sea breathed in and out beyond the dunes like something enormous and patient.

* * *

It was Ivo who heard them first — of course it was; desert ears, Brannoc would learn, were better than any diver's — a whisper of boots on packed sand, two sets, moving with the deliberate softness of men who had been trained to it.

"Fire's out," she said, already moving, and Brannoc killed it with a handful of wet sand, and the three of them went flat in the hollow's lip with the cold dune grass prickling through their clothes.

Two shapes came over the far dune and stopped at the top, silhouetted against the stars. Fleet scouts. Brannoc knew the silhouette the way you know a scar — the short spears, the oilskin capes, the lantern hooded at the belt. One of them struck a light, briefly, cupping it, and the glow showed a face Brannoc knew too.

Pell Wicker. Third dive crew, once. Brannoc had taught him to equalize his ears on his first deep dive. Pell had taken the clerks' silver two winters ago and moved to the watch, and everyone pretended not to know why, and Pell pretended hardest of all.

The lantern-light swept the hollow. Once. Twice. Brannoc held his breath — eleven years of practice — and felt Sefa trembling beside him, not from fear, he thought, but from the effort of staying still, like a dog told to *wait* at a full bowl.

"Nothing here," the second scout said.

"Campfire ash," Pell said. "Still warm."

A long pause. Brannoc could hear the sea, and his own heart, and the terrible arithmetic happening in Pell Wicker's head on that dune — because Pell knew whose fire style that was. Low, blue, driftwood. Only one man in the fleet built a fire like that. Brannoc had taught him that too.

"Whose camp do you think it was?" the second scout asked.

And Brannoc closed his eyes and waited for the name, for the whistle, for the spears to come down the dune —

"Drifters," Pell said. "Dune fires are a copper a dozen. Come on. Corvane wants the headland watched, not the beach."

The boots crunched away. The stars came back out, all of them at once, it seemed.

They lay in the cold grass for a full hundred count after the sound faded, and then Sefa started to laugh — silent, shaking, helpless laughter pressed into the sand — and Ivo put a hand over her mouth, and Brannoc found he was laughing too, the kind that hurts, the kind that comes out of you when you've been holding something for eleven years and someone finally, finally lets you set it down.

"He knew," Sefa whispered, when she could. "That was your fire. He knew, and he let us go."

"Or he didn't know," Brannoc said. But they both looked at him, and he sighed, and admitted it. "He knew."

"Why?"

Brannoc looked out toward the dark line of the sea, toward the Teeth standing black against the stars, toward the drowned rooms and the strongroom and the forty-one names.

"Maybe some debts don't get collected," he said. "Maybe some get *paid*." He stood, brushed the sand off, and held out a hand to each of them. "Five tides now. We dive at the next slack water. Get some sleep — both of you. Tomorrow I teach the girl who can't swim how not to die, and I teach the ranger who's afraid of water how to be afraid *properly*, and then we go through the rivergate."

Ivo took his hand and let him pull her up. "You're very bossy for a man who was eating stew alone an hour ago."

"I'm very bossy for a man with forty-one reasons," Brannoc said. "Come on. We've got a wreck to wake up."

And far out past the breakwater, where the fleet's lanterns bobbed at anchor like patient, hungry eyes, a signal lamp began to blink — short, short, long — the fleet's old code, the one every diver learned first, the one that meant *found something*.

Brannoc looked at it, and his stomach went cold as deep water.

The scouts hadn't been looking for their fire after all.
