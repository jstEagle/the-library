## 5. Drowned Rooms

The water in the first chamber came up to Ivo's chin and did not care that she had been avoiding it for twenty years.

She stood on the ledge where the cave floor dropped away, torchlight shivering across the surface, and told herself very firmly that she was a desert ranger, that she had crossed the Glasswash flats in a sandstorm with half a waterskin, that she had once talked a scorpion out of her boot without screaming. The water did not listen. Water never listened. That was rather the whole problem with it.

"You're doing the face," Sefa said behind her.

"What face?"

"The one where you look like you're negotiating with the sea and losing."

"I don't have faces. I have expressions of tactical neutrality."

Sefa snorted — a small, unguarded sound, the first one Ivo had heard from her since the salt flats, since the compass, since everything. She was crouched at the water's edge in her grandmother's diving coat, sleeves rolled, checking the knots on their rope line with quick, certain fingers. "My grandmother used to say the sea can smell fear."

"Your grandmother sounds like she was trying to scare children into behaving."

"She was. It worked on me for years." Sefa tested the line one last time and looked up. "Then she taught me the songs instead, and I stopped being afraid. Mostly. There was still the incident with the moray eel, but we don't talk about the incident with the moray eel."

"I feel like we're going to talk about the incident with the moray eel."

"Not today." She held out a hand. "Ready?"

Ivo looked at the hand. Then at the black water. Then at the hand again.

Twenty-two years ago there had been a well in her village, and a dare from an older boy whose name she had mercifully forgotten, and six feet of cold dark water that closed over her head like a mouth. She remembered the silence most of all. Not drowning exactly — Brannoc's cousin had hauled her out in time — but the silence, and the way the light above her had wobbled like something alive and indifferent, and how for weeks afterward she'd washed her face with a cloth rather than lean over a basin.

She had built a whole life on dry ground after that. A good life. A dusty, sensible, landlocked life.

And now here she was, about to swim into a drowned cave because a thief with kind eyes and a stolen inheritance had asked nicely.

"No," Ivo said. "But let's go anyway."

* * *

The first chamber was the easy part, if you could call anything easy when your heart was hammering against your ribs like a prisoner demanding release. They swam the length of it underwater, following the rope, and came up gasping in an air pocket shaped like a tilted cathedral. Stalactites hung from the ceiling, dripping. Their torchlight turned the whole space the color of amber and honey.

Ivo clung to the rock shelf and breathed, and breathed, and discovered to her enormous irritation that she was not dead.

"One," Sefa counted softly, treading water beside her. "Four more chambers. The third is the long swim — no air pocket, about forty strokes. After that it opens up."

"Forty strokes," Ivo repeated. Her voice came out steadier than she'd expected. "You've done this before."

"When I was fourteen. With my mother." Something flickered across her face, there and gone, like a fish under ice. "She counted the strokes out loud so I wouldn't lose track. Forty is nothing if you count it. It's only forty breaths you don't get to take."

"That's the least comforting arithmetic I've ever heard."

"My grandmother wrote a song about this passage, actually." Sefa pushed wet hair from her eyes. "A work song. The divers used to sing it on the way down — it keeps your rhythm, keeps you calm. *Down past the door where the daylight ends, the rivergate opens for family and friends—*"

"*—and closes its teeth on the ones who pretend*," Ivo finished.

Sefa went very still. "You know it."

"It's a Rivergate song. My mentor used to hum it when he thought I wasn't listening." Ivo laughed, short and disbelieving. "He grew up in these ports, before he went inland. He never said. All those years, and he never once said."

"Maybe it hurt to say."

"Maybe." She turned the thought over. Old Halvern, with his sun-cracked hands and his terrible tea and his compass that pointed seaward instead of north — pointing, all along, toward whatever waited below. Toward this. Had he known what he was leaving her? Or just what he was leaving undone?

"Hey." Sefa's voice pulled her back. "Chamber two. It's shallow — mostly wading. You can touch bottom almost the whole way."

"Almost."

"Almost is my favorite word. It means the story isn't over yet."

* * *

Chamber three was the one.

They stood on the last lip of stone, four feet of black water between them and a submerged archway that swallowed the rope line whole. Beyond it: forty strokes of nothing. No air. No light but the torch sealed in its waterproof horn lantern, which would glow like a drowned star and show them nothing except how much nothing there was.

Ivo's body knew what was coming before her mind agreed to it. Her breath went thin. Her hands, ranger's hands, steady on a bowstring in a crosswind, were shaking.

"I can't," she said. It came out flat and honest, stripped of all tactical neutrality. "Sefa. Twenty-two years ago I nearly drowned in a well. Six feet deep. And this is—" she gestured at the archway, at the ocean's whole throat waiting beyond it, "—this is considerably more than six feet."

Sefa didn't laugh at her. That was the thing Ivo would remember later, in the years afterward, when this cave had become a story they told at tables — that Sefa didn't laugh, and didn't pity either. She just nodded, as if Ivo had reported a weather condition.

"Okay," she said. "New plan. You don't swim it alone."

"There's no other way through."

"There's always another way through. My grandmother's rule." She began uncoiling rope from her waist, fast and sure. "We tie ourselves together, chest to chest, back to front. You hold the lantern. I do the swimming — you're taller, your kicks will reach me fine. You don't have to think about strokes or counting or anything. You just have to hold on and breathe when I tell you. Can you do that? Hold on and breathe?"

"I have been holding on and breathing my entire life. It's practically my specialty."

"Good." Sefa tied the knots herself, checking each one twice, and then pressed her forehead briefly against Ivo's shoulder — a diver's blessing, maybe, or just a girl who needed it too. "Forty strokes. I'll count them in the song. When you feel me tap twice on your arm, that's halfway. Tap twice back so I know you're with me."

"And if I panic?"

"Then you'll be panicking attached to someone who refuses to drown, so honestly you'll be in the safest place in this entire cave." She grinned, and it was the grin of someone standing at the edge of the dark on purpose. "Rivergate opens for family and friends."

"We're not family."

"Not yet," Sefa said, and dove.

* * *

The water took them.

It was cold and total and it pressed on Ivo's ears and chest like a hand, and every animal part of her screamed the scream from the well, from twenty-two years ago, *up, go up, up toward the wobbling light.* But there was no up. There was only Sefa's back against her chest, strong and sure, and the rope binding them, and the lantern glowing green-gold in her fist, lighting a corridor of stone that rolled past like the inside of some enormous sleeping creature.

*I'm not alone in the dark,* she realized, somewhere around stroke ten, when the panic crested and broke instead of drowning her. *That's the difference. In the well I was alone. Here I am held.*

Sefa's rhythm carried her. Shoulders rolling, legs driving, steady as a heartbeat, and Ivo let her own body fall into it, matched to it, until she couldn't tell whose pulse was whose. Twice — tap tap — on her arm. Halfway. She tapped back. The archway's far mouth appeared ahead, a gray bloom of lighter water, and Sefa put her head down and drove for it, and Ivo held on, and breathed when she was told, and trusted.

They broke the surface together in chamber four, coughing and laughing and clinging to each other and the rocks in equal measure, and the sound that came out of Ivo was not dignified at all. It was wild and cracked and young, a sound she hadn't made since before the well, and Sefa was laughing too, forehead pressed to hers again, both of them streaming and shaking and gloriously, stupidly alive.

"You counted," Ivo gasped. "I heard you counting under the water."

"Couldn't help it. It's the song. It gets in your bones." Sefa pushed back to look at her, and her eyes were bright in the lantern light. "How do you feel?"

Ivo took inventory. Terror: spent. Lungs: burning. Hands: still shaking, but from something else now, something that felt suspiciously like joy.

"Like I swam through a cathedral holding onto a stranger," she said, "and I'd do it again."

"Stranger," Sefa repeated, mock-offended. "I've robbed you, confessed to you, and saved your life. That's practically a courtship where I come from."

"Heaven help Rivergate, then."

Beyond the fourth chamber, the passage climbed, and the sound reached them before the sight did — a low, vast breathing, the echo of open water moving in a great hollow dark. They rounded the last bend and stopped.

The fifth chamber was no chamber at all. It was a flooded hall the size of a temple, its vaulted ceiling lost in blackness, and rising out of the middle of it, draped in weed and lit by their single lantern like something waking from a century's sleep, lay the ribs of a ship. Not the wreck they'd seen from outside — this was deeper in, older, a second vessel entirely, masts snapped, hull split along its length like a fruit. And lashed to its broken deck, half-visible in the gloom, stood a small iron-banded structure with a door.

A strongroom.

Sefa made a sound Ivo had no name for. Then she pointed past it, to the far wall of the hall, where the lantern light caught something that turned Ivo's blood cold:

Fresh rope. New-cut stone. And moored in the shadow of the wreck's hull, riding gently on the swell — a fleet skiff, its sail furled, its divers' gear laid out neat as Sunday.

Someone had gotten here first.
