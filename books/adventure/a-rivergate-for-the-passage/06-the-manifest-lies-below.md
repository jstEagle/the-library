## 6. The Manifest Lies Below

The strongroom door was smaller than Sefa had dreamed it would be.

She had carried the image of it for eleven years — through the workhouses of the salt towns, through the fleet's orphan ledgers where her name was entered as *Vane, Sefa, no kin, no claim* — and in all that time the door in her mind had been tall as a cathedral gate, iron-banded, terrible. Something you would need a war to open.

It was barely taller than Ivo. A round hatch of green bronze set into the cave wall behind the third drowned chamber, its surface furred with a century of soft coral, and at its center, uncorroded, unbroken, a wheel of polished brass with three keyholes arranged in a triangle, like three stars in a child's drawing.

Sefa pressed her palm flat against the cold metal and laughed. It came out of her sideways, wet and startled, and Brannoc — treading water beside her, his salvage lamp throwing a trembling gold coin of light across the hatch — gave her a look of professional concern.

"You all right, little heir? People who laugh in flooded rooms usually have a reason."

"It's small," she said. "It's *small*. I've been running for eleven years from a door I could have opened with my shoulder."

"Could you have, though?" Brannoc tilted his head at the wheel. "That's Corvane work, that mechanism. Fleet pattern, older mark. He didn't build it to keep out the sea. He built it to keep out everyone who wasn't meant to be here."

The lamp-light shivered. Somewhere behind them, in the drowned corridor they had swum through to reach this chamber, the water made a long, patient sound against the stone — the sound of a tide that kept its own schedule and did not care about theirs.

Ivo surfaced at the edge of the lamplight, breath ragged, one hand pressed to the wall. She had not stopped shaking since the second chamber. Sefa had watched her do it — watched the ranger's jaw go tight and her breathing go shallow and her hands find the rock the way a drowning person finds a spar — and Ivo had said nothing, and Sefa had said nothing, because there are fears a person carries like a knife between the ribs, and pointing at them only drives them deeper. But she had sung louder in the second chamber. Her grandmother's tide-songs, the ones that mapped the old passages in their verses — *where the eel sleeps, turn your left shoulder; where the ceiling drinks, do not linger* — sung clear and steady, not because Ivo needed the directions, but because a voice in the dark is a rope, and everyone knows it.

"You found it, then," Ivo said now. She pushed wet hair from her face and looked at the hatch, and Sefa saw the ranger take in the three keyholes, and count them, and arrive — as Sefa herself was arriving, with a slow cold turning in her stomach — at the arithmetic.

Three keys.

Sefa had one. It hung on a cord around her neck, worn smooth by her grandmother's thumb and then by her own: a sliver of whalebone carved into the shape of a wave, small enough to swallow, precious enough to kill for. *The Vane key,* her grandmother had called it, pressing it into her hand on the last night. *You are the Vane key now, little fish. Remember what that means.*

She had thought — she had let herself think, down in the third chamber, swimming through her grandmother's songs into her family's stolen heart — that it would be enough. That the door would know her. That it would swing open like a story and give her back her name.

Three keyholes. Three heirs.

"Corvane holds one," Brannoc said. He had gone very still, the way he did when he was reading a mechanism instead of a face. "The admiral's line was the fleet's factor before the seizure — his grandfather witnessed the confiscation. He'd have taken an heir-key as part of the spoils. It'll be in his cabin, probably, or on his neck where he can touch it when he needs to feel like a legitimate man." He spat, and the lamp-light caught the bubbles. "The third—"

"The third was my uncle Oren." Sefa heard her own voice go flat, the way voices go when they are carrying something too heavy to let them bend. "My grandmother's son. He had the second key. He ran north with it after the seizure, and the fleet caught him at the rivergate at Harrow Mouth, and—" She stopped. Started again. "There's a stone in the fleet cemetery with his name on it. Corvane made sure of that. Made sure everyone knew the last heir was dead."

The chamber was quiet except for the water. Ivo had stopped shaking. Sefa noticed that — noticed the ranger go deliberately, fiercely still, the stillness of a woman planting her feet against a current.

"So the door needs a dead man's key," Ivo said.

"It needs *Oren's* key," Sefa said. "Which is not the same thing. Keys can be buried. Keys can be lost. Keys can be—" She stopped herself before she could say *found*, because hope was a current too, and if you leaned into it too hard too fast it would sweep you onto the rocks. She had learned that. She had the scars.

Brannoc was looking at her with an expression she was beginning to know: the defector's expression, the one that said *I served that man for nine years and I am still finding new reasons to be sick about it.* "There's a way to know," he said. "Fleet records. Corvane keeps everything — he's a magpie of a bureaucrat under all the gold braid. If Oren Vane's key was recovered at Harrow Mouth, there'll be an intake ledger. A line. A number. And if there's a number, there's a place." He paused. "And if it wasn't recovered — if Oren was smart enough to sink it, or give it away, or swallow it — then somewhere north of here there's a key with no owner, waiting for someone stubborn enough to go looking."

"And the monsoon," Ivo said quietly, "is in four tides."

Nobody answered that. The water answered for them, a soft knock against the bronze.

* * *

They retreated to the air pocket above the second chamber — a low stone throat where the cave exhaled with each wave, where they could sit with their shoulders out of the water and pass a waterskin around like a communion. Brannoc's lamp hung from a nub of rock, and by its light Sefa watched the other two do the arithmetic she was refusing to do.

"Four tides," Ivo said. "One to rest, or we're useless. Two to dive and work the mechanism if we somehow have all three keys. That leaves—" 

"Nothing," Brannoc said. "It leaves nothing. Even if the key's in the admiral's cabin, we can't get it. Even if it's in the fleet's intake vault at Corvane's anchorage, that's a full day's sail and a fortress. Little heir, I love you like a sister I've known for six days, but the sums don't sing."

"They don't sing yet," Sefa said.

She heard herself say it and was surprised by how steady it came out. Because here was the thing she had not told them — the thing she had barely told herself, in eleven years of running: she had never actually expected to open the door. She had expected to *reach* it. The reaching had been the whole of her for so long that the door itself had become almost theoretical, a horizon you walked toward, and horizons are not for arriving at, they are for keeping you moving.

But now she had touched the bronze. Now she had put her grandmother's whalebone sliver against the keyhole — she had done it, down in the chamber, with shaking hands — and the key had *leaned* toward the lock, the way iron leans to a lodestone, the way her grandmother had promised the old mechanisms were made to lean. The door knew her family. The door was waiting. It was only the arithmetic that was wrong, and arithmetic, unlike doors, could be argued with.

"My uncle Oren," she said, "kept a woman in Harrow Mouth. A net-mender. Tamsin. My grandmother wrote her name on the back of the song-map — I thought it was just a friend, but grandmothers don't hide names on the backs of maps unless the names matter." She pulled the oilskin packet from its waxed sleeve at her belt, unrolled the soft leather of the song-map, and turned it over. There, in her grandmother's cramped, salt-faded hand: *T. of the Mouth, who keeps what we keep.*

"Keeps what we keep," Brannoc read. He was silent a moment. "Or," he said slowly, "who keeps what *Oren* kept. If he knew the fleet was closing on him — and he'd have known, he was no fool — he wouldn't carry the key to the rivergate. He'd leave it with someone the fleet had no reason to search. A net-mender. A woman with no name on any ledger." He looked up, and his grin came out crooked and fierce. "Sefa Vane. The sums might sing."

"Four tides," Ivo said again. But she said it differently this time — not as a wall, Sefa thought, but as a drumbeat. A count to move by. "Harrow Mouth is a day's hard sail. The fleet's scouts will be watching the approaches once the weather turns." She was already doing it, Sefa could see — the ranger's mind laying out the route the way she'd lay out a tracking line across salt flat, reading the country before it read her. "But the monsoon drives everyone off the water. Corvane's fleet will run for the deep anchorage to shelter. The coast will be empty except for fools."

"Are we fools?" Brannoc asked.

"Speak for yourself," said Ivo. "I'm a ranger. We're professionally stubborn."

Sefa laughed — really laughed this time — and rolled the song-map closed, and pressed it back against her heart where it lived. Above them, through the stone, she could hear the sea turning over, patient as a debt. Four tides. Somewhere north, a net-mender named Tamsin was keeping what her grandmother had trusted her to keep, and had probably been keeping it for eleven years without once knowing why, and Sefa found she could not stop smiling at the thought of it — at the idea of a stranger holding a piece of her family's heart all this time, faithful without knowing, the way the sea holds a wreck.

The door was small. The door was waiting. The arithmetic was terrible.

Sefa Vane had run from smaller odds for longer.

"Then we sail with the storm," she said, "and we knock on Tamsin's door before the rain does."
