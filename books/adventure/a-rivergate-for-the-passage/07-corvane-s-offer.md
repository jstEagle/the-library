## 7. Corvane's Offer

They took Brannoc at dawn, and they let Ivo know it the way a cat lets a mouse know it's been caught — by leaving the evidence somewhere she'd be sure to find it.

She woke in the sea cave where they'd made camp, Sefa still asleep against the wall with her grandmother's songbook open on her chest like a shield, and Brannoc's bedroll empty. Cold. Cold for hours. At first she thought he'd gone out to check his dive lines, because that was the kind of man Brannoc was — up before the gulls, fussing over knots nobody had asked him to tie.

Then she saw the boot prints. Fleet-issue boots, the kind with the coral-scraped soles, six sets of them, coming in from the north path and going back out again. And pinned under a stone in the middle of camp, a square of grey paper with a wax seal stamped by a hand she recognized from every wanted poster between here and the salt flats: an anchor wrapped in a serpent.

The note said only: *The lighthouse. Midday. Come alone or don't come.*

"Well," said Sefa, who had woken and read it over her shoulder, "he's dramatic."

"He's an admiral."

"Those aren't mutually exclusive." Sefa folded her arms. "You're not actually considering going alone."

Ivo looked at her. Six days ago she'd have said *yes* without blinking, because going alone was what rangers did, and because Sefa Vane was a thief and a liar and a debt with legs. But six days was a long time in a drowned kingdom. Six days was learning that Sefa hummed when she was nervous and lied about why. It was watching her stand in the wreck's shadow with her hand pressed flat against barnacled wood like she was greeting a grandmother.

"I'm considering," Ivo said, "that if I don't go, they'll hurt him."

"And if you do go alone, they'll hurt all three of us, just more efficiently."

"You have such a comforting way of putting things."

"I'm told it's my charm."

* * *

The lighthouse stood on the headland above the cove, dead these forty years, its lamp room black-eyed and its spiral stair half-eaten by salt wind. Ivo climbed it at midday exactly, unarmed in the visible sense — though the ranger's habit of hiding a knife in her boot and another in her sleeve and a third one she would not disclose even to herself died hard.

Corvane was waiting in the lamp room with two guards and a table that had no business being there, set with tea things, as if piracy were best conducted over porcelain. He was smaller than Ivo expected. That was the trick of men like him, she supposed; the posters made them ten feet tall, and then you met them and found a tidy grey man with neat hands and eyes like ledger entries.

"Ranger Marchetti." He poured tea without asking. "Sit."

"I'll stand."

"Everyone stands at first." He smiled as if this were charming. "Tea?"

"I'm fine."

"You're dehydrated. You've been drinking cave seep for four days. Have the tea." He pushed the cup across. And because she was thirsty, and because refusing twice would look like fear, she drank it. It was good tea. She hated how good it was.

"Brannoc," she said.

"Is alive. Comfortable, even. My divers tell me he's been teaching them knot tricks and complaining about our rope quality." Corvane sat, folding those neat hands. "He's a difficult man to dislike, which is precisely why he's dangerous to me. Loyalty is contagious, Ranger. I contain outbreaks."

"What do you want?"

"Sefa Vane." He said the name lightly, like flicking ash. "And the manifest she's so certain is down there. You bring me both, and I make you a wealthy woman."

"I already have a contract."

"A contract for one hundred crowns." He opened a leather folio — of course he had a folio, the man probably slept with a folio — and turned it toward her. "Two hundred instead. Plus this."

He slid a second paper across the table. Ivo read it, and the world went narrow and ringing.

It was a debt instrument. Her debt. The one she'd carried six years — the salvage-fleet lien on her mentor Odessa Marchetti's estate, called due the week Odessa died, the debt Ivo had inherited along with the compass and the grief and the name. Two hundred crowns owed to the fleet, accruing interest, the reason she chased bounties through salt and storm while other people slept in beds. The reason she couldn't afford to stop.

"This document," Corvane said gently, "could be marked paid in full. Today. By me."

Ivo set the paper down very carefully, the way you set down something that might bite. "Odessa never borrowed from your fleet."

"No. She *found* things the fleet preferred unfound. The lien was administrative. Corrective." He sipped his tea. "Your mentor was a meddler, Ranger. A beloved one, I'm told, but the sea keeps honest books, and someone has to balance them."

"She was dying when you called that debt."

"Yes." No apology in it. Not even the performance of one. "Timing is a tool like any other."

The lamp room was very quiet. Below, the tide was turning, the water sliding back off the reef flats in long silver sheets, uncovering the wreck rib by rib like a body being slowly revealed. Somewhere down there was a strongroom with a case sealed by three keys, and one key in a dead man's vault, and one key missing, and one key — Ivo realized, with a cold drop in her stomach — sitting on the admiral's own chain around his neck, because of course it was. He'd been wearing the answer the whole time.

"You want me to give her to you," Ivo said. "Hand her over, point you to the strongroom, and walk away rich and free."

"I want you to stop making this hard on yourself. Look at the arithmetic." He counted on neat fingers. "Sefa Vane: a stranger of six days' acquaintance. Your mentor's memory: six years of companionship, plus everything she gave you. One hundred crowns difference in pay, and a debt that has shaped every day of your adult life. This isn't even a choice, Ranger. It's a sum."

And that was the cruelty of it — he wasn't wrong about the arithmetic. He was only wrong about what it added up to.

Because here was the thing Ivo could not say aloud: the debt was Odessa's dying wish. Not the owing of it — the *clearing* of it. Odessa in her last week, thin as driftwood, gripping Ivo's wrist: *Pay it, girl. Pay it clean. Don't you dare die owing that man anything.* Every bounty since had been a prayer in that direction. Every mile of salt flat. If she burned this chance, she dishonored the last instruction of the only person who had ever —

Except.

Except Odessa had also taught her to read tracks, and Ivo had spent six days reading Sefa Vane's, and tracks didn't lie the way ledgers did. Sefa's trail didn't run like a thief's. It ran like a daughter's — straight to the wreck, straight to the strongroom, doubling back only to protect the people walking with her. Thieves ran *from*. Sefa kept running *toward*, and she'd been running toward the same place for fifteen years.

"Here's my counteroffer," Ivo said.

Corvane raised an eyebrow, amused. "Rangers don't negotiate."

"This one does. You release Brannoc, unharmed, today. In exchange, I don't testify before the coastal magistrate about the fleet's dive manifests I've seen — the ones with the erasures. Brannoc talks in his sleep, Admiral. He talks quite a lot."

The amusement drained out of Corvane's face by degrees, like tide off a flat. "That's blackmail."

"It's arithmetic," Ivo said. "Yours. I'm told it's very persuasive."

For a moment she thought he might simply have her thrown from the lamp room, and she measured the distance to the stair and the knife in her sleeve and decided, calmly, that she'd take one guard with her. But Corvane only refolded his hands, and the ledger-eyes recalculated, and when he spoke again his voice was soft as silt settling.

"You misunderstand your position. So let me clarify it." He stood, buttoned his coat with two precise movements. "You have until the fifth tide. Bring me Sefa Vane and the manifest, and we speak of wealth and forgiveness. Don't—" he paused at the stair, and smiled, and it was the worst smile Ivo had ever seen, patient and proprietary, "—and I'll assume the ranger died on the reef with the rest of the wreck's ghosts. Your mentor's debt will pass to whatever poor soul inherits *your* estate. Do you have anyone who'd inherit it, Ranger? Anyone at all?"

He left. The guards left. The tea steamed gently in the sea wind, and Ivo stood alone in the dead lighthouse doing sums of her own.

Two hundred crowns. A debt erased. Odessa's last wish, gift-wrapped, handed to her by the man who'd strangled her mentor's name into a ledger line.

Against: one thief who sang to calm herself and pretended she didn't. One diver who complained about rope quality to his captors because silence scared him worse. One wreck full of somebody's grandmother's house, waiting for somebody's granddaughter to come home.

Ivo picked up the debt instrument. She held it over the tea.

Then she stopped, and laughed at herself — a short, rough sound with no humor in it — and tucked the paper inside her coat instead. Burning it now would feel wonderful and mean nothing. Documents could be reprinted. Debts lived in ledgers, and ledgers lived in offices, and offices could be *opened*.

But there was a fifth tide coming, and after it a monsoon, and between here and the strongroom lay flooded passages, fleet divers, and a mechanism that needed three keys — one of which was currently hanging around a tidy grey man's neck.

Ivo looked out at the turning tide, at the wreck emerging gleaming and patient from the retreating sea, and began, quietly, to plan a theft of her own.

After all — she knew a thief. Two, counting herself as of roughly five minutes ago.

She just hoped Sefa wouldn't ask too many questions about where she'd learned to pick a lock.
