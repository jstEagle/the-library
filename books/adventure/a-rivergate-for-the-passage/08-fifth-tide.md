## 8. Fifth Tide

The surrender was Ivo's idea, which Sefa hated immediately and on principle.

"You're walking onto that ship," Sefa said, pacing the length of the sea cave with her arms crossed tight, "to hand yourself to the man who owns half the bounty on your head. Do you hear how that sounds?"

"I hear how it sounds," Ivo said. She was checking the knots on her rope harness for the fourth time, mostly to have something to do with her hands. "It sounds like the only door into a locked room."

"It sounds like drowning with extra steps."

"Everything worth doing does." Ivo looked up. "Brannoc is on that ship because of us. Because he chose us over Corvane's ledger. I'm not leaving him in a hold to think about what loyalty costs."

Sefa stopped pacing. In the lantern light her face did something complicated — anger folding down into something rawer. "You've known him four days."

"I've known you six," Ivo said. "Look how that turned out."

That earned her a short, unwilling laugh, the kind Sefa gave like payment on a debt she disputed. Then it faded, and she came close, and took Ivo's wrist — the one with the compass scar from years of carrying it wrong-side against the bone.

"When you're in there," Sefa said quietly, "and Corvane offers you everything — and he will, he's good at offering — remember what the compass pointed at. Not north. Seaward. Your mentor sent you here, Ivo. Whatever she told you about debts, she meant this."

Ivo hadn't told her that part. She searched Sefa's face. "How—"

"Grandmother's songs say the desert always sends someone when the rivergate opens. It's in the third verse. Nobody believes the third verse." She squeezed Ivo's wrist once, hard, and let go. "Fifth tide. The wind turns an hour before landfall. If you're not at the stern cable by then, Brannoc and I come in after you, and we all die stupidly together."

"That's the worst rescue plan I've ever heard."

"It's the only one we have. Welcome to my family's luck."

* * *

The *Corvane's Mercy* — because the admiral had named his flagship after himself, which told Ivo most of what she needed to know — sat at anchor beyond the breakers, lanterns strung along her rails like a festival that had forgotten to be fun. Ivo rowed out alone under a white rag tied to an oar, wearing her ranger's coat with the badge still on it, because the badge was the point. Corvane wasn't buying a defector. He was buying a symbol: the law itself, delivered to his deck, kneeling.

They took her oar, her knife, her boots. They left her the coat, which she understood as theater, and the compass, which she did not understand at all until she saw Corvane looking at it.

He received her in his cabin, which smelled of lamp oil and old paper, and he was smaller than his reputation — a neat, grey man with diver's hands and a voice like a ledger being balanced. He poured two glasses of something amber without asking.

"Sergeant Marchetti." He set a glass before her. "Or is it Ranger? I'm told the rangers disowned you the moment you crossed the salt flats off-contract."

"The contract's in my pocket," Ivo said. "You can read it if you like. It says one thief, dead or alive, payable on delivery."

"And instead you bring me yourself." He smiled thinly. "And a proposal, I'm told. You'll give me the Vane girl and the diver both, in exchange for double bounty and—" he tapped the table twice— "the Marchetti debt. Six years outstanding. Old Haldis carried it to her grave, didn't she? Dying wish, they say. *See it paid.*"

The words went through Ivo like cold water finding a crack. She kept her face still, but inside, everything tilted. He knew. Of course he knew — Corvane bought information the way other men bought bread, and a dying woman's debt was exactly the kind of soft thing he liked to grip.

"She did," Ivo said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it.

"Then let me pay it." He leaned forward. "Haldis Marchetti was the finest tracker the flats ever produced, and she raised you to finish what you start. This isn't betrayal, Sergeant. It's completion. The girl is a rumor with a grudge. The manifest, if it even exists, is paper. But you — you're real. Deliverable. And I pay my debts." He slid a folded paper across the table. "Double rate. Plus the Marchetti account, settled in full, witnessed by my clerk. All you have to do is walk back to that cave and be very convincing."

Ivo looked at the paper for a long time. Long enough that Corvane's smile began to widen, because he thought he'd won.

She picked up the pen.

*Here it is,* she thought. *The moment. The dying wish, served up on a plate with a signature.* And underneath it, quieter, the truth she'd been circling since the salt flats: Haldis had taught her to track, but she'd also taught her what tracking was *for*. *We don't follow footprints to catch people, little bird. We follow them to find out who needs finding.*

The compass pointing seaward. The third verse nobody believed.

She signed.

Corvane exhaled, satisfied, and reached for the paper — and Ivo held it a heartbeat longer than necessary, angling it toward the lamp, memorizing the wax seal, the clerk's name, the exact weight of the ink. A forgery needed a true original to copy from, and she now had one burned into memory.

"Your key," she said, standing. "For the strongroom mechanism. You'll want it back before I go ashore."

Corvane paused. Then he laughed — genuinely, warmly, a man enjoying the competence of a new purchase — and drew a cord over his head. On it hung a small iron key, tide-marked, worn smooth by three generations of guilty hands.

"One of three," he said. "The girl has one, or had. The third belonged to old Maren Vane, and Maren's dead, so the mechanism stays shut no matter what you steal." He dropped it into Ivo's palm and closed her fingers over it. "Bring me the other two and the manifest burns tonight, and everyone's debts are settled. That's the beauty of it, Sergeant. Everyone wants settlement."

*I do,* Ivo thought, closing her hand around the key. *Just not yours.*

* * *

The hold of the *Mercy* smelled of bilgewater and despair, and Brannoc sat in the middle of it like a man who had been expecting company. His wrists were chained to a cargo ring, and there was a fresh bruise along his jaw, but when the guard opened the hatch for Ivo's "final conversation with the prisoner" — Corvane's phrase, delivered with a wink — Brannoc looked up and grinned through split lips.

"They sent the law," he rasped. "Should I confess?"

"Confess later." Ivo knelt, hands moving on the chain lock, the key she'd palmed from the armory rack already turning. "Two guards change at the bell. Hatch ladder's unwatched till then. Can you swim?"

"I'm a salvage diver, girl. I *am* swimming. Everything else is just waiting."

"Then listen. Fifth tide, the wind turns early — monsoon's ahead of schedule. When it hits, every hand topside will be fighting rigging. You go over the stern cable, down to the skiff Sefa's holding past the breakers. I take the long way." She tapped her coat, where the forged contract lay folded over the original's memory. "I'm going to leave the admiral a receipt."

Brannoc studied her face, and whatever he saw there made him stop grinning. "You signed his paper."

"I signed *a* paper. There's a difference, and it's going to ruin his night." The lock clicked open. She worked the chain loose link by link, quiet as tide. "Brannoc. Why did you defect? Really."

He flexed his freed hands, wincing. "Because I pulled bodies out of wrecks for eleven years, and Corvane taught me they weren't accidents. Ships *helped* to sink. Heirs helped to drown. And every time, the fleet wrote it down as weather." He looked at her. "Your mentor — Haldis. She fished my brother out of a squall once, no fee, no contract. Just did it. So when I heard a Marchetti was hunting the Vane girl, I figured the story had to be more than a bounty." He stood, rolling his shoulders. "Turns out I was right. Go on, then. I'll see you at the cable."

"One more thing." Ivo pressed the iron heir-key into his palm. "If anything goes wrong for me, this reaches Sefa. Swear it on your brother."

"On Tam," Brannoc said solemnly, and pocketed it. "Now get off my ship. It smells terrible and the management's worse."

* * *

Getting off the ship proved harder than getting on, because the storm had decided to skip the appointment entirely. By the time Ivo reached the main deck, the wind had swung a full quarter-point south and was climbing, and the *Mercy*'s crew were already hauling canvas with the particular profanity of sailors betrayed by the sky. Rain came in flat and sideways. Somewhere aft, Corvane's voice cut through it, ordering divers armed and boats lowered — he wanted the cave taken *before* landfall, schedules be damned.

Which meant the plan's comfortable margin had just drowned.

Ivo moved along the leeward rail, coat streaming, and did the last thing on her list: at the chart table under the quarterdeck awning, where the clerk's satchel hung fat and unattended, she slipped in a folded page. Not the original — that stayed against her heart, dry in oilcloth, the true confession of thirty years of sunk ships and drowned heirs in Admiral Corvane's own accounting hand, copied fair while he'd been gloating over his pen. What she left behind was a note, brief and courteous:

*Your clerk writes a beautiful hand. So do I. Check your strongroom inventory. — M.*

Let him tear his own ship apart looking for what was already gone.

She went over the rail at the stern cable, hand over hand, into black water and driving rain, and the sea took her like an old debt coming due. She surfaced gasping behind the skiff, and Sefa's arm hooked her collar and hauled her over the gunwale, and for a moment neither of them spoke — just rain hammering the boards, lightning showing the *Mercy*'s lanterns swinging wild, and Brannoc already aboard, laughing breathlessly at nothing.

"Well?" Sefa demanded.

Ivo spat seawater, held up the iron key, and then — because some things deserved ceremony — pulled the oilcloth packet from her coat and put it in Sefa's hands. "One heir-key, stolen from the thief who stole it first. One confession, written in the admiral's own numbers. And one forged contract sitting in his cabin, about to make him trust his clerk slightly less than the weather."

Sefa stared at the key. Then at her. Then, horribly, she started to cry and laugh at once, which Ivo would learn was simply how Vanes celebrated.

"You absolute—" Sefa shook her head. "You signed his paper. You *signed*—"

"I signed my name," Ivo said. "He just hasn't read the fine print yet. Haldis taught me contracts too." She looked east, where the horizon had gone the color of a bruise and the wind was screaming now in earnest, pushing the monsoon toward them like a fist. "Sixth tide's coming early. Corvane's boats are already in the water. We dive on the next calm, or we never dive at all."

Brannoc hauled the sail up. "Then pray for a gap in the storm, girl."

"No need," Sefa said, and there was something in her voice Ivo had never heard before — not hope exactly, but its stubborn ancestor. "Grandmother's songs say the rivergate opens in the worst of it. Fourth verse." She grinned through the rain. "Nobody believes the fourth verse either."
