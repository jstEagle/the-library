## 9. Landfall

The monsoon did not arrive like weather. It arrived like a verdict.

Ivo felt it first in her teeth — a pressure, a hum, the same sensation she'd known before sandstorms broke over the flats and buried everything that hadn't had the sense to kneel. She stood at the cave mouth with the admiral's heir-key warm in her fist, watching the horizon go the color of a bruise, and thought: *we are out of tides.*

"Fifth tide's early," Brannoc said behind her, hauling the last of their rope coils into the dry chamber. He said it the way he said everything now — flat, practical, as if naming a thing made it manageable. "Storm's come up the coast faster than any chart I've seen. Corvane will have read it too."

"Then he'll hurry."

"He was never going to stroll." Brannoc tied off the coil with hands that had spent thirty years underwater and still shook, sometimes, when he talked about the fleet. "Question is whether we're in the strongroom or in his chains when he gets here."

Ivo turned the key over. It was heavier than it looked, brass gone green at the edges, stamped with the Vane crest that Corvane had kept like a trophy for twenty years — proof, Sefa said, that he'd always known whose ship it was. That he'd erased the Vanes from every ledger and kept their name on his own key ring.

"Sefa!" she called. "How long to swim the flooded passage?"

Sefa emerged from the inner dark, hair bound back, grandmother's songs humming under her breath the way they always did when she was frightened and refusing to admit it. "Four minutes down, six back with weight. Less if the surge is kind." She looked at the sky through the cave mouth and her jaw tightened. "It won't be kind."

"Then we go now," Ivo said. "All three. Before the water takes the choice away from us."

* * *

The passage swallowed them one lamp at a time.

Brannoc went first, because it was his sea and his shame and his amends all tangled together, and because he knew the way the drowned rooms breathed — where the air pockets sat, where the current pinched, where a diver could rest a hand on stone and feel the ocean deciding what to do next. He had led Corvane's crews through these chambers twice, years ago, stripping anything that glittered while the manifest case sat untouched because no one alive held its keys. He had told himself then that untouched meant safe. He knew better now. Untouched meant waiting.

He paused at the second air pocket, tilting his head back so the lamplight caught the others' faces above him. Ivo came through the narrow throat of rock with the grim economy of a woman who had nearly drowned as a child and had made an enemy of water ever since — and who had, somewhere between the fourth chamber and this one, stopped fighting it. She moved *with* the swell now. Brannoc had watched that happen and decided not to mention it, because some mercies were better given silently.

"You feel that?" he asked.

"The pull's changed," Ivo said. "Stronger. Outbound?"

"Inbound. Storm surge. The whole cave's going to start inhaling soon." He checked the line, checked her face, found both holding. "You good?"

"I'm *adequate*," Ivo said, which from her was practically a love letter, and Brannoc grinned around his regulator and dropped back into the green dark.

Behind them both, Sefa swam the passages like a daughter coming home. Her grandmother had sung the route to her before she could walk — *three doors and a lie, little fish, three doors and a lie* — and the song mapped true even now, even with the water rising, even with the wreck groaning overhead like an old man turning in his sleep. The third door was the lie: a beautiful archway leading nowhere, carved by some hopeful ancestor to fool exactly the kind of men who had come with chains and ledgers. Sefa patted the false arch as she passed. *Not today,* she told her grandmother silently. *Today we go through the ugly crack behind it.*

The ugly crack opened into the strongroom antechamber, and there they hung in the lamplight, three thieves of very different pedigrees, looking at the door.

It was black iron furred with twenty years of barnacle, set into stone that had been fitted by masons who believed their work should outlast kingdoms. Three keyholes. Three locks. One dead heir, one missing heir, one disinherited one — and one stolen key, pried from an admiral's own cabin two nights ago by a ranger who was supposed to be arresting somebody.

"We're short," Ivo said quietly.

"We're not," Sefa said.

They both looked at her. She pulled something from inside her shirt — a small key on a cord, worn smooth, salt-white with age. It had been around her neck since the night she ran from the salvage fleet's orphan pens, since the night a dying woman pressed it into her hand and said *when the rivergate opens.*

"This was my mother's," Sefa said. "She wasn't dead when Corvane's reports said she was. She made it to the outer islands. She lived four more years." Her voice cracked and steadied itself, the way old rope does. "She sent me this. I didn't know what for. I thought — I don't know what I thought. A keepsake."

"And the third?" Brannoc asked.

Sefa looked at him. Then at Ivo. And Ivo understood, with a lurch, why Sefa had let herself be chased across half the desert instead of losing the ranger in the first hundred miles. Why the compass had pointed seaward. Why every step of this pursuit had felt less like a hunt and more like an invitation.

"The third key isn't a key," Sefa said. "Grandmother's song. *Three doors and a lie.* The third lock opens to blood-kin of the Vane line — the mechanism reads the hand, not the metal. My grandmother built it that way after the first theft. So that only family could ever finish it." She swallowed. "I can't do it alone. The rite needs kin. Chosen counts, in the old law — but it has to be *given*, freely, witnessed, before the lock."

The water shivered around them. Somewhere far above, thunder rolled across the surface of the sea like furniture being dragged.

"So ask us," Ivo said.

* * *

Sefa asked them in the flooding dark, and it was not the speech she'd rehearsed in her head for six years. It was shorter. It was mostly her hands shaking.

"I have no one," she said. "That was the whole point of what Corvane did — not just take the ship, take the *family*, erase the line so no one would ever come asking. He almost managed it. I'm what's left, and I'm not enough, and I know it." She held out her hands, palms up, the way you do when you're either begging or surrendering and can't tell which. "So I'm asking. Under the old law, before the old door. Be my kin. Not for the manifest. For me. Because I'd rather share a drowning with you two than open this door alone and live."

Brannoc laughed — a wet, cracked sound — and took her right hand. "Thirty years I worked for a man who called his divers 'assets.' You're asking me to be family. Best offer I've had this decade." He squeezed. "Given. Witnessed."

Ivo took the left hand last. She thought about the contract folded against her heart, the bounty, the debt, her mentor's dying wish that she collect what was owed. She thought about how debts were just another kind of chain, and how some people spent their whole lives mistaking chains for purpose.

"Given," she said. "Witnessed. And Sefa — if we live through this, I'm going to be an insufferable sister. Fair warning."

"Deal," Sefa whispered, and pressed both their palms, and hers, flat against the black iron.

The third lock drank the touch like thirst. Something deep in the door went *clunk* — a sound Brannoc felt in his molars — and all three keys turned, and the strongroom of the *Rivergate's Passage* opened for the first time in twenty years.

Inside: not gold. Corvane's divers would have been disappointed. Inside was paper, wax-sealed and oil-clothed and stacked with a librarian's devotion — the manifest, the true one, every seized cargo and forged sale and signature of Admiral Corvane's own hand, twenty years of erasure written down by the people he'd erased. Enough to hang him in any court on the coast. Enough, Sefa breathed, to give everything back.

And beneath the manifest chest, a smaller box, labeled in a dead woman's handwriting: *For whoever finishes this. You're family now. Take care of each other.*

Sefa got the manifest chest strapped to her harness with hands that wouldn't quite obey. That was when the cave inhaled.

* * *

The surge hit like a fist through the whole body of the mountain. Water that had been waist-deep became chest-deep became *gone*, the air pocket collapsing, the passage ahead turning from corridor to river to something with opinions. Brannoc grabbed Ivo; Ivo grabbed the guide line; the line held, and the three of them were swept toward the exit in a brown roaring dark full of debris and destiny.

The chest was too heavy.

Ivo saw it happen — saw the strap bite into Sefa's shoulders, saw the current take the chest like it weighed nothing and Sefa like she weighed nothing, dragging her backward into the deep gallery where the wreck's fallen mast had pinned a tangle of netting against the wall. Sefa fought it. Sefa was strong and stubborn and she fought it for eleven seconds, which was nine seconds longer than most people manage, and then the netting took her ankle and the water pinned her against the stone and the lamp light swung away and Ivo screamed into her regulator where no sound could go.

She went in anyway.

Later, she would not be able to explain it. The child who had nearly drowned at seven, who had feared water her whole life, who had learned to move *with* the flood only days ago — that child did not decide anything. What decided was simpler: *she's mine now. Family means you go.* Ivo dove into the black, followed the strap by touch, found Sefa's harness in the churning dark, and cut — knife out, one stroke, severing the chest free. It tumbled away into the deep and Sefa came up light into her arms, convulsing, mask flooded, and Ivo hauled her along the wall toward the last pocket of air with her own lungs burning and her own terror screaming and her hands, thank every patient god, steady.

Brannoc met them at the pocket. He'd braced himself across the exit current like a doorframe, and he took Sefa's weight and dragged her face-up into the thin bell of trapped air, and for a moment the three of them hung there gasping — Sefa coughing seawater, Ivo seeing spots, Brannoc laughing again, that same wet cracked laugh, because what else could a man do.

"Chest," Sefa rasped, when she could speak. "The manifest—"

"Went to the deep gallery," Ivo said. "I'll get it. After."

"After *what*?"

Ivo tilted her head. Through the water, through the stone, through the roar of the monsoon making landfall above them, came a new sound: boots on rock. Lantern light, many lanterns, spilling down the upper passage. Corvane's divers, arriving at the strongroom they'd find empty except for one small box addressed to family.

And behind the boots, clear as a bell across the floodwater, a voice Ivo knew from a tent and an offer and a night of temptation:

"Marchetti! I know you're down there!"

Brannoc looked at her. Sefa, gray-lipped and shaking, looked at her.

Ivo smiled, spat out her regulator, and said, "Stay here. Both of you. I bought us a meeting — time to sell him something."

She kicked off into the dark, toward the lanterns, toward the admiral, with nothing in her hands but a knife and a choice already made.
