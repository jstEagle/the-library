## 10. A Rivergate for the Passage

The tribunal at Rivergate was held in a salt-bleached hall that had once been a customs house, back when the drowned kingdom still collected duties instead of memories. Ivo Marchetti stood at the back with her arms crossed, watching Sefa Vane lay the manifest on the magistrate's bench the way a person lays down something heavy they've carried too long — not triumphantly, but with the particular exhaustion of someone who has finally set down a weight and isn't yet sure their spine remembers how to be straight.

The manifest itself was a miracle of spite. Corvane's own clerks, decades ago, had recorded every seized vessel, every forged title, every family stripped of salvage rights so the fleet's admiral could grow fat on other people's inheritances. The ink had survived the water better than anyone expected. Bureaucracy, it turned out, was nearly as durable as greed.

"Admiral Corvane," the magistrate read aloud, her voice carrying that particular flatness officials use when they are about to ruin a powerful man, "did knowingly falsify forty-one claims of dereliction—"

Ivo stopped listening to the list. She'd heard it already, in the days since Landfall, recited in taverns and fish markets and once, memorably, by a child selling candied kelp who had clearly rehearsed it for effect. What she watched instead was Brannoc, standing beside Sefa in his best — and only — coat, fidgeting like a man who distrusted dry land on principle.

"You're staring," he muttered without turning his head.

"I'm observing."

"You're staring. I can feel it. Like gulls on a mooring post."

"I'm deciding whether you'll cry."

"I will *not*—" He stopped. Swallowed. "It's the salt air," he said, with tremendous dignity. "Diver's lungs. We weep easily. It's documented."

Sefa, up front, caught Ivo's eye over her shoulder and grinned — quick and crooked, the grin of someone who had spent six years being hunted and had only recently remembered what it was to be found.

The verdict, when it came, took less time than the reading. Corvane was convicted on all counts, stripped of rank, and remanded to the very salvage fleet he'd built, which — in a piece of justice so tidy Ivo suspected the magistrate had been saving it up for years — would now be crewed under new admiralty while he sat in a cell reviewing, presumably, the error of his ways.

Outside the hall, the tide was coming in over the flats, gold-green and unhurried. The monsoon had passed two weeks prior, leaving the air scrubbed clean and the whole coast smelling of wet stone and possibility.

"Well," said Brannoc, exhaling like a man surfacing. "That's that."

"That's that," said Sefa.

And then, because she was Sefa, and because six years of running had taught her that important things should never wait for convenient ones, she turned to face them both squarely in the middle of the causeway, blocking foot traffic, ignoring the indignant cluck of an old woman hauling a basket of eels.

"There's a rite," Sefa said. "Old one. From before the drowning. My grandmother used to sing it — it's how families were made, back when blood wasn't enough or wasn't there." She took a breath. "You're supposed to stand where the river meets the sea. Where the passage opens. And you name each other kin, and the water witnesses it, and after that it's binding. Legally. There's paperwork."

"There's *paperwork*?" Brannoc said.

"The old kingdoms loved paperwork. It's why the manifest mattered." She was talking fast now, the way she did when courage was costing her. "Anyway. I'm asking. Both of you. Because I spent six years thinking I was the last of my line, and it turns out inheritance isn't just what's written down. It's who shows up. And you both showed up. In a flooding cave. Which is more than most blood relatives manage."

The old woman with the eels had stopped to watch. So had several others. Word traveled fast in Rivergate; half the town knew these three by now, knew the cave, knew the manifest, knew the ranger who'd burned her bounty contract in front of a fleet officer with the calm of someone lighting a cooking fire.

Ivo looked at Brannoc. Brannoc looked at Ivo. His eyes were doing the thing divers' eyes do — bright and wet and blaming the salt air.

"If I say yes," Ivo said slowly, "does that mean I have to attend holidays?"

"Yes," said Sefa.

"Songs? There'll be songs?"

"So many songs. My grandmother wrote verses for every occasion including *unchoking*."

Brannoc made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been the salt air again. "Aye," he said gruffly. "All right. Kin. Though I warn you both, I snore like a bilge pump."

"He does," Ivo confirmed. "I've shared a campfire with him. It's less snoring than geological event."

"Then it's settled," Sefa said, and her voice cracked exactly once, on *settled*, and none of them pretended otherwise.

* * *

They performed the rite three evenings later, at the rivermouth, where the fresh water ran silver into the green of the sea and the passage between them opened and closed with the breathing of the tide.

Sefa sang. Her voice was not trained but it was true, and the old words came out of her like they'd been waiting underwater all this time for someone to come down and let them up. She named Brannoc first — *brother-of-the-deep-water, keeper-of-passages* — and he stood in the shallows in his boots, fully clothed, because he said if the rite wanted him wet it could do the job itself. Then she named Ivo.

*Sister-of-the-long-road,* she sang. *Who chased me across the salt and stopped when I needed stopping.*

Ivo had prepared exactly nothing to say. She was a woman of few words at the best of times, and this was not the best of times; this was the kind of time that dissolved people. But the rite required answering, so she stood in the meeting of the waters and said, "I came here for a debt. I'm staying for the family. That seems like good trade."

Brannoc blew his nose loudly. "Salt air," he explained, to no one, to everyone.

Afterward they sat on the shingle and ate smoked fish out of paper and watched the light go long and amber over the water. Somewhere out past the headland, the ribs of the wreck showed at low tide, and salvage crews were already working her under new colors — honest ones, Sefa had checked personally, twice.

"Oh," Sefa said suddenly, sitting up. "Almost forgot. Your mentor's compass. You should have it back. It was hers, not mine — I only borrowed it to find my way to the cave, and, well." She dug in her coat and held it out. "Debt repaid. All of it. Even the part from six years ago. Consider us square."

Ivo took the compass. It sat warm in her palm, brass worn smooth by her mentor's thumb over thirty years of desert miles. On impulse — or perhaps not impulse at all; perhaps some instinct older than thought, the same one that had told her the needle pointed seaward when it should have pointed north — she pried at the backing plate.

It gave with a small click.

Inside, folded into a space no bigger than a stamp, was a scrap of paper gone soft with age. Ivo unfolded it with hands that were, for once, not entirely steady.

*Ivo —*

*If you're reading this, then either I'm dead or you've finally gotten curious enough to open the case, and honestly either way I'm proud of you.*

*The debt you think you owe me — the training, the years, the second chance — it was never a debt. Debts get paid and forgotten. What I gave you was a gift, and gifts don't come due. They pass through. You pay them forward or you don't, but nobody collects.*

*So put the ledger away, girl. Chase whatever's worth chasing.*

*— R.*

Ivo read it twice. Then a third time, though the words didn't change, which was somehow the point of them.

"She knew," Sefa said softly, reading over her shoulder. "She knew you'd follow me. She sent you after me on purpose."

"Or she knew I'd need permission to stop chasing someday, and she hid it where only a thief could lead me." Ivo laughed — a short, surprised sound, rusty from disuse. "Six years I carried that debt like a stone. And it was never a debt at all. It was a door."

"What's a rivergate?" Brannoc asked, apropos of nothing, squinting at the horizon. "Sefa keeps saying it. The rite, the passage. What's it actually mean?"

Sefa smiled and leaned back on her hands, letting the last light catch her face.

"It means the place where two waters meet and neither has to win," she said. "River water and sea water, fresh and salt, past and future. The gate doesn't choose. It just opens, and lets everything through that's willing to make the crossing." She glanced sideways at Ivo. "Some people spend their whole lives standing on one bank or the other. Kin are the ones who cross to meet you."

The tide turned. Out at the rivermouth, the passage opened wide, silver and gold and green all at once, wide enough for anything — a stolen compass, a burned contract, a dead mentor's blessing, three people who had started as hunter and hunted and stranger and ended as something the old kingdoms had thought worth writing laws about.

Ivo slipped the letter back inside the compass, closed the case, and tucked it into her coat over her heart, where debts used to live and gifts would stay.

"So," she said. "These holidays. When's the first one?"

Sefa's grin went crooked and bright as a compass needle finding true north — or, better, seaward.

"Harvest-tide," she said. "Three weeks. You're both helping with the cooking."

Brannoc groaned. Ivo smiled, and the rivergate stood open behind them, letting everything through.
