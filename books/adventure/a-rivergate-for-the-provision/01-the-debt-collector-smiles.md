## 1. The Debt Collector Smiles

The Salt Hollow bar had flooded twice this season, and the third time it simply gave up trying to be anything else. Water lapped at the ankles of the stools, warm as bathwater and smelling faintly of diesel and drowned ambition, and nobody moved to bail it out. In Salt Hollow, you learned to drink with the sea. The sea, after all, was the only neighbor who never asked you for money.

Freya Zhou sat at the far end of the counter with his boots on a dry crate and a beer sweating in his fist, doing the arithmetic he did every night and every night arriving at the same answer: *not enough*. Not enough in the account. Not enough in the boat. Not enough, if he was honest, in the man. The beer was the good kind, which meant he hadn't paid for it, which meant someone had paid for it *for* him, and that was the whole trouble with his life in a single bottle.

"You look like a man doing sums," said the voice behind him, cheerful as a church bell.

Freya didn't turn around. You didn't have to. Everyone in Salt Hollow knew that voice the way you knew the foghorn—distant, pleasant, and a warning all the same. Pell. The Optimists' man on the coast. He wore a pale linen suit that never seemed to get wet no matter how high the water rose, and a smile that arrived a half-second before the rest of his face, like thunder in reverse.

"Pell," Freya said to his beer.

"Freya Zhou. Storm chaser. Boat owner—" Pell slid onto the stool beside him with the delicacy of a man sitting down to a piano recital, "—*technically*. I do love that word. Technically. It's doing so much work in that sentence."

The bar went quiet around them, the way a room goes quiet when a dog that never barks starts growling. Old Sefa behind the counter found something fascinating to polish at the far end. A fisherman by the window developed a sudden deep interest in the rain.

"I'm drinking," Freya said.

"You're *sinking*," Pell said warmly, "and I'm here to throw you a line. That's the whole business, Freya. That's what the name means. Optimists. We believe in you. Nobody else on this coast believes in anybody anymore—it's all salt and grudges out here. But we believe. We believed in you when the banks wouldn't. We believed in you when the insurers wouldn't. We believed in you right up through the *Kestrel* going down with—" he paused, consulted a small leather notebook he didn't need to consult, "—four hundred and sixty thousand in equipment aboard. Insured, of course. Underinsured, rather. We covered the gap. Because that's what we do. We believe."

Freya took a long pull of the beer. It had gone warm and tasted like every bad decision he'd made in the last two years, which was to say like beer.

"You've said all this," Freya said. "Twice. Last time you said it with a man holding a crowbar."

"And how is your hand, by the way?"

"Fine."

"Liar. Splendid. You'll fit right in with our other clients." Pell closed the notebook and laced his fingers on the counter, which was underwater to a depth of four centimeters, and which he somehow kept dry. "Here's the situation, and I want you to hear it as the good news it is. The monsoon makes landfall in three weeks. Maybe four. The government says five, but the government also says the ferries run on schedule, so we discount accordingly."

"Three weeks until what?"

"Until Cape Verren goes underwater for the season. The *whole* season. Possibly forever, if the projections the Optimists have purchased are accurate." Pell's smile widened by a millimeter, the way a door opens onto a dark room. "The drowned shelf off Cape Verren. Twelve square kilometers of seabed that the charts haven't touched in sixty years. The Optimists want it surveyed. Mapped. Soundings, hazards, channels, anything that looks like it might be worth owning when the water comes back down. And we want it done *before* the monsoon, because after the monsoon there's no shelf, no survey, and no reason for us to keep believing in you."

Freya set the bottle down. "That's a death contract. Nobody surveys the Verren shelf in three weeks. The currents alone—"

"The currents are your specialty, aren't they?" Pell said brightly. "I read your file. Wonderful file. *Zhou, F. Reads water like other men read weather. Lost crew. Lost nerve. Lost everything except the boat, and the boat is ours.* That last part's mine. I added it to the file myself. I thought it was poetic."

The rain picked up outside, drumming on the tin roof, and somewhere down the street a shutter banged and banged and nobody went to close it. Freya looked at his reflection in the flooded floor—broken up by ripples, a man in pieces—and thought about the *Kestrel*, and the four names he still said to himself on bad nights like a man counting stitches in a wound.

"And if I say no?" he asked, though he already knew. He'd known since Pell sat down. That was the trick of Pell—you always knew, and he always let you know that he knew, and somehow you still ended up feeling like the whole arrangement had been your idea.

"No?" Pell said, delighted. "No is a *wonderful* word. Let's spend some time with it. If you say no, then tomorrow morning the *Mara's Wake*—" and Freya flinched at the name, and Pell noted the flinch the way a jeweler notes a flaw, "—is impounded. Salvage lien, unpaid berth fees, and a small matter of the collateral clause on page nine, which your signature made very clear. And then, because the Optimists believe in proportionate response, we'd come for the fingers. Not all of them. We're not monsters. The two on the right hand. A helmsman can steer with eight fingers. A surveyor, though—" Pell clicked his tongue, sorrowful as a funeral bell, "—a surveyor needs ten."

"You'd cripple the very thing you're lending against."

"We'd be lending against your *skill*, Freya, not your hands. The hands are just how the skill gets out into the world. Like a tap." He spread his own hands, pale and dry and immaculate. "You keep the tap, we keep the water. Everyone prospers."

The bar's one lamp flickered. Sefa quietly poured himself a drink he hadn't charged anyone for.

Freya looked out the flooded doorway at the harbor, where the *Mara's Wake* rode her mooring in the dark, patched and stubborn and the last thing in the world he owned that still answered when he called her. He thought about the shelf. He thought about the currents. And underneath all of it, in the cellar of himself where he kept the things he didn't look at directly, he thought about the storm, and the data he'd chased instead of the harbor, and the four men who had trusted his reading of the water.

"Three weeks," Freya said.

"Three weeks. Soundings by the fifteenth. And Freya—" Pell stood, brushed water from his trousers that had never touched them, and beamed down with genuine, radiant, unbearable warmth, "—I want you to know that everyone at the Optimists is *rooting* for you. Genuinely. We've got a pool going."

"A pool."

"On how far you get. The favorite is 'not past the cape.'" Pell tucked the notebook away. "But my money's on you. I've always had a soft spot for drowning men. They swim so *hard*."

He left a coin on the counter—more than the beer was worth, less than the debt was growing—and stepped out into the rain without getting wet, or so it seemed to everyone watching, and was gone.

* * *

Freya stayed until the lamp burned low and the water in the bar rose another finger's width, lapping at the crate under his boots like a patient creditor. Sefa came down the counter at closing time, sloshing, and refilled Freya's bottle without being asked.

"On him," Sefa said. "He paid for the whole bottle."

"Then he paid for my funeral too, at these prices."

Sefa snorted and began stacking stools on tables, working around the water as if the water were a regular. "You're not going to do it," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

Freya turned the bottle in his hand. Outside, the harbor lights smeared themselves across the flooded street, and the *Mara's Wake* creaked at her mooring, talking in her sleep.

"I'm going to do it," Freya said.

"Because of the fingers."

"Because of the boat." He said it fast, before the truer answer could get out. Because of the debt. Because of the four names. Because three weeks of honest work on dangerous water was the closest thing to a clean death the coast offered a man like him, and some part of him that he refused to examine had heard *Cape Verren* and felt not dread but *relief*.

Sefa looked at him for a long moment with the flat, unsentimental kindness of a man who had buried two wives and a fishing fleet. "You know what I learned, running a bar in a flood zone?" he said. "Everybody who drowns, drowns smiling. The water's warm. The water's friendly. The water says, *come on in, you were always going to*. That's how it gets you. Not with teeth. With optimism."

He took the empty bottle and shuffled off, and Freya sat a while longer with the coin Pell had left, turning it over. On one face, a sunburst. On the other, a motto in small cheerful letters: *THE TIDE WILL TURN.*

Freya laughed—short, ugly, alone—and pocketed it anyway, because a man in his position couldn't afford to throw away money, even cursed money.

* * *

He walked to the harbor through water to his knees, and the *Mara's Wake* rose out of the dark to meet him, patched hull and patched sails and patched heart, and he put his hand on her rail the way you'd take a feverish friend's wrist.

"Three weeks," he told her.

She creaked. She always creaked. It was either agreement or complaint, and after nine years he'd stopped being able to tell the difference, which he suspected was the definition of marriage.

He climbed aboard, lit the little lantern in the cabin, and unrolled the old charts across the chart table—the good ones, the ones from before, when the Survey Office still mapped this coast and men still believed the water could be known. Cape Verren sat at the edge of the last sheet, drawn in faded ink, the drowned shelf sketched in with a note in some dead surveyor's hand: *unsurveyable at any tide on record*.

Beneath it, in different ink, newer, someone had written: *the sea gives back what it takes, if you wait long enough.*

Freya stared at that line for a long time. It wasn't the kind of thing surveyors wrote. It was the kind of thing madmen wrote, or believers.

He was still staring at it when he noticed the tide tables pinned above the table, and the figure circled in red ink that he didn't remember circling—tomorrow's low water at Cape Verren, and the number beside it so low, so far below anything in the tables, that it looked less like a prediction than a dare.

The lantern flickered. Out in the harbor, the water began, very quietly, to fall.
