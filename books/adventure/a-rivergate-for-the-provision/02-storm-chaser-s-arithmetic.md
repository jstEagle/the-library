## 2. Storm Chaser's Arithmetic

The ledger lived in a tin biscuit box under the bunk of the *Provision*, and Freya Zhou had not opened it in eleven days, which was eleven days longer than he'd ever managed before.

He opened it now, in the gray hour before dawn, because Pell's smile was still stuck to the inside of his eyelids like a fishbone. The bar had smelled of brine and spilled rum and the particular sweetness the Optimists' collectors carried with them, a kind of cologne made, Freya suspected, from distilled confidence. *One last contract, Mr. Zhou. Survey the drowned shelf at Cape Verren. Simple work for a man of your reputation.* And then the pen, laid across the table like a small silver bone.

He had signed. Of course he had signed. Optimism, he was learning, was the most dangerous currency in the archipelago, and he'd been spending it his whole life.

The tin box held three things: a ledger with a cracked spine, a stub of pencil worn down to a thumbnail's length, and a photograph of five people squinting into the sun on the deck of a boat that no longer existed. He set the photograph face-down. He always set it face-down first. That was the ritual. Face-down, then arithmetic.

* * *

The arithmetic went like this.

What Freya Zhou owed: four thousand, two hundred and sixty marks to the Optimists' Provident Lending Concern, a figure that had begun life as nine hundred and had grown the way mold grows in a wet hull—quietly, patiently, in the dark, with every intention of eating the whole ship. Interest compounded monthly. Penalties compounded whenever Pell felt cheerful, which was often, because cheerful men found more reasons.

What Freya Zhou owned: one boat, the *Provision*, forty-one feet of patched hull and borrowed engine, worth perhaps eight hundred marks if sold to someone who didn't look too closely at the stern, where a repair job from two winters ago was held together by fiberglass and prayer. One depth sounder that worked on days it felt like it. One lamp. One coil of survey line, three hundred feet, fraying at the hundred-foot mark. One waterproof jacket with a zipper that had opinions. One sextant, his father's, brass gone soft at the joints, accurate to within a generous smile.

And one set of hands, one pair of eyes, and a head full of weather.

That was the whole inventory. He wrote it out in the ledger because writing made it real and real things could be fought. *Owes: 4,260. Owns: 800, give or take a lie.* The gap between those two numbers was the shape of the rest of his life, and the shape of the rest of his life was Cape Verren, a drowned shelf at the ass-end of the map where the charts stopped pretending and the sea did what it liked.

*Simple work*, Pell had said. Freya had been a surveyor long enough to know that when a lender called work simple, it meant either *impossible* or *fatal*, and usually both, and always *underpaid*.

He sharpened the pencil with his knife and got to the part he hated, which was the part where he planned to survive.

* * *

The *Provision* needed work before she'd survive the run to Verren, and the work needed money he didn't have, so he did what he always did, which was to trade skill against coin and come out behind. He spent the morning at the chandlery in Salt Hollow harbor, talking old Mateo into a drum of epoxy he couldn't afford in exchange for recalibrating the shop's entire stock of compasses, a job Mateo had been putting off since the spring.

"You'll do all twelve," Mateo said, suspicious. Freya was known in Salt Hollow as a man who could fix anything except his own situation.

"All twelve. Deviation tables for each. You could navigate to the moon with them."

"Nobody navigates to the moon, Freya."

"Nobody *should*," Freya said. "Doesn't mean the tables won't be beautiful."

Mateo laughed and rolled the epoxy across the dock, and Freya caught it against his chest with a grunt, and for a moment—just a moment, in the flat morning light, with the gulls arguing over something dead two piers down—he felt almost like a man with a plan instead of a man with a debt.

Then he went back to the boat and started scraping old caulk, and the feeling passed.

* * *

The storm had a name, officially. The Weather Service had called it Cyclone Vesta, which sounded like a woman you'd meet at a garden party, and Freya had always hated that, because what came ashore off the Teeth on the night of the ninth of March had not been a garden party. It had been a wall of water and wind that erased three fishing villages and one crew of five, and the crew of five was the part that lived in him.

He'd made the call. That was the arithmetic underneath all the other arithmetic, the sum he could never balance. He'd been chasing the storm's eye for data—for *readings*, for the survey contract that would finally clear his debts and buy the *Provision* a new engine—and the readings had been extraordinary, the pressure falling like a stone down a well, and he had said *one more pass*, and the crew, because they trusted him, because he was the one with the instruments and the reputation and the head full of weather, had said *all right, Captain*.

Rosa had said it. Wen had said it. Little Dov, who was twenty-three and still wrote letters home to his mother in careful pencil, had said it. Old Harrow, who'd survived forty years of sea and said the ninth of March was the first time he'd ever been afraid on water, had said it too, in his way, which was to look at Freya for a long moment and then nod once.

*One more pass.*

The wave that took the deck took Wen and Dov and Harrow with it. Rosa made it to the liferaft; the raft made it four minutes. Freya made it because the sea, for reasons it never explained, spat him onto a reef two miles down-coast, and he lay there in the rain with two cracked ribs and listened for voices that weren't coming, and somewhere in that long wet dark he understood that he had traded four lives for one more set of pressure readings, and that the readings, when the Weather Service finally recovered them from the wreck of his instruments, were *extraordinary*.

He'd never filed them. They were in the tin box, under the ledger, under the photograph. Extraordinary numbers. He couldn't burn them and he couldn't use them, and so they sat there, the most valuable thing he owned, earning nothing, costing everything.

* * *

By afternoon the *Provision* was half-scraped and fully reproachful. Freya sat on her foredeck with his legs dangling into the cockpit and ate a lunch of bread, sardines, and the last of a lime, and did the second ledger, the one that wasn't in the tin.

*What I owe that isn't money:* four names. A mother in Corren who still wrote to Dov's last address, care of a boat that was a reef now. He'd gotten one of her letters once, forwarded by accident, and had read it standing in the post office until his legs went numb, and had never answered it, because what was there to write? *Your son trusted the wrong man. The pressure readings were extraordinary.*

*What I own that isn't things:* the ability to read water. To feel a squall building forty miles off by the way the light went pewter on the horizon. To know, without instruments, when a current was lying about its depth. His father had called it *the ear for the sea*, and had said it couldn't be taught, only inherited or earned, and Freya had earned his the hard way, which was the only way it came.

It was, he reflected, a strange kind of wealth: the one asset he had in surplus was precisely the asset that had gotten four people killed. Skill and guilt, the same coin, both faces worn smooth.

A gull landed on the rail and regarded him with the frank appraisal of a creditor.

"Don't start," Freya told it.

The gull started anyway.

* * *

He worked until the light went copper, and the work was good for him the way penance is good, which is to say it hurt in a clean, honest way that thinking didn't. He re-caulked the seam at the stern. He fed the fraying survey line through his hands foot by foot, re-whipping the bad sections, and found, coiled in the middle of the line, a knot he didn't tie—a figure-eight on a bight, Harrow's knot, the old man's signature, the one he'd used on everything, mooring lines and tea strings and the lanyard of his one possession, a brass whistle.

Freya sat with the knot in his hands for a long time.

The line was new. He'd bought it in Corren, two years after. Harrow had never touched it. And yet there it was, or there his hands insisted it was, and Freya understood that his hands were haunted and that this was fine, that this was maybe even the arrangement: the dead didn't haunt houses in this country, they haunted *work*, and if you did the work well they left you alone, mostly, and if you did it badly they tied knots in your lines to remind you.

"Okay," he said to the knot, to Harrow, to the copper water. "I hear you."

He finished the coil. He did not untie the figure-eight. Some knots you keep.

* * *

That night, with the harbor going violet and the lamps coming on along the quay one by one like a slow, forgiving thought, Freya opened the tin box one last time and made the final entry.

*Assets:* one boat, sound enough. One lamp, one line, one sextant, one jacket. One ear for the sea, haunted but functional.

*Liabilities:* 4,260 marks, compounding. Four names, non-compounding, non-forgivable, non-negotiable. One contract, signed, the terms of which were *simple*.

*Balance:* he came up short. He came up short in everything except the thing that mattered, which was that he knew how to do the work. Cape Verren's drowned shelf wanted mapping, and nobody alive mapped water better than a man with something to prove and nothing left to lose, and the Optimists knew it, which was why the contract existed, and Freya knew it too, which was why he'd signed, and the whole arrangement had the clean, ugly symmetry of a trap built by people who were very good at arithmetic.

But underneath the trap, underneath the debt and the dread and the fishbone smile he couldn't scrape off the inside of his eyelids, there was a smaller, quieter thing, and he made himself write it down, because writing made it real and real things could be fought:

*The sea took four from me. Tomorrow I go take something back.*

He closed the ledger. He turned the photograph face-up for the first time in a year—five squinting faces, Wen mid-laugh, Dov holding up a fish that was clearly Rosa's fish—and he said, out loud, to the harbor, to the gull, to whoever was listening on whatever frequency the dead used:

"One more pass. But this time it's mine."

The *Provision* creaked at her moorings, which was either agreement or the tide, and Freya chose agreement, because optimism was the most dangerous currency in the archipelago and he was, apparently, still spending.

He was up before dawn, provisioning, checking the sounder, running the engine until it stopped complaining. By the time the sun cleared the headland he was standing at the helm with the harbor falling away behind him and Cape Verren lying somewhere out there to the south—a smudge of a place on the edge of the charts, where the locals, Mateo had mentioned with a frown, were said to be unfriendly to surveyors, and where a cave was said to exist that *charges admission*, though nobody could say what it charged, or in what coin.

Freya turned the bow south and tried not to wonder about the price.
