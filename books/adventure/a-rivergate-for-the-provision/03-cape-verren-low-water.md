## 3. Cape Verren, Low Water

The *Provision* didn't so much arrive at Cape Verren as limp into it, the way a man limps into a dentist's office—knowing full well what waits inside and hoping, against all evidence, that today might be different.

Freya brought her in on the last of the flood tide, threading the channel markers with the kind of concentration that made his jaw ache. The cape itself rose ahead of him like a fist someone had forgotten to unclench: black basalt stacked in layers, gull-streaked, crowned with a lighthouse that hadn't been painted since before his mother was born. Below it, a scatter of tin roofs and drying nets huddled together as if for warmth. The village had no name on any chart he owned. The locals called it Verren, which was less a name than a shrug.

He tied off at the one working berth—a stone quay worn smooth by two centuries of hulls—and was greeted immediately by a dog of indeterminate breed who looked at him with the flat professional contempt of a customs officer.

"Morning," Freya said.

The dog did not return the greeting.

He climbed the quay steps and found the village arranged around a single street that ran downhill toward the water, as if even the buildings were trying to leave. A chandlery. A chapel with a leaning spire. A tavern called the Drowned Bell, its sign swinging on a bracket shaped like a fish that had given up mid-carving. And everywhere, people. Mending nets. Splitting fish. Watching him.

Not hostile watching, exactly. Not yet. It was the watching of people deciding something, and Freya had learned long ago that being decided about was rarely good for his finances.

The first one to speak was an old woman mending a net outside the chandlery, her fingers moving through the mesh with the speed of someone who could do it blind, drunk, or dead. She looked up at him once, took in the salt-crusted jacket, the surveyor's case slung over his shoulder, and the particular hungry set of a man working off a debt, and said:

"You're here for the cave."

It wasn't a question. It wasn't even an accusation. It was delivered with the weary tone of a woman who had said this sentence before and expected to say it again.

"I'm here to survey the shelf," Freya said. "Coastal mapping contract."

"Mm." She pulled a knot tight. "That's what the last one said too."

"The last one?"

"Tomás." She said the name the way you'd set down something heavy. "Came through nine years back, all brass instruments and good manners. Asked about the cave same as you will. I told him what I'm going to tell you." She finally looked at him properly, and her eyes were the pale gray of weather coming. "The cave charges admission."

"Admission," Freya repeated. "Like... a fee?"

"Like a price." She went back to her net. "The sea gives things back at Cape Verren, boy. That's the trouble with it. Most places, the sea takes and keeps. Here it takes and *returns*, and what it returns depends on what you can afford to lose. You look like a man who's already lost most of what he had." The knot tightened. "So whatever's left—be careful what you carry when you go in."

"That's very atmospheric," Freya said. "Do you know if the chandlery sells lamp oil?"

The old woman laughed—one short bark, genuine despite herself—and jerked her chin toward the door. "Ask for Sef. Tell him Bess sent you, or he'll charge you double." A pause. "Everything here charges double. It's how we know we're still free."

* * *

Sef was a barrel of a man behind a counter of a man, and he sold Freya lamp oil, spare wicks, a coil of good line, and—after some negotiation—a second-hand storm lantern with a cracked globe that Sef swore held "in all weathers that matter." He also sold Freya, unprompted and free of charge, a warning.

"You'll want to see the tide tables," Sef said, wrapping the lantern in newspaper. "Except there aren't any. Not proper ones. The Survey Office gave up on Cape Verren forty years ago. Said the tides here don't follow the moon."

"What do they follow?"

Sef shrugged, a geological event. "Nobody's ever gotten a straight answer out of them. Out of the tides, I mean. Or the Survey Office. Same thing, really." He leaned across the counter, lowering his voice to the register men use for superstition they half believe. "My grandfather kept a log. Sixty years of high and low water, written down every day, rain or shine. You know what it shows?"

"No idea."

"Nothing. No pattern. No rhythm. Some months the tide behaves like a tide. Then it'll fall three feet below low water on a cloudless Tuesday and come back like nothing happened, and everyone pretends not to notice because noticing is bad luck." He handed over the parcel. "Bess tell you about the admission?"

"She did."

"Good. Saves me the speech." Sef rang up the sale on a till older than the chapel. "One more thing, and then I'll let you spend your money elsewhere. The cave mouth only shows at extreme low water. Once in a generation, usually. My grandfather saw it twice. My father never saw it at all." He paused. "It showed last week."

Freya felt something cold walk up his spine wearing small boots. "Last week."

"Aye. Lowest water anyone living remembers. Stood open for the better part of a day, like a mouth waiting to be fed." Sef's face was carefully neutral, but his hands weren't; they'd gone still on the counter. "And now here you come. A stranger. With instruments." He shook his head slowly. "I don't believe in omens, mind. But I believe in arithmetic, and the arithmetic on this cape has gotten strange lately."

* * *

Freya spent the afternoon doing what he did best, which was work instead of thinking. He walked the shelf below the village with his transit and his notebook, shooting bearings, sounding depths where the water allowed, filling pages with the honest, boring numbers that paid for lamp oil and kept the Optimists' collectors at arm's length for another week.

The shelf itself was remarkable, he had to admit. The charts showed a gradual shoaling off Cape Verren—sand and gravel sloping away into the gray—but the reality was a drowned terrace of black rock, cut into channels and basins, ribbed like the gills of some enormous thing sleeping under the village. Water moved through it strangely. In the channels between the ridges, the current didn't run seaward with the ebb. It ran *in*. Gently, persistently, inland, against every law Freya had spent his life respecting.

He checked his compass. Checked it again. Walked twenty meters and checked a third time, in case the basalt was playing tricks with the needle.

Inland. Every channel. Running inland at slack water, when by rights it should have been running nowhere at all.

"Well," he said aloud, to no one. "That's fine. That's completely fine."

A gull overhead screamed what sounded suspiciously like laughter.

He ate dinner at the Drowned Bell, because the alternative was eating alone on the boat listening to the water run the wrong way, and he'd done enough of that kind of evening to last a lifetime. The tavern was low-ceilinged and smelled of woodsmoke, fried fish, and the particular damp melancholy of a building that had lost arguments with the sea and kept rebuilding anyway. Maybe a dozen locals, all of whom stopped talking when he walked in, then resumed talking in a lower key, the universal music of a village discussing a stranger.

The barkeep was a young man with forearms like mooring line and the resigned expression of someone who'd wanted to leave this place at fifteen and was now thirty-two. He poured Freya a beer without being asked.

"You're the surveyor," he said.

"Word travels fast."

"It's a small place. Word doesn't travel, it just stands still until everyone's heard it." He wiped the bar in front of Freya though it was already clean. "You'll want to know about the cave."

"Let me guess. It charges admission."

The barkeep almost smiled. "Bess got to you. Good. Look—" He glanced down the bar, then leaned in. "I'm not going to tell you not to go. Everyone tells you not to go, and everyone who says it has a cousin or a grandfather who went anyway. So instead I'll tell you what my grandmother told me, and she was the sanest person this cape ever produced." He set down the cloth. "She said the cave doesn't want your money and it doesn't want your blood. It wants your *reasons*. Go in for greed, you come out poorer. Go in for grief, you come out sadder. Go in for curiosity—" he spread his hands— "well. Nobody's ever come out of that one to report."

"And what did your grandfather go in for?"

The barkeep straightened up, expression closing like a shop shutter. "Never said he went in."

"He didn't come back from somewhere, though. I've been watching people's faces all day. Half this village is grieving somebody the regular way, and the other half is grieving somebody they won't explain."

For a moment the barkeep looked at him with something close to respect. Then he shrugged. "Tide's falling strange tonight," he said, which was not an answer, and turned to serve someone else.

* * *

Freya woke at midnight to a silence he didn't recognize, and it took him a full minute in the dark of his bunk to identify what was wrong.

No water sound.

A boat at anchor makes noise—the slap of swell against the hull, the gurgle of the wake line, the mutter of current under the keel. The *Provision* was making none of it. She sat on wet stone.

He was up the companionway in seconds, barefoot, heart going. The moon stood high and fat over the cape, and by its light Freya saw the sea leaving.

Not receding—*leaving*, the way a crowd leaves a square, all at once, with purpose. The water was pulling back off the shelf in long silver sheets, sliding down the channels between the black ribs, draining away toward a horizon that had gained half a mile since sunset. Kelp lay exposed and gleaming. Rocks that had never seen air in recorded history dripped and shone. Somewhere out in the newly born mudflats, stranded fish flopped with soft percussive sounds, and the dog from the quay—for reasons known only to dogs—was racing delighted circles through the puddles.

Freya stood at the rail and did the arithmetic, because arithmetic was the one prayer he still knew.

Spring tide, yes. Full moon, yes. But spring tides fell a foot, maybe eighteen inches below mean low water here. This was six feet down and dropping. He watched a marker buoy he'd noted at dusk settle gently onto the seabed like a bird landing. The lowest tide in a century, Sef had said, had come last week.

This one was lower.

Lights were appearing in the village windows. Doors opened. People came out onto the quay in nightclothes and oilskins, and nobody shouted, and nobody seemed surprised, and that frightened Freya more than the tide itself. They stood along the seawall in a silent row, looking out at the exposed shelf the way relatives look at an open coffin.

Bess was there, wrapped in a blanket, her gray eyes finding his across the dark water without any trouble at all.

"Told you," she called, not unkindly. "Sea's making its offer again. Best decide what you're carrying before you answer."

Freya looked down at the shelf. His instruments were aboard. His lamp was aboard. His line was aboard. And there, far out across the bared rock, where the channels converged like roads meeting at a crossroads no map admitted to, the retreating water was uncovering the base of the cape itself—and in the base of the cape, blacker than the black rock around it, a shape.

Ribbed. Arched. Open.

Like a throat, clearing its voice.

Freya told himself he was going below to check the tide tables he didn't have. He told himself he'd wait for morning, consult the villagers, do this properly, by daylight, with witnesses.

Instead he found himself reaching for the lantern, and the line, and the case of instruments, and his hands—his traitorous, debt-ridden, curious hands—were already moving before his caution finished drawing up its terms.

Behind him, out on the mudflats, the water kept withdrawing, patient as a lender, and the cave waited at the end of the newly made road with its admission price hidden somewhere in the dark.

The land, Freya thought, clipping the lantern to his belt, was making him an offer.

And God help him—he was already walking toward the counter.
