## 4. What the Tide Gives Back

The sea went out like a guest who'd remembered an urgent appointment elsewhere.

Freya noticed it first as a smell—the mudflats breathing out their ancient sourness across the anchorage—and then as a sound, a long wet sighing that came from everywhere at once, and only finally as a fact, when he climbed the mast to check his rigging and saw that the water was leaving.

Not receding. Leaving. The way a dog leaves a room it has decided against.

He sat on the spreaders for a long while with one arm hooked around the shroud and watched the ocean withdraw from Cape Verren in slow, embarrassed stages. Sandbars surfaced first, glistening like the backs of sleeping animals. Then a shelf of black rock that no chart in his possession admitted existed. Then a forest of kelp standing upright in the open air, dripping, absurd, like a congregation caught mid-prayer by a fire alarm.

His depth sounder read 0.4 meters where yesterday it had read eleven.

"Well," Freya said to the boat, because there was no one else to say it to, and because saying things out loud kept the arithmetic in his head from getting loud. "That's not right."

*The Vela*—his boat, his debt collateral, his entire net worth if you were feeling generous about a thirty-year-old sloop with a patched jib and an engine that started on persuasion rather than fuel—rocked gently on her mooring as the last of the water slid away beneath her keel. She would ground soon. He should be worried about that. He noted, distantly, that he should be worried about that, the way you note a bill arriving from someone you've been avoiding.

Instead he watched the sea keep going.

By late afternoon the flats stretched half a mile toward the cape's foot, and the locals had come out of their houses to stand at the edge of the dry world and look at what had been uncovered. Freya rowed ashore—it took some doing; he dragged the dinghy over two hundred meters of seabed that squelched and sucked at the oars like the ground itself had opinions about visitors—and joined the small crowd at the tideline.

An old woman stood nearest the water's ghost, wrapped in a coat too heavy for the season. She looked at Freya sidelong.

"You're the surveyor," she said. Not a question. News traveled fast in a place this small, especially bad news wearing a clipboard.

"Coastal mapping," Freya said. "Contract work."

"Mm." She turned back to the flats. "My grandmother saw a tide like this once. She said the sea only gives back what it means to collect on."

"That's not how tides work," Freya said, and immediately regretted it, because it sounded like the kind of thing a man says right before the wilderness makes an example of him.

"No," the old woman agreed pleasantly. "I expect it isn't."

She wandered off. Freya stood there being wrong in the salt wind.

* * *

He told himself he was going back to the boat. He got as far as the black rock shelf before the lie ran out of fuel.

The cave mouth opened in the cliff below the cape like something between a doorway and a wound. It was ribbed—there was no other word for it—arched stone ridges running up and over the entrance in regular curves, worn smooth, so that the whole thing resembled a throat seen from the unfortunate end. Water still trickled out of its floor in thin silver threads, gathering in pools that reflected the low orange light like coins at the bottom of a well.

Every instinct Freya owned stood in a row with its arms crossed, shaking its head.

Instinct number one: caves that appear when the sea leaves are caves that close when the sea comes back. Instinct number two: locals who make jokes about admission charges are not joking. Instinct number three—he checked this one twice, patted his pockets, confirmed it was still there—no contract in the world required him to go into a hole in the earth that looked like it had teeth.

But.

The Optimists' ledger did not have a column for instinct. Pell's smile did not have a setting for *the tide was weird today, boss, I stayed on the boat.* Freya owed eleven thousand four hundred and sixty marks, plus interest accruing at a rate he tried not to think about in units smaller than weeks, and the contract specified a complete survey of the drowned shelf at Cape Verren, and here was the drowned shelf, un-drowned, offering itself up like a gift with a receipt attached.

Besides. He was a storm chaser. His whole profession was walking toward the thing everyone else walked away from, on the theory that the thing usually couldn't hurt you if you understood it, and that understanding it was worth the walk. This theory had killed four people. He carried it anyway. It was the only inheritance he had.

He went back to the boat for lamp, line, chalk, and the emergency rations he told himself he wouldn't need, which is the traditional prayer offered before entering any cave.

* * *

The line he anchored to a boulder on the rock shelf and paid out behind him, coil by coil, the way his father had taught him—*the rope remembers the way home even when you don't*—and stepped through the throat.

Inside, the cave was bigger than the outside had any right to promise. The lamplight reached maybe ten meters and gave up; beyond that, the dark had texture, a sense of volume, like standing inside a held breath. The ribbed arches continued overhead, each one further apart than the last, so that walking deeper felt like descending into the vertebrae of something enormous and patient. Drip water counted time somewhere off to his left, unhurried, indifferent to him.

The floor was sand and shell and, here and there, things the sea had carried in and forgotten: a boot sole, a bird skeleton light as paper, a fishing float gone green with age. Freya marked the walls with chalk every twenty paces and let the line run through his gloved hand, and told himself the tightness in his chest was the air, which was fine, actually, moving faintly from somewhere deeper in, which meant somewhere deeper in connected to the outside, which meant—

Which meant the cave went *in*. Toward land. Against everything.

He stopped. Held the lamp high. The flame leaned, very slightly, inland.

"Huh," Freya said, and heard his own voice get swallowed politely by the dark.

It was another hundred meters before he found the camp.

He smelled it first—old smoke, old cloth, the particular staleness of a space someone had lived in and left—and then the lamp found it: a shallow alcove off the main passage where the ribs of the cave curved around a pocket of floor like cupped hands. A rusted stove, cold. A bedroll rotted to lace. Tins with their labels dissolved, their contents long since become geology. And propped against the wall, facing the entrance as though its owner had spent his last stretch of light watching the way out—

A skeleton.

Freya's whole body performed several operations at once, most of them involuntary. He did not scream, which he counted as professional. He did step backward onto his own coiled line and nearly strangle himself with the rope home, which he elected not to count at all.

The bones were browned and settled, clothed in the remains of a heavy surveyor's coat, brass buttons green with verdigris. One hand lay palm-up on the thigh. The other was extended toward the entrance, fingers slightly curled—not grasping, exactly. Reaching. The posture of a man who had died watching the tide come back in.

"I'm sorry," Freya said, because there didn't seem to be anything else to say to that. "I'm sorry. That's—" He stopped. Started again. "That's a bad way. I'm sorry."

The cave accepted the apology without comment.

He crouched, at a respectful distance, and made himself look properly, because the man deserved a witness and because Freya's training had a cruel habit of functioning even when the rest of him wanted to be anywhere else. Surveyor's coat. Surveyor's boots, good ones, resoled twice. And beside the femur, half-buried in drifted sand:

A brass instrument.

Freya lifted it with both hands, the way you lift a sleeping animal. It was a theodolite—or had been once, before decades of salt air had furred it green—but not any pattern he knew. Smaller. Denser. The engraving on the base plate had been worn nearly smooth by handling, but under the lamp, tilted just so, letters surfaced: *T. ILESANMI — SURVEY OFFICE — PROPERTY OF THE CROWN, AND BEFORE THAT, OF NO ONE.*

Tomás Ilesanmi. The name snagged on something in Freya's memory, some file or rumor from his Survey Office days—a field man who'd gone out along this coast nine years ago and never come back, written off as weather, case closed, condolences issued to no one because there was no one to issue them to.

Nine years. The bones said nine years. The coat said nine years. The tide, presumably, said nine years too, in whatever language tides used for bookkeeping.

And beneath the instrument, wrapped in oilcloth tied with tarred cord, flat and rectangular and heavier than it looked:

A chart.

Freya's heart did something unsporting. He untied the cord with fingers that had suddenly developed a tremor he chose to attribute to the cold, and unfolded the oilcloth, and there it was—waterproof stock, waxed, the kind of medium that outlasts its owner, drawn in a surveyor's hand so precise it looked typeset.

It showed a river.

That was the first thing, and it took him a moment to see past it to the impossible thing, so let it stand: a river, inked blue-black, wide and confident, running from the coast inland through country Freya half-recognized—the middle reaches, the gorge country, the high plateau. Tributaries marked. Depths sounded. A settlement noted in tiny capitals. Someone had surveyed this river with love, or obsession, or both, which in Freya's experience were frequently the same trade.

The impossible thing was the arrow.

Rivers flowed downhill. Rivers flowed to the sea. Every river Freya had ever mapped, sailed, fallen into, or cursed flowed obediently toward the coast, because gravity was not a system you could negotiate with. But this arrow pointed inland. The current markings—those careful little chevrons every surveyor uses—ran from the sea, up the channel, into the mountains. The soundings deepened going upstream. The annotation beside the source, in handwriting grown shakier as the years in the cave accumulated, read:

*IT FLOWS BACKWARD. IT HAS ALWAYS FLOWED BACKWARD. STOP CHECKING. START BELIEVING.*

And below that, smaller, pressed hard enough into the waxed stock to emboss it:

*THE SOURCE REMEMBERS YOU BACK.*

Freya sat down on the cave floor without entirely deciding to. The lamp threw his shadow huge against the ribbed stone, a giant hunched over a dead man's treasure, and he was aware of the image even as he lived it, aware of how it would look to anyone finding him here, and he found—to his mild horror—that he did not care.

Because the arithmetic had started again. It always started. Eleven thousand four hundred and sixty marks. Interest compounding like mold. Pell's smile, which Freya now understood was not a facial expression but a financial instrument. And here, in his hands, a document that could not possibly exist, describing a phenomenon that broke the fundamental courtesy rivers owed to gravity, located—if the annotations were honest, and they had the ring of honesty, the ring of a man who had stopped caring whether anyone believed him—in country that was, by every law Freya knew, unmapped, unclaimed, and therefore *priceless*.

The Optimists would pay for this. The Survey Office would pay for this. Governments, universities, syndicates, collectors—there was no shortage, Freya realized with a sensation like warmth spreading through a frozen limb, of people in this world who would empty their accounts for a river that flowed the wrong way. Names, coordinates, provenance. He could clear the debt twice over. Three times. He could buy *Vela* outright, new sails, a new engine, an engine that started out of respect rather than pleading. He could—

He could stop being afraid.

The thought arrived quietly, in the drip-water silence, and it was worse than all the greedy thoughts combined, because it was true. That was the thing greed was actually made of, Freya suspected, sitting in a dead man's camp with a dead man's chart: not the wanting of things, but the wanting of the fear to stop. Every terrible transaction he'd ever signed had been a down payment on that.

The skeleton watched the entrance with its reaching hand.

"You were going to sell it too?" Freya asked him. "Is that why you're here? Did you come back for it?"

The bones declined to answer, but the alcove answered for them, in its way. The rotted bedroll. The stove burned down to slag. Nine years of one man alone in the dark with a discovery worth a fortune, and nothing sold, nothing sent out, nothing but a chart wrapped in oilcloth and an instrument engraved like a vow. Whatever Tomás Ilesanmi had decided about his river, he had decided it down here, by lamplight, with the tide coming in, and he had decided *against*.

Freya looked at the chart a long moment. Then, carefully, he rolled it, rewrapped it in the oilcloth, and tucked it inside his coat, against his chest, where he could feel it with every heartbeat like a second pulse.

"For safekeeping," he told the skeleton. "That's all. Safekeeping. We can argue about the rest later."

Somewhere far above and behind him, faint as a memory, the sea sighed against the rock shelf.

Freya froze. Checked his watch. Did the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do since afternoon—the tide tables, the anomaly, the rate of fall—and arrived at a number he liked considerably less than the price of a new engine.

The water was coming back. And it was coming back early.

He gathered the brass instrument, snuffed nothing because the lamp was electric and merely dimmed it, and turned for the line, moving fast now, the chalk marks sliding past in the lamplight like a countdown. Behind him the camp sank back into the dark, the reaching hand, the cold stove, the nine patient years.

At the mouth of the cave, pausing with the orange evening light on his face and the first thin sheet of seawater already sliding silver across the rock shelf toward his boots, Freya put his hand flat against the ribbed stone—throat-stone, door-stone, toll-gate-stone—and felt, or imagined he felt, a pulse under the rock. Slow. Deep. Going the other way.

"The source remembers you back," he repeated aloud, testing the weight of it.

Then the sea took back the shelf, and Freya ran.
