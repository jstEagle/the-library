## 5. The Dying Cartographer

The skeleton coughed.

Freya had been standing in the throat of the cave for the better part of a minute, lamp held high, telling himself a very reasonable story about how the bones against the far wall were just bones—old ones, patient ones, the kind of bones that had made peace with being bones—and then the skeleton coughed, and every reasonable story he had ever told himself died at once.

"Oh," said Freya, to nobody. "Oh, no."

The sound came again: wet, rattling, unmistakably alive. It rose from a hollow behind a shelf of rock where the lamplight didn't quite reach, and it was followed by a voice—thin as fishing line, but threaded with something that refused to be pitied.

"If you're one of Okonjo's people," the voice said, "you're eleven years too late for the survey and four hours too early for my funeral, so kindly make yourself useful in the interval."

Freya's legs informed him they were considering resignation. He overrode them. He picked his way across the ribbed stone floor, ducking under a low fold of rock that the sea had spent centuries polishing smooth, and swung the lamp toward the hollow.

An old man lay there on his side, curled like a comma, wrapped in a rotting oilskin that might once have been Survey Office gray. His beard was white and magnificent, the beard of a man who had decided long ago that grooming was a problem for people with shorter to-do lists. His eyes, when they found Freya's, were clear and dark and furious with life.

"You're not a warden," the old man said.

"No."

"Pity. I had insults prepared." He tried to push himself up on one elbow, failed, and settled for glaring upward with dignity. "You're a salvager. Or a smuggler. You have the boots of a man who owes money."

"The boots are new," Freya said weakly.

"New boots bought on debt. Yes. I know the type." The old man's breath caught, and the cough took him again, long and terrible, his whole frame shuddering like a boat in weather. When it passed, he lay back and studied Freya with an expression that was doing arithmetic of its own. "How did you get in? The tide's been up for days."

"It went out. An hour ago. The lowest water anyone's seen in a hundred years, according to the woman who threatened me on the dock."

Something moved across the old man's face—not surprise so much as recognition, the look of a man hearing a knock he'd been expecting for decades. "So it opened for you." He said it flatly, without wonder, which somehow frightened Freya more than wonder would have. "It doesn't do that for everyone. It did it for me, once. Eleven years ago." He laughed, a dry papery sound with no humor in it anywhere. "I thought it was luck. That was my first mistake. Everything after that was interest."

*Interest.* The word landed in Freya's chest like a hook finding flesh. He crouched down, keeping the lamp between them, mostly because his hands needed something to do. "You've been trapped here eleven years?"

"Trapped?" The old man considered this. "Trapped implies I intended to leave. For the first year I told myself I was waiting out the tides. For the second year I told myself I was finishing my work. After that I stopped lying, which was very restful." He gestured vaguely with a hand that was mostly knuckle and sinew. "There's water seeping through the rock—a spring, brackish, enough to drink if you don't mind tasting stone. And the cave keeps fish. Small ones, silver, come in with the high tide and forget the way out. We had an arrangement, the fish and I." A pause. "I ate them raw. Don't make that face. You'll eat worse before your story's done; everyone does."

"I'm not—" Freya started.

"You came in through a sea cave that legend says charges admission, following a chart-shaped itch in your brain, alone, in debt, at the edge of monsoon season." The old man's eyes glittered. "I know exactly what you are. I was you. Give it thirty years and a beard."

"My name is Freya Zhou."

"Tomás Ilesanmi." The name came out of him like something he'd been carrying so long he'd forgotten its weight. "Cartographer. Third class, then second class, then, briefly and disgracefully, first class, until the Survey Office decided I'd gone native." He smiled, and it transformed his whole ruined face into something young. "They were right. I had."

Somewhere above them—or below them, or behind them; in a cave it was impossible to say—the sea made a sound like a door being tested.

Freya felt it in his teeth before he heard it: a change in pressure, a deep subsonic shift, the whole cavern adjusting itself around incoming weight. He looked back the way he'd come. The passage floor, dry and ribbed when he'd entered, now wore a thin skin of water that hadn't been there ten minutes ago. It reflected his lamplight back at him like a warning written in his own handwriting.

"The tide's turning," he said.

"It turns early here," Tomás agreed. "That's rather the point of everything I'm about to tell you, so do pay attention, because I will only have the strength to say it once, and because we have perhaps forty minutes before this becomes a tomb with two occupants instead of one." He said it cheerfully. Gently, even. As though discussing dinner plans.

"We should go *now*, then."

"And leave you carrying nothing? No." Tomás shook his head, and the effort cost him; he had to stop and breathe. "No, Mr. Zhou. I have waited eleven years in the dark for someone to walk in through that mouth, and I am not going to waste the appointment by letting you leave empty-handed and ignorant. Ignorant men with charts are how we got into this mess in the first place." He fumbled beneath the oilskin, beneath a second layer of sacking, and drew out a bundle wrapped in waxed canvas tied with cord that had gone green with age. "Help me sit up. Slowly. My ribs and I have a truce, and I'd rather not renegotiate."

Freya slid his hands under the old man's shoulders and lifted. Tomás weighed almost nothing, the way driftwood weighs nothing, all the living part of him already spent. But when he was propped against the rock, he gathered himself with such fierce deliberation that Freya instinctively straightened, the way you do in the presence of rank.

Tomás unwrapped the bundle.

Inside was a brass instrument—some hybrid of sextant and compass that Freya had never seen, its arc graduated in markings that weren't degrees—and beneath it, folded into eighths, a chart.

Even in the unsteady lamplight, Freya knew quality when he saw it. This was survey-grade drafting, the ink still dark after years in the dark, the linework confident and clean. And it showed a river. A river running from the coast inland, up through gorges marked with soundings, past a ferry crossing, past a village rendered in careful detail, up and up into canyon country—to a source. The source was drawn as a small circle, and around the circle, in Tomás's precise cartographer's hand, someone had written and then scratched out and then written again a single word.

But that wasn't what stopped Freya's heart.

What stopped it was the arrows. Every river flows downhill. Every river runs to the sea. The arrows on this chart ran the other way—from the saltwater coast, inland, uphill, against every law of water Freya had ever trusted to keep him alive.

"That's not possible," he said.

"Mm." Tomás tapped the chart with one yellowed nail. "I said the same thing, standing on the shore at Cape Verren with my instruments telling me the current was running upstream at three knots. I recalibrated the instruments twice. Then I threw one in the river, which solved the problem by removing the witness." His grin flashed. "The river flows backward, Mr. Zhou. From the sea to the source. Against gravity, or around it, or through some loophole in it—I never determined which, and I had eleven years to determine it, which tells you something about how well determination goes."

"Why?" Freya heard his own voice go quiet. "Why does it flow backward?"

"Because of what's at the top." Tomás's cheerfulness drained away, and what was underneath it was older and much heavier. "A spring. Water that holds what falls into it. Not reflects—*holds*. You look into it and it looks back with your own face wearing your own memories, and it keeps them. All of them. Perfectly. Forever." He exhaled. "The locals call it the Provision. The old charts call it the Rivergate. The Survey Office calls it Asset Verren-9, which is the most obscene name of the three."

Freya looked at the chart. He thought about Pell's smile, and the contract clause about salvage rights, and the number of his debt with its cheerful compounding interest, and he felt something rise in him that he recognized with shame: the hot bright animal arithmetic of *this could pay for everything.*

Tomás watched him think it. He had clearly watched men think it before.

"There it is," the old man said softly. "There's the look. Don't be ashamed of it. Greed is just hope wearing work clothes." He pressed the bundle into Freya's hands. The waxed canvas was cold and damp and heavier than paper had any right to be. "Take it. That's why you're here. That's why the tide went out—that's the only reason the tide has ever gone out, in the whole history of this coast. It wasn't making you an offer, boy. It was making me a delivery."

"The cave charged admission," Freya said. "The woman on the dock said—"

"It does. It always does." Tomás's gaze drifted somewhere past the lamplight. "It takes a memory. One. You won't miss it at first. You'll notice the hole later, some night, reaching for something ordinary and finding it gone—your mother's voice, the smell of a particular kitchen, the name of the street you grew up on. It takes the one you can least afford, and it never explains why." He looked back at Freya, and his eyes were terribly kind. "You'll find out what it took from you when you need it most. That's the admission price. Everyone pays. I paid. Ask me what I paid."

"What did you pay?"

And Tomás Ilesanmi, third-class cartographer turned first-class ghost, opened his mouth—

—and closed it again. Frowned. A small, lost furrow appeared between his eyebrows, the furrow of a man patting his pockets for keys.

"I don't remember," he said, and laughed, and the laugh broke halfway through and became the cough, and the cough went on too long, and when it ended he was smaller than he had been, and Freya understood with a lurch that the forty minutes were going to be generous.

"Water," Freya said. "Do you want—there's a flask in my kit—"

"Save it. You'll need it for the swim." Tomás waved him off, then gripped his wrist with a strength that startled them both. "Listen to me. The water's coming and I'm not going to be rude by outstaying it, so the important part comes now, and you will carry it out of here if you have to carry it in your teeth. Are you listening?"

"Yes."

"The source remembers you back." Each word came separately, hammered into place. "Whatever you bring to it, it returns with interest. Bring guilt, it shows you forgiveness until you drown in it. Bring grief, it shows you the dead until you won't leave. Bring greed—" his grip tightened, "—and it shows you everything you ever wanted, priced perfectly, payable forever. Men have walked into that water smiling. I watched one. He was still smiling when the current took him, three days later, because he wouldn't stop looking."

"Then why give me the chart at all? If it's a trap—"

"Because it's also true." Tomás's voice cracked open, and underneath the crack was nine years—no, eleven years—of love. "My daughter thinks I abandoned her. She was fourteen. I left her a note that said *surveying trip, back by autumn,* and I never came back, and she has spent her whole life since believing she wasn't worth returning to." His breath hitched. "She was worth everything. I stayed because the Survey Office wanted to bottle the source and sell memory by the dram, and someone had to stand in the doorway, and I was the only one holding a map. That's the truth of it. That's what she deserves to know. And you—" he jabbed a finger at Freya's chest, "—are going to tell her, because I cannot, because I am *done*, Mr. Zhou, I am finished, and a man's last map should be delivered by someone else's hands anyway. That's the whole trade. That's what cartography *is*."

"Your daughter," Freya said. "Her name."

"Mara." The old man said it the way you say a prayer you've worn smooth. "Mara Ilesanmi. Salt Hollow. She'll be angry. Good. Anger means she'll listen. Grief without anger just sits down and stops."

The water was at their knees now. Freya hadn't seen it arrive; it simply was, black and patient, sliding across the ribbed floor with the confidence of something that owned the property. The lamplight wavered. Somewhere in the upper dark, the cave made a sound like swallowing.

"Go," Tomás said.

"Not without—" Freya looked at the old man, at the hollow, at the arithmetic that was suddenly very simple and very obscene. "I can help you. I can get you out."

"No, you can't." Tomás said it without cruelty, the way you'd correct a child's navigation. "I weigh nothing because there's nothing left to weigh. The swim out is hard enough with a chart and a lamp and two good lungs. Take the third thing and leave the rest." He smiled, and it was the young smile again, brief and blazing. "Besides. I've been dead for years, son. Today is just paperwork."

The water reached their waists. Freya's lamp guttered. In its failing light, Tomás pressed the bundle against Freya's chest with both hands, folding Freya's fingers over it, sealing it there like a wound dressing.

"The source remembers you back," he said, one last time, close enough that Freya felt the words more than heard them. "So decide what you're bringing. That's the only choice you'll ever really get. Everything else is current."

Then he let go, leaned back against the stone, and closed his eyes with the composure of a man checking into lodging he'd booked long ago.

Freya swam.

He swam through black rising water with a chart against his heart and a lamp he refused to drop, through the throat of the cave, past the fold of polished rock, into the passage where the ceiling came down to meet the flood. Twice the current shoved him sideways and twice he found the line he'd run on entry, slick and singing, and hauled himself along it hand over hand while the sea breathed in behind him like something enormous deciding. Once—and he would never tell anyone this, would spend years not telling anyone this—he heard, distinctly, under the roar, an old man's voice saying *left at the rib, boy, mind the rib,* and obeyed it, and lived.

He came out of the cave mouth into gray evening light and gray evening air, and collapsed onto wet shingle, and lay there breathing like a bellows while the tide climbed the rocks behind him with the unhurried satisfaction of a collector closing a ledger.

When he finally sat up, the cave was gone. Not hidden—gone. The sea had sealed it the way a hand closes over a coin, and the water where the mouth had been lay smooth and blank as a page.

Freya unwrapped the chart with shaking hands. The ink was still dark. The arrows still ran backward. Around the little circled source, scratched out and rewritten, the single word stood clear in the lamplight he no longer had:

*Remember.*

He thought of Pell's smile. He thought of the number he owed. He thought of a girl named Mara in Salt Hollow who had spent nine years believing she wasn't worth coming back for.

The first weight of it settled onto his shoulders then—not the chart's weight, which was nothing, but the other thing, the living thing, the obligation with a heartbeat. He had carried debts before. This one was different. This one had a name, and the name was Mara, and the man who'd given it to him had paid the admission price without ever learning what it was.

Freya looked out at the darkening sea, at the place where a legend had swallowed itself, and heard himself say aloud, to no one, to the water, to the old man:

"I'll deliver it."

The wilderness, which had taken its payment and closed its account, said nothing at all.

But far down the coast, in a port town called Salt Hollow, a woman with her father's stubborn jaw was already asking questions about a stranger's salvage gear—and the tide, patient as interest, was already bringing things ashore.
