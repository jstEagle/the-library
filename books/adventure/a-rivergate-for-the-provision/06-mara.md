## 6. Mara

Nine years is a long time to learn the geography of a waiting room.

Mara Ilesanmi could have drawn it from memory, which was ironic, given that the Survey Office had never once drawn anything for her. The Salt Hollow annex was a converted customs house on the harbor road, salt-stained brick, windows fogged with the breath of a sea that never quite committed to being beautiful. Inside: a clerk's counter, a bench worn smooth by the backsides of the patient, a wall calendar that someone updated with the enthusiasm of a man being paid by the month rather than the day. The calendar said it was the fourteenth of the wet season's eve. Mara knew it was the fourteenth because she had filed a petition on the fourteenth of every month for one hundred and eight consecutive months, and the clerk, a kind, defeated man named Brannic, had once told her so.

"One hundred and nine," she said, sliding the folder across the counter.

Brannic took it the way he always did, with both hands, as though it might be fragile or possibly explosive. "Mara. It's monsoon season coming. The survey launches are being pulled back to—"

"I know what they're being pulled back to. I've heard it every year. The launches get pulled back, the petitions get shelved, and the shelf gets a coat of dust, and in spring I come back and we do this again, and you tell me the Survey Office appreciates my persistence."

"The Survey Office does appreciate your persistence," Brannic said, with the flat sincerity of a man reading from a plaque bolted to his own forehead.

"Then appreciate it out loud. Loudly. In the direction of someone with a boat."

He almost smiled. That was the thing about Brannic—after nine years he had stopped being a bureaucrat to her and become something closer to a fellow prisoner. "I filed your last one to the regional director myself. Hand-carried. I watched him set it on the corner of his desk, and I want you to know it stayed on the corner of his desk for eleven days before it migrated to the pile."

"The pile," Mara said. "The famous pile."

"The famous pile."

She looked out the fogged window at the harbor, where the fishing boats were doing their slow, grumbling morning arithmetic—nets out, nets in, fuel costs against catch weights. Somewhere out past the drowned shelf at Cape Verren, her father had vanished nine years ago with a survey charter, a brass theodolite, and a promise to be home before her birthday. He had missed thirty birthdays since, if you counted the ones before he left, which Mara did, because she was keeping score and the Survey Office was not.

"Brannic," she said, "what would happen if I stopped coming?"

He looked genuinely alarmed, which she found touching. "I'd file a petition about it."

She laughed, short and unamused, and took back her receipt—copy one of one hundred and nine, each one folded into a tin box at home that had once held her mother's sewing needles. Her mother was eleven years gone, which meant Mara had spent nearly half her life waiting on institutions: first the hospital's slow mercy, then the Survey Office's slow nothing. She had gotten very good at waiting and very tired of being good at it.

The bell over the door clanged as she left, and the harbor wind hit her with its usual cocktail of brine, tar, and frying fish from the Pier Nine stall, whose proprietor, a woman named Dessa, had been feeding Mara breakfast at cost for nine years on the theory that grief burned calories.

"The usual?" Dessa called.

"The usual."

"You look like the petition went well."

"You look like you need new spectacles."

Dessa laughed and cracked eggs into the pan. Mara sat at the counter's end, her back to the door, and let the harbor noise wash over her. It was in this posture—shoulders down, guard lowered, the first unclenched moment of her day—that she heard the auctioneer's voice from the salvage yard across the road, and it was in this posture that she heard the word that turned her spine to a rod of iron.

"—lot forty-one, brass theodolite, marine pattern, tripod included, minor corrosion, sold as-is—"

Mara did not run. Running would have meant hoping, and hoping was a currency she had stopped minting years ago. She walked. She walked fast enough that Dessa called after her about the eggs, but she walked.

The salvage yard was a fenced acre of drowned men's belongings: winches, anchors, hull planking gone soft as bread, the perpetual estate sale of the sea. The auctioneer was a thin man with a voice like a rusty hinge and a gavel he used more for rhythm than authority. Lot forty-one sat on a plank table between a crate of fog-bells and a coiled length of chain, and Mara looked at it, and her whole body went cold and then very, very hot.

It was her father's.

Not a theodolite like her father's. *Her father's.* The brass was pitted the same way, the tripod leg with the mended crack—the one Tomás Ilesanmi had splinted with a shim of teak and a strip of copper after dropping it down a ravine in the Karst survey, the year Mara was twelve—was mended the same way. She had grown up watching him calibrate it on the kitchen table, telling her, *A surveyor's instrument is honest, little fish. It only tells you where you are. Everything else is what you decide to do about it.* She had etched a small M into the underside of the base plate when she was eight, with a nail, in secret, so that the instrument would carry her name into the wild places even if she never did.

She flipped the base plate over with fingers that did not shake, because she had spent nine years teaching them not to.

There was the M. Crooked. Eight years old. Hers.

"Sold!" said the auctioneer, because the world was like that.

"Who brought this in?" Mara's voice came out level, which surprised her. Level, and quiet, and with an undertone like deep water.

The auctioneer looked at her over his ledger. "Salvage lot. Came in on the *Kestrel's Grace* yesterday, off a boat called the—hold on." He licked his thumb, turned a page. "*Tern*. Out of here, actually. Skipper's name's Zhou. Freya Zhou."

"Where is he?"

"Where's who?"

"The skipper, man, the *skipper*—"

"Down the yard, I expect, haggling with Ferro over his winch. Or the harbor office, settling his moorage, if the harbor office will see him, which between you and me they may not, on account of his accounts." The auctioneer shrugged, a man whose sympathies extended precisely to the edge of his ledger. "You want to bid on the theodolite? It's sold. Buyer's from the chandlery. He'll melt it down for fittings, most likely."

Mara put her hand flat on the theodolite, on the crooked M, on thirty birthdays of nothing, and heard herself say, in a voice she did not entirely recognize, "No. He won't."

* * *

She found Ferro's winch stall first, and Ferro, who was seventy and built like something a ship would be built out of, took one look at her face and pointed down the lane without a word. Mara had a reputation in Salt Hollow. It was not a large reputation. It consisted entirely of the fact that she had never once given up on anything, and that people found this inspiring at a distance and unnerving up close.

She found Freya Zhou at the harbor office steps, arguing with the harbormaster's assistant, and her first thought was not fury, which surprised her. Her first thought was: *he looks like a man carrying water in both hands and being asked to carry more.*

He was tall in the way of men who stooped to apologize for it, with a face that had been handsome before the weather got at it and had decided, sensibly, to become interesting instead. His jacket had been mended more times than her father's tripod. He was arguing about moorage fees with the desperate cheer of a man who had learned that smiling at clerks was cheaper than drowning.

"—and if you check the ledger from the spring, you'll see the moorage was paid through the—"

"The ledger from the spring shows a payment *promised*, Mr. Zhou. Promised in writing. In your handwriting. With a little drawing of a smiling sun next to it."

"The sun was a flourish."

"The sun was a lie with rays."

Mara stood at the bottom of the steps and looked at this man—this debtor, this salvage-seller, this stranger with her father's theodolite in his sold lot—and waited for the fury to arrive. It did not arrive. What arrived instead was colder and more useful: a question. *How did he get it?* Her father had not been robbed. Her father had been *left*. The Survey Office's own report—file 4471-C, sealed, she had seen the cover once through a clerk's carelessness—said the survey vessel *Provision* was lost with all hands off Cape Verren in a squall, no wreckage recovered, search suspended after six days.

Six days. She had counted them like a miser.

And now a boat called the *Tern* came into Salt Hollow selling brass instruments with her name scratched inside them, and its skipper was broke, and broke men did not sell instruments they had bought. Broke men sold instruments they had *found*.

Freya Zhou lost the argument, as the assistant turned away with the satisfaction of a small official exercising the only power he owned. Zhou stood a moment on the steps, shoulders doing that stooped apologizing thing, and then he saw her.

She watched him see her. She watched him take in the set of her jaw and do what men did—glance behind her for whoever she must be angry at on someone else's behalf. There was no one. She was the whole weather front.

"You're Zhou," she said.

"Depends," he said, with a smile that had clearly been deployed at creditors so many times it had worn smooth, "on whether you're holding a ledger."

"I'm holding a question. Where did you get a brass marine theodolite, tripod with a teak shim in the left leg, sold this morning at Ferro's yard, lot forty-one?"

Something moved behind his eyes—fast, quickly shuttered, but she had spent nine years learning to read the micro-expressions of clerks, wardens, and liars, and she caught it. Not guilt exactly. *Recognition of guilt's arrival.* He had been expecting this question the way you expect a bill.

"That's an oddly specific inventory," he said.

"I'm an oddly specific person. Where did you get it?"

"Salvage."

"From where?"

"Salvage," he said again, gently, the way you'd repeat a word to a person you suspected of being unwell, "is a broad profession."

"Salvage," Mara said, "is what you call it when you pick up something the sea dropped. What the sea dropped off Cape Verren nine years ago was a survey vessel called the *Provision*, and every man on her was left there, and the Survey Office suspended the search after six days, and I have filed one hundred and nine petitions asking them to go back, and this morning I watched a man with a smiling-sun signature sell my father's instrument to be melted into chandlery fittings." She took a breath. The harbor noise seemed to have gone quiet around them, or perhaps she had simply stopped hearing it. "So you can tell me where you got it, Mr. Zhou, or you can tell it to whoever you'd tell it to if I were a warden instead of a daughter. I'm told the warden's office takes a great interest in unreported salvage."

That landed. She saw it land. Unreported salvage was the small print that hung over every man who made his living off drowned things, the charge that turned a bad season into a criminal one.

Freya Zhou looked at her for a long moment. Then he did something she had not expected, and which she would think about, later, for a long time.

He sat down. On the harbor office steps, in the wind, in front of the assistant's disapproving window, he sat down like a man whose legs had finally filed their own petition, and he said, "You'd better be who I think you are."

"I'm Mara Ilesanmi."

He closed his eyes. "Tomás's daughter."

"You knew his name."

"I know the name of the man whose chart is in my cabin," Freya Zhou said, "and whose last words were about you."

* * *

Later—much later, after the harbor office, after the chandlery, after she had stood in Ferro's yard and bought back lot forty-one from a bewildered fittings-buyer at three times its auction price and not cared even slightly—Mara sat across a scarred table from Freya Zhou in the back room of the Pier Nine stall, while Dessa brought food neither of them touched, and the theodolite sat between them like a witness.

He told it straight, which she gave him credit for. The contract from the Optimists. Cape Verren. The impossible tide. The cave. And then the part she had waited nine years for and had never once, in all her rehearsed versions of this moment, imagined would arrive: the skeleton that was not a skeleton. The man trapped by the tide. The chart pressed into a stranger's hands.

"He was dying," Freya said. "The tide was coming back. I couldn't—there was no way to carry him. He knew it before I did. He said—" Zhou stopped. He looked at the theodolite. "He said, *Tell my daughter it wasn't the Survey Office that left me. It was worse than that, and she should know the difference.* And he said the source remembers you back. And then he made me go, because the water was rising and he—" Freya's voice did something small and involuntary. "He pushed me. Toward the exit. A dying man pushed me to safety and stayed."

Mara sat with it. The fury she had carried for nine years—fury at the sea, at the office, at the six days, at the pile, at the whole indifferent machinery of the world—sat with her too, and she found, to her astonishment, that it had changed shape. It was no longer a wall. It was a door, and it was open, and through it she could see a thing she had never had before: a *how*. A chart. A route. A source. A father's last work, unfinished, waiting for exactly the kind of stubborn, specific person she had spent nine years becoming.

"He said it wasn't the Survey Office," she said.

"He said it was worse."

"Then the Survey Office will want to know that." She was already rearranging the world in her head—petitions, wardens, the sealed file, the word *worse* like a splinter working toward the surface. "And the Optimists will want the source, and you owe the Optimists, and you were going to sell it to them."

Freya Zhou had the decency not to lie. "I was going to try," he said.

"And now?"

"Now," he said slowly, "there's a daughter sitting across from me holding her father's theodolite, and I'm beginning to suspect the arithmetic has changed."

Mara put her hand on the brass base plate, on the crooked M, eight years old and eternal.

"Mr. Zhou," she said, "you're going to take me up that river."

Outside, far out past the harbor bar, a smudge of cloud was gathering on the horizon in no hurry at all, the way the first storms of the season always gathered—patient, enormous, and already on its way.
