## 7. Accusation at the Harbor

Mara had learned to read a man by his gear.

Nine years of petitioning the Survey Office taught you patience, and nine years of waiting tables in Salt Hollow taught you people, but it was the salvage stalls that taught you everything else. Every Saturday she walked the harbor rows, cataloguing what came out of the water, because the sea kept better records than the government did. A rusted depth gauge might mean nothing. A brass theodolite with a hairline crack in the housing and a strip of red tape on the tripod leg—that meant something. That meant Tomás Ilesanmi's kit, which had gone into the water with Tomás Ilesanmi nine years ago and never come out until now.

The stall keeper was a broad woman named Doss who sold eel traps in summer and other people's tragedies in winter.

"That one," Mara said, pointing at the theodolite laid out on oilcloth between a ship's bell and a jar of pickled something. "Where'd it come from?"

Doss squinted at her. "Came off a boat. Storm chaser fellow, patched hull, patched everything. Selling off what he doesn't need to pay for what he does." She shrugged. "You want it? Fifty."

"I want to know where he found it."

"Found it? He *had* it, girl. It was his."

"It wasn't his."

"Everything's somebody's," Doss said comfortably, "until it's on my cloth. Then it's mine till it's yours."

Mara put fifty on the table because her hands were shaking and she needed them to be holding something. The tape on the tripod leg was faded but intact—red electrical tape, wound twice, the way her father marked every instrument he loved so he could find his own in a crowded survey camp. *Thieves are lazy,* he used to say. *They steal the thing, not the mark.*

She turned the theodolite over. Scratched into the base plate, small as an apology: **T.I. — for Mara, when she's ready.**

She sat down hard on a bollard and held her father's instrument against her chest while gulls argued overhead about nothing, and by the time she looked up, the storm chaser's boat was coming into the harbor like a wounded animal returning to the herd—slow, listing slightly, patched in eleven places she could count from here.

* * *

Freya knew the harbor was watching him before he tied off.

It always watched him. Debtors get a particular kind of attention—not hostility exactly, more the way you watch weather. But today the attention had a temperature to it, and when he stepped onto the dock with his duffel over his shoulder, a woman was standing in his path with the stillness of someone who had rehearsed this moment for a long time.

She was maybe thirty, sun-dark, with the forearms of someone who worked lines for a living and the eyes of someone who worked ledgers of a different kind. She held a brass theodolite the way other people held knives.

"You're the one selling at Doss's stall," she said.

"Among other crimes, yes."

"This is mine."

He looked at the instrument. He'd found it wedged in the cave mouth, half-buried in silt below the skeleton that hadn't been a skeleton, and he'd taken it without thinking much about it—the way you take anything in a place like that, because taking is what desperate people do. Now he thought about it quite a lot.

"Ma'am," Freya said carefully, "I think we should talk somewhere with fewer listeners."

"We'll talk right here." Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening part. "My name is Mara Ilesanmi. The man who owned that instrument was my father. Tomás Ilesanmi. Survey cartographer, second class. Missing nine years, presumed drowned at Cape Verren, except nobody ever went to look because the Survey Office filed him under *inconvenient* and closed the folder." She took a step closer. "So you're going to tell me where you found this, and you're going to tell me now, and if the word *bought* comes out of your mouth I will put you in the harbor and let the Optimists collect what's left."

There were perhaps forty people within earshot, and all of them had stopped pretending to work.

Freya considered his options. Running was one; he was good at running, it was practically his profession. But she had said her father's name like it was a door she'd been knocking on for nine years, and Freya knew something about doors that stayed shut. Also—and this was the thought that decided him—she deserved to hear it before anyone else did. Before Pell. Before the government. Before the world got its hooks in it.

"Your father is dead," he said quietly. "I'm sorry. I found him three days ago, in a cave under Cape Verren, exposed by the low tide. He'd been trapped there a long time. He was alive when I reached him." He paused. "Barely. But alive."

Something moved through Mara's face like weather crossing water—grief and fury and a third thing underneath both, some terrible bright relief that her father hadn't simply been forgotten by the ocean. She didn't cry. She gripped the theodolite tighter instead, knuckles going pale.

"What did he say?"

"He gave me a chart. Waterproof case, river chart, drawn in his hand. And he told me—" Freya stopped, because saying it aloud made it real again, and he'd been enjoying the two days it had taken to feel unreal. "He said, *the source remembers you back.*"

Around them, the harbor resumed its noise, disappointed. A good confrontation needs shouting; this had only truth, which draws smaller crowds.

"The source," Mara repeated.

"That's all he had time for. The tide was coming back in. I nearly didn't make it out myself." He hesitated, then did the thing that would define the rest of his life whether he liked it or not: he unslung the duffel, dug past spare line and a tin of biscuits, and drew out the waterproof case. "I could have sold this. I considered it. I'm in debt to people who consider things for me. But it seemed—" He searched for the word. "*Wrong* seemed too small for it. Here."

He held it out.

Mara stared at the case as though it might bite, then took it with both hands, the way you take communion or a verdict. She opened the seals. Inside, the chart lay flat and immaculate, ink unfaded after nine years in the dark: a coastline she knew, and then a river that ran the wrong way—inland from the sea, up through country no chart showed, toward a blank space in the interior marked with a single symbol her father used for things he couldn't explain. A circle with a dot. His notation for *I saw it, don't ask me how.*

"He drew this," she whispered. "In there. In the dark. With a dying man's hands." Her thumb traced the backward current. "He found it. Nine years ago he found it, and they left him."

"Who's *they?*"

"The Survey Office. The government. Whoever decides what maps are allowed to exist." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "You. You found this and your first instinct was to sell it."

"Yes," Freya said. "That's fair."

* * *

Cass Okonjo had been nursing the same cup of tea at the Harborlight Café for forty minutes, which was standard procedure. Wardens who wanted to see a port clearly did not walk the docks; they sat above them with a window and a cup going cold, and they watched what people did when they thought no one official was looking.

What she was looking at, specifically, was Freya Zhou. The file on him was thin but colorful: storm chaser, licensed, insured until he wasn't, currently carrying debt paper held by the Optimists' regional office—a fact Cass knew because knowing facts about indebted men in coastal towns was half her job. The other half was charts. Unregistered hydrographic surveys were contraband. A chart of waters the Survey Office hadn't mapped was not just illegal; it was, per the statutes she'd sworn to, a threat to navigational order, which was bureaucratic language for *we don't know what's out there and that frightens us.*

And the woman confronting him—Ilesanmi, daughter of the lost cartographer, the one who'd filed eleven petitions that Cass's own office had denied—was now holding a document case that Zhou had produced from his bag in full public view.

Cass set down her tea and felt the specific weariness of a person about to do their job when their job has started to look wrong.

Through the window she watched the exchange continue. Zhou wasn't running. That interested her. Guilty men run, and men with debts to the Optimists run faster than most. This one stood in the open with his contraband in a grieving woman's hands and his shoulders down, taking whatever she said like a man accepting a bill he agreed was accurate.

Then Ilesanmi said something sharp—Cass read it off her posture, the forward lean, the jabbing finger—and Zhou answered, and to Cass's professional surprise, he pointed at the chart and then at himself, and whatever he said made the woman go still.

Not calm. Still. There was a difference, and Cass had spent fifteen years learning it. Calm was peace. Still was a decision being made at great speed behind a quiet face.

Cass left money on the table and went down to the harbor, because whatever happened next, she needed to be standing in it.

* * *

"You memorized his last words," Mara said, "and your first plan was to sell his map to pay a loan shark."

"Not a loan shark. Worse. An optimist." Freya spread his hands. "They smile while they ruin you. It's in the name."

"Don't joke."

"I'm not. Joking is what I do when I'm not terrified, and I've been terrified since Tuesday." He nodded at the chart. "Look at the annotations. Look at the margins. Your father didn't draw that for buyers—he drew it for someone who'd follow it. There's a warning system in the notation, tide marks, safe camps. That's not merchandise. That's a letter."

Mara looked. He was right, and she hated that he was right, because it was easier to hate a thief than to stand next to one and agree about anything. The margins were dense with her father's small careful script, and near the head of the backward river, almost hidden in the hatching of the canyon walls, three words in a language of symbols only she would fully read:

**Finish it, little fish.**

Her childhood name. Her father's private joke, from summers on survey boats, when she'd been small enough to fit in the equipment locker and had loved it.

The harbor tilted. Or she did. Freya's hand came out and steadied her elbow, and she let it stay there for exactly two seconds before removing it.

"Why tell me?" she asked. "You could have disappeared. You still can. You owe dangerous people money and you're holding the most valuable document on this coast. Giving it to me costs you everything."

Freya looked out at the water for a moment. When he spoke, it was without any of his earlier deflection.

"Because I watched a man die alone in the dark with his life's work in his hands, and the last thing he did with that work was give it away. To me. A stranger. Because I was there and breathing." He shrugged. "Seems rude to profit off the lesson."

"That's a stupid reason."

"It's the only kind I have."

Mara laughed—one short, cracked sound, surprised out of her—and hated that too, and then stopped hating things for a moment because she was tired and her father was dead and the map in her hands said *little fish*, and grief does strange arithmetic.

"All right," she said. "Here's how this goes. You know the route's start. I know my father's notation better than anyone alive. We go up that river together. You get whatever you were going to sell—I don't care what, I care about the truth of how he died and who signed off on leaving him. I get that. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Freya said. "With one amendment. If we find what he found—if the legend's true—we decide together what happens to it. No selling behind backs. No burning behind backs."

"No burning," Mara repeated, and something in Freya's face flickered, quick as a fish under ice, and she noted it and let it go.

"Deal," he said, and spat in his palm, and she looked at the offered hand with such contempt that he wiped it on his trousers and they shook dry like civilized people.

* * *

"Excuse me."

They both turned. The woman approaching wore the grey-and-green of a Warden Service field coat, and her badge caught the afternoon light with the cheerful indifference of all badges everywhere. She was tall, close-cropped, with the patient expression of someone who had already won whatever argument was about to happen.

"Warden Okonjo," she said. "Survey Enforcement. I couldn't help noticing—" everyone always said this, Cass reflected, as though the entire harbor hadn't been watching—"the document you're holding, Ms. Ilesanmi. May I?"

Mara's arm curled around the case. Freya, to his credit, stepped slightly forward rather than back.

"It's a family heirloom," Mara said.

"It's an unregistered hydrographic survey of restricted waters," Cass said pleasantly. "Which makes it contraband. Which makes confiscating it my job." She let that sit, then added, quieter: "It also makes your father's death an enforcement matter, Ms. Ilesanmi, and I've read his file. All of it. Including the parts that were sealed."

Mara went very still again. Freya watched the two women look at each other and had the distinct sensation of standing between two weather fronts.

"Sealed parts," Mara said slowly.

"A Survey Office vessel withdrew from Cape Verren nine years ago, three days after your father reported a significant discovery. The withdrawal log lists 'equipment failure.' The equipment failure lasted long enough for the monsoon to trap him." Cass's voice stayed level, but her jaw was tight. "I've been a warden for fifteen years. I know what a lie in a logbook looks like. It looks like that one."

"And your job?" Freya asked. "Is it to help us, or to take the map?"

"My job," Cass said, "is to recover that chart and deliver it to my superiors, who will seal it in a vault along with everything else that frightens them." She paused. "What I do after my job is done is a separate question I haven't finished answering. You have until I do. Don't leave Salt Hollow before tomorrow's tide—but do leave before the day after. There are people asking about you, Mr. Zhou, and they aren't wearing uniforms. They're wearing smiles."

She touched her cap brim, turned, and walked away up the dock, leaving the two of them in the gold late light with a stolen chart, a dead man's last words, and the strong impression that they had just been threatened and warned by the same sentence.

"She knows about Pell," Freya said.

"She knows more than she's saying." Mara tucked the case inside her coat, against her heart, where it had apparently lived for nine years already. "Question is whose side she's on when it matters."

"There are sides?"

"There are always sides." She looked at him—really looked, cataloguing the patched boat, the debt-thinned face, the man who'd handed over the one valuable thing he had because a dying stranger's manners embarrassed him. "You realize we're partners now. Against wardens, syndicates, and the entire Survey Office. Over a river that flows the wrong way."

"When you say it like that it sounds insane."

"How does it sound the other way?"

Freya considered the horizon, where the first high cloud of the season was building itself patiently out of nothing, white and enormous, like a promise the sky was making to itself.

"Honest," he said.

Mara followed his gaze to the cloud, and to the invisible river beyond it, and to the blank space on her father's chart where a circle with a dot waited like a held breath.

"Tide turns at dawn," she said. "Be at the north pier. Bring everything you own that floats."
