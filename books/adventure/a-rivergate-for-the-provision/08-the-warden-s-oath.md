## 8. The Warden's Oath

The Survey Office in Salt Hollow occupied a building that had once been a customs house, then a granary, then briefly—during the year the government tried to make tourism happen—a welcome center. It had settled into its current identity as a place where paperwork went to be forgotten. Cass Okonjo had worked there eleven years and had learned to love it the way one loves an old dog: not for its beauty, but for its loyalty to certain small rituals.

Her ritual this morning was tea, taken standing at the window that overlooked the harbor, watching fishing boats go out under a sky the color of unpolished pewter. Her second ritual was reading the overnight incident log, which was usually a comedy of errors involving drunk fishermen, lost dogs, and one recurring entry about a man on Gull Street who insisted his neighbor's chickens were transmitting naval codes.

Today's log contained neither chickens nor drunks. Today's log contained a note from the harbormaster: *Warden Okonjo — you'll want to see what came off the boat at Pier 4. Or maybe you won't.*

Cass drank her tea, set down the cup, and went to see what she wouldn't want to see.

* * *

The chart lay flat on the harbormaster's desk between them like something recently deceased.

"Storm chaser brought it up from Cape Verren," the harbormaster said. He was a broad, sun-cured man named Doss who treated all government business as a personal inconvenience he was willing to endure for her sake. "Or that's the story. Woman at the chandlery says he was selling salvage gear too—good gear, salt-damaged, deep-water gear. The kind you don't come by unless you've been somewhere the water doesn't usually let you go."

"And the woman?"

"Mara Ilesanmi." Doss said the name carefully, the way you'd handle a hook still baited. "Tomás Ilesanmi's girl. She recognized the gear before anyone said a word about charts."

Cass looked at the chart. It was beautiful in the way old things are beautiful—not despite their damage but through it, the way a scar can be more interesting than smooth skin. Hand-inked coastlines. Soundings in a cramped, patient script. And running inland from the sea, drawn with a confidence that made her chest tighten, a river that flowed backward against every arrow of common sense.

She knew the legend. Everyone on this coast knew the legend. The cave at Cape Verren that charged admission; the spring that remembered; the cartographer who went looking for it nine years ago and never came home. Cass had been a junior warden then, newly badged, full of procedures. She'd filed the missing-persons report herself. She remembered thinking, as she stamped it, that Tomás Ilesanmi had seemed like the sort of man who would find exactly what he was looking for, and that this was the most dangerous quality a person could have.

"You're going to report this," Doss said. It wasn't a question.

"I'm required to report this."

"Sure." Doss picked up a pencil and began sharpening it with great attention, though nothing needed sharpening. "And I'm required to tell you that the last time somebody reported something from Cape Verren, we spent four months filling out forms about whether a sea cave constitutes navigable waters."

"Doss."

"I'm just saying the forms exist. I've seen them. They have sub-sections." He set the pencil down. "You could lose the paperwork. Things get wet around here. Monsoon season."

Cass almost smiled. That was the trouble with Salt Hollow—everyone assumed corruption was available like fresh bread, warm and local. "It doesn't work that way, and you know it."

"I know it doesn't," Doss agreed. "I just like saying it out loud once a year. Keeps my soul aired out." He slid the chart toward her. "Take it, then. But Cass—" and here his voice changed, dropped its harbor-town laziness, "—that's a dead man's handwriting. Whatever you do with it, do it like it matters."

Cass carried the chart back to the Survey Office in a waterproof document case, which was ironic, given where it had come from, and logged it into evidence, which was the only thing she knew how to do with a problem: write it down until it had a number and therefore, theoretically, a solution.

* * *

The regional office was in Corvane, three hours inland by rail, housed in a building that had never been anything but a government building and wore it like a uniform two sizes too small. Cass made the trip twice a year for reviews and once a year for the holiday party nobody enjoyed, and each time she noticed the same thing: the further inland you went, the less the sea mattered, until by Corvane the ocean was an abstraction, a blue margin on a map, a place where resources were located.

Deputy Director Havelock Vane received her in an office with no windows, which Cass had always suspected was deliberate. Vane was a neat man with neat gray hair and the particular calm of someone who had never once been surprised by anything, because everything had already happened in a memo.

"Warden Okonjo." He gestured to a chair. "You've brought something from Salt Hollow."

"A recovered document, sir. A nautical chart of unregistered provenance, apparently originating from the Cape Verren shelf. It depicts—" She hesitated, and hated herself for hesitating. "It depicts a river system flowing inland from the sea. Annotated in the hand of Tomás Ilesanmi, the surveyor who disappeared in '41."

Vane did not move for a moment. Then he opened a drawer, removed a pair of white cotton gloves, and put them on with the unhurried care of a man dressing a wound.

"May I?"

She passed him the chart. She watched his face while he read it, and what she saw frightened her more than any expression could have: nothing. Absolute, practiced nothing. Vane looked at the backward river the way an accountant looks at a figure he has seen before in another ledger.

"Where is the surveyor's daughter?" he asked.

"That's not—sir, the question of jurisdiction—"

"The daughter, Warden. Mara Ilesanmi. Is she in contact with the man who recovered this?"

"She confronted him at the harbor. Publicly. There may be some arrangement between them now. I don't believe either of them fully understands what they're carrying."

"No," Vane said softly. "They rarely do." He laid the chart flat and studied the annotations along the river's course, and when he spoke again it was in the tone of a man reading scripture he personally found tedious. "Thomas Ilesanmi filed a preliminary report in the spring of '41. Did you know that?"

Cass kept her voice level. "His file was sealed. I requested it after his disappearance. Access denied."

"It was sealed because it was wrong. Not incorrect—wrong. He described a hydrological feature of significant strategic value and recommended its permanent non-disclosure. His exact phrase was 'a well that should remain a well.' The Office determined that his judgment had been compromised by isolation and, frankly, by grief—he'd lost his wife that year—and the matter was reassigned." Vane removed his gloves, finger by finger. "The reassignment was never completed. And now, fourteen years later, the matter has walked itself into your evidence room."

"What matter, sir? What exactly am I holding?"

Vane folded his hands. For the first time, he looked at her directly, and Cass understood that whatever came next had been decided long ago, in a memo she would never see, by people whose names she would never learn.

"You are holding a map to a freshwater source of unusual properties. Properties that, if verified and controlled, would be worth more than every fishery, mine, and port on this coast combined. Properties that, if uncontrolled, would cause a stampede that would kill hundreds and end in the source's destruction anyway. There is no version of this, Warden, in which the public benefits from knowing. There is only a version in which they suffer from it."

"So the Office will study it quietly. Verify it. Protect it."

"The Office will do what it does with assets of this class." Vane stood, which ended meetings the way a period ends sentences. "You will recover the document—it appears to be traveling, so plan accordingly. You will locate and secure the source. And then you will seal it. Permanently. Demolition charges have already been authorized; a convoy departs within the week. You'll receive the engineering specifications tomorrow."

Cass did not stand. "Seal it permanently. Sir, that's—that's a geological feature. Possibly a unique one. You're ordering me to destroy it rather than understand it."

"I'm ordering you to destroy it rather than *sell* it," Vane corrected gently, "which is what everyone else who finds it will try to do. Consider the alternative owners, Warden. Consider the Optimists." He said the name without distaste, which was somehow worse—as if the syndicate were simply weather, a front moving across the ledger of the world. "A hole in the ground remembers nothing. People remember everything. Which would you rather be responsible for?"

He held out the chart, and she took it, because her hands were already reaching and refusing would have required a different person.

"One more thing," Vane added, at the door, almost kindly. "The Ilesanmi file. Unseal it. You'll want the full picture before you act. Consider it professional courtesy."

* * *

She read the file on the train home, under the little brass lamp, while the coast scrolled past the window in grays and greens.

The first forty pages were ordinary: commendations, survey reports, a disciplinary note for insubordination that read, in its entirety, *"Refused to falsify sounding data. Reprimanded. Note: his data was correct."* Cass found herself liking Tomás Ilesanmi page by page, the way you grow fond of someone through their marginalia—a joke in the corner of a tide table, a pressed flower that had no business surviving fourteen years in a government folder.

Then the preliminary report. His real handwriting, looser than the chart's, urgent.

*I have found the spring. I will not describe its location in writing, and I advise my successors not to seek it. What it holds is not treasure. It is every hour of every life that ever touched it, intact and waiting, and I have watched a man drink and weep for a morning he lost fifty years ago, and I have understood that we are not meant to carry our whole lives at once. Forgetting is not failure. Forgetting is mercy. Whoever controls this controls grief itself, and grief is the last thing that should ever have an owner.*

*If you are reading this and considering exploitation: I have seen what remembering costs. Ask the villages of the middle reach. Ask them why they cannot bury their dead.*

*Destroy this report if you must. But leave the well a well.*

Below it, in a different hand—Vane's hand, unmistakable, the same tidy strokes that had signed her orders—an assessment: *Surveyor compromised. Recommend termination of project and continued surveillance of subject. Source remains unverified. Priority: low.*

And beneath that, dated eight months later, a single line: *Subject entered Cape Verren shelf during anomalous low tide. No recovery operation authorized. Assets unrecoverable. File closed.*

No recovery operation authorized.

Cass sat very still while the train swayed, and outside the window the sea appeared and disappeared behind headlands, appearing and disappearing, indifferent and enormous.

They hadn't failed to rescue Tomás Ilesanmi. They had *declined* to. He had found the thing they wanted and refused to give it up, so they had waited for the tide to do what tides do, and then they had closed the file, and fourteen years later the same tidy hand had written orders for Cass Okonjo to recover the dead man's map and blow the dead man's discovery into rubble—partly, she now understood, to destroy the proof that the Office had left him in a cave to die.

Duty had always tasted like salt to her—bracing, clean, the flavor of the thing she'd chosen over an easier life. This tasted different. This tasted like ash, like something burned down to a structure that still stood but would never hold weight again.

The conductor passed through. "Corvane line ends at Salt Hollow, miss. You'll want to gather your things."

"I live there," Cass said. "I'm going home."

Home. Where a storm chaser and a cartographer's daughter were, at this moment, negotiating terms over a dead man's chart, sailing toward a spring that remembered everyone who ever touched it—including, Cass realized with a cold settling in her stomach, including the men who had signed Tomás Ilesanmi's death warrant, and what it had shown them, and whether they had flinched.

The convoy would take a week to assemble. The monsoon was coming early; every barometer on the coast said so. And somewhere upstream, on a river that flowed the wrong way, two grieving people were following a dead father's ink toward the one place in the world where the truth about him was still kept, alive and waiting, in the water.

Cass closed the file. Then, after a long moment, she opened it again, tore out the single page bearing Vane's assessments, and fed it to the little brass lamp.

The paper caught, curled, and was gone. The train carried her home through the dark, and she watched her own reflection ride the black glass of the window—badge, braid, and oath—and wondered which of the three would survive the week.

Tomorrow she would request a launch. Officially, she would be pursuing the stolen document.

Unofficially, she intended to reach the source before the charges did—and she had not yet decided whether she would light the fuse or stand on it.
