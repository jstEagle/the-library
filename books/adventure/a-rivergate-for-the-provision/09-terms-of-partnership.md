## 9. Terms of Partnership

### FREYA

The back room of the Gull's Rest smelled of tar, old rope, and the particular despair of a place that had been losing money since before either of them was born. Freya had chosen it for exactly that reason. Nobody came here to celebrate anything, which meant nobody would come here at all.

Mara Ilesanmi sat across from him with her arms folded and her father's chart—the copy, not the original; she'd insisted on holding the original herself, wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a sleeping child—between them on the table.

"Rules first," she said. "You don't touch the chart without asking. You don't lie to me, even usefully. And if I find out you've sold so much as a coordinate without telling me, I will feed you to the Optimists myself, feet first, so they can enjoy it."

"That's three rules."

"You want four?"

"I want to know what *I* get," Freya said. "Since we're doing rules."

She leaned back. The lamplight caught the ink stains on her fingers—she'd been up half the night copying her father's annotations into a separate notebook, he'd noticed, though he hadn't said so. Nine years of petitioning the Survey Office had taught her to keep duplicates of everything.

"Fine. Your terms. Go."

Freya had rehearsed this on the walk over. He'd rehearsed it twice more while pretending to study the menu, which was a single laminated page offering fish, fish stew, or yesterday's fish. It still came out of his mouth sounding worse than he'd hoped.

"The source's location. That's mine. I sell it to clear my debt—my debt only—and then I'm out. No syndicate follows us upstream, no collectors, no Pell. You get your father's truth, I get my hands and my boat, and we never speak again after."

"Charming ending," Mara said. "Very romantic."

"It's not supposed to be romantic. It's supposed to be survivable."

"Everything about you is survivable," she said, and there was something in her voice—not quite contempt, not quite its opposite—that made him sit up straighter. "That's your whole personality, isn't it? Surviving. You survive storms, you survive debts, you survived walking out of that cave with a dead man's chart and a dead man's last words, and you didn't even—" She stopped. Pressed her knuckles against her mouth for a second. "You didn't even know his name until I told you."

"No," Freya admitted. "I didn't."

"He waited nine years in that cave. Or however long. Long enough to starve down to a skeleton with a pulse. And his last act on this earth was handing a stranger a map." Her voice didn't crack. That was somehow worse. "So no, Zhou. We don't 'never speak again.' Because whoever left him there—whoever knew where he went and let the tide come in behind him—I'm going to find them. And you're going to help me, because you're the only living person who heard him die."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere behind the wall, the barkeep dropped something glass and swore with real artistry.

"Those are my terms," Mara finished. "Truth and justice. Yours can be money. One of us should want something noble."

"And if they conflict?" Freya asked. "If finding out who left him means the source stays secret, and the source staying secret means I drown in debt?"

"Then you'll have to decide what kind of man you are," she said pleasantly. "I've already decided what kind of woman I am. It took me nine years and it was free."

He almost laughed. It startled him, the almost-laugh—he hadn't produced one of those in months, maybe years, and here it was, showing up uninvited in a tar-smelling back room across from a woman who had every reason to hate him.

"Deal," he said, and put out his hand.

She looked at it like he'd offered her a wet fish.

"We shake on it or it doesn't count," he said.

"I know how contracts work. I've read enough of yours." But she took his hand, and her grip was stronger than he expected, and she held it one beat longer than a handshake strictly required, as if sealing something in wax.

* * *

### MARA

Here was the thing Mara would not say out loud, not yet, possibly not ever: Tomás Ilesanmi had taught her to read charts before he'd taught her to read words. She could look at his linework the way other people recognized handwriting from a parent's letter, and the chart in her lap was his, unquestionably, down to the little hooked way he drew depth soundings, like commas, like pauses for breath.

Which meant the last thing her father had done with his life's great skill was draw a map for a stranger.

She was still deciding how she felt about the stranger. He kept doing things that complicated the decision. When she'd accused him at the harbor, any sensible thief would have run. Instead he'd stood there in the rain holding out a waterproof case like an apology, saying *your father's name was Tomás, he told me the source remembers you back, I'm sorry, I'm so sorry,* and the sorry had sounded real, which annoyed her, because she'd built nine years of armor against sorries that weren't.

Now he was counting coins onto the table between them, frowning.

"What are you doing?"

"Arithmetic. Fuel, food, spare line, lamp oil. I have—" he moved coins into two piles, one much smaller than the other, "—this. Which gets us maybe two days upstream if the current behaves, and the current has shown zero interest in behaving."

"How far is the source?"

"Your father's chart says eleven days' travel by river. His numbers might be generous. Dead men tend to round optimistically."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't a joke." He swept the small pile toward her. "That's everything liquid I own that isn't bolted to the boat. Call it a good-faith payment."

Mara stared at the coins. It wasn't much. It was, in fact, insultingly little, and also it was literally everything he had, and both of those facts were true at once, which seemed to be the general condition of Freya Zhou.

"I don't want your money."

"You'll need money."

"I want to know why you didn't go straight to the Optimists with the chart. You had it for two days before I found you. Two days of a head start on a debt that's going to cost you your fingers." She narrowed her eyes. "Why am I sitting here instead of reading about your dismemberment in the harbor gazette?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then he turned his cup around slowly, a full rotation, like a man checking a compass.

"Because when he gave it to me, he said *the source remembers you back.* And I've been thinking about what that means. And I think—" he exhaled through his nose, "—I think a thing that remembers you back isn't the kind of thing you sell. I think it's the kind of thing that finds out."

"Superstition."

"Probably. But I once watched a storm turn because I'd named it out loud, and I don't test the wilderness twice." He shrugged. "Also, honestly? Selling it felt wrong, and I wanted to see if the feeling would pass. It hasn't. Give it time."

There it was again, that almost-funny thing he did, the joke with a trapdoor in it. She was starting to suspect the trapdoors were load-bearing.

"New term," she said. "If we reach the source and it turns out you *can* sell it without the sky falling, you still tell me first. Before any buyer. Before Pell. Before anyone."

"Before anyone," he agreed. "Even though that term costs me leverage."

"Especially then. Leverage is how men like you get people killed."

"Men like me," he repeated. "What kind of man is that, exactly?"

"The kind who chases storms for data. My father read your file, you know. After the Verren disaster. He said—" she hesitated, because this part hurt, "—he said you were the best surveyor on the coast, and that the storm wasn't weather, it was arithmetic, and somebody had done the arithmetic wrong. He felt sorry for you. He used to say grief is just math you can't finish."

Freya had gone very still. In the lamplight she could see the exact moment the sentence landed somewhere below his ribs.

"He talked about me?"

"He talked about everyone. He was a cartographer. Mapping people was his hobby." She softened despite herself. "He liked you, Zhou. From a distance. From a file. Take that however you want."

### FREYA

He took it badly, was the answer. He took it like a man handed a bill he'd assumed was forgiven.

Tomás Ilesanmi, dying in the dark, had known who Freya was. Had known the storm, the crew, the arithmetic gone wrong—and had still pressed the chart into his hands. Freya had spent two days telling himself the old man had been delirious, choosing anyone, choosing the nearest warm body. Now he had to live with the alternative: that Tomás had chosen him *on purpose*, with full knowledge, the way you'd choose a tool you knew was cracked but still held an edge.

"So we're partners," he said, mostly to move past the feeling. "Provisionally. With terms."

"With terms," Mara agreed. "Shall we write them down? I have paper."

"I have a better memory than paper."

"Everyone says that right up until the flood."

He grinned despite himself, and she wrote the terms anyway, in her precise surveyor's hand, on the back of a rejected Survey Office petition form, which they both found quietly satisfying:

1. Chart remains joint property. Neither sells, copies, nor alters without the other present.
2. Zhou may sell the source's location ONLY after informing Ilesanmi first, and only to clear his existing debt.
3. Ilesanmi leads all inquiry into Tomás Ilesanmi Sr.'s abandonment. Zhou assists fully.
4. Either party may void the agreement by honest declaration. Lying voids it automatically.
5. Nobody drowns anybody.

"Number five seems oddly specific," Freya observed.

"You met my father in a flooding cave. I'm covering my bases."

They signed it. She had a pencil; he had a stub of charcoal; the signatures looked mismatched and lopsided together, hers neat as typeset, his like a squall line crossing a calm horizon.

Then, because the terms were signed and honesty was technically now contractual, Freya said: "For the record. If it comes down to your father's justice or my debt, I don't actually know which I'd pick. I'd like to be the man who picks justice. I haven't been that man in a long time."

Mara studied him for a moment with those unsparing surveyor's eyes, taking his bearing, fixing his position.

"Noted," she said. "It's going in the file."

"What file?"

"The one I'm keeping on you." She tucked the contract away. "Everyone gets one. Yours is currently titled *Thief, Possibly Repentant.*"

"And hers?" he asked, nodding at her notebook. "Yours, I mean. What does the wilderness call you?"

She paused at the door, and for the first time all evening she smiled—a small, dry, dangerous smile, the first spark off a flint.

"*Daughter, Armed,*" she said. "Come on. If we're leaving on the morning tide, you need to show me this boat of yours, and I need to pretend it doesn't frighten me."

### FREYA

The *Provision* sat at her mooring looking, as always, like a argument between a fishing trawler and a rumor. Freya watched Mara walk the deck with her father's habit of pressing her palm flat against things—gunwale, mast, winch—reading the wood grain like terrain.

"She leaks," he warned. "A little. Character."

"She lists to port."

"She's thinking about it."

Mara snorted—an actual, involuntary snort—and then looked briefly horrified at herself, as if her face had committed treason. Freya filed the sound away next to the almost-laugh from earlier, in the small growing collection of evidence that this arrangement might become something other than a contract.

Then he saw it, and stopped filing.

Out past the breakwater, riding the last grey light, a launch with a white hull and a cheerful pennant was standing in toward the harbor. He knew that hull. He knew that pennant. The Optimists flew sunshine-yellow everywhere they did business, and their launches were always immaculate, always smiling, always exactly where a debtor least wanted them.

"Pell," he said.

Mara followed his gaze. "Friend of yours?"

"The opposite of a friend of mine, rendered in excellent craftsmanship." His stomach performed a slow, professional capsize. "He's early. He's never early. Early is for people who doubt."

"Terms of partnership," Mara said calmly, stepping aboard and coiling a line with unsettling competence. "Clause five. Nobody drowns anybody—which implies, I think, that nobody gets drowned, either. Can this boat outrun that launch?"

Freya looked at his beloved, listing, leaking, thinking-about-it vessel, and then at the white hull sliding into the harbor mouth like a signature on a contract he hadn't read.

"Not tonight," he said. "But the tide turns at dawn, and the river's too shallow for his draft. We leave at first light, and we leave *fast*."

"Then I'll sleep aboard," said Mara, "and you'd better hope your boat thinks fast."

Down the harbor, the yellow pennant dipped cheerfully in the evening wind, like a wave hello.
