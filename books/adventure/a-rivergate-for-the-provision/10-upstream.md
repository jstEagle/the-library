## 10. Upstream

The tide was still going out when we left Salt Hollow, which meant the river was coming in—upstream, inland, against every law of water I'd ever trusted. It slid past the harbor pilings in a long silver ribbon, patient as a debt collector, and it did not care one whit what the almanacs said about it.

"Your boat," Mara said, standing on the dock with her arms crossed and her father's chart rolled under her arm like a baton she intended to beat me with, "has a name?"

"The *Provision*."

She looked at the boat. The boat, to be fair, deserved the look. She was a thirty-year-old survey skiff I'd patched with fiberglass, prayer, and parts from three different wrecks. Her paint had once been blue. Her bilge pump worked on a toggle switch I had to hold down manually, like feeding a sick animal.

"You named this *Provision*."

"I named her that before she got like this." I tossed the last coil of line aboard. "She's earned redemption. Haven't you, girl?" The engine coughed, thought about it, and caught. "See? Character."

Mara stepped aboard the way a person steps onto ice they don't trust—weight low, knees bent, dignity intact. She took up station at the bow without being asked, which told me two things: she'd spent time on boats, and she'd already decided she was going to be more useful than me at everything. Nine years of petitioning clerks will do that to a person. You learn to occupy space like you own the deed.

We cast off at dawn, and the river took us the wrong way, and I have never in my life been so happy to be incorrect.

* * *

Here's the thing about a backward river that nobody tells you, because nobody's supposed to exist: it doesn't fight you. Every current I've ever run pushes against your hull, drags at your keel, makes you earn every mile. This one carried us. We came off the plane into the lower channel and the water simply picked the *Provision* up by the scruff and moved her inland, smooth as a hand sliding a letter across a desk.

I cut the engine just to feel it.

The silence was enormous. Gulls behind us at the coast, forest ahead, and between them this river running uphill toward the hills like it had somewhere important to be and no intention of explaining itself.

"That's not possible," I said, mostly to myself.

"You've said that four times since the harbor," Mara said. "You might want to retire the phrase."

"It helps to say it out loud. If I keep saying it, maybe physics will get embarrassed and fix itself."

"Physics isn't embarrassed. Physics is watching you the way a cat watches a moth." She unrolled the chart across the foredeck and weighted the corners with her boots, her water flask, and my lunch, which she commandeered without negotiation. "Tomás marked six stations on the lower reach. Depth soundings, flow rates. Look at his handwriting—it doesn't shake until station five. Whatever he found between four and five is what scared him."

I leaned over the chart beside her. Tomás Ilesanmi had drafted like a man writing scripture: fine lines, exact numbers, little annotations in a cramped engineer's shorthand. And there, at station three, a note in the margin that made the hair on my arms stand up:

*Flow measured 1.4 m/s against gradient. Re-measured. Same. Re-measured drunk. Same.*

"He tested it drunk," I said, with real reverence.

"He tested everything twice," Mara said quietly. "He used to say a map is a promise. You don't make promises sloppy." She smoothed the paper flat with both hands, and for a moment she wasn't talking to me at all. "Nine years I thought he'd drowned stupid. A bad crossing, a squall, some ordinary tragedy. He wasn't careless. He was careful right up until the sea ate him."

The guilt arrived on schedule—the same old passenger that lived in my hull alongside the debt. I'd pulled a dead man's gear out of a cave and sold half of it before I even knew whose it was. But standing next to his daughter, looking at his handwriting, I understood something I hadn't back in that throat of stone: he hadn't died lost. He'd died *working*. There was a difference, and the difference mattered to her, so it mattered to me.

"Then let's be careful too," I said. "Twice on everything."

"Three times," said Mara. "You strike me as someone who needs the third pass."

* * *

By midmorning the coast had folded away behind us and the river narrowed into country that didn't appear on any chart I owned—which, given that I owned charts of most of this coastline, legally or otherwise, was its own kind of message. The banks rose steep and green, hung with vines that trailed their fingers in the current. The water ran clear over red gravel bars, and every few hundred meters we passed stones stacked in cairns, old ones, moss-eaten, deliberate. Somebody had been marking this channel long before Tomás. Long before anybody with a license.

"Those aren't survey markers," I said.

"No," Mara agreed. "Survey markers don't have offerings." One cairn near the waterline held a rusted tin cup, a child's shoe gone stiff with age, and a knot of braided hair tied off with copper wire. "These are tolls. Or thanks. Hard to tell the difference out here."

"Out here being where, exactly? Because according to the Hydrographic Office, we are currently traveling through a hillside."

"According to the Hydrographic Office," Mara said, "my father never existed either. His file's sealed. I checked every year for nine years." She said it evenly, the way you recite something you've repeated so often it's worn smooth. "Offices are very good at not existing things. Rivers apparently are too."

I let that sit. Some silences you fill because you're nervous; others you fill because the other person needs you not to. I steered around a submerged log and gave her the quiet.

Around noon the current did something new. It slowed—not slackened, but *slowed*, deliberately, the way a crowd parts around a funeral—and the *Provision* drifted through a stretch where the water went glass-still and mirrored the sky so perfectly that for a full minute I genuinely could not tell which way was up. My reflection looked back at me from below, sitting in the boat upside down, and I had the distinct, unhinged impression that he was also steering, and that our courses were somehow the same course.

"Freya." Mara's voice, careful. "Your hands."

I looked down. My knuckles were white on the wheel. I hadn't noticed. Somewhere in that mirror-still mile, my body had remembered the storm—the one that took my crew, the one I didn't talk about except to creditors and wardens—and it had started bracing for a wave that wasn't coming.

"Sorry," I said, and shook them loose. "Old weather."

"I know old weather," she said, and didn't ask, and I loved her a little for that, in the way you love a stranger who doesn't poke a bruise.

The stillness ended. The current picked us back up, brisk and businesslike, and the river bent north into hills that no honest map acknowledged, and I laughed out loud—actually laughed, alone at the wheel of a debt-crippled skiff, running a felony river through unmapped country with a woman who had every reason to hate me.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing I couldn't have explained to Pell or the Optimists or any clerk at the Survey Office: for the first time in three years, nothing on this water was lying to me. The tide tables lied. The ledgers lied. The Optimists smiled while they counted your fingers. But this impossible river ran exactly as hard as it ran, hid exactly what it hid, and asked nothing of me except attention. The wilderness, it turned out, was the only party in my life that kept honest books.

"You're grinning," Mara called from the bow. "Stop it. It's unsettling."

"Can't help it. Everything's finally telling me the truth."

"That's how it gets you," she said, but she was smiling too, down at the chart where her father's careful lines climbed toward the interior like a staircase drawn in ink.

* * *

We made the first of Tomás's stations by late afternoon—a gravel shelf beneath a leaning fig tree, exactly where the chart promised, depth markings matching within a finger's width. Three times, per the new house rules. Mara sounded it herself with a weighted line, lips moving as she counted, and when she finished she sat back on her heels and looked at the number on the line like it was a letter from the dead.

"He was here," she said. "Actually here. Standing where I'm standing."

"Nine years ago, give or take."

"Give or take." She coiled the sounding line with quick, angry precision. "The Survey Office told me he was probably lost in the Verren Deep. Open water. They said searching the shelf was 'resource-prohibitive.' Resource-prohibitive." She stood, slung the coil into the boat hard enough to make the hull complain. "They knew. Didn't they. They knew he'd found something and they sealed the file and let him—"

"We don't know that yet."

"Don't tell me to be reasonable, Zhou. Reasonable is what people say when they want you to stop asking."

"I wasn't going to say reasonable." I held up both hands. "I was going to say: whoever signed that seal has a name, and names are findable. But first we need proof, and proof is upstream. So—" I nodded at the fading light, the narrowing channel, the dark green walls closing in friendly and ominous all at once. "We camp, we run daylight only, and tomorrow we chase your father's staircase."

She glared at me a moment longer, then nodded once, sharp, and began setting out the chart weights with the exaggerated calm of someone choosing not to throw something. I recognized the technique. I invented it.

* * *

We camped on the gravel shelf under the fig. I built a small fire because the night off the water goes cold fast, and because fire is the oldest lie humans tell the dark—that we're safe—and I needed the lie after the day I'd had.

Mara cooked. That surprised me. She produced rice, dried fish, and a spice tin from her pack with the efficiency of a field veteran, and when I raised an eyebrow she said, "Nine years of filing petitions means nine years of eating badly on purpose. I learned to cook well out of spite." The fish was, annoyingly, excellent.

We ate in companionable silence while the river murmured past, still flowing the wrong way, still unbothered by our opinions. Overhead the stars came out in numbers the coastal towns had forgotten existed. No lights out here. No grids, no syndicates, no offices. Just the old honest dark.

"Tell me about the storm," Mara said suddenly.

I nearly dropped my bowl. "That's abrupt."

"You watched me grieve all day. Fair trade." She wrapped her hands around her tea mug. "You don't have to. But you flinch at still water, and I'd rather know why than guess."

So I told her. Not the version I gave creditors—the sanitized one, the one with the weather as villain—but the true one. Four years ago, a category system forming two days out, data suggesting it would curve east, my whole crew wanting to run the survey window anyway because the contract paid double for completion before monsoon. And me, holding the barometer, reading the pressure falling faster than any model predicted, and making the call to push for one more day. One more day of data. Data over safety. The system didn't curve east. It came straight up the coast at night, and the *Meridian* went down with three of my friends aboard, and I survived because I was the one who'd insisted on wearing the harness during the final sounding run, and they hadn't, because I'd been too focused on the instruments to repeat the order.

"And then the Optimists bought the debt," I finished. "Salvage costs, contract penalties, funeral expenses I guaranteed. They call it compassionate lending. Pell calls it 'weathering together.'" I stared into the fire. "Every month I pay them for the privilege of having been wrong."

Mara was quiet for a long time. The river ran. Somewhere upstream an animal cried out, high and strange, and got no answer.

"My mother used to say," she finally offered, "that grief is just love with nowhere to put itself." She turned her mug slowly. "You've been carrying yours with nowhere to put it for four years. I've been carrying mine for nine. Maybe that's why we're both so tired."

"That's the most depressing thing anyone's ever said to me, and I've had my kneecap appraised by a man in a yellow suit."

"You're welcome." She lay back against her pack and pulled her blanket to her chin. "First watch is yours, Zhou. Wake me at moonset. And Freya?" Her eyes were already closing. "Thank you for not selling the rest of his gear."

"I almost did."

"I know. Thank you for almost not."

* * *

Moonset came gray and early. I woke Mara, took the second sleep, and dreamed of still water with my reflection steering underneath it, and woke feeling more rested than I had any right to.

We broke camp at first light and ran the river hard. By midday the channel had narrowed to fifty meters, then thirty, the banks rising into bluffs striped with old flood lines—except the flood lines were wrong, stacked too high, way above any water this channel should ever hold, like scars from a fight the river had won against gravity itself. The current strengthened. The *Provision* didn't even need the engine; I left her idling and let the water do the work, and the speed built and built until spray came off the bow and Mara had to weight the chart corners with her boots again.

And that was when I saw it.

Aft, far back down the valley we'd climbed, a smudge on the horizon where the river met the distant sea-glare. At first I took it for cloud. Then it moved against the wind. Then it grew a wake.

White hull. Twin engines. Running our exact line at a speed no honest survey vessel would attempt in an uncharted channel.

I knew that hull. I'd watched it idle outside Salt Hollow harbor for three years, cheerful as a shark in a good mood.

"Mara," I said. "Don't turn around. Turn around very casually."

She turned around very casually. "Is that—"

"Pell's launch. Yes."

"How? We left before dawn, we've run—"

"Pell doesn't catch up to people, Mara. Pell is always already arriving." I fed the throttle forward and felt the old girl answer with a rattle that was either determination or a death rattle. "There'll be a tracker. In the hull, the engine, my spare boots, wouldn't surprise me. Question is whether he knows where we're going or just where we are."

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously. If he knows where we're going, he's not chasing us—he's escorting us. Letting us do the dangerous part first." I checked the fuel gauge, which read optimistic in the way of all Optimist products. "If he only knows where we are, we can lose him in the narrows ahead."

Ahead, the river threaded between two bluffs into a slot barely wider than the boat, the current funneled and singing, the green walls swallowing the light. Tomás's chart showed the passage marked with a single notation in that cramped, careful hand:

*Narrows. Trust the water, not your eyes.*

Behind us, the white launch's engines rose to a growl I could feel in my teeth.

"Hold onto something," I told Mara, and pushed the throttle to the stop, and the wilderness—honest to the last—carried us both into the dark.
