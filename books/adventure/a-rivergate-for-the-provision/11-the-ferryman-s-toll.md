## 11. The Ferryman's Toll

The river did not care that Mara Ilesanmi had a plan.

Her plan had been a good one, too—she'd worked it out over two days of hard rowing, in the margins of her father's chart, in the careful block letters she'd learned at the Survey Office: *follow the current, mark the banks, do not trust the water.* She had estimated distances. She had noted where the tributaries forked. She had even, in a moment of optimism she would later be embarrassed by, drawn a little arrow where she thought they might stop to sleep.

The river had responded by flowing backward, ignoring every tributary, and delivering them—on the third morning, in a mist so thick Freya kept saying *this is fine, this is normal, mist is normal*—to a ferry crossing that did not appear on any map she had ever seen, including the one drawn by her father, who had seen this river with his own eyes and drawn it anyway.

"Well," Freya said, shipping the oars. "That's new."

The crossing sat in a bend where the river widened and slowed to something like thought. A wooden dock, silvered with age, jutted out from a bank of reeds. Beside it, a flat-bottomed ferry bobbed at its rope, big enough for six people and a mule, though there was no road on either bank that Mara could see—no road, no village, no reason for a ferry to exist except that someone, long ago, had decided this exact stretch of water needed crossing and had built the means to cross it and then, apparently, walked away.

Except someone hadn't walked away.

An old woman sat at the end of the dock on an upturned crate, mending a net that could not possibly have caught anything, because it had holes in it the size of dinner plates. She was tiny and brown as a walnut, wrapped in three shawls despite the warm morning, and she did not look up when the boat scraped against the pilings. She looked, in fact, like she had been sitting there since before the river learned which way to flow.

"Morning," Freya said, in the voice he used on hostile locals, which was to say cheerful with a thin wire of caution running through it. "We were hoping to—"

"I know what you were hoping." The old woman's voice was dry as the dock planks. She tied off a knot in her impossible net and finally raised her head. Her eyes were the pale gray of river fog, and they went to Mara first, and stayed. "You have his chin."

Mara's whole body went still. "I'm sorry?"

"Tomás. The mapmaker." She said it the way you'd say *the rain, the morning.* "He crossed here. Going up, like you. Came back down alone." A pause, and then, with terrible mildness: "Mostly alone."

"You knew my father?"

"I knew everyone who crossed. That's the job." She set down the net. "I'm the ferryman."

"You're—" Freya glanced at the ferry, at the empty river, at the woman's small hands. "With respect, ma'am, who poles the boat?"

"The boat knows the way. I just collect." She held out one palm, smooth and uncalloused as a stone from a riverbed. "Toll."

"Of course." Freya was already digging in his coat. "We don't have much—there's some coin, not much, and a compass that lies, and—"

"Coin." She said the word the way Mara's mother used to say *weevils.* "No. Not coin. Memory."

The mist moved a little. Somewhere in the reeds, a bird stopped singing mid-note, as if it had been listening.

"I beg your pardon," Freya said.

"Payment in memory. One recollection. The boat takes it, and you cross, and you don't look back at the water while it's working." She said it all in the tone of a woman reciting a ferry schedule. "You can choose which one. That's the rule. The boat doesn't steal. It *trades.*"

"Absolutely not," Freya said, at the same moment Mara said, "How much do you need?"

They looked at each other. Freya had the particular expression of a man watching someone walk toward a cliff edge with a pleasant smile. "Mara."

"It's a fair question." She turned back to the old woman. "How much? One memory gets us both across?"

"One memory per head." The ferryman's gray eyes didn't blink. "But yours will be enough for both of you, I think. Yours are—" she searched for the word, found it, and Mara wished she hadn't— "*dense.* You've been carrying them a long time. The boat likes a heavy load. Balances it."

"That's not how memory works," Freya said. "Memories aren't cargo. They're not— you can't just take one out. They're all tangled up with each other. Take one thread and the whole—"

"You're a storm chaser," the old woman said. "You know about pressure. Take enough weight off a system and everything moves." She shrugged, a small earthquake of shawls. "Or stay here. The reeds are comfortable. I've had people stay. They always think they'll find another way across." She nodded at the river, wide and slow and utterly without a bridge in either direction as far as the mist allowed them to see. "There isn't another way across."

Mara looked at the ferry. At the rope. At the far bank, invisible, somewhere out there past the white air, where her father had gone and not come back, where the answer to nine years of petitions and letters and closed office doors was waiting like a stone at the bottom of a well.

She thought about what she would give.

That was the mistake. She thought about it, and the thinking was the answer, because the moment she opened the door to the question, she already knew which memory stood closest to it. Not her father. Never her father—she'd sooner swim. Not the storm, not the night the Survey Office sent the letter that said *presumed lost, file closed,* not the funeral with no body where she'd been the only mourner under a tent in the rain.

Her mother.

Her mother, who had died the ordinary way, three years before the river took Tomás, and whose face Mara had been carrying carefully, the way you carry a bowl of water across a room—both hands, eyes down, don't spill.

"Mara." Freya's voice had gone quiet. He'd seen it too, then. He'd watched her face the way he watched weather. "Whatever you're about to do—don't."

"It's one memory."

"You don't get to pick which one. Not really. You think you do, but—"

"It's *my* memory." And it came out sharper than she meant, sharp enough that the old woman raised her eyebrows with professional interest. "I've been trading pieces of myself for nine years, Freya. To the Survey Office. To landlords. To anyone with a door I needed opened. At least this one tells me the price up front."

"That's exactly what worries me. Honest thieves are still thieves."

The ferryman made a sound that might have been a laugh, or a cough, or the river clearing its throat. "I like him," she said. "He argues. The last one just cried and paid."

Mara stepped onto the dock. The planks were warm under her boots, warmer than the morning justified, like something sleeping under them. She crouched in front of the old woman, so they were eye to eye, and the gray eyes were kind, which was somehow worse.

"Tell me the rules," Mara said. "All of them."

"You choose the memory. You hold it in your mind, clear as you can, and you put your hand in the water. The boat does the rest. It takes the memory and it gives you passage, and it doesn't take anything else, and it doesn't give anything back, and it doesn't make change." She counted these off on small fingers. "You will keep the *fact* of what you lost. You'll know you had a mother. You'll know her name. You'll know you loved her. You'll know she loved you, because some things are too big for the boat. But the face—" the old woman's voice gentled, "—the face goes where faces go. You'll know there was one. You won't be able to find it."

"And if I choose something smaller? A bad memory? Give it something I don't want."

"Then you'll cross lighter and meaner, and the boat will know you tried to cheat it, and the river has a long memory of its own." She smiled, and her teeth were small and even and very white. "You can't pay a toll in something you don't value. That's not a toll. That's litter."

*You've been carrying them a long time. Dense.*

Mara sat down on the dock, which was not in the rules, but the old woman let her. The mist drifted through. Freya stood at the boat with his hands in his pockets, not quite coming closer, not quite going away, a man who had learned the hard way that you couldn't hold other people back from ledges, you could only stand where they could see you.

"Can I ask you something," Mara said. "When my father crossed. What did he pay?"

The ferryman was quiet for a long moment. The net lay forgotten in her lap.

"He held his hand over the water for a long time," she said. "Longer than anyone. I thought he wouldn't pay. I thought he'd turn back." Her eyes went soft and distant, watching something nine years gone. "Then he paid, and he paid big, and he didn't tell me what it was, because you don't have to tell me, that's another rule. But he stood up after, and he said—" she mimicked it, a low voice, a man's careful diction, "—*'I'd like to file a complaint with whoever runs this river. That's not a toll. That's a robbery.'* And then he laughed. He laughed like it was funny. He laughed all the way onto the boat."

Mara's throat closed. Her father's voice, out of a stranger's mouth, on a dock in the mist. *I'd like to file a complaint.* That was him. That was exactly, precisely, unbearably him.

"What did he pay?" she whispered.

"Like I said. You don't have to tell me." The ferryman looked at her, and for a moment the gray eyes seemed very old, older than the dock, older than the ferry. "But he came back down this river alone, girl, with the tide chasing him, and he held that chart above the water the whole way like it was a child, and when he passed this dock he didn't stop, and he didn't look at me, and he was *smiling.* Whatever he'd paid—he thought it was worth it. He thought all of it was worth it. Remember that, when you get where he didn't."

Mara wiped her face with the back of her hand, briskly, the way you do when there's work to do.

"All right," she said. "I'm ready."

"Mara—" Freya started.

"Watch the boat," she told him. "If I come back meaner, you have my permission to say *I told you so,* once, and then never again."

She knelt at the edge of the dock and put her hand into the river.

The water was warm. That was the first shock of it—warm as bathwater, warm as a held hand. And the moment her fingers broke the surface, the memory rose in her like a bubble finding air, and she didn't even have to try to hold it, because it was the one her whole chest had been leaning toward since the old woman said *toll*:

Sunday morning. The kitchen with the cracked blue tiles. Her mother at the stove with her sleeves rolled up, humming something off-key, turning to say something to nine-year-old Mara—turning, and the light from the window coming over her shoulder, and the smile starting before the words did, and the face—

The face.

The river took it the way the tide takes sand: gently, completely, without malice. There was no pain. That was the horror of it. There was just a softness, a loosening, a thread sliding out of a weave so smoothly she felt the shape of the absence before she understood it was an absence. She gripped the dock. She held on to the name—*Amara, her name is Amara, she was Amara Ilesanmi and she hummed off-key and she burned the rice every single Sunday and she—*

The face went.

Not the hands—she kept the hands, flour-dusted, turning the rice. Not the voice—she kept the voice, saying *mara-mara-mara, come taste.* Not the warmth, not the Sunday, not the love, which was indeed too big for the boat. But the face itself was gone the way a word goes when you repeat it too long: she knew there had been one, she knew it had been the most important face in the world, and it was simply not there, a doorway in her memory now standing open onto white mist.

She was still kneeling there when she realized the ferry was beside the dock, and the old woman was beside her, and Freya was crouched down too, saying her name.

"Mara. Hey. Look at me. You're on the dock. You're fine."

"I'm fine." Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "She burned the rice," Mara said. "Every Sunday. She'd scrape it into a bowl and we'd eat the good part off the top and she'd eat the burnt part herself and say she liked it better." She was crying and smiling at the same time. "I kept that. The boat didn't want that one."

"No," the old woman said quietly. "It wanted the face. They always want the face. Faces are the heaviest thing people carry, and the thing they hold loosest, because they think the face is the least of it." She helped Mara up with hands much stronger than they looked. "It's not the least of it. That's the lesson. That's the whole toll."

Freya was looking at her with an expression she couldn't read—something between grief and fury and a strange, fierce tenderness. "You didn't have to—"

"Yes, I did." She straightened her shoulders. "And you'd have done the same, and we both know it, so don't make it a speech." She turned to the ferryman. "Him next. He pays his own toll. Don't let him give you something small and bitter, he'll try."

"I will not," Freya said.

"He absolutely will," Mara said. "He'll try to pay with the storm. He's been trying to pay everyone with the storm his whole life. Make him give you something he *likes.*"

The old woman looked at Freya with new interest, the way a moneylender looks at a debtor who turns out to have hidden assets. "Is that so."

"We don't have time for this," Freya said. "The mist's lifting, and I heard an engine an hour ago, downstream, and I'd bet the boat it's a launch with a smiling man on it—"

"Then pay," the old woman said, "and be quick about it. The boat's already eager." She nodded at the ferry, and Mara could have sworn the old flat-bottomed thing sat a little higher in the water, like a dog that had heard the word *walk.*

Freya stood at the dock's edge a long moment, jaw working, looking at the warm river like it was a contract with fine print. Then he glanced at Mara—just once, quickly, checking she was still whole—and knelt, and put his hand in the water, and Mara watched his face as the river reached into him, and whatever he gave it, he gave it with his eyes open, and when he stood up he looked lighter and sadder and somehow *relieved*, and he didn't tell her what it was, because you don't have to tell.

The ferryman collected them aboard with the brisk tenderness of a woman who had done this ten thousand times and never once gotten used to it. She took up the pole. The boat moved without a current, without a push, sliding out into the mist toward a far bank that was slowly, slowly resolving into trees.

"Rule four," the old woman said, as the dock sank away behind them. "Don't look back at the water while it's working."

Mara looked forward. The river ran beside them, backward and patient, carrying everything it had ever taken somewhere far upstream, to a spring that remembered.

She kept her mother's name. She kept the burnt rice. She kept the humming, the off-key humming, and somewhere on the far bank, when they finally stepped off onto solid ground, Mara found herself humming it without knowing why, a tune with no face on it, and Freya listened for a moment and said, gently, "That's nice. What is it?"

And Mara opened her mouth to say *I don't know,* and found, to her astonishment, that she was smiling.

Behind them, far downstream, faint through the lifting mist, came the sound of an engine, and a voice—cheerful, carrying, terribly patient—calling across the water like a man reading terms aloud.
