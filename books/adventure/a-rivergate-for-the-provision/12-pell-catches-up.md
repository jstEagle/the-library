## 12. Pell Catches Up

The river had a way of making you forget you were being hunted.

That was the trouble with it, Freya decided, watching the current slide past the hull in long silver ribbons under a half-moon. Up here, past the salt line, the water didn't care about ledgers. It didn't care about interest rates or collateral or the cheerful arithmetic of the Optimists' syndicate. It just went where it was going, backward and uphill and against every law Freya had learned in twenty years of chasing weather, and it did so without once asking him to sign anything.

He should have been sleeping. Mara was asleep, curled in the bow under the tarp with one hand still resting on the chart case, as if even her dreams were guarding it. The boat ran itself on the gentle stretch of water below the second bend—Freya had lashed the tiller hours ago—and the night was warm enough that sleep should have come easy.

Instead he sat at the stern with a cold cup of tea he'd forgotten to drink, doing the thing he always did when his gut itched: counting lights.

There were three kinds of light on a river at night. There was starlight, which shimmered. There was shorelight—the occasional fisher's lamp, a village fire—which held steady. And there was engine-light, which moved against the current in a straight, patient line, because engines did not drift and men with business did not wander.

Freya had spotted the third kind an hour ago. A single white running lamp, far back down the river, holding its distance like a man following you through a market who wants you to know he's there.

Pell.

* * *

"You could wake me," Mara said, when he finally shook her shoulder. She came awake the way she did everything—completely, all at once, no soft edges. One blink and her eyes went from closed to sharp.

"Didn't want to spoil your evening."

"What evening? I was dreaming about my father teaching me to draw contour lines." She sat up, pushed the tarp back, and looked at the river behind them. "How long?"

"Hour, maybe. He's not hurrying."

"That's worse."

"It's much worse," Freya agreed. "Pell doesn't hurry. Hurrying is for people who think they might lose. Pell travels at the speed of certainty."

Mara pulled her boots on. "Options?"

"We outrun him." Freya nodded upstream, where the river narrowed between black shoulders of hills. "There's a side channel around the next bend, if Tomás's notes are honest. Shallow. His launch draws too much water."

"And if they're not honest?"

"Then we find out together, which is how we've found out everything so far."

She almost smiled. Almost. "And option two?"

"There is no option two. Option two is talking to Pell, and I've done that, and it cost me my boat's papers and most of my optimism, which—" he gestured at the river, the moon, the whole improbable backward-flowing world "—as it turns out, I needed."

But the launch wasn't slowing, and the side channel was two bends away, and the river, honest as it was, had never once cared what Freya wanted. The white lamp grew. Then a second lamp beside it. Then the low diesel mutter that you felt in the hull before you heard it.

"They're going to catch us before the bend," Mara said.

"Yes."

"So we don't get a choice."

"We always have a choice," Freya said. "It's just that sometimes every choice is terrible, and then it's less a choice than a flavor."

The launch came alongside ten minutes later, running dark except for that single patient lamp, and it did not ram them, did not hail them, did nothing so crude. It simply matched their speed, close enough that Freya could see the deck gleaming wet and six figures standing on it in matching yellow slickers, arranged with the neatness of cutlery.

And at the rail, hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring a garden, stood Pell.

"Pleasant evening!" Pell called across the water. His voice carried perfectly, pitched with the precision of a man who had practiced being heard over storms. "Lovely stretch of river. I do admire what the wilderness has done with the place."

* * *

They let the launch put out fenders and come alongside, because the alternative was a rifle, and Freya had counted four rifles among the slickers. He killed his engine. The two boats knocked gently together, hull to hull, like old friends greeting.

"Mr. Zhou." Pell stepped aboard without asking, which was very like him. He was a compact man, silver at the temples, with the unlined face of someone who had never once worried about anything that hadn't first worried about him. He wore a dry suit under his slicker, pressed. Pressed. On a river. "You look well. River agrees with you."

"Pell."

"Please, sit. Or stand. Whatever's comfortable." Pell looked around the little boat with genuine, infuriating appreciation. "Cozy. You know, I've always said there's nothing so honest as a small vessel. Everything you own is within arm's reach, and everything you owe is too." He smiled at Mara. "Ms. Ilesanmi. We haven't formally met. My condolences regarding your father. A remarkable man. Terrible with money, I understand, but remarkable."

Mara's hand moved toward the boathook. Freya caught her wrist—not restraining, just present—and she stopped, breathing hard through her nose.

"I'd keep your voice off my father if I were you," she said.

"Of course. Forgive me. Grief makes me clumsy." Pell settled himself on the cooler as though it were an ottoman in a drawing room. From inside his slicker he produced a leather folio, and from the folio a single sheet of paper, which he held up to the moonlight as if checking it for flaws. "Now. Business, briefly, and then I'll leave you to your lovely evening. Mr. Zhou, you currently owe the Optimists"—he consulted the paper unnecessarily; Freya was certain Pell knew the number to the cent, to the decimal, possibly to the dream—"fourteen thousand, two hundred and sixty-one marks, plus accrued interest, plus the modest fee for this excursion, which we'll call a service charge. Call it fifteen thousand even. I'm feeling generous."

"The tide was low," Freya said. "Not me."

"Mm. Here is my difficulty." Pell folded the paper away and spread his empty hands, a man confiding a burden. "My superiors believe you intend to sell us something valuable. A location. And they are prepared to be *very* generous about it. But I look at you, Mr. Zhou, and I see a man who has spent three weeks on a river with a woman whose father died finding that location, and I begin to worry—I genuinely worry, it keeps me up at night—that you're developing *sentiments*. That you might decide the debt matters less than the map. That you might do something foolish and noble and financially catastrophic."

"I would never," Freya said.

"No," Pell agreed pleasantly. "You would. That's exactly the sort of man you are. It's why I like you. It's also why you're a bad investment." He leaned forward, and the lamplight made his smile look carved rather than grown. "So. Two offers. Consider them a menu.

"Offer one: you hand me the chart now, tonight, and we part friends. But you've handled it, you've read it, you've shown it to Ms. Ilesanmi, and frankly the asset has been exposed to risk, so the syndicate will need to adjust for that. Your debt doubles. Thirty thousand. You'll spend the rest of your life paying it, but you'll live, and you'll keep the boat, and your fingers, which I know you're fond of."

"And offer two?" Mara asked, flat.

Pell turned to her with the delight of a teacher whose best student has raised her hand. "Offer two. You deliver the source. Alive, mapped, surveyed, coordinates verified, access documented. My people bottle what's in it, my superiors sell it, and everyone gets rich except the river, which won't mind, rivers never do. In exchange—" he opened his hands again, magnanimous, moonlit "—the debt is forgiven. Entirely. Cleared. Zeroed. You walk away owing nothing to anyone, Mr. Zhou. Can you imagine it? Waking up owning yourself?"

The silence after that was the worst silence Freya had ever sat in, because the answer was yes. He could imagine it. He could imagine it with terrible clarity: the weight lifting, the ledger closing, the morning with no collector's smile waiting in any flooded bar. Fifteen years of dread, gone, for the price of one spring.

Pell watched him imagine it, and Freya understood suddenly that this was the whole design—that the offer was built to be imagined, that Pell's true product was not the source but the wanting.

"You've thought about it," Pell said softly. "Good. Honest men think about things. Only liars refuse instantly."

* * *

Freya made himself breathe. Made himself look at the river instead of the folio, at the black hills, at the moon laying a road of light on the water that went nowhere and everywhere.

Here was the trap, and it was beautiful, the way good traps always are. If he refused both offers, Pell took the chart by force and probably took Mara too, because grief made leverage and leverage made punctual men. If he took offer one, he was ruined forever but alive. If he took offer two—

If he took offer two, he got everything he'd signed that contract in the flooded bar to get, and he lost the only things the river had given him since: the woman in the bow, the truth about Tomás, the strange unearned lightness that had been growing in his chest like moss on a north-facing stone.

And Pell would know, either way. That was the truly elegant part. Stall, and Pell read greed. Hesitate toward acceptance, and Pell read weakness. Every answer told Pell something.

So Freya did the only thing left. He answered like a storm chaser answers a sky that can't be trusted: slowly, and with instruments.

"Before I agree to anything," he said, "I want terms in writing. Full survey rights. My name off the collections list in front of witnesses. And Ms. Ilesanmi walks free regardless—her father's chart, her father's claim. She's not collateral."

Pell's eyebrows rose a fraction, and Freya saw—just saw, in the lamplight, in the half-second before the smile reassembled itself—that he had surprised him. Not much. A fraction. But Pell was a man who priced surprises, and this one had cost him.

"Well," Pell said. "Negotiating. How encouraging. Yes, terms can be drafted. My clerk works quickly." He patted the folio. "Though I'll note, for the record, that asking for paperwork is what guilty men do."

"Asking for paperwork is what drowning men do," Freya said. "Guilty men run. Where would I even go?"

"Upstream," said one of the slickers, and several of them laughed, a tidy synchronized sound.

Pell laughed too, warmly, and stood, brushing nothing whatsoever from his trousers. "Draft the terms tonight. I'll send them across at dawn with breakfast—you look underfed, both of you, it worries me. Sleep well. Truly. The river's kind at night." He paused at the rail, one hand on it, and looked back with an expression that for one unguarded moment contained no charm at all, only a vast patient bookkeeping attention, the gaze of something that measured weather fronts and human beings on the same scale and found neither frightening.

"Oh—one small thing." Pell's smile returned like a lamp being relit. "Tomorrow, when you sign, you'll sign knowing that my launch will follow you the rest of the way. For your safety, naturally. Rivers are dangerous." He tapped the rail twice, a little goodbye. "And Mr. Zhou—if the side channel around the next bend looks tempting, do remember that shallow water is only shallow until it isn't. We've mapped this river too."

The launch drew away, smooth as a signature, and its lamp receded downstream until it was indistinguishable from a star lying on the water.

* * *

For a long time neither of them spoke. The current carried them on, backward, faithful, indifferent.

"He offered to erase you," Mara said finally. "Everything you owe. Gone."

"He did."

"And you asked for paperwork."

"I panicked," Freya said. "Paperwork is what I reach for when I'm terrified. It's a character flaw."

Mara turned to look at him, and in the moonlight her expression was doing something complicated—half fury, half something warmer and more dangerous. "You're going to stall him. Lead him up the river by the nose for days while you figure out what Tomás really hid in that chart."

"I am going to attempt to stall him," Freya corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is usually whether I survive."

"He'll figure it out."

"He'll figure it out eventually. But Pell believes the world is a ledger, and ledgers balance on schedule. He'll keep assuming I'm calculating right up until the moment I'm not." Freya looked upstream, at the black gorge waiting beyond the bend, at the faint pale thread of the falls that fell upward into the dark. "Besides. He said they've mapped the river."

"So?"

"So Tomás mapped it first, thirty years ago, alone, on foot, and the Survey Office buried his work. Which means whatever Pell's maps show—" Freya allowed himself, for the first time all night, a real grin, thin and crooked and alive, "—the river has changed its mind since. Rivers do that. It's the one thing they're better at than the Optimists."

Mara looked at him a moment longer, then pulled the tarp over her knees and took the tiller.

"Then steer," she said. "Dawn comes early, and I intend to be somewhere inconvenient when his clerk arrives with breakfast."

Freya took the tiller from her hand, and their fingers overlapped on the wood a beat longer than steering required, and neither of them mentioned it, which was its own kind of signing.

Behind them, far down the black water, the white lamp held steady.

Ahead, the gorge opened its throat.
