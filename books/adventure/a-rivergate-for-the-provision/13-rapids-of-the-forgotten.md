## 13. Rapids of the Forgotten

### Freya

The river told them it was angry a full hour before it did anything about it.

Freya had learned to read water the way other men read faces—there was the sulk of a river before rain, the flat gray patience of one holding its breath. This one had gone from talking to shouting in the space of an afternoon. The current that had been flowing backward, gentle and impossible, now ran backward *fast*, hauling them upstream toward the source at a pace that made the tiller hum like a struck tuning fork.

"That's not right," Mara said, for the fourth time.

"No," Freya agreed, for the fourth time. "It's not."

The sky over the canyon walls had gone the color of wet slate. Somewhere above them—miles upstream, maybe days—the monsoon's advance scouts were dumping themselves onto high country that had never learned to hold water. All of it would come down this channel eventually. The only question was whether it would come down politely or all at once.

Freya was betting on all at once. He'd been betting wrong his whole life; no reason to change now.

"Should we pull over?" Mara asked. She stood at the bow with her hand on the chart case strapped to her chest, the way she'd taken to carrying it—like a reliquary, or a grudge.

"Where?" He gestured at the banks. The canyon had narrowed through the morning until the walls rose sheer on either side, streaked with old flood lines ten feet above their heads. There was no beach, no eddy, no gravel bar. Just stone and water and the two of them between.

Mara followed his gesture and went quiet in the particular way of someone doing arithmetic they don't like.

The first drop hit them as a sound—a long rolling boom from upstream, like a door slamming somewhere enormous. Then the water beneath the boat *lifted*, a swell moving through it like a shudder down a spine, and the river began to rise while they watched.

"Freya."

"I see it."

"It's coming *down* the—" she stopped, recalibrated. "It's coming up the—"

"Yes."

Because the river flowed backward. Of course the flood would too. The water wasn't rising past them; it was arriving beneath them, surging inland from the sea in a great green fist, and every gallon of it wanted passage through this canyon at once.

The boat slewed. Freya hauled the tiller across and got her nose into the current just as the first real wave came through—a standing wall of brown water where none should have been—and then the world became very simple and very loud.

* * *

There is a thing that happens when a boat stops being a boat and becomes a projectile. Freya knew it intimately. The hull leaves the water entirely, hangs for a heartbeat in the air like a promise being reconsidered, and comes down on whatever the river has decided to give it. He rode the first three drops with something like professional respect. On the fourth, the starboard gunwale met a submerged rock with a crack he felt in his teeth, and the *Provision*—his patched, mortgaged, long-suffering *Provision*—began to take the river aboard as cargo.

"Bail!" he shouted.

"With what?"

"Anything! Enthusiasm!"

She found the dented kettle and started hurling water back over the side with the fury of a woman who had been promised justice and was instead getting hydrology. Freya fought the tiller and lost the argument twice, three times, caught it again each time by luck and by the strange fact that the backward current seemed to have opinions—it kept nudging them *upstream*, away from the worst of the standing waves, as though the river disapproved of drowning people in its own house.

Then the fifth rapid took the choice away from everyone.

He didn't see the hole. Nobody sees the hole; that's what makes it a hole. One moment the boat was planing down a chute of white water, and the next the water simply ended beneath him, a pit of churning nothing, and the *Provision* pitched forward into it like a diver, and Freya went over the side without ceremony, without breath, without anything but the sudden intimate knowledge that the river had him now.

Cold. Cold like a debt called in. He tumbled, struck rock with his shoulder, tumbled again, and the water folded around his head and ears until the roar became a kind of silence, and in that silence—

In that silence, the storm came back.

Not the memory of it. The storm itself. He was on the deck of the *Albatross Line* eleven years ago with rain going sideways and Renn shouting something at him from the wheelhouse, and he could hear the words now, every word, clear as bells: *Freya, the cell's turning, we should run for harbor.* And he could feel himself answering, feel his own mouth shape it: *One more pass. The data's worth one more pass.*

The data. God. He had said *worth*. He had stood in the wheelhouse with lightning walking on the horizon like something looking for a door, and he had weighed four lives against a dataset and called it even, and the river was showing him the ledger now, line by line, in perfect detail: Renn's face when the mast went. Old Sefa in the water, reaching not for the line Freya threw but for the young deckhand beside her, pushing the boy toward safety with the last of her strength. The exact temperature of the sea. The exact sound of the hull going down. Every second preserved, every second offered to him again as if the water itself had been keeping receipts.

*You remember,* the river seemed to say, wrapping him in the cold. *We remember. Here, hold it. Hold all of it.*

And the terrible thing—the thing he would never be able to explain later—was how easy it would be to stay. The water was soft. The memories were soft. His crew was alive in there, whole and wet and laughing, Renn complaining about the coffee, Sefa telling the story of the storm as if it had happened to someone else, and all he had to do was stop fighting. Stop kicking. Let the river carry him the way it carried everything it refused to forget.

His lungs burned. His hands had gone distant. Somewhere far above, filtered through fathoms of green light, there was a shape breaking the surface—a small dark shape, diving.

He thought, with the last coherent part of himself: *that's going to be someone else's ghost too, if you don't move.*

He moved.

### Mara

Later, Mara would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and fail, because terror does not keep minutes. What she retained was fragments: the boat spinning free of him, empty, the tiller swinging like a hanged man's legs. The place where he'd gone under closing over smooth. Her own voice saying his name once, uselessly, into wind that ate it.

Then the long gray interval in which she did the only reasonable thing, which was to dive into a flooded canyon after a man she had known for nine days and hated for most of them.

Her father had taught her to swim in the harbor at Salt Hollow, salt in her eyes, his hands under her ribs. *The water wants you to panic,* he'd said. *Don't lend it anything.* She thought of that now with a bitterness that cut through the cold: nine years hating the sea for taking him, and here she was, lending it her whole self, diving after a stranger because the stranger had her father's chart and her father's last words and, apparently, some claim on her she hadn't agreed to.

She found him by his boot. It snagged her ankle in the murk and she nearly screamed underwater, which uses up air remarkably fast, and then her brain caught up—boot, leg, person—and she grabbed a fistful of his coat.

He was heavy the way drowned things are heavy, all his weight surrendered, and for one black moment she hauled against him and gained nothing, and the river pulled them both deeper toward whatever it kept at the bottom. Then his arm moved—not much, a twitch, a refusal—and she got her shoulder under his and kicked for the light.

They surfaced in an eddy behind a boulder the size of a chapel, gasping, coughing, clinging to wet stone while the main current went by an arm's length away, brown and furious and full of trees.

"Don't," Freya rasped, "tell anyone," cough, "you saved me."

"Noted." She spat out river. "I'll tell everyone."

A weak laugh shook through him, half drowned, mostly disbelief. His eyes were wrong—wide and unfocused, still looking at something that wasn't there. She'd seen that look before, on returning sailors in Salt Hollow, men who'd been under too long in bad water. She put her palm flat against his cheek, rough with stubble, freezing cold, and made her voice as level as she could manage over her own hammering heart.

"Hey. Surveyor. You're in the present. The present is terrible, but it's yours."

His eyes found hers. Something in them reeled in, slowly, like a line brought home hand over hand.

"Sefa pushed the boy," he said. "Toward the line. I never—I always thought she was reaching for me."

"Mm." She didn't know who Sefa was. She held on anyway.

"The river showed me. All of it. Like it had been—" he stopped, shuddered, water running off his hair into his eyes. "Like it had been saving my mail."

"Freya." She tightened her grip on his collar. "Listen to me. Whatever it showed you, it wasn't charity. That water plays tricks. My father wrote it in the margin—*the river flatters, do not believe it*—and I used to think that was about the source. Maybe it's about all of it."

"He wrote that?" A flicker of the old stubbornness lit in Freya's face, and she could have kissed him with relief, except that relief was a currency neither of them traded in yet. "That miserable old fox. Nine years dead and still annotating."

"Nine years missing," Mara corrected, and heard her own voice crack on it, and hated it, and let it stand.

* * *

They found the *Provision* a quarter mile downstream—or upstream, rather; the river's directions had given up pretending—wedged in a willow tangle with six inches of water in her bilge and one oar missing and, miraculously, the kettle still lashed to the thwart. Mara bailed while Freya limped around the hull checking seams, lips blue, moving like a man twice his age, refusing help with every visible joint in his body.

"She'll hold," he announced finally, in the tone of a doctor delivering news he doesn't fully believe.

"She'd better. I'm not diving in after you twice. My arms are tired and you're heavier than you look."

"All ballast. Pure guilt."

"There it is." She wrung out her shirt and tried not to notice that her hands were shaking, or that his were too, or that when their fingers brushed passing the kettle they both pretended it hadn't. "What happened down there? Truly. You looked like a man reading his own obituary and finding it generous."

Freya sat down hard on the thwart. For a while he watched the water go by, and she let him, because she knew about the long silences grief needs, having spent nine years renting rooms to them herself.

"My crew," he said at last. "The storm I told you about. The river gave it back to me. Not the way I keep it—curated, guilty, the worst version. The whole way. Everything true." He rubbed his face. "Do you know what I remembered tonight, that I've managed not to remember for eleven years? Renn didn't want to make the last pass either. He argued. And I outranked him, so we made the pass, and the data we got was—" he laughed, a broken little sound, "—it was fine. It was completely unremarkable. A decade of chasing, and the storm that killed them gave me numbers any graduate student could have collected from shore."

Mara sat down beside him. The willow leaves dripped. Somewhere upstream the thunder kept up its slow argument with the canyon walls.

"My father left me a letter," she said. "Before his last survey. I was nineteen and furious at him for going, so I didn't open it for two years. When I finally did, it said he was sorry the work mattered more to him than I did, and that he hoped I'd understand someday, and I threw it across the room." She hugged her knees. "I've read it maybe four hundred times since. I can recite it. And every time, I find a new way it's worse—he *knew*, Freya. He knew exactly what he was choosing, and he chose it anyway, and he apologized in advance like that makes it better. And I loved him for knowing and hated him for choosing and I have never once managed to do both at the same time."

Freya was quiet a moment. "The river would probably show you the truth of it," he said. "If you asked nicely."

"The river can mind its own business." But she said it softly, and he heard the softness, and neither of them pursued it, because some doors you point at and don't open.

* * *

They ran the rest of the gorge by nightfall, riding the flood surge inland at a speed that should have frightened her and instead felt, absurdly, like being carried. The river had spent its anger. It ran big and steady now, a highway of borrowed ocean pushing them toward the source, and Freya steered with the ease of a man who had made peace with the fact that the water was driving.

"You realize," he said, as the first stars came out over the canyon rim, "we're making excellent time. Best conditions of the whole trip. Catastrophic weather, structural damage, near-drowning—perfect sailing, honestly. If the Optimists ran charter tours—"

"If you say 'silver lining' I'm putting you over the side myself."

"—I was going to say," he continued, wounded, "that I owe you. Properly. Not contract-owes. Real ones."

Mara looked at him—at the bruise blooming along his jaw, the coat ruined beyond salvage, the ridiculous stubborn set of his shoulders—and felt the strange new thing settle in her chest where the fury used to live. It wasn't forgiveness. Forgiveness was a finished building and they were nowhere near done laying stone. But it was the ground cleared for it. It was trust, arriving ahead of schedule, the way floods do.

"You owe me a father," she said. "Start with the truth about him and work your way up."

"Deal." He hesitated, then, quieter: "Mara. Back there. Under the water. Why'd you come in after me? You could have taken the chart and the boat and been at the source by tomorrow."

She considered lying, found she didn't want to, and was alarmed by that.

"Because you looked at me before you went under," she said. "Right at the end. Like I was the last thing worth looking at. Nobody's ever—" She stopped, restarted. "Nobody looks at me like I matter more than the mission. My father didn't. The Survey Office certainly doesn't. And you did it by accident, while drowning, which is possibly the least romantic way to earn a person's loyalty ever devised."

Freya opened his mouth.

"Don't ruin it."

He closed it. Grinned instead, crooked and exhausted and alive, and steered them on into the dark.

It was Mara, at the middle watch, who saw it first—a faint glow ahead, deep in the canyon, where no lamp burned and no lightning lived. A pale gold shimmer against the stone, pulsing slow, like a heartbeat seen through skin.

"Freya," she whispered. "Is that supposed to be there?"

He came to stand beside her, and she watched his face as he saw it, and saw the fear and the wonder arrive together and refuse to leave.

"No," he said. "Which means we're almost there."

Behind them, far downstream, a searchlight swept the gorge wall—once, twice—and went dark.

Someone else had entered the river.
