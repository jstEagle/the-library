## 14. The Survey Office File

The Survey Office in Salt Hollow kept its records the way a miser kept coins: counted twice, touched once, and never spent on anything that mattered. Cass Okonjo had worked there eleven years, and in all that time she had learned exactly three things about the building. First, that the radiators clanked in a rhythm suspiciously close to a sea shanty, which made long nights feel like being haunted by a cheerful ghost. Second, that the coffee was a war crime. Third—and this was the one that had gotten her promoted—that sealed files were never sealed for the reasons written on the seal.

She sat alone on the third floor with Tomás Ilesanmi's file open in front of her and the radiator keeping its off-key time behind her, and she understood, finally, why her orders had come down so fast and so clean.

The file was thick. That was the first wrong thing. Missing-person files from nine years ago were supposed to be thin—a report, a search log, a form closing the case with a signature and a shrug. This one was two inches of paper, and every page after the third was stamped RESTRICTED — DIRECTORATE EYES ONLY in ink so red it looked freshly offended.

Cass put on her reading glasses, which she resented, and began at the beginning.

The beginning was ordinary enough. Field cartographer, senior grade, twenty-two years of service. Commendations for the coastal surveys of the Verren Shelf. A note from a supervisor describing him as "meticulous to the point of stubbornness," which in Cass's experience was the highest praise the Office ever gave anyone, since meticulousness was the only virtue that couldn't be faked from a desk.

Then the commendations stopped, and the tone changed.

*Subject reports discovery of hydrological anomaly, interior Cape Verren region. Claims freshwater outflow of significant volume originating inland. Subject declined to provide coordinates pending "independent verification."*

Independent verification. Cass turned the phrase over. In the Service, that meant one of two things: either the subject didn't trust his superiors, or his superiors hadn't yet taught him why he should have.

The next memo was dated six days later. It was not written by a field officer. The letterhead was Directorate, and the language had the smooth, oiled quality of prose that has been through three lawyers and still wants something.

*Subject is instructed to surrender all survey materials relating to the above anomaly by end of week. Failure to comply will be considered dereliction. The Office thanks Subject for his years of diligent service.*

*The Office thanks Subject.* Four words doing the work of a knife.

There was a copy of Ilesanmi's reply, handwritten, transcribed by some clerk whose pen had pressed hard enough to emboss the next page. Cass read it twice.

*I have surveyed coastlines for this Office for twenty-two years. I have mapped shoals so men do not drown on them. I will not hand you coordinates so that someone can decide who is allowed to drink. Find your own water.*

Cass sat back. The radiator clanked. Somewhere below, a clerk laughed at something, the sound rising up the stairwell like a bubble through oil.

She knew what came next. She had known since she'd read the chart in Freya Zhou's hands at the harbor—the backward river, the annotations in a careful old man's script. But knowing a thing in the chest was different from knowing it on government paper, and government paper had a way of making even monstrous things look tidy.

The next document was a logistics requisition. Dated four days before Tomás Ilesanmi's last confirmed departure for Cape Verren. It authorized a survey vessel—*the Kestrel*, which Cass knew for a fact had been decommissioned two years prior for hull rot—to transport Subject Ilesanmi to the Verren shelf for "final anomaly assessment." The vessel's maintenance record was attached. Or rather, the absence of one was attached: a single line noting that *scheduled inspection deferred at Directorate request.*

Deferred. At whose request. Signed by whom.

Cass turned the page and found the signature block, and her stomach did something slow and cold, like water finding a crack in a hull.

*Deputy Director Emeric Voss.*

She knew the name. Everyone in the Service knew the name. Voss had signed her own transfer orders to Salt Hollow eight months ago. Voss had signed the directive that sat in her satchel right now, the one instructing her to recover the Ilesanmi chart and then demolish the source with shaped charges, burying the site "pending future evaluation." Voss had signed it with the same unhurried flourish that now, on this yellowed requisition, had sent an old man to sea in a rotten boat.

Same pen. Same hand. Nine years apart, and the ink hadn't changed its mind about anything.

Cass took off her glasses and cleaned them on her sleeve, though they didn't need cleaning. It was something her mother used to do when a conversation got too honest for looking straight into. Her mother had been a harbor pilot, back when the harbor had needed piloting, and she had taught Cass exactly one piece of professional wisdom: *When the paperwork and the weather disagree, believe the weather.* Cass had built a whole career on the corollary—that when the paperwork and the truth disagree, believe the truth, and then figure out how to make the paperwork confess.

She picked the file back up.

The last section was the thinnest and the worst. It was the search log—or rather, the performance of one. Three entries. Day one: coastal patrol, no sign. Day two: weather deteriorating, search suspended. Day three: case closed, subject presumed lost at sea. Each entry initialed by a patrol officer whose name Cass recognized as a man who had, per the duty roster of that same week, been stationed ninety miles north the entire time.

Somebody had forged a search that never happened. Somebody had closed a file on a living man with a rotten boat and a forbidden map, and had gone to lunch.

And then, tucked into the back cover like a receipt someone hoped would be forgotten, a single memo in Voss's hand, undated, unsent—or perhaps sent, and copied here by someone with a conscience and a copying machine:

*Re: Ilesanmi. If subject recovers the anomaly data independently, he will attempt to publish. Publication cannot be permitted. If subject does not recover the data, the matter resolves itself. Either outcome is acceptable. Do not expend further resources.*

*Do not expend further resources.* Nine words. Cass had read casualty reports with more tenderness.

She closed the file and sat very still for a while, listening to the radiator attempt its shanty and fail at the same bar, over and over, like a heart stuck on a bad memory.

Here was the arithmetic, then, laid out as plainly as a tide table. Tomás Ilesanmi had found the source years ago. He had refused to give it up—not out of greed, but out of the opposite of greed, if such a thing existed; out of a conviction that water wasn't merchandise. So the Directorate had given him a boat with rot in its bones and a patrol that existed only on paper, and had waited for the sea to do the work that policy required. And when the sea had obliged—if it had obliged; if a man could really spend nine years dying slowly in a cave while his own government filed him under *resolved*—the Directorate had moved on to the next step without breaking stride: send Okonjo, blow the site, bury the evidence, thank everyone for their service.

Her orders weren't policy. They were the last page of a murder, asking her to initial it.

Cass thought about the warden's oath she'd taken at nineteen, standing in a rain-soaked courtyard with forty other recruits, repeating words about the public trust and the protection of wild places. She had meant it then. She meant it now, which was precisely the problem, because the oath didn't say *the public trust unless a deputy director finds it inconvenient,* and it didn't say *wild places except the ones we've decided to drown in red ink.* The oath was simple. It was everything wrapped around it that had grown complicated.

She also thought—because she was honest with herself if nothing else—about the part of her that wanted to keep believing. There was a comfort in the chain of command that no one admitted out loud. You passed the order down or carried it out, and whatever happened, your hands stayed at the level of your conscience. She had watched good officers lean on that comfort their whole careers, the way sailors leaned on rails, and she had told herself she wasn't the leaning type. But a rail is a rail. You only find out it's painted scenery when you put your weight on it.

Her radio crackled. Dispatch, checking in, wanting her position and status. She looked at the satchel where the demolition orders slept in their official envelope, and she said, "Stationary. Reviewing records."

"Anything actionable?"

"Not yet," Cass said, and heard how easily the lie came, and wondered whether that was corruption or clarity.

She signed the file back in—there were procedures, and until she decided otherwise she would follow them, because the day she stopped following procedures was the day she became the kind of person who forged patrol logs—and took the stairs instead of the elevator, needing the climb, needing her legs to be doing something honest.

Outside, Salt Hollow was doing its evening business. Gulls arguing over fish heads. A boy selling fried dough outside the chandlery, calling out prices with the shameless optimism of youth. The harbor water lying flat and gray-green, keeping whatever secrets it kept.

Cass stood on the steps and let herself think the unthinkable thought all the way through, testing it like ice.

Zhou and Ilesanmi's daughter were upstream somewhere, heading for a spring that the Directorate wanted erased. Her orders said intercept, recover, demolish. Her oath said protect. And between those two commands lay a gap wide enough to drive a convoy through, and at the bottom of the gap, a dead man's boat and a forged log and a signature she now recognized the way you recognize a voice in a dark room.

Three days. That was what the convoy timeline gave her—three days before the charges arrived and the question stopped being hers to answer.

She bought fried dough from the boy, because her mother had also taught her that no decision should be made hungry, and ate it walking along the seawall, watching the light go bronze on the water.

By the time she reached the far end of the wall, she had made a decision of sorts, though she'd have denied under oath that it was one. She went back to her office, pulled a fresh sheet of Office letterhead, and wrote a request for the complete personnel file of Deputy Director Emeric Voss—citing, with impeccable bureaucratic correctness, a "routine integrity audit." Then she opened her safe, looked at the demolition orders one more time, and did not burn them. Not yet. Burning was a statement, and statements were permanent, and she was a woman who believed in keeping her options loaded.

But she wrote one more thing before she left that night, on plain paper, unsigned, and slipped it into the pocket of her coat where it rode against her ribs like a second heartbeat:

*To whoever is following the river: the convoy is three days behind me. He knows the route. Trust the daughter's map, not the Office's.*

She didn't know yet how it would reach them. She only knew that somewhere upstream, an old man's work was still moving through the world, carried by strangers, and that the least the law owed him—least, mind, the bare minimum—was a warning.

The radiator clanked behind her, still stuck on the same bar. Cass locked the office door, and for the first time in eleven years, she checked the hallway twice before leaving, like a woman with something worth stealing.

Which, she reflected, stepping out into the salt wind, she now did.

* * *

The message reached her the next morning, and it nearly stopped her heart in the chandlery doorway.

A runner from the northern relay station, breathless, hat in hand, holding out a telegram with the Office's crest already broken open. Cass read it standing among the coils of rope and jars of pitch, and read it again, and felt the floor tilt very slightly, the way a deck tilts.

*DIR. VOSS EN ROUTE SALT HOLLOW STOP CONVOY ACCELERATED STOP DEMOLITION ADVANCED TO FORTY-EIGHT HOURS STOP VOSS REQUESTS PERSONNEL FILE REQ. BE WITHDRAWN STOP ACKNOWLEDGE.*

He knew. Someone upstairs had seen her audit request and read it exactly right—not as routine, but as a threat. And instead of answering it with an inquiry, a hearing, a paper trail, he had answered it the way he answered everything: by moving faster.

Forty-eight hours. The monsoon was maybe four days out. Which meant Voss intended to have his charges planted and his legend buried before the weather could complicate his paperwork—and anyone standing near the source when the demolition met the storm would simply become another line in another closed file. *Presumed lost at sea.* He had done it before. He had a system.

Cass folded the telegram along its creases, very carefully, the way you fold something you intend to keep as evidence.

"Acknowledge," she told the runner. "Standard confirmation. Nothing further."

The runner trotted off. Cass stood a moment longer among the rope and pitch, and the fried-dough boy waved at her from across the street, and she waved back, because the world insisted on continuing to contain small kindnesses no matter what was in the telegrams.

Then she walked back to her office, unlocked her safe, and took out the demolition orders and held them over the cold stove.

She thought about her oath. She thought about a rotten boat and a forged log and nine words—*do not expend further resources*—that had been a man's entire eulogy. She thought about the gap between the law as written and the law as signed, and about which side of that gap she intended to stand on when the water rose.

The stove was cold, but she lit it anyway, and fed it kindling, and waited for the iron to glow.

Loyalty, she had always believed, was a load-bearing thing—you didn't discover its true strength until the weight came on. Hers was coming on now, all at once, and creaking, and she listened to it creak with the professional attention she gave all failing structures.

Then Cass Okonjo, warden of the Salt Hollow district, eleven years of unblemished service, held her orders over the fire and let them catch.
