## 15. Villagers of the Middle Reach

The village announced itself by smell before sight—woodsmoke and river mud and something sweeter underneath, like honey left too long in the sun. Mara smelled it from the bow while Freya wrestled the tiller through a bend so tight the backward-flowing river seemed to be checking their papers.

"Middle Reach," Freya said, consulting the chart. "Tomás marked it with three little dots. No name."

"Maybe he couldn't spell it," Mara said. "Or maybe he didn't want to."

They tied off at a stone landing worn smooth as a kneecap, and the village rose around them in tiers: houses on stilts, gardens on terraces, laundry strung between them like signal flags. It was beautiful in the way of places that had stopped trying to impress anyone. Chickens regarded them with the flat suspicion of customs officials.

A woman met them at the head of the landing. She was old—not elderly, *old*, the way furniture is old, the way rivers are old—with white hair braided into a crown and eyes the color of tea steeped twice.

"You're strangers," she said.

"We're surveyors," Freya said, which was true in the way that a drowning man is a swimmer.

"No," the woman said pleasantly. "You're not. You're the debt man and the daughter. You've been on the river nine days. You argue about navigation but not about anything that matters. The tall one snores." She nodded at Freya. "I'm Sefu's grandmother. He'll be delighted you've come. He collects strangers."

Mara looked at Freya. Freya looked at Mara. The river behind them flowed patiently inland, carrying its impossible cargo of water toward the hills.

"You knew we were coming," Mara said.

"Child, we know everything that happens on this river. That is rather the whole trouble with us."

* * *

Her name was Ama Doss, and she installed them in a guest house that smelled of cedar and old rain, fed them fish stew and a fermented drink that tasted like a lemon having second thoughts, and then sat them down in her kitchen as though they were specimens she'd been waiting years to pin.

"The source," Freya began.

"Is upstream, yes. Two days' walk past the falls that go the wrong way. Everyone who comes here asks about the source eventually, usually around the second bowl of stew." She refilled his bowl without asking. "Eat first. The truth keeps. It's very good at keeping. We would know."

Mara set down her spoon. "What do you mean, the whole trouble with you?"

Ama Doss folded her hands. They were hands like tree roots, knuckled and patient. "How much has the river told you?"

"The river hasn't told us anything," Freya said. "It's been busy flowing backwards."

"Mm. Then I will tell you, and you must understand that when I tell you something here, it does not fade. There is no softening. In Salt Hollow, if someone insults you, in ten years you remember that they insulted you, but the sting has gone out of it, yes? Like an old scar. Here—" she tapped her own forearm, where the skin was brown and unlined despite her age, "—here the sting stays exactly as fresh as the day it was given. Every kindness too. Every kindness stays perfectly kind forever. But so does every cruelty."

"That sounds like a gift," Freya said carefully.

"It is a gift," Ama Doss agreed. "That is what everyone says for the first year. Then the first decade. Then you stop saying it."

* * *

They saw the village properly the next morning, and Mara understood that she had been looking at it wrong from the landing.

There were no graves.

She noticed it the way you notice a missing tooth—by its absence, by the tongue of the eye going to the empty place again and again. Terraces, gardens, a shrine to some river spirit crusted with offerings, a schoolhouse with children chanting arithmetic in two languages. But no burial ground. No row of leaning stones. Not even the small cairns that river people sometimes raise when the ground won't take a spade.

"There's a funeral today," said a boy who appeared beside her on the path. He was perhaps twelve, gap-toothed, carrying a basket of yams with the ease of long practice. "First one in sixty-one years. You should come. Nobody else will remember it afterward except all of us, forever, so it's quite well attended."

"Sixty-one years?" Mara said.

"Old Nantambu finally died. Fell asleep in his boat and drifted into the reeds. Very peaceful, everyone says, and they say it knowing exactly how peaceful, because they were there, in a sense, watching him decline for forty years and remembering every single afternoon of it." The boy shrugged with a maturity that sat oddly on his small shoulders. "He wanted to be buried under the fig tree. His son argued for the terrace. They argued about it in 1919, when the son was young and Nantambu was merely stubborn, and the argument never ended because neither of them could forget his position. So now we bury him under the fig tree AND hold a feast on the terrace. Compromise by exhaustion."

"What's your name?" Mara asked.

"Kofi. You're the cartographer's daughter. Your father died in a cave at Cape Verren on the third of the flood month, nine years ago, between the second tide and the third. He spoke kindly of you even at the end. He said—" Kofi paused, and his face did something complicated, the face of a child handling an adult grief like borrowed luggage, "—he said you'd have come for him if you'd known. He was right, wasn't he? You'd have come."

Mara stood very still on the path. Somewhere below, the river ran the wrong way, patient as a held breath.

"Yes," she said. "I'd have come."

"I know," Kofi said simply. "Everyone here knows things like that. It's why we don't lie. Lying is pointless when everyone remembers the truth." He hoisted his yams. "Feast is at sundown. Wear something you don't mind getting stew on. Auntie Bintou cooks like she's angry at the pot."

* * *

The funeral was the strangest thing Mara had ever attended, and she had once watched her father argue with a lighthouse.

There was no priest. Old Nantambu's descendants stood in a ring around the fig tree—forty-some people, from ancient to infant—and one by one they spoke a memory of him. Not a eulogy. A memory, exact, dated, complete. A granddaughter described a fishing morning from 1888 with the wind direction and the exact words of a joke. A great-nephew recounted an argument over a debt of three fish, settled in 1902, including the apology Nantambu had made and the exact tone in which he'd made it—which, apparently, had been insufficiently sincere, and had been resented for seventy years by the great-nephew's family, who stood now at the edge of the ring, arms crossed, attending their own ancestor's funeral like auditors.

And nobody wept.

That was the thing Mara kept circling. There was reverence, and there was ritual, and there was even laughter—a rich, rolling laughter when someone told of Nantambu losing his trousers to a monkey in 1874—but there was no weeping. Grief requires a shape, she thought. It requires the memory to soften at the edges until what remains is love with the details worn away. These people had love with every detail intact, sharp as broken glass, and you cannot hold broken glass close enough to warm it.

Afterward, an old man found her by the stew fire. He introduced himself as Pa Sefu—the grandmother's grandson, the stranger-collector—and he was perhaps eighty, with a face like a topographical map of somewhere difficult.

"You're going upstream," he said.

"We are."

"To the spring."

Mara didn't answer, which was an answer, and he nodded as if she'd signed something.

"My wife went up there," he said. "Fifty-two years ago. Our daughter had died—you remember your dead here, perfectly, every day, every hour, the weight of it never lightening—and she went up to the source to see the child again. To drink and live inside the memory where our girl was still alive." He turned his cup slowly. "She came back after a season. She said the water showed her our daughter, true and whole, laughing, aged as she would have been. And my wife stayed another season. And another. I brought her food. I begged. In the end she waved to me from the cave mouth—waved, and smiled, and would not come out, because coming out meant the memory became a memory again instead of a life, and she preferred the false living one to the true dead one."

"What happened to her?" Mara asked, though the cold in her stomach already knew.

"She is still there. Or the part of her that drank is. The body came down the river four years later, and we buried it—" he gestured at the fig tree, at the fresh-turned earth where Nantambu lay, "—the first grave since Nantambu's father, may his exactness rest precisely. But the part of her that mattered is still drinking, I think. Still in the water. Sometimes on quiet nights the river shows us things. I have seen her. She looks happy." His voice did not break. It simply stopped being able to continue for a moment, and then resumed, level as a surveyor's line. "Happy forever. Do you understand? That is the horror of it. She is happy forever, and I am unhappy forever, and neither of those things will ever wear down into peace. The spring does not heal. It preserves. There is a difference, and the difference is everything."

* * *

Freya found her at dusk on the landing, sitting with her feet over the black water. He lowered himself beside her with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions, and for a while they watched the current run uphill.

"Ama Doss gave me the tour of grievances," he said. "Did you get the tour?"

"I got a funeral."

"Ah. Mine was grievances. There's a woman, Bintou, the one who cooks angry—her family and another family have been feuding since before the monsoon records began. Over a goat. One goat, stolen or not stolen, in 1841. Both families remember the theft perfectly, and the counter-theft, and the insult at the well, and the insult at the wedding, and the wedding itself, which both sides attended and both sides remember differently and both are *certain*, because certainty is the one thing this place grows in abundance." He shook his head. "Two hundred years of feud over a goat nobody alive has seen. The goat is definitely dead. The grudge is in excellent health."

"It's not funny," Mara said.

"No," Freya agreed. "But if I don't laugh at it I'll have to think about it, and I've been thinking about it all day and it's sitting on my chest like a cat." He was quiet a moment. "My crew. After the storm. For six years I've been able to make the memories smaller. Not gone—smaller. The way you squint at something bright. And these people can't squint. Ever. Their worst day is always exactly as bad as it was."

"Is that why you chase storms?" Mara asked. "To keep the bright thing moving so you don't have to look straight at it?"

Freya looked at her. The lamplight from the village caught half his face. "That's a very rude question."

"Yes," Mara said. "I learned it here. Nobody lies, so I'm practicing."

He laughed—a short, surprised sound—and then didn't laugh anymore. "Ask me again sometime," he said. "Not tonight. Tonight I want to eat whatever Bintou is angry at the pot about, and sleep, and pretend the river isn't watching us."

"The river is definitely watching us."

"I know. I've started saying good morning to it. Don't tell anyone."

* * *

That night, Ama Doss came to the guest house with a lamp and a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and Mara woke fully at the knock, hand finding the knife under her blanket out of nine years of habit.

"Peace, child," the old woman said. "I've brought you something better than a knife. I've brought you a warning, and warnings are heavier, but they cut both ways."

She unwrapped the oilcloth on the table. Inside was a journal—water-stained, salt-crusted, its spine repaired with fishing line. Mara knew the handwriting before she knew the words. She had grown up watching it march across survey ledgers: her father's hand, upright and impatient, the letters of a man who thought faster than ink could travel.

"Where did you—" Her voice failed and restarted. "Where did you get this?"

"He passed through," Ama Doss said. "Eleven years ago. Coming downstream from the source, the only man I ever saw leave it voluntarily. He stayed a week. He was sick already—something in his lungs, the wet cold of the caves—and he knew it. He spent the week writing and talking and eating Bintou's stew, and on the last night he gave me this and said, 'If a girl comes asking, with my chin and my temper, give her this. If anyone else comes asking, give them nothing and point them at the rapids.'"

Mara touched the cover. Under her fingers the leather was cool and real and unbearable.

"He talked about you," Ama Doss went on gently. "He remembered everything about you—of course he did; he'd drunk from the spring years before, on his first expedition, before he understood what it costs. Perfect memory of you at four, at seven, at nine. He recited it like scripture. Your first word. The way you corrected his maps at the kitchen table. The time you cried over a gull with a broken wing and made him bury it with honors." The old woman's tea-dark eyes held hers. "He carried you perfectly, child. All the way back to that cave. Whatever else he was running from, he was running toward that."

Mara opened the journal with hands that shook. The first pages were survey notes—depths, bearings, the mechanism sketched in her father's cramped shorthand. But halfway through, the notes became a letter, and the letter began:

*Mara. If you are reading this, then either you are cleverer than the Survey Office, which is likely, or the river has decided something, which is certain.*

She read until the lamp burned low. Freya slept in the next room, snoring as advertised. Outside, the village of perfect memory lay silent around its single new grave, holding its two-hundred-year grudges and its preserved dead and its happinesses that would never fade, and Mara understood at last what the source was selling, and what it charged, and why her father had written in the margin of the final page, in letters pressed hard enough to tear the paper:

*Some things must be allowed to end. That is not loss. That is the mercy that makes love bearable. Burn it, Mara. Burn it all.*

She closed the journal. Through the wall, Freya turned over in his sleep and murmured something that might have been a name, or several names, and did not wake.

Tomorrow they would walk upstream. Tomorrow the falls that flowed upward, and the stair cut in stone, and the spring that remembered everyone back. But tonight Mara sat alone with her father's voice in her hands, and for the first time in nine years she was not looking for justice.

She was looking for a way to keep a promise to a dead man—and she was terribly afraid that the man sleeping in the next room, the debt-ridden storm chaser with his squinted-at ghosts, was planning to sell the very thing her father had died trying to bury.
