## 16. Freya's Confession

The rain found them an hour after dark, which by the river's standards was practically polite. It came down in long silver curtains that hissed against the canopy, and they hauled the boat up a mud bank beneath an overhang of black stone where the current bent away from the channel and pooled, sullen and slow, like water that had somewhere better to be.

Freya built the fire small. That was habit — a storm chaser learned early that light was a debt you paid for in visibility — but tonight he built it smaller than usual, because there was a thing sitting in his chest the size of a sea chest, and he had been carrying it since Cape Verren, and he suspected it was going to come out whether he opened the lid or not.

Mara sat across the flames with her father's chart unrolled across her knees, studying it by firelight the way other people studied scripture. She had been doing that every night since the rapids. Not reading it, exactly. *Listening* to it. He'd caught her tracing the backward river's course with one fingertip, over and over, like she was trying to feel a pulse through the paper.

"You're staring," she said, without looking up.

"I'm assessing."

"You're staring."

"Fine. I'm staring." He fed a twig to the fire. "You've had that expression for three days. The one like you're about to ask me something and have decided against it four hundred times."

"And you've had *your* expression for nine years," she said. "I've only known you two weeks and I can already tell it's nine years old. So maybe we're even."

The rain filled the silence for a while. Somewhere upstream, the river made a sound like a throat clearing.

"Mara." His voice came out lower than he intended. "There's something I should tell you. About my crew. Before we go any further up this river. Before—" he gestured vaguely at the chart, at the dark, at the whole impossible enterprise, "—before anything else happens that makes it harder to say."

She folded the chart. Slowly. She set it aside with the care of someone putting down a weapon so both hands could be free.

"All right," she said.

* * *

He told it badly at first, the way he always did in his head, and then — because Mara Ilesanmi had a face that did not permit bad telling — he told it true.

"There were six of us. The *Kestrel*. We ran weather data for insurance syndicates, same as I do now, except back then I believed the data was the point. I thought if you measured the sky carefully enough, it would owe you honesty." He turned his hands toward the fire. They were steady hands. He hated how steady they were. "We were anchored off the Sable Shoals when the barometer started doing something no model predicted. A pressure drop like nothing in forty years of records. Every instinct I had said run for harbor. Every instrument I had said *stay, stay, this is the reading of a lifetime.*"

The fire popped. Mara didn't move.

"I told them it was safe. I didn't lie, exactly. I just arranged the truth until it looked like staying was the brave scientific thing and leaving was cowardice. Dara wanted to go. She had a daughter. I remember her standing at the rail looking at the horizon and saying, 'Freya, the sky looks wrong,' and I said, 'The sky always looks wrong before it gets interesting.'" He laughed, once, a sound with no humor in it whatsoever. "Interesting. That's what I called it."

"What happened," Mara said. Not a question. A door held open.

"The front came in sideways. Off the water, not off the land — nobody forecasts that, it shouldn't be possible, and I knew that, and I stayed anyway because the impossible reading was *mine*, do you understand? It was going to make my name. Six people trusted me with their lives because I could read weather the way priests read omens, and I chose the omen over the people." He looked at her. "The boat broke apart in eleven minutes. I survived because I was thrown clear against the mast step and the sea decided, for reasons known only to the sea, to give me back. Dara's daughter is fourteen now. She has my last letter to her mother, in which I promised, in writing, that the conditions were well within tolerance."

The rain came harder. The fire leaned and recovered.

"After that I stopped chasing storms and started running from debts," he said. "Which is its own kind of arithmetic. You can't drown in numbers quite as thoroughly as you can in water, but you can get close. The Optimists bought my note off the salvage brokers, and Pell smiles at me the way I smiled at my crew, and some nights I think that's the punishment. Not the fingers. The mirror."

Mara was quiet for a long time. Long enough that he began composing apologies for having spoken at all, cataloguing the ways he'd ruined the partnership, itemizing the silence —

"My father wrote me a letter too," she said.

Freya looked up.

"It came eight months after the Survey Office declared him missing. Some port clerk found it in Tomás's effects and mailed it because regulations required it. No cover note. Just his handwriting." Her jaw worked. "It said: *If you are reading this, I have found something worth more than my return, and I am sorry, and do not come looking, and I know you will come looking.*" She laughed — a broken twin of his own laugh. "Nine years I've carried that letter. Nine years I've read it ten thousand ways. *Worth more than my return.* What does that even mean? What's worth more? And every year I hated him a little more for writing it, and hated myself more for hating him, because he was dead, probably, and you're not supposed to hate the dead, and—"

She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes.

"And here's the thing I've never said out loud," she went on, steadier now, the way water is steadier just before it goes over the falls. "I would have traded this. All of it. The journey, the source, the legend, the truth — everything we're walking toward. I'd have traded it in a heartbeat for him walking through my door with salt in his beard and nothing in his hands. No discovery. No justice. Just him, ordinary, home late for dinner." She lowered her hand. Her eyes were wet and furious and free. "Everyone thinks grief wants answers. Grief doesn't want answers. Grief wants *dinner*."

Freya found that his throat had closed entirely.

"So when you tell me you traded your crew for a reading," Mara said, "and I tell you I'd have traded the world for a dinner — I don't think either of us is confessing. I think we're just finally saying the price out loud."

* * *

The fire needed feeding. Neither of them moved to feed it.

"Do you believe in forgiveness?" Freya asked eventually. "Actual forgiveness. Not the kind you perform at funerals."

"I believe in it the way I believe in the backward river," Mara said. "I've seen evidence. I haven't seen the source."

That startled a real laugh out of him — short, surprised, warm in the chest. "That's the most honest theology I've ever heard."

"Well. I'm a cartographer's daughter. We don't draw coastlines we haven't sounded." She pulled her knees up, wrapped her arms around them, and regarded him across the flames with an expression he was beginning to recognize as her thinking face, which was dangerous, because her thinking face usually preceded something true. "But I'll tell you what I noticed, Freya Zhou. You said the sea gave you back. And you've spent nine years acting like that was a clerical error. Like any day the sea will notice its mistake and send Pell to correct it."

"Pell does a fair impression."

"Pell is a bill collector wearing a smile. But the sea—" she nodded at the dark beyond the overhang, where the river ran on, patient and enormous, "—the sea gave you back. My father's river gave me a chart instead of a body. Maybe the wilderness doesn't forgive. Maybe it just... keeps accounts differently than we do. Longer ledgers. Kinder entries."

"You sound like the ferrywoman."

"Don't compare me to the woman who ate my childhood."

"She took one memory, Mara. One. And you've mentioned your mother's face is gone, but you talk about her like—" he searched for it, "—like someone you're introducing, not someone you've lost. Does that make sense?"

Mara was silent. Then, slowly: "I still know she laughed with her whole shoulders. I can't see her face, but I know that. Is that worse or better?"

"I don't know," Freya said honestly. "I think it might be both."

"Both," Mara agreed. "Everything worth having is both." She reached over and pushed a log into the heart of the fire, and sparks went up, and for a moment the whole overhang was full of drifting orange constellations. "For what it's worth — and I'm not a priest, and I don't forgive on behalf of dead women — but Dara stood at the rail and told you the sky looked wrong, and then she stayed. Sailors aren't children, Freya. She made her own arithmetic. It doesn't absolve you. But it means you weren't alone in the choosing."

He hadn't known he needed to hear that. He discovered it the way you discover a cracked rib — all at once, and breathing hurt differently afterward. He put his face in his hands for a moment, which was not dignified, and found that he did not especially care.

When he looked up, Mara was watching him with something that wasn't pity. Pity he could have survived easily. This was worse. This was recognition.

"We're a pair, aren't we," he said. "A man paying interest on a forgiven debt and a woman carrying a letter that says *don't come looking* in a boat headed exactly there."

"A matched set," she agreed. "The Survey Office should catalogue us."

"They'd seal us."

"They sealed my father."

The joke fell between them and turned, gently, into the thing underneath it, and neither of them picked it back up. The rain softened to a whisper. Downstream, far behind them, the world they'd left — debts, wardens, collectors, offices full of people signing things — went on with its business. Upstream, the river waited with whatever it kept.

"Freya." Mara's voice had changed. Quieter. "At the ferry crossing. When the old woman took my mother's face — you offered her coin first. Real coin. You tried to pay my toll yourself."

"It didn't work."

"No. But you tried." She looked into the fire. "Nobody's ever tried to pay my tolls before. People mostly hand me the bill and admire how well I carry it."

Freya thought about saying something large. He was, by temperament, a man who reached for large things the way drowning men reach for masts. Instead he said, "I'll keep trying. Fair warning. I'm bad at it, but I'm persistent. Ask any debt collector in Salt Hollow."

"Noted," she said, and the corner of her mouth did the thing — the small crooked thing that was, he was beginning to suspect, the single most valuable landmark on this entire unmapped river.

They divided the watch. She took first; he lay down under the overhang with the rain talking overhead and the fire ticking, and sleep came faster than it had in nine years, dreamless and dark and kind.

* * *

He woke near dawn to Mara's hand on his shoulder and her voice pitched low and flat in a register he'd learned, these past weeks, to fear.

"Freya. Wake up properly. There's a lantern on the water."

He came up out of the blankets with his heart already climbing. Beyond the overhang, downstream where the river bent, a light swung — steady, methodical, sweeping the banks in the practiced arcs of someone who had done this many times and expected, eventually, to be obeyed.

Not Pell. Pell traveled with noise and laughter and too many engines. This was a single quiet light, moving like a search warrant.

And beside it, fainter, high on the ridge across the channel: a second light. Red. Stationary. The kind of lamp a government warden used when she wanted you to know she'd found you and hadn't yet decided what to do about it.

Mara crouched beside him, the chart already rolled and strapped, her father's compass in her fist. "Two of them," she whispered. "One on the water, one on the land. Which do you suppose is the trap?"

Freya watched the lantern swing its patient arcs and felt, absurdly, calm — lighter than he'd felt since Cape Verren, as if confession had cut ballast he'd forgotten he was carrying.

"Neither," he said. "Or both. Depends who asks first."

The red lamp on the ridge blinked once. Twice. Then, unmistakably, deliberately: three times.

Signal code. Old Survey Office code. And Mara went very still beside him, because she knew it too — everyone raised on a cartographer's knee knew it — and three flashes meant the oldest message in the book, the one Tomás Ilesanmi had surely sent his daughter's name attached to, years ago, from the middle of nowhere:

*Come carefully. I have something to show you.*
