## 17. Okonjo on the Water

Cass Okonjo had been a warden for eleven years, and in that time she had learned that the river told you everything about a person before they ever opened their mouths. The way they sat in a boat. The way they read the water ahead. Whether they flinched at the sound of rapids or leaned into it.

Freya Zhou sat in a boat like a man apologizing to it. Shoulders hunched, hands light on the tiller, as if the vessel were something he'd wronged and was hoping to make amends. The woman beside him—Mara Ilesanmi, according to the file Cass had memorized and then, two nights ago, burned into her memory properly by reading it three times—sat like she'd been born in one. Knees braced, eyes always moving, cataloguing.

Cass watched them from the shadow of a dead cedar leaning out over the middle reach of the river, her patrol skiff tied off beneath its roots where the overhang swallowed it whole. She had been waiting since dawn. Her tea had gone cold twice. She had rehearsed what she would say approximately forty times, and every version sounded either like an ambush or a confession, which, she was beginning to suspect, amounted to the same thing.

The demolition convoy was three days behind her. Three days, four hours if the lead barge lost another engine part to the shallows, which it would, because the Survey Office had bought its equipment from the lowest bidder and the lowest bidder had never once seen this river. Cass knew because she'd filed the report saying so, and because filing reports that changed nothing had become, lately, the primary activity of her professional life.

She checked her sidearm out of habit, then holstered it again out of decision. That distinction mattered. She wanted them to see the holster. She did not want them to see the gun.

* * *

They came around the bend an hour past midmorning, Freya's patched boat riding low with supplies, the backward current doing strange, lovely things to their wake—water flowing inland from the sea, pushing them gently toward a place none of them should go. Cass let them pass her hiding spot. Then she let them get fifty meters further. Then she keyed the skiff's engine and slid out from under the cedar like a rumor coming true.

She saw Freya's shoulders drop before he even turned—the specific slump of a man who has been running long enough to be almost relieved. Mara's hand went under the thwart, came back empty, and her expression flickered through disappointment so fast most people would have missed it. Cass didn't miss it. She made a note: *the daughter expected weapons. Plan accordingly.*

"Survey Warden Okonjo," Cass called, when the hulls were close enough. "Cass. I'd appreciate it if nobody did anything memorable for the next five minutes."

Freya cut his engine. The two boats drifted together on the impossible current, bumping gunwales with a soft wooden knock, like old friends greeting.

"You're alone," Freya said. It wasn't a question. He was scanning the banks behind her, the canopy above, counting exits. A man who'd spent years chasing storms learned to count exits the way other men counted money.

"I'm alone," Cass confirmed. "No escort. No recorder." She held up her field tablet and, deliberately, powered it off in front of them. Set it face-down on the seat. "Which means this conversation is happening nowhere, officially. If anyone asks, I spent today inspecting culverts."

Mara's eyes narrowed. "Why would you lie for us?"

"I'm not lying for you," Cass said. "I'm lying for me. There's a difference, and it matters, and I'll get to it." She looked at Freya. "Zhou. You've got a chart that doesn't exist, heading toward a place the Survey Office has spent sixty years pretending doesn't exist. I'm supposed to arrest you, confiscate it, and wait for a convoy."

"And instead?" Freya asked.

"Instead I'm going to tell you what I know, and then I'm going to sit here and not decide anything, and you're both going to find that deeply uncomfortable." She paused. "Tea? I have tea. It's terrible."

* * *

They rafted the boats together in the lee of a gravel bar, and Cass poured tea from a dented thermos into three tin cups, and it was, as promised, terrible—oversteeped and faintly of diesel—but Mara drank hers like it was a sacrament and Freya wrapped both hands around his like he needed something warm to hold onto more than he needed to drink it.

"The convoy left Fort Meridian six days ago," Cass said. "Two barges, a demolition crew of nine, and enough shaped charge to bring down half the gorge. Officially, they're conducting 'hydrological remediation.' Unofficially—" she turned her cup slowly, watching the leaves settle, "—they're going to seal the source permanently. Collapse the cavern, redirect the spring, bury it under a hundred tons of rock. And bury any proof of Tomás Ilesanmi's discovery along with it."

Mara went very still. Cass had seen that stillness before, in people standing at the edge of grief they'd been carrying too long. "My father found it," Mara said quietly. "Years ago. Didn't he."

It wasn't a question either, and Cass hated that she was the one who had to answer it. "He found it nine years ago. He reported it. And then he refused to file the follow-up survey that would have made it exploitable." She met Mara's eyes. "Your father chose to lose himself rather than hand it over. I've read the file. I wasn't supposed to read the file. The file reads like a man writing letters to no one, hoping someone would eventually read them."

Mara set down her cup with exaggerated care, the way people do when the alternative is throwing it. "Who signed the order?"

"That's the second thing." Cass took a breath. Here was the part she'd rehearsed forty times and still couldn't make sound like anything but what it was. "The order authorizing the demolition bears the signature of Deputy Director Halloway. The same Halloway who, eight months ago, received a very generous donation to the Survey Office benevolent fund from a lending syndicate called the Optimists. The same Optimists who employ a collector named Pell, who has been asking questions about this river in three port towns, and whose launch was spotted on the lower reach two days ago."

Freya's cup stopped halfway to his mouth. "Pell's on the river."

"Pell's on the river," Cass confirmed. "And Pell isn't working for the Survey Office. The Survey Office is, increasingly, working for Pell. He bought influence—not bribes, nothing so crude. Endowments. Grants. A new research wing with his syndicate's name tastefully engraved above the door. Half my colleagues think he's a philanthropist. The other half think he's a philanthropist." She smiled without any humor in it. "You'd be amazed how far charm gets you when nobody's willing to check the arithmetic underneath it."

* * *

The silence that followed was the long kind, the kind filled with water sounds and the distant complaint of a fish hawk. Cass let it run. She'd learned that from interrogations: whoever spoke first after real news gave away what they cared about most.

Mara spoke first. "Three days," she said. "Can we reach the source before the convoy?"

"You can reach it in two, if the river cooperates. The river won't cooperate. Nothing about that river cooperates; that's rather the point of it." Cass turned to Freya. "But here's my problem, Zhou, and I want you to hear it plainly, because I don't think anyone's been plain with you in a long time."

"Plainness is in short supply," Freya agreed. His voice was dry, but his eyes weren't.

"My oath says I stop you. My orders say I help the convoy seal it. My conscience says—" She stopped. Started again. "I've served eleven years because I believed the Survey Office was how civilization kept its promises to wild places. Draw the maps. Guard the edges. Keep the greedy from grinding everything beautiful into revenue. And now I've discovered the man signing my orders sold the edges to a debt collector, and the map I'm sworn to protect is being demolished to hide a crime, and every tool I have—every form, every regulation, every procedure—is built to serve exactly the people it was written against." She laughed, short and bitter. "Do you know what that's like? Discovering the law has fault lines? That it's not bedrock at all, just plates floating on something hotter, and everyone aboard pretending the ground doesn't move?"

"Yes," said Freya quietly.

Cass looked at him. "Yes?"

"I chased storms for a living. Spent years trusting instruments, protocols, the whole architecture of knowing things. Then one night the architecture failed and my crew paid for it." He said it flatly, without performance, and Cass recognized the flatness too—it was the voice of a man who had told himself this story so many times the telling had worn smooth. "So yes. I know what it's like when the thing you built your life on turns out to have a crack running through it."

For a moment neither of them spoke, and the river carried them all a little further upstream, gentle as a lie.

"So you haven't decided," Mara said. Not accusing. Almost gentle herself, which from her, Cass suspected, was rarer than mercy. "Whose side you're on."

"No," Cass admitted. "I haven't. And I want to be honest about that, because you're about to do something dangerous based on what I tell you, and you deserve to know that the person telling you might still be the person who arrests you tomorrow." She held up a hand before either could respond. "Here's what I *have* decided. One: I will not radio the convoy your position. Two: I will not confiscate the chart. Three: if Pell's collectors reach you before I make up my mind, I will intervene, because whatever else is true, I did not put on this uniform to work for a loan shark. And four—" she hesitated, then committed, "—when I decide, you'll be the first to know. Both of you. Whatever I decide."

"That's not much," Mara said.

"It's everything I have," Cass said. "I'm offering you the truth that I am unreliable, which is more than the Survey Office has ever offered anyone."

* * *

Freya studied her for a long moment, and Cass had the uncomfortable sensation of being surveyed herself—measured against some internal ledger of trustworthiness that a man like him kept the way she kept her logs.

"The convoy," he said finally. "Nine crew, two barges. Who leads it?"

"A man named Drescher. Competent. Obedient. The dangerous combination." Cass dug into her jacket and produced a folded sheet of paper—actual paper, because paper didn't leave records on networks. "Convoy route, schedule, the blast plan Halloway signed. I copied it by hand. If anyone asks, it doesn't exist, same as this conversation."

Mara took it, read it fast, and Cass watched her lips move slightly over the numbers—a cartographer's daughter doing cartographer's arithmetic. "They're placing charges at the gorge narrows," Mara said. "That's upstream of the source. They'll flood the whole upper canyon."

"They'll flood everything," Cass agreed. "Including, potentially, anyone inside it. The plan accounts for 'acceptable environmental loss.'" She let the phrase sit in the air between them, ugly in its officialese. "Halloway's own wording."

Freya and Mara exchanged a look—one of those conversations that happened entirely in the space between two faces, and Cass felt, briefly and unexpectedly, lonely. Eleven years of procedure, and these two strangers had a language she wasn't fluent in.

Then Freya turned back. "Why give us this? Really. Off the record, no recorder. What do you get?"

And there it was—the question she'd been avoiding through forty rehearsals and one cold pot of tea. Cass looked out at the river, at the backward-running water catching the midday light like hammered tin, and answered honestly, because she'd promised herself honesty even if she couldn't yet promise allegiance.

"I get to find out what I am," she said. "When the law breaks, somebody has to stand in the gap and decide what holds. I've spent my whole career believing I'd know the right side when I saw it. Now I'm looking at a debtor, a grieving daughter, and a spring that remembers everything, and I can't tell anymore whether I'm a warden or just a well-dressed wall for other people's greed." She stood, steadying herself against the gunwale as the boats knocked together. "So I'm going to sit on this gravel bar tonight, drink worse tea than this, and try to figure out whether my oath means protecting the institution or protecting the thing the institution was invented to protect. And whichever answer I reach—" she met their eyes, one and then the other, "—I'll live with it. That's the only promise worth making."

She stepped back onto her skiff, untied the line, and paused with her hand on the ignition.

"One more thing," she said. "Pell's launch was armed. Not syndicate-standard. Military surplus. Somebody's selling him more than influence." She let that land. "Whatever you two are to each other—and it's none of my business, though for the record, it's extremely obvious—you should assume he's not playing bookkeeping anymore. He's playing war."

Freya's jaw tightened. Beside him, Mara had already pulled out the hand-copied route sheet and was studying it like it might confess something the ink hadn't said aloud.

"Warden," Freya said, as her engine caught. "If you decide the institution's worth saving—"

"Then I'll arrest you politely," Cass finished. "And if I decide otherwise, I expect you'll shoot at me impolitely. Fair warning both ways."

She pushed off from the gravel bar and let the backward current take her downstream, toward the convoy she was technically scouting for and the conscience she was increasingly scouting against. Behind her, the patched boat turned upstream, into the gorge, into the narrowing stone.

Cass watched them go until the bend took them, and then she sat for a long while with her cold tea, listening to the river run the wrong way, thinking about fault lines.

It was nearly dusk when she noticed it: fresh boot prints on the gravel bar, downstream edge, half-washed by the tide. Someone had landed here after they left—or before she arrived—and stood for a long time. The prints were deep-heeled, weight forward.

A man who leans in, she thought, remembering the file photograph, the easy smile, the tastelessly excellent shoes.

Pell had been on this bank. Pell had heard everything.

And somewhere upstream, in the gathering dark, the first far-off grumble of monsoon thunder rolled down the gorge like a warning nobody had asked for.
