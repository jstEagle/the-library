## 18. The Chart's Second Layer

The rain came in off the middle reach an hour after dark, soft as an apology, drumming on the boat's canvas awning while Freya rigged the storm lamp over the little table in the cabin. Mara sat across from him with her father's chart unrolled between them, pinned at the corners by a tin cup, a spool of survey line, and the brass instrument they'd pulled from the cave—Tomás's old alidade, its glass fogged with nine years of salt.

"You've been staring at it for two days," Mara said. "You've memorized it. I've watched you recite it in your sleep, which, for the record, is deeply unsettling."

"I recite tide tables in my sleep. It's a professional deformity."

"You said 'the eastern fork bends wrong' at three this morning and then apologized to someone named Bello."

Freya winced. Bello had been his navigator, once. He let that sit where it landed and bent back over the chart. "There's something wrong with it. That's the point. Everything about it is *right*, which is what's wrong."

Mara raised an eyebrow, which was her way of saying *go on, then*, and he did, because he'd learned by now that she didn't interrupt so much as she aimed.

"Look at the survey work itself. Your father was a master. Every sounding is consistent, every bearing cross-checked twice, the scale's true to the half-furlong. This is the cleanest chart I've ever held. And then—" he tapped the inked river as it climbed into the canyon country, "—the source. Here. The spring symbol. It's drawn in a different hand."

"It's his hand. I'd know it anywhere."

"It's *his* hand drawn *carefully*. That's the difference. Everything else on this chart he drew the way a man draws when he's thinking faster than his pen—quick, confident, a little wild at the edges. Beautiful, but wild. The spring symbol is slow. Deliberate. He drew it the way a man draws when he's deciding exactly how much to tell."

Mara leaned in. The lamp threw her shadow across the paper, and for a moment her silhouette lay over the canyon like a second river. "You're saying he faked his own symbol."

"I'm saying he *buried* something. Cartographers do it all the time—trap streets, false shoals, a reef that isn't there to catch plagiarists. But you don't fake a detail on your own masterpiece unless you're hiding the truth of it under something you can live with people finding." He sat back. "And there's one more thing. The paper's heavier than it needs to be. Survey stock is thin so it folds small. This is double-laid. Two sheets, pasted."

Mara went very still. "You think there's another chart underneath."

"I think your father was the kind of man who pasted his secrets where only someone willing to destroy the surface would ever find them. And I've been putting off testing it for two days because—" he gestured at the chart, at the whole drowned, impossible, backward country it described, "—this is the last thing he ever made. I didn't want to be the one who ruined it."

Mara was quiet a moment. Then she reached across the table, took the brass alidade out from under its corner, and held it up.

"His blade edge is ground fine," she said. "He used to sharpen it on the doorstep every Sunday morning. I'd bring him the whetstone. He'd let me hold the instrument after, and tell me the rule." Her voice didn't waver, but Freya saw her hand tighten around the brass. "A chart that can't be questioned is a chart that can't be trusted. He said it so often I thought it was just a thing old men said."

She set the alidade down at the chart's edge, blade flat, like a surgeon laying out a knife.

"Do it," she said. "Question it."

* * *

He worked slow. That was the whole craft of it—slow, and wet, and patient. He warmed water over the lamp and applied it with a sponge no bigger than his thumb, working from the margins inward, softening the old paste a grain at a time. The rain kept its rhythm on the canvas. Mara held the lamp steady and didn't speak, which he understood was the hardest work she'd done all week.

The first corner lifted after twenty minutes, curling back like the edge of a leaf in autumn. Underneath: paper. Blank, at first glance, and Freya's stomach dropped with the fear that he'd ruined a masterpiece for nothing.

Then the lamp caught it at an angle, and the blankness resolved.

Faint, in a water-mixed ink that was nearly the color of the paper itself—ink meant to be invisible unless you knew to look—was a second river. The same river, the same course, but annotated. Densely. Margins crowded with a small, precise hand, figures stacked on figures, and arrows, and little symbols Freya didn't recognize at first and then did, with a cold sliding feeling, because he'd seen them before: on the flood-gates at the old salt works, on the sluice diagrams in his first employer's office, on every piece of hydraulic engineering he'd ever cursed at in a storm.

"Those aren't map marks," he said. "Those are *mechanism* marks."

Mara's breath had gone shallow. "Read the margins."

He read. The hand was Tomás's, but younger—the annotations spanned years, he could see it, the ink shifting, the shorthand evolving. The earliest notes were survey notes: depths, strata, the geology of the canyon above the source. The later notes were something else entirely.

*"The spring sits in a basin of limestone, and the limestone sits on clay. The clay can be moved. The mountain has a throat, and a man with time and stone can close it."*

*"Gate A, west wall, natural fissure widened. Six feet of drop if the plug is pulled. The water will do the rest. Water always does the rest. That is the whole lesson of my life."*

*"Gate B, the old channel under the falls. Sealed with rubble by hands older than mine. Openable. Barely. Once."*

*"If the source must die, it can die drowned. Redirected into the deep strata, sealed under its own mountain. The river returns to its natural course. The legend drowns. Let the sea have back what the sea was always owed."*

Freya read it twice, three times, the lamp hissing, the rain keeping time. Then he looked up at Mara, and found her already looking at him, and neither of them had anything to say for a long moment.

"He wasn't mapping a treasure," she said finally. Her voice was strange—flat and vast at once, like the sea before weather. "He was mapping a *funeral*."

"He was mapping a choice." Freya's finger traced the gate symbols, the little arrows showing flow, the figures for volume and drop. "Mara, look at the engineering. This is decades of work. The fissure at Gate A—the notes say he widened it himself, over seasons, coming back year after year. He built the means to destroy the source into the very chart everyone would kill to possess. And then he drew a spring symbol on top of it and sailed into a cave at Cape Verren with the only copy."

"Why?" The question cracked out of her harder than she meant it. "Why hide it? Why not just—tell people? Seal it openly? Why die in a hole with the truth pasted under an ink drawing?"

Freya thought about the villagers of the middle reach, who remembered everything and forgave nothing. He thought about Pell, smiling across a flooded bar, explaining interest the way other men explained weather. He thought about the Survey Office, and wardens, and syndicates, and every ledger ever kept.

"Because a secret that's announced is a secret that's owned," he said. "If he'd published the mechanism, the first government to read it would have dug the gates out and kept the spring running under guard forever—bottled it, sold it, weaponized it. The only way to protect a thing like this is to make the protection itself a secret, so that the only people who ever find it are the people who were never looking for a weapon in the first place." He paused. "Or people led there by a dying man in a cave."

Mara's jaw worked. "He gave the chart to you."

"He gave the chart to whoever the tide brought him. I've thought about that every night since. He was trapped. The water was coming. He had minutes. And he looked at me—a stranger, a debt-ridden salvage man, the single least trustworthy person in that cave—and he gave it to me anyway." Freya laughed, but there was no humor in it. "Because he wasn't choosing me. He was choosing the tide. He trusted the sea's arithmetic more than any man's."

"He always did say the water keeps better books than people do."

They sat with that. The lamp burned. Outside, the river ran backward past the hull, patient and strange, and somewhere upstream a night bird called three notes and got no answer.

Mara reached out and touched the hidden annotations, her fingertip resting light on the words *the water will do the rest*. "Nine years I wanted justice for him. I imagined it every way—trials, confessions, the Survey Office men in a dock, someone, anyone, punished. And the whole time, the justice was already made. He made it himself. He spent his whole last decade building the thing that would make him safe to kill." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and, underneath it, something Freya had never seen in her before: relief. "He wasn't a victim who needed avenging. He was an engineer who needed *finishing*."

"Then we finish it," Freya said. It came out of him simply, without the negotiation he'd have given it a month ago, without weighing it against debt or survival or the Optimists' cheerful arithmetic. He noticed that about himself with mild surprise, the way you notice a knot has come untied. "We go up, we find the gates, we do what he built them to do."

"You understand what you're saying. Your debt. The source was your way out. Sell the location, clear the ledger, keep your boat and your fingers."

"I've done the arithmetic again," Freya said. "Turns out I'm still short in everything but skill. But skill's not nothing. And there's a difference, I've finally learned, between a debt you owe and a debt you've been *told* you owe." He tapped the hidden chart. "Your father spent his life paying the second kind. I'd like to spend a week paying the first."

Mara looked at him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled—a small, crooked, exhausted smile, the first one he'd ever seen her spend on him.

"My father would have liked you," she said. "He had a weakness for men who did the right thing late, after exhausting all other options. He called it *thoroughness*."

"He sounds like a generous man."

"He was a *surveyor*. Generosity is just accuracy about people." She began rolling the chart, carefully, both layers together now, the secret and its disguise reunited. "We should sleep. We make the gorge by midday, and if the annotations are true, the stair is cut into the west wall above the falls."

Freya doused the lamp to a coal. In the dark, the river's sound filled the cabin, and he lay listening to it, thinking about a man sharpening a blade on a doorstep every Sunday while his daughter held the whetstone, building a funeral into a map one hidden stroke at a time.

He was nearly asleep when Mara spoke from her bunk, quietly, the way people say things they've decided not to decide yet.

"Freya. The notes said Gate B is openable. *Barely. Once.*"

"I know."

"And the last entry, the newest ink, at the very bottom of the margin—it's not engineering. It's just one line." A pause, long enough that the rain filled it. "It says: *If you are reading this, I am sorry, and I hope the water chose better than I could have.*"

Freya stared into the dark, the words settling over him like the weight of the whole mountain upstream.

Because in the morning, as they came up the narrowing gorge with the falls roaring ahead—falls that fell *upward*, silver against the grey stone—they both saw it at the same moment.

Parked in the lee of the canyon wall, canvas lashed down, cookfire smoking, sat a launch he knew far too well.

Pell had found the stair.
