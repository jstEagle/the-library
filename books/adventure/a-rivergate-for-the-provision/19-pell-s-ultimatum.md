## 19. Pell's Ultimatum

The Optimists kept their ledgers the way other men kept diaries, and Pell Voss kept his the way other men kept ledgers.

He sat in the stern cabin of the *Cheerful Prospect*—a name he had chosen himself, over the objections of no one, because no one objected to Pell Voss—and turned the pages by lamplight while the river muttered against the hull. Outside, the monsoon was still a rumor on the horizon, a bruise-colored suggestion of weather. Inside, everything was in order.

That was the thing people never understood about him, Pell reflected, wetting his thumb to turn a page. They thought the Optimists were cruel because they were cheerful, when in fact they were cheerful because they were never cruel without cause. Cruelty without cause was waste, and waste was sadness, and sadness was bad bookkeeping. Every entry in his ledger had a reason. Every reason had a number. And every number, given time and patience and a sufficiently persuasive associate, came due.

*Zhou, Freya.* He read the entry again with something close to affection. Storm chaser. Boat owner, technically—the boat was collateral, which meant it belonged to the Optimists and was merely on loan to its former owner's optimism. Outstanding balance: substantial. Payment history: catastrophic. Risk assessment: high.

And yet.

Pell liked Freya Zhou. That was not sentiment; it was analysis. A man who had survived the kind of storm Zhou had survived—the one that had taken his crew, the one whose details lived in a sealed incident file Pell had read twice for pleasure—was not a man who broke easily. He bent. He bent like a mast in a gale, groaning all the way down to the waterline, and then he straightened up again and kept sailing, which was exactly the quality you wanted in an asset. Broken men stopped paying. Bent men paid forever.

The problem with Freya Zhou was not that he would fail to find the source. The problem was that he might succeed and then do something stupid with it, like keep it, or destroy it, or—Pell shuddered delicately at the thought—give it away. Sentiment was the only form of bankruptcy the Optimists could not refinance.

Which was why the ledger needed a second column.

He turned to the page marked *Ilesanmi, M.* and considered what he saw there, which was almost nothing. Daughter of Tomás Ilesanmi, cartographer, deceased-or-presumed. Nine years of petitions to the Survey Office, each one denied, each one filed, each one a small monument to a grief that refused to be processed. No debts. No assets worth naming. No leverage whatsoever, on paper.

On paper. Pell smiled at the phrase. Paper was where most people hid their real holdings.

A knock at the cabin door, two beats, polite. "Enter," Pell said, and did not look up, because looking up suggested urgency and urgency suggested doubt, and Pell Voss did not doubt. Doubt was interest accruing on confidence, and he paid his debts promptly.

His senior collector came in—a broad, patient woman named Hesk who had once been a schoolteacher and had found the transition to collections remarkably painless, since both professions involved making people understand what they owed. She stood at attention in the doorway, dripping a little, because it had begun to rain in the way coastal rain begins: not falling so much as arriving.

"You sent for me, Mr. Voss."

"I did." Pell closed the ledger and folded his hands on top of it, a gesture he had practiced in a mirror until it looked effortless, which was the entire point of practice. "Tell me what you know about grief, Hesk."

Hesk blinked. In eleven years she had never known him to open a meeting with a question he didn't already have answered. "That it makes people unreasonable, sir."

"Wrong." He said it kindly, the way you'd correct a promising student. "Grief makes people *punctual*. It's the only appointment some of them ever keep. A grieving man will cross a flooded province in a leaking boat to stand where his father died, and he'll be on time, Hesk. Early, even. Grief is the most reliable scheduling force in nature. More reliable than tides." He tapped the ledger. "More reliable than debt, if you can believe it."

"And this matters because?"

"Because we have two grieving people on that river instead of one." Pell stood and went to the chart table, where the river's course was inked in careful lines—the middle reach, the gorge, the canyon country beyond where the surveys gave up and the maps went honest about their ignorance. His finger found the gorge. "Zhou is up there somewhere, following a current that flows backward, in a boat I own, carrying a document I want, toward a location I intend to monetize. He has stalled me twice now with that smile of his. Do you know what a stall is, Hesk, in accounting terms?"

"A deferral, sir."

"A *cost*. Every day he stalls, the monsoon gets closer, the Survey Office's warden gets closer, and the value of our exclusive access gets smaller. Deferral is just loss wearing a polite face." He traced the gorge with one finger. "So we stop deferring. We collect."

"Zhou won't come quietly. He's got nothing left to lose."

"He has one thing left to lose." Pell let the silence do a little work. It was good work; silence always was. "He has her."

Hesk frowned slightly. "The Ilesanmi woman? Sir, she's not an asset. She has no accounts, no collateral, no—"

"She's not an asset." Pell turned from the chart, and for a moment his face did something rare, which was to become entirely sincere. He saved sincerity for moments like this, the way other men saved their best wine. "She's an *entry*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything. An asset is something you hold. An entry is something you write down, and once it's written down, the story it tells becomes true. If I write *leverage* next to her name, she is leverage. The universe adjusts. It's very accommodating that way."

"That's not how the universe works, Mr. Voss."

"It's exactly how the universe works. It's how it worked on your old schoolhouse, when the district wrote *surplus* next to your position and closed it. Did the universe object?" He watched her jaw tighten and noted it, mildly, in the margin of his mind. Hesk's resentment of the district was a small, well-tended garden; he watered it occasionally, when he needed her sharp. "No. The universe adjusted. People adjust. It's the one thing they're reliably good at."

He returned to the table and began, with a fountain pen, to draft. He drafted the way he did everything—neatly, cheerfully, with the calm of a man balancing columns that had never once failed to balance.

"The plan is simple, because simple plans survive weather and complicated ones drown. We take the woman at the gorge. Cleanly, courteously, without harm—I want that understood and underlined. We are not thugs. Thugs create grievances, and grievances compound worse than any interest schedule I've ever seen. We take her, we feed her, we give her dry socks, and we send word to Zhou: the chart for the girl, or the girl stays with us indefinitely, and her upkeep goes on his account." He glanced up, eyes bright. "Do you see the elegance? Her upkeep. On *his account*. He'll be paying us to hold his own conscience hostage. There are days I love this work, Hesk. Truly."

"And if he refuses? If he calls it?"

"Then we've lost nothing but a few days' provisions, and we've learned something priceless: that Zhou values a stranger's daughter over his own survival, which means he can't be leveraged at all, which means we stop wasting effort on persuasion and start preparing for the endgame differently." Pell capped the pen. "A good collection attempt is never wasted, even when it fails. Failure is data. Data is future profit. Honestly, sometimes I think failure is the most underrated line item in the whole profession."

Hesk studied him for a moment. "You talk about them like weather fronts, sir. Zhou, the woman. Fronts moving across a map."

"Because that's what they are." Pell said it without malice, which somehow made it worse, and he knew it made it worse, and said it anyway, because honesty between colleagues was a form of efficiency. "Weather fronts with feelings. And here's the secret nobody teaches you, Hesk: you can't fight a front. You can only get ahead of it. Set up where it's going to make landfall and sell umbrellas." He smiled. "We're going to sell umbrellas at the gorge."

* * *

They made the gorge by midafternoon of the second day, running the collectors' launch hard against the current, which fought them every furlong like a living thing with opinions. Pell stood at the rail in his good coat—it was a ridiculous coat for fieldwork, pale gray, spotless, and he wore it precisely because it was ridiculous, because a collector who dressed for comfort admitted he expected to stay long, and Pell expected nothing except results—and watched the canyon walls rise around them, red stone streaked black with old water-marks, higher than any cliff had a right to be.

The locals downstream called this stretch the Throat. Pell approved of the name. Names were the first invoices a place ever sent you, and this one had told them plainly: passage costs.

"Contact," said the lookout, a young man named Ferrow with excellent eyes and poor nerves. "Upstream, hugging the north wall. Small boat. Patched sail. It's them."

Pell raised his glass—not out of necessity but ceremony; a man should mark his professional milestones—and found them. The little patched boat, absurd and stubborn, nosing upriver against a current that flowed the wrong way. Two figures aboard. One at the tiller, lean and sun-darkened, moving with the economy of a man who had spent his life arguing with weather and occasionally winning. One in the bow, leaning into the wind, hair whipping, watching the water ahead with an intensity that looked, from this distance, almost like devotion.

"There they are," Pell said warmly, to no one in particular. "Look at them. Working together. Sharing rations, I'd wager. Taking watches. Trusting each other." He lowered the glass. "It's remarkable, isn't it, how quickly people build things. Boats, partnerships, hope. And how much faster those things come apart when you apply the correct pressure at the correct joint." He handed the glass to Hesk. "Not the boat. Not yet. Let them round the bend into the narrows, where the walls pinch in and there's nowhere to run but forward or back. Then we take the bow, not the stern. The woman first, gently. Zhou will try to be heroic; let him. Heroics burn fuel, and fuel is on our side—we have more of everything, including patience."

"And after?"

"After?" Pell settled back against the rail, watching the little boat dwindle upstream into the throat of the world, and felt the particular contentment of a ledger about to balance. "After, we write the letter. Short one. Zhou responds better to brevity than threat—he's a numbers man under all that salt spray, and numbers respect concision. *Come to the source. Bring the chart. Come alone, come honest, and the girl comes home.* Something like that. Maybe a pleasantry at the end. He strikes me as a man who'd appreciate a pleasantry."

He paused, and something flickered across his face—not doubt, never doubt, but a cousin of it, some distant relative visiting briefly from out of town.

"It occurs to me," he said, more quietly, "that he may simply give her up. Trade the chart, walk away, wash his hands. Some men do. Most, even." He turned the thought over like a coin of uncertain denomination. "But he won't. I've read his file. I've read the storm file. A man who carries his dead crew around inside him for years, paying interest on a debt no one's collecting—" Pell shook his head, almost tenderly. "That man doesn't trade people, Hesk. That man *drowns* for them. It's his single greatest weakness and, I confess, the reason I've never quite managed to dislike him."

The launch rounded the bend. Ahead, through the narrowing walls, the patched sail vanished into shadow, and the backward-flowing river carried them—carried all of them—toward whatever waited at the top of the world.

"Set the watch," Pell said pleasantly. "We move at dusk."

* * *

Dusk came down the gorge like slow water, filling the cracks first.

Pell stood at the rail of the *Cheerful Prospect*, anchored in the narrows, and listened to the river. It sounded wrong here—no one had told him it would sound wrong. Rivers were supposed to run downhill, chattering toward the sea like children running home. This one ran uphill, silent and deliberate, hauling itself inland through the dark as though it had somewhere important to be and all the time in the world to get there.

Somewhere above, past the falls that fell upward, past the stairs cut in stone by hands older than any nation, there was a spring that remembered everything. Pell intended to own it. Not the water—water was bulky, perishable, a logistics nightmare—but the *location*, the exclusivity, the right to say yes or no when governments and syndicates and lonely rich men came asking. The spring itself was just inventory. The gate was the product.

*A Rivergate for the Provision,* he murmured to himself, trying the phrase on. The locals had a saying like that, he'd been told. He rather liked it. There was money in a good name.

Hesk joined him at the rail, and behind her, in the launch's shadowed belly, he could hear the collectors making ready—ropes coiled, boots laced, the soft metallic grammar of a quiet operation assembling itself. Somewhere in that darkness, too, sat Mara Ilesanmi's future: a clean bunk, dry socks, three square meals, and a letter with her name in it. Pell had ordered the socks personally. Details mattered. Kindness, applied strategically, was the sharpest tool in the kit, because it disarmed people far more thoroughly than fear ever could. Fear made them clutch what they had. Kindness made them wonder why they'd been clutching at all.

"Ferrow says the current's strengthening," Hesk reported. "Whatever's feeding this river, it's feeding harder tonight. And the barometer's falling fast. Monsoon's coming early—Survey Office bands put landfall inside four days, maybe three."

"Three days." Pell nodded slowly, doing the arithmetic he loved best: the arithmetic of closing windows. "Then we take her tonight, deliver the letter by morning, and give Zhou seventy-two hours to be reasonable. After that, the weather does our negotiating for us. Rain raises rivers, raised rivers flood gorges, flooded gorges close roads, and closed roads mean the only way out of this canyon is the way we say." He smiled up at the first fat drops beginning to tick against the deck, gentle as an opening bid. "You see, Hesk? I told you. We're not fighting the storm. We're standing where it lands, selling umbrellas."

"And if Zhou isn't reasonable?"

Pell watched the black water slide past, uphill, patient, going wherever it was going with or without anyone's permission, and for one uncharacteristic moment he envied it.

"Then," he said lightly, "we'll learn what he's actually made of. And I'll write it down." He tapped the ledger under his arm. "Everything goes in the book eventually, Hesk. That's not a threat. It's just the terms."

Above them, far up the gorge, a light appeared—small, warm, bobbing—moving steadily deeper into the canyon. Lamplight. Someone's lamp, someone's fire, someone's hope, traveling upstream into the dark.

Pell watched it go the way a lender watches a debtor walk away from the table: with perfect courtesy, perfect patience, and the serene certainty that no one, in the end, ever really leaves.

"Mr. Voss," Hesk said. "The boats are ready."

"Of course they are," said Pell Voss, and led his people into the dark.
