## 20. Ambush at the Gorge

**FREYA**

The gorge announced itself a full hour before they reached it, first as a change in the river's voice—a low, grinding mutter, like an old man reading a bill he disagreed with—and then as a wall of stone rising on both banks, red and layered and cut so clean it looked less carved by water than by argument.

"Side's too steep to land," Mara said, shading her eyes. "We'll have to run the narrows."

"I love it when you say *we'll*," Freya said. "Very communal. Very reassuring."

"You're the one with the boat."

"I'm the one with the *debt*," he corrected. "The boat is a shared burden now. Like a mortgage, or a cat."

She didn't laugh, but her mouth did something at the corner that he had learned, over these weeks of current and cold camps and one disastrous attempt at frying fish, to count as laughter. He had a private ledger of his own going—small entries, nothing the Optimists would recognize as currency. The way she said *starboard* like an insult. The way she checked his knots without being asked and then pretended she hadn't. The way she'd stopped saying *my father's chart* and started saying *the chart*, as if it had become theirs by some process neither of them had signed for.

He was thinking about that ledger, in fact, when the first stone came off the rim of the gorge.

It wasn't thrown. It was *dropped*—which was worse, because thrown stones are panic and dropped stones are patience—and it hit the water two meters off the bow with a slap that Freya felt in his back teeth.

"Mara," he said, very calmly. "How attached are you to the concept of arriving at the narrows?"

"Moderately."

"Then I'd hold on to something."

The second stone hit the gunwale. The third hit the mast step. And then the gorge rim bloomed with figures—six, eight, ten of them, in the Optimists' cheerful yellow slickers, which up close looked less like sunshine and more like the color of a wasp's intentions. Ropes snaked down. Grapnels, Freya saw with professional dismay. They'd brought *grapnels*. Pell had sent people who planned.

"*Freya Zhou!*" a voice called down, bright as a doorbell. "The Optimists extend their warmest regards and a formal request for your immediate docking!"

"Tell them I'm closed!" Freya shouted back, and threw the tiller hard over.

The boat swung toward the narrows. The narrows looked back at him like the inside of a bell. And then the first grapnel caught the stern rail with a sound like a tooth being pulled, and the planning began in earnest.

* * *

**MARA**

Mara had spent nine years learning to fight with paper—petitions, appeals, letters of inquiry that died in filing cabinets—and she had believed, in her more honest moments, that she had forgotten how to fight any other way. She learned otherwise somewhere between the third grapnel and the first collector's boots hitting the deck.

He came down the rope fast, a big man with a smile that had been issued to him by the same department as his slicker. She hit him with the boathook. Not elegantly. Not with any technique whatsoever. But with the full, accumulated fury of nine years of forms, and he went over the side with a splash that, she felt, the river appreciated.

"Mara!" Freya was wrestling the tiller against two ropes' worth of drag. "The chart—"

"No."

"They'll take it anyway—"

"Then they'll take it from your cold hands," she said, and swung at the next one.

But there were too many, and the boat was slowing, and the narrows were still fifty meters of screaming white ahead, and she understood, with a cold clarity that felt almost like her father's voice, that this was the arithmetic now: the chart or her. They would not take both. They did not need both. Pell's whole terrible genius was that he never asked for more than the ledger required.

The collector who grabbed her was smaller than the first, and quicker, and had clearly done this before. He pinned her arms with a practiced, almost apologetic efficiency. "Nothing personal," he said. "You seem very nice."

"She's *terrible*," Freya said. "You've made a terrible mistake. She bites."

"Freya." Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Don't."

He looked at her across the chaos of the deck—rope, rain, the yellow figures swarming the rails—and something moved through his face that she had seen once before, on the night he'd shown her the chart instead of running. He was, she understood, doing sums. And for once, for the first time since she'd known him, the sums were not about what he owed.

"The chart," he called out, loud enough for the rim of the gorge. "You want the chart, you let her go. Both feet on shore, all of you, before I let go of the tube. That's the price. Pell can invoice me."

"You're not in a position to set prices, Mr. Zhou."

"Neither are you," Freya said. "Because if she goes in that water tied up, I go in after her, and the chart goes with me, and Pell can explain to his ledger how he lost the source over a rope."

There was a silence, except for the river. Then the smaller collector, with the air of a man consulting an invisible abacus, nodded. The grip on Mara's arms loosened. She stepped back—free, furious, alive—and watched Freya hold the chart tube out over the black water like a man offering a hostage.

"Take it," he said.

They took it. A collector on the rim hauled the tube up on a line, and there was a moment of cheering, of yellow slickers slapping backs, and in that moment Mara saw Freya's face do something she had never seen it do.

He was *smiling*. Not the debt-collector smile he wore like a scar. A real one. Wide, unguarded, and—she would think about this for years—completely unafraid.

"What," she said, as the boat, suddenly light, began to gather way toward the narrows, "did you do."

* * *

**FREYA**

What he had done was very simple, and he had done it three nights ago in the orange light of a campfire, while Mara slept with her back to a warm stone and the river talked to itself about nothing.

He had redrawn the chart.

Not the whole thing. He was a surveyor, not a forger, and Tomás Ilesanmi's ink was beyond imitation. But a chart is a language, and languages have grammar, and Freya had spent twenty years reading the grammar of coastlines. The source's coordinates sat inside a system of reference marks—bearing lines, depth notations, the little crosses where Tomás had taken his sights. Freya had moved three of those crosses. Just slightly. Just enough. Any surveyor who followed the altered marks faithfully would find themselves at the head of a box canyon forty kilometers east, a place Freya had named, in his own private log, *Pell's Folly*, with a small sketch of a smiling face beneath it.

The real coordinates lived somewhere safer than ink. They lived in the one place the Optimists had never managed to repossess.

"You gave them the chart," Mara said. They were through the narrows now—the boat had gone through the white water like a coin through a slot, both of them drenched, both of them laughing in the ragged, post-terror way—and the gorge was falling away behind them into long green water. "You gave them the *chart*. Your debt. Your one thing of value. You just—"

"I gave them a drawing of a box canyon," Freya said.

The silence that followed had texture. He kept his eyes on the channel ahead, mostly.

"A *what*."

"Memorized the real ones years ago—well, weeks ago. The night you fell asleep before I did, which, for the record, was the only time. I copied the reference marks, shifted them, and redrew the crosses by lamplight. It took four hours and I have never in my life enjoyed a task more." He glanced at her. "The chart they're holding will lead Pell's surveyors to a canyon with no spring, no source, and, as of my last survey, one very smug goat."

"You *lied* to them."

"I *altered a document*, which I believe is a crime, and I did it *calmly and in advance*, which makes it premeditated, and I would do it again before breakfast." He heard his own voice climbing and made himself lower it. "Mara. I was always going to trade the chart for you. From the second they grabbed you, there was never any other arithmetic. The only question was whether I traded the real one or the fake one, and I want you to know—" and here it was, the thing he had not planned to say, arriving in his mouth like a bird through an open window, "—that I would have traded the real one too. I want you to know that. You should know what you're worth. You're worth the whole debt. You're worth the boat and the fingers and every other thing Pell keeps in his ledger."

The river said nothing. The gorge walls slid by, red and patient.

"That was a lot of words," Mara said finally.

"Yes."

"You rehearsed some of them."

"Only the goat part."

And then she laughed—really laughed, the first time he'd heard it, a sound like a lock coming open—and steered the boat toward a side channel he hadn't even seen yet, a thin green throat of water opening in the eastern bank, half-hidden by willow.

"Side channel," she said. "They'll expect us to run the main stem. My father's notes mention it—there's a portage at the head, and it rejoins below the gorge. It'll be shallow and miserable and full of insects."

"Sold," Freya said. "I've had enough of deep and pleasant."

They turned into the narrow water, and the gorge, and everyone in it, vanished behind the willows like a bad debt forgiven.

* * *

**MARA**

The side channel was everything promised: shallow, miserable, and full of insects with ambitions. They waded half of it, dragging the boat over gravel bars the color of old bone, and Mara found, to her surprise, that she was happy. Not the bright, brittle happiness of the petition granted, but something lower and warmer, banked like a coal.

He had traded the chart for her. He had stood on a swarming deck with ten collectors and a rope around his stern and traded his only asset, his whole miserable net worth, without hesitating—and then it turned out he hadn't traded it at all, because he'd already decided, days ago, quietly, in firelight, that she was worth more than the debt, and had arranged the world accordingly.

That was the part that kept catching in her chest. Not the sacrifice. The *preparation*. He had redrawn the crosses before there was any ambush, any ultimatum, any reason at all—except that some part of him had apparently already understood what she was to him, and had gotten to work.

"Freya," she said, as they hauled the boat over the last bar, "the coordinates. The real ones. Where exactly do they live?"

He patted his own forehead. "Between the smug goat and a complete inventory of everything I owe. It's very crowded in there."

"And if you drown? If you hit your head on a bar like this one and forget?"

"Then the source is safe forever," he said, "and you'll have to introduce yourself to me again, which, given how well our first meeting went, might not be a tragedy."

"Our first meeting involved me accusing you of grave robbery in front of the entire harbor."

"See? Great material. I'd hate to lose it." He grinned, and then, because he was Freya, and because the light was going gold on the water, he got quieter. "It's not just memory, though. I wrote them down. Encoded. In the back of my logbook, under the fuel tallies. My father taught me—no, sorry, that's not right, my *survey instructor* taught me—"

He stopped. Mara watched him stand in the shin-deep water with his hand still on the gunwale, and watched something cross his face that she recognized, because she had seen it in the antechambers and the carvings and her own mirror: the small, ongoing earthquake of a man remembering his dead.

"My father taught me ciphers," Freya said, correcting himself gently, deliberately, the way you'd set a broken bone. "He was a coast pilot. He used to say a number written plainly is a number anyone can steal, but a number written as a *story*—that's a number only family can read." He looked at her. "I never thought of myself as having family to write to. Turns out I was wrong about that too."

The insects sang. The water moved. Mara Ilesanmi, who had spent nine years learning the exact weight of words like *deceased* and *case closed* and *no further action*, found that she had nothing formal to offer, and so offered the true thing instead.

"I would have traded the chart for you," she said. "Back at the gorge. If it had been reversed. If they'd held *you*." She looked at the water rather than at him, because some things are easier said to rivers. "I want that on the record. I'm a surveyor's daughter. We keep records."

The silence went on long enough that she finally made herself look up.

Freya was looking at her with an expression she had no chart for—no bearing lines, no depth notations, no little crosses. Just open water, all the way down.

"Noted," he said, "and entered into the log," and his voice was not entirely steady, and neither, she noticed, were his hands, and she thought: *there it is. The word we're both not saying. It's standing right here between us in the shallows, getting its boots wet.*

Neither of them said it. Neither of them needed to. It climbed into the boat with them when they pushed off, settled itself on the thwart between them like a third passenger, and rode downstream toward the monsoon with the air of someone who intended to stay.

* * *

**FREYA**

They made camp that night on a gravel shelf above the channel, under a sky going slowly silver with early stars. The monsoon was somewhere out on the coast now—you could feel it the way you feel weather coming in a tooth, a pressure behind the world—and Freya lay with his hands behind his head and did the new arithmetic, the honest kind.

He owed the Optimists everything. He now possessed, officially, nothing: no chart, no collateral, no source to sell. Pell would figure out the forgery within days—within hours, once his surveyors reached the goat—and when he did, he would not send stones. He would send the ledger itself.

And Freya found, testing the fear the way you test a knot, that it did not frighten him. Something had come loose in the gorge, some old mooring line, and what remained was strangely buoyant.

"Mara," he said into the dark. "When this is over. When the source is dealt with and the monsoon's passed and we're whatever we're going to be." A pause. "There's a cove south of Cape Verren. Good holding ground. I've anchored there maybe forty times and never once had anyone to show it to."

In the dark, her hand found his, with the confidence of someone checking a knot.

"Show me," she said.

He was still awake when the first far thunder came up the gorge, rolling toward them from the coast like a drumbeat before a door. He counted the seconds between flash and sound out of old habit, and the number was small, and getting smaller.

Three days, maybe four. And somewhere behind them, a yellow slicker was about to open a chart tube and find a goat.

Freya smiled at the stars, and slept.
