## 21. Monsoon Watch

The satellite feed came in at 0400, and Cass Okonjo was already awake, because she had been awake for three days, sleeping the way a sentry sleeps—one ear open, one hand near her sidearm, her dreams full of paperwork that screamed.

She stood in the Survey Office field station at Port Anselm, a converted lighthouse keeper's cottage with a dish bolted to the roof at an angle that offended her sense of engineering, and watched the storm system crawl across the screen like something alive. Which, in fairness, it was.

The monsoon had turned.

Every model she'd been issued said landfall in nine days. The models were elegant, peer-reviewed, and wrong. The storm had found warm water nobody had charted and leaned into it like a runner finding a second wind. The new track put it ashore in four days. Maybe three and a half, if it stayed greedy.

Cass did the arithmetic the way she did everything: twice, silently, without flinching.

Four days to reach the source. Two days' travel upriver, minimum, if the river behaved—and the river, by every report she'd read and none she believed, did not behave. That left perhaps a day to place the demolition charges, rig the sequence, and withdraw before the gorge flooded with them inside it.

Her orders assumed nine days. Her orders had been written by a man who signed things in an office with air conditioning and had never once, as far as she could tell, been rained on.

"Understaffed," she said to the empty room, "underwater, and under-informed."

The room agreed with her by saying nothing.

* * *

She spent the morning doing what she'd always done when the numbers stopped adding up: she worked the problem from the ground up. She checked the charges—twelve shaped demolitions, enough to bring the canyon walls down and bury whatever spring-fed miracle lived at the head of the river under a hundred tons of rock. She checked the detonators. She checked the waterproof casing, because the Survey Office's idea of waterproofing was a coat of wax and prayer.

Then she sat down with Tomás Ilesanmi's sealed file for the fourth time, because it had become a habit she couldn't break, the way some people bite their nails.

The file was thin. That was the first thing wrong with it. A senior cartographer, twenty-two years of service, missing presumed dead for nine years, and his entire personnel record fit in a folder lighter than a menu. Cass had handled files for clerks who'd embezzled staplers with more pages than this.

But thin wasn't empty. Thin was curated.

Page six held the transfer order that had sent Tomás upriver alone. No support team. No extraction window. A solo survey of a hydrological anomaly that the Office's own preliminary reports called "significant beyond classification." She'd read those words so many times they'd worn grooves in her. Significant beyond classification. In bureaucrat, that meant *we know what this is worth and we are not writing it down.*

Page seven held the authorization signature. Deputy Director Emmerich Voss. The same name, she'd confirmed last week with a records request that had taken her four months and two favors, that appeared on the demolition order now sitting in her kit. The same hand that had sent an old man up a river alone had, nine years later, ordered the river sealed behind him.

Cass laid the two documents side by side on the table and looked at them for a long time.

There was a version of this where it all made sense. Where Voss was a careful man managing a dangerous secret, where Tomás Ilesanmi had been a necessary sacrifice in a war against knowledge that could unmake people, where the charges in her kit were mercy rather than erasure. She had built that version herself, brick by brick, over years of service, because the alternative was unbearable.

The alternative was that she was the necessary sacrifice this time. That the convoy behind her—the twelve soldiers, the engineers, the man from the Ministry who kept asking about "asset recovery timelines"—was not there to seal a legend but to secure a patent. That when the dust settled over the buried canyon, the only report filed would be hers, and it would say whatever Voss needed it to say, and she would be either complicit or dead, and history would be written by whichever of them survived the paperwork.

She thought about Freya Zhou's face when she'd warned him off the river. The man had looked at her like she was weather—not a threat, just a condition he'd have to plan around. She'd resented that at the time. Now she recognized it. It was the look of someone who had already learned what she was only now learning: that institutions don't protect you. They invoice you.

* * *

At noon, the man from the Ministry found her on the seawall, eating lunch she didn't taste.

"Deputy Director Voss sends his regards," he said. He was young, polished, and carried his briefcase like a weapon he hadn't been cleared to fire. "He wanted me to confirm your timeline. The operation proceeds on schedule?"

"The schedule has changed," Cass said. "Landfall moved up. Four days, possibly less."

The young man smiled the smile of someone whose job description did not include believing field officers. "The Director anticipated scheduling pressures. He asks whether you anticipate any... complications requiring additional resources."

What he meant, translated out of Ministry, was: *are you going to be a problem?*

"No complications," Cass said.

"Wonderful." He produced a tablet. "If you'll just confirm receipt of amended operational parameters."

She took the stylus. She signed. Her hand didn't shake, which surprised her; she'd expected it to feel like something. Instead it felt like page seven. Like a transfer order sending someone upriver alone.

The young man left, satisfied, and Cass stood on the seawall watching the horizon, where the sky over the open water had begun to organize itself into long gray ranks, patient as creditors.

* * *

That evening she did something she hadn't done since her oath day. She took the orders out of their case and read them aloud, alone, in her quarters, word by word, listening for the part where they became monstrous.

They were beautifully written. That was the horror of them. *Recover all documentation pertaining to the Verren anomaly. Deny access to unauthorized parties. Render the site permanently inaccessible.* Not a word about the people upriver. Not a word about the cartographer's daughter, whom the Office had turned away eleven times while her father drowned by inches in a cave. Not a word about what *render permanently inaccessible* would mean, executed four days early, with a flash flood coming down a canyon that had exactly one exit.

*Deny access to unauthorized parties.* If she followed these orders literally, she was authorized to deny access with gunfire. The convoy behind her certainly would.

Cass sat with the papers in her lap for a long time. Outside, the first outriders of the monsoon tapped at the window like polite but insistent visitors.

She thought about her oath. She had memorized it at nineteen, standing in a hall in the capital with two hundred other recruits, and she had meant every word: *to serve the provision of the common good, to hold the map honest, to let no knowledge be hoarded nor hidden from those whose lives depend upon it.*

*To let no knowledge be hoarded nor hidden.*

She'd recited that line a thousand times without hearing it. The Office had spent nine years hiding the source—from Mara Ilesanmi, from the coastal towns, from everyone—while planning, eventually, to sell it. And now it planned to hide it forever, under rock, with witnesses inside.

Her oath didn't say *obey the Deputy Director.* Her oath said *hold the map honest.*

The orders in her lap were a lie drawn on official paper.

"Well," Cass said quietly, to the room, to the rain, to a dead cartographer she'd never met. "There it is."

* * *

She burned them at midnight, in the iron stove in the corner of her quarters, because she was a thorough woman even in treason.

It took longer than she expected. Official paper is dense stuff, treated against damp, and the amended parameters fought her, curling and blackening by degrees, Voss's signature glowing orange for a moment before it folded into ash. Cass fed the pages in one at a time and watched each one go, and with each one she felt—not lighter, exactly. Nothing so clean as lighter. She felt *specific*. For nine years she had been a general instrument of general policy, and now, standing at a stove in her socks feeding her career into the fire, she was suddenly, precisely, one person making one choice.

It was the most free she had felt since enlistment. It was also, she noted professionally, extremely illegal.

She kept three things. Her sidearm, because the world hadn't improved. Her badge, because she might still need to lie convincingly, and lying was easier in uniform. And the demolition charges—because here was the thing she had decided on the seawall, the decision that had made the burning inevitable:

The source should never belong to anyone. Not to Voss, not to the Optimists, not to the Survey Office, and not to her. But *sealed* and *destroyed* were different words, and Voss had never learned the difference. Sealed could mean protected. Sealed could mean the kind of secret that stays wild—known to no ledger, owned by no ministry, guarded by nothing but distance and difficulty and the sea's own bad temper.

Or it could mean flooded, collapsed, gone. She wouldn't know until she got there. Until she saw it. Until she met the people Voss wanted buried with it and heard what they had to say.

So: the charges came with her. As leverage, maybe. As a choice, definitely. A warden who arrived unarmed was just another pilgrim. A warden who arrived armed with twelve shaped demolitions and a conscience was a negotiation.

She packed by lamplight, fast and spare. Then she sat down at the little desk and wrote two documents.

The first was brief and would be found on her desk by the convoy's liaison in the morning: a formal request for delayed departure pending weather assessment, countersigned by nobody, witnessed by nobody, addressed to an office that would never answer it. It bought her perhaps half a day of confusion. Not more. She was too good at her job for her absence to go unnoticed, which was, she reflected, the cruelest joke of her career: competence had made her trustworthy, and trustworthiness had made her indispensable, and indispensability had made her the perfect person to send up a river to do an unforgivable thing. Nobody watched the reliable ones.

The second document was longer. She wrote it in her own hand, in ink that wouldn't run, on paper she'd bought herself with her own money so that no Office stamp could claim it. It was an account. Everything. Page six, page seven, Voss's two signatures nine years apart, the convoy's real cargo manifest, the Ministry man's questions about asset recovery. She named names. She dated it. At the bottom she wrote: *If I do not return, deliver this to the Harbor Registry at Salt Hollow and to the family of Tomás Ilesanmi.*

She sealed it and addressed it to the one person in the port she trusted, a retired ferry clerk named Odile who owed her a favor from a flood season neither of them talked about, and who had the incorruptible temperament of a woman who had spent forty years taking tickets and giving change and had seen every variety of human nonsense and been impressed by none of it.

Odile would deliver it or die trying. Probably die complaining.

* * *

She left at 0300, ahead of the storm, ahead of her own convoy, running dark up the coast road in a requisitioned skiff with the charges lashed under tarpaulin and the rain beginning in earnest—fat, warm drops that hit the water like fingers drumming a table, impatient for what was coming.

Halfway up the coast, she passed the mouth of the river. Even in the dark she could see it was wrong—she'd known it was wrong since the charts, but seeing was different. The current ran inland, steady and strong, against the tide, against sense, against everything she had spent twenty years enforcing. The water went up into the country like a rumor traveling upstream, carrying whatever it carried toward the center of the map.

Somewhere up that current were a debt-ridden salvage pilot and a cartographer's daughter who had every reason to hate the uniform Cass was still wearing. They had no reason to trust her. By every reasonable measure, she was their enemy: the law, arriving late, armed.

Cass throttled down and let the skiff drift a moment, listening to the rain thicken.

She had spent her whole life believing that duty was a thing you were given—that it came down from above, signed and sealed, and your whole job was to carry it without dropping it. Standing in the mouth of a backward river at three in the morning, a traitor with a boatload of explosives and a letter to a ferry clerk, she understood at last that she'd had it backwards. Duty wasn't handed down. It was chosen, moment by moment, out loud, in the dark, usually at cost. Everything else was just obedience, and obedience was what Voss counted on.

She opened the throttle and turned upriver.

Behind her, far to the south, the sky over the horizon had begun to glow a bruised, electrical green—the monsoon gathering itself, four days out and closing, patient as a debt.

Ahead of her, somewhere in the dark, the river ran backward toward a spring that remembered everything.

And Cass Okonjo, warden, traitor, keeper of small truths, went to find out which of them she was.
