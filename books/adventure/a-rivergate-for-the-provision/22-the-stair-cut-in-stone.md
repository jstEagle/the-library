## 22. The Stair Cut in Stone

The river narrowed the way a fist does—slowly at first, then all at once.

For two days we'd been climbing without climbing. That was the thing about the backward current that Freya had stopped trying to explain to himself: the water ran inland, uphill in every way that mattered, and his patched old boat *Provision* rode it like a leaf riding a held breath. The banks had gone from mud to gravel to stone. The trees had thinned from mangrove to pine to nothing at all, just lichen-crusted rock and a sky so wide it felt like an accusation.

Mara stood at the bow with her father's chart unrolled across her forearms, though by now she barely needed it. "Canyon," she said. "Tomás drew it as a dotted line. He wasn't sure it existed."

"Your father drew a river that flows the wrong direction," Freya said, working the tiller against a current that pushed like a living hand. "I'd say his confidence was already well established."

"He dotted things he was afraid of."

Freya considered that. He thought about all the things he'd drawn solid lines around in his own life—the storm cells he'd chased, the debts he'd signed, the crew he'd sworn he could outrun—and how many of those lines had been fear wearing the costume of certainty.

"Then we should be flattered," he said. "We're inside a dotted line."

* * *

The canyon walls came together overhead until there was more stone than sky, and the light went the color of weak tea. The river squeezed between walls close enough that Freya could have touched both at once if he'd stretched, and the sound changed—the water's murmur deepening into something with a pulse to it, a slow rhythmic breathing, as if the canyon itself were asleep and dreaming of the sea.

And then they heard the falls.

"Waterfalls run down," Mara said. It wasn't a question. It was a woman checking her sanity against a witness.

"Generally, yes."

"Freya."

"I see it."

He saw it. Ahead, where the canyon bent left, a curtain of water rose. Not spilled upward, not thrown by wind—*rose*, steadily, the way smoke rises, the way a prayer is supposed to rise, a broad silver sheet climbing the cliff face in perfect silence except for a low hum that Freya felt in his back teeth. It went up perhaps sixty feet, gathered itself into a kind of trembling crown, and then—this was the part his eyes kept refusing—dissolved into mist that drifted back down and rejoined the river, which turned around and climbed again. A wheel with no axle. An argument the water was having with the world, and winning.

"Provision" drifted to a stop on her own. The current beneath them had gone slack, almost courteous, as if the river were pausing to let them look.

"Well," Freya said finally. "That's going to be difficult to put on an invoice."

Mara laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly bitten off, but real. Nine days on the river had taught him her laughs the way he'd once taught himself cloud formations: rare, meaningful, worth dropping everything for.

"Is this where you tell me you're turning back?" she asked.

"Is this where you tell me you'd let me?"

"No."

"There you have it, then." He swung the tiller over. "Besides, I've come too far to be defeated by plumbing."

* * *

They found the stair on the right-hand wall, and Freya understood immediately why Tomás had dotted it.

It began at the waterline: a single step cut into the living stone, then another above it, then another, ascending beside the rising falls in a long diagonal line that vanished into the mist-crown and beyond, up into shadow. Each step was wide enough for two feet and no more, worn smooth in their centers by what Freya could only estimate was millennia of desperate feet. There was no railing. There was no path at the top that he could see. There was only the stair, the falls humming beside it, and the dark above.

"Somebody built this," Mara said quietly. She had her hand flat against the first step, reading it like braille. "Somebody stood here with tools and decided people would need to climb."

"People who couldn't swim, maybe."

"People who were running from something."

"That too." Freya looked up the full height of it and felt his stomach perform a slow, dignified revolt. He was forty-one years old, he owned one boat with a name that had become a private joke between him and his creditors, and he was about to climb a staircase carved by unknown hands beside a waterfall that violated at least three laws he'd learned before he was twelve. "You know what my grandmother used to say? 'Never trust a door that opens away from you.'"

"This isn't a door."

"It's a door, Mara. Everything past this point is a door."

* * *

They moored Provision in a shallow alcove below the first step, and it was while securing the line that Freya noticed the carvings.

They started small—grooves in the stone near the waterline that he took at first for erosion, for the idle work of water and time. But when he crouched and angled his lamp, the grooves resolved. Figures. Tiny human figures, no bigger than his thumb, cut in procession along the base of the wall: a line of travelers climbing toward the stair, each rendered with startling economy—a stroke for a spine, a dot for a head—and each carrying something.

He traced the nearest figure with one finger. It carried a shape on its back, round-shouldered, heavy-looking. A burden. And behind it, cut deeper into the stone, fresher or perhaps simply more insistent, was a second figure: identical posture, but empty-handed, arms loose, head tilted upward toward the falls.

"They're carrying something up," Mara said. She had come to stand beside him, her shoulder nearly touching his. "Look—they all start with the load. And here—" she pointed further along the procession, where the figures climbed higher, passing a carved band that might have been the falls themselves—"here they've set it down. Or lost it. Or given it away."

"And the ones above the falls are lighter." Freya moved the lamp. It was true. The upper figures stood straighter. The carver had even changed the angle of their heads—downcast below, lifted above. Whoever had cut these images had understood something about weight, and about what it cost, and about what waited on the other side of surrender.

At the very top of the frieze, where the stone curved out of lamplight, there was one final carving, larger than the rest. Freya stretched to reach it, boots slipping on wet rock, and got his lamp under it at an angle.

A pair of open hands. Palms up. Empty.

Below them, in characters older than any alphabet Freya knew—older than the Survey Office, older than Salt Hollow, older possibly than the concept of debt—a single line of script he could not read but whose meaning arrived in him anyway, whole, the way you know the meaning of a face.

*Leave it here. Climb lighter.*

"Tomás must have seen this," Mara said. Her voice had gone strange—careful, the voice of someone walking on ice over deep water. "His annotations. The second layer. He wrote about a toll."

"He wrote about a toll," Freya agreed. At the ferry crossing, days ago, an ancient woman had taken one of Mara's childhood memories as passage—one of Mara's mother's face, gone now, traded for a few miles of river. Freya had watched her grieve it and had privately, shamefully, thanked whatever gods governed rivers that the toll hadn't been his to pay. His worst memories were load-bearing. Take them out and he wasn't sure what shape remained.

"You're thinking about the ferrywoman," Mara said.

"I'm thinking that everything on this river charges admission, and the prices keep getting more personal."

"Would you pay it?" She turned to look at him directly, and in the lamplight her eyes were steady and terribly serious. "If the stair asked. Your worst memory. The storm. Would you give it up?"

Freya opened his mouth to say something clever and found, to his surprise, that he didn't have any. He looked at the little carved figures instead—at the ones below the falls, bent double under their invisible loads, and the ones above, standing straight, faces lifted.

"I used to think that memory was the only honest thing I owned," he said slowly. "Everything else got repossessed. The boat twice, the house once, my survey license for eighteen months. But the storm—that stayed mine. Even when it was killing me, it was mine." He rubbed the back of his neck. "And then your father's spring showed me—I mean, we haven't seen it yet, but the villagers did, and Cass's reports, and—" he waved a hand at the impossible waterfall, at the whole upside-down architecture of the world they'd entered—"the source doesn't just hold memory, Mara. It holds it perfectly. Every grievance. Every guilt. Forever, in high definition, with no mercy of forgetting."

"The village," she said. "Where nobody could bury anything."

"Right. So maybe the question isn't whether I'd trade the storm away." He looked up the stair again, at the dark where the steps disappeared. "Maybe the question is whether I'd trust whoever's collecting to take only what I can spare. Because I don't know what I can spare. I thought I knew what I owed everyone. Turns out I was wrong about that too."

Mara was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "My father climbed this."

It wasn't a question either, but Freya answered it anyway. "Yeah. I think he did."

"And he carried something up, and he set it down, and he came back down with the chart and the mechanism and thirty years of silence." Her jaw tightened. "He gave up his worst memory and it still killed him alone in a cave."

"Or," Freya said gently, "he gave it up and it bought him enough clarity to spend his last decades building a way to protect the thing he loved. We don't know what it cost him. We only know what it made."

She didn't answer. But after a moment she reached out and took the lamp from his hand, and started up the first step.

* * *

The stair was worse than it looked, which Freya had not previously believed possible.

Each step was knee-high and slick with spray from the falls, and the hum of the rising water grew louder with every meter until it wasn't a sound anymore but a pressure, a hand laid flat against his chest. The mist-crown passed beside them at head height—cold, tasting faintly of iron and, absurdly, of rain on hot stone, of the first monsoon of some childhood summer he couldn't place. Climbing through a waterfall that fell upward meant climbing through weather that had already happened somewhere else. He kept catching fragments in the spray: a smell of salt and diesel that belonged to Cape Verren; a snatch of laughter that sounded like his dead crewmate Okafor; the exact temperature of the air in the bar where Pell had smiled at him over a contract.

The falls were showing him things. Small things, discarded things, the sediment of other people's climbs.

"Don't drink it," Mara called down, reading his face. "The ferrywoman's rule. Nothing goes in that isn't yours."

"Wasn't planning on it. My mouth is firmly shut." Though he noticed, and pretended not to notice, that he'd begun climbing faster whenever the spray offered him something sweet.

Halfway up, they found the offerings.

They sat in niches cut into the wall beside the steps—dozens of niches, hundreds, each holding its small surrendered cargo. A rusted wedding band. A child's shoe, leather gone stiff as bark. A folded letter sealed with wax, addressed to no one. A soldier's medal, ribbon rotted to thread. A photograph curled into a tube, face turned to the wall. A tooth. A lock of hair gone white. A brass button, a broken compass, a doll with one eye.

People had been leaving their worst memories here for longer than nations had existed, and the niches held them the way a reliquary holds bones—not displayed, exactly. Kept. Tended. Someone, once, had cared enough about other people's grief to build it shelves.

Mara stopped at one niche and went very still. Freya climbed the last few steps to join her and saw why.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone green with age, lay a surveyor's instrument case. Brass corners. The Survey Office stamp, half worn away. And scratched into the leather flap, in a hand Freya recognized now from the chart's annotations—tight, precise, the handwriting of a man who measured everything because feeling frightened him:

*T. ILESANMI — PAID IN FULL.*

Mara's breath came out ragged. She didn't touch it. Her hands hovered over the case the way you hover over a sleeping animal you're afraid of waking.

"He paid the toll," she whispered. "He actually—he climbed this, and he paid, and he marked it like a ledger entry. Like closing an account." A sound escaped her that was half laugh, half sob. "He was an accountant of his own suffering. Of course he was. Of course."

"Mara—"

"He never told me. Nine years I spent hating the Survey Office for losing him, hating the sea, hating anyone who said he was probably dead, and the whole time he was up here settling his accounts." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "What could a man like him have carried that was heavy enough to leave at a magic waterfall?"

Freya looked at the case. At the stamp. At the words *paid in full*, and thought about ledgers, and Optimists, and interest compounded on grief, and a man who had found the one spring on earth where nothing is ever forgotten and had chosen, deliberately, to forget one thing anyway—to buy his way up a stair with his own worst night.

Whatever Tomás had surrendered here, it had been heavy enough to bend his whole life around the absence.

"Maybe we'll see it," Freya said. "At the source. If the water holds everyone who ever drank from it—"

"Then it holds what he gave up." She straightened. Wiped her face with the brisk efficiency of someone who has decided crying is a scheduled activity and the schedule has closed. "Then we'll know what he couldn't carry."

"Yeah."

"Good." She said it like a woman loading a weapon. Then, softer, with the ghost of the laugh from the boat: "Also, if my father's worst memory turns out to be me as a teenager, I'm going to be devastated, so prepare your condolences in advance."

"I'll draft something appropriate. 'She was a bright girl with strong opinions about navigation.'"

"She once redrew half his coastal charts because she thought his depth soundings were lazy."

"Were they?"

"*Yes.*" And she laughed properly this time, wet and surprised, and the sound went up the stair ahead of them like a lantern.

* * *

Near the top, the niches ran out, and the last stretch of stair climbed through the mist-crown itself, cold and blind and roaring silently, and then—

Sky.

They came up out of the canyon's throat onto a shelf of pale stone, and the world opened. Above them the walls fell away on both sides, and the river they'd followed lay far below, silver and obedient again, flowing uphill toward the falls that fed it from the sky. And ahead—upstream, inward, deeper into the dotted lines of Tomás's chart—the canyon continued, but changed. The stone here was worked. Terraced. Carved with the same processions of figures they'd seen below, but larger now, life-sized, whole galleries of climbing travelers cut into the walls on either side, generations of them, ascending forever toward something the carvers had left deliberately blank: a smooth, unmarked wall at the canyon's end, high and clean, waiting.

Waiting to be carved, Freya realized. Or waiting to be understood. With ancient stonework, it was hard to say which.

The light was doing something strange up here—coming from everywhere and nowhere, gold without a sun, the particular illumination of a place that had opted out of ordinary geography. The air smelled of rain and iron and, underneath it, faintly, of the sea. Which was impossible. They were a hundred leagues inland and climbing.

"Pilgrims built this," Mara said. She was turning slowly, taking in the terraces, the galleries, the blank wall. "Not prisoners. Not an army. Pilgrims. You don't carve ten thousand grateful faces unless people arrived here terrified and left here lighter."

"Terror becoming pilgrimage," Freya murmured. He'd read the phrase somewhere, in some Survey Office primer on coastal folklore, filed between tide tables and superstitions to be ignored. He doubted the author had ever imagined standing inside it.

He looked back the way they'd come. Far below, tiny as a toy, Provision sat moored in her alcove, and beyond her the canyon ran back down toward the ordinary world—toward Pell and his collectors, toward Cass Okonjo and whatever orders she'd chosen to burn or obey, toward the monsoon gathering itself off the coast like a debt coming due. Four days, Cass had said. Maybe less.

Somewhere behind them, down that long stone throat, he heard—or thought he heard—an engine. Faint. Patient. The sound of a man who balanced people like weather fronts, closing the distance at a courteous speed.

Mara heard it too. Her head came up. "How far behind us do you think they are?"

"A day," Freya said. "Maybe less. Pell travels like optimism spreads—faster than you'd credit and always downhill." He turned back to the blank wall, to the terraces climbing toward the source of everything, and felt the strangest sensation settle over him: not dread, exactly, and not peace, but the specific steadiness of a man who has finally stopped pretending he has a choice.

"Then we don't stop," Mara said.

"No," Freya agreed, and shouldered the pack, and started up the last terrace toward the blank wall that ten thousand pilgrims had climbed to reach—and that his partner's father had died trying to seal. "We knock."

Behind them, far down the canyon, the engine sound grew one degree louder, patient as compound interest.
