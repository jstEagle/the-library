## 23. Mara's Reckoning

The antechamber announced itself before we saw it—a sound like a choir humming through wet stone, low enough to feel in the teeth rather than hear in the ears. The stair cut into the canyon wall had ended at a landing no wider than a kitchen table, and from there the river's upward-falling spray had soaked us to the skin for the last hour of climbing. Freya went first, lamp held high, and I followed close enough that his elbow kept bumping my forehead like an apology on repeat.

"Sorry," he said, for the eleventh time.

"You could walk slower."

"If I walk slower I'll think about how high up we are, and then I'll stop walking entirely, and then you'll have to drag me, and you've already carried enough of me this trip." He said it lightly, but it wasn't a joke, not really. Since the rapids—since he'd gone under and come up weeping without knowing why—he'd been different. Softer at the joints. A man whose scaffolding had been quietly removed.

The antechamber opened off the landing like a breath held for nine thousand years.

It was round, maybe forty feet across, and the ceiling rose into darkness the lamp couldn't reach. But it was the walls that stopped us both mid-step.

They were carved. Not decorated—*carved*, every inch of them, in bands of figures that spiraled up and around like the growth rings of some impossible tree. Travelers. Rivers. Boats. People kneeling. People walking away. People holding out their hands as though giving something over or receiving something precious, and in the lamplight the shadows moved between the figures so that they seemed almost to breathe.

And then the walls began to show us things that weren't carved at all.

I don't know how else to describe it. The stone stayed stone. But light gathered in the grooves of the carving—not reflected, *gathered*, the way water gathers in a cupped palm—and images formed there, moving, living. My father's handwriting. I knew it instantly, the way you know your own name shouted across a crowded street.

"Mara," Freya said quietly. "Mara, look at—"

"I see it."

My father was younger in the image than I'd ever known him. His hair still black, his beard trimmed with the fussy precision he'd used when he wanted the Survey Office to take him seriously. He stood at a drafting table in a room I didn't recognize, and he was arguing with someone just outside the frame. I couldn't hear the words—the visions came silent, like a film with the sound torn out—but I knew the set of his shoulders. I'd seen it across dinner tables my whole childhood. It was the posture of a man who has decided something and is prepared to be difficult about it.

"He found it," Freya said. "Years ago. Before—" He stopped himself. Before the cave. Before the tide took him. Before I spent nine years writing letters to an office that never answered.

"Yes," I said. "He found it years ago."

The vision shifted. My father, older now, gray threading through the black, standing in front of a desk so large it looked like furniture designed to intimidate rather than work upon. Behind the desk sat a man whose face I knew from the Survey Office lobby—a portrait, gilt-framed, of Director Emmerich Voss. In the portrait he wore the expression of a benefactor. In the vision, alive and moving, he wore the expression of a man being told *no* by someone who could not afford to say it.

My father put something on the desk. A chart—I knew its shape, its fold-pattern, the way it creased along lines worn soft by handling. The same chart that had ridden against Freya's chest for weeks, wrapped in oilcloth, warmer than any document had a right to be.

Voss reached for it. My father picked it back up.

Even silent, even rendered in light and shadow on a wall of ancient stone, I heard the refusal. Some things don't need sound.

* * *

We sat down on the cold floor, because our legs gave out roughly in unison, and the walls kept showing us everything.

There was my father refusing money—actual coin, counted out onto the table by hands wearing rings I recognized from Survey Office functions where I'd eaten tiny sandwiches and been told what a promising career my father had. There was my father refusing a promotion, sliding the letter back across a desk with two fingers, the way you return something spoiled. There was my father standing in the rain outside the Office headquarters, chart inside his coat, watching the windows above him where men decided the fates of coastlines.

And there—this one I had to look away from, and then made myself look back—there was my father packing a boat.

Alone. No crew. Every colleague he approached in the preceding visions had turned away, and I understood why: Voss hadn't needed to threaten Tomás Ilesanmi directly. He'd only needed to make sure that anyone who helped him would find their careers ending like candles pinched between wet fingers. So my father had loaded survey gear and provisions and a lifetime of stubbornness into a boat barely bigger than Freya's, and he'd sailed north alone, toward Cape Verren, toward the cave the locals charged admission to, toward the source he'd sworn to protect by making sure no one else could ever reach it.

"Why alone?" Freya murmured. "Why not tell someone? Why not tell *you*?"

The walls answered before I could.

A final vision, smaller than the others, tucked into a corner of the spiral near the floor, easy to miss. My father at a table in some port tavern, writing a letter by lamplight. The camera of the stone—whatever patient intelligence arranged these images—drifted close enough that I could read the words forming under his pen.

*I cannot tell my daughter. She would come. She is her mother's daughter and she would come, and this place does not forgive rescue. If I fail, let her believe I abandoned her. Let her hate me. Hatred keeps a person moving. Grief would make her sit down by the sea and wait, and she must never wait by this sea.*

I stopped breathing.

Nine years. Nine years of hating him—not constantly, not cleanly, but in the way you hate a toothache, always somewhere in the background, flaring when you let yourself think about it. Nine years of *he chose the map over me*. Nine years of composing letters to the Survey Office with hands that shook, and receiving silence, and letting that silence confirm the worst version of the story.

And the truth was that he had chosen me. He had chosen me so completely that he'd chosen to be hated by me, because he'd done the arithmetic of love and concluded that my hatred would keep me safer than my hope.

"That absolute idiot," I said, and my voice cracked in the middle of it, and then I was crying.

Not the way I'd cried before—at his memorial-that-wasn't, at funerals for other people that I attended and borrowed grief from, in the dark of my rented room in Salt Hollow after reading another form letter from the Records Division. Those cries had been tidy. Controlled. The crying of a woman keeping one eye on herself at all times, making sure the feeling stayed within approved dimensions.

This was not that. This was the ugly, honking, graceless crying of a thirty-one-year-old woman sitting on a stone floor in a canyon at the top of the world, sobbing *he didn't leave me, he didn't leave me, he thought he was saving me,* while Freya Zhou—debt-ridden, storm-chasing, emotionally laminated Freya Zhou—put his arm around my shoulders and held on like a man securing cargo in heavy weather.

"I've got you," he said, which was absurd, because I was the one holding him together half the time, and I laughed through it, a horrible wet sound, and he laughed too, and somehow that was right. Grief and laughter have always lived next door to each other. They share a fence with a hole in it.

* * *

The walls weren't finished with us.

While I cried, the spiral of carvings continued its slow rotation of images, and I saw my father's last days. Not imagined this time—shown. The cave. The tide. He'd mapped the passage carefully, left himself notes, planned his exit with the thoroughness of a man who'd survived forty years of fieldwork by respecting water. And then something had gone wrong. A rockfall, maybe, or a miscalculation—the vision showed him hauling himself up the ribbed throat of the cave as water poured in below, faster than his charts had promised, faster than anything should move.

He'd made it to a ledge. The one where Freya had found him. And there he'd waited, because there was nothing else to do, and the sea came for him twice a day and retreated twice a day, and each time it retreated a little less far.

But here is what undid me all over again: in the visions of those final days, my father did not despair. He worked. Weak, trapped, drowning by inches twice daily—and he worked. He annotated the chart. He hid the second layer beneath the ink, the coordinates and the mechanism, the flood-gates he'd apparently spent years preparing in secret, planning for a day when someone else might need to finish what he'd started. He wrote warnings in margins. He wrapped the chart in oilcloth and waterproofed it with wax and care.

And once—in the last vision, the smallest one, pressed into the stone near the antechamber's entrance like a signature—he looked up at the cave ceiling, at the dark water rising, and he smiled.

He smiled because, the image seemed to say, he'd finally finished it. The work was complete. Whatever happened next, the source would have its guardian's testament, hidden in plain ink, waiting for eyes desperate or stubborn enough to look closely.

"He knew someone would come," I whispered.

"The cave charges admission," Freya said softly. "His words were: only the desperate get in. He bet on desperation. He bet that someday, someone with nothing left to lose would walk in and find everything." He paused. "That's a hell of a wager. That's the kind of wager only a man makes who's decided the world is fundamentally worth trusting."

"My father trusted the world?"

"Your father trusted *people*. There's a difference. The world he seems to have regarded as a large wet animal of uncertain temper." Freya's mouth twitched. "Accurate assessment, honestly."

I laughed again—really laughed, this time, which felt like a small miracle performed on the ruins of the last nine years—and then the tears came back, and I let them. For the first time since I was twenty-two, I let myself grieve without managing it. I grieved the father who'd died in a cave. I grieved the father I'd invented, the abandoner, the man who'd chosen maps over daughters, and I grieved him too, because I'd carried him for nine years and he deserved a burial same as anyone. I grieved my mother, whose face I'd paid to forget at a ferry crossing and whose smile I suddenly remembered anyway—not the memory itself, but the shape of having been loved by it, which turned out to live somewhere deeper than the toll-taker's reach.

Freya sat with me through all of it. He didn't fix anything. He didn't offer arithmetic or plans or the nervous jokes he used like sandbags against feeling. He just sat, shoulder to shoulder with me, and watched my father's life turn slowly on the walls, and at some point he said, very quietly:

"He'd have liked you, you know. The you that you are. Stubborn as a barnacle, suspicious of institutions, terrible at accepting help." A beat. "You're very much his daughter."

"And you," I managed, "are very much a man who cries at other people's family memories."

"I contain multitudes. Most of them are damp."

* * *

Hours passed, or what felt like hours. The lamp burned low; Freya refilled it from our stores without either of us discussing whether we should stay. We stayed. You do not rush a cathedral, and you especially do not rush one built out of your own father's final courage.

Near the end, the walls showed one more thing.

My father, on his ledge, in his last hours. He'd taken out a stub of pencil and written on the oilcloth wrapping itself, in letters so small you'd need lamplight and patience to find them—the same patience, I realized, that had led Freya to discover the second layer. The stone zoomed close, tender as a hand.

*To whoever finds this: I am sorry for the trouble. Check the margins. Finish the work. And if my daughter Mara is with you—or if you ever meet her—tell her the tide was not her fault, the silence was not cruelty, and I loved her past the edge of the map and well beyond it.*

I pressed my palm flat against the stone, against the fading image of his face, and the light gathered briefly around my fingers like the water remembering warmth.

"Justice," I said, when I could speak. "I came all this way for justice. I had it all planned out. I was going to find whoever signed the order that left him there, and I was going to ruin them. Publicly. Thoroughly. With footnotes."

"And now?"

"Now I know the man who signed the order is the same man my father refused, and that my father knew exactly what it would cost him, and he paid it anyway, gladly, to keep a spring of memory out of the hands of people who'd sell it by the ounce." I wiped my face with a sleeve already beyond salvation. "Voss will answer for it. I still want that. But it's not justice anymore. It's just... telling the truth where truth can be heard. That's all justice ever was, maybe. Somebody finally saying what happened, out loud, in daylight."

Freya was quiet a moment. Then: "For what it's worth, you're very good at saying things out loud. I've been on the receiving end for weeks."

"It's a gift."

"It's a *menace*," he said warmly, and bumped his shoulder against mine, and I leaned into it, and for a long moment neither of us moved, two battered pilgrims in a chapel of light and stone, while above us the falls roared their upward roar and the monsoon gathered itself somewhere over the sea like a debt coming due.

Then the walls flickered.

All of them, at once—the whole spiral dimming like lamps in a house where someone has thrown the master switch. The images of my father thinned to outlines, then to grooves, then to plain carved stone, ancient and silent again, as though the chamber had shown us everything it intended to and closed its account.

And beneath our feet, faint but unmistakable, the rock shuddered.

Freya was on his feet instantly, lamp raised, head cocked. "Do you feel—"

The shudder came again, longer this time, and somewhere far below us—farther down than the source, deeper than the spring—something vast and old cleared its throat.

From the passage ahead, the one leading onward toward the source, drifted a sound I'd hoped never to hear in this place: voices. Several of them. And one, smooth and courteous as a banker's handshake, calling out cheerfully into the dark:

"Hello? Anyone home? We've come about the property!"
