## 24. The Source

### Freya

The river stopped making sense about an hour before they arrived, which Freya supposed was its way of giving them fair warning.

It had been flowing backward since Salt Hollow—west to east, sea to mountain, an affront to everything he'd learned in thirty years of reading water. But here, in the last mile, it did something worse than reverse. It hesitated. The current slowed to a crawl, then to a shiver, then to a kind of patient stillness, like a held breath, like the river was waiting to see what they'd do.

"Is it supposed to do that?" Mara asked. She was standing in the bow with the chart open across her forearms, and her voice had the careful flatness of someone refusing to be impressed.

"Rivers aren't supposed to do anything," Freya said. "That's the whole problem with them."

The canyon had been narrowing for hours, the walls rising on either side in shelves of black stone streaked with mineral green, and now it simply ended—a wall of rock with a cleft in it, barely wide enough for the boat, and through the cleft came light. Not sunlight. Not lamplight. Something warmer and older, the color of the inside of a shell, and it moved on the water in slow ribbons.

Freya cut the engine and let them drift.

"You could turn around," he said. "I want that on the record. Right now, this is still a survey. We could log it, turn back, and you'd have your father's route and I'd have—" He almost said *my debt* and couldn't, because it wasn't true anymore and hadn't been since the rapids, since her hands had closed on his collar and hauled him out of water that had been showing him his own drowned crew with perfect, loving detail. "I'd have something. It doesn't have to be this."

Mara didn't look at him. She was looking at the light.

"Nine years I filed petitions," she said. "Nine years of clerks telling me the Middle Reach was uncharted because it was unchartable, which is a sentence that means nothing and takes a whole afternoon to say. My father is in there, Freya. Not his body. I know that. But the last true thing he did is in there, and I'm not turning around." She glanced back at him then, and there was something in her face that wasn't fury for once—something rawer and more frightened. "Are you?"

The honest answer was yes. Every instinct Freya owned was screaming at him in the voices of dead men. *Turn around. Some water you don't swim twice.* But his instincts had been wrong about the storm, wrong about Pell's contract, wrong about nearly everything except the shape of the woman in his bow, and so he did what he'd been doing since Cape Verren: he steered into the thing he was afraid of.

"Keep your hand on the line," he said. "If anything happens, you swim for the entrance and don't wait for me. Promise me."

"I promise nothing," Mara said. "You're the one who almost drowned in the rapids. I'm the one who saved you. Statistically, I'm the swimmer in this partnership."

"Statistically," Freya said, "we're both going to die of old age in a port town arguing about this exact moment, and I intend to win the argument."

The cleft took the boat.

* * *

The cavern opened around them like a held secret.

It was larger than the cape's sea cave by an order of magnitude—their lamplight couldn't find the ceiling, only a suggestion of stone going up and up into a soft ambient glow that came from everywhere and nowhere. The water beneath them was shallow here, clear as glass, and the bottom was pale stone worn smooth as river ice, and the light—the light came from the water itself. From below. From everywhere the water touched.

"Freya," Mara said quietly. "Look at the water."

He looked.

The water held images.

Not reflections—reflections would have shown him his own face, the boat, the lamp. This was something else. The spring was shallow, a foot of water over smooth stone, and in that foot of water moved pictures the way light moves through a prism: whole scenes, whole lives, drifting and folding into one another. A woman laughing in a kitchen he didn't know, bread on the table, a child tugging her apron. An old man standing in a doorway at dusk, waving at someone leaving. A boy running down a beach. A soldier coming home. A thousand small moments, layered like sediment, and every one of them was somebody's *best* day. Freya understood that without being told. The spring didn't keep everything. It kept what people had loved.

"It's them," Mara whispered. "The ones who drank from it. They're all still—"

"Living," Freya said. His voice came out rough. "They're living them. Right now. In there."

He thought of Tomás Ilesanmi, dying in the dark of the sea cave with the tide climbing his chest, and the old man's last words came back to him with the weight of a stone dropped down a well: *the source remembers you back.*

He hadn't understood. He understood now, and the understanding went through him like cold water, because if the spring kept what people loved—

The image surfaced directly beneath the boat.

It was the *Kestrel's* deck. He knew it instantly, the way you know your own hands— the scarred rail where Dario had split his knuckles, the coiled line on the pin rail, the compass binnacle with its hairline crack that they'd never fixed because the crack didn't affect the reading and Dario said it gave the boat character. And on the deck, laughing at something, whole and sunlit and *alive*, were Sefa and Dario and old Nkem at the wheel, and the storm was nowhere in the picture, because the spring kept what people had loved, and what they had loved was the working of the boat on a good afternoon, the three of them and the wind fair and nowhere they had to be.

Freya stopped breathing.

Sefa looked up. Across the impossible distance between the living air and the remembered water, across the boundary that no one had ever crossed and come back from, she looked directly at him, and she smiled, and she said something. He couldn't hear it. The water didn't carry sound. But he could read her lips the way he'd read them across a loud deck for six years, and what she said was:

*There you are.*

Freya made a sound he would deny for the rest of his life. Something between a laugh and a sob, punched out of him without permission. His hands were white on the gunwale.

"Freya." Mara's hand on his shoulder. "Freya, look at me."

"They're fine," he said. "Mara, they're *fine*, look at them, they're—" He was leaning over the side. He didn't remember leaning over the side. The water was right there, a foot below him, warm light rising, Sefa's face looking up at him with an expression he'd spent four years trying to earn and had just been handed for free. "I could just—"

"No." Her grip tightened. "No, you couldn't. Freya. *Look at me.*"

He looked at her. Her face was pale and set and terrified, and behind the terror was something that understood him completely, because she was looking at the water too, and he could see her scanning it, hunting it, the way he'd scanned it. Looking for a kitchen. For a woman's face she'd paid a ferrywoman to lose and wanted back so badly her hands were shaking.

"We don't drink," she said. "We don't touch it. We don't stay. That was the deal, remember? That was the whole point of the last three weeks. We came to see it and then we end it, so no one else ever has to choose."

"I know the deal."

"You're leaning over the side of the boat."

"I'm *aware*—"

"Sefa's got her whole afternoon," Mara said, quiet and merciless. "She's had it for four years. She'll have it forever. She doesn't need you to join her to keep it. That's the trap, Freya. That's what the spring does. It shows you the one thing you can't live without and then it lets you think staying is the same as loving."

The words landed like a hook set deep. Freya looked at the water one more time—Sefa, Dario, Nkem, the good afternoon going on and on—and then, with both hands, deliberately, he pulled himself back into the boat and sat down hard on the thwart.

"Okay," he said. His voice shook. "Okay. That was close."

"That was the closest yet," Mara said. "And we're not done."

* * *

### Mara

Mara had prepared for this her whole life, in the way that people prepare for things they refuse to admit they're preparing for. Nine years of petitions was nine years of imagining. She had imagined finding a body. She had imagined finding nothing. She had imagined, in her worst and most secret hours, finding her father alive and having to say all the angry things she'd saved up, and then finding him dead and having nowhere to put them.

She had not imagined this.

The spring showed her the kitchen.

It was her mother's kitchen in Salt Hollow, the one with the blue tiles and the window over the sink, and her mother was standing at the counter with flour on her hands, and she turned—she *turned*, she looked straight out of the water at Mara, and her face was her face. Not the blurred, dissolving thing the ferrywoman had left in its place. Not the photograph. The living, moving, exasperated, laughing face, saying *Mara, you're tracking mud, I just swept—*

Mara's knees nearly went.

She had paid for this. That was the thing that made it unbearable. She had stood at the derelict ferry crossing and let an ancient woman reach into her and take her mother's face as the toll, and she had told herself it was a fair price for the truth about her father, and every night since, she had woken reaching for a face that wasn't there. And now here it was, floating a foot beneath her, offered freely, forever, and all it would cost was everything.

Because she understood the spring now. She'd understood it in the boat, watching Freya nearly go over the side, and she understood it better with every image that surfaced: the water didn't trap you with lies. It trapped you with the truth. Her mother was in there, sweeping the same floor, saying the same words, caught in the amber of her own best afternoon, and if Mara drank, if she stepped in, she wouldn't be joining her mother. She'd be *becoming* this—a loop, a lovely moment repeating until the end of the world, a picture in a spring that a stranger's boat would drift over someday. Her mother got to be a memory. That was what the dead were for. The living were supposed to do the other thing. The harder thing.

"Is that her?" Freya asked softly. He'd moved to the bow without her noticing, giving her space the way he did—clumsily, loudly pretending not to watch.

"That's her." Mara's voice was steady. She was proud of that. She'd pay for it later. "That's the kitchen. I broke a plate in that kitchen when I was seven and she told my father it was his fault for buying cheap plates, and they argued about it for a decade. It was the happiest argument I ever heard."

Freya huffed. "My crew argued about a compass crack for six years. There's a theme."

"Everything's a theme with you."

"I'm a surveyor. We think in themes." He was quiet a moment. Then: "Do you see him? Your father?"

She'd been looking. She couldn't help looking, scanning the drifting layers the way you scan a crowd. And there—there, between the woman with the bread and the boy on the beach—a flash of a chart room. Lamplight. A younger Tomás Ilesanmi bent over a drafting table, ink on his fingers, and he was *drawing*, and he looked up at someone off-frame and smiled the particular smile he'd had when a coastline came out right, and the image folded away into the sediment before Mara could see who he'd smiled at.

She hadn't known she was crying until Freya handed her a rag without a word, which was such a Freya thing to do that it made her cry harder.

"He's in there," she said. "Just—working. That's his forever. Not the source, not the discovery. The *work*. He loved the work." She pressed the rag to her eyes. "Nine years I wanted justice. Somebody to blame for leaving him. And he's in there drawing coastlines, and he looks *happy*, and I don't know what to do with that."

"Maybe justice was always the wrong shape," Freya said. "Maybe it was just grief wearing a surveyor's coat."

"Careful. That's almost wise."

"I'm under a lot of pressure."

She laughed—a wet, cracked laugh, but real—and it broke something loose in her chest, some knot she'd been carrying since Salt Hollow, since the harbor, since the day she'd recognized his salvage gear and thought *finally, someone to hate.* She didn't hate him. That was the trouble. Somewhere between the rapids and the firelight and this impossible glowing room, the hate had quietly packed its things and left, and what remained was worse and better: a man she'd chosen, standing beside her in the place she'd given nine years to reach.

The water shifted beneath them.

It wasn't like the other shifts. The images didn't drift; they *parted*, folding away from a single point in the center of the pool the way a crowd parts, and the light there changed—warmer, older, steadier. The true spring. The source of the source. And rising through the parted water came an image so clear it might have been cut from glass:

The cavern itself. This cavern. But different—dry, lit by torches, and full of people. Hundreds of them, generations of them, standing at the water's edge in clothes from a dozen centuries, and every one of them was drinking, and every one of them was smiling, and the smiles were the same smile, and it was the smile of someone who has just decided to stop leaving.

The image held for three heartbeats. Then it folded away, and the water closed over it, and the parted center began, very slowly, to heal.

"It's showing us its own history," Freya whispered. "It's *proud* of it."

"It's showing us the menu," Mara said. Her mouth had gone dry. "Freya. The center. Where the water parts. That's the actual source—the place you'd bottle. The place Pell would bottle. If we're going to do what Tomás planned, that's where the gates have to be."

Freya looked at the healing water, then at the chart spread across the thwart, then at her. And Mara watched him do the arithmetic she'd already done—the debt, the collectors, the warden somewhere behind them, the monsoon somewhere ahead of them—and watched him arrive, with visible reluctance, at the same sum.

"We can't reach it from the boat," he said. "The water's too shallow. We'd have to wade."

"Wade into the water that keeps you forever."

"I did say that."

"You did say that." Mara rolled the chart closed and looked at the parted center, at the warm light rising from it like a held breath, and thought about her mother's kitchen, and her father's drafting table, and the funeral that had never happened, and the one that someday would. "Then we'd better be quick," she said. "And Freya—whatever you see in there, whatever it shows you—"

"I know."

"Because it'll show you the thing. Your thing. The exact thing."

"I *know*, Mara."

"And you'll want to—"

"Mara." He took her hand. His was steady, which surprised them both. "Four years ago I would have walked into that water for Sefa's smile and never looked back. But I've got a boat to rebuild and a debt to settle and a woman who saves me from rivers and argues with me about statistics." His mouth twitched. "Turns out I've got a future. It's a recent development. I'm still reading the documentation."

Mara squeezed his hand and did not say the word. Neither of them said the word. The word was going to have to wait, because the water in the center of the spring had parted again—wider this time, deliberate, an invitation—and at the bottom of it, plain as a signature, was a shape neither of them had expected.

Stone. Cut stone. A ring of carved blocks around a dark opening in the cavern floor, and set into the nearest block, unmistakable even through a foot of glowing water, was the worn shape of a hand.

Tomás's annotations hadn't just described the source.

They'd described a door.

"That's not on the chart," Freya breathed.

"No," Mara said, and her heart was hammering, because she knew her father's hand-carving when she saw it—she'd grown up watching him letter coastlines. "That's not on the chart because he didn't draw it for the Survey Office. He drew it for whoever finished the work." She looked up at Freya, and the warm light of the spring was in her eyes, and somewhere far behind them, faint as a rumor, came the first low roll of thunder off the sea.

"And I think," she said, "we just found out why the monsoon matters."
