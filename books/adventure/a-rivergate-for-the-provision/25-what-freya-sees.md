## 25. What Freya Sees

The water did not look like water.

That was the first thing Freya understood, standing at the edge of the spring with his boots half-sunk in silt and the whole cavern breathing light around him. Water was supposed to be a surface—a thing between you and everything else. This was not between anything. It was a window laid flat on the world, and through it moved people.

Not reflections. People.

He saw an old woman laughing at something no one else could hear, her hands full of river reeds. He saw a boy diving off a rock in some summer that had ended before Freya was born, frozen mid-arc, joyful forever. He saw faces he didn't know and faces he almost knew and—his chest went tight—one face he knew better than he wanted to.

"Freya." Mara's voice came from somewhere behind him, thin as thread. "The water's showing me my mother."

He couldn't answer. His mouth had gone dry as old rope.

Because there, drifting in the current like leaves on a slow bend of river, were his crew.

* * *

He'd told the story so many times it had worn smooth, the way a stone wears smooth in a pocket—handled every day until all the edges were gone. The storm off the Sable Shelf, six years ago. The barometer falling like a man jumping from a bridge. His call to push through instead of turning back, because the data was good, because the cell was rare, because Freya Zhou had never once in his life met a horizon he didn't want to argue with.

Three names. Okafor, who sang off-key and cooked like an angel. Dell, who owed him forty dollars and paid it back in jokes. Junie, who was twenty-three and had signed on because she thought storms were beautiful, and who had been right, God help her, right up until the end.

The official finding was weather. The unofficial finding—the one Freya carried around inside him like a swallowed anchor—was Freya.

Now they were in the water.

Okafor first, big shoulders and that terrible singing voice, standing hip-deep in memory with a pot in one hand and a wooden spoon in the other, stirring something that steamed even though it was only light. And then Dell, sprawled across the current like a man in a hammock, grinning that grin that always meant trouble or dinner, usually both. And then Junie—

Junie turned around.

Freya made a sound he would deny for the rest of his life. Something between a word and a wound.

She looked older than twenty-three. That was the thing that broke him open—not seeing her young, not seeing her as she'd been, but seeing her as she might have been, the woman the storm had stolen before she got to be. Laugh lines starting at her eyes. A scar on her chin he didn't recognize, from some adventure he'd never gotten to hear about.

She wasn't drowning. None of them were drowning. They were standing in the last moments of their lives the way you stand in a doorway—easily, without hurry—and they were looking at him.

"No," Freya said out loud, because some part of him still believed in refusing gifts. "No, this isn't—I don't get this. I don't get to see this."

But the water didn't care what he believed. The water only remembered.

* * *

It took him into the wheelhouse.

Not literally—he was still standing on the bank, Mara's hand suddenly gripping his arm hard enough to bruise—but the cavern had stopped being a cavern. Rain hammered glass that wasn't there. The deck pitched under feet that weren't moving. The smell of salt and diesel and fear filled his nose so completely he tasted it.

He was back on the *Provision*, the night it ended.

Except he wasn't in his own body. He was watching from the corner of the wheelhouse, from some vantage no living man had ever occupied, and he was watching himself—younger, soaked, shouting over the wind into the radio—and he was watching Okafor.

Okafor stood by the chart table, braced against the roll, and he was looking at Freya-young with an expression Freya had spent six years inventing versions of. Anger. Betrayal. The silent accusation of a man following a fool into hell.

That was what Freya had seen every night behind his eyelids since. That was the picture he'd built his penance around.

It was wrong.

Okafor wasn't angry. Okafor was afraid—of course he was afraid, any sane man was afraid—but underneath the fear, plain as daylight under a door, was trust. Absolute, unguarded, almost stupid trust. The look of a man who has decided that the person steering is the person steering, and that is enough. Okafor's hands were steady on the table. He nodded once at something Freya-young said. His mouth moved, and though there was no sound in the memory—or maybe there was, maybe the sound was just too large for Freya's ears—he could read the words as clearly as if they were carved:

*Your call, Captain. We're with you.*

"We're with you," Freya whispered, and his knees went, and Mara caught him, and the water kept giving.

Dell next. Dell in the corridor below, hauling the emergency kit toward the deck, joking even then—Dell would have joked at the gates of heaven, probably asked St. Peter if the drinks were comped—and Dell paused at the base of the ladder and looked up toward the wheelhouse, toward the captain he couldn't see through the rain and the dark, and he saluted. Sloppy, mocking, perfect Dell salute. *Forty dollars I still owe you,* said the grin. *I'm good for it.*

And Junie.

Junie on the aft deck, clipped to the rail, hair plastered to her face, and she was looking at the storm. Not away from it. At it. Even now, even at the end, she was looking at the storm the way you look at a cathedral—with her whole face open—and then she turned and looked up at the wheelhouse light, the small yellow glow where her captain stood making his terrible, human, best-he-could call, and she smiled.

She smiled.

Six years Freya had carried their deaths like stones in a sack, and every stone had a face, and every face had blamed him. He had built a whole architecture of guilt, floor by floor, load-bearing wall by load-bearing wall, and he had lived in it because living anywhere else seemed like a betrayal of the dead.

And the dead, it turned out, had never lived there at all.

They had trusted him. Not blindly—that was the unbearable part, the part that made grace feel like a knife—they had trusted him *with their eyes open*. They knew the risk. They were sailors and chasers and grown people, and they had weighed the same wind he weighed, and they had stayed. Not because he ordered them. Because they chose it, the way he chose it, the way everyone who ever goes to sea chooses it: because the horizon argued back and they liked the argument.

His debt—the one he'd been paying interest on for six years, the one he'd folded into the Optimists' ledger as if money were just another denomination of the same coin—had already been cleared. Cleared on the deck of the *Provision* by three people who forgave him before the wave ever came. He had simply refused the receipt.

*You've been paying interest on a debt that's paid,* the water said, not in words, in the way the light bent. *Who taught you arithmetic, Captain?*

"I did," Freya said, and laughed, and it came out cracked down the middle. "I did. I'm the worst lender I know."

* * *

He didn't know how long he stood there. Time in the cavern moved like honey off a spoon—slowly, sweetly, without regard for clocks. At some point Mara let go of his arm, and he understood without looking that she was having her own reckoning somewhere in the current, and that whatever she saw was hers, and that the privacy of grief was the last courtesy the wilderness had left to offer.

When he finally surfaced from it—when the wheelhouse and the rain and the three faces receded back into the general shimmer of the spring—he found he was crying without any of the usual machinery. No sobbing, no drama. Just water leaving his eyes the way water does, finding its level, going home.

"Well," he said, to nobody, to everybody. "Huh."

"You okay?" Mara's voice, hoarse, from ten feet away. She was kneeling at the edge of the spring, her fingers trailing the surface, her expression doing several things at once that he politely pretended not to catalog.

"No," he said honestly. "Yes. I don't—" He wiped his face with his sleeve, which accomplished nothing except redistributing the salt. "Mara, I need to tell you something, and I need you to understand I'm not being poetic, I'm being literal."

She looked at him.

"They forgave me." His voice wobbled like a compass near iron. "Before it happened. During it. Whatever tense forgiveness uses when it happens underwater in a storm—they used it. Okafor trusted me. Dell saluted me. Junie—" His throat closed. He worked past it. "Junie smiled at me. Six years I've been telling myself they died hating me, because hating myself felt like the only honest thing left, and it turns out I invented the whole thing. I convicted myself of a crime nobody pressed charges for."

Mara was quiet for a long moment. The spring murmured its endless murmur.

"That sounds familiar," she said finally, and there was something raw and new in her voice, something that hadn't been there nine years ago or nine days ago. "Spending years being sure of something that isn't true, because the truth hurts worse."

"Yeah?"

"Yeah." She looked down at the water, at whatever it was showing her, and her jaw worked. "We'll talk about it later. Maybe. On dry land. If there is such a thing anymore."

"There's the boat," Freya offered. "The boat's dry-ish."

"The boat leaks, Freya."

"Dry-ish," he insisted, and she laughed—a wet, broken, real laugh—and the sound of it did more healing in two seconds than the spring had done in an hour, because it was hers, unscripted, alive, here.

* * *

He went back to the edge once more before they left. He couldn't help it. He knelt where Mara knelt and looked into the current, and his crew were still there—of course they were, they were always there, that was the whole point of the place—and Okafor raised his spoon in a lazy salute and Dell mimed checking an invisible ledger and shaking his head mournfully, *still unpaid, Captain, shame, shame,* and Junie just sat on the surface of the memory with her knees drawn up, watching him with the patience of someone who has all the time that ever was.

*I know,* Freya told her silently. *I know you're not really her. I know you're the spring wearing her face, or her face wearing the spring, or some third thing I don't have a word for.* But it didn't matter, and that was the miracle of it—it didn't matter whether it was truly her soul or merely the water's perfect memory of her, because either way the message was the same, and the message was: *Go on, Captain. We're fine. You're the one who isn't.*

"I have debts," he said aloud, because saying things aloud was how he kept from floating away entirely. "Real ones. Ugly ones. There are men coming up this river who want to bottle you and sell you by the ounce, and a warden who wants to blow you into gravel, and I brought both problems here by being the kind of man who signs contracts in flooded bars."

The current swirled, unbothered. The spring had watched empires drink from it, probably. One cheerful loan shark more or less wouldn't ruffle it.

"But I'm going to fix it," Freya said. "Not for the ledger. For—" He gestured vaguely at everything: the cavern, the crew, the girl kneeling beside him, the whole improbable gift of still being alive at thirty-four with his fingers attached. "For the provision. Tomás's word. I finally looked it up in my head, what the old man named his boat after. Provision. What you carry for other people. Not what you take."

Junie smiled again, and this time Freya managed to smile back, and it hurt like setting a bone and felt like setting a bone, which is to say it hurt exactly as much as healing requires.

Then the light changed.

It was subtle at first—a shift in the cavern's glow, the way afternoon leans toward evening. The images in the water thinned, the crew fading like breath off glass. And beneath them, rising slowly from the deep of the spring, something else began to show through. Not a person. A shape. Dark, angular, deliberate.

Stone steps. Carved stone, descending into the spring itself, and on the nearest step, unmistakable even through the distortion of the water, a shape Freya had seen before—etched into the walls of the canyon stair, drawn by hands centuries dead.

A sluice gate.

Tomás's annotations. Tomás's mechanism. And unless Freya's eyes were lying—and after today he trusted nothing, least of all his own certainties—the source itself was showing them where it lived.

"Mara," he said quietly. "Mara, I think the water just told us how to drown it."

She looked up. Behind her, far back down the passage they'd come by, a sound reached them—faint, echoing, horribly patient. Boots on carved stone. More than one pair.

And beneath the boots, rolling in from beyond the canyon walls like a promise kept by an old enemy, the first distant thunder of the monsoon.
