## 26. What Mara Sees

The water remembered her mother before Mara did.

That was the cruelty of it—or the mercy, she couldn't yet decide. Nine years of petitioning the Survey Office, nine years of forms and denials and clerks who pronounced her father's name like a mispronounced foreign word they'd given up on, and in all that time her mother's face had been going soft at the edges, like a photograph left in a window. She could still summon the shape of her: the broad shoulders, the laugh that started in the belly and embarrassed strangers. But the face itself, the particular architecture of it—the way the left eyebrow sat higher than the right, the small scar above the chin from a fishhook incident her mother told like a comedy and Mara suspected had been closer to tragedy—that had gone the way of all unexamined things. Smoothed over. Approximated.

And now the spring held it, perfect, undimmed, the way the sea holds a dropped coin.

Mara stood at the edge of the water with her boots still on, because some stubborn clerk in her chest insisted that you did not take your boots off in a cavern, and looked down at her mother looking up at her.

* * *

The cavern did not behave like a cavern. Light came from nowhere in particular—the water itself seemed to be the lamp—and it fell in that particular gold of late afternoon in a kitchen, the light of her childhood, though they were a hundred fathoms and nine years and one impossible river away from any kitchen she'd ever stood in. The spring pooled in a basin of smooth stone, wide as a courtyard, and its surface was not a surface. It was a crowd.

Faces moved in the current. Hundreds of them. Thousands. People she didn't know, living moments she'd never witnessed—a man laughing so hard he had to grip a table, a woman holding a newborn with the terrified tenderness of someone who has just been handed the most important assignment of her life, an old man teaching a child to whistle and failing, both of them delighted by the failure. Every person who had ever come here, she understood, or ever drunk from it, or ever stood where she stood and let the water take their likeness. The spring didn't store memories the way a ledger stores debts. It kept them *alive*, the way a mother keeps a child's drawings—not filed, but loved.

And there, near the center of the basin where the current turned slow and thoughtful, was her mother.

Not a memory of her mother. Her mother. Kneeling on the deck of the *Perseverance*—Tomás's boat, the boat Mara had spent her childhood pretending not to love—mending a net with the quick, impatient hands of a woman who considered patience a virtue other people could afford. She was laughing at something off-frame. Her left eyebrow sat higher than her right. The scar above her chin caught the light.

Mara's knees went. She didn't so much kneel as get outvoted by her own body, and she found herself on the stone at the water's edge with her hand out and her heart going like a bilge pump in a storm.

"Careful," Freya said quietly, from somewhere behind her. He didn't touch her. She was grateful for that. There are moments when a hand on the shoulder feels like a rescue and moments when it feels like an interruption, and she was fairly sure which one this was, but not sure enough.

"I'm not going to fall in," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she'd earned. "I'm a surveyor's daughter. I know how edges work."

"Sure," Freya said. "I'm just a man who nearly drowned in a river yesterday, standing here for no particular reason."

* * *

The water shifted. The current turned, and her mother's face dissolved into another, and Mara's whole body lurched forward after it like a dog at the end of a chain—before she caught herself, caught the stone, caught her breath.

The new face was her father's.

Not the father of the sealed file, not the gaunt legend of the dying man Freya had described in the cave—*the source remembers you back*, Tomás had said, and then the sea had closed over him like a book. This was her father *whole*. Younger than she remembered him, or perhaps exactly as old as he'd been the last time she'd seen him, which was the same thing and not the same thing at all. He was at the chart table in the cabin of the *Perseverance*, and he was drawing, and he looked up—directly up, out of the water, at her—and he smiled.

Not a recorded smile. Not an echo. A smile that arrived *now*, in the present tense, aimed at her with the full accuracy of a man who had spent his life reading coastlines and could read a daughter at a glance.

"Mara," the water said, in his voice, and it wasn't a trick of the current. It was the way he'd always said it—*MAH-rah*, with the weight on the first syllable, as though her name were a place he was glad to have reached. "You found the annotations."

"I found them," she said. Her throat was full of sand. "It took me nine years and a debt-ridden storm chaser and a warden with a guilty conscience, but yes. I found them."

"He'd be proud of the warden," the water said, with the dry note her father deployed when he was being funny and wanted deniability. "He always said the Survey Office would produce one honest person eventually, out of sheer statistical inevitability."

"Is this real?" she asked. "Or is this the water showing me what I want?"

The water was quiet for a moment, in the way her father had always been quiet when she asked a question he thought deserved a real answer. Around the basin, the other faces went on living their small eternities—the whistling lesson, the laughing man, the woman and her newborn—but her father's image held still, holding her gaze.

"Does it matter?" he asked.

"Yes," she said. "It matters enormously. It's the entire question. It's the question I've been carrying for nine years like a stone in my boot."

"Then here's a surveyor's answer." He gestured, the way he used to gesture at charts, at the whole visible world. "The spring keeps what was given to it. I gave it nothing. I never drank. I never touched it—I found it and I walked away and I spent thirty years making sure no one else would walk toward it. So what you're seeing isn't me. It's the shape of me, held in the memory of a place that remembers everything it has ever been shown." He smiled again, and this time the smile was tired, and it was the tiredness that convinced her. A forgery would have been kind. This was true. "I'm dead, Mara. I have been dead since the tide came in on a man who stayed too long looking at a map. But the shape of me—the part of me that's in you, that's in the annotations, that's in the way you check a knot twice and distrust easy answers—that part is realer than this water will ever be. This water keeps. It doesn't live."

"I know," she whispered.

"Do you?" her father asked gently. "Because your hand is still out."

* * *

She looked down. Her hand was out. It had been out for some time, hovering a finger's width above the surface, close enough that the gold light trembled against her skin, close enough that she could feel it—not warmth, exactly, but *attention*, the way you feel a room full of people turn to look at you. The water had begun to rise to meet her, just slightly, just a swell of a centimeter, like a dog lifting its head when it hears its name.

And in the swell, the images braided together. Her mother's face and her father's, side by side, and behind them—deeper in the current, dimmer, patient—the life she could have. She saw it the way you see the whole of a coastline from a boat: all at once, undeniable. Dinners that had never happened. Her mother teaching her to mend nets properly instead of the slapdash way she'd learned from books. Her father walking her through the source's mechanisms, answering every question she'd carried through her twenties and thirties like a hold full of unmanifested cargo. The argument they'd have about whether to seal it, and the resolution, and the years after. Whole decades, offered whole. The spring was not teasing her. The spring was *generous*. That was the horror of it. It wasn't a trap with teeth; it was a trap with open arms.

"You could stay," the water said, and now it was her mother's voice, warm and belly-deep, the laugh already loaded in it like a spring. "We're all here, love. Everyone you've ever missed. Nobody leaves. Nobody dies. Nobody forgets your birthday, and nobody's face goes soft at the edges."

"I know," Mara said.

"You've been so tired," her mother said. "Nine years of clerks and forms and men who say *no* in expensive rooms. You could rest here. You could *stay* rested."

"I know," Mara said again, and to her own surprise she laughed—a wet, cracked, entirely human laugh. "You always did know how to make an offer."

"I learned from your father. He could sell salt to the sea when he set his mind to it."

"He could," Mara agreed. "He sold the Survey Office on the idea that some knowledge should stay wild, and they hated him for it, and he was right, and it killed him."

The water rippled. Her father's face came forward again.

"Mara." The weight on the first syllable. The gladness. "Tell me what you see when you look at the water. Not what's in it. What you *see*."

She looked. She made herself look the way her father had taught her to look at charts—not at the lines, but at what the lines were doing; not at the coast, but at what the coast wanted.

"I see a room," she said slowly. "A very beautiful room with no doors. Everyone in it is happy. Everyone in it is *staying* happy, the way a jar stays sealed." She swallowed. "I see that nobody in it is doing anything. The laughing man has been laughing at that table for—how long? Since he came here? The woman with the baby is always just *holding* her, never watching her grow. It's a photograph pretending to be a life."

"Yes," her father said.

"And I see," Mara went on, and her voice shook, and she let it, "that if I step in, I stop too. I become another face in the current. Someone might come along in a hundred years and see me, and think, *there's a woman who looks happy*, and they'd be right, and they'd be looking at something that ended the moment it began being kept." She drew her hand back—not far. A hand's width. But back. "A memory kept here is a life not lived."

The water went very still. All the faces, all at once, went still, like a crowd listening.

Then her father smiled—not the tired smile, not the glad one, but a third one she had no memory of and recognized instantly anyway, because it was the smile she'd seen that morning in Freya's face at the fire, the one people wear when someone they love has just said the truest thing available.

"That," he said, "is the answer I waited thirty years to hear. I just didn't know it would be you who said it."

"I had a good teacher," Mara said. "And a good map. And, admittedly, a debt-ridden storm chaser with a talent for nearly drowning."

"Those run in short supply," her father agreed gravely. "Hold on to him."

"I *beg* your pardon—"

"Mara." Her mother's voice now, and the laughter in it had gone soft at the edges, the way all true things eventually do. "Love. We have to tell you something, and then we have to let you go, and you have to let us."

"Please don't," Mara said. "Please don't do the thing where you're wise and then you're gone. I hate that thing. I've read about it in books and I hate it."

"Nine years," her mother said, "and you still argue like your grandmother."

"Nine years," Mara said, "and you still *dodge* like her."

The current turned. The faces of her parents drew together in the gold light—not fading, she noticed, not yet, but gathering, the way a family gathers in a doorway when someone is leaving on a long trip and everyone has agreed not to cry and everyone is failing.

"Here is what we have to tell you," her father said. "The annotations. The sluice gates. You've found them, and Freya has read them, and you're going to use them. We know, because the water knows, because the water knows everything that comes near it, and it has known since the day you entered the river." He paused. "We want you to know that we don't object. We *celebrate* it. This place was never meant to be a vault. It was meant to be a spring—something that gives and moves on. I built the gates to give it back to the dark where it belongs, and I have been dead too long to pull them myself, and my daughter is here, and my daughter is a surveyor, and my daughter checks her knots twice."

"I check them three times," Mara said, crying now, not bothering to hide it, because there was no one left in the world whose opinion of her crying mattered more than these two faces in the water.

"Three times," her father agreed. "Good. Because the mechanism is old, and the river is angry, and you'll want the third check."

"And the letting go," her mother said. "That part. Listen. The face you lost—the one that went soft at the edges? You'll get it back. Not from the water. From *living*. You'll see me in strangers on the dock. You'll hear my laugh come out of your own mouth at some ridiculous moment and embarrass everyone. That's not a copy of me, love. That's me, *continued*. That's what the dead actually do, when the living let them. We don't sit in jars. We *spread*." She smiled, and the scar above her chin caught the light one last time. "The water can keep a picture of me. But you—you get to keep the *moving* version. Don't trade the moving version for the picture. Never trade the moving version."

Mara knelt there at the edge with her boots on and her hand in her lap and wept—not the cold, furious, nine-year grief that had driven her like an engine, but the other kind, the kind that has somewhere to go, the kind that *ends*. It went through her like weather. It took its time. Neither of her parents hurried her, and the water held still around them, patient as stone, generous as ever, asking nothing.

When it was done—when the weather passed and she could breathe again—she leaned forward and did not touch the water. She held her palm a finger's width above it, close enough to feel the attention, the warmth-that-was-not-warmth.

"Goodbye," she said. "Both of you. I'll see you in strangers. I'll hear you in my own stupid laugh. And I'll pull the gates, and I'll check the knots three times, and I'll tell Cass Okonjo you'd have liked her, and I'll—" she laughed, wet and cracked, "—I'll probably hold on to the storm chaser, since you asked."

"Statistical inevitability," her father said, with the dry note, and then the current turned one final time, slow and deliberate, like a man turning back to his chart table because there was work to do and not much night left, and the faces of her parents folded into the gold water and were everywhere in it and were gone.

* * *

She knelt there a while after, the way you stay in a doorway after someone has gone, on the chance that doors work differently than you think.

The spring went on around her—the whistling lesson, the laughing man, the woman and her newborn—and Mara found, to her astonishment, that she could look at them now without wanting. They were beautiful the way a museum is beautiful. She wished them well. She hoped, sincerely, that someone would come and flood the whole gorgeous room and let them all finally, *finally* move on.

"Mara?" Freya's voice, careful, from behind. "You've been down there a while. Cass is getting twitchy, and Cass twitchy makes *me* twitchy, and I've had enough twitch for one lifetime."

She stood. Her knees objected. She let them.

"She let me go," Mara said. "They let me go. Or I let them. The grammar's still settling."

Freya came and stood beside her at the edge, and she watched him look down into the water, and she watched the water look back at him, and she saw his face do the thing that faces do when the spring offers them something they'd walk through fire to refuse. He had already had his turn—she'd seen him come out of it gray-faced and shining, talking about his crew, about forgiveness, about interest paid on a debt already cleared. But the water was patient. The water never stopped asking.

"Don't," she said, gently. "I know that look. I invented that look. I had it for about twenty minutes just now."

"I'm not—" He laughed, shaky. "I'm just admiring the architecture."

"Freya."

"I know." He put his hands in his pockets, physically removing the temptation of his own hands, and Mara thought, absurdly, *he checks his knots three times too.* "I know. It's just—it never stops offering. That's the thing nobody tells you. It's not a door you close. It's a host who keeps refilling your glass."

"Then we pull the gates," Mara said, "and we drown the host."

She said it lightly, and it landed in the cavern like a dropped anchor, because at that moment—she would never be able to explain the timing, and would never try—the light in the water changed. Not the gold of kitchens anymore. Something colder moved in the current, far upstream of the images, a disturbance rippling down through the faces like a word passed along a crowd: *someone is coming, someone is coming, someone is coming.*

The faces turned, all of them at once, away from Mara, toward the dark mouth of the passage.

And in the current, distorted but unmistakable, the spring showed her a reflection that had not been there before: a broad-shouldered man in a dry, cheerful coat, smiling as he descended the carved stair, with collectors behind him and survey equipment on their backs and the look of a man who has come, at last, to collect.
