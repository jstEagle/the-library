## 27. Pell Arrives Early

The villager's name was Odum, and he had held out for eleven hours, which Pell considered, professionally speaking, a triumph.

"Remarkable man," Pell said now, watching two of his collectors carry the old fisherman down to the boat on a stretcher. "You have to admire that kind of stubbornness. Most people break by hour four. Hour five if you're thorough." He checked his watch, a gold thing with a second hand that ticked like a small, patient heart. "He broke at eleven because I stopped asking about the route and started asking about his granddaughter's school fees. You can't train for that question. Nobody trains for that."

His collectors said nothing. They never did. Pell had hired them precisely for their silence, the way other men hired musicians for their noise. There were six of them aboard the *Provision*, his survey launch, and they moved with the unhurried competence of men who had long ago made peace with what they were. Pell liked that in an employee. Ambivalence was expensive. He'd built an entire syndicate on the principle that certainty was the cheapest commodity on earth—you could buy it wholesale from anyone desperate enough.

The river had narrowed an hour ago into a canyon of black stone, and the walls rose on either side of them like the pages of an enormous closed book. Stairs were cut into the rock—ancient ones, worn smooth in their centers by feet that had been climbing toward something for longer than any ledger could record. Above them, impossibly, the falls ran upward, silver threads ascending against gravity as though the water itself had somewhere better to be.

Pell stood at the bow and smiled at it.

"You see," he told the silent collectors, gesturing at the inverted water with the fondness of a man pointing out a sunset, "this is why I love this business. Everyone told me the chart was a fake. A dying man's fantasy. And here it is—the whole impossible thing, running backward right over our heads." He tapped the rail. "The market for miracles is infinite, gentlemen. The supply, however—" he glanced up the canyon, where the light went green and deep, "—is about to be one spring. Do you understand what that means? Scarcity plus wonder equals whatever price we say it does."

The tallest collector, a slab-faced man called Brannoc, shifted his weight. It was the closest he came to speech.

"Yes," Pell agreed, as though the shift had been a full oration. "It means we're rich. It means the Optimists are rich. It means every grieving widow and ambitious general and frightened old man on three continents will pay us anything we ask for a bottle of yesterday." He laughed—a bright, genuine sound, and that was the thing about Pell that unsettled people who expected villains to brood: he meant it. Every smile was real. Every cruelty was cheerful. He had never once done an unkind thing in a bad mood.

* * *

They moored at the foot of the stairs as the light failed, and Pell slept badly, which was unusual for him. He dreamed of ledgers. In the dream, every line of figures resolved into a face, and every face was someone he had ruined, and none of them were angry—they were all smiling at him, politely, the way debtors smile at collectors, and the smiles were worse than screaming. He woke before dawn with the dream still clinging to him like damp, and he stood on the deck looking up at the stairs until the sun found the canyon floor.

"Gloomy night," he announced cheerfully, when Brannoc emerged. "Bad dreams. The mind balancing its books, no doubt. Well—nothing a little compound interest won't fix."

They climbed the stair cut in stone for most of the morning. The collectors carried the equipment: brass bottles with wax-sealed stoppers, sample cases lined with felt, a portable assay kit, coils of rope, lamps. Pell carried nothing but his ledger book and his good mood, because a man in his position had learned that carrying things was for people who hadn't yet learned to make other people carry things.

Twice they passed carvings in the walls—travelers, mostly, rendered in a style so old the stone had begun to forget them. In each carving, the travelers were shown giving something away. A figure pressing a hand to its own chest. A figure letting a small bird fly from open palms. A figure kneeling before a pool, empty-handed.

"Paying admission," Pell murmured, amused. "Even the ancients understood tolls. Good instincts, our ancestors. Terrible follow-through, though—look at this place, completely unmonetized." He patted the wall as he passed, companionably, the way you might pat an old horse. "Don't worry, old girl. We'll take care of that."

The air changed as they climbed. It grew cooler, then warm again, then cool, cycling strangely, like breath. And there was a sound beneath the sound of the upward-falling water—a low, layered murmur, almost like voices, almost like a crowd heard through a wall. One of the younger collectors, a wiry man named Sef, stopped once and tilted his head at it.

"Hear that?" Pell asked brightly. "That's inventory, Sef. That's the sound of product."

But privately—and Pell was honest with himself in the way that only truly self-satisfied men are honest—he thought it sounded less like product and more like a harbor crowd. Like everyone he had ever collected from, all talking at once, just below hearing.

He put the thought away. Thoughts like that were bad for morale, and the only morale that mattered was his own.

* * *

They reached the antechamber near midday. The passage opened without warning, the way a held breath opens, and then they were inside.

Pell had seen the great banking halls of the capital. He had seen cathedrals, auction houses, the vault of the Mercantile Guild with its doors thick as ship hulls. None of it prepared him.

The cavern was vast and lit from below. That was the first impossibility: the light came up out of the ground, sourceless, green-gold, the color of sunlight through harbor water. And the spring itself lay in a basin of smooth stone at the chamber's center—not large, not dramatic, perhaps ten paces across—but the water moved. Not with current. With images.

Faces drifted in it. Lives drifted in it. As Pell approached the rim, the surface showed him a woman dancing at a wedding forty years gone; a boy falling asleep against his father's shoulder on a boat; an old man laughing so hard he had to sit down on a curb. Whole days, whole decades, moving in the current like fish.

"Well," Pell said softly. His voice came out hushed, which annoyed him, so he cleared his throat and said it again at proper volume. "Well! Gentlemen. Behold the asset."

Brannoc set down the sample case. Sef began unpacking the brass bottles with careful hands. The other collectors fanned out along the basin's rim, and Pell watched each of them pause, just briefly, as the water showed them something—a face, a place, a moment—and watched each of them look away from it quickly, like men touching a stove.

"None of that," Pell said pleasantly. "No drinking without authorization. This is a commercial operation now. Everything gets catalogued, assayed, bottled under seal. We are standing on the founding inventory of the Optimists' next century, and I will not have it drunk by accident."

He knelt at the rim himself, ledger open across his knee, and began, professionally, to appraise.

The water showed him things as he looked. That was its nature, apparently—it offered. It showed him his mother's kitchen, steam on the window, bread on the board. It showed him his first collection run, age nineteen, taking a sewing machine from a woman who cried, and how good he had felt afterward, how certain, how *right*. It showed him the faces of a hundred debtors arranged like weather fronts across twenty years of ledgers.

"Extraordinary," he breathed. "It doesn't just preserve memory. It curates. Do you understand the retail implications? We won't sell water. We'll sell *visits*."

He filled the first bottle himself. The water came up glowing faintly in the glass, and when he held it to the light, he could swear the images kept moving inside it, tiny and distant, like ships on a far horizon. He sealed it with wax, labeled it, set it in the felt with the tenderness of a man handling his firstborn.

Then he did the thing he had been planning since Salt Hollow. The thing any sensible businessman would do. You did not sell a product you had never tested. Quality control was not indulgence; it was diligence.

"One taste," he said, to the room, to the water, to the ancient carvers whose warnings lined the stair behind him. "For the assay. Strictly professional."

He cupped both hands, leaned over the rim, and drank deeply.

* * *

The first thing the water gave him was free.

His mother's kitchen, whole and warm. Steam on the glass. Bread. He was eight years old and nothing had ever gone wrong yet, and he stood in the middle of the memory and wept with gratitude, because he had forgotten how it smelled, and here it was, perfect, permanent, *his*—

Then the interest began.

That was the only way he would ever be able to describe it afterward, and he was a man who described everything in those terms. The water gave him the kitchen, and then it gave him the kitchen *plus*—the day he left home and never wrote. The kitchen plus the look on his mother's face the last time he saw her, which he had never bothered to remember, and which the water remembered for him, in full, from her side of the door. Principal and interest. The memory he wanted, and the memory he owed.

He straightened up, wiping his mouth, and found the chamber had changed. Or he had. The green-gold light seemed closer now, thicker, and the murmur beneath the water was louder—not a crowd through a wall anymore, but a crowd through a door, and the door was thinning.

"Assay complete," he said briskly. His voice echoed oddly. "Note the vintage. Note the clarity. Bottling begins immediately—we'll want—"

The water showed him the widow Anselm.

She had owned a fishing boat, twenty-two years ago, and she had defaulted, and Pell had taken the boat, and she had stood on the dock in the rain while his crew carried it away piece by piece, and she had not cried. She had simply looked at him. He had forgotten her entirely within the month—that was the trick of his trade, forgetting was the service he provided himself, free of charge, unlimited withdrawals.

The water had not forgotten. The water showed him her face in the rain, frame by frame, and then it showed him the twenty-two years after: the rooms she rented, the work her hands did, the way she taught herself to sleep hungry, the night she died quietly in a charity ward with a photograph of the boat on the table beside her. And threaded through all of it, patient as tide, the water showed him what he had never once let himself calculate: the total. The true balance. Twenty-two years of a life, itemized, accruing.

"That's not—" Pell said, and his voice cracked in the middle, which horrified him more than the vision did. "That's not how accounting works. You can't collect on—there's no statute of limitations on—"

The water showed him the sewing machine woman. Then the schoolmaster whose library he'd burned as collateral. Then a boy in Salt Hollow whose father's leg had been broken by collectors working under Pell's charter, and the boy's face at the funeral, and the boy's face thirty years on, grown old around the wound.

Every cruelty he had committed, returned to him from the receiving end. Every smile he had ever smiled, seen from across the desk. And underneath it all, rising like floodwater, the understanding that he had built his entire cheerful empire on a simple clerical error: he had believed that forgetting was forgiveness. That a debt unremembered was a debt dissolved. But the wilderness kept books too, and its books did not close, and its interest compounded in the dark, patiently, at a rate no syndicate could match.

"Stop," Pell said.

The water did not stop. The water was very good at what it did.

He saw his collectors' faces as they would remember him. He saw the Optimists' founding ledger, and the first entry in it, which was a lie. He saw, with terrible clarity, the exact shape of the man he had chosen to be, presented for review, no annotations permitted, no appeals process, the full account laid open in green-gold light—

Somewhere far away, Brannoc was saying his name. Somewhere far away, brass bottles were being dropped, rolling, ringing against stone. The chamber's murmur had become a roar of voices, thousands of them, all of them his creditors, all of them polite, all of them waiting to be paid.

Marcus Pell, who had never feared anything because he had never remembered anything, sat down hard on the ancient stone and put his head in his hands and began, for the first time since he was eight years old in his mother's kitchen, to cry.

And upstream—two days' hard climb behind him, though he didn't know it yet—Freya Zhou stood at the mouth of the gorge, watching the monsoon's first clouds pile up black over the canyon rim, and felt the river pull at his keel like a hand.
