## 28. Drowning in Yesterday

*Pell*

The water was warm.

That was the first thing Pell noticed, and the last ordinary thought he would ever have. The spring was warm, like bathwater, like a nursery, like every comfortable thing he had ever sold to someone who couldn't afford it. He had waded in up to his shins with the survey equipment humming on its tripod behind him and he had thought, *this will bottle beautifully.*

He had drunk deeply because that was what you did at a tasting. Because a man who didn't sample his own product was a man who deserved to go under. Because—though he would never have phrased it this way, not even to himself, especially not to himself—he was tired, and the water looked like rest.

It tasted like nothing. That should have warned him. Nothing in his experience had ever tasted like nothing. Every deal had an aftertaste. Every smile cost something. This water was honest all the way down, and honesty, in Pell's long professional experience, was the most violent substance known to man.

The first memory arrived politely. That was the trick of it—he understood that much, even as it happened, even as the chamber's soft light bent and doubled around him. The source didn't grab. It *invited.* It was the perfect collector: it never raised its voice, never broke a kneecap, never left a mark. It simply opened the ledger and said, *shall we review your account?*

He was twenty-three years old, standing in a rain-slick alley behind a counting house in Merrow's Bend, holding a man's wrist while another Optimist set the fingers, one by one, like tuning an instrument. The man had been crying for his daughter. Not for mercy—for his daughter, because she was sick, because the loan had been for her medicine, because the medicine money was now the debt money and there was no more medicine. Pell remembered smiling at him. He remembered the exact shape of the smile, how he'd practiced it in a cracked mirror that morning until it sat on his face like it grew there. He remembered thinking: *he signed. Nobody made him sign.*

The water held him in that alley. It did not move on.

"Next," Pell said aloud, into the empty chamber, in the firm voice he used with subordinates.

The source was not a subordinate.

He was forty-one, in a widow's parlor, explaining—with genuine warmth, that was the horror of it, he had *liked* her—that her husband's policy contained a clause she really ought to have read, and that the boat she was sitting in the shadow of now belonged, regrettably, to the syndicate. She had served him tea. He had drunk the tea. He remembered complimenting the tea. He remembered the small sound she made when he stood to leave, not a word, just a sound, the sound of a woman discovering that courtesy could be a form of theft.

"Next."

He was thirty-six, and it was raining, and a boy of maybe nineteen was swimming after the skiff Pell's launch had cut loose from its mooring, because the boy's father owed eleven marks and the mooring fee was leverage, and Pell had watched from the wheelhouse with his hands folded, watching the boy swim, watching the storm come in off the shelf, doing the arithmetic. Eleven marks against one boy. The ledger had balanced. The boy had not surfaced.

Pell tried to look away. There was no away. The chamber was small and bright and everywhere, and the memory was not playing *at* him—it was playing *as* him. He was inside his own hands. He could feel the tea going cool. He could feel the rain. He could feel how easy it had all been, how light his heart had been, how he'd slept afterward like a paid-off note.

That was the part the source would not let him skip. Not the cruelties themselves—he could have survived those, he told himself, he was a professional—but the *ease.* The warmth of his own bed. The breakfast he'd enjoyed the morning after the boy drowned. The joke he'd told at lunch about swimmers.

"No," Pell said, and his voice came out small, a clerk's voice, a debtor's voice. "No, I don't accept these terms."

The source, being perfectly fair, reminded him that he had accepted far worse terms for far less consideration, and that he had offered them to people who were drowning too.

* * *

*Freya*

Freya heard Pell screaming before they reached the chamber—a high, continuous sound with no words in it, the sound of a man being read his own life aloud.

They came up the last stair together, Mara ahead, Cass behind them with her sidearm drawn and lowered twice already, and stopped at the lip of the cavern. The spring-lit water glowed the way it always glowed, patient and golden, images drifting in it like leaves. And in the middle of it, standing knee-deep with his fine coat soaked to the elbows, was Pell—screaming at nobody, at everybody, at himself.

"He drank," Freya said.

"Of course he drank." Mara's voice was flat. "He'd have made us drink if it improved margins."

The collectors were already gone. Freya had passed two of them on the stairs below, white-faced, dropping equipment as they ran—one of them had actually apologized to him, which told Freya everything about what they'd seen. Whatever the source had shown them through their boss's eyes, they wanted no further accounting.

Pell staggered, caught himself, screamed again. And Freya saw it happen—the moment the recursion tightened. Pell fell to his knees in the shallows and began to beg, but not to them, not to the water. To someone in the water. A boy, maybe. A widow. Himself, at twenty-three, in a cracked mirror.

"He'll drown," Freya said. "Standing up. In shin-deep water."

"That would be tidy," Mara said.

It would be. Freya felt the pull of it, the clean arithmetic of it—the man who had spent his whole career letting other people drown, drowning in three inches of perfect memory while the people he ruined watched. There was a justice so neat it could be printed in a pamphlet.

But Freya had knelt in that same water yesterday. He had seen his crew, and he had learned what the source actually did: it didn't punish. It *remembered.* All of it. Even the parts you'd built a career on never looking at. And he knew—if you stayed in it alone, it didn't stop. It couldn't stop. It was a spring, not a judge. It would hold Pell in that alley until the tide of his own heart went out.

Mara was already moving. She didn't look at Freya for permission, which he was grateful for, because he wasn't sure he'd have given it, and he was more grateful still that she hadn't needed it.

"Help me," she said, and grabbed Pell under the arms.

He fought them. Of course he fought them—he was reliving every hand that had ever been laid on him without consent, and theirs counted too, and he screamed *don't touch me, don't touch me* in a voice that had never once said those words to anyone he'd hurt. His heel caught Freya across the shin. His elbow found Mara's mouth. She spat blood into the glowing water and kept pulling.

"You don't get to die here," she told him, hauling, her voice cracking with effort and something older than effort. "You don't get the ending. You don't get to make it *tidy.* You're going to live with all of it. Every single day. That's the sentence."

Between them they dragged him out of the spring and up onto the stone, and the moment his skin left the water he collapsed—not asleep, nothing so kind—shivering, eyes open, mouth moving through memories no one else could see. But breathing. Breathing on his own.

Cass stood over him with her weapon finally holstered, looking down at the most feared man on the coast, drooling softly on ancient stone.

"Well," she said. "There's a report I'm not writing."

* * *

*Mara*

Her hands wouldn't stop shaking, and it wasn't the blood on her lip.

She had wanted this—she admitted it to herself now, kneeling beside Pell, checking his pulse the way you'd check a trap line—for nine years she had wanted the men responsible for her father to suffer exactly this: to know everything, to feel everything, to be unable to look away. And here it was, delivered. Here was suffering beyond anything she could have designed, and she had pulled him out of it anyway.

She kept waiting to regret it. The regret didn't come. What came instead was her father's voice, or the memory of it, worn smooth by nine years of handling: *a chart is a promise, little fish. You draw the coast as it is, not as you'd like it.* The truth was the truth. Pell had drowned in himself, and she had hauled him out, and both facts belonged on the map.

His eyes found hers. They were wrong—unfocused, swimming—but some pilot-light of the man was home behind them, and he whispered something. She leaned close.

"...didn't sign," he breathed. "None of them... I made them think they signed..."

"I know," Mara said, which was mercy of a kind, and also true, and also the worst thing she could have said, because his face crumpled and the whispering started again, faster now, the ledger reopening.

She sat back on her heels. Above them, through the chimney of the gorge, the light had changed—the flat green-gray of a sky loading itself with weather. Somewhere far south, the monsoon was finding its sea legs.

"We can't carry him," Freya said quietly, crouching beside her. "Not through the gorge. Not before landfall."

"No," Mara agreed. "But we're not leaving him here either."

She looked at the old man's face—at Tomás's annotations waiting in her pack, at the sluice gates somewhere deeper in the mountain, at the water that remembered everyone—and made the decision the way her father would have: quickly, honestly, and without asking anyone's permission.

"The antechamber," she said. "The dry one, above the flood line. We drag him there, we wedge the door, we leave water and the truth. If he lives, he lives with it. If the mountain takes him—" she shrugged, and hated herself a little, and let it stand, "—then the mountain does what the Survey Office wouldn't. Quickly."

From the floor of the cavern, catatonic and ruined, Pell laughed.

It was a terrible sound—cracked, wet, half-drowned—but it was unmistakably a laugh, and between the broken syllables came words, addressed to no one, to the air, to the memory of a mirror:

"Clever... clever little fish... he called you that..."

Mara went cold. Her father had never met Pell. Her father had died alone in a cave with a chart and a warning.

Unless.

Unless the source remembered everyone who ever came near it. Unless the water held her father too—his last days, his last thoughts, the things he'd said to the dark—and unless Pell, drowning in yesterday, had just been shown a dying cartographer among all the other accounts in the book.

"What did you see?" she demanded, grabbing his collar. "What did the water show you? *What did my father say?*"

Pell smiled up at her—the practiced smile, the cracked-mirror smile, dissolving even as he wore it—and whispered four words that rearranged the floor of her chest:

"He forgave the river."

Then his eyes rolled back, and the monsoon's first thunder rolled in over the gorge, and somewhere below them, deep in the mountain, the spring went on remembering—patiently, perfectly, and for what Mara suddenly understood were its final days—everything at all.
