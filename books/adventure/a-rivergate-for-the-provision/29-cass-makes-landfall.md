## 29. Cass Makes Landfall

The rain found Cass Okonjo forty minutes before the coast did.

She was still two ridges inland, hauling the demolition satchel up a goat track that the Survey Office's maps insisted was a road, when the first band of the monsoon came through the gorge like a landlord changing the locks. It didn't fall so much as arrive—a gray wall of water moving sideways, filling the air, erasing the horizon, erasing the track, erasing, for a long disorienting moment, the distinction between up and down.

She stood in it and let it soak her, because there was no point doing otherwise, and because some stubborn part of her wanted to feel it. The first rain of the season. The rain the old charts had promised for next week, and the week after, and the week after that—right up until the satellites had redrawn the schedule without consulting anyone.

*Early,* the forecasters kept saying, in the tone of people betrayed by a colleague. *Four days early.*

Cass had stopped being surprised by anything that moved faster than the paperwork.

She adjusted the satchel strap across her shoulder. Twenty-two kilograms of shaped charges, detonators, and official intent, wrapped in waterproof canvas and stamped with a serial number that would, in a just universe, have been the last thing she ever carried for the Survey Office. It rode against her hip like a sleeping animal. Heavy. Warm, almost. Patient.

She had burned her orders three days ago in a ferryman's brazier, and the ashes had gone into the river, and the river had carried them somewhere downstream where they could do no harm. That should have felt like freedom. It mostly felt like carrying a heavier bag. The charges didn't care that the paper was gone. The charges were still orders—orders you could hold, orders with mass, orders that would do exactly what they were told the moment she asked them to.

The question she had been avoiding for three days and forty minutes of rain was the same question: *asked by whom?*

She climbed.

* * *

The canyon took her in like a throat taking a pill.

She had studied the survey photographs a hundred times—the narrows, the stair cut in stone, the falls that the locals' legends insisted flowed upward, which no hydrologist had ever confirmed because no hydrologist had ever been fool enough to come look. In the photographs it had been dry, sun-bleached, geological. In the rain it was a living thing. Water poured off every ledge. The stair itself had become a waterfall in slow sections, and Cass climbed it on hands and knees in places, the demolition satchel banging against her spine, her sidearm a cold weight on the other hip like a counterargument.

Twice she stopped and simply breathed.

Not from exhaustion, though there was that. From the sense—unscientific, unprofessional, and absolutely undeniable—that the gorge was watching her climb. Not hostile. *Assessing.* The way a bank assesses a loan applicant. The way a border official assesses a passport.

She had grown up in a river town herself, a muddy place called Dolo Bend where the water rose every year and took something and gave something back and nobody made legends about it because everybody was too busy sandbagging. Her grandmother used to say the river keeps books. Cass had thought it was a figure of speech. She had built an entire career on the assumption that it was a figure of speech—water was water, stone was stone, and anything else was folklore, and folklore was what you wrote in the appendix.

The gorge did not feel like an appendix.

Near the top of the stair, she found the carvings.

She had seen them in the photographs—friezes worn shallow by centuries of flood, catalogued by some junior archivist as *decorative motifs, possibly ritual, function unknown.* In the rain, with her lamp held close, she could see what the archivist had missed or had declined to see. Figures climbing. Figures kneeling. Figures with their hands open, offering something small and invisible to the stone. And along the base of the frieze, repeated like a refrain, a line of glyphs that the Survey Office's own comparative tables translated as:

*PAY HERE WHAT YOU CANNOT CARRY FURTHER.*

Cass stood in the downpour and read it three times.

Then she laughed—a short, ugly, exhausted sound that the rain swallowed whole—because she had climbed a thousand stairs carrying twenty-two kilograms of other people's intentions, and the stone had known it before she did.

"Fine," she told the gorge. "Fine. I hear you. I'm working on it."

The gorge, being stone, said nothing. But the falls ahead—she was almost sure, and she would never put this in any report, and she was no longer entirely sure there would be any report—seemed to fall a little more gently as she passed.

* * *

The last chamber opened without warning.

One moment she was squeezing through a cleft with water sheeting down her collar, and the next the walls simply stepped back, and the rain stopped mattering, and she was standing in a cavern that the Survey Office had spent sixty years and eleven sealed files insisting did not exist.

She had prepared herself for it. She had read Tomás Ilesanmi's recovered field notes, she had interviewed the villagers of the middle reach until the elders had grown tired of her and one of them had finally said, with the weary precision of a man describing weather, *you want to know what it looks like. It looks like remembering.* She had told herself it would be a spring in a cave. Water in rock. Geology with a reputation.

It was not a spring in a cave.

The water lay in a basin at the chamber's heart, wide as a courtyard, lit from beneath by a light that had no source she could find—soft, gold-green, patient, like lamplight through honey. And the water was *full*. Not of fish, not of silt. Of images. Faces moved in it the way weather moves across a valley—slow, layered, overlapping. She saw a woman laughing at someone out of frame. She saw a boy on a dock. She saw a hundred small ordinary moments, all of them happening at once, all of them *kept*, the way a good clerk keeps records: completely, without judgment, forever.

Cass stood at the threshold with rain running off her and did not move for a long time.

Every instinct she had trained into herself over nineteen years of service said *document this.* Her camera was in her pack. Her sample vials were in her pack. Her orders—burned, but present, the way amputated limbs are present—said *recover, then destroy.* And underneath all of it, older than her training, her grandmother's voice said: *the river keeps books, Cass. Be careful which side of the ledger you stand on when it balances.*

"Okonjo."

The voice came from the far side of the basin, and her sidearm was out of its holster before her brain finished processing the sound, because nineteen years of service also trained that into her.

Freya Zhou stood up from the rocks near the water's edge.

He looked terrible. That was her first coherent professional observation. Soaked, hollow-eyed, a fresh cut over one eyebrow, his jacket torn at the shoulder where something had very recently tried to remove the shoulder. He stood between her and the water with his empty hands slightly raised—not surrender, she noted. *Position.* He was planted there like a man who had decided exactly where he would fall.

Behind him, half in the shadow of an outcropping, a second figure: a woman, dark-skinned, rigid, one arm protectively around a third figure slumped against the stone.

Cass knew the third figure. Everyone in the coastal trade knew the smile even before they knew the name. Pell. The Optimists' most celebrated collector, the man whose courtesy was a byword and whose ledgers were a byword for something else. He sat against the rock with his hands in his lap, palms up, and he was smiling.

That was the wrong part. That was the part that made the hair on Cass's arms stand up under her wet sleeves. He was smiling, and his eyes were moving—quick, darting, tracking things that were not in the chamber—and his lips were shaping words, over and over, words with no sound in them, like a man reading a letter that only he could see and that never ended.

"What happened to him?" Cass asked. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later.

"He drank," Freya said.

"From the spring."

"From the spring."

Cass looked at the water. At the faces in it. At Pell, smiling his way through whatever the water was showing him, forever, at compound interest. She thought of the file she had read in the Survey Office archive—Tomás Ilesanmi's sealed file, the one with the deputy director's signature at the bottom, the same signature on her own orders. *Abandoned subject. Non-cooperative. Asset value: negative.*

Asset value: negative. That was what the Service had written about a man who had found this and refused to sell it.

"Zhou," she said. "Step away from the water."

"No."

It was not defiant. That was what unsettled her. It was the tone of a man stating the time of day. He didn't move. The woman behind him—Mara Ilesanmi, it had to be, she had the father's field sketches in the set of her jaw—didn't move either.

Cass raised the sidearm.

It was a service weapon. She had carried it for nineteen years and had drawn it eleven times and fired it twice, both times at animals, both times with regret. It was a tool for making people do things, and she had always understood that about it, and she had always told herself that the making-people-do-things was the whole job. The Survey Office drew a perimeter and the perimeter held because somewhere on the perimeter was a person with a sidearm, and the person with the sidearm was the law, and the law was the orders, and the orders came from above, and above was the structure, and the structure was the reason the coasts had charts at all.

She sighted on Freya Zhou's chest. He didn't flinch. He looked at her with an expression she had seen once before, years ago, on a warden named Adjei who had caught a poacher feeding his family from a protected estuary, and who had stood there for a long moment with his weapon half-raised before lowering it and writing a report that ruined his career and saved the family. She had been young then. She had thought Adjei was weak.

She thought now that Adjei had simply read a different document. One the Service hadn't issued.

"Zhou," she said, "I am authorized—"

"You burned your authorization," Mara Ilesanmi said. Her voice was hoarse, and there was something in it that wasn't hostility—something almost like pity. "We heard about it from the ferry woman. The whole river heard about it. You've been carrying those charges up a mountain with no orders for three days, Warden. So who exactly is authorized?"

The rain they had left behind made itself heard at the chamber mouth—a low, rising roar, the gorge filling. The monsoon, making landfall in earnest now, four days early, right on schedule for everything that had ever gone wrong in Cass Okonjo's career.

She stood there. The weapon did not waver—her hands were steady, they had always been steady—but something behind her eyes wavered, and she let it, because she had been letting it waver since the archive, since the ferry brazier, since the frieze on the stair that said *pay here what you cannot carry further.*

The chamber was quiet except for the water and the distant roar and Pell's silent, smiling recitation.

And Cass Okonjo, nineteen years of service, looked at the spring that the world's most cheerful predators had crossed a wilderness to bottle, at the man standing in front of it with empty hands, at the daughter of a murdered cartographer standing behind him, at the demolition charges on her hip that were still, in every way that mattered, an order—

—and lowered the sidearm.

She holstered it. The sound of the clasp was very small and very loud.

"Both of you," she said, "get away from the water. Not because I'm ordering you. Because I've read the file on what it does, and because that man over there is what it does, and because the monsoon is filling the gorge behind me and we are all about to have a much larger problem than each other."

Freya Zhou lowered his hands slowly. He studied her face the way he'd probably study a sky about to turn—looking for the change in the weather.

"You're not going to seal it," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I was going to seal it," Cass said. "Sixty years of files say seal it. My orders said seal it. And then I read whose signature was on the orders, and I read what they did to Tomás Ilesanmi, and I climbed a stair that told me to put down what I can't carry." She unshouldered the demolition satchel and set it on the stone between them, deliberately, where everyone could see it. "So no. I'm not going to seal it. But I'll tell you what I am going to do, and you're not going to like it either."

Mara's eyes narrowed. "Which is?"

Cass looked at the spring—at the faces moving in it, patient and golden and utterly indifferent to jurisdiction—and felt nineteen years of service settle over her like the rain had, cold and total and, in the end, simply weather.

"I haven't decided yet," she said. "And I suggest none of us decide anything until we figure out how we're getting out of a gorge that's about to be a river. Because I came up that stair on the dry side of a storm that isn't dry anymore, and the water's coming, and it's coming *fast*."

The roar at the chamber mouth rose a register, as if in agreement.

Freya Zhou looked at the demolition satchel, then at Cass, and something that was almost a smile crossed his wrecked face.

"Then I guess," he said, "we'd better have that argument quickly."
