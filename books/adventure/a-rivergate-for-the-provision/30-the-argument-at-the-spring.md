## 30. The Argument at the Spring

The water held the light the way a cupped hand holds rain.

Freya stood at its edge and did not touch it. He had learned, somewhere in the last hundred miles of backward river, that there were things you could want so badly they stopped being wants and became weather—and this spring was all of them at once. His crew moved in the current below: Okafor laughing with his head tipped back, Sefu arguing about knots, little Wren skipping stones that never sank. Whole. Forgiving. Waiting.

He made himself look away.

"Okonjo." He said it quietly, because the chamber carried sound like a church. "Put the charges down."

Cass Okonjo stood ten paces off, sidearm holstered, demolition satchel still slung across her chest like a bandolier she'd forgotten how to wear. Monsoon light came down through the fissure above in long gray blades, and rain had begun to find its way through, spotting the stone with dark coins.

"You don't give me orders," she said. "I'm still—"

"Are you?" Freya asked. "You burned your orders three days ago."

"I burned *one* order." Her jaw worked. "There's a difference. There's always a difference. That's what law is—the difference between one thing and another thing, written down so nobody has to guess."

"And what's written down now?"

She didn't answer. That was answer enough.

Mara crouched at the water's rim, not drinking, not touching, just watching her own reflection waver over the images beneath—her father's hands, younger than she'd ever known them, drawing lines on vellum. She had wept here an hour ago, or a year ago; time in the chamber ran like the river, whichever direction it pleased. Now she stood, wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and turned to face them both with the expression of a woman who had run out of patience for men with plans.

"Before either of you decides anything," she said, "you should know I've already decided."

"Mara—" Freya started.

"No. Listen." She pointed at the spring. "That's my father down there. Nine years I thought he'd abandoned us. Nine years I built a man out of spite and called it him. And he was *here*, doing this—" her voice cracked, and she hated it, and kept going anyway, "—doing *this*, protecting it, planning to bury it, hoping some stranger would finish the work because he couldn't trust his own office. Because his own office left him in a cave to die." She looked at Cass. "Your Survey Office. Your chain of command. The same signature on his death warrant and your demolition permit."

Cass flinched. Freya saw it land, saw the warden carry it without setting it down, the way she seemed to carry everything.

"So no," Mara said. "Nobody is selling this. Nobody is studying this. And nobody—" she looked at Freya then, and her voice went low and terrible, "—nobody is blowing it up and calling it protection. We flood it. My father's way. His gates, his mechanism, his choice. We finish his work and we get out before the monsoon finishes us."

* * *

Cass set the satchel down on the stone, deliberately, where everyone could see it, and sat beside it like a woman guarding a prisoner that happened to be herself.

"Say I agree," she said. "Say we drown it. Walk me through what we've done." She ticked points off on her fingers, a bureaucrat even now, even here. "A hydrological anomaly that reverses a coastal river. A spring that preserves memory—demonstrably, catastrophically, as our friend Pell can attest." She nodded toward the far wall, where Pell lay wrapped in a survey tarp, breathing in shallow, fluttering sips, his eyes moving behind closed lids like a man watching something unbearable. "An entire watershed whose behavior violates every model the Office maintains. And we erase it. No samples. No records. No peer review. Just four witnesses and a story nobody will believe."

"The villagers will believe it," Mara said.

"The villagers are one village."

"The ferrywoman will believe it."

"One woman who takes payment in *memories*, Ilesanmi. You see how this sounds from outside?"

"I see how it *is* from inside," Mara said. "Which used to be your job."

Cass laughed—a short, unhappy bark that echoed off the vaulted stone. "My job. Right. Let me tell you about my job." She got to her feet, and the chamber seemed to lean in. "I joined the Survey Office at nineteen because my mother's village lost its well to a logging concern, and the Office sent a surveyor who mapped the aquifer, published the data, and saved the water. Data, Freya. Records. Peer-reviewed, boring, beautiful *records*. That's what beat the loggers. Not a legend. Not a secret kept wild. A file number."

"And the same Office," Freya said quietly, "left Tomás Ilesanmi to rot in a cave."

"Yes." She said it flat, unflinching. "And if I seal this place with charges, I'm the same Office. And if I drown it with gates, I'm the same Office wearing a different hat. And if I walk away, I'm the same Office that walked away from Tomás." She spread her hands. "Every road out of here runs through the same building. So don't tell me the world doesn't deserve to know. The world deserves to know *everything*. That's the whole point of the world. It's the only inheritance we have that doesn't rot."

"Then why did you burn your orders?" Freya asked.

The question hung there. Rain came harder through the fissure, and somewhere above, thunder rolled down the canyon like furniture being dragged across a ceiling.

"Because the orders were wrong," Cass said finally. "Not because knowing is wrong. Because *they* were wrong. There's a difference." She was repeating herself and knew it and didn't care. "Bottle this water, and within five years every rich coward on the coast drinks immortality while the source dies of overuse. Blow it up, and maybe the Syndicate digs it out anyway, because Pell's friends hold leases on half this coastline. But publish it—map it properly, guard it openly, make it a matter of public record with public wardens—" 

"With wardens like the ones who abandoned my father," Mara said.

"With wardens better than that," Cass said. "Wardens like whoever comes next. That's the bet. That's the only bet there ever is. You don't protect a thing by hiding it. You protect it by making too many people love it to lose it."

* * *

Freya listened to them go back and forth and felt something old and familiar rising in his chest—the storm-chaser's sickness, the itch to be the one who called it. *Chase the cell, ignore the gauge.* He had buried two friends under that voice. He would not bury a third argument the same way.

"Can I say my piece?" he said.

"Finally," said Mara.

He walked to the edge of the spring and crouched, careful not to let his shadow fall across the water, though he suspected the water didn't need light to see him.

"Down there," he said, "that's Okafor. That's Sefu. That's Wren, who was twenty-three years old and afraid of eels and came out anyway because I told her the data was worth it." His voice held steady, which surprised him. "I've spent six years paying interest on that day. Real interest, to real monsters—" he glanced at Pell's tarp, "—and worse, the kind you can't invoice. And yesterday I stood here and the water showed me they forgave me. Do you understand what that did to me? Six years of self-hatred, cleared in an afternoon. Like a debt."

"Sounds like an argument for keeping it," Cass said.

"It sounds like an argument for exactly why we can't." Freya stood. "Because it's too good. Okonjo, listen to me—it's *too good*. Nobody walks away from this. I nearly didn't. Mara nearly didn't. Pell *didn't*, and look at him. You want to publish it? Fine. Publish it, and you don't get wardens who love it too much to lose it. You get pilgrims. You get the grieving, by the million, because grief is the one crop every nation grows. They'll come here to see their dead, and they'll drink, and they'll stay, and the source will drink them right back until there's nothing left but a cavern full of people living yesterday." He shook his head. "Some knowledge should stay wild. Some knowledge should stay *unmapped*. I'm a cartographer's problem, not a cartographer—I know exactly what happens when you put a line around a thing. The line becomes a claim. The claim becomes a fence. The fence becomes a tollbooth."

"That's cynicism dressed as wisdom," Cass said.

"It's experience dressed as scars," Freya said. "Ask my crew. Oh—wait."

Mara winced. Even Cass did.

"Too far," Mara said quietly.

"Yeah." Freya rubbed his face. "Sorry. That was—that was the debt talking, not me." He took a breath. "But the point stands. Tomás found this place decades ago. Smarter than me, more honest than any of us, and he had the Office behind him—or he thought he did. And what did he conclude? Seal it. Bury it. Build the gates himself and wait for someone to pull them. The best man any of us ever met looked straight into this water and chose to drown it. Maybe we should have the humility to take the dead man's answer."

"Or maybe," Cass said, "he was one grieving man alone in the dark, and grief makes terrible policy."

"Grief makes *all* policy," Mara said. "Yours included. Yours especially."

* * *

It might have gone on for hours. Later, none of them could say how long the argument lasted, because the chamber kept its own time and lent none out. They went around and around—records against wilderness, fences against floods, the dead man's answer against the living woman's bet—and the rain thickened overhead, and the thunder came closer, and none of them noticed the water.

It was Pell who noticed.

He sat up under his tarp with a sound like a hinge that hadn't moved in years, and his eyes—red-rimmed, scoured, ancient—went past all three of them to the base of the far wall, where the spring's overflow channel ran toward the gorge.

"It's climbing," he rasped. His voice was ruined, sanded down to gravel by whatever he'd lived inside the water. "The overflow. Look at it."

They looked.

The channel that had been a trickle when they arrived was now a rushing ribbon, knee-deep and rising, brown with the first wash of the plateau above. And beyond the chamber mouth, down the stair-cut canyon, the river's voice had changed pitch—the deep, patient murmur replaced by something with teeth in it.

"The monsoon," Cass said, and pulled out her battered field barometer, and read it, and read it again. "Landfall's early. Twelve hours ahead of the revised estimate. The whole catchment's dumping into the gorge at once." She looked up, and for the first time since Freya had known her, Warden Cass Okonjo looked genuinely afraid—not of the water, but of the arithmetic. "This chamber sits forty feet above the normal river level. In a surge event, the gorge backs up through the lower galleries. We have—" she did the math with visible reluctance, "—maybe an hour. Less if the surge is dirty."

"Then decide fast," Mara said.

"No," Freya said. "Decide *now*. All of it, now, because there won't be a second sitting." He turned to Cass. "Last chance, Warden. Charges or gates. Publication or erasure. Pick."

Cass looked at the satchel. At the spring. At the tarp-wrapped ruin of the Optimists' finest collector. At the two people standing between her and her own chain of command, both of whom she should, by every regulation she'd memorized, be arresting.

And the gorge roared again, louder, hungry.

"I pick neither," she said.

"Oh, wonderful," said Mara.

"I pick *both*." Cass was already moving, hauling the satchel open, pulling charge cylinders out and lining them up on the stone with brisk, practiced snaps. "The gates flood the galleries and redirect the spring deep—Tomás's design, fine, it's elegant, I'll grant the dead man his elegance. But the gates alone leave the upper chamber intact. Dry. Mappable. Findable, once the flood passes and somebody with a sonar rig comes looking." She pressed a detonator into Freya's hand and kept the charges. "So we do both. Gates first, to send the water where it can't be followed. Then charges at the throat of the inlet, timed to collapse after the flood does the rough work. The spring goes deep, the chamber goes shut, and the record goes—" she hesitated, and Freya watched her choose, watched the word cost her, "—the record goes unwritten. One sealed site, location unknown, cause listed as natural collapse during monsoon surge. I'll sign it myself. It'll be the first honest lie in Survey Office history."

Mara stared at her. "You're destroying your career."

"My career," Cass said, feeding wire, "was signed by the same man who killed your father. I've been meaning to return the paperwork."

Freya looked down at the detonator in his palm, cold and ordinary as a river stone. Below him, in the water, Okafor raised a hand—not accusing, never accusing—just waving, the way you wave from a train platform, the way you wave at someone going somewhere you can't follow.

"Not yet," Freya told him softly. "Thirty seconds. Give me thirty seconds of forever first."

The gorge answered with a sound like the sky splitting along a seam, and the first true wall of the monsoon came down the canyon toward them, and the overflow channel jumped its banks and went licking across the chamber floor toward the spring, toward the charges, toward the four of them standing in the last dry room of a legend.

"To the gates," Freya shouted. "All of us. *Now.*"

And the water began to rise.
