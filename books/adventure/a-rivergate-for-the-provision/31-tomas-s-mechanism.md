## 31. Tomás's Mechanism

The argument at the spring had ended the way all good arguments end: with everyone too tired to keep winning.

Cass had gone back up the stair cut in stone to watch for the demolition convoy and the monsoon both, which were arriving in a dead heat. Pell lay where they'd dragged him, wrapped in a survey tarp like a parcel nobody wanted to sign for, breathing in small, bewildered breaths. And Mara sat on the lip of the spring with her boots off, her feet in water that showed her things she'd chosen not to look at, staring down into it the way you stare at an old photograph of someone who's still alive.

Freya sat beside her and unrolled Tomás's chart across his knees.

"Tell me if this is a bad idea," he said.

"It's a bad idea," Mara said, without looking up. "Everything we've done since Salt Hollow has been a bad idea. It's been working anyway. Keep going."

He'd been studying the second layer of ink since the night he'd found it beneath the lamplight—Tomás's hidden annotations, pressed so fine under the surface that you needed exactly the wrong kind of light to see them, which was very much the sort of joke a careful man would make. The coordinates ran along the eastern wall of the cavern. But it was the symbols that had kept Freya awake, when he'd bothered sleeping at all: little glyphs like gate valves, like sluices, like a hand-drawn diagram of something meant to open and let a great deal of water go somewhere else.

A cartographer didn't draw machinery by accident. A cartographer drew machinery because he'd stood in front of it.

"He came here before," Freya said. "Years ago. He found the source, and then he went home and spent decades drawing a way to kill it."

Mara finally looked up. In the spring-light her face did something complicated—the grief and the pride fighting for the same ground and, for once, calling a truce.

"That sounds like him," she said. "My mother used to say he never met a door he didn't want to know how it locked."

"Well." Freya tapped the glyph nearest the eastern wall. "Let's go find out if he left us the key."

* * *

The eastern wall of the cavern was limestone the color of old teeth, veined with mineral streaks that glowed faintly where the spring-light touched them. The water here ran shallow over shelves of stone, and every shelf held its own small memory—a fish turning, a raindrop, a child's hand—and Freya had learned not to look directly at them, the way you learn not to look directly at the sun or at your ex-wife's new husband.

The first glyph from the chart was carved into the rock at shoulder height: a circle bisected by a line, worn smooth by hands. Not one pair of hands. Many pairs, over many years, polished the way a church door gets polished.

"Somebody's been using these gates," Mara said quietly.

"Somebody built them," Freya corrected. He set his palm against the circle and pushed.

Nothing happened. He pushed harder. Then, feeling foolish, he pulled.

Stone grated somewhere deep inside the wall—a sound like a giant clearing its throat after a long silence—and a seam appeared in the rock, hairline-thin, running floor to ceiling. Beyond it, darkness, and the smell of cold moving air.

Mara had her knife out before he'd finished flinching. "You could have warned me."

"I could have," Freya agreed. "But your face is better than any alarm."

"I'm going to remember that."

"You say that like it's a threat. We're standing next to a spring that remembers everything forever. You want to be careful what you commit."

She almost smiled. Almost. It was progress; he'd take it.

They widened the seam together, prying with the boat iron Freya had carried the whole way upstream on the grounds that a man without a boat iron is just a pedestrian with opinions. The gap opened onto a passage barely wide enough for their shoulders, sloping gently downward, the walls scored with tool marks—old ones, patient ones, the work of a man who'd had nothing but time and a reason.

Lamps lit. They went in.

* * *

The passage opened, forty paces on, into a gallery that stopped them both mid-step.

It wasn't a chamber so much as a throat—a vaulted channel of dressed stone, wide as a road, running away into the dark in both directions. Along its length stood the gates: three of them, massive slabs of fitted granite riding in channels cut into the walls, each one rigged with a mechanism of bronze chains, counterweights, and worm-gears that had no business surviving fifty years underground and yet looked ready to be cranked that morning. Someone had greased them. Recently. The fat was still soft under Freya's thumb.

And on the wall beside the nearest gate, in a hand Freya now knew as well as his own signature, a message had been chiseled into the stone.

Not carved quickly. Carved carefully, the letters deep enough to outlast the man who cut them:

**TO WHOEVER THE RIVER BRINGS —**

**THE SPRING CANNOT BE KILLED. IT CAN ONLY BE SENT SOMEWHERE NO ONE WILL FOLLOW. OPEN ALL THREE GATES IN ORDER. THE FLOOD WILL TAKE THE CAVERN AND TURN THE WATER DOWN INTO THE DEEP FAULT. THE LEGEND DROWNS. THE MEMORY GOES WHERE MEMORY BELONGS — UNDER EVERYTHING, HOLDING EVERYTHING UP.**

**I COULD NOT DO IT MYSELF. I HAD LOOKED TOO LONG. I TOLD THEM I WOULD SELL IT, SO THEY WOULD LET ME LEAVE, AND THEN I SPENT THIRTY YEARS BUILDING THIS AND TELLING NO ONE, NOT EVEN MY WIFE. ESPECIALLY NOT MY WIFE. SHE WOULD HAVE HELPED, AND THEN I WOULD HAVE HAD TO ADMIT WHAT I WAS DOING, AND I WAS NOT BRAVE ENOUGH FOR THAT.**

**IF YOU ARE READING THIS, YOU ARE BRAVER THAN ME, OR MORE DESPERATE, WHICH IS MOSTLY THE SAME THING.**

**CHECK THE COUNTERWEIGHTS BEFORE YOU COMMIT. AND WHATEVER YOU LOVE — SAY IT FIRST. THERE IS NO TIME AFTER.**

**T.I.**

The silence afterward was the kind that has weight.

Mara put her hand flat against the chiseled letters, tracing them the way you'd touch a face through glass. She didn't cry. Freya had learned by now that Mara's crying was a private country with strict borders, and he was honored simply to be allowed near the customs post.

"Thirty years," she said finally. Her voice came out level, mostly. "He knew. All those years he was missing—he wasn't lost. He was *working*. Down here. Alone."

"He came back," Freya said. "At the end. The tide caught him in the cave at Cape Verren. He was coming back to check his own work."

"Or to change his mind."

"No." Freya shook his head. "I saw him, Mara. Whatever he'd come to do, he'd already decided. He gave me the chart because he wanted this finished. He just couldn't finish it himself." He paused. "He said the source remembers you back. That looking too long—" He gestured vaguely at the dark, at the whole impossible architecture of the last weeks. "Maybe he'd looked too long to pull the lever. Maybe you can't drown a thing you've seen yourself in."

Mara was quiet a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had that dry edge that Freya had privately begun to think of as the family resemblance.

"So my father spent three decades engineering the destruction of the most important discovery in human history, lied to an entire government about it, hid the evidence in invisible ink, and died holding the only copy." She exhaled. "And people ask why I'm difficult."

"I've never once asked why you're difficult. I've made peace with it. It's one of your best features."

"Gentle humor," she said. "That's what you call it."

"It's what the style guide calls it."

* * *

They worked.

There was nothing else for it and, Freya discovered, nothing else he wanted. The mechanisms were beautiful in the way that only stubborn things are beautiful—every chain link forged and fitted by hand, every gear tooth filed to mesh, the counterweight shafts lined with lead shot poured around scrap iron because lead was expensive and Tomás Ilesanmi had apparently been neither rich nor wasteful but simply *relentless*. Freya found a tin of tallow grease behind the third gate, half full, and beside it a clay lamp still bearing a thumbprint in its dried soot.

He didn't point it out to Mara. Some fingerprints are not for sharing.

The gates themselves were simple once you understood the logic—or rather, they were simple the way a guillotine is simple. Each slab was held raised by a counterweight in a vertical shaft. Release the pawl, and the weight dropped, and the gate fell, and whatever was on the other side of the gate became two different things separated by granite. Open all three in order—east, center, west—and the flood path Tomás had cut would carry the spring's water down into the deep fault below the gorge, drowning the cavern, collapsing the delicate plumbing of the place, sending the memory-water down where it would seep through miles of stone and arrive nowhere, changed into ordinary water by the journey.

"The legend drowns," Freya read aloud again, because saying it helped. "The memory goes where memory belongs."

"And the debt collectors get nothing," Mara added. "And the Survey Office gets nothing. And Pell gets—" She glanced back toward the main cavern, toward the tarp-wrapped shape of the man who had tried to bottle forever. "Pell gets whatever happens to people who drink from a spring that's about to be very upset."

"Do we feel bad about that?"

"I'm workshopping it."

Freya tested the east gate's release pawl. It moved stiffly, then freely, oiled by thirty years of maintenance visits he was only now beginning to imagine: a thin man with a surveyor's squint, rowing up a backward river alone, year after year, telling no one, greasing chains in the dark. Freya thought of his own thirty years of storms, of all the data chased and the people lost, and felt something turn over in his chest—not guilt, exactly. Kinship. There were more ways than one to spend a life apologizing.

"Mara," he said. "Your father built an escape hatch into this, didn't he? For himself? In case he ever got brave?"

She followed his pointing finger. Behind the third gate, half-hidden in the shadow of the counterweight shaft, a narrow side passage climbed steeply upward, air whistling down it cool and clean. An exit. A survivor's door. Cut by a man who had prepared everything for the flood except his own participation in it.

"Yeah," she said softly. "That's his handwriting on the whole project, isn't it. Everything ready. Himself excused."

"We won't need it if we time it with the monsoon," Freya said. "The gorge floods anyway when the rains hit. Water rises, we ride it out through his escape hatch, and the spring goes down the fault ahead of us." He looked at the gates, at the chains, at the thumbprint in the soot. "He planned our way out before either of us was born. That's either engineering or prophecy, and with this family I honestly can't tell."

"My father would be furious that you called it prophecy. He'd insist it was load calculation."

"Then it's load calculation," Freya said gravely, "and I stand corrected before a court of dead cartographers."

She laughed—actually laughed, short and startled out of her, the first real laugh since the ambush at the gorge—and the sound went echoing down Tomás's throat of stone, and somewhere in the dark it sounded, briefly, like three people laughing instead of two.

* * *

They spent the rest of the light checking everything twice, because the message on the wall had told them to and because ignoring instructions chiseled in granite by a ghost seemed unwise even by their standards. The counterweights seated true. The pawls threw clean. The flood channel below the third gate ran clear when Freya lowered a lamp on a line and watched the flame bend away from the draft of the deep fault, hungry and waiting.

On his knees by the east gate, greasing the final pawl, Freya heard Mara's voice behind him, oddly formal.

"Freya."

He turned. She was standing in the middle of the gallery with the chart in her hands, the original, Tomás's life's work, the thing men had killed and bribed and drowned for.

"When the gates open," she said, "this goes into the water with them. That was always the plan. No chart, no source, no proof for anyone to follow—including us." Her jaw worked. "I just wanted to say it out loud while there was still time to argue."

"I'm not going to argue."

"You argued about the boat iron for twenty minutes."

"The boat iron was a matter of principle. This is a matter of—" He searched for it. "This is the whole point. Your father didn't build all this so the Optimists could invoice the afterlife. He built it so it would end *clean*."

Something in her shoulders came down an inch, some tension she'd been carrying since Salt Hollow, maybe since the day nine years ago when the Survey Office had sent its polite letter regretting that no further resources could be allocated.

"He wrote 'say it first,'" she said. "Whatever you love. There's no time after."

Freya looked at her. The spring-light didn't reach this far into the gallery; the only glow came from their lamps, small and human and entirely insufficient, which somehow made it easier.

"I know what I'm going to say," he said. "When we get back to the water. Before the gates. I've known since the rapids, probably. Possibly since the ferry, when you paid a woman your mother's face and then spent the next week being kinder to me than anyone's been in ten years, which is the most backwards trade I've ever witnessed."

"Don't ruin it," Mara said, but her voice had gone rough.

"I never ruin anything. I'm an optimist. Ask my creditors."

She crossed the gallery and put her forehead against his, lamp-oil and limestone dust and the smell of a long hard road, and for a moment neither of them said anything at all, which was its own kind of chiseled message, deeper than granite.

Then, from far above—up the stair cut in stone, down through the throat of the gorge—came Cass Okonjo's voice, hoarse and urgent, echoing off the falls that flowed upward:

*"The monsoon's turned early! First band's over the coast NOW—you've got hours, not days! And there's boats on the lower river—I count four—somebody's coming UP!"*

Freya straightened. Mara wiped her face with the heel of her hand, once, businesslike.

"Hours," she repeated.

"Four boats," Freya said grimly, reaching for the boat iron. "Somebody's collection agency works weekends."

Above them, faint through the stone, the first thunder of the monsoon rolled inland like a door being closed at the far end of the world.
