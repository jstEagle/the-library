## 32. Thirty Seconds of Forever

The water was still rising when Mara found the words she'd been carrying since the antechamber.

"Tomás's gates are on the far wall," Freya said, already moving toward them, lamp held high. "I can see the mechanism from here—there's a wheel, a chain run. Give me five minutes and we can—"

"No." Mara caught his sleeve. The word came out harder than she intended, so she softened it the only way she knew how: by telling the truth. "Not yet. Look at them."

Cass stood at the spring's edge with her sidearm holstered and her hands empty for perhaps the first time since Salt Hollow. Pell lay where they'd dragged him, catatonic, his lips moving around syllables no one could parse. And the water—the source itself—turned in its basin like a slow eye, holding images the way a throat holds a song.

"We came here to end it," Freya said. His voice had that careful flatness he used when he was afraid of what he felt. "The monsoon's making landfall early. Cass said four days; it'll be one. If we wait—"

"If we rush," Mara said, "we'll do it the way the Survey Office did everything. Hurried. Unwitnessed. Ashamed." She looked at him, and something in her chest ached pleasantly, terribly. "My father spent thirty years learning how this place works. He left us a mechanism, Freya. He also left us the manners to use it properly."

Freya opened his mouth. Closed it. She watched him do the arithmetic he always did—not of debt now, but of seconds—and then he nodded once, slow, like a man agreeing to weather.

"Thirty seconds each," Cass said quietly. She had come up beside them without sound, which was her gift and her curse. "That's all any of us should take. More than that and the water starts making offers you can't refuse." Her jaw tightened. "I've read the old reports. I know what happened to the survey teams who lingered."

"You've known about those reports all along," Mara said.

"I've known about worse ones." Cass's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "But yes. All along. I'm sorry, Ilesanmi. I'm sorry it took me this long to be sorry."

Mara hadn't expected that. She'd expected procedure, or defiance, or the particular stubbornness of a woman who'd burned her own orders rather than follow them. What she got instead was an apology offered plainly, like a coin placed flat on a table, and she found she could accept it.

"Thirty seconds," Mara agreed. "Then the gates."

* * *

They drew lots with three pebbles and a scrap of chart backing, because Freya insisted that fairness mattered even—or especially—at the end of the world. Cass went first. She walked to the water's edge the way she walked everywhere: spine straight, boots deliberate, a woman reporting for duty even now.

She knelt. She did not touch the water. She only looked.

And the source, generous to a fault, gave her whatever it was she needed, because within ten seconds her shoulders changed. Mara watched the tension leave them—not all at once but in stages, like a tide going out of a harbor, and Cass Okonjo's face, which had been carved from regulation and grief, became briefly, unguardedly young.

Whatever she saw, she didn't share. When she rose, she pressed two fingers to her lips and then to the surface of the spring—a warden's gesture, or a daughter's, Mara couldn't tell—and walked back to them with wet eyes and a dry voice.

"My commission," Cass said. "Twenty-two years. My whole self was built on it, brick by brick, and every brick had someone else's name stamped on it." She exhaled. "It's gone. Or—I let it go. It's strange. It doesn't feel like losing anything. It feels like setting down a pack I forgot I was carrying."

"What will you carry instead?" Freya asked.

Cass considered this with the seriousness she brought to everything. "A vial of ordinary river water," she said. "And better questions."

Then it was Freya's turn, and Mara watched him hesitate at the edge of the light the way he must have hesitated at the edge of storms his whole life—knowing exactly what it would cost and going anyway.

He knelt. He touched the water with one hand, palm down, the way you'd touch the forehead of someone sleeping.

Mara couldn't see what the source showed him. But she saw what it did. She saw his breath catch and hold, catch and hold, like a man surfacing again and again from deep water. She saw his mouth form names—she caught *Renn*, and *Oduya*, and a third she didn't know—and she saw him laugh, actually laugh, a broken bright sound that echoed off the cavern walls and came back sounding forgiven.

When the thirty seconds were up, he didn't move. Cass put a hand on his shoulder. He flinched, then covered her hand with his own, and stayed kneeling another moment with his head bowed like a man finishing a prayer he'd started years too late.

"They weren't angry," he said finally, to no one, to everyone. "All this time I thought—they were angry. They trusted me. Right up until the wave took them, they trusted me, and the water says—" His voice cracked clean through. "The water says trust isn't something you lose when you die. It's something you leave behind. And I've been drowning in interest on a debt that was cleared the day it happened."

Mara's throat closed. She thought of nine years of petitions, of sealed files, of a father the paperwork had erased, and understood suddenly that they had been carrying the same weight in different currencies.

"Freya," she said gently. "Your time."

"I know." He wiped his face with the back of his wrist, unashamed. "I know. It's just—" He laughed again, softer. "It's just that forgiving yourself turns out to be the one thing nobody can sell you. You have to be given it. And I was given it by people who aren't even here anymore."

He rose. He kissed his fingertips and touched the water, mirroring Cass, and came back to stand beside Mara, close enough that their shoulders touched, and neither of them moved away.

* * *

Which left Mara herself, and Pell, and the arithmetic of mercy.

Pell had not spoken since they'd pulled him from the spring. He lay on his back on the stone, eyes open, tracking things that weren't there. Occasionally his hands made small precise motions—signing documents, Mara realized. Signing ledgers. Even now, even drowned in his own recollection, the man was balancing accounts against enemies only he could see.

"He should have some," Freya said quietly. "If we're doing this. He should get his thirty seconds too."

"He had more than thirty seconds," Cass said. "He drank deeply. That was his choice, and the source is collecting on it."

"That's not the same as being given a moment," Freya said. "Believe me. I know the difference now."

Mara looked at the broken collector and felt something complicated move through her—pity wearing armor, maybe, or justice wearing mercy's coat. This was the man whose syndicate had put a knife to Freya's fingers. This was the machine that had left her father in a cave to drown, or at least the same species of machine. And here he was, reduced to a ledger with a heartbeat.

"Help him to the water," she said. "Don't let him drink. Just—let him see."

They half-carried, half-dragged Pell to the spring's edge and propped him up, and for a long moment nothing happened. Then the water turned its slow eye toward him, and Pell went rigid, and a sound came out of him that Mara would spend years trying not to hear again—the sound of a man reading his own account and finding the balance owed.

But he didn't scream. That was the strange thing. He whispered.

Mara leaned close, and caught fragments: *"...didn't mean... the girl in the harbor, I only... everyone signs, everyone signs, why does it count against me if everyone..."*

And then, quieter, almost tender, in the voice of a much younger man:

*"Mother. I kept the receipts. I kept every receipt. Wasn't that enough?"*

Mara closed her eyes. Somewhere behind Pell's cheerful ruthless arithmetic there had been a boy keeping receipts for a mother who never counted them, and the source had found him, and was showing him, and he was drowning in it while kneeling dry-eyed at the water's edge.

"Time," Cass said softly, and they drew him back.

Pell came away from the spring hollow-eyed and quiet, and when they settled him against the wall he looked at Mara with something that might have been clarity, or might have been the last coherent thought of a breaking mind.

"The gates," he rasped. "Open the gates. Before I remember how to want it again."

For once, Mara believed him completely.

* * *

Then there was nothing left between her and the water except herself.

Mara walked to the spring alone. The others gave her the distance she hadn't asked for and needed absolutely, and the cavern hushed around her—the monsoon's first distant thunder arriving through stone like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

She knelt.

*I have thirty seconds,* she told the water. *Use them well.*

The source showed her her mother first.

Not the face she'd lost at the ferry crossing—that face was gone, traded for passage, spent like coin—but the face beneath the face. The way her mother had looked at her over a breakfast table, exasperated and delighted in equal measure. The smell of ink and salt that clung to everything her mother owned. A hundred small mornings the ferrywoman's toll had taken the surface of and left the depths of, and Mara understood, with a gasp that was half sob, that the old woman had taken a memory but not the love. The love remained. The love had been underneath all along, load-bearing, invisible, hers.

*Oh,* Mara thought. *Oh, you can't take that. No toll can reach that.*

Then her father.

Tomás Ilesanmi stood in the current the way he'd stood in doorways—slightly stooped, ink-stained, patient. Not alive. She knew the difference; the source was honest that way. It showed her memory, not resurrection, and the memory was enough. He was drawing. Of course he was drawing. Even in the water he was drawing, lines flowing off an invisible page, and when he looked up at her he smiled the specific smile he'd saved for her maps, the crooked one, the proud one.

*Finish the work,* the smile said. *And then go live your life, little fish. That was always the point.*

"I know," Mara whispered. "I know, Papa."

She reached toward the water. She couldn't help it. Every orphan reaches; it's the first law of grief. Her fingertips hovered a hair's breadth above the surface, above the face, above the voice she could almost—

And stopped.

Because she understood, in the last second of her thirty, what the elders of the middle reach had tried to tell her and what her father's annotations had confirmed: a memory kept here is a life not lived. That if she stayed, she would have her parents forever and never again feel the wind off a bow wave. That grief, allowed to finish, becomes something you can build with. That her father had died so this water could stop being drunk, and honoring him meant walking away from the one thing she wanted most.

*Renunciation,* she thought. *The hardest form of love.*

She withdrew her hand. She kissed her own fingertips and pressed them to the spring—for Papa, for Mama, for the crew of a boat she'd never met—and stood.

"It's done," she said. Her voice didn't shake, which surprised her. "Let's bury it."

* * *

Freya was already at the far wall, lamp raised, hands moving over the stone with the reverence of a man touching scripture. Tomás's mechanism was beautiful in the way old solutions are beautiful: a bronze wheel gone green with age, chains running into channels cut deep and true, sluice gates sized for a flood the cartographer had planned decades before his daughter was born.

"He built all this," Freya murmured, "and never used it. Waiting for someone he could trust with the choice."

"He was waiting for us," Mara said. "He just didn't know our names."

Above them, through the canyon's throat, the sky had gone the color of a bruise. The monsoon's leading edge arrived not as rain but as sound—a vast rolling pressure, the sea inhaling—and the river outside began to rise in visible pulses, climbing the ancient stair, licking at the threshold.

"Cass!" Freya shouted. "The wheel needs two!"

Cass came at a run. Together they threw their weight against the bronze, and it resisted, groaned, resisted—and then Mara put her hands beside theirs, all three of them, the chaser and the warden and the cartographer's daughter, and the wheel remembered its purpose and turned.

Chains paid out. Gates shuddered upward in their channels. And somewhere below the floor of the world, a door opened that had been waiting thirty years to open, and the source began, slowly, mercifully, to pour itself away.

The water in the basin sank. The images dimmed—Freya's crew, Mara's parents, the endless litany of the remembered—all of it sinking into the dark like lanterns lowered into a well, and the cavern filled with a sound like a long exhale after a lifetime of held breath.

Mara watched the last image go. Her father's smile, sinking. She blew it a kiss, because some farewells deserve theater.

"Goodbye, Papa," she said. "Thank you for the map."

The thunder arrived for real then, and the first rain came down the stair cut in stone in silver ropes, and the gorge began to roar, and they ran—up the ancient steps, through the falling dark, four soaked figures fleeing a miracle they had chosen to end—while behind them the legend drowned, and ahead of them, impossibly, the rain fell warm as forgiveness, and the river, freed at last, began to flow the way rivers were always meant to flow: forward, toward the sea, carrying nothing but itself.
