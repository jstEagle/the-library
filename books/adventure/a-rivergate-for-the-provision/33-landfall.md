## 33. Landfall

**Freya**

The gates opened the way Tomás must have imagined them opening—decades ago, alone in this dark, chiseling his arithmetic into stone for strangers he'd never meet.

They opened quietly.

That was the terrible part. Freya had expected thunder, a groan of ancient mechanism, some theatrical announcement that the world was about to end. Instead there was a sound like a held breath finally released, a long exhalation from deep beneath the spring, and then the water at their feet began to move—not toward the falls that flowed upward, but down, spiraling inward, draining into the throat Tomás had cut through the mountain's bones.

"It's working," Mara said. Her voice came out strange, flattened by awe and grief and the sheer volume of what they had just done.

"Then why," Freya said, "do I feel like we've made a mistake?"

The answer arrived as a sound from above: not rain yet, but the *promise* of rain—a pressure change so sudden his ears popped, followed by a wind that came howling down the canyon from the sea, forty miles inland, carrying the smell of salt and ozone and the monsoon itself, arriving early and angry.

Cass looked up at the ceiling of the cavern, where water had begun to seep through cracks that hadn't existed an hour ago. "The river," she said. "When the source collapses, the river loses its anchor. It'll reverse."

"To its natural course," Mara said. "Downstream."

"Yes." Cass was already moving, shouldering her pack, her face lit green by the failing emergency lamps she'd carried up the stair cut in stone. "And everything the backward current has been holding up this canyon for a thousand years is going to come down with it."

Freya thought about the ferry crossing. The derelict village. The middle reach, where elders who remembered everything perfectly were sitting by their fires right now, and the river under their feet was about to remember which direction it was supposed to flow.

"We have to warn them."

"We can't even warn ourselves," Pell said.

Everyone turned. The collector stood near the drained spring, swaying, his fine clothes soaked black, his eyes tracking something none of them could see—the recursive memory still chewing on him, every cruelty he'd ever committed replaying behind his pupils like a ledger being audited by a very patient accountant. He looked, Freya thought, like a man who had finally read the terms and conditions.

"The water's going to come fast," Pell said, and his voice was almost normal, almost the cheerful bookkeeper's voice that had haunted Freya's nightmares for two years. "Floods always come faster than you calculate. I used to tell clients that. About debt." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing Freya had ever seen. "Compound interest. Nobody believes it until it's due."

"Move," Freya said.

* * *

**Mara**

They ran up out of the source chamber through the antechamber where the walls had shown Mara her father's last days, and she felt the carvings' attention pass over her one final time—not judgment, she decided, just witness. *He was here. He did this. He meant it kindly.* Then the first true wall of the monsoon hit the canyon mouth above them and the world turned to noise.

Rain in the tropics wasn't weather; it was architecture. It built walls around you, ceilings over you, rivers where paths had been. Mara had grown up in Salt Hollow hearing stories of landfall, and every story was wrong, because no story could convey the way the sky simply *gave up*, all at once, dumping a season's worth of grief onto a single gorge.

The stair cut in stone became a waterfall running in reverse—downward now, the old steps drowned under six inches of racing water. Freya went first, testing each step with the iron pry bar he'd taken from Tomás's sluice mechanism, calling back footholds like a man reading scripture. "Left side! The groove! Mara, the groove!"

She found the groove. Her hands found the wet stone her father's generation had carved, and something in her chest broke open and reassembled itself in the same motion. *He walked these steps. He counted these footholds. He left them for us.*

Halfway down, the river announced itself.

It didn't roar—that word was too polite. It *arrived*, a brown-black wall of the canyon's own history coming home: silt and branches and the shattered timbers of the ferry crossing, all of it moving downstream at the speed of a decision already made. Above them, the falls that had flowed upward for centuries shuddered, hesitated—and reversed, joining the descent, and the whole gorge filled with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat.

"Off the stairs!" Cass screamed. "The ledge—there—" She pointed to a shelf of rock jutting from the canyon wall, twenty feet below and ten feet out, reachable only by a jump no sane person would make.

Nobody hesitated. That was the strange mercy of terror: it simplified. Freya jumped first and landed rolling. Mara jumped second, and for one suspended moment she hung in the rain with the whole drowned world roaring past beneath her, and she thought—not of death, but of her mother's face, which the ferrywoman had taken and which the source had offered back and which Mara had refused, choosing instead to carry the absence like a lantern. *I know your shape,* she told the memory, or the hole where the memory had been. *I'm keeping that.*

She landed hard enough to crack something in her shoulder and didn't care.

Cass came down third, precise as a surveyor even in freefall. Pell came last, and for a horrible half-second he didn't jump at all—he stood at the top of the drowned stair, staring into the water, and Mara understood he was seeing something else entirely, some other stair in some other year, and that the flood rising around his boots might be a mercy or might be exactly what he deserved.

"Pell!" Freya bellowed. "The ledger closes either way! Jump!"

Pell jumped.

* * *

**Cass**

Cass had spent eleven years in the Warden Service learning to read water the way other people read reports. What she read now, clinging to a ledge in a gorge that was filling like a bathtub with the plug pulled in reverse, was a fatality report waiting for signatures.

The ledge was thirty feet long, eight feet wide at its best, and shrinking. Below them the new river ran white and black by turns, hauling entire trees. Above them the rain fell with the weight of small animals. Somewhere inland, the source chamber was collapsing inward on Tomás's sluice gates, and the mountain was rearranging its plumbing with a series of concussions they felt through the rock more than heard.

"Options," she shouted at Freya.

"Two!" He had the pry bar hooked into a crack, anchoring himself, shouting against the din. "Ride the ledge to the narrows and climb out—or swim for the side channel we came up through!"

"Swimming is not an option."

"It's the *fast* option!"

"I've read your file, Zhou. You drown beautifully. It's practically a specialty."

Something that might have been laughter crossed his ruined face, there and gone, and Cass felt the strangest flicker of affection for these two lunatics she'd spent three weeks trying to arrest. She checked Pell—catatonic but breathing, wedged between Mara and the rock, muttering numbers under his breath, reconciling accounts with a God who wasn't answering—and made the warden's calculation, the one they trained into you before they taught you anything else: *who can you save, and what will it cost?*

The answer arrived from the mountain.

A crack split the air, deeper than thunder, and the far wall of the canyon shed a slab of cliff the size of a chapel. It hit the new river and the river *stood up*—a wave, brown and absolute, taller than a man, sweeping down the gorge toward them with the unhurried confidence of geology.

"Channel!" Cass decided. "Now! All of us!"

Mara hauled Pell upright. Freya wrapped the rope from his salvage kit twice around Cass's waist without asking permission, and she let him, and the wave came, and they jumped together into the smaller water, four bodies and one rope and a legend drowning behind them.

The side channel took them like a hand closing. For a long time there was no up, no down, no Cass Okonjo, Warden Second Class, author of seventeen commendable reports—only tumbling black water and the rope burning across her palms and somewhere ahead of her the sound of Freya Zhou laughing, actually laughing, the madman, laughing like a man who had finally paid off a debt.

* * *

**Pell**

Drowning in yesterday had prepared him, oddly, for drowning in fact.

The water was honest in a way the source hadn't been. The source had shown him his cruelties in high definition, itemized, cross-referenced, each one annotated with the victim's name and the exact figure of what it had cost them. The river just tried to kill him, no commentary, no interest accrued. Pell found this almost restful.

He surfaced inside the channel's calmer elbow because someone was holding him up—Mara Ilesanmi, the daughter of the man whose chart he'd spent nine days trying to acquire, whose leverage he'd ordered collected like any other asset. She was holding his collar with one arm and a rock with the other, and her expression was not forgiveness. It was bookkeeping. She was keeping him alive because letting him die would have been easier, and she had decided, visibly, deliberately, to do the harder thing.

"You're welcome," she said, when she saw he was looking at her.

"I don't understand you," Pell said. Water came out of him in quantities that seemed structurally impossible. "I offered to erase his debt. I offered to double it. I did everything the model said—"

"The model," Mara said, "doesn't have a line item for my father."

And Pell, floating in a flooded gorge while the legend of the backward river drowned forever somewhere above him, found that he had no entry for that either. His whole career he had balanced books that assumed people were weather fronts—predictable, movable, harvestable. But the girl held on. The storm chaser laughed underwater. The warden burned her orders. And the ledger, for the first time in Pell's tidy life, refused to close.

He stopped counting. It was, he would think later, the first honest act of his adult life.

* * *

**Freya**

They came out of the side channel an hour after landfall, spat onto a gravel bar two miles downstream, alive in the specific undignified way of things that have been thoroughly chewed and returned.

The rain kept falling, but gentler now, the monsoon settling in for its long occupation. Freya lay on his back on the gravel and watched the gorge behind them, where the last light of day showed the river running—running *downstream*, ordinary, lawful, boring, beautiful. The falls were gone. The upward water was gone. Somewhere under a hundred tons of collapsed rock, the spring that remembered everyone was learning to forget, sealed in the dark by a dead cartographer's patience and two living fools' trust in his annotations.

"Tomás," Mara said softly, beside him. Not a prayer. A report. "It's done. Your work held."

Freya reached over and found her hand in the dark. She let him.

Cass sat a few yards off, wringing out her uniform jacket, watching the water with the expression of a woman composing and deleting a report simultaneously. And Pell lay flat as a drowned saint, eyes open to the rain, whispering. Freya caught only fragments—*no further entries, account closed, account closed*—and decided that whatever reconciliation was happening inside the collector, it was above his pay grade.

"Well," Freya said to the sky. "I had a boat."

"You had a *boat*," Mara agreed.

"It was a good boat. We had history."

"The boat is a tree in the ocean now, Freya."

"A tree with excellent lines." He sat up, and the exhaustion hit him all at once, the good kind, the kind you earn. Downstream, faint through the rain, he could see the lights of the middle-reach village—still there, still standing, the elders' fires burning yellow through the gray. They'd have seen the river change. They'd have felt the ground renegotiate. By morning the whole valley would know the legend was dead.

And by morning, Freya knew, the Optimists would know something too: that their collector had gone silent, that their collateral had vanished into a storm, that the map they'd sent him to find had been fed back to the wilderness along with the debt it was meant to clear. Pell's syndicate didn't forgive. But Pell himself, catatonic on a gravel bar with rain washing his face clean, couldn't sign an order anymore—and whatever testimony waited in that broken head, Freya intended to carry it back to Salt Hollow himself.

One debt at a time.

Cass stood, buckled her empty holster—she'd lost the sidearm in the channel and seemed lighter for it—and looked at the three of them sprawled on the gravel like wreckage. "Officially," she said, "you are all missing persons as of tonight. Presumed lost in the landfall. It's the cleanest paperwork I've ever filed."

"And unofficially?" Mara asked.

Cass looked upstream one last time, at the place where a spring full of perfect memory was busy becoming ordinary groundwater. "Unofficially," she said, "the river gave back everything it was holding. I suggest we do the same."

They started walking toward the lights. Behind them the rain came down steady and warm on the drowned gorge, on the buried gates, on the legend sinking gently into geology—and Freya, limping, soaked, penniless, holding the hand of a woman whose father had saved his life twice without ever meeting him, felt the impossible thing arrive anyway, the thing the Optimists had never once managed to sell him:

He felt optimistic.

Then Mara stopped walking. Ahead, at the edge of the firelight, the villagers of the middle reach were coming down to the water's edge—all of them, every elder, every child, drawn out by the river's great reversal—and at their head stood the ancient ferrywoman, holding out one open palm.

In it lay a small brass instrument, river-polished, unmistakably Tomás's.

"She says," the village head translated, eyes wide, "that the water gave it back. And that it wants to go home."
