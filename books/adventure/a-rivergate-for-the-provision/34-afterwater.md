## 34. Afterwater

The river spat us out the way a cat spits out something it regrets eating.

I came up coughing black water and what I sincerely hoped was silt, one hand still knotted in the line I'd tied around my waist three days and a lifetime ago. The other hand found Mara's collar before my eyes found anything at all, because some instincts survive even when everything else is drowned.

"I have you," I said, or tried to say. What came out was mostly river.

She had me too, as it turned out. Her grip on my arm was the kind of grip that leaves fingerprints on bone, and she hauled me toward the bank with the efficiency of a woman who had spent nine years hauling herself through grief and had gotten very good at it.

The bank, when we reached it, was ordinary mud. That was the first miracle. After the cavern—after the light that came from nowhere and the water that remembered everyone who ever touched it—ordinary mud felt like a gift wrapped in brown paper. I lay on it face-down for a long moment, just breathing, letting the current tug at my boots like a dog that wanted to keep playing.

Somewhere behind us, upstream, the gorge was swallowing itself. We'd heard it happen: a sound like the world clearing its throat, then a long groan of stone, then nothing but rain. The monsoon had made landfall two hours early, as if it too had been waiting for the gates to open and hadn't wanted to miss the show.

"Freya." Mara's voice, close to my ear. "Freya. Are you dead?"

"Not yet," I managed. "Give me a minute."

"You've had a minute."

"That was for breathing. I need another one for existing."

She let me have it. She lay down beside me in the mud, on her back, arms spread, rain hitting her face, and laughed—a raw, cracked, unhinged laugh that I joined without meaning to, until we were both lying in the wreckage of the world howling like a pair of drowned fools, which was, technically, what we were.

* * *

Cass found us an hour later.

I heard her before I saw her—the deliberate crunch of boots on wet gravel, the particular rhythm of a woman who has decided not to shoot anyone today and wants you to know it. I rolled over. She stood above us with her sidearm holstered, her uniform ruined, and a canvas bag of demolition charges slung over one shoulder like a fisherman's catch she'd decided to throw back.

She looked down at the two of us—mud-caked, bleeding in small decorative ways, grinning like lunatics—and shook her head slowly.

"I had orders," she said.

"We know," Mara said.

"I burned them."

"We saw the smoke," I said. It was true. Halfway down the flooded canyon, we'd passed her standing on a ledge, feeding official Survey Office paper into the rain, page by page, with the solemnity of a woman performing last rites. She'd saluted the ashes. I hadn't asked. Some ceremonies you don't interrupt.

Cass crouched, checked Mara's pupils, then mine, with two fingers and no tenderness whatsoever. "Concussions, both of you, probably. Broken ribs, you—" she jabbed a thumb at me, "—definitely. Hypothermia, all of us, pending. And officially?" She sat back on her heels and looked out at the swollen brown river. "Officially, none of this happened."

"Cass—"

"No, listen. This is the part where I do my job, so shut up and let me do it well for once." She pulled a battered notebook from inside her jacket, shielded it from the rain with her body, and began writing as she talked. "Warden Cass Okonjo, field report, final. Pursued two suspects into the upper gorge during monsoon landfall. Suspects failed to clear the flood surge." Her pen scratched. "Bodies not recovered. Search suspended due to weather. Case closed."

The rain filled the silence while I understood what she was saying.

"Lost persons," Mara said quietly.

"Declared lost. Presumed drowned. Which means—" Cass snapped the notebook shut, "—no warrants. No debts enforceable against deceased persons, not even Optimist ones; their lawyers are vultures but they're *procedural* vultures. No Survey Office interest. No questions at any port from here to the equator. You are, as of this morning, free in the only way the law knows how to make anyone free: by pretending you're dead."

I thought about that. Lying in the mud with rain running into my ears, ribs screaming every time I breathed, I started to laugh again, and this time it hurt enough that I meant it.

"What's funny?" Cass demanded.

"Everything," I said. "Nothing. I spent six years drowning in debt, and the way out was to actually drown. There's a joke in there. Pell would appreciate it. He'd probably write it in the ledger."

Mentioning Pell sobered us all. The last we'd seen of him, his collectors were carrying him down the gorge trail ahead of the flood, catatonic, murmuring yesterday to himself in a voice like a man reading his own confession forever. Whether he survived, whether he could ever be *on*, was a question for a kinder day.

"He'll talk," Cass said, reading my face. "When he comes back—if he comes back—he'll talk about a spring that remembers. But he'll be talking as a madman, and there's no spring left to prove him right. You flooded it. The river runs forward now. I checked the gauge stations on my way down." A pause. "It runs forward, Freya. First time in recorded history. Whatever Tomás set in motion, it held."

Mara made a small sound. Not quite a word. I put my hand over hers in the mud and left it there, and she didn't pull away, and that was its own report, filed and accepted.

* * *

We walked downstream most of the day, because Cass knew a warden cache at the old Middle Reach landing—dry blankets, a stove, tinned peaches, the holy trinity of survival.

By the time we reached it, dusk had come down gray and soft, and the rain had gentled into that steady monsoon drizzle that sounds less like weather and more like the world thinking out loud. Cass got the stove going with the competence of someone who'd spent years alone in places like this. Mara wrapped herself in a blanket by the door and stared at the river. I sat where I was told, which was new for me, and ate peaches, which was not new for me but had never tasted like this—like being alive on purpose.

"You're going to have to tell me," Cass said eventually, poking the coals. "What it showed you. Both of you. Not for the record. For me."

I looked at Mara. She looked back. We'd agreed on nothing, but agreement happened anyway, the way it had been happening between us since the harbor.

"It shows you what you already know," Mara said. "But true. All the way through. My father, at the end. He wasn't angry. He was *hoping*. Nine years I built him into a martyr, and he was just a stubborn old man who loved maps more than money and trusted strangers more than institutions." Her voice caught. "He trusted me. Before he ever met me again. That's what broke me open."

"And you?" Cass asked me.

I turned my peach tin in my hands. "My crew," I said. "The storm. I've carried it like a debt—like everything I did after was interest payments on it. And the water showed me their last minutes, and they weren't blaming me. They were trusting me. Right up until the end, they were trusting me, because I was the one holding the wheel." I swallowed. "Turns out I've been paying interest on a debt that was forgiven six years ago. Nobody told me the account was closed."

Cass was quiet a long time. The stove ticked. Outside, the river ran forward, honest as arithmetic.

"My whole career," she said finally, "I believed the law was memory. That if we wrote things down hard enough, kept them, enforced them, the world couldn't lie to itself." She fed a stick into the fire. "Then I read Tomás's file. Then I watched a syndicate try to bottle grief and sell it by the liter. And now I'm going to go back and write a report saying none of it existed, and it'll be the most truthful document I've ever filed." She almost smiled. "Don't tell anyone I said that."

"Your secret drowns with us," Mara said. "Since we're dead."

"Since you're dead," Cass agreed, and this time she did smile, briefly, like sun through monsoon cloud.

* * *

Sleep took me like the tide takes a boat off its mooring—suddenly, completely, without negotiation.

I dreamed of nothing, which after the source felt like mercy. Or maybe I dreamed of the boat, my boat, the *Provision*, patched and patient somewhere downstream where we'd left her lashed above the flood line, and maybe that's the same thing. When I woke, gray dawn was seeping through the cache door, and Mara was already up, and Cass was gone.

There was a note weighted under the peach tin. Warden handwriting, block capitals, no flourishes:

*Gone ahead to file the report. By the time it processes, you'll be ghosts. The landing at Salt Hollow has a fuel pump that doesn't ask names. — C.O.*

P.S. *You were never here.*

"She could have arrested us," Mara said, reading over my shoulder. Her chin nearly rested on my head; we'd apparently reached that stage. "Three days ago she'd have shot us."

"People change," I said. "Rivers too."

That got me an elbow in the ribs—the broken ones—and I decided some jokes weren't worth it.

* * *

Getting downriver to the *Provision* took two days, and I will spare you most of it: leeches, a collapsed footbridge that cost us a swim I did not enjoy, and one long argument about whether Mara's emergency rations constituted "food" or "punishment." But near the end of the second day we rounded a bend and there she was—my patched, ugly, beloved boat, riding high on her mooring lines exactly where I'd left her, as if the flood had taken everything in the world except the one thing I actually needed.

She'd shipped some water. The deck was littered with leaves. The chart locker—

I opened it before I could stop myself, some old reflex, and found it empty except for a smear of ink and a scrap of waterproof paper gone soft as skin. The chart. Tomás's chart, the thing we'd bled for, traded for, memorized and altered and finally surrendered to Pell's collectors—it was gone, truly gone, dissolved into pulp and rumor. Even the copy I'd redrawn from memory had gone down in the flood, soaked to illegibility in my jacket pocket.

I stood there holding the mush that used to be the most dangerous document on the coast, and I felt—light. Impossibly, indecently light.

"It's gone," I said.

Mara came to stand beside me. "Good."

"You're sure? Your father's work—"

"Was finished," she said. "By him, and then by us. He didn't want it preserved, Freya. He wanted it *ended properly*. There's a difference, and I think he'd say we finally learned it." She took the pulp from my hands, considered it, and scattered it off the rail into the current, where it swirled once and vanished downstream. "There. Filed."

The river carried it away forward, the way rivers do now.

* * *

We ran down the coast that evening under a sky scrubbed clean by the storm, the kind of washed blue that makes even a debt-ridden wreck of a man believe in second chances. Mara took the tiller without being asked. I worked the sheets. Neither of us spoke much, because there's a language boats speak when the crew stops fighting them, and we were both fluent by now.

Salt Hollow's lights came up just after dark, strung along the quay like a handful of coins spilled on black cloth. Somewhere in that town was a harbor office, a fuel pump that didn't ask names, and a future with no shape yet—which, I was beginning to understand, is the same thing as freedom.

"One problem," Mara said, as I brought us in slow past the breakwater.

"Just one?"

"The Optimists don't accept death certificates. Pell's collectors know your face. The moment you walk into Salt Hollow, someone makes a report, dead or not."

"They won't," I said. "Not yet."

"How can you be sure?"

Because I'd been doing arithmetic in my head the whole way down, the one kind of math I'd never been bad at. "Because Pell isn't talking to anyone, and his collectors saw what the source did to him. Because Cass's report says I'm fish food. And because—" I killed the engine and let the boat drift the last few meters to the quay, "—there's a version of settling this that doesn't run through their ledger at all. Testimony. Affidavits. Names, dates, the contracts they forced, the men they buried in debt. Mara, you spent nine years learning how institutions fail people. Teach me. We're going to walk into Pell's office carrying nothing but the truth, and it's going to hit harder than any flood."

She looked at me across the dark deck for a long moment.

"That's either very brave or very stupid," she said.

"With me, historically, it's both."

Her mouth twitched—the ghost of a smile, warming. "Tomorrow, then. Tonight we eat something that isn't tinned, sleep somewhere that doesn't leak, and pretend we're alive. Since officially we aren't, there's no one to stop us."

We tied off. I stepped onto solid ground and my legs nearly buckled, and Mara took my arm without comment, and we walked up the quay together like a pair of ghosts who'd decided, against all regulation, to keep going.

Behind us the tide crept up the shore at Cape Verren, covering the place where a cave had been, smooth and silent, keeping its own counsel.

And ahead—somewhere past the harbor lights, past the fuel pump that didn't ask names, past the office of a smiling man who owed the universe a reckoning—waited the last piece of unfinished business.

But that was tomorrow's water.
