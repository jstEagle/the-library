## 35. Settling with the Optimists

The Optimists kept their Salt Hollow office above a bakery, which Freya had always found to be the most honest thing about them. Sweet smells drifting down the stairs while men upstairs calculated how many fingers a debt was worth. You could smell the whole business model from the street: something pleasant covering something that would take your hand off.

He stood across the lane for a long moment, watching the windows. Rain had followed him all the way down from the high country—thin, gray, post-monsoon drizzle that couldn't decide whether it was finished or just resting. His coat was still stiff with river silt. His boots had dried crooked. He'd slept two nights in the boatyard under a tarp, and he suspected he looked exactly like what he was: a man with nothing left to lose, which made him either the most dangerous kind of visitor or the most pathetic one.

Probably both.

In his breast pocket, folded twice, was the only thing he carried. Not a chart. Not coordinates. Paper and testimony and Mara's careful, furious handwriting—notarized by a harbor clerk who owed Cass Okonjo a favor and knew better than to ask questions.

"You can stand out there until you rot," Mara had told him that morning, tightening the last strap on her pack. She was leaving for the middle reach villages—there were affidavits still to gather, elders who remembered every promise Pell's collectors had ever broken, preserved in perfect detail for decades. "Or you can walk up those stairs. But you're walking up them alone. This part isn't mine."

"It's your affidavit."

"And I signed it. That's my whole contribution." She'd kissed his cheek—salt and ink and something warmer underneath—and stepped back to look at him the way she looked at charts she didn't trust yet. "Freya. Whatever they offer you in there—and they will offer you something, because that's all they know how to do—the answer is no. Practice saying it on the stairs."

"I've been practicing for three days."

"Practice more."

* * *

The stairs smelled of cinnamon. Of course they did.

A receptionist with a cheerful yellow scarf looked up as he entered, and Freya watched her face perform a small, professional catastrophe. She recognized him. Everyone in Salt Hollow recognized Freya Zhou; he'd been the Optimists' favorite cautionary tale for two years, the storm chaser who'd borrowed against his own boat and then against his own future earnings like a man betting on a horse he intended to shoot.

"Mr. Zhou," she said, recovering beautifully. "What a lovely surprise. Are you here to make a payment?"

"In a manner of speaking."

"He's not seeing clients this week," she said, and her voice did something delicate around *clients*, and Freya understood before she said anything else. Word traveled fast even downstream of a drowned legend. "Mr. Pell has been... unwell."

"So I heard."

There had been a fishing crew at the mouth of the gorge who'd pulled Pell out—or rather, who'd watched Freya and Mara drag him out and then done the rest, wrapping him in oilcloth and rowing him downriver while he screamed at things that weren't there. By the time the launch reached Salt Hollow, Pell had stopped screaming and started reciting. Ledgers, mostly. Every entry he'd ever made, read aloud in a voice like a man counting his way out of a burning house. They'd taken him to the charity ward, where he sat by the window and balanced books that existed only in his head, and where the nurses reported—with the gentle humor people develop around the incurable—that his accounts always balanced perfectly now. Every cruelty, every foreclosure, every finger, entered twice.

Freya had gone to see him once. He wasn't proud of it. He'd stood in the doorway expecting to feel justice settle over him like good rain, and instead he'd felt the source's lesson land one more time: *a memory kept is a life not lived.* Pell lived in yesterday the way a drowning man lives underwater. It was punishment, yes. It was also just what happened when you drank too deep of yourself.

"He'll see me anyway," Freya said now. "Tell him the Zhou account came in."

The receptionist hesitated, then knocked on the inner door, murmured, and came back looking faintly relieved, as though she'd expected to be murdered and had merely been asked for tea.

* * *

Pell's office hadn't changed. The same optimistic green walls. The same framed motto behind the desk—*EVERY CLOUD, A SILVER LEDGER*—in cheerful gilt letters. The same little brass scale on the corner of the desk that Pell used to weigh coins in front of clients, a bit of theater Freya had once found intimidating and now found almost touching. A man who needed props.

But the office was empty of Pell, and full of strangers.

Three of them. Two women and a young man, going through the filing cabinets with the unhurried efficiency of people who had warrants and wanted everyone to know it. One of the women looked up. She had a harbor-clerk's ink stains on her fingers and a magistrate's pin on her collar, and Freya recognized her distantly: Vess Andrade, one of Cass's allies, the quiet network of clerks and magistrates and survey officers that Cass had spent her resignation week assembling like a woman building an ark.

"Zhou," Andrade said. Not unkindly. "You're early. We told Okonjo we'd have the records seized by Thursday."

"The monsoon taught me not to trust schedules."

That got a short laugh out of the young man, quickly smothered.

Andrade gestured with a folder. "For what it's worth, we've been through most of it. Your name comes up forty-one times." She let that sit. "Thirty-eight of them are ordinary predation. Three involve clauses our magistrates are calling, and I quote, 'the kind of thing that gets syndicates dissolved.' The Zhou account dies with the syndicate, Mr. Zhou. Uncollected. Unenforceable. There won't be an Optimists' lending charter in this province by spring."

Freya felt something in his chest loosen that he'd been carrying so long he'd mistaken it for a rib.

"That's why I came," he said. "I brought testimony anyway. Signed statements. Mara Ilesanmi's affidavit covers the collectors' methods on the river—extortion, coercion, taking a hostage. Mine covers the contract terms and what Pell offered me at Cape Verren and again on the water. Dates, places, witnesses where we have them."

"We have more witnesses than you think," Andrade said. "Half the middle reach remembers every deal Pell ever made. Perfectly." Her mouth twitched. "It turns out the wilderness was keeping records too."

She took his papers, read the first page standing up, and nodded once—the nod of a woman adding a nail to a ship that was already seaworthy.

"Pell's in the back room," she said. "His sister asked that he be allowed to receive visitors during the seizure. Family prerogative. He's lucid some mornings." She paused, and her professional neutrality cracked just enough to show the person underneath. "Do you need to see him? Because nobody needs to see him."

"Yeah," Freya said. "I do. Not for me. For the version of me that signed that first contract in a flooded bar and thought he was being charming."

* * *

The back room had been Pell's private office once—a smaller green room, a smaller scale, a window overlooking the bakery's yard where flour sacks came and went. Now it held a cot, a chair, and Pell himself, sitting upright in the chair as though it were still his desk, wearing a nightshirt and a cardigan some sister had fetched him, his hands resting on his knees in the shape of holding a ledger that wasn't there.

He looked smaller than Freya remembered. That was the thing about monsters; you gave them your fear, and fear was a generous tailor.

"Zhou," Pell said, without turning. "Footsteps. Yours are heavier on the left. Guilt does that." Then he turned, and smiled, and it was the old smile, the bar smile, the smile that had made Freya sign, except now it was aimed slightly past Freya's shoulder at something only Pell could see. "You're late on a payment."

"No, I'm not."

"Everyone's late on a payment." Pell's eyes came back into focus, found Freya, and for a moment—just a moment—he was entirely present, sharp as a ledger line. "Ah. The river man. The one who wouldn't drown." He tilted his head. "Did you bring the chart?"

"There is no chart."

"There's always a chart." He said it gently, patiently, the way you'd explain weather to a child. "Every debt is a chart. Every chart is a debt. I tried to explain that to the water." His hands moved, turning invisible pages. "The water didn't keep books. Do you understand? It just—remembered. Everything, all at once, no interest, no terms. No *terms*, Zhou. Do you know what that does to a man?"

Freya crouched down to Pell's eye level, slowly, the way you'd approach a spooked animal or a grieving one.

"It freed me," Freya said.

Pell stared at him. And then something happened that Freya would carry forward for the rest of his life, one of the small weights the wilderness had chosen for him: Pell laughed. A real laugh, brief and rusty, startled out of him like a bird.

"Freed you." He shook his head, marveling. "I offered you forgiveness. On excellent terms. Double-or-nothing, deliver the source alive, and I'd burn your account myself—I'd have done it, I always honor—" His hands stopped their phantom page-turning. He looked at his own fingers as if checking whether they were still attached. "I always honor my terms. That was the whole point of me. That was the *whole point* of me."

"I know," Freya said. "That's why I never believed you."

Another laugh, shorter, ending somewhere painful. Pell looked out the window at the flour yard, at the drizzle coming down on the sacks, and when he spoke again his voice had gone quiet and terribly reasonable, which was the most frightening register he owned.

"They should have let me stay in the water. I'd finally have understood the accounts."

"No," Freya said. "You'd just have stopped paying attention to this one." He stood up. His knees crackled; he was getting too old for crouching at the edge of other men's abysses. "Get well, Pell. Or don't. But the ledgers are gone. The syndicate's done. And I'm not here to collect anything from you, and nothing's collecting from me. We're both paid up. That's the whole transaction."

Pell was silent for a long moment. When he answered, he was already turning pages again, eyes gone soft and inward, but he said it clearly enough:

"Paid in full. Huh." A pause. "Nobody ever balances, Zhou. Nobody ever—"

Freya closed the door on the rest of it, gently, because some mercies are just doors.

* * *

Outside, the drizzle had thickened into proper rain, the friendly kind, the kind that meant the season was turning over and starting fresh accounts of its own. Freya stood under the bakery awning and discovered his hands were shaking—not fear, not anymore, just the aftermath, the body's slow bookkeeping catching up to the soul's.

Mara was leaning against the wall beside the door. She was supposed to be on the road north with two packed bags and a ferry ticket.

"You stayed," he said.

"I missed the tide." She fell into step beside him, and they walked without deciding to, down toward the harbor where his patched boat sat riding at anchor like a dog that had learned to stop flinching. "Also I wanted to see your face when you came out. Did you get what you walked in for?"

"I got told my debt died with the syndicate. Uncollected. Unenforceable." He tested the words aloud, waiting for the catch, the hidden clause, the interest compounding somewhere in fine print. It didn't come. "Thirty-eight counts of ordinary predation and three that dissolve the whole charter. Apparently I was a bargain."

"You were always a bargain," Mara said. "Terrible negotiator, though. You traded a priceless chart for one cartographer's daughter."

"Best exchange rate I ever got."

She laughed—that real one, low, the one he'd crossed a backward river and a drowned legend and thirty-nine chapters of his own stupidity to earn—and hooked her arm through his, and for half a block neither of them said anything, which was its own kind of affidavit.

At the harbor steps she stopped. Below, the boat knocked softly against its mooring, and beyond it the sea lay flat and gray and innocent, keeping whatever secrets it had decided to keep.

"Cass sent word," Mara said. "She's filed her report. The source officially never existed. There's a vial involved. Don't ask." She glanced sideways at him. "And the village sent word too. They're holding a funeral next week. First one in generations. They want us there. They want to say his name."

Freya looked out at the water—at the place where the tide would rise tomorrow, and the day after, ordinary tides, honest tides, tides that gave back only what they took—and felt the last weight of the old arithmetic finally come off the books.

"Then we'd better get the boat ready," he said. "We've got a funeral to sail to."

Mara smiled and handed him the tiller of the morning, and together they went down the steps toward the water, which remembered them kindly after all.
