## 36. The Village Remembers Differently

The river ran the right way now.

Mara noticed it before anything else—the current pulling them seaward, obedient, ordinary, as if it had never once defied a law of nature in its long strange life. Freya handled the tiller with the easy economy of a man who had stopped fighting the water and started listening to it. Cass sat amidships with her boots off, which Mara had learned was the warden's version of celebration. And Pell lay wrapped in a tarp at the stern, breathing, blinking, occasionally murmuring numbers to no one.

They came around the last bend of the middle reach in late afternoon light, and the village rose out of the willows the way it always had—low stone houses, drying racks, smoke from cookfires standing straight up in the windless air. But something was different, and it took Mara a long moment to understand what.

There were people on the road.

Not watching them warily from doorways. Not standing in the fields with that terrible stillness she remembered from their passage upstream, when every elder had looked at her like a ledger entry, cataloguing what she owed and what she'd taken. No—there were people *walking*, unhurried, some of them laughing, one old man chasing a dog with a stick and clearly losing.

"They look..." Freya squinted. "Looser."

"Everything looks looser," Cass said. "Look at the fences."

Mara looked. The fences were crooked. They had been crooked before too, but before they'd been crooked with intention, each rail placed exactly where it had stood for two hundred years because moving it would have meant admitting someone's great-grandfather had been wrong about where fences go. Now they were just crooked, the way fences get when nobody is keeping score.

They tied up at the same stone landing, and the ferry woman was already there.

Mara's hand went instinctively to her chest, to the hollow place where her mother's face used to live. The old woman stood at the head of the landing with her arms folded, and for a moment Mara braced herself—that voice, that price, that gentle terrible arithmetic of memory for passage.

But the woman only looked at them, and then at the tarp-wrapped shape in the stern, and something moved across her face that might have been pity, or might have been its opposite.

"You went up," she said.

"We went up," Freya said.

"And you came back down." She said it the way other people said *you survived a fire*. Then her eyes found Mara's. "You're Ilesanmi's girl."

The name landed in Mara like a stone dropped into still water. "You knew him?"

"Everyone who ever passed my crossing, I remember. That's the toll I paid myself, long ago—I keep what others give up." The old woman's mouth twisted. "Your father crossed twice. Going up, he gave me a memory of his wedding day. Coming back down, he tried to give me more and I wouldn't take it. He was carrying too little already."

"He told you about the source?"

"He told me he meant to drown it." She shrugged, a small dry motion. "I thought it was grief talking. Grief talks a lot, in a village like this one. Or it did." She turned and looked up the road toward the village, and her expression did something complicated. "Come. There's something you should see. All of you. Even him, if he can walk."

Pell could walk, barely, with Freya on one side and Cass on the other, moving like a man learning again that his legs belonged to him. He didn't speak. His eyes kept fixing on things—a fence post, a chicken, the sky—and staying there, as if he couldn't be sure any of it was happening now rather than sometime else.

* * *

What they should see was in the village square: a bier.

Mara stopped walking when she understood what it was. It was made of woven reeds and river birch, plain and beautiful, and an old woman lay upon it—not dead-yet-dead, not arranged, but simply lying there with her hands folded, wearing a shawl that had clearly been knitted by someone who loved her, because the pattern wandered in places, went wrong, corrected itself.

People were gathered around it. Weeping. Openly, messily, without shame.

"There hasn't been a funeral here," the ferry woman said quietly, "in ninety-one years. Do you know why?"

Mara did know why. She had seen it in the elders' faces on the way upstream, heard it in their stories: when you remember everything perfectly, no one is ever really gone. Every grievance stayed green. Every argument could be replayed word for word until the end of time. Death became not an ending but an archive, and archives don't heal—they just accumulate. The village had preserved its dead so completely that it had forgotten how to lose them.

"The spring shifted," the old woman said. "Three days ago, in the night. The water changed taste. Old Sefu drank from his well the next morning and wept, and when we asked what was wrong, he said—" she paused, savoring it, "—he said he couldn't quite recall what his brother had said to him at his daughter's wedding. Couldn't recall whether it was cruel or kind. Just that it had mattered once."

She spread her hands.

"And do you know what happened? Nothing. The world went on. Sefu laughed, actually—he laughed like a young man. And the next elder forgot a slight, and the next, and within two days half the village was walking around apologizing to each other for things neither of them could fully remember. It was chaos. It was wonderful. Amara there—" she nodded at a gray-haired woman near the bier, "—hadn't spoken to her sister in forty years over a boundary dispute, and yesterday they couldn't reconstruct whose fault it was, so they split the difference and had tea."

Freya made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been awe. "So the spring wasn't preserving them. It was *preserving them from living*."

"It was preserving everything," the old woman said. "And everything is very heavy. You'd know." Her gaze flicked, briefly, to Pell.

On cue, Pell stopped and stared at the bier with an expression of dawning, childlike confusion. "Is she—" he began.

"Dying," the old woman said, not unkindly. "Old age. She asked for this. A funeral. She says if everyone's going to start forgetting anyway, she'd like to be properly mourned while anyone still remembers her at all. She says it's the most fashionable thing to happen to this village in a century."

* * *

They stayed the night. There was no question of not staying; the villagers pressed food on them, beds, dry clothes, and when Cass mentioned, carefully, that she was technically still a government warden, the headman waved this away with the observation that the government was four days' journey downstream and probably underwater.

Mara sat by the kitchen fire of the house they'd been given, mending a tear in her sleeve she didn't particularly care about, when the dying woman's granddaughter found her.

"You're the cartographer's daughter," the girl said. She was maybe sixteen, with ink-stained fingers and her grandmother's directness. "The one going downriver. People are saying you flooded the source."

"People are saying a lot of things."

"Are they true?"

Mara considered lying, decided against it, and considered the truth, which was worse. "We opened the gates my father built. The monsoon did the rest."

The girl sat down across from her, uninvited, comfortable. "Grandmother wants to ask you something. She's been asking all evening but people keep crying at her and it's very inefficient." She leaned forward. "She remembers your father. Everyone here does—we're a village that remembers, even now, even loosening. He stayed with us eleven days, going up. He fixed our mill. He drew maps of our reach of the river for free, which nobody does. And he talked about a daughter."

Mara's needle stopped.

"He talked about you the way—" the girl hesitated, searching, "—the way our elders used to talk about things they wanted to preserve forever. Like he was trying to memorize you. In case."

"In case what?"

"In case he didn't come back." The girl said it plainly, the way children say devastating things. "He left drawings here. Of you. He gave them to Grandmother for safekeeping, and Grandmother being Grandmother, she kept them perfect. Would you like them?"

Mara followed the girl through the dark house on legs that felt borrowed. In a back room, the old dying woman lay propped on cushions, and beside her bed was a wooden box, and inside the box were drawings.

Eleven of them. Pencil and ink on survey paper, the margins full of the neat annotations of a working cartographer—and every one of them was Mara. Mara at nine, scowling over a chart. Mara asleep in a boat. Mara pointing at something out of frame, mid-argument, mid-laugh. Her father had drawn her the way he drew coastlines: obsessively, precisely, with love in the accuracy.

"He did these instead of sleeping," the old woman said. "I told him he was wasting lamp oil. He said, 'If I die in that cave, someone should know exactly what I was going home to.'" Her eyes, milky-bright, found Mara's. "He knew. Going up, he already knew he might not come back down. And he went anyway. Because of the source, yes—but also because he thought if he buried it, the world would be safer for you. That was the whole of his arithmetic. One map, one daughter, one choice."

Mara held the drawings against her chest and finally, finally, let herself cry—not the furious tears of nine years, the ones she'd sharpened into weapons, but the slow helpless kind, the kind that undo you. The old woman didn't tell her it would be all right. She was a woman of a remembering village; she knew better than to promise. She just took Mara's hand and held it, and outside the funeral drums began.

* * *

The funeral was held at dawn, as the first light came down the river gorge and turned the water gold.

The whole village came. They stood around the bier in the square, and for the first hour nobody spoke, because they were all doing the thing their people had done for generations—reciting, silently, everything they remembered of the dead woman, every kindness, every quarrel, every day of her long life, hoarding her.

But then old Sefu stepped forward, cleared his throat, and said, "I have been trying all night to remember what my brother said at my daughter's wedding, and I cannot, and I find—" his voice cracked, and steadied, "—I find that I am *free*. So I will say instead what I know: she was kind to me when my wife died, and I never thanked her properly, and I am thanking her now, out loud, in front of everyone, late."

And the dam broke—not the water dam, the grief dam. Elder after elder stepped forward and said things they had never said, apologies and thanks and confessions, imperfect memories offered up like flowers, and the weeping changed quality, from hoarding to releasing. The granddaughter sang. Someone who couldn't remember the words hummed anyway.

When the bier was carried down to the river, Mara walked behind it with the drawings under her arm, and Freya walked beside her without saying anything, which was exactly right, and Cass walked behind them both with her cap in her hands like a civilian, which she was, now, more or less.

At the water's edge, the headman raised a hand. "The family speaks," he said. "Or those who are family, or nearly."

Mara hadn't planned anything. But she had spent nine years rehearsing speeches to a Survey Office that never listened, and all of them had been about justice, about negligence, about names on files. None of them had been about this.

She stepped forward, unrolled the top drawing—her father's rendering of her at nine years old, furious over a chart, alive as morning—and held it up so the village could see.

"My name is Mara Ilesanmi," she said. "My father was Tomás Ilesanmi, cartographer, of Salt Hollow. Nine years ago he went up this river to bury something dangerous, and the sea closed over him, and the world filed him as missing, and I spent nine years trying to make the world say his name out loud." Her voice shook. She let it. "He fixed your mill. He drew your maps. He drew me. He loved this river enough to try to give it back its freedom, and it cost him everything, and I am telling you his name now, in front of witnesses, so that it goes into the ground with this good woman and comes up wherever names come up."

She paused. The river moved, seaward, steady, right-way-round.

"I never got to bury him," she said. "But today I got to bury the waiting. And I find that I am free too."

The headman nodded slowly, and then, in the manner of villages everywhere, the ritual absorbed her: an elder pressed a handful of river earth into her palm to cast onto the bier, the granddaughter took one of the drawings to copy for the village record, and when the reed bier slid out onto the current and the current took it—seaward, gently, the way rivers carry things when they are behaving themselves—the whole village sang.

Mara cast the earth. It pattered onto the reeds like rain.

Beside her, Freya said quietly, "He'd have liked this. The whole town showing up. He hated working alone."

"He didn't work alone at the end," Mara said. "He had us."

* * *

They left at midmorning. The ferry woman met them at the landing one last time, and Mara braced again out of habit.

"No toll," the old woman said. "The crossing takes coin now. Imagine. I'm practically honest." She looked at Mara a long moment. "You'll forget parts of him. It's starting already—you can feel it, can't you? The edges going soft. Don't fight it. What stays will be truer for having room to breathe."

"I don't want to forget the drawings."

"You won't. Those aren't memory anymore. They're yours." She touched Mara's wrist, brief and dry and warm. "Go home, Ilesanmi's girl. Mourn him properly. It's the best thing the living get to do."

They pushed off into the current. Behind them the village shrank into the willows, and ahead the river ran wide and golden toward the coast, toward Salt Hollow, toward whatever waited there—debts, offices, the ordinary machinery of the world.

Pell sat up for the first time since the source. He looked at the water, at the sky, at his own hands, and said, in a voice rusty as an unused hinge:

"I can't remember the ledger."

"Good," said Cass.

"I can't remember *any* of it. The accounts. The interest. What I was owed." He sounded less like a ruined man than like a man waking from a fever, bewildered and almost light. "Who am I, if I can't remember what everyone owes me?"

Nobody answered. The river carried them on.

It was Freya, after a while, who spoke, gazing down the long bright water toward home. "There's a storm system building off Cape Verren. Big one. Early season." He glanced sideways at Mara, careful, hopeful, the way he'd started looking at her since the flood. "Salt Hollow's going to need its coastline mapped. Properly. By somebody who knows the river."

Mara looked at him—at this debt-ridden, drowned-coast, impossible man who had traded a fortune for her father's grave and called it a fair price—and felt the last hard knot of nine years loosen somewhere beneath her ribs.

"Well," she said, reaching for the chart table. "Somebody should warn them."

The river ran seaward, and they ran with it, and behind them the village bells rang—for a funeral, for a naming, for the first time in ninety-one years, for an ending that was allowed to be one.
