## 37. Cass's Report

The Survey Office in Salt Hollow smelled the same as it had for eleven years: damp paper, cold coffee, and the faint mineral tang of the dehumidifier that nobody had ever fixed because fixing it would require admitting something was wrong. Cass Okonjo sat at her desk at six in the morning, three days after the monsoon made landfall, and looked at a blank incident report form with the expression of a woman deciding whether to lie to an old friend.

Form 11-C. *Incident Summary — Unlicensed Hydrographic Activity, Cape Verren to Middle Reach.* She'd filled out four hundred of these in her career. They were honest documents, mostly. That was the thing about bureaucracy that outsiders never understood: it wasn't built on lies. It was built on the assumption that everyone involved was too tired and too busy to lie well, and so the truth, ground down into small enough pieces, could be trusted to roll through the system on its own.

Cass had spent her whole career believing that.

She picked up her pen. Put it down. Picked it up again.

*Item one,* she wrote. *Responding to reports of unlicensed survey activity along the coastal river, this officer proceeded upstream over a period of nine days.*

That much was true. Every word of it was true. The trick, she was learning, was not in lying but in choosing which truths got to stand next to each other. Her instructors at the Academy had called it "narrative integrity." Her mother had called it something shorter and less flattering.

She thought about the orders she'd burned.

It had been a small fire, as fires went—a handful of pages in a rusted brazier on the deck of a government launch while the first bands of the monsoon came sideways off the sea like a fistful of thrown gravel. The orders had been signed by Deputy Director Anselm Voss, the same man who, she now knew, had signed Tomás Ilesanmi's death warrant nine years ago with a signature so small and tidy it might have been a signature on a lunch order. *Recover document. Seal source permanently. No survivors' testimony required.* That last phrase had been Voss's particular genius. Not *no witnesses.* *No testimony required.* As if the survivors were simply a paperwork problem.

Cass had read those orders in a government office with good lighting and felt her whole career turn to ash in her mouth before she ever struck the match.

*Item two,* she wrote. *This officer located the alleged source of the backward-flowing river. After thorough investigation, this officer can confirm: no such source exists.*

She stopped and read the sentence back. It was, technically, the truest sentence she had ever written. The source did not exist anymore. She had stood in the flooding cavern with water rising past her knees—black, furious, alive—and watched the last light of the spring go under as Tomás Ilesanmi's sluice gates groaned open and the river took back what it had lent. By the time the water reached the ceiling, there was nothing left to find. No spring. No images in the current. No chamber. Just rock and river and rain, the way it had presumably been before some ancient hand cut stairs into the canyon wall and gave the place a story.

A story, Cass reflected, was exactly the kind of thing that got people killed. It had gotten Tomás Ilesanmi killed. It had very nearly gotten Freya Zhou killed, and Mara Ilesanmi, and—if she was honest—it had gotten a piece of herself killed too, some part that had believed the Survey Office was a shield rather than a knife that happened to be pointed in a convenient direction most of the time.

So: no such source exists. Let the exploiters come. Let them bring their drilling rigs and their bottling contracts and their cheerful men with ledgers. Let them stand in the drowned canyon with their instruments and find nothing but ordinary water doing ordinary things. The best protection she could offer that river was not a garrison. It was boredom.

*Item three,* she wrote. *The individual known as Pell, agent of the Optimists lending syndicate, was recovered from the gorge in a state of psychological collapse. Medical assessment attributes his condition to prolonged exposure during the flood event. This officer recommends no further inquiry.*

Pell was in the charity ward in Salt Hollow now, in a bed by the window, smiling at things that weren't there. The nurses said he was pleasant. He told them, they said, that he was having a wonderful time. He relived things, apparently—the doctors used words like *intrusive recollection syndrome*—every cruelty he'd ever committed, from the inside, from the victim's chair, on a loop with no exit. Cass had visited him once. She'd meant to feel satisfaction. What she'd felt instead was a cold, complicated pity, because the man behind the smile had been drowning in yesterday, and there was no sluice gate for that.

Freya Zhou and Mara Ilesanmi had dragged him out anyway. Cass kept coming back to that. She'd arrived with demolition charges in her pack and her sidearm half-raised, and she'd found two half-drowned people holding a third above the waterline—an enemy, a monster by any reasonable accounting—and their only thought had been *don't let him die here.* Not *save him.* Just: not here. Not in that water. Nobody should end in that water.

She had lowered the gun. Then she'd helped carry him.

*Item four,* she wrote, and paused again, because item four was where the report either became a lie or became hers.

*Item four. The chart recovered from the cave at Cape Verren was destroyed during the flood event. All annotations, coordinates, and associated materials are unrecoverable. This officer's investigation is closed.*

Also true. The chart had gone into the black water with everything else. Freya had traded it to Pell's collectors in the gorge, and Pell's collectors had lost it to the flood along with their employer's mind, and the river had taken it the way the river took everything eventually. There was no chart. There was no proof. There was only the word of a debt-ridden storm chaser, a cartographer's daughter, and a warden who had burned her own orders—which was to say, there was nothing a court would touch and nothing a syndicate could buy.

She signed it. *C. Okonjo, Field Warden, First Class.* Eleven years of service in five letters and two words. Then she took out a second sheet—not a form, just paper—and wrote her resignation.

It took her four tries. The first was too angry. The second was too apologetic. The third sounded like a form, which frightened her more than the anger had. The fourth said:

*I resign my commission effective immediately. I have found that the Office is capable of great work and I am proud of most of it. I am also aware that the same machinery that protects coastlines can be turned to burying truths, and I find I can no longer tell, from the inside, which job I am doing on any given morning. I would rather be a person who chooses than an instrument that functions. Respectfully, C. Okonjo.*

She read it twice. It didn't say anything about a spring that remembered you back, or a dead cartographer who had refused to sell a miracle, or a village of elders who were learning, slowly and painfully, how to hold a funeral. It didn't need to. Some truths you file. Some truths you keep.

*

The resignation went in at nine o'clock sharp, because if she was going to blow up her life she was going to do it punctually. The clerk at the intake window—a young man named Dov with the permanent startled expression of someone who had never yet seen weather—stamped it without reading it, which Cass found both infuriating and deeply reassuring. The machine didn't care. The machine had never cared. That was its charm and its horror, and she was free of both as of the timestamp 09:04.

Deputy Director Voss was not in the building. Voss was, according to the duty board, "on assignment," which Cass had learned—via Mara's affidavit and the records her allies in the Archive Division had quietly pulled—was Voss's phrase for the periods when he was making problems disappear. He had made Tomás Ilesanmi disappear. He had tried to make a spring disappear. He would, in time, try to make Cass disappear too, once he learned what she knew. She had already mailed copies of the sealed file to three newspapers, two archivists, and one retired judge who owed her a favor and hated Voss personally. Whatever came next, it would not come quietly.

But that was tomorrow's weather. This morning, Cass walked out of the Survey Office for the last time carrying a single canvas bag, and the bag weighed almost nothing, and the lightness of it scared her more than the weight of the sidearm ever had.

She stopped at the harbor. Of course she stopped at the harbor. Salt Hollow's harbor was where everything in this story had started to go sideways—where Mara had cornered Freya over stolen salvage gear, where Cass had watched two griefs collide and made the mistake of writing it all down. The boats were in after the monsoon, most of them battered, several of them sunk to their gunwales in the gray chop. And tied up at the far end of the pier, patched and re-patched and ugly as sin, was a boat Cass recognized.

Freya Zhou was aboard, arguing with an outboard motor. Mara Ilesanmi was aboard, winning the argument on the motor's behalf. They moved around each other with the easy friction of people who had nearly died together and were now negotiating the terms of breakfast.

Cass considered walking past. She genuinely considered it. But Freya looked up—he always looked up, the man had the situational awareness of a startled deer—and raised a hand.

"Warden," he called. "Come to arrest us?"

"I'm not a warden anymore." She held up the canvas bag like evidence. "Resigned this morning. Nine-oh-four."

Mara came to the rail. Up close she had the look of someone who had finally slept after nine years of not sleeping, and it had changed her face the way rain changes a drought field—not younger, exactly, but softer at the edges. "What does a former warden do now?"

"Undecided." Cass looked out at the harbor, at the gray water, at the cape beyond it where a certain cave was currently thirty feet under a returned tide, keeping its own counsel. "I've been thinking about the middle reach. The village."

"The elders?" Freya said.

"They're changing. Since the spring shifted. Old grievances are—" She searched for the word. "Loosening. They held their first funeral in living memory last week. For a man who died forty years ago." She shook her head. "Somebody should document that. Properly. Kindly. Not the way the Office would do it—forms and extraction. Somebody should sit with them and write down what healing looks like when it arrives eighty years late."

"You're volunteering," Mara said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I'm considering volunteering. I'm newly unemployed. I'm told the position comes with terrible pay and excellent company." She glanced between them. "I hear the current staff are tolerable."

Freya grinned—that crooked, debt-ridden grin that had somehow survived everything the sea and the Optimists had done to it. "We're heading upriver next week. Mapping the coastline communities. For them, this time. Not for anyone with a ledger." He hesitated, then added, carefully, "There's room."

Cass looked at the boat. At the patched hull, the mismatched paint, the outboard motor currently losing its argument with Mara. At the two people on it who had every reason to distrust her and had, somewhere in the black water of a flooded canyon, decided to trust her anyway.

"I'll bring my own tent," she said. "And I warn you, I alphabetize."

"Heaven help us," Mara murmured, but she was smiling.

*

That evening, alone in the rented room she would give up at the end of the month, Cass unpacked her canvas bag. It contained, in total: two shirts, a notebook, a pen, a folding knife, her late father's compass, and one small glass vial.

The vial was the joke.

She'd filled it downstream of the gorge, in the aftermath, in water that was by every measurement completely ordinary. Clean, cold, chemically unremarkable river water. No images in it. No memories. If you looked through it you saw your hand and nothing else, which was, Cass had come to believe, the single most precious property any water could have.

She held it up to the lamplight. Somewhere in that flooded dark was—or had been—a spring that showed you everyone you'd ever loved, living in the current, forgiving you whether you deserved it or not. People would spend fortunes chasing it. Governments would burn villages for it. A man named Pell was smiling at a ceiling in the charity ward because he'd drunk it like a man taking a bonus.

And Cass Okonjo, former Field Warden First Class, keeper of forms and burner of orders, had walked away with a vial of absolutely nothing.

She smiled at it. It was, she decided, the finest piece of evidence she'd ever collected: proof against certainty itself. Whenever some official or academic or cheerful man with a ledger told her, in future years, that the world was fully mapped, fully known, fully owned—she would take out the vial, look at the plain water, and remember that she had stood in the presence of a miracle and chosen to let it stay wild. That the greatest thing she'd ever protected was a hole in the map. That sometimes the truest report is the one that says *there's nothing here*, written by the one person who knows better.

She corked it and put it in her breast pocket, over her heart, where a less sensible woman might have kept a locket.

Outside, the rain had stopped. Through the window she could see the harbor lights, and beyond them the dark line of the cape, and beyond that the river running—she checked, out of eleven years of habit, and confirmed—downhill toward the sea, the way rivers were supposed to run, minding its own business, keeping its secrets, remembering whatever it pleased.

Tomorrow she would buy a tent. Tomorrow she would write the first letter to the elders of the middle reach. Tomorrow, probably, Voss would learn her name in a context he disliked.

Tonight she had a vial of nothing, a notebook full of blank pages, and—for the first time since she'd pinned on the warden's badge at twenty-three—an entirely open map.

She fell asleep with the lamp still burning, and dreamed, pleasantly, of paperwork she would never have to file.
