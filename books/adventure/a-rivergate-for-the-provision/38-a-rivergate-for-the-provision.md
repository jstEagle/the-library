## 38. A Rivergate for the Provision

The tide came back on a Tuesday.

I know because Mara told me it was Tuesday, and because I had spent the better part of three weeks learning that when Mara Ilesanmi said a thing was so, arguing with her was like arguing with the moon about where to put the water. The tide came back on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday morning the cave at Cape Verren was gone—swallowed whole, ribbed throat and all, under eleven fathoms of ordinary, indifferent sea.

I stood on the shingle with my hands in my pockets and my boots in the foam and felt nothing so much as relief. Somewhere down there, behind stone that no chart would ever find again, was the place where I had watched a dead man's crew forgive him in moving water. Somewhere down there, Pell had drunk himself into yesterday and never come all the way back out. And somewhere down there, if the sea had any poetry in it at all, lay the splinters of a chart I had fed to a stove in Salt Hollow one page at a time, watching Tomás Ilesanmi's careful ink curl and go dark.

Good riddance, I thought. And then, because I am who I am: I hope it's comfortable down there.

"You're talking to the water again," Mara said, coming up the beach with two mugs of coffee balanced in one hand and a coil of rope over her shoulder like a stole. "It doesn't answer."

"It answered plenty last month."

"Last month you were drowning." She handed me a mug. It was terrible coffee, boiled twice and tasting faintly of tar, and I drank it like it was communion wine. "This month you're just a man standing on a beach talking to fish."

"There are worse things to be," I said. "There's being Pell, for one."

Mara looked out at the water where the cave had been. Her face did the thing it always did now when she thought of her father—not the flint-hard thing from Salt Hollow harbor, not the grief-cracked thing from the antechamber, but something newer and quieter, like a scar that had finally decided to stop itching.

"The Survey Office divers went down yesterday," she said. "Cass told me. They found the outer passage silted shut. Not collapsed—silted. Like the sea tucked it in." She paused. "They also found a boot. One of Pell's collectors lost it running out. Cass says the report will describe it as 'a boot.'"

"Cass is learning poetry."

"Cass is learning mercy," Mara said. "Poetry she had all along. You should read her resignation letter. She quotes tide tables like scripture."

* * *

We had come back to Cape Verren for the boat.

That sounds simple. It wasn't simple. The *Provision*—my boat, my debt, my coffin-that-floated—had taken a beating in the gorge that would have killed a lesser vessel and nearly killed me besides. When we'd limped her into the fishing anchorage below the cape, listing and groaning like an old man climbing stairs, the local fishermen had looked at her the way you look at a soldier home from a war nobody won.

"She'll need everything," old Sefu had said, walking her hull with his palm, reading her wounds. "New planking amidships. Keel's sprung. Ribs cracked here, here, and—" he tapped a third spot, "—here, where somebody clearly hit a rock at speed while being shot at."

"That's a fair summary of events," I said.

Sefu had squinted at me for a long moment, then at Mara, then back at me. Word travels on rivers, even backward ones. He knew what we'd done—or thought he did, which amounted to the same thing in a place like this. He spat thoughtfully into the water.

"I've got seasoned teak in my shed," he said. "Been saving it for a hull worth the name. Cost you labor. Yours, both of you, every day until she floats. And cost you one true story, told straight, before we start."

So that was the price. We paid it that first night, in Sefu's kitchen, with his whole family crowded around the stove—the story of the river that ran backward, told without the source itself, without the spring, without anything anyone could follow or sell. Just the shape of it. A dying man in a cave. A daughter's nine years. A warden who burned her orders. A flood that took our secret and gave us back each other.

When I finished, nobody spoke for a while. Then Sefu's youngest granddaughter, maybe seven years old, said, "Did the water really flow uphill?" and I said, "For a while, little one. For a while, everything there flowed toward what it loved instead of away from it," and Sefu laughed until he had to sit down, and shook my hand, and that was the contract signed.

* * *

Boat work is honest work, which is more than I could say for most of what I'd done in forty-one years. There's no cleverness in it. There's a rotten plank and you take it out and you put a good one in and you caulk the seam and then you stand back and the world is very slightly less leaky than it was before. After a lifetime of arithmetic that never came out even—debts against assets, skill against luck, the dead against the living—it was a balm to do something where the sum was visible at the end of the day.

Mara turned out to be good with her hands in a way that surprised me, though I don't know why it should have. Her father had been a cartographer; precision ran in the family like blood type. She fitted planks the way she read charts, with a patience that made my hammering look like what it was—a man trying to outrun his own thoughts.

"You're doing it wrong," she said on the fourth day, watching me fight a scarf joint.

"It's holding."

"It's holding *angrily*. There's a difference. My father used to say a bad fit holds until exactly the worst possible moment, and then it lets go all at once, like a promise made by a lender."

"Pell would disagree. His promises let go slowly. Over years. With interest."

She smiled despite herself. That was another thing I was learning about her—that underneath the iron there was a quicksilver humor that surfaced when she forgot to guard it, and that I had begun arranging my days, shamelessly, around the possibility of making it surface.

We worked. The monsoon season passed overhead in long gray processions, rain marching up the coast like armies changing garrison, and we worked under canvas through most of it, and on the clear days we worked in sunlight so clean it felt rinsed. My hands, which had held a lamp in a drowning cave and a stranger's hand in white water and a pen over a ledger I hated, learned the grain of teak. Some nights I fell asleep mid-sentence, sanding dust in my hair, and woke to find Mara had draped a tarp over me and kept working by lantern light, humming something tuneless and content.

Once—I want to set this down honestly, because honesty is the only currency I have left that isn't counterfeit—once I caught myself whistling while caulking a seam, and stopped, astonished. I couldn't remember the last time I'd whistled. Possibly before the storm. Possibly before the Optimists. Possibly before I'd ever learned that optimism, the syndicated kind, was just greed wearing a flower in its buttonhole.

But this was different. This was the other kind. The kind that comes unasked.

* * *

Cass came through in the third week, traveling light, out of uniform, looking like a woman who had recently set down something very heavy and was still adjusting to the weightlessness.

She stood on the beach and inspected the half-reborn *Provision* with a professional eye. "She'll float," she allowed.

"She'll fly, eventually. Give us till the equinox."

"Mm." Cass crouched, touched the new planking, nodded once—the highest praise her office had ever dispensed, I suspected, even before she'd resigned it. Then she straightened and looked at me with those level warden's eyes that had once been measuring whether to shoot me and were now measuring, apparently, nothing more urgent than my coffee supply.

"You heard about Pell?"

"Heard they moved him to the care house at Meridian Bay. Heard he sits by the window and tells the nurses it's 1998 and he owes everyone an apology." I turned my mug in my hands. "He does, too. But not to me. I got mine paid."

"You dragged him out of that water," Cass said. It wasn't a question. It was the thing she'd turned over for months, I think, the way she turned over tide tables.

"He was drowning in every cruelty he ever committed. Nobody should die like that. Even him." I shrugged. "Besides, Mara was already pulling. I'd have looked cowardly standing there philosophizing."

Cass almost smiled. On Cass, an almost-smile was a festival. "The affidavit held. Mara's testimony plus mine. The Optimists' charter's suspended pending investigation. Half their collectors turned crown's evidence the moment the wind changed—they always do, that breed. Your debt record was seized as evidence of extortion." She paused, delicately. "Which means, legally speaking, it no longer exists."

"I know."

"You keep saying 'legally speaking' like there's another kind."

"There is," I said. "The kind where a man spends nine years owing money to people who smile too much, and pays it off in terror and salt water, and gets a piece of paper saying the debt was never lawful to begin with. The paper helps. But the man remembers the arithmetic." I looked out at the sea, at the hidden cave. "Though I'm learning to carry lighter ledgers."

Cass studied me for a moment longer, then reached into her coat and produced a small glass vial, stoppered, half full of muddy brown water. River water. Ordinary, silt-heavy, going-nowhere river water.

"For you," she said. "A keepsake."

"What is it?"

"Nothing. That's rather the point. I filled it downstream of the village, the day of the funeral. It's just water. It flows the way water flows. It forgets everything it carries by the time it reaches the sea." She pressed it into my hand. "I keep one myself. Whenever I'm certain of something, I look at it. It helps."

She left the next morning, walking north along the coast road with a pack and no destination she'd admit to. At the top of the bluff she paused, raised one hand—not quite a wave, more a salute between soldiers—and was gone. I keep her vial on the *Provision*'s shelf now, next to the compass. Two instruments for finding your way: one points north, and one reminds you that certainty is heavier than water.

* * *

The boat was finished on a morning in late autumn, when the light came low and gold across the water and the air smelled of wood shavings and cold salt. Sefu walked her hull one final time, palm flat against the teak, and pronounced judgment.

"She's sounder than she ever was," he said. "You've ruined her, in a way. She'll outlive you now."

"That's the idea," Mara said.

We launched her at mid-tide. The villagers came down to watch, which embarrassed me until I understood they weren't there for me—they were there for the boat, the way you attend a wedding for the couple and not the guests. Sefu's granddaughter broke a coconut against the stem in the local fashion, and the *Provision* slid off her cradle and took the water like a woman stepping back into a dress she'd missed, and I swear on every chart I've ever misread, she sat higher and steadier than she had any right to.

We sailed her that afternoon, just the two of us, no cargo, no contract, no current pushing us anywhere we hadn't chosen. The wind came out of the southwest, gentle, and Cape Verren fell away astern, and the drowned cave slept under its eleven fathoms, and I stood at the tiller feeling something I had no ledger entry for.

Mara sat forward on the thwart, face into the wind, and I watched her and understood that I was doing the thing again—the thing I'd done my whole life, asking the wilderness the questions that belonged to people. I'd asked the storm for absolution. I'd asked the river for a way out. I'd asked the source for forgiveness, and grace of a kind it gave me—but grace isn't an answer, it's a beginning. The questions that mattered had faces. They'd always had faces. I'd just been too busy reading charts to look up.

So I didn't ask the wind. I didn't ask the water. I asked her.

"Mara."

She turned. Wind-whipped hair, sunburnt nose, a smear of caulking compound on her jaw that she'd been carrying since morning like war paint. The most beautiful sight my particular eyes had ever surveyed, and I include in that competition several storms of great distinction.

"When we make port," I said—and my voice did something embarrassing halfway through, so I started again—"when we make port. There's a surveying season opening in the spring. Coastal communities, all down the reach. Mapping the shallows so the fishing fleets stop losing boats. Honest work. Poor pay." I swallowed. "I'd like a partner. Not a contract partner. The other kind. The kind where you stay because staying is what you choose, every morning, fresh, with no interest accruing either direction."

The sail ticked. The water talked its endless talk under the hull. Somewhere behind us a gull made its opinion known, as gulls do.

"And I'm asking you," I said, "not the sea. The sea's given me enough answers. I'm asking you whether you'll stay."

Mara looked at me for a long moment. Long enough that I had time to catalog every way this could go wrong, which is a habit I intend to keep working on. Then she did something I will remember on whatever day they lay me down, however many decades hence.

She stood, crossed the cockpit, and held out her hand.

In it was the tiller.

She pressed it into my palm, closed my fingers around the smooth worn wood herself, and said, "Keep her steady, then. I'm staying."

And that was the whole of it. No speech. No vow. Just the weight of a boat's helm passing from her hand to mine, which is, if you think about it, the oldest proposal there is—you steer, I'll crew; I'll trust your heading, you trust my hauling; we'll get where we're going together or not at all.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came up out of me like a cork from deep water, thirty years of dread coming off in one bright burst, and Mara laughed too, and Sefu's granddaughter's coconut must have brought us luck because the wind chose that exact moment to fill the sail full and clean, and the *Provision* leaned into it and ran.

We ran all afternoon. Toward evening we anchored in a lee below the cape, and cooked fish over a charcoal bucket, and Mara fell asleep against my shoulder with the tide table still open in her lap, and I sat up a while longer keeping the anchor watch, which is a job for one person but is much nicer performed for two.

Out past the point, the sea breathed against the cliffs where the cave used to be. In it, if it remembered anything, it remembered kindly. I'd stopped needing it to. The wilderness had carried me a long way—through the throat of the world and back out again—but the last mile, the mile that mattered, I'd walked on my own feet, beside someone who chose the same road.

Tomorrow we'd sail north. There were coastlines wanting maps, and communities wanting them drawn for love instead of ledgers, and a whole wet wild world that owed us nothing and gave us everything anyway.

I took the tiller in both hands, and held our heading, and waited for morning.
