## 39. The Storm They Chase Together

The storm had a name now—*Serephine*, which the radio announcers pronounced three different ways and which Mara refused to say at all, on the grounds that naming a storm was like naming a debt: it made it feel official.

"It's a Category Two," Freya said, squinting at the horizon from the wheelhouse of the *Provision*, which was no longer a patched wreck but a genuinely seaworthy vessel with only two cosmetic dents and one dent that whistled in high wind, a feature they'd stopped trying to fix because it harmonized with the kettle.

"It's a Tuesday," Mara said, marking their position on a chart she'd drawn herself, in her own hand, for a community that had asked them to map the shoals off Little Verren so their fishing boats would stop finding them the hard way. "You get excited by Tuesdays."

"I get excited by *organized weather*. There's a difference."

"There is not."

There wasn't, really. That was the thing neither of them said out loud anymore, because saying it would have required admitting how much joy they took in something most sensible people ran from. But here they were, running *toward* it, sails trimmed, instruments humming, the gray-green wall of Serephine rolling up the coast like something enormous coming home.

Cass Okonjo sat in the corner of the wheelhouse with a mug of tea she'd brought herself, because she no longer trusted anyone else's tea, and watched them bicker with the expression of a woman who had once commanded demolition convoys and now found herself chief meteorologist, navigator, and designated adult for two people who treated a hurricane the way other people treated a county fair.

"You know," Cass said, "when I burned my orders, I imagined a quieter retirement."

"This *is* quiet," Freya said. "No one's shooting at us."

"The day is young."

"The day is *Tuesday*," Mara said, without looking up.

* * *

They worked the way they'd learned to work in the gorge, in the flood, in the black water where the legend drowned—without needing to discuss it. Freya read the sea; Mara read the charts; Cass read the sky and occasionally read them both the riot act when they got too cheerful about wave heights.

Their business, if you could call it a business, had grown out of nothing and into something. Coastal villages paid what they could—fish, mending, sometimes actual money—and in exchange the *Provision* arrived before the storms did, with maps of where the water would go and where the boats should be. No syndicates. No ledgers balancing people like weather fronts. Just ink, soundings, and the particular satisfaction of drawing a line on a page that would keep someone's roof on their house.

"Little Verren's harbor master says if we get the shoal survey done before landfall, he'll pay us in smoked eel," Mara said.

"How much eel?"

"A lot of eel."

"Then we get the survey done before landfall."

Cass lowered her tea. "The survey is on the *other side* of the storm track."

Freya and Mara looked at each other. It took perhaps a second and a half.

"No," Cass said.

"We could cut inside the—"

"*No.*"

But she was already moving to the chart table, because that was the thing about Cass: she had spent her whole career learning exactly why a thing shouldn't be done, which meant she was uniquely qualified to find the one way it could be. She drew the line herself, muttering about idiots and eel and the general decline of institutional standards since she'd stopped being an institution.

* * *

The outer bands hit at dusk, warm rain like applause, and the *Provision* ran the inside channel with the whistle-dent singing and all three of them grinning in a way that would have alarmed their former adversaries considerably.

Pell, they heard, had been moved to a facility inland where the windows were painted to look like open water, which everyone agreed was either very cruel or very kind depending on the day. The Optimists' records had been seized, scattered, and in several memorable cases used as kindling by villages with long memories and short tempers. Freya's debt had died uncollected—not forgiven, exactly. *Outlived.*

He thought about that sometimes, in the quiet hours. How he'd spent years doing arithmetic in his head—what he owed against what he owned—and how the equation had finally been solved not by payment but by subtraction: remove the ledger entirely, and see what remains.

What remained was a boat, a crew of two, a whistling dent, and a great deal of smoked eel incoming.

"Mara," he called forward, where she stood in the rain with the anemometer, hair plastered to her face, laughing at the wind gauge like it was telling her jokes. "Reading?"

"Sixty-one knots and climbing! Harbor master's going to need a bigger eel budget!"

"That's not how budgets work!"

"Neither is chasing hurricanes for fish, and yet!"

Cass, at the helm, shook her head with the slow, deep contentment of a woman who had made peace with the fact that this was her life now. She kept the vial of ordinary river water on a cord around her neck. She never explained it. Once, when a village child asked, she'd said, "It's a reminder that some things are exactly what they appear to be, and that's rarer than magic." The child had nodded solemnly and gone back to trying to eat a crab.

* * *

They made the lee of Little Verren's point as the eyewall grazed the coast, and the survey went fast after that—the storm, obligingly, stirred up the bottom so thoroughly that the shoals practically introduced themselves. By midnight they had the chart drafted by lamplight in the harbor master's office while the wind tried the shutters and lost, and the harbor master—a wide woman named Odile with forearms like dock rope—looked at the finished map and then at the three of them and said:

"You could have sold this. To anyone. Insurance men, developers, the government. Why give it to us?"

Mara answered first, the way she always answered first now, her father's chart case slung over her shoulder out of habit though it held nothing but spare pencils.

"Because maps are promises," she said. "Depends who you're promising."

Odile looked at Freya.

"He agrees with me," Mara said.

"I'm learning to," Freya said, and Odile laughed, and the eel was excellent.

* * *

Afterward, in the eye's strange calm, they walked the village seawall under a sky full of stars and the distant roar of the storm's far side still working itself out over open water. The villagers had come out too—it was tradition here, walking the wall during the eye, checking lines and lanterns and each other—and the whole scene had the feel of a town exhaling.

A child tugged Freya's sleeve. One of Odile's grandchildren, maybe; they all seemed to be Odile's grandchildren.

"Is it true you found the river that flows backward?"

Freya crouched down to the child's level. Behind him he felt Mara go still, listening, and Cass stop pretending not to listen.

"It's true I found where it used to be," he said.

"What happened to it?"

"It went home." He considered. "Some things are better underground. Like potatoes. And secrets."

The child frowned with tremendous seriousness. "My grandmother says the river remembers people."

Freya looked past the child, at the dark line of the coast, at the water beyond it where a cave mouth had been and wasn't, where a spring had held every face that ever drank from it until the faces were set free.

"Your grandmother," he said, "is right."

* * *

They slept aboard that night, rocked by the storm's trailing swells, and in the morning the world came back rinsed and shining, and they chased the storm's exit the way you'd see off a friend—out past the point, into the clean northwest wind, mapping as they went because mapping was just paying attention, and paying attention was just love with instruments attached.

Cass stood at the bow with her tea, watching the swell patterns. "Coastline north of here's unmapped since the shift," she said. "Fishing communities, three of them. Storm season's not over."

"So we go north," Mara said.

"So we go north," Freya agreed, and put the tiller over, and the *Provision* leaned into the wind like something glad.

He watched the water slide past—water that had taken a cave and a legend and a debt and given back, in exchange, a life he hadn't known to ask for—and he understood, finally, in the way you understand things not with your mind but with your hands on the wood and salt in your teeth:

the wilderness forgot nothing. It simply chose, like them, what to carry forward.
