## 40. Chapter 40

The wedding, if it could be called that, happened on a Tuesday, in the rain, with an audience of one gull and a very judgmental crab.

"You're standing on my foot," Freya said.

"I'm standing on the deck," Mara said. "Your foot is a scheduling error."

They had anchored the *Provision* off a spit of sand near the middle reach, where the river met the sea in a handshake that had finally, after everything, stopped arguing about which direction it wanted to flow. The water ran seaward now, honest and gray, and the tide behaved the way tides were supposed to, which Freya still found slightly suspicious, like a dog that had stopped barking at the mail carrier and now watched him with polite interest.

Cass had come out on the mail skiff, because of course she had. She stood at the rail in civilian clothes she clearly didn't trust, holding a paper bag that smelled improbably of cinnamon.

"You know," she said, "in thirty-one years of service, I have attended exactly one wedding, and it was between two hydrographic surveyors, and it was rained out, and the groom caught pneumonia, and I swore I'd never do this again."

"Then why are you here?" Mara asked, adjusting the knot on the line she'd strung between the mast and the rail as a kind of canopy. It was not a canopy. It was a line. But the intention was there, and intention, Freya had learned, was most of what a ceremony needed.

"Because I burned my orders," Cass said. "And when you burn your orders, you have to show up to things. It's in the regulations. The imaginary ones."

* * *

The rain came down in the soft, steady way of the early monsoon season—not the landfall monster of that terrible spring, but its smaller cousin, the kind of rain that made the world feel rinsed. Freya had argued for waiting for clear weather. Mara had argued that they had both spent their whole lives waiting for clear weather, and look where it got them: debt, drowning, and a dead man's chart. The rain was honest. The rain showed up.

So: Tuesday. Rain. One gull, which had landed on the rail with the proprietary air of a landlord inspecting renovations. One crab, which had come aboard at Salt Hollow and survived three attempts at eviction and now lived under the bait locker, where Mara had named it Registrar.

"You named the crab Registrar," Freya had said.

"Somebody on this boat should have a stable job."

Cass cleared her throat. She had offered, weeks ago, to officiate, on the grounds that she had once notarized a fishing license and therefore understood the sanctity of documents. Freya suspected she'd offered because she wanted to be present for something that wasn't a burial, a resignation, or a report stating that a miracle had never existed. He didn't say so. Some kindnesses you let stand unexamined, like a load-bearing wall.

"Dearly beloved," Cass began, and then stopped. "Actually, no. I refuse to say that. Dearly present. We are here to witness the merger of two independent operators into a single concern, hereinafter referred to as—"

"Don't," said Freya and Mara together.

"—as *them*," Cass amended. "Fine. Freya Zhou. Do you have anything to say?"

Freya had prepared things. He'd written them on the back of a tide table, which was fitting, because everything important in his life had been written on the back of something else—a debt on the back of a dream, a confession on the back of a fire, a proposal on the back of a hull repair estimate that had taken him four tries to spell *fiberglass*.

But standing there, with the rain ticking on the deck and Mara looking at him with that expression that was half challenge and half harbor light, the prepared things evaporated, and what came out was simpler and truer.

"I spent years doing arithmetic," he said. "What I owed against what I owned. I always came up short. And then I met you, and you looked at my books—the real ones, the ones with my crew and my guilt and my terrible boat—and you didn't audit me. You just... climbed aboard." He turned his hand over; she took it. "I'm not going to promise you calm water. I'm constitutionally incapable of it. But I promise you I'll always tell you which way the current's running. Even when it's running backward. *Especially* then."

Mara laughed, the short, surprised laugh he'd spent a year learning to earn.

"My father mapped a river that flowed the wrong way," she said. "Everyone told him he was mistaken. The Survey Office told him he was mistaken. The whole coast told him he was mistaken. And he kept drawing it, because he believed the water over the paperwork." She squeezed his hand. "I spent nine years wanting justice for him. What I found instead was the truth, and a man who also believes the water over the paperwork. That's better than justice. That's *company*." She looked at Cass. "We'll take it. Whatever the paperwork is."

"There is no paperwork," Cass said. "There's a cinnamon bun, and there's my word, and as of four months ago my word is the only government instrument I trust." She handed over the paper bag. "By the power vested in me by absolutely no one, and witnessed by one gull of dubious character—I now pronounce you a going concern."

The gull screamed. The Registrar emerged from under the bait locker, considered the ceremony, and returned to the shadows, unmoved. The rain kept falling, gentle as a hand on the shoulder.

They kissed. It was, Freya would later insist, entirely dry, given the circumstances, and entirely the best moment of his life, which was saying something, because his life had recently included a spring that remembered everything and a flood that forgave most of it.

* * *

Afterward, they sailed down the coast to Cape Verren, because there was a grave to visit.

Tomás Ilesanmi's bones had come home in the spring, carried up from the drowned cave by Cass's people in sealed boxes and laid to rest in the churchyard at Salt Hollow, under a stone that Mara had designed herself. It was a simple stone. It read:

**TOMÁS ILESANMI**
**CARTOGRAPHER**
*He drew the water as it was, not as we wished it.*

And beneath, in smaller letters, at Mara's insistence over the stonemason's objections:

*He was right.*

The village had argued about that last line for a week. The Survey Office, post-Cass, had argued longer. But Mara Ilesanmi had inherited her father's stubbornness the way other people inherited chin shapes, and the line stayed, and now fishermen touched it for luck, and children dared each other to read it aloud in one breath, and the stone had gone slightly shiny at the corners from all the handling, which was, Mara said, exactly the kind of erosion her father would have approved of.

They stood there in the drizzle, the two of them and the stone.

"I brought him something," Freya said. He pulled a folded paper from his jacket. It was a chart—hand-drawn, ink still smelling faintly of the cheap stuff he bought from the chandler who watered it, because some things never changed. It showed the coastal river from the sea to the middle reach, every bend corrected, every shoal marked, the backward stretch now running forward, annotated in two hands: his own cramped lettering and Mara's precise, beautiful script.

"He never got to see the finished survey," Freya said. "So. Finished survey."

Mara read it for a long time, her finger tracing the river's spine. At the place where the source had been, there was a small symbol—a spring, crossed out, and beside it, in the margin, a note: *Here the water chose to forget, so that we could choose to remember. See village records, passim. See also: the two of us, currently standing in a churchyard.*

"You put that in the margin of a navigational chart," she said.

"Charts should be honest about what they don't know."

"He would have hated it."

"He would have hated it and then drawn a better version and never shown us."

She laughed, and pressed her palm flat against the stone, and for a moment she wasn't laughing at all, and Freya stood quietly beside her while she told her father, in the wordless way that the dead and the living manage it, that the work was done, and that she was all right, and that she had, against every projection, come out of the wilderness with more than she'd carried in.

Then she tucked the chart under her arm and said, "Come on. The tide's turning, and I want to make the middle reach before dark. The elders are holding that funeral for old Sefu, and I promised we'd sing."

"You sing?"

"Badly. You'll harmonize worse. It'll be perfect."

* * *

The middle reach had changed, and was changing still, the way a river changes—slowly, and then all at once.

The elders no longer kept the ledgers of grievance that had once filled three chests in the meeting house. The chests were still there, Mara had told him, but they'd been repurposed: one held seed corn now, one held the village's collection of children's drawings of the old upward-flowing falls (which had become, in the retelling, taller and more golden every year, the way all true legends grow), and the third held, at the insistence of the eldest woman, a single jar of river water that everyone knew was just river water and everyone agreed, without discussion, never to test.

The first funeral in four generations had been held in the summer. The second was old Sefu's, this evening, under a sky going soft and violet behind the rain clouds.

Freya stood at the back with Mara, both of them soaked, both of them singing a mourning song whose words he only half knew and whose tune he got wrong in a way that made the children giggle, which he suspected was the song's real function. The elders had buried Sefu with his fishing spear and a lock of his wife's hair, and the eldest woman had spoken the words that were new to the village and old as grief: *We will not keep you. We will carry you.*

Afterward, an elder Freya's age—young for a keeper of memory, old in the way of people who had watched their whole village learn to forget on purpose—brought them cups of something warm and sat down on the wet grass beside them without ceremony.

"The river runs right again," the man said. "Most days I'm glad. Some days I miss the falls that went up. My grandmother's face, I still have. My grandmother's *anger*, though—" he shook his head, half laughing, half not. "Gone with the water. Good riddance, my wife says. She's wise."

"She sounds it."

"You're the ones who drowned the spring." It wasn't an accusation. It was said the way you'd note the weather.

"We're the ones who opened the gates," Freya said. "Tomás built them."

"Tomás built them," the man agreed. "But you pulled the lever. There's a difference. My grandmother used to say: the carpenter makes the door, but it's the one who's fleeing who opens it." He drank. "The village talked, after. About what we owe you."

"You don't owe us anything."

"Of course we don't. That's what makes it a gift." He reached into his shirt and produced a small oilcloth packet, worn soft with handling. "But we made you something anyway. The elders. It took all summer."

Mara took it and unwrapped it with the care of a woman who had learned, the hard way, what old paper could hold.

Inside was a chart.

Not of the river. Not of the coast. It was a chart of the *Provision*—her lines, her patched hull, the mended place where the collectors' grapnel had torn her, the new strake Freya had steamed and bent himself—drawn with the loving, obsessive precision of people who had once mapped a spring that remembered everything, applied now to a boat that deserved it less and needed it more. In the corner, in the eldest woman's hand, was an inscription:

*So that wherever you go, someone has already remembered you kindly.*

Mara didn't say anything for a while. Freya looked out at the river, which was running seaward in the dusk, carrying the last of the light down to the sea, and found that his eyes were doing something embarrassing, and decided, as he had decided many times that year, to let them.

"We'll keep her in trim," he managed finally. "She'll be worth the chart."

"She doesn't have to be," the elder said, standing, brushing grass from his robe. "That's the whole point of the chart."

* * *

They sailed for Salt Hollow the next morning, and it was there, at the harbor office, that the hook of the future finally showed itself above the waterline.

Cass was waiting on the quay with the mail, which these days meant actual mail, plus one item she was holding the way she used to hold demolition charges: carefully, and like it might go off.

"Two things," she said. "First, the Survey Office, in its infinite post-scandal wisdom, has created a new position. Coastal Liaison. Independent. Unbudgeted. Reporting to no one, because everyone who might have been my superior has either resigned or been arrested. They're offering it to me."

"Are you taking it?"

"I'm considering it. It comes with a skiff." She paused. "And a budget for hiring consultants."

"We're not consultants," Mara said.

"I haven't asked you anything yet."

"You were about to."

"I was *going* to be about to," Cass said with dignity, "after the second thing." She held out the envelope. It was heavy paper, official, sealed with the crest of the Provisional Authority—the same crest, Freya noted, that had once signed Tomás's death warrant and Cass's orders and every other piece of ash that had ever tasted like duty.

Freya opened it. Read it. Read it again.

"What is it?" Mara asked.

"It's a request," Freya said slowly. "From the villages on the outer coast. Twelve of them. They've petitioned the Authority for a proper survey—charts of the shoals, the storm routes, the safe harbors. The old charts were syndicate property, and the syndicate's records were seized, and nobody's mapped that coast in thirty years." He looked up. "They asked for us. By name. Both of us. And—" he turned the page over, and there it was, in a clerk's careful hand, "—they've heard we work for the water, not the paperwork."

Cass smiled the small, dry smile of a woman who had burned her orders and never once regretted the smoke.

"Well," she said. "The wilderness forgot nothing. It just chose what to carry forward." She nodded at the letter. "So. Consultants. Twelve villages, one coast, one monsoon season, and a skiff that leaks. Are you in?"

Freya looked at Mara. Mara looked at the horizon, where the clouds were stacking up over the outer banks in the tall, bright towers of a storm that hadn't decided yet whether to be beautiful or catastrophic, and which would, either way, be worth chasing.

"We'll need a bigger boat," she said.

"We'll need a *second* boat," Freya said. "And a crab-sitter."

"Registrar comes with us. He's got seniority."

The gull screamed from the harbor roof, as if seconding the motion. The rain held off, just barely, the way rain does when it wants to see what you'll do next.

Freya folded the letter, put it in his jacket over his heart, where he kept the things that had survived every flood, and turned the *Provision*'s bow toward the open water.

"Then let's go get lost," he said, "professionally this time."

And they did.
