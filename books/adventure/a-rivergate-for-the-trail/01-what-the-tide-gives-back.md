## 1. What the Tide Gives Back

The storm had done in one night what Isandro Almeida had spent eleven years refusing to do: it had gone looking for his mother.

He was on the bluff above Greywater Strand when the light found him, a truck's high beams raking the dune grass at half past four, long before any honest surveyor should be wanted anywhere. He'd been sleeping in the cab of his own vehicle, parked where the coast road bent inland, because the tide tables said the spring low would strip another two meters of beach off the strand and he wanted to see what it gave back. That was the job. That was all the job had ever been. Read the ground, mark the boundaries, bill the hours, move on before anyone asked why a man with his credentials kept taking contracts this far down the coast, this close to Rivergate.

He climbed down to the road. The truck idling there was matte black, municipal-ugly, a salvage rig with a winch and a light bar and too much money spent on it for anything legal. The man who leaned out wore a yellow rain shell over a frame like stacked cargo pallets.

"Almeida? The surveyor?"

"Depends who's asking."

"Name's Ostrick. Crew boss, Meridian Salvage." The man handed out a folded printout instead of a hand. "Storm opened something up north of the point. Cave mouth, big as a church door, never on any chart. My people won't go in without a ground assessment. You were recommended."

"By whom?"

"Does it matter?" Ostrick's grin had teeth in it somewhere. "You're the only licensed surveyor within forty klicks who'll work a dawn call. Word gets around."

Isandro took the printout because not taking it would have been worse. Aerial shot of the coastline, grainy, taken maybe an hour ago by drone. The storm surge had peeled back a shelf of collapsed rock below the headland, and behind it — black against grey foam — a cave mouth. Wide. Arched. Water running out of it in a smooth, deliberate ribbon, like something being poured.

He knew that shape.

Not from any chart. Not from any survey archive. From a battered field journal he kept in a waterproof case at the bottom of his gear bag, wrapped in a shirt he no longer wore but couldn't throw away, because his mother had drawn that arch freehand on page 114 and written beneath it, in her slanted, impatient script: *Local legend — "the rivergate." Dismissed. No karst formation on this coast could sustain an opening of this size at sea level. The fishermen are telling each other stories.*

She'd been wrong about the opening. She'd been wrong about a lot of things, or right about them in ways that got her killed, depending on which sleepless hour you asked him.

"I'll need access terms," he heard himself say. "And hazard rates. And I go nowhere near the water line without my own rope."

Ostrick shrugged like a man being handed small change. "Get in the truck."

* * *

The drive north took twenty minutes along a coast road that Isandro had surveyed twice and hated both times. The land here didn't behave. It rose and fell in folds like cloth someone had crumpled and half-smoothed, and underneath it the limestone went down in swallowed channels and drowned galleries that ate wells, ate roads, occasionally ate houses. His mother had loved it. *The ground is honest,* she used to say, tapping her boot on some outcrop, *it just doesn't speak your language.* He had inherited her eye for it — the way a slope's grass grew greener over a seep, the way a hollow held fog longer than the land around it, the way certain stone rang hollow underfoot — and he had spent his whole adult life pretending the inheritance was a coincidence, like eye color, like nothing.

They came down onto the strand as the sky went from iron to dirty pearl. The Meridian crew had already strung floodlights from a generator, and the light turned the wet sand into hammered pewter. Four figures moved around the cave mouth, small against its arch. The sea was still agitated, still working off the storm, waves coming in long and heavy, and every ninth wave or so pushed a white tongue up the beach toward the rocks.

Isandro stood at the edge of the lights and read the ground the way other men breathed, without deciding to.

The collapse shelf was fresh — clean fracture faces, no lichen, edges sharp enough to draw blood. The storm hadn't just exposed the cave; it had removed a plug that had been seated there a very long time. The talus fan below it was sorted wrong, coarser material downslope than up, which meant water had been moving through that pile recently and with intent. And the stream running out of the arch — he crouched, touched it, tasted the tip of one finger — ran cold and faintly mineral, but not saline. Fresh water. Coming out of a sea cliff at the tide's low ebb.

A river that lived underground and came to the door when the ocean stepped back.

His hands were steady. His hands were always steady; that was the joke of him, the surveyor with the surgeon's calm while everything underneath rotted. But his chest had gone tight in a way he recognized from funerals and from the one time he'd opened the box of her things.

"Problem?" Ostrick asked beside him.

"No." It came out flat and true, which surprised him. "It's a resurgence. Tidal. The flow will reverse when the water comes up — probably fast. Anyone inside after the turn is swimming out or not at all."

"So we've got a window."

"You've got a window," Isandro said. "I've got a rate card."

He walked toward the arch. Up close it was worse, or better, or both — the kind of scale that made a man feel correctly sized, which is to say small. The rock around the mouth was cut, and he saw the cuts before he understood he was looking for them: a vertical line crossed by three horizontals, chiseled deep, weathered soft, repeated at intervals up the arch like a tally or a warning. Old. Older than any salvage company, older than the charts.

But at knee height, low on the left jamb, sheltered under an overhang where spray wouldn't reach —

There was a newer mark. Small. A single horizontal stroke with a downward hook at its end, precise as a signature.

He knew that hook. He had grown up watching that hook get made, hundreds of times, in notebooks and on bedrock and once, memorably, on the dining table in ballpoint, for which he'd been blamed. His mother cut her survey marks with a hooked terminal stroke because she was left-handed and impatient and never once in her life corrected it.

Dated marks told you when. Style told you who.

Isandro put his fingers on the stone and did not touch it, hovering a centimeter off the surface, reading heat and texture through the air like a man afraid of waking something. The cut was weeks old at most. Maybe days. The storm had stripped the beach two nights ago.

The dead don't leave fresh marks.

"Almeida." Ostrick's voice, from behind, too casual. "You know whose mark that is?"

The question landed wrong — too knowing, too soon. Isandro straightened slowly and turned. In the generator light, Ostrick's face was professionally blank, and beyond him, up the strand, a fifth figure stood apart from the crew, tall, silver-haired, hands in the pockets of a coat too good for this beach, watching Isandro with the particular patience of a man who has already bought what he's come to collect.

Isandro had never met Corvin Hale. He had seen the man's signature often enough — on the incident report, on the closed file, on the letter that had ended the search for Dr. Vera Almeida eleven years ago. *Presumed lost to coastal flooding. Remains unrecovered.*

"The legend says there's a river runs backward in there," Ostrick said. "Says it goes inland, under the hills, further than anybody's ever followed. We want a route assessment before we commit divers. You're the man for ground truth." He paused. "Unless there's a reason you'd rather not."

Every instinct Isandro owned — the ones he trusted, the ones he'd built his whole evasive life on — told him to walk back to his truck, drive until the coast was a rumor, and send an invoice from another province. Every other part of him, the part with his mother's hooked stroke in its hands, was already inside the dark.

"How much?" he said.

Hale smiled from up the beach, a small, satisfied movement, like a man watching a lock accept his key.

"Come aboard," Hale called, "and we'll discuss it properly. The tide turns in ninety minutes, Mr. Almeida. I'd hate for us to waste the window — your mother never did."

The sea breathed out around their ankles, and somewhere under the headland, faint as a pulse, the river ran on.
