## 2. The Man Who Signed the Report

Corvin Hale's boat was called the *Providence*, which Isandro took as an early sign of bad faith.

She sat at anchor off the salvage barge like something that had wandered in from a better story — sixty feet of white fiberglass and polished teak, radar mast, tender davits, a name stenciled in gold that had already begun to peel at the edges. The salvage crew's barge beside her was all rust streaks and diesel stains, a compressor coughing on the aft deck, coils of rope salted stiff. Between the two vessels lay the whole arithmetic of the expedition: money on one side, work on the other, and Isandro standing in a skiff in the gray morning light, being invited to cross.

"You'll want to tie off yourself," called the deckhand, a broad woman with a sunburned scalp. "Captain doesn't let anyone else touch his lines."

Isandro tied off. He had been on the water since before dawn, first summoned to the cave by a radio call he should have ignored, then kept there by the survey marks he couldn't stop seeing behind his eyes — the double-tick and bar, the date carved clean into flowstone, *V.A. 14/III* — three years after the sea had officially finished with his mother. He had slept maybe two hours on the barge, in a bunk that smelled of kerosene, and woken with his father's old advice sitting on his chest like a cat: *When a rich man asks you aboard, count the exits first.*

There was one exit. The skiff. He noted it, and went up the ladder anyway.

Hale was waiting under the aft canopy, and he was not what Isandro had expected, which was itself a kind of disappointment. Eleven years of imagining the man who had signed his mother's death certificate had built him tall and cruel, some granite monument of officialdom. The man folding a nautical chart at the cockpit table was shorter than Isandro, silver at the temples, with the soft hands and careful posture of someone who had spent decades in offices and only recently begun apologizing for it. He wore a fleece vest over a pressed shirt. He looked, absurdly, like a man about to recommend a good accountant.

"Isandro Almeida." Hale said the name as if checking it against a list. "Sit. Coffee's real, not the freeze-dried horror they drink on the barge."

"I didn't come for coffee."

"No." Hale set the chart aside without hurry. "You came because I asked, and you're angry that you came. Both of those are fine. Sit anyway."

Isandro sat. The canopy shaded them from a sun that hadn't yet committed to rising fully; the water around the anchorage was the color of tarnished pewter, and somewhere behind the headland the cave mouth waited with its impossible date carved into stone.

"You signed the recovery report," Isandro said. No greeting, no preamble. He'd rehearsed nothing, and it came out flatter than he'd intended, which was maybe better. "Eleven years ago. Cause of death: drowned, presumed. Remains: not recovered. You closed the case."

"I did." Hale didn't flinch, didn't perform regret. Isandro found that almost respectable. "I was the regional rescue coordinator. I had a storm, a missing woman last seen entering the water, four days of searching, and a coastline that gives back nothing it takes below the tide line. The report was the truth as I could establish it. I've never believed it was the truth."

The admission landed harder for being delivered so mildly. Isandro's hands, he noticed, had closed on the edge of the table.

"Then why sign it?"

"Because a report is a door that has to close so other doors can open." Hale poured two coffees anyway, set one in front of him. "Because the alternative was leaving a case open that would have kept dredging that coast for a decade and found nothing, and your mother — if she was alive, which I did not then believe — would have been found by dredges. I signed the report to stop the searching. I've spent eleven years doing the searching properly instead."

"Properly," Isandro repeated. "With a salvage crew and a compressor."

"With patience." Hale leaned back. Outside, a gull screamed once and gave up. "I'll be direct with you, because I'm told you respond poorly to indirection. Eleven years ago I was the coordinator on your mother's last expedition support. She came to me two days before she vanished with a theory she wouldn't fully share — an underground river on the Rivergate coast, tide-fed, running inland against every chart. She wanted a permit extension and a second dive team. I refused the extension. I told her the legend she was chasing was a fisherman's story and she was too good a scientist to chase it. She went out anyway. Alone, or alone enough."

"You refused her." Isandro's voice came out very even, which surprised him. Under the evenness was something molten. "She asked for help and you refused her, and then you wrote the paper that buried her."

"I wrote the report that protected her," Hale said, "from people like the men I'm about to describe to you. And then I spent my own money, year after year, trying to find the river she died — or didn't die — chasing. The cave your crew exposed two nights ago is the first hard feature I've ever had. The first thing I could put a line on a chart." He turned the chart back around and Isandro saw it: the coastline, the headland, and a penciled circle with coordinates that matched, to the decimal, the ones in his mother's journals. Not similar. Identical.

Isandro looked at the circle for a long time. The coffee steamed untouched between them.

"Where did you get those coordinates?"

"Where do you think?"

"I think you went through her effects," Isandro said. "I think you went through *my* mother's effects while my family was still planning a memorial, and you copied her numbers, and you've been hunting her work ever since without telling a soul, and now the sea has conveniently opened the door for you and you need someone who can read the ground because your own people nearly drowned in there two nights ago." He stood. The skiff bobbed below, patient. "I'll save us both the afternoon. No."

"Sit down, Mr. Almeida."

"No."

"Then hear the rest standing." Hale didn't rise. That was a choice too, Isandro realized — a man making himself small and harmless at the exact moment he stopped being either. "I know about your licenses."

The morning went very quiet.

"I know that the coastal survey credentials you've been working under for six years were issued on the strength of a transcript from a program you attended for one semester," Hale continued, in the tone of a man reading a tide table. "I know the renewal stamps are real — you've got a friend in the registrar's office in Porto Serra who's fond of you — but the foundation is paper. I know that if it came apart, the engineering firm that subcontracts you would be liable for every channel survey you've ever stamped, and they would come after you for all of it, and there is a version of the next two years where you lose the work, the license, and most of what you've earned. I'm not threatening you. I'm telling you what I know, because I'd rather you understood your position than discovered it later."

Isandro stood very still. The gull again. The compressor on the barge, coughing. His own heartbeat, annoyingly loud.

Six years of work. Six years of reading coastlines for other people, of being the man they called when the ground didn't make sense, and the whole time standing on a floor he'd built out of one semester and a friend's rubber stamp. He had told himself it didn't matter — that the talent was real even if the paper wasn't, that his mother's eye lived in him regardless of what any registrar said. He had told himself a great many things.

"You went through her effects," Isandro said slowly, "and mine."

"I went through everything. That's what patience is." Hale finally stood, and up close his eyes were the pale, unremarkable gray of winter water. "Here's what I'm offering, and it's the only version I'm offering. You come aboard as terrain guide. Not surveyor — I'm not asking you to stamp anything, and your paper never touches this expedition. You read the ground, the rock, the water, the way your mother read it, because I have watched your work for three years, Mr. Almeida, and you do read it the way she did, and we both know the credentials were never the point. You take us to the cave mouth and you interpret what's in front of the entrance — the approach, the tide behavior, the rockfall. That's all. Past the mouth, my team works. You're free to leave."

"And the licenses?"

"Will never be mentioned by me to anyone, for any reason, whether you say yes or no." Hale said it plainly, and Isandro searched the man's face for the hook in it and couldn't find one, which was worse. "I don't need leverage on you. I need your eyes. Leverage buys compliance; it doesn't buy care. I want you to care."

"You want me to care," Isandro said, "about your treasure hunt."

"I want you to care about the date carved inside that cave." For the first time, something moved under the mildness — a current under ice. "Three years after I declared her dead. I've read her published work, Mr. Almeida. I've read the survey conventions she invented. That mark is hers or it's a forgery good enough to break my heart, and either way I need to know, and you are the only person on this coast who can tell me. Name your fee. I'm not going to insult you with a first number."

Isandro looked at the chart. The penciled circle. The coffee going cold.

He thought about the money — he made himself think about the money, made himself hold it up like a lamp: the debts on the boat he was still paying for, the registrar's quiet generosity that could not last forever, the small apartment where he kept his mother's journals in a waterproof case under the bed like contraband. A fee like the one Hale would pay would buy him clean. New credentials, real ones, a life where no one could ever again look at him the way this man was looking at him now — like a file that had been read.

*You're in it for the money,* he told himself. *That's the whole story. Walk in, read the rock, walk out, get paid. Nothing else.*

He was aware, distantly, of how much of him was already lying.

"One condition," Isandro said. "I guide you to the mouth. I read the approach, the tide, the rock. Inside, I go no further than the first chamber — far enough to look at those marks with my own eyes. Then I'm done. You can keep my fee whether you find your river or not."

"Done," Hale said immediately, and extended his hand, and Isandro looked at it for a moment — the soft palm, the careful man, the signature that had ended his mother's life on paper — and shook it, because a deal was a door and he wanted through it.

"Teodora Reyes will run your dive support," Hale added, as Isandro turned to go. "She asked for you specifically. Said you pulled her brother's boat off a bar at Caster's Reach in a fog no sane person would have worked in. Said you read water like it was talking to you."

"It doesn't talk," Isandro said. "It repeats itself. You just have to have been listening long enough."

Hale smiled for the first time, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "That," he said, "is exactly what she used to say."

Isandro stopped with one hand on the rail.

The water below was gray and patient. Somewhere behind the headland, the tide was beginning to think about turning.

"When do we go in?" he asked, and hated how much of him wanted the answer to be *now*.

"Tonight's low tide is at 21:40," Hale said. "We go in on the ebb. Mr. Almeida — welcome aboard the *Providence*."

* * *

He told himself the fee was the reason all the way across the skiff, all the way through the salvage crew's preparations, all the way through the long gray afternoon while the dive gear was checked and the barge repositioned into the shadow of the headland. He told himself it while Teodora Reyes, compact and quiet, showed him the rebreather rigs without being asked whether he'd used one. He told himself it while the sun went down and the tide began its long withdrawal, and the cave mouth emerged from the sea like a held breath finally let out.

But when the first chamber's lantern light found the wall — the double-tick and bar, fresh-edged, unmistakable — Isandro understood that the money had only ever been the story he'd brought to keep the other thing from eating him.

Because the mark wasn't alone. Below it, in the same hand, in the same confident cut, was a second line. A direction.

An arrow, pointing deeper.
