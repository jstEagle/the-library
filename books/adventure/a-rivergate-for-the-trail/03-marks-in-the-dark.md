## 3. Marks in the Dark

The cave mouth swallowed the morning whole.

Isandro stood at the threshold while the salvage crew rigged lights behind him, watching the tide drain out of the rock like the sea exhaling. The opening was a ragged arch maybe four meters high, hung with kelp and crusted barnacles that told him everything he needed to know: the sea came in here twice a day, and it came in angry. Below the arch, a channel of standing water ran back into blackness, smooth as poured glass, too still for something that connected to the ocean.

"Air's moving," said Teodora Reyes beside him. She had her head tipped back, eyes closed, reading the cave the way he read it — with her skin. "Feel it? It breathes."

He felt it. A faint cool current against his face, flowing *out* of the dark, carrying the smell of wet limestone and something else — something mineral and old, like the inside of a bell.

"River behind it," Isandro said. "Underground. The tide's holding it back right now. Give it three hours and this whole mouth will be a siphon."

Hale came up the shingle behind them, boots crunching, already sweating in his expedition fleece. "Then we have three hours. Reyes, lights. Almeida, you're on point. That's what I'm paying for."

Isandro didn't answer. He stepped down onto the wet rock at the cave's lip and crouched, letting his fingers rest on the stone the way his mother had taught him before he was old enough to know he was being taught. *Ground tells you what it's carried.* The rock here was polished in a band a meter wide — water-worn, but not by salt spray. Freshwater polish. And cut into that band, at knee height, where a person wading would naturally brace a hand—

He stopped breathing.

A survey mark.

A chiseled cross, four centimeters, with a short diagonal slash through the lower-left quadrant and a tick above the right arm. He'd seen that mark ten thousand times. In the margins of her journals. On the doorframe of the field station in Cazalla where he'd grown up counting days until she came home. It was her personal variant, a habit so specific that when he'd once asked her about it she'd laughed and said, *It's how I sign the ground, mijo. So the ground remembers me.*

The mark was old — edges softened by mineral film, years of it — but it was there, and it was hers, and it was cut into rock inside a cave that had not existed in any survey, any chart, any record, until a storm tore it open three weeks ago.

"Almeida." Hale's voice, impatient. "Problem?"

Isandro stood. His heart was doing something ugly and fast against his ribs. "No," he said. "Let's move."

* *

They went in with four headlamps and a spool of guide line, wading the channel where it shallowed, the water thigh-cold and tasting of stone. The passage opened quickly — that was the first wrong thing. Caves this close to a coast were usually choked, collapsed, cramped. This one widened like a throat accepting a swallowed thing, the ceiling rising until their lights couldn't find it, the walls sheeted in flowstone the color of old bone.

Isandro counted paces and marked turns in his head, the way he always did, and at forty meters he found the second mark.

Then the third. A benchmark this time, a triangle with the same diagonal slash, cut at the base of a flowstone column — and beside it, numbers. Elevation, in her shorthand: a superscript decimal, the digits slanted the way she slanted everything, left-leaning, hurried, like the writing was always trying to outrun her hand.

He crouched over it for a long time. Behind him the crew's voices echoed — Okafor and Brandt arguing about load distribution, Grell complaining about the water in his boots — and their noise felt very far away, like sounds from another country.

"Almeida." Teodora had drifted back to him, her light angled low. "You've stopped walking twice in ten minutes. What are you seeing?"

"Survey marks."

"So? Hale said you'd find evidence of prior entry. That's the whole—"

"Dated." He ran his thumb along the numerals beside the benchmark. Mineral film had grown over the cuts — a thin skin of it, translucent, the kind of deposit that took *years*. Not months. Years. "These were cut at least eight, nine years ago. Maybe more."

Teodora was quiet a moment. "So someone surveyed this cave before the storm opened it."

"Someone surveyed this cave before the storm *existed*." He kept his voice flat. "The collapse that exposed the mouth took out forty meters of cliff. There was no way in. No way in, no way to know it was here — unless you already knew."

"Then it's coincidence. Some old surveyor used the same notation. Marks get copied. Styles propagate."

Isandro looked at her. Her light caught the mark, and the diagonal slash, and the tick above the right arm, and he watched her see it — really see it — and watched her expression change.

"That's not a style," he said. "That's a signature. And the woman who used it has been dead for eleven years."

* *

Hale called a halt at sixty meters, where the passage split around a pillar of flowstone, and the crew broke out instruments. Isandro used the stop to work. He didn't decide to; his hands just did it, the old discipline rising in him like water into a dry channel. He found four more marks in twenty minutes, each one dated in her notation, each one older than the last as they went deeper — a sequence, a *progression*, someone working inward over multiple visits, multiple years.

Eleven years of dead. And the marks kept going.

He was crouched at the fifth — a station mark with a date he had to make himself read twice, because the mineral film over the numerals was thicker than the last, meaning *older*, meaning the sequence ran backward into years when Vera Almeida was, according to every record and every court and every search grid on this coast, a body never recovered — when Grell's voice came echoing up the passage, pitched wrong.

"Hey. Hey, the water's—"

Isandro was already moving. Not because of Grell. Because of the sound.

The channel behind them had changed its voice. Water that had been silent was now making a low, guttural note, a sound he'd heard once before, at a flood site in the north country, seconds before a dry wadi became a river. He turned his headlamp down the passage and saw it: the standing water at the entrance channel, a hundred meters back, had begun to *creep*. Moving toward them. Smooth, silent, fast.

"Out," Isandro said. Not loudly. The word carried anyway — something in the flatness of it made every head turn. "Now. The tide turned early. Move."

"Early? My tables say—" Okafor started.

"Your tables are for the open coast. This is a siphon system — it doesn't run on the same clock. It runs ahead of it." He was already pushing past them, toward the rear, toward the water. "Line. Keep the line. Single file, *move*."

The water was at their calves before they'd gone thirty meters. Then their knees. The cave's gentle breathing had become a wind in their faces, cold and steady and wrong — air being *pushed*, displaced, driven ahead of the flood like a warning shot. Isandro ran the arithmetic as he moved: inflow rate, passage volume, the rise he could see lipping over the channel walls behind them. The entrance arch was still open. Barely. A black slot of night sky, shrinking.

"Left wall!" he shouted. "There's a shelf — high ground — *up*!"

They went up it in a scrabble of hands and boots, Grell last, hauled up by Brandt's grip on his pack strap, the water chasing their heels up the rock. And then it stopped rising. Just like that. Settled a half meter below their ledge and went smooth again, glass-smooth, patient, as if it had made its point.

The cave breathed around them. In, now. The current had reversed.

For a long moment nobody spoke. Then Hale, perched on the ledge with his light shaking only slightly, said: "Your tables were wrong, Almeida."

"My tables were right." Isandro was looking back down the passage, at the water, at the dark beyond it. "This system runs early. It's been running early for years. Someone learned that. Someone survived learning that." He turned. "Someone who left marks in there dated after her death. You want to tell me again it's coincidence, Hale? Because you've read her file. You know her notation better than anyone alive. You *signed the report*."

Hale's light held steady on him. In the beam, the older man's face was unreadable, and that unreadability was itself an answer.

"The tide's in for six hours," Hale said finally. "We're here until it drops. So we might as well work." He turned away, calling the crew to set camp on the shelf, and the conversation was over, and nothing was settled at all.

* *

Isandro didn't sleep.

He sat at the edge of the ledge with his light off, listening to the water breathe, and in his lap he held the field journal — his mother's, the one Hale's money had bought its way into his hands, the one he'd told himself he'd brought for professional reference.

He found the page he'd been avoiding since dawn. A sketch, eleven years old, of a cave mouth that shouldn't exist, in a legend she'd dismissed in the same entry: *the rivergate story is a fisherman's fable, no hydrology supports it.* And beneath the sketch, in her hurried left-leaning hand, a line he'd read a hundred times and never understood:

*If the ground remembers me, I'll come back for it.*

The water breathed. Somewhere behind them, deeper in, past the flood, past the marks that could not exist, the dark went on.

And Isandro understood, with a certainty that felt less like hope than like a wound reopening, that the dead don't leave fresh marks.

Someone was in there. And whoever it was had been keeping the river's schedule for eleven years — and had known, somehow, that they were coming.
