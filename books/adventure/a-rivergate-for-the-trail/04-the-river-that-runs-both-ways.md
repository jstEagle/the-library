## 4. The River That Runs Both Ways

The river did it again at 14:07, and this time Isandro was watching.

He'd been crouched at the water's edge for two hours with a thermos of coffee gone bitter, pretending to log sediment samples while the cave's underground channel ran past his boots. For most of the morning it had flowed seaward — a clear, quick current carrying leaf-rot and silt toward the drowned mouth of the cave. Then, without any drama at all, a floating twig he'd been tracking stalled, shivered, turned, and began moving inland. Against the light. Against everything the charts said an underground river should do.

He watched it go for thirty meters before it slipped beneath a shelf of black stone.

"Reyes," he called, not raising his voice. "Come look at your river."

Teodora Reyes picked her way down the slick rock in her drysuit, half-zipped, hair still damp from the morning's dive. She crouched beside him, followed his pointing finger to the current, and said nothing for a long moment. He'd learned that about her in three days: she didn't waste words on wonder. She measured it.

"Tide's turning," she said finally.

"It's four kilometers from open water."

"So?"

"So there's no tidal influence documented this far into any cave system on this coast. I've read every survey." He heard how it sounded — *I've read every survey* — like a man confessing to memorizing a dead woman's handwriting. Teodora glanced at him but let it pass.

"Then your charts are wrong," she said, "or they're not your charts."

She pulled a strip of surveyor's tape from her kit, weighted it with a bolt, and dropped it into the current. It streamed inland, taut as a fishing line. Then she took out her notebook — an actual paper notebook, which had earned his quiet respect on day one — and wrote the time, the direction, the depth reading from her gauge.

"You're logging it," he said.

"I log everything. Habit." She snapped the elastic closed. "Hale wants flow rates by tonight. He's very interested in where the water goes when it runs backward."

"Everyone's interested in where the water goes."

"No," Teodora said, standing. "Everyone's interested in what's at the other end of it. The water's just the road." She looked back down the passage, toward the main camp where Grell was arguing with Okafor about generator fuel and Hale stood at the map table with his back to them all, the posture of a man who trusted no one to see his face while he worked. "You should ask yourself why a man who signed a death certificate eleven years ago is spending this much money on a legend she wrote down."

Isandro felt the words land somewhere below his ribs.

"She dismissed the legend," he said. "Her journal says so. 'Folkloric accretion, no hydrological basis.'"

"Mm." Teodora was already walking away. "Funny thing about people who dismiss things, Almeida. Sometimes they're wrong. And sometimes they know exactly what they're dismissing, and want everyone else to stop looking."

*

They ran the dye test at slack water, when the river hung between its two directions like a held breath.

Okafor poured the fluorescein into the channel upstream and the water went green, a bright arterial stain unspooling through the dark. Isandro tracked it with a UV lamp while Grell and Hale waited at the secondary station forty meters along, and for six minutes everything behaved. The dye moved seaward, thinning, dying.

Then the tide turned early.

Not gradually — not the lazy reversal of the morning. The current simply stopped, quivered, and reversed inside ninety seconds, and the green came back at them like something recalled. It pooled against the rock shelf where the channel bent inland, swirled, and began climbing — visibly climbing, the surface tilting, the whole river leaning uphill through a passage the laser level had shown descending.

"The hell is it doing?" Grell's voice cracked high in the dark.

"Filling," Isandro said quietly. He was watching the water climb the walls, marking the old tide lines — bands of mineral staining on the stone, one above another above another, going up further than his lamp reached. "It fills from the far end. There's a reservoir inland. A big one. When the sea pushes, the source pushes back harder, and the whole system backs up until—" He stopped.

"Until what?"

"Until it finds another way out." He played the lamp across the ceiling. Forty years of flood debris hung in cracks eight meters overhead: kelp stems, driftwood, a fisherman's float, gray and soft as bone. "This isn't a cave with a river in it. It's a pipe. And somebody's been turning the tap for a long time."

Hale arrived at his shoulder without sound, which Isandro was beginning to understand was a skill the man had never lost from his rescue-coordinator days — moving through disaster sites like a man who'd learned that noise attracted the dying.

"You've seen this before," Hale said. Not a question.

"In my mother's notes. She hypothesized a pressurized inland reservoir feeding the coastal seeps. Her peers tore it apart. No geology to support it." He kept his eyes on the water. "The geology's here. She just couldn't prove it from outside."

"And you can prove it from inside." Something moved behind Hale's voice, patient and cold as the deep water. "Which is why you're here, Almeida. Whatever else you tell yourself."

The dye-light faded. The river ran inland, steady now, obedient, as if the whole display had been a courtesy — a demonstration for visitors.

*

That night Isandro couldn't sleep in the heat of the tents pitched just inside the cave mouth, and went out to the tideline to sit with the sound of the sea working the rocks.

Teodora was already there.

She sat on a fallen slab with her boots off, smoking, which she did only after dark and only alone, or so she believed. She didn't startle when he sat down at the other end of the slab. They watched the phosphorescence stitch itself through the surf for a while.

"Two expeditions," she said.

He turned.

"Hale's. Before yours. Before mine." She drew on the cigarette, and the ember lit the flat, unreadable geography of her face. "Three years ago, a salvage outfit out of Corvo — he bankrolled it, ran it from the support boat, never got wet himself. They found a cave system north of here. Two divers went in on a flooding tide. One body came back. The other never did. Hale paid the widow quietly and the incident report says 'unauthorized recreational diving.' His name isn't in it. I checked."

"How did you check?"

"Because I dove for the second one." She flicked ash. "Eighteen months ago. Marine archaeology contract, legitimate, or so the paperwork said. Six of us. We were mapping seeps along this coast when Hale's people bought our client out from under us mid-survey and repointed the whole operation at one specific headland. This one." She looked at him. "We found the seep field. We found warm water coming out of solid granite, which doesn't happen. And then Grell — who was on that crew too, ask him sometime why he owes Hale — dropped a charge to widen an entrance, and the whole section collapsed, and we spent nine hours digging Maritza out of a crack while the tide came in. Nobody died that time. Barely counts as a success."

The sea filled the silence. Isandro found his hands had gone cold around the thermos.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because tomorrow we follow the river inland, and you're going to be making decisions down there, and a man makes better decisions when he knows who's paying him and why." She stubbed the cigarette against the stone. "Hale didn't sign your mother's death report because he believed she was dead, Almeida. I've read it. I've read all of them. The report is too clean. Too complete. You don't write a document that thorough unless you need it to close a door."

"He was the rescue coordinator. Closing doors is the job."

"Sure." She stood, pulling her boots on. "And eleven years later he's spent — conservatively — two million of his own money chasing a folkloric accretion with no hydrological basis. Through two buried expeditions. Using a man whose licenses he owns and a diver he bailed out of a lawsuit." She slung her gear over her shoulder. "I'm not telling you to run. I'm telling you I'm done being lied to by employers, so I stopped lying to myself. I'm here because I want to see the source before anyone decides what it's worth. Whatever Hale thinks is at the end of that river — he knew your mother was looking for it. Maybe he knows why she stopped."

She walked back toward the tents. Isandro stayed on the slab a long time, listening to the tide come in, thinking about the word *stopped*, and whether a woman who stopped looking would leave fresh survey marks cut into stone dated after her own death.

He thought about his mother's last journal entry, the one he'd read a thousand times and never understood: *The water keeps honest accounts. So must we.*

He'd always taken it as a professional note. Sitting there in the dark with a stranger's warnings settling into him like silt, it sounded suddenly like a warning meant for someone specific.

For whoever came after her.

For him.

Behind him, deep in the cave, the river changed direction with a sound like a long exhale, and began, once more, to run both ways.
