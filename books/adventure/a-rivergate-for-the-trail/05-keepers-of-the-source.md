## 5. Keepers of the Source

The tide gave them four hours, and Teodora called it like a woman reading a clock only she could see.

"Water's turning at eleven-forty," she said, crouched at the river's edge where it slid dark and glassy between walls of wet black stone. "After that, this channel runs the wrong way. We go now or we don't go."

Isandro checked his watch against hers out of habit. They matched to the minute. It was the only thing about this expedition that did.

Behind them, Hale stood with his arms folded, watching the water with the flat patience of a man who had spent money on worse gambles. Grell shifted his weight from foot to foot, his lamp swinging wild arcs across the ceiling. The other two — Okafor and the kid they'd hired in Rivergate whose name Isandro still hadn't bothered learning — checked their gear in silence. Four days since Grell had nearly drowned them all in the first sump. Three days since Isandro had watched Hale's face while they waited for Grell to surface and understood, finally and completely, what kind of man he was following. He had not said anything. He had written nothing in his log. He had simply started sleeping with his boots on.

"Lead," Hale said. Not a request.

Isandro shouldered his pack and stepped into the shallows.

The river ran both ways here — he'd stopped questioning that on the second day. At low tide it crawled inland through channels no chart acknowledged, threading deeper into the headland than any cave system had a right to go, and at high tide the sea came up its throat and drowned everything they'd walked. The maps in his mother's journals had shown a coastline. They had not shown this. Either she'd never found it, or she'd found it and chosen not to write it down, and Isandro had spent three days turning that thought over like a stone looking for what lived underneath.

The passage narrowed, then opened without warning into a gallery tall enough to lose their lamplight in. Isandro swept his beam across it and stopped walking so abruptly that Okafor nearly went into his back.

"Problem?" Hale asked.

Isandro didn't answer. He was looking at the floor.

Stone channels ran across the gallery in deliberate lines — cut, not eroded. They branched and rejoined like the veins of a leaf, shallow troughs worn smooth at their edges, each one angled to catch water seeping down the far wall and carry it somewhere inland. Someone had engineered this. Someone had maintained it, because the channels were clear of the silt and rubble that choked every natural passage Isandro had ever surveyed. Clear the way a garden path is clear.

"That's not karst," Teodora said quietly beside him. She'd gone very still. "That's work."

"It's drainage." Isandro heard his own voice come out level, professional, and hated how much effort the levelness cost. "Diverted seepage. Could be old."

"How old?"

He crouched and put his fingers in one of the channels. The stone at the bottom was polished to a soft gloss. Water, given centuries, could do that. So could hands, given years. He wiped his fingers on his thigh and said nothing, because the honest answer was *I don't know*, and the true answer was *not long enough ago*.

They followed the channels upstream.

* * *

An hour in, the offerings began.

The first was almost nothing: a shelf of natural rock at shoulder height, swept clean, holding three objects arranged with a precision that made Isandro's skin prickle. A cup carved from pale stone. A knot of dried kelp, tied off in a pattern too regular to be accidental. And a fish bone, split lengthwise, laid flat as if it mattered that someone see it whole.

Okafor laughed — a short, nervous sound that died fast in the big dark. "Someone's shrine."

"Shrines get dusty," Teodora said. Her lamp was fixed on the shelf. "That shelf's been wiped."

She was right. There wasn't a grain of grit on it. Isandro photographed it twice, then a third time with a scale bar, and his hands were steady and his mind was not, because fieldwork discipline said *document, classify, move on*, and underneath the discipline something older and stupider was whispering: *someone comes here. Someone came recently. Someone will come again.*

The next offering was a warning.

It was cut into a slab of limestone that jutted into the passage at chest height, forcing them to pass close whether they wanted to or not. The characters were angular, dense, nothing like Latin script — an old dialect, maybe the same family as the fragments in the coastal parish records, the ones scholars called decorative because they couldn't read them. Isandro didn't read them either. But he knew carving when he saw it, and this carving was deep, re-cut, fresh-edged in places where the stone hadn't yet dulled.

"Somebody keeps the letters sharp," Teodora murmured. She'd come to stand at his shoulder. Her voice had dropped to almost nothing. "You don't maintain a warning unless you expect people to need it."

"Or unless you expect people to ignore it," Hale said behind them. His voice was pleasant. It always was, when he was deciding something. "Grell. Torch on that slab. I want it photographed before we move."

Grell's light swung over. In the glare, the carved lines threw hard little shadows, and Isandro looked at them and felt the awe in his chest curdle slowly into something colder. Warnings were a language. This one said: *the water keeps its own schedule.* He'd watched it enforce that schedule on Grell once already. He didn't need the translation.

They moved on. The channels multiplied, converging, until they were walking inside a lattice of them, and the air changed — warmer, wetter, carrying a smell that took Isandro a moment to name and then another moment to believe.

Smoke. Old smoke, banked smoke, the smell of a fire kept low and alive for a long time.

Nobody said anything. Nobody had to. The kid's breathing had gone quick and shallow, and even Hale had stopped issuing instructions, which frightened Isandro more than the smoke did.

* * *

The settlement announced itself in pieces.

A doorway first — not built but chosen, a gap in the flowstone curtained with woven rush that had been replaced often enough to leave ghost-lines of older weavings pressed into the mineral. Then a floor: swept bedrock, scattered with mats of dried grass gone grey with age, layered like sediment. Niches in the walls holding cups, bones, coils of cord, small stones worn glossy by handling. Everything arranged. Everything tended. A human place, grown into the cave the way lichen grows into rock — patiently, and over a long time.

"Christ," Okafor whispered. "People *live* here."

"Lived," Hale corrected. But his voice had lost its pleasantness, and Isandro marked that.

Teodora drifted toward the niches with her camera held loose at her side, not shooting, just looking. Isandro stayed near the entrance, doing the thing he was actually good at, the thing his mother had taught him without ever meaning to: reading ground. He read the traffic patterns in the dust. He read which mats were slept on and which were ceremonial. He read the fire pit — small, efficient, flued by a crack in the ceiling that carried the smoke up into some higher gallery — and he read the wood stacked beside it, split and seasoned and dry.

Dry wood. Underground. That meant trips to the surface. That meant a route they hadn't found. That meant—

He stopped thinking. He was looking at the floor near the fire pit, at a print pressed into a patch of clay-rich mud that never fully dried this close to the water.

One boot print. Small. Narrow heel, deep tread at the ball of the foot where a walker pushed off, worn smooth across the outer edge by years of the same gait on the same ground. Modern rubber sole. Not a keeper's bare foot, not a wrapped sandal, none of the ancient traces this place otherwise offered.

Small. Worn. Familiar.

Isandro knelt. His knees hit the stone and he didn't feel them. He put his hand beside the print, palm down, the way surveyors measure, and his hand covered most of it, and his hands were not large. He knew that sole. He had bought those boots — no. He had *seen* those boots, eleven years ago, drying on the porch rail of a rented house on the Rivergate coast, laces undone, the outer edges of both soles worn pale from a woman who walked cliffs the way other people walked hallways. He had been nineteen and furious with her and he had noticed anyway, because noticing was the inheritance she'd actually left him.

His heart was making a liar of his body. It hammered like it wanted out of his chest, like it knew something his discipline wouldn't let him say.

*Vera Almeida wore size six.*

"Almeida." Hale's voice, close. Too close. "You've found something."

Isandro looked up. Hale was standing over him, and Hale's eyes weren't on the print — they were on Isandro's face, reading it the way Isandro read ground, and Isandro understood with cold clarity that he had just handed this man information for free.

"Mud's disturbed," Isandro said. "Recent traffic. We're not alone down here."

"Recent." Hale crouched, unhurried, and studied the print. "Small boot. One set?" He glanced around the chamber, and Isandro saw him do the arithmetic — one keeper, one pair of hands maintaining all these channels, wiping all these shelves, re-cutting all these letters. One person keeping a dead place alive for years alone.

"One set I can see," Isandro said.

Hale smiled. It was the worst thing Isandro had seen in four days, because it was patient. "Then whoever it is knows we're coming. Has known since the tide turned us loose in their front hall." He straightened, and his voice lifted to fill the chamber, easy and carrying, a man addressing a room he believed was listening. "We're not here to take anything. We're looking for the source of the river. That's all. You have my word."

The word fell into the dark and lay there. Nothing answered. Nothing needed to. Isandro looked at the swept floor, the wiped shelf, the sharpened letters, and understood that silence *was* the answer, and that it was not a welcome.

"We should post a watch," Teodora said quietly. She'd come back from the niches. Her face in the lamplight was carefully blank, and her eyes weren't on Hale — they were on Isandro, and there was a question in them she was too disciplined to ask aloud. *What did you see? What did that print do to you?*

"Agreed," Hale said briskly. "Two-hour rotations. Nobody wanders. We rest, we eat, and when the tide turns we follow the water to its head." He clapped his hands once, softly, a man closing a meeting. "We're close. I can feel it."

The crew settled. Grell took first watch at the entrance, his silhouette hunched against the black of the passage. Okafor got a stove going, and the small flame made the chamber feel briefly, falsely domestic. The kid lay down and was asleep in minutes, the sleep of the young and the ignorant.

Isandro didn't eat. He sat apart with his journal open on his knee, pen uncapped, and wrote nothing for a long time. Fieldwork discipline said: *record the print, its dimensions, its probable age, its significance.* Hope said something else entirely, something with his mother's voice, something that would make him insane if he let it speak.

In the end he drew the boot print. Just the outline, just the tread pattern, rendered precisely, dated, located by channel and bearing. Evidence, not prayer. He closed the book on it before hope could read over his shoulder.

Then he sat watching the dark mouth of the passage beyond the firelight, where the channels ran on toward whatever source fed this impossible river, and he listened.

The cave breathed. Water moved somewhere below. And once — only once, so faintly he could have invented it, and part of him was already arguing that he had — a sound came down the passage from upstream. Soft. Rhythmic. Deliberate.

Footsteps. Small ones. Retreating.

Not fleeing. Withdrawing — unhurried, the way a person walks when they have checked on something and found it exactly where they expected it to be.

Isandro rose without a light and drifted to the edge of the camp, past Grell's dozing watch, to the lip of the passage. The dark ahead was total. Somewhere in it, a woman with his mother's hands was walking away from him at a pace that said she had all the time in the world.

"Wait," he whispered, and had no idea whether he was talking to her or to himself.

The footsteps faded. The river ran on, inland, toward its source, and the tide would turn in under two hours, and Isandro stood in the dark understanding that whatever waited upstream already knew his name — because someone had taught it to the water, eleven years ago, along with everything else she'd chosen to forget.
