## 6. The Price of the Passage

The river gave them one hour and forty minutes, and Grell spent the first twenty arguing about it.

"We're burning daylight we don't have," he said, standing at the edge of the flooded channel where the water ran black and glass-still, deep enough to swallow a man to the chest. "Hale wants the source by tomorrow's low. That means moving now."

"It means moving when the water says," Isandro said. He crouched at the channel's edge with his lamp angled low across the surface. The current had turned — he could see it in the way the silt drifted, a slow gray smoke pulling upstream instead of down, the river inhaling ahead of the tide's turn. Above them, cut into the limestone in that old dialect Teodora was still laboring over, the warning repeated for the third time since they'd entered the upper galleries: *the water keeps its own clock; the man who argues with it pays in breath.*

"Your mother wrote the same thing," Hale said behind him. Isandro didn't turn. He'd stopped turning when Hale said *your mother* three days ago; the man used it like a hook, testing where the flesh was softest.

"My mother dismissed half of what she wrote," Isandro said. "That doesn't make it wrong."

Grell snorted. He was a big man, salvage-crew muscle gone soft at the edges, and he wore his impatience like gear — always on, always rattling. "The water's knee-deep at the far bend. I've waded worse off a shrimp boat."

"You've waded worse under an open sky," Teodora said quietly. She was checking her dive rig for the third time, though she hadn't committed to wearing it. "This isn't open sky."

Isandro read the ground the way other men read faces. The channel floor sloped away past the bend into a low arch — a sump, the kind of drowned passage that divers called a restriction and everyone else called a coffin. The water there wasn't knee-deep. It was a lung held shut, waiting for the tide to exhale. In his mother's journals, in the margins of the pages that dealt with this coast, she'd drawn the same profile twice: a narrowing, a hatching of lines, and beside it, in her cramped hand, *do not enter against the fill.*

He stood. "We wait for the exhale. Forty minutes, maybe less. Then the sump drops and we walk through dry."

Grell looked at Hale. That was the thing Isandro had been watching for two days without letting himself name it: the crew looked at Hale before they decided anything, the way sailors look at weather. And Hale never said *go*. He just let his silence do it, a silence shaped exactly like permission.

"Grell," Hale said mildly, "your call. You're the strongest swimmer aboard."

It was beautifully done. Isandro would have admired it in another life, another story, one where he wasn't standing in it. Hale handed the man the decision the way you hand someone a lit fuse, and stepped back far enough that the burn wouldn't touch him.

Grell went in.

* * *

The first fifty meters were as Grell promised — thigh-deep, cold enough to make the breath come short, but walkable. Isandro followed because the alternative was letting the man go alone into a passage he didn't understand, and because some part of him that predated bitterness still couldn't watch a person walk toward a marked grave. Teodora came behind him with the spare line. Hale stayed at the channel mouth with the others, and Isandro noted that too, filed it in the ledger he was keeping against the man: *sends, does not go.*

At the bend the walls closed in. The arch came down to meet the water, black stone sweating in the lamplight, and the sound changed — the river's breathing gone from a murmur to something with pressure behind it, like a held note. Isandro put his palm flat on the surface and felt it: not flowing anymore. Filling. The tide had turned early, or the river had simply decided it was done being generous.

"Grell. Stop."

Grell was already at the arch, lamp beam swallowed by the low ceiling of water ahead. "It opens up past here. I can see air on the other side." His voice had the bright false cheer of a man talking himself into something. "Thirty seconds' swim. Maybe less."

"The sump's filling. The airspace on the far side is closing with it."

"You said forty minutes."

"I said *maybe* forty minutes. The river doesn't file reports." Isandro grabbed for the line. "Back out. Now."

And here was the moment Isandro would carry afterward, the one that would wake him on the estuary mud days later with his heart going like a pump: Grell laughed. Not cruelly. Laughed the way men laugh at funerals when they can't think what else to do, and ducked under the arch, and the water took him the way water takes anything — completely, without malice, without ceremony.

Teodora screamed his name. Isandro dropped to his chest in the flood, arm through the arch up to the shoulder, fingers raking black water that pushed back with the patient strength of something much larger than itself. The line went taut once — once — and then the current peeled it from his grip and hauled it away downstream like the river reclaiming a debt.

For eleven seconds — he counted them, he would always count them — nothing surfaced. Then Grell's lamp bobbed up against the underside of the arch, guttering, and went out, and the dark closed over the place where he'd been as neatly as a lid.

* * *

They got the body out an hour later, after the exhale came and the sump drained to a trickle, as if the river were returning change. Grell lay on the wet stone with his face slack and ordinary, looking smaller than a man who'd filled a doorway that morning. Teodora knelt over him and did the things you do, though there was nothing left to do, her hands steady and her jaw working like she was chewing through wire.

Hale arrived with the others and stood at a respectful distance, and Isandro watched him, and watched him, and made himself see it.

There was no surprise in the man's face.

Not grief — grief has a stagger to it, a hitch before it finds its footing. Hale's face did something else entirely. It settled. Like a ledger balancing. Like a cost arriving that had been entered weeks ago, in an office, in advance.

"You knew," Isandro said.

Hale didn't flinch. That was the worst part. A lesser liar would have flinched. "I knew the river was dangerous. I said so. Everyone heard me say so."

"You knew the window was narrow. You knew Grell couldn't read water. You handed him the choice anyway, because a dead man makes better arguments than a live one — his body tells the rest of the crew exactly how serious you are." Isandro heard his own voice come out flat and cold and recognized, with a lurch, that it wasn't entirely his. It was the voice of every report he'd ever resented, every official document that had weighed his mother's life and found it closed. "You spent him."

Hale looked at Grell's body for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was quiet, almost gentle, which was somehow more obscene than shouting. "Eleven years ago I coordinated a rescue on this coast. Four days, thirty-one volunteers, every boat in Rivergate harbor. We found a boot. One boot." He crouched, straightened Grell's arm along his side with two fingers, a small decency performed with terrible ease. "Do you know what I learned? That the coast doesn't negotiate. It prices things. And the people who get through it are the ones willing to pay." He rose and met Isandro's eyes. "I'm sorry about Grell. I'll write to his sister myself. But we are four hours from the source chamber, and I will not bury this expedition with him."

*The ones willing to pay.* Isandro turned the phrase over and felt its edges, and understood that Hale had been saying it to himself for years, polishing it, until it no longer cut his hands.

What frightened him — what he stood very still in the dripping dark and admitted, because the cave allowed no other honesty — was that some part of him had known too. Had seen Grell look to Hale for permission and had seen Hale's silence shape itself around the man like a noose, and had said nothing sharp enough, loud enough, final enough to stop it. He'd told himself the river would decide. He'd told himself Grell was a grown man who'd made his own choice.

He'd told himself he was in it for the money, back at the harbor, and he'd been lying then too. He was in it for the marks in the dark, for the handwriting dated after a death, for the small worn boot print in a place no living stranger should stand. And he had let a man drown on the way to it, or nearly let him, or failed to save him — the distinctions mattered less than he wanted them to.

Survival had already taught him its arithmetic. He just hadn't known he'd enrolled.

* * *

They buried Grell above the flood line, under a shelf of stone where the keepers' old offerings sat in niches — dried reeds, river-smoothed stones, cups of bronze gone green. Teodora laid the man's lamp beside him, unlit. No one spoke the dialect words carved overhead, but Isandro caught the shape of them now without translation: *what the water takes, the water keeps.*

Hale posted Voss and the kid, Marchetti, at the channel mouth with orders to hold camp, and the order had a new weight to it, the weight of a man who has demonstrated a lesson and expects it remembered. The remaining party — Hale, Isandro, Teodora, and two others whose names Isandro realized with a small shame he still hadn't learned — moved upstream as the exhale began, the river draining out of the high galleries like the cave emptying its lungs.

Teodora fell in beside him at the first bend. She didn't look at him. Her voice came low, pitched under the water-sound.

"He's done this before," she said. "Buried failures. Two expeditions before this one — I told you that. What I didn't tell you is that both of them lost people. And both times, the loss happened right before a breakthrough. Right when the crew started asking whether it was worth going on." She finally turned, and her eyes in the lamplight were hard and wet at once. "Grell's death buys obedience. That's what it's for. So whatever you're carrying around in your chest right now — grief, guilt, all of it — set it down somewhere you can find it later. Because we're close. I can feel it in the way the carvings are changing. They've stopped warning us."

She nodded upstream, toward a gallery where the lamplight caught fresh-cut lines in the stone — recent, clean-edged, unmistakable.

"They've started counting us."

Beyond the bend, the river narrowed to a single voice, and somewhere far ahead, faint as a pulse through stone, Isandro could hear water falling — not draining, not filling, but falling, endlessly, from somewhere the charts had never mapped.
