## 7. A Woman Called Nothing

The flood pulse came without warning, the way the river's moods always did.

One moment Isandro was wading chest-deep through the third gallery, headlamp beam skittering across water the color of old pewter, Teodora's rope taut in his fist. The next, the river behind them exhaled — a low, subterranean groan that he felt in his molars before he heard it with his ears — and the water rose like something standing up from a long sleep.

"Go back!" he shouted, but the sound was swallowed. The gallery had narrowed to a throat of wet limestone, and the current shoved him forward instead of letting him retreat, a hand at his spine, insistent as a customs officer. His boots lost the bottom. He went under, and the world became pressure and cold and the green-black blur of his own light spinning away from him.

He did not drown because the river did not want him drowned. That was the only explanation he could construct afterward, lying on smooth stone with water lapping at his ribs and no memory of how he'd come to be there. The flood pulse had carried him the way a strong man carries a drunk — not gently, but without malice — and set him down in a chamber where the air tasted of moss and cold iron and something faintly sweet, like honey left too long in a cellar.

His headlamp was gone. His pack was gone. The rope was gone.

He lay still and listened. Water moved somewhere ahead of him — not the violent rush of the galleries behind, but a slower, more deliberate sound, the hush of water being *tended*. That was the word his mind offered and he hated it. Rivers weren't tended. Rivers were endured.

Then light came toward him. Not electric. Not fire, exactly. A soft amber glow that arrived around a bend in the rock, borne by figures who walked without splashing, which should have been impossible on wet stone.

They were small — smaller than they'd seemed in the glimpses the crew had caught over the past two days, the shapes at the edge of the headlamp beams that Hale insisted were shadows. Three of them, wrapped in oiled wool the color of river clay, their faces seamed and calm. The eldest of the three, a man whose beard had gone the white of chalk deposits, looked down at Isandro without surprise.

"You were given," the man said, in accented English worn smooth as river stone. "The water does not give often."

"I wasn't given," Isandro rasped. "I was taken."

The man considered this. "Yes," he said finally. "That also happens." And he extended a hand.

Isandro took it, because the alternative was staying on the stone until the crew found him or the river changed its mind, and he had already learned what the river did when it changed its mind. Grell's body was still somewhere downstream, wedged in the sump where the tide had turned early. They hadn't been able to recover it. Hale hadn't tried very hard.

*You knew the risks,* Hale had said, and Isandro had said nothing, and the nothing had felt like a stone he'd swallowed that would sit in him for the rest of his life.

He let the keepers lead him.

* * *

The passage they took him through was one his own survey instincts screamed about — it shouldn't exist, the gradients were wrong, the air flowed backward against every law of cave ventilation he'd ever trusted — and after eleven minutes of walking he stopped trying to reconcile it and simply followed. There was a freedom in that surrender that frightened him more than the flood pulse had.

The chamber opened like a held breath released.

It was vast, and it was lit — softly, from sources he couldn't immediately parse: lamps of some kind of phosphorescent paste smeared on flat stones, arranged in patterns that repeated, deliberate, almost textual. Water ran through the center of the chamber in channels cut into the living rock, shallow and precise, branching and rejoining, and the channels were *clean*. No silt. No debris. Someone maintained them the way a gardener maintains beds.

And there were people. A dozen, perhaps more, moving among the channels with the unhurried economy of long practice. Some knelt to clear gravel from the cuts. Some poured offerings — grain, oil, flowers gone brown at the edges — into small basins at the channel junctions. None of them looked up when Isandro entered, and that absence of curiosity was somehow more unnerving than alarm would have been.

"The source is beyond," the white-bearded keeper said, gesturing deeper. "You will want to see it. Everyone wants to see it. That wanting is why we are here."

"Where is here?" Isandro asked. "What is this place?"

The man smiled, and it was a kind smile, and it gave nothing. "A gate," he said. "For a road that most people have forgotten exists."

Then a woman came around the far bend of the channels, carrying two stone basins, one in each hand, and Isandro forgot how to breathe.

She was older than his mother should have been — her hair gone fully gray, cropped short and practical, her frame thinned to wire and sinew. She wore the same oiled wool as the others. Her face was weathered past easy reading, lined in ways that time alone doesn't draw; these were lines of squinting into spray, of sleeping rough, of years lived where the air itself is damp salt.

But her hands.

He knew those hands. He had watched them for the first eighteen years of his life. Broad-palmed, with short, strong fingers and a scar bisecting the left thumb where a trowel had slipped in a trench outside Lisbon when he was nine and she'd laughed it off and kept working. The scar was there. The particular way she gripped — thumbs braced against the basin rims, weight balanced through the forearms, a posture she'd drilled into him at dig sites: *lift with your whole arm, Isandro, your hands are for feeling, not carrying.*

She set the basins down at a junction of channels, straightened, pressed her lower back with both palms — the same gesture, the exact same gesture, she'd made at the end of every long field day — and turned toward him.

Her eyes found his face and held it, and there was nothing in them. Not cruelty, not coldness. Nothing. A polite, patient blankness, the look you give a stranger at a bus stop.

"Do you need tending?" she asked. Her voice was lower than he remembered, roughened by disuse or smoke or years of speaking a language he didn't know. "You are wet. You will go cold."

"No," Isandro managed. "I'm— I'm fine."

She nodded, satisfied, and turned back to her basins.

The world tilted. He put a hand against the channel wall to steady himself and the keeper — the white-bearded one — touched his elbow, light as a bird landing.

"You know her," the man said. It wasn't a question.

"No," Isandro said.

"Yes," the man said again, with the same terrible mildness. "Your face is doing what faces do. We see it sometimes, in those who come seeking the source. Recognition. Grief. You are not the first to find someone here who was lost to you elsewhere." He paused. "But you may be the first she was lost *from*."

"What happened to her?" The question tore out of him raw. "Eleven years ago. There was a storm. A search. They declared her dead. I signed—" His voice cracked. "There was a report. A man named Corvin Hale wrote a report saying she was dead, and everyone believed it, and I believed it, and she was *here*?"

The keeper was quiet for a long moment. Around them, the work of the channels continued, unhurried, the water making its small committed sounds.

"She came up the river," he said at last, "as you did. With others. The others wanted to take the source. She wanted only to understand it. But understanding is its own taking, in this place. The river does not distinguish between the thief and the scholar. Both carry knowledge out, and knowledge is the only currency the source accepts." He gestured at the woman, now kneeling at another junction, her hands moving in the water with unconscious grace. "To leave with what she learned, she paid. What she paid was herself. Her name. Her history. Every year of the life that led her to our door. The river keeps it. She stays, and tends, and does not know why the work feels true to her. Only that it does."

"Can it be undone?" Isandro whispered. "Can she get it back?"

"Nothing the river takes is returned," the keeper said. "It is only sometimes... redirected." His eyes were old and sorry. "Ask yourself, son of hers, whether you would want it returned. What she was, the world consumed once already. It sent her out with her brilliance and her hunger, and it used her up, and when she was spent it declared her dead and moved on. Here, she is empty, yes. But she is also — " he searched for the word — "*unclaimed.*"

* * *

Isandro didn't approach her right away. He made himself useful instead, because usefulness was the only language he trusted in the field, and because if he stood still too long he would shake apart.

He cleared gravel from a channel. He carried basins. He watched her work from ten feet away, then five, mapping her the way he'd map any terrain — cataloguing the details that proved his hypothesis, hating that he needed proof at all. The way she tested stone with her thumbnail before trusting it. The way she hummed, tuneless, when concentrating. The way she tucked hair behind her ear with her pinky extended because her fingers were wet.

At last she noticed him watching and smiled — a stranger's courteous smile — and said, "You carry well. For an outsider."

"You taught me," Isandro said.

Her smile faltered, just slightly, a crack in ice. "Did I?" She looked at her own hands as though seeing them newly. "I don't remember teaching anyone anything. I don't remember much of anything." She said it without self-pity, the way you'd report the weather. "The others say I came up the river a long time ago. They gave me work, and the work fits me, and that is enough. A woman called Nothing needs very little."

"A woman called Nothing," Isandro repeated.

"It is what they call me. Because I could not tell them my name." She tilted her head at him, curious now, the blankness in her eyes stirring the way silt stirs when you disturb the bed. "Do you have a name for me? You look at me like a man carrying a name in his pocket."

Every cell in his body screamed *yes*. Say it. Say *Vera*. Say *Mãe*. Drag her out of this hollowed half-life by the collar, haul her into daylight, put her name back on her like a recovered artifact with its provenance restored—

And then he saw it, clearly, the way he read ground: the fault line running under everything. If he spoke her name, he didn't get his mother back. He got a woman with a label she couldn't open — grief without content, a wound handed to someone with no skin. And he got the world's attention. Hale was upstream with four men and a rifle and a dead crew member he'd already written off. Hale had spent eleven years looking for this coast, and the thing he was looking for was fifty meters behind this woman, running clean and bright through its carved channels. If Isandro confirmed who she was, he handed Hale the one hostage the keepers couldn't afford to lose.

*She paid so you would never be pulled into this,* he thought. *You were pulled in anyway.*

"Not yet," he said aloud. "I might have a name for you. But names are heavy here. Let me make sure it's yours before I give it."

She studied him for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes — not memory, nothing so kind, but maybe the shape memory leaves behind, the dent in the cushion.

"All right," she said quietly. "I trust you. I don't know why. Isn't that strange? I trust you the way I trust the water."

From upstream, deep in the rock, came a sound that froze every keeper in the chamber mid-motion: voices. Boots on stone. The flat, echoing bark of a man giving orders in a voice Isandro knew better than his mother's.

Corvin Hale had found the source chamber.

And the woman called Nothing turned toward the noise, and her hands — his mother's hands — closed slowly into fists, as if her body remembered something her mind had sold.
