## 8. What Vera Chose

The keepers did not speak so much as they let the cave speak through them.

Isandro sat in a chamber above the source pool, lamplight moving on wet stone like something breathing, and listened to an old man named Sef translate for the others — or perhaps the others translated for him; their dialect slid between tongues the way the river slid between salt and fresh. Around him, the keepers moved with unhurried purpose: tending wicks, pouring grain into carved channels, touching the walls in places that looked like any other place until you noticed the stone there was polished smooth by centuries of hands.

And at the center of it all, on her knees beside a basin of spring water, scrubbing silt from a channel with a broken-handled brush, was his mother.

She worked the way Vera Almeida had always worked — methodical, absorbed, her left hand braced against stone as if the ground might tell her something if she pressed hard enough. But when Isandro had said her name that morning, she had only looked up with mild, polite confusion, the look of a woman interrupted at a task.

"You have me mistaken," she'd said. "I'm called Nothing here. It isn't unkind. It's just what's left."

Now he watched her from across the chamber and understood that he had spent eleven years grieving a woman who had never died. She had simply been spent.

Sef lowered himself onto a stone bench beside Isandro, joints cracking like river ice. He was older than the cave seemed, or maybe the cave made everyone seem young. In his hands he held a bundle wrapped in oilcloth: field notebooks, water-stained, their covers gone soft as cloth.

"She brought these to us herself," Sef said. "Eleven years ago. Before the forgetting. She said —" he paused, choosing among languages — "she said someone should know what was paid, even if she couldn't."

The first notebook opened to handwriting Isandro knew better than his own. His mother's letters, sharp-angled, leaning forward like a runner. *Day 1. The tide chart is wrong. The river runs inland against the ebb — impossible, but I've measured it twice.* The entries marched forward through days of survey work, of wonder, of the mounting certainty that she had found something the maps refused to hold. Then the handwriting changed. Slower. Careful, the way writing becomes careful when the writer knows these pages may be read by strangers.

*They call themselves keepers. They were here before Rivergate, before the charts, before whatever empire drew its lines across this coast. They don't guard the source. They serve it. There's a difference, and I understand it now, and understanding it has frightened me more than anything in thirty years of holes in the ground.*

*The river gives. That's what they say. It gave this coast its fish, its fresh water, its sheltered passage. But it keeps accounts. Everything taken from the source must be paid back, and the coin it accepts is memory. Not metaphor. Memory. The old ones who dive the deep channels come up unable to name their children.*

Isandro turned the page. His hands were not steady.

*I asked them: why does it want memory? Sef's grandmother answered — she's older than the oldest chart, and she laughed at me, kindly. She said: because the river forgets nothing else. Because everything it carries, it carries forever, and it needs somewhere to put what it takes from us. We are how the river lets go.*

*So here is my choice, written down while I still own it. I can leave. They will show me the flood channels; I'll be home inside a week. And the knowledge leaves with me — the source, the passages, the tide-law. Hale will find it eventually, or someone like him. Men like Corvin Hale do not stop looking; they only change sponsors.*

*Or I stay. And the price of staying — of being allowed to live beside the thing I found — is that I give the river what I carried in. All of it. My notes will remain, but the knowing won't. Not the source. Not the way in. Not even, they warn me, myself. The river takes the whole account or none of it.*

He had to set the notebook down. The chamber tilted gently, the way a boat tilts, and righted itself.

There was one entry left, dated the day before her disappearance — the day the world had decided she drowned.

*Isandro. If you are reading this, then either the keepers broke faith — and they will not — or you followed me here, which means you have my gift for ground and my talent for trouble, and I am sorry for both. Listen to me. I am not abandoning you. I am making sure the river never learns your name. That is the only inheritance worth leaving you: a blank page where this should be. Live small, my love. Survey bridges. Marry badly once. Stay out of caves.*

*But if you're reading this anyway — then you already paid something to get here. So hear the last thing I know: the river doesn't take what we love. It takes what we refuse to release. Let go of me. That's the toll. That's the whole toll.*

*— V.A.*

Above the pool, water dripped in slow metronome time. Across the chamber, the woman who had been Vera Almeida wrung out her rag and began on the next stretch of channel, humming something tuneless and content.

Isandro closed the notebook. He did not cry. He had used up his crying years ago, at fourteen, at a memorial service full of people who spoke about his mother in the past tense while he sat there knowing — knowing the way he knew ground — that the official story fit wrong. Everyone had told him grief made people irrational. Grief had been the only rational thing in the room.

She hadn't abandoned him. She had built a wall of forgetting between him and this place, brick by brick of her own self, and he had walked around it because the storm tide knocked a hole in the coast and Hale came with money and threats.

He was pulled in anyway. She had paid everything, and it hadn't been enough, because the world did not honor bargains struck underground.

"The river took her name," Sef said quietly. "But watch her hands. The hands remember. She tends the channels better than any keeper born here. Whatever the river kept, it kept the part of her that serves." He tapped the oilcloth bundle. "It could not take what she wrote. Paper is not memory. That is her loophole, and ours."

"And Hale?" Isandro asked. "What does he know?"

Sef's face went old and grim. "The man who signed the report of your mother's death has been paying fishermen to watch this coastline for nine years. He does not want the source for science. He wants it the way a man wants a thing that humiliated him. Your mother escaped his report. He means to correct the record."

* * *

They came at dusk, when the tide stood slack and the river ran quiet, and they came armed.

Isandro heard them before he saw them — boots on wet stone, the wrong rhythm, men walking fast in a place that punished haste. He was on the ledge above the approach channel with Teodora, and below them Grell's body had already been given to the water that took him, and now four figures rounded the last bend with headlamps cutting white cones through the dark.

Hale led them. Of course he did. Behind him, Voss and the two remaining hired divers, carbide lamps slung low, and — Isandro's stomach dropped — rifles. Not shotguns for flare lines. Rifles.

"Hale," Isandro called down, keeping his voice level. "You're standing in a church. Put them away."

Hale's lamp swung up and pinned him. Eleven years had silvered the man, but the face under the gray was the same one from the inquiry room, patient and reasonable, a man who had never once doubted his own paperwork. "Almeida. Good. You saved me a search." He stepped forward, boots splashing the sacred shallows without noticing. "Where is she?"

"You signed a report saying she was dead."

"I signed a report saying the evidence supported a finding of death." Hale smiled, thin as a knife edge. "I've had eleven years to reconsider the evidence. A body was never recovered. A journal was never recovered. And now a cave appears that matches a legend she wrote to me about, once, over dinner, the year before she vanished." His voice dropped, intimate, terrible. "She told me about the reversing river, Isandro. Over wine. She thought I was a friend. I have spent eleven years and two expeditions and a great deal of other people's money proving her right. Where. Is. She."

Teodora shifted beside Isandro, and he felt her weight change — felt her deciding. He put a hand on her arm. Not yet.

"She's here," Isandro said. "And she wouldn't know you. The river took it. Took all of it. You can't take what's already paid."

Something moved behind Hale's eyes — not doubt. Hunger meeting a locked door. "Then I'll take the source instead. The water, the passage, the location. Knowledge doesn't need her consent." He gestured, and Voss started forward along the channel toward the source pool. "You can stand there and philosophize, boy, or you can remember that I know about your licenses. Both of them. Falsified. You'll guide us or you'll guide us from a cell."

Isandro looked past him, down the channel, to where the woman called Nothing knelt by her basin, brush in hand, watching the armed men with the calm of someone observing weather.

His mother had given everything so the river would never learn his name. And here he stood, having led it straight to her door.

The rage that rose in him was not hot. It was cold and clear as the source pool, and it settled his hands.

"Sef," he said quietly, in English, knowing the old man would understand anyway. "Get her out. Flood channels. Now."

Sef was already moving.

Hale heard the motion, turned, and raised his lamp toward the far chamber — and in the sudden sweep of light, Vera Almeida rose to her feet, water dripping from her hands, and looked at him with the mild, empty courtesy she offered every stranger.

"Gentlemen," she said pleasantly. "You're trespassing on the river's patience."

And beneath their feet, faint but unmistakable, the water changed its mind.
