## 1. The Debt on the Dock

The morning the clerk came to Rivergate Landing, Petra Brightwater was sitting on the roof of his own boat, counting shingles he couldn't afford to replace and pretending it was a leisure activity.

"You've got a leak," said Marisol from below, her voice carrying up through the deck planks with the practiced flatness of a woman who had delivered bad news over breakfast for eleven years. "Over the galley. It dripped into my flour."

"That's not a leak," Petra called back. "That's character."

"It's mold, is what it is. Character doesn't grow in a canister."

He let that lie where it fell and went back to his arithmetic, which was the kind of arithmetic that made a man want to take up drinking as a profession rather than a hobby. The Wreck's Fortune owed the Terrapin Landing bank four hundred and twelve dollars by the first of the month. He had sixty-one dollars in the strongbox, a cargo hold full of nothing but river smell, and an engine that Old Sable swore would run forever if you loved it enough — which was easy for Sable to say, since Sable loved the engine more than he'd ever loved anything with a pulse.

Four hundred and twelve dollars. The number sat in Petra's chest like a swallowed stone. Eleven years he'd kept the Fortune afloat — through the low-water years, through the year the fever came upriver, through the winter he'd burned half his own cabin furniture to keep the boiler warm and told the crew they were "testing the wood." And now one payment stood between him and the auction block, where some fat-bellied freight man would buy his boat at a discount and rename her something sensible like *Commerce* or *Profit*, and Petra Brightwater would stand on the dock watching another man's smoke come out of his stacks.

"Captain!" Pip's voice, high and urgent, from the bow. "Captain, there's a fella coming down the dock like he's being chased by bees!"

Petra swung down off the roof, landing on the main deck with a creak of boards that sounded, to his ear, like the boat herself complaining about her diet. He looked up the dock.

The man coming toward them was dressed in government gray, which on the Rivergate meant either a tax collector or trouble, and the two were frequently the same animal in different coats. He was young — twenty-five, maybe thirty — with the soft hands of a man who'd never hauled a line, and he walked the way men walk when they're trying very hard to look like they aren't running. His eyes kept flicking behind him. His briefcase was gripped so tight his knuckles had gone the color of the fog.

"Morning," Petra said, leaning on the rail. "You lost?"

"No." The man stopped at the gangplank and looked at it as though it might be a trap. "No, I'm — are you Captain Brightwater? Of the *Wreck's Fortune*?"

"I'm captain of something," Petra allowed. "Depends who's asking, and what they're asking for."

The man fumbled in his coat, produced a card, and held it out with a hand that trembled just slightly — the tremor of a man who hadn't slept, not the tremor of drink. Petra had seen both kinds often enough to tell them apart at forty yards.

*Aldous Renwick, Clerk, Third Class. Department of Surveys and Interior Waters.*

"Department of Surveys," Petra read aloud. "Funny. I heard your department lost something lately."

"We have misplaced—" Renwick stopped, corrected himself with visible effort. "We have temporarily lost contact with a survey expedition. Upstream. Above Miller's Bend."

"Above Miller's Bend." Petra whistled low. "That's past the last charted water. That's where maps go to become rumors."

"The expedition was led by Surveyor Edmund Vane. Seven men. They departed six weeks ago with instructions to chart the upper gorge and report back by packet boat every ten days." Renwick's jaw worked. "There has been no report. Not one."

"And the Department wants her found."

"The Department wants *answers*. I want answers." Something moved behind the clerk's eyes then — something that didn't match the gray suit at all. "I'm authorized to offer three hundred dollars for the recovery of the survey party or evidence of their fate. Plus expenses. Plus two hundred more upon delivery of their records and instruments."

Petra did the sum without meaning to. Five hundred dollars, plus expenses. Enough to pay the bank twice over. Enough to re-shingle the roof, buy Marisol real flour, put coal in the bunkers until the hold smelled of money instead of mildew.

It also smelled wrong, and Petra had spent fifty years learning to trust his nose. A government that paid five hundred dollars to find seven men it had sent upriver itself was a government that wanted those men found by somebody — or wanted something else found along with them, or wanted the whole business handled quiet. Clerks didn't come down docks alone. Clerks came with escorts, with paperwork in triplicate, with other clerks. This one came sweating, alone, looking over his shoulder like the river itself might be taking notes.

"Mr. Renwick," Petra said slowly, "I'm going to ask you a question, and I'd take it kindly if you'd answer it honest, because I'll know if you don't. Why does a third-class clerk come all the way down here himself, without soldiers, without lawyers, to hire a broke sternwheeler captain whose boat is famous mostly for almost sinking?"

Renwick's face did several things at once, none of them composed. He glanced up the dock again. Then he leaned in close, close enough that Petra could smell ink and fear on him in equal measure.

"Because the official search was called off after nine days," he whispered. "Called off from above my head, with no reason given. Because the Vane expedition's supply requisitions were flagged and frozen before they even left. Because I signed the order releasing their funds and now nobody in that building will say the word 'Vane' out loud, and I am a small man, Captain, a very small man, but I am not yet the kind of small that lets seven men drown in silence." He straightened, smoothed his coat, and became briefly, desperately official. "The contract is standard. Do you accept?"

Petra looked past him, down the length of his boat. Old Sable was already in the engine room — Petra could hear him talking to the machinery, low and coaxing, the way other men talked to horses. Pip and Fen were coiling rope on the foredeck with the lazy synchronization of twins who'd been doing it since they could walk. Marisol stood in the galley doorway with her arms crossed, watching the clerk the way she watched weather coming off the bluffs.

One payment from losing everything. And here was a stranger offering more than everything, wrapped in paper that stank of secrets.

"Marisol," Petra called. "How's our stores?"

"Thin," she said. "Real thin. We could make Miller's Bend and back if we ate the twins' cooking instead of mine."

"Hey," said Pip.

"She means we're broke," Fen translated.

Petta turned back to Renwick. "I'll take you upriver, Mr. Renwick. As far as this boat can go and maybe a little farther than she should. But I'm not a rescue service and I'm not a gravedigger. I find your surveyors, I bring back what there is to bring, and I bring back my crew breathing. That's the whole of my terms."

"Agreed." Renwick set the contract on the rail and signed it with a hand that shook worse than before. "There's one more thing." He hesitated, and for a moment looked younger and more frightened than ever. "If you find them — if you find any of them — send word to me. Only to me. Not to the office in Terrapin Landing. To me. Aldous Renwick, personally. Can you remember that?"

"I can remember it," Petra said. "Though I'll admit it makes me curious."

"Don't be curious, Captain." Renwick picked up his briefcase, and his knuckles went white around the handle. "Curiosity is how the last seven men got lost."

He paid the advance in crisp new bills — too new, Petra thought, too crisp, like they'd come straight from a press that didn't usually feed this particular office — and hurried back up the dock without once looking at the river.

Petra stood at the rail a long moment, turning a bill over in his fingers. The paper felt heavy. Money always did, when it came with strings attached; you just had to guess where the strings were tied.

"Well?" Marisol appeared at his elbow. "Are we rich, or are we dead?"

"Ask me in three weeks."

"That's what you said the time we took the gunpowder run."

"And we lived."

"Barely. And Sable's eyebrows never grew back." She squinted upriver, where the water ran brown and fast between the bluffs, already swollen with spring. "Something's wrong up there, Petra. Even the gulls won't go past Miller's Bend this year."

Petra looked upriver too. The Rivergate lay stretched out ahead of them, wide and slow and patient as it always was, hiding whatever it hid. He'd been born on that water. It had raised him, fed him, nearly drowned him twice, and never once lied to him — unlike governments, banks, and clerks with trembling hands.

"Sable!" he shouted down the hatch. "How's my girl feel about a trip above Miller's Bend?"

From the depths came a pause, then a clank, then Sable's gravel voice: "She says the river's higher than it ought to be for April. She says somebody upstream is holding water back and letting it go mean."

Petra felt the stone in his chest shift, just slightly, toward something that might have been dread — or might, against all his better judgment, have been hope.

"Cast off in an hour," he called to his crew. "We're going to find some ghosts."

* * *

They cast off as the afternoon light went gold on the water, the *Wreck's Fortune* groaning awake beneath them, paddle wheel slapping the current like an old dog shaking off sleep. Pip took the bow watch, Fen the lines, Marisol the galley, Sable the engine room and its eternal conversation.

Petra stood at the wheel and steered them upriver, into the widening green dark of the gorge country, with five hundred dollars in his strongbox and a question riding on his shoulder heavier than any cargo he'd ever carried.

Seven men had gone up this river and vanished clean.

And somewhere ahead, in the bluffs above Miller's Bend, the river was keeping whatever it knew — waiting, patient as always, for someone fool enough to come asking.
