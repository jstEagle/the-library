## 2. Gear With No Ghosts

The Rivergate had a way of telling you things, if you knew how to listen. Petra Brightwater had spent forty years learning her grammar — the mutter of gravel bars shifting in the night, the particular hush a stretch of water took on when it was lying to you about its depth. He could read a snag from a quarter mile off. He could smell rain coming down the gorge two hours before the first drop fell.

None of that helped him now. Because what the river was telling him at Miller's Bend, in the flat gold light of late afternoon, was that six grown men had walked into these woods and simply stopped existing.

"Cap'n?" called Old Sable from the deck, her voice carrying the particular skepticism she saved for things she thought were a waste of good coal. "You seeing what I'm seeing?"

"I'm seeing it," Petra called back. He didn't say *and I don't like it*, because a captain who said that out loud too often ended up with a crew that said it first.

He brought the *Wreck's Fortune* alongside the old survey landing with more care than the water strictly demanded. The sternwheeler groaned her way in — she always groaned; Petra had long ago stopped apologizing for her — and the paddle wheel churned to a stop with a sound like a tired animal settling down. Pip and Fen were over the rail before the lines were fast, the twins moving in that unnerving synchrony of theirs, one to port, one to starboard, like a pair of bookends that had decided to see the world.

"Nobody touches anything," Petra said.

"Define anything," said Fen.

"Everything."

"That's going to be hard," said Pip, "because everything is *right there*."

He had a point. The survey camp stood on the low bench above the landing, and it was not a ruin. It was not a wreck. It was not even, properly speaking, abandoned. It was *waiting*. Three tents stood pitched and taut, guy-lines humming faintly in the breeze, as though the men who'd raised them had stepped away to stretch their legs and would be back before the coffee went cold. A tarp was strung between two trees over a work table, still shedding the last of the morning's dew. A stack of split firewood sat by the fire ring, neat as a church offering, sized for a stove nobody had lit in — Petra crouched and touched the ashes — a long, long time.

"Marisol," he said over his shoulder. His cook had come down the gangway with her arms folded and her mouth already set in the shape of an opinion.

"I see it," she said. "I don't like it."

"You don't like anything before supper."

"I like supper. This is not supper. This is a graveyard wearing a camp's clothes, and I will not be cooking in it." A pause. "I will be cooking *near* it, obviously. People don't stop being hungry because a place is wrong."

That, Petra thought, was the truest theology on the Rivergate.

* * *

He walked the camp the way his father had taught him to read a wreck site: slow, from the outside in, letting the place speak before he interrupted it.

The tents were the first wrong thing. Six men did not pitch tents this well. Survey crews were notorious for shelters that looked like arguments between canvas and rope, and Petra had slept in enough of them to know. But these were soldier-neat, corners square, flaps buttoned down against weather that hadn't come. Inside the nearest, bedrolls lay unrolled, blankets folded back at the corner — the universal gesture of a man who meant to come back to his bunk. A tin cup sat on a crate. A book lay face-down on the bedroll, spine cracked, a pencil marking the page.

Nobody left a book like that. Nobody left a book like that and then vanished off the face of the earth along with five colleagues, their instruments, and every trace of what had happened to them.

The kettles were the second wrong thing. Two of them sat on the fire ring, and when Petra lifted the lid of the nearer one he found the water inside still clear, still sweet, with nothing growing in it. Not green. Not sour. The river damp got into everything up here — he'd seen kettles furred with moss in a week — but these were clean as the day they'd been bought. Which meant, his mind reported reluctantly, that someone had tended this camp after the surveyors left it. Or that the surveyors had left it *very* recently, in a way that left no footprints in the soft black loam, no broken brush, no sign of six men carrying sixty pounds of gear apiece toward anywhere at all.

The third wrong thing was the quiet. Not the good quiet of the woods — the woods were chattering away, wrens and squirrels going about their business. It was the quiet *inside* the camp. A camp lived by its clutter: the dropped mitten, the whittled stick, the boot prints, the place where somebody had spit tobacco juice. This camp had none of it. It was a stage set. It was a photograph of a camp, printed in three dimensions, and Petra kept waiting for the paper to rustle.

"Cap'n." Fen's voice from over by the supply tent, pitched carefully flat. "You're going to want to see the crates."

* * *

The supply tent held the survey stores: crates of hardtack, tins of lamp oil, a case of surveyors' pencils sharpened down to nubs by honest work. And against the back wall, the instrument crates — six of them, stenciled with the crown-and-river mark of the Territorial Survey Office — all of them empty. Not broken open. Not pried. The lids were up, the packing straw pulled out and folded, the crates left *tidy*. Somebody had removed every instrument with the patience of a woman packing a trunk for a long journey.

"Robbery doesn't fold the straw," Old Sable said. She'd come up from the boat without being asked, which meant she'd been worried, which meant Petra should be worried too. Sable worried about boilers and nothing else on this earth.

"No," Petra agreed. "Robbery doesn't."

He was turning to go when he saw it. The last crate in the row — a plain pine box, unstenciled, the kind that held nothing worth stealing — had writing on it. Not writing. Scratching. Someone had taken a knife to the soft pine and cut letters into the lid, quick and deep, the strokes of a man in a hurry but not a panic.

Petra knelt. The light through the canvas was dim, so he tilted the crate toward it, and the letters came up out of the shadow one by one:

**N 44° 12′ — FOLLOW THE BLUFFS — V**

"V," said Sable behind him.

"Vane," Petra said. "The surveyor. Elias Vane." The clerk's papers had been thorough on that one point — names, ranks, the date the expedition went silent. Vane had been the senior man, a river engineer with thirty years and a daughter, according to the file, though the file had spent more words on the instruments than on the girl.

"Follow the bluffs," Sable read, in the tone she used for boiler gauges that were reading wrong. "Follow them to where, Cap'n? There's nothing up there but rock and goats."

"Then it's a good thing," Petra said, standing, "that we're not goats."

But he didn't smile, and neither did she. Because here was the arithmetic that wouldn't sit still in his head. Six men. Six tents. Six empty instrument crates, packed by careful hands. And one name scratched into pine by a man who'd had time to think about who might come looking — which meant Vane, or whoever Vane had become by then, had *expected* someone to come looking. Expected it enough to leave a sign. And had still chosen to leave no tracks, no note, no body, no blood, no broken anything — nothing but a bearing, cut into wood, pointing up into the bluffs like a finger raised to the lips.

*Shh,* said the camp. *Shh, and follow me, and don't ask what happened here.*

The river had swallowed men before. Petra had pulled bodies out of her every spring of his life — drowned loggers, drowned drunkards, drowned fools who'd raced a freshet and lost. The river was honest that way. She took what she took and left the rest for the gulls, and the grieving could stand on the bank and know, at least, *where*.

This was not that. This was a door closed from the inside.

"Cap'n," Pip called from the landing, and there was something in the boy's voice that put Petra's heart up into his throat before he'd even turned. "There's a light up in the bluffs. Just for a second. Fen saw it too."

"Fire?" Sable asked.

"No," said Fen, coming up the bank at a run, and for once the twins were out of step, one ahead of the other, both of them pale. "It moved wrong for a fire. It moved like — like somebody holding up a piece of glass to catch the sun."

A signal. Petra looked down at the crate, at the neat little letters pointing up into the rock, and then up at the bluffs themselves, gray and sheer and stitched with shadow, and somewhere in that maze of stone, someone was watching the survey camp. Someone had been watching it long enough to know when a beat-up sternwheeler came nosing up the bend. Someone who had spent — how long? weeks? — alone up there with six empty crates and a story the river refused to tell.

"Well," Petra said, and heard his own voice come out steadier than he felt, which was, he supposed, the entire job of being a captain. "I reckon we've been invited."

He took the bearing crate under his arm, told Marisol to keep the supper fires small and the twins on the lines, and started up the bluff trail with the sun going red behind him and the Rivergate muttering below, and the whole way up, the thought kept pacing circles in his chest like a cat that wouldn't settle:

Whatever was waiting up there had watched six men disappear without leaving a ghost. And it had chosen, out of everyone on this river, to show *him* a light.
