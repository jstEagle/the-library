## 3. The Girl in the Bluffs

I had counted the days by the river's moods. Eleven days since the men came with lanterns and quiet voices and took my father's crew down into the dark of the cutbank, and the river had been gray and sullen the whole time, as if it knew what it had been made to swallow.

I lived in the bluffs now. That sounds more romantic than it was. The bluffs were a shelf of crumbling sandstone above the old survey camp, pocked with hollows where the wind had done its patient carving, and I had chosen the deepest hollow, the one with a chokecherry bush hung across its mouth like a curtain. I had a blanket that had stopped being warm in any meaningful sense. I had half a sack of hardtack gone soft as chalk. I had my father's instrument case, sealed with his own wax and his own signet, pressed against my ribs every night like a second heartbeat.

And I had a rule: trust no one who comes upriver smiling.

The man who came on the twelfth day was not smiling, which is the only reason I let him live past the first hour.

I heard the Wreck's Fortune before I saw her — a sound like a great tired animal coughing itself awake. The sternwheel slapped the water in that lopsided rhythm I'd learned meant a boat with a bad paddle and a worse budget. I lay flat at the lip of my hollow and watched her nose into the abandoned camp below, and I watched the man at her rail.

He was big in the way river men get big, not from meat but from weather. Gray in his beard, a hat that had lost an argument with the wind years ago, and a walk — when he finally stepped onto the bank — that belonged to someone who had never once hurried in his life. He stood among the cold kettles and empty tents of my father's last camp and turned in a slow circle, and I saw him do what my father used to do: read the ground like a page.

He read it for a long time. Then he said, out loud, to no one, "Well. Somebody's been keeping house."

My blood went cold. I had been careful. I had swept my tracks, buried my ash, taken water only at dawn when the current would scrub the mud. But careful is a word for people with full stomachs, and I had been living on chalk and river water for eleven days, and apparently hunger leaves its own tracks.

He looked up. Not around — up, directly at my hollow, as though the chokecherry bush had politely stepped aside.

"Miss Vane," he said. "I'd take it kind if you didn't put an arrow in me. I've had a long week and I'd hate to waste the ending."

I did not have an arrow. I had a skinning knife and a boat hook I'd sharpened on a stone, and I told him so, from the shadow of my hollow, in a voice I barely recognized as mine — rusty, cracked, furious.

"Stay there," I said.

"Wasn't planning on climbing," he said. "Cliffs and me have an understanding. I stay off them, they stay off me." He sat down on an old crate, unhurried, and set his hands flat on his knees. "Petra Brightwater. Captain of that ugly thing in the eddy. I expect you watched me come up. I'd have waved, but I didn't know yet whether you were the shooting sort."

"Who sent you?"

"Government clerk. Fellow named Quill — Mr. Aldous Quill, Bureau of Surveys. Paid me to find the lost expedition." He said it plainly, and I'll grant him this: he didn't dress it up. "Paid me more than the boat's worth, which I'll admit made my teeth itch."

"Then your teeth are smarter than you are."

"They generally are." He tilted his hat back. "Your father's team walked into the river country in the spring and didn't walk out. Quill wants to know what happened to them. So, I'm beginning to suspect, do you — seeing as you're up here eating hardtack and glaring at me like a hawk with a grudge."

"They're dead," I said. "Most of them."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not surprise — I think he'd already guessed — but a settling, like a man shifting weight onto a floor he knows is rotten. "Most."

"Six of them. The soldiers came at night and took them down to the cutbank. I heard it." My voice did something I didn't authorize. "I heard everything."

The silence that followed was long. Below, the Wreck's Fortune creaked against her lines, and the river ran on with its gray, knowing patience.

"Soldiers," he said at last. "Not river. Not accident."

"Soldiers with a barge and lanterns and orders." I came forward to the mouth of the hollow then, because I was tired of shouting and tired of hiding, and because I wanted him to see my face when I said the rest. "They were looking for the instruments. My father's instruments. They didn't find them, because my father was a stubborn man who taught his daughter to be a stubborn woman, and the case is with me, and it stays with me, and if you've been paid to take it you can tell your clerk he's wasted his money."

"I wasn't paid to take anything," he said. "I was paid to find a missing survey team. Found most of one, near as I can tell." He looked at me a long moment — not at the knife, not at the sharpened boat hook, but at my face, at what eleven days of chalk and grief had made of it. "You're Isla Vane."

"I'm the girl with the knife."

"That too." He rubbed his jaw. "Miss Vane, I'm going to say a thing, and you're welcome to throw the boat hook at me after. I don't know what's in that case. But I know what a man looks like when he's lying about why he hired me, and Quill sweated through his collar the whole time he did it. And I know what a government does with a survey it doesn't like." He stood, slowly, hands still visible. "I grew up on this river. She's mean, but she's honest. Men who drown you at night in a cutbank — they're not honest. They're worse than the river, and I have hated the river my whole life, so that's saying something."

I did not throw the boat hook. I want it recorded that this was a decision, not an accident.

"Come down to the fire," he said. "Marisol's cooking. She's my cook, and she'll feed you whether you trust me or not — she considers it a moral obligation. You can sit with the case between you and the whole crew and the knife in your lap, and in the morning I'll run you wherever you want, or leave you here if that's what you want. But you'll eat first. I won't have a Vane starving to death on my watch. Your father surveyed the channel above Kettle Bend in '71 and saved my father's boat from the shoals there. I remember the name. Rivers are small places, Miss Vane. Word gets around."

I told him I'd think about it. I told him it in a voice that meant no. Then I watched him walk back down to his boat, unhurried, and I sat in my hollow with the case against my ribs and did the arithmetic of my body — eleven days, half a sack of chalk, hands that shook when I didn't tell them to — and I hated the answer.

* * *

I came down at dusk, because dusk is when pride goes hungry.

The fire was on the bank, tucked behind the boat, small and tidy. The cook — Marisol, a small round woman with forearms like dock rope and eyes that missed nothing — took one look at me and said, "Sit," in the voice of a woman who had fed armies, children, and idiots, and saw no meaningful distinction.

I sat. With the case on my knees and the knife in my belt and my back to a piling.

She put a bowl in my hands. Stew. Real stew, with beans and salt pork and something green I couldn't name and didn't care to. I meant to eat slowly, with dignity. I ate it like a drowning woman, and she watched me do it and refilled the bowl without a word, and when I came up for air she said, "Eleven days?"

"Twelve."

"Mm." She handed me bread. "Eat. Judge the captain after."

The crew assembled themselves around the fire in ones and twos, the way a family assembles when a stranger's in the room. An old man with a beard like steel wool — Sable, the engineer, who nodded at me once, the nod of a man who'd assessed me and filed me under *not a threat, possibly an asset*. Twin boys, maybe fifteen, Pip and Fen, who stared at me with the frank hunger of the young for anything new, and who were silenced by one look from Marisol.

And Petra, at the edge of the firelight, deliberately not looking at me, giving me the one thing nobody had given me in twelve days: room.

"You said soldiers," he said at last, to the fire, not to me. "Night work. Lanterns."

"Two barges. They came down from the diversion works above the falls." I said it and felt the case grow heavier on my knees. "They asked my father where the readings were. He told them the readings were the readings. They took the crew down the bank." I stopped. The fire popped. "I was meant to be asleep in the survey tent. My father put me in the bluffs that afternoon. He knew. I don't know how he knew, but he knew, and he sealed the case and told me, *if anyone comes upriver, Isla, you watch them longer than they watch you.*"

"And you have," Petra said.

"Twelve days."

"Good habits." He looked up then, and there was no pity in his face, which is the only reason I kept talking. Pity would have broken me. "What's in the case, if it's yours to say?"

And here is where I made my mistake, or my choice — I've never decided which. I looked around that fire, at the old engineer and the round cook and the twin boys who had stopped staring and started listening, at the big slow captain who had fed me before he'd questioned me, and I thought: *my father trusted a river full of strangers with his life for thirty years, and the only people who ever hurt him wore a government's buttons.*

"It's my father's soundings," I said. "Every mile of the Rivergate above Kettle Bend. Depth, flow, seasonal levels, going back three years. And the levels are wrong. They've been wrong for two years, dropping in ways the rain doesn't explain. Someone is taking the water out of the river above the falls — damming her, diverting her — quietly, without a survey on record, without an act of the legislature. And if they keep on, the water that three towns drink and farm and float their goods on—" my hands had gone steady now, steadier than they'd been all day, "—will simply stop coming."

Nobody spoke. Sable spat into the fire, thoughtfully. One of the twins — Fen, I learned later — said, "Three towns? Like Rivermouth?"

"Like Rivermouth," I said.

"My gran's in Rivermouth," said Pip.

"Then your gran," said Isla Vane, "is being robbed, and so is everyone she knows, and the men who did it drowned six people to keep it quiet."

The fire crackled. Somewhere out in the dark, the Rivergate ran on, gray and patient, carrying the news of it to every town downstream whether anyone read it or not.

Petra Brightwater leaned forward, elbows on knees, and looked into the flames for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed — slower, heavier, the voice of a man setting down a weight he can't pick back up.

"Quill didn't hire me to find a survey team," he said. "Quill hired me to find whatever was left of one. And I'm starting to think he didn't send me upriver to bring anybody home." He looked at me. "Miss Vane — how fast can you be ready to run?"
