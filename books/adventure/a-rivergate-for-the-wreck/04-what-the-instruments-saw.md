## 4. What the Instruments Saw

The case had three locks, and my father had trusted me with exactly none of them.

That was the joke, if you wanted to call it one. Eleven years of apprenticeship — grinding lenses, calibrating the theodolite, learning to read water the way priests read scripture — and when it came time for him to vanish up the Rivergate and leave me holding the only proof of what he'd found, he'd handed me a box I couldn't open and a look that said *you'll figure it out, Isla-girl, you always do.*

I had figured it out. It had taken me four days, a bent hairpin, and a blister I still resented. But I hadn't opened it. Not once in all those weeks alone in the bluffs, with my stomach eating itself and the river talking to itself down below like an old man muttering in his sleep. Because opening it meant the thing inside became real, and I was not at all certain I wanted it to be real.

Now there was a river captain sitting across my fire with his hat off and his hands folded, watching me the way men watch a kettle they're too polite to say they want boiled.

"You don't have to," Petra Brightwater said. "I want that understood first. Whatever's in there, it's yours. I was hired to find your father's party, and I've found one of them, and as far as I'm concerned that's the contract done and we can all go home."

"You were hired by the Survey Office," I said.

"I was hired by a clerk who sweats through his collar like a mule in August, if that counts."

"Mr. Quill." I said the name the way you'd spit out a fishbone.

"You know him?"

"I know the office. I know what the office does with things it doesn't like." I turned the case over in my lap. The brass fittings were cold even this close to the fire. "They didn't send anyone to look for my father, Captain. Not one boat, not one rider, in five months. And then suddenly a clerk comes downriver with a contract fat enough to choke on. You don't spend that kind of money looking for a dead man."

Petra was quiet a moment. Firelight moved on his face and made the lines around his eyes deeper. "No," he said finally. "You don't."

"So either you're a better liar than you look, or you're the only man on this river who doesn't know what he's carrying." I looked at him straight. "I'd like to know which, before I open this."

"And if I'm the first one?"

"Then I've made a terrible mistake and I'll be gone before your crew wakes up."

He nodded slowly, like a man agreeing to terms he didn't fully enjoy. "And if I'm the second?"

"Then you're about to become the most dangerous man on the Rivergate, and you won't even have done anything wrong."

He laughed — a short, surprised sound, as if it had escaped without permission. "That's the most honest offer anyone's made me since I bought this boat." He leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Open it."

* * *

The first lock was the hairpin. The second was patience. The third was a trick my father had shown me when I was nine and had sworn me to secrecy about, which I supposed was void now that he was missing and the secret was the whole point of me.

Inside, the case was lined in green felt, and the instruments sat in their fitted beds like sleeping animals: the theodolite wrapped in oilcloth, the barometer nested in wool, and beneath them, flat as a Bible, three leather notebooks and a sheaf of survey charts tied with waxed string.

My hands were shaking. I hated that they were shaking.

"Take your time," Petra said. "The river's not going anywhere." Then, glancing at the black water sliding past below the bluff: "Well. It's going somewhere. Just not quickly."

That helped, absurdly. I untied the string.

The notebooks were in my father's hand — small, ferociously neat, the handwriting of a man who believed a crooked number was a small sin and a crooked sentence was a large one. I knew the format by heart: date, station, reading, remark. I started at the beginning, in the spring, and read aloud, because I wanted him in the air, and because I wanted to watch the captain's face while I did it.

"May the fourth. Station twelve, above Kettle Bend. Discharge measurement, current meter, three traverses." I turned the page. "Remark: discharge reads forty percent below seasonal norm. Rechecked. Rechecked again. Remark, second entry: *the river is being stolen.*"

"Stolen," Petra repeated.

"Diverted." I pulled out the charts. My father had drawn the Rivergate's main channel in his careful ink, and then — this was the part that had made him write *stolen*, I was sure of it — he'd drawn the rest. A web of feeder canals, sluice cuts, and culverts, branching off the upper river like roots from a pulled weed, all of them running toward a valley that wasn't on any map I'd ever seen. "There's a dam going up in the high country. A big one. And these—" I tapped the feeder lines, one by one, "—these are taking the water around three towns. Marrow's Bend. Two Rivers. Little Ferry."

Petra had gone very still. "Those towns are on my run. I've landed at Two Rivers forty times. There's a woman there who makes a peach pie that could end wars."

"Then you should know that in two years, maybe three, the channel past Two Rivers will be a mud flat with a creek in it. The Office's own figures say so." I found the page — I'd read it so many times I could have recited it — and read it aloud. "Projected low-water stage at Two Rivers landing: eighteen inches. *Unserviceable for all commercial traffic.*"

"Eighteen inches." He said it flat, testing the weight of it. My father had been the same way — when a number was too big to feel, he'd hold it up to the light and turn it over until it cut him.

"There's more."

"There usually is," he said grimly, and I almost smiled.

The last notebook was thinner. It was the one he'd kept in the last weeks, and the handwriting had begun to lean, as if the man writing it was being pushed. The entries stopped being measurements and started being testimony.

"They found out he knew," I said. "I think that's what this is. He started recording everything — the survey crew that came up from the Office in June, the men who told the team their contract was rescinded, the ones who followed them from camp to camp. He wrote down names, Captain. He wrote down who gave the orders." I turned a page and my breath caught, because there it was, in my father's tidy hand, underlined twice: *Quill, T. — clerk, Survey Office — signed the requisitions for the diversion works. Also signed the order rescinding our contract. Same pen, same ink, same week.*

"Quill," Petra said. "My Quill."

"Your Quill."

He sat back and rubbed his jaw, and for a long moment the only sound was the fire and the river. I watched him do the arithmetic I'd done alone in this bluff a hundred times: if the clerk who'd hired him was the same clerk who'd erased my father, then the contract wasn't a rescue. It was a search. They hadn't sent him to find the survey team. They'd sent him to find the *instruments* — and whoever was left holding them.

"I'm going to say something," Petra said at last, "and I want you to hear all of it before you decide what it means. If I take this case downriver and deliver it to Quill, I get paid. Paid enough to keep the *Wreck's Fortune* afloat for years. My crew eats. My debts go away." He looked at the fire. "And three towns die of thirst with a river running right past them, and I'd know it, and I'd have to be a man who knew it every day for the rest of my life."

"That's a very honest speech for a man I met yesterday."

"I'm told it's a character flaw." He looked up. "So what do you want to do with it?"

I had imagined this moment so many times — alone, starving, talking to a box. In the imaginings I was always brave about it. In the imaginings there was never a gunboat, never a crew of strangers whose livelihoods hung off my decision like laundry off a line.

"I want to take it to Terrapin Landing," I said. "There's a newspaper there. The *Terrapin Clarion*. The editor's name is Hobb, and my father trusted him — he's the only man downriver who's ever printed something the Office didn't like and lived. If the readings get printed, if the towns hear it from a press and not from a rumor, the Office can't bury it. They can bury a survey team. They can't bury three thousand people who all read the same paper at breakfast."

"And Quill?"

"Quill will do anything to stop us. I've read what his pen did to my father's contract. I don't think his hands are gentler than his ink."

Petra nodded slowly. Then he did something I didn't expect. He reached out, and instead of taking the case, he took one of the notebooks — gently, like it might bite — and read a page for himself, lips moving slightly, a riverman sounding out the language of water. When he finished he set it down and said, "Your father wrote *the river is being stolen* and then he rechecked it twice. That's the most riverman thing I've ever read in a government book."

"He was very thorough."

"He was very angry," Petra said. "Thorough is how angry men write when they're also right." He stood, and rolled his shoulders, and looked downriver, where the dark had settled over the water like a lid. "All right, Isla Vane. Here's how it is. My engineer will tell me I'm a fool. My cook will tell me I'm a fool and then feed me anyway. And the twins will think the whole thing is a grand adventure, which is somehow the most alarming part." He picked up his hat and settled it on his head. "We take you and the case down to the boat tonight. We run for Terrapin at first light."

"Captain." I stood too, and the case was heavy in my arms, heavier than brass and felt had any right to be. "Do you understand what you're carrying? Once this leaves this bluff, you're carrying the thing three towns' lives depend on and the one thing the government will burn a county to get back. Some cargo sinks ships. My father used to say that."

Petra looked at the case a long moment. Then at me.

"My father also used to say," I went on, before I could lose my nerve, "*a riverman who won't run water he's afraid of shouldn't have bought a boat.*"

Something moved across his face — grief and humor sharing a bench. "Your father," he said, "sounds like a man I'd have liked to argue with over cards." He held out his hands for the case, and I gave it to him, and it looked small against his chest and weighed as much as the river.

We were halfway down the bluff trail, the fire banked behind us, when he stopped so suddenly I walked into his back.

Below us, far down the Rivergate where the channel straightened before Kettle Bend, a light was moving against the current. Not a campfire. Not a fisherman's lantern. A white, disciplined, steady light, sweeping side to side across the water — and under it, faint as a heartbeat, the sound of a steam whistle, long and low and official.

"Get down," Petra breathed. "That's no packet boat."
