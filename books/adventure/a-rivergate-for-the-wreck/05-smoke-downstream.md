## 5. Smoke Downstream

Petra Brightwater had a rule about smoke on the Rivergate: you could smell a cookfire from two miles if the wind was right, and you could smell a lie from further still. The trouble that morning was that the wind was wrong, and the smoke came anyway.

He was on the roof deck splicing a frayed hawser when he saw it — a gray smudge hanging low over the water downstream, where no town stood, no fish camp, nothing that ought to be burning breakfast. He held the rope still and watched it the way his mother had taught him to watch weather: not with his eyes but with his gut.

"Marisol," he called down the hatch. "How many eggs we got?"

"Six," she said, appearing with flour on her forearms like a woman who'd been wrestling a ghost. "Why? You planning a last supper?"

"Just wondering how long we can afford to sit still."

* * *

They'd made good time since the bluffs. Isla Vane had come aboard with her father's instrument case strapped to her back like a second spine, and for three days she'd barely let it out of arm's reach. She slept sitting up against it in the pilothouse corner. She ate with one hand on its latch. Petra had seen men guard gold with less devotion and more sense.

Old Sable didn't like her much, which was how Old Sable showed affection. "Girl sits like she's waiting to be robbed," he grumbled over his engine, wiping forty years of grease off hands that would never come clean. "Anybody who trusts nobody's usually got something worth stealing."

"She's got something worth killing for," Petra said. "Different animal."

The twins, Pip and Fen, had adopted her the way river children adopt anything strange — cautiously at first, then completely. They'd taught her cribbage. She'd taught them star bearings. By the third night she was laughing at their bickering, a small startled sound, like she'd forgotten the machinery of it and was pleased to find it still worked.

Petra watched all this from the wheel and felt the old warm weight settle into his chest, the one that meant he was starting to care again, which historically had never once worked out cheap.

So when the smoke appeared, he did what any sensible captain does: he lied to himself about it for exactly as long as it took to eat lunch.

* * *

It was Fen who spotted the boat. She was up the mast pretending to work, which mostly meant spying, which was honestly her best quality.

"Sternwheeler!" she shouted down. "No flag. Paddlebox painted gray. She's coming up the channel slow, like she's looking for something."

Gray paint. No flag. Coming upriver at survey speed, nosing along the banks. Petra put down his coffee and felt the morning go cold around the edges.

There were exactly three reasons a government gunboat traveled without colors: secrecy, deniability, and shame. Sometimes all three at once.

He climbed to the pilothouse and raised Old Sable's battered spyglass. The vessel below was maybe four miles down, working the eastern bank, and riding low in the way boats ride when they're carrying more men than furniture. He could make out figures on the foredeck. He could make out, glinting on the rail, the particular dull shine of things that were not fishing gear.

"Well," said Isla quietly. She'd come up behind him without a sound, which should have been impossible on a wooden boat full of creaks. She was looking through the glass before he thought to offer it. "That'll be for me."

"You don't know that."

"I know my father's office sent six men upriver and told the towns they went home early." Her voice was flat and steady, the voice of someone who had done all her crying in private and budgeted carefully. "I know a clerk paid you triple rate to retrace their route. And I know that boat is checking every slough and landing between here and the bluffs, which is what you do when you're hunting something small and tired." She looked at him then, and her eyes were the color of the river in November. "You're the only thing on this stretch of water big enough to carry me. He didn't hire you to find wreckage, Captain. He hired you to flush me out."

Petra set the spyglass down. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Inside, some part of him that had been quietly doing arithmetic since the dock at Terrapin Landing finally finished the sum and turned the ledger around to face him.

Triple rate. Paid half up front. A clerk who trembled too much, asked too few questions, and knew too precisely where to send him. He'd told himself the man was just a coward. Cowards existed; the river bred them like mosquitoes. But cowards didn't write contracts with clauses about *delivering persons or effects recovered*.

He'd read that clause. He'd signed under it anyway, because the Wreck's Fortune owed the bank eleven hundred dollars and the bank did not accept excuses, only money.

"He offered me a bonus," Petra said. It came out before he decided to say it, which was often how the truth left him. "For recovery of persons. I figured it meant bodies."

"And now?"

"Now I figure the bonus has a name, and she's standing on my boat eating Marisol's eggs."

Isla's hand moved to the instrument case strap. Not clutching it. Just resting there, the way you rest a hand on a friend's shoulder. "Say the word and I'm gone before dark. I won't sink your ship."

"It's a boat."

"It's your whole life. I've watched you talk to her."

"That's normal," said Pip from the ladder, having materialized the way twins do. "Fen talks to the mast."

"The mast listens," said Fen, also on the ladder, also immediately.

* * *

Petra ran the numbers while the gray boat ate the miles below them. Four miles. At their speed, call it an hour and a half before they were close enough to hail him, and a hail was all it would take — *Captain Brightwater, heave to, we'd like a word about your passengers* — and then everything would depend on how good a liar he was under a rifle's attention. He was a fair liar on dry land. On his own deck, with his crew watching, he wasn't sure.

But there was another option. There was always another option on the Rivergate, if you'd grown up on her like a barnacle, if you'd spent thirty years learning her moods and her secrets and the places she kept hidden the way a miser keeps coins.

Four miles upstream of the Narrows, on the western shore, the river split around a drowned island called — by the three people alive who remembered it — the Widow's Tooth. The main channel ran east, wide and honest. But on the west side, behind the island's broken crown, lay a slough: a backwater no wider than the Fortune herself, choked with deadfall and overhung with cottonwoods, invisible from the channel unless you knew to look for the crooked lightning-scarred pine on the bluff above it. Petra's father had shown it to him when Petra was nine, hiding from a revenue cutter after a misunderstanding involving untaxed whiskey and a very fast canoe.

Nobody else alive knew it. Nobody else alive cared.

"Marisol," he said, "how quickly can you make black smoke?"

She looked at him, flour-dusted and unblinking. "Depends on why."

"A broken engine. Boilers fouled. We're drifting, helpless, embarrassed."

"Rubber, tar, and wet leaves," she said. "Ten minutes. It'll stink like the devil."

"The devil and I have an arrangement."

* * *

By the time the gray boat was two miles out, the Wreck's Fortune was performing the finest theatrical collapse of her career. Black smoke rolled from her stack in greasy ropes. Old Sable hammered something inside the engine box with tremendous conviction and language that would have blistered paint. Petra stood at the rail waving both arms at the approaching vessel with the desperate energy of a man whose world was ending, which was true, but not in the way he advertised.

"They're signaling," Fen reported from aloft. "Flags. I think it says 'assistance required?'"

"Tell them yes! Very required! Extremely!"

The gray boat slowed a quarter mile off and lowered a skiff. Six men in plain clothes rowed toward the Fortune, and Petra counted rifles the way other men counted sheep. In the bow sat a figure he recognized even at distance: narrow shoulders, nervous hands, a hat worn like an apology. The clerk. Of course the clerk. Bait didn't stay home; bait came along to make the identification.

Petra arranged his face into ruin.

"Gentlemen," he called as the skiff bumped alongside, "you're a sight for sore eyes. My boiler's given up the ghost, my engineer's given up hope, and I've got cargo rotting in the hold. I'll pay for a tow, I'll pay for parts, I'll pay for someone to tell me it isn't as bad as Sable says."

Sable, hearing his cue, chose that moment to emerge from below covered head to foot in soot, coughing theatrically, and pronounce the word "catastrophic" with such relish that two of the soldiers actually stepped back.

The clerk peered up at the deck, and Petra watched those nervous eyes do their work — flicking past Marisol, past the twins, past Sable, lingering a half-second on the pilothouse, where Isla stood in shadow behind the glass with her father's case held against her chest like a shield. A half-second. Then the eyes moved on.

Because the pilothouse was empty. Isla wasn't there. She was already gone, slipped below through the back hatch while every eye was on the smoke, taking the case with her into the hold's deepest cranny, the one behind the spare boiler plate where Petra hid contraband tobacco from himself.

The clerk's shoulders dropped a fraction. "We're... surveying," he said. "Government hydrographic work. You haven't seen anyone on the river? Travelers? A young woman?"

"Can't say I have," Petra said warmly. "River's been empty as a church on dice night. Mostly us and the herons. Say, about that tow—"

"We don't tow civilians." The clerk was already climbing back into the skiff, eager as a man leaving a dentist. "You'll want to effect repairs quickly. Water's rising. There's flood warnings posted downstream."

"Flood warnings," Petra repeated. "In planting season?"

The clerk didn't answer. The skiff pulled away. The gray boat turned, resumed its patient climb upriver, and began checking the eastern banks, the landings, the mouths of creeks — everywhere except behind the Widow's Tooth, because nobody looked behind the Widow's Tooth.

* * *

An hour later, with the gunboat a diminishing smudge to the north, Petra backed the Wreck's Fortune gently off her drift and slid her west, quiet as a rumor, through the gap in the deadfall into the slough. Cottonwood branches brushed the roof deck. The water went still and green and secret around them. From the channel, ten feet away through the trees, they were a ghost, a story, a trick of the light.

He killed the engines. The silence rang.

Then he went below, knocked twice on the boiler plate, and helped Isla out of her hiding hole. She emerged dusty, composed, and furious in the quiet way she did everything.

"You could have taken the bonus," she said. It wasn't gratitude. It was an audit.

"I could have," Petra agreed. He leaned against the boiler plate and looked at her, and let himself feel the full weight of what he'd just thrown away: eleven hundred dollars of debt, a bank, a boat, thirty years of staying afloat by never, ever betting on anything but himself. "Triple rate plus recovery fee. Enough to own the Fortune outright and retire to Terrapin Landing and drink coffee until I die of comfort."

"Why didn't you?"

Petra considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty and because he hadn't yet answered it for himself. Above them, faintly, he heard Pip explaining cribbage strategy to Fen, loudly and wrongly. He heard Marisol singing something in her grandmother's Spanish, off-key and unbothered. He heard Old Sable muttering to the engine, apologizing to it, probably.

"Because I signed a contract with a man who burns towns," he said finally, "and I've been trying to figure out how to break it without breaking myself. Turns out the answer was standing right here, guarding a box." He pushed off the wall and headed for the ladder. "Get some sleep. We move at dusk, when the light's bad and their eyes are tired. And Isla—"

"Yes?"

"When we get to Terrapin Landing, whatever's in that case better be worth what it just cost me."

She almost smiled. "It's worth more than that."

"Good," said Petra Brightwater, climbing into the green dark of the hidden slough, "because I have a feeling the river's about to send us the bill."
