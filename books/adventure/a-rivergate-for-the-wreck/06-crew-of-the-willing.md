## 6. Crew of the Willing

Petra found Old Sable where he always found him when the world went wrong: down in the engine room, elbow-deep in the heart of the Wreck's Fortune, talking to her like she was a stubborn aunt.

"She's knocking again," Sable said without looking up. "Number two cylinder. Been knocking since Smoke Downstream, and don't tell me you didn't hear it, because I heard *you* hear it, standing on the wheelhouse roof pretending to study the current like a man with a plan."

"I have a plan," Petra said.

"You have a woman with a case full of trouble, a gunboat somewhere behind us, and a plan." Sable finally turned, wiping his hands on a rag that had been gray since before the war. His face was a topographical map of seventy years, and every line of it was skeptical. "So which is it, Captain? Do I fix the knocking and pretend we're a working boat, or do I start thinking about what a man my age carries when he jumps ship?"

Petra sat down on the toolbox across from him. In the close heat of the engine room, with the oil smell wrapping around them like an old coat, he told it all straight. The clerk. The contract. The girl in the bluffs. The instruments, and what the instruments proved — that somebody upstream was strangling the Rivergate, and that three towns downstream, Marisol's hometown among them, were scheduled by some gentleman's arithmetic to drown.

Sable listened the way he listened to engines: completely, without interrupting, one hand resting on the hull as if taking her pulse.

When Petra finished, the old man was quiet a long moment. Then he patted the deck plates under him.

"She's been running scared since the smoke," he said. "I figured it out around the same time you did. And I'll tell you what I told her, because I tell her everything." He fixed Petra with a look that had outlasted two owners, one bankruptcy, and the great boiler fire of '09. "I was on the *Cormorant* when she went down. Eleven years I've been on this boat, and she's the only thing that never lied to me. So the question isn't whether I stay. The question is whether you're man enough to ask."

"I'm asking," Petra said.

"Then get out of my engine room," Sable said, turning back to the cylinder, "and let me fix her knocking. If we're going to be outlaws, we'll at least be *quiet* outlaws."

* * *

Marisol heard the whole story standing at her galley stove, and her only reaction was to keep stirring.

That was the thing about Marisol that people mistook for calm, right up until they learned it was something considerably more dangerous. She had fed deckhands through two floods and a fever season. She had once talked a boarding officer out of inspecting the cargo hold by feeding him tamales until he forgot what he'd come aboard for. She stirred, and she listened, and when Petra ran out of words she said, "Sit down. You look like a heron that lost an argument."

He sat. She put a bowl in front of him — pepper stew, the kind she made when the news was bad — and then she stood over him with her wooden spoon like a scepter.

"My sister lives in Willow Bend," she said.

"I know."

"Willow Bend is one of the three."

"I know, Mari."

"Then stop looking at me like you're about to break it to me gently, because if you do that, I will hit you with this spoon, and I have ended better men than you with less provocation." She sat down across from him, and for the first time her voice came down out of the galley voice and into the real one, low and steady. "You're going to tell me there's a way out for you. You're going to say I could take the skiff, go downstream, be safe in Terrapin Landing before the week's out. You've been rehearsing it since the bluffs. I can hear it rattling around in you like a loose rivet."

Petra looked at his stew.

"So here's what I'm going to do instead," Marisol said. "I'm going to make enough food for six, because the twins will say yes, they always say yes, that's their whole tragedy. And tomorrow I'm going to start teaching Isla to make bread, because that girl is skin and bones and secrets, and nobody survives either one without bread." She stood, and took the spoon, and pointed it at him one last time. "And you're going to stop deciding for this crew what we're willing to lose. That's not the captain's job. That's the captain's *vanity*."

* * *

Pip's version, told to Fen that night on the bow while they split a watch neither of them needed to split:

"—and she just *said* it. Like it was nothing. Like telling the government to go swim was the same as passing the salt."

"She didn't tell the government to go swim."

"She told the *clerk* to go swim. Same thing, smaller hat." Pip lay back against the deckhouse and put her boots up on the rail. "Anyway. We're in it. You heard her."

"I heard *her* say yes. I haven't said yes."

Pip turned her head and looked at her brother. Fen was eighteen minutes older, four inches taller, and had never once in his life made a decision without first checking whether Pip was making it too. It was, she felt, the least he could do to let her have the moment.

"Fen," she said. "There's a gunboat."

"Yes."

"There's a flood coming, probably."

"Probably."

"There's a girl our age who hasn't laughed once since she came aboard, and yesterday I caught her watching Marisol knead dough like it was a magic trick, and I thought, that girl has never had a family that stayed." Pip shrugged. "Also, if we jump ship now, we're deckhands on some mudboat the rest of our lives, and if we stay, we're the deckhands who ran the gunboat. Which story do you want to tell at the pilot's hall when we're old?"

Fen considered this with the gravity he brought to all things, including breakfast.

"The second one," he said.

"Then you've said yes."

"I've said yes."

"Good." She settled back. "Because I already told Sable we'd take the night watches, and he said if we were going to volunteer him for things we should at least volunteer to stand half of them ourselves."

* * *

Isla came up to the wheelhouse near midnight, when the river had gone silver and quiet and the Wreck's Fortune sat at anchor in the slough like a secret kept well.

Petra was at the wheel out of habit, though the wheel needed nothing from him. She stood in the doorway a while before she spoke — she did that, he'd noticed. Took the temperature of a room before entering it. A girl who'd learned that doors were where the danger lived.

"They told me," she said. "The crew. What they chose."

"They chose fast," Petra said. "I didn't give them much of a choice, honestly. Marisol would've hit me with a spoon."

"I heard about the spoon."

"The spoon is famous in three counties."

Isla came the rest of the way in and stood at the window beside him, the instrument case still slung across her body — she slept with it, ate with it, wore it like a second skeleton. Below them, lamplight showed in the galley where Marisol was putting up bread for morning, and the low murmur of the twins carried across the water, arguing about something with the easy rhythm of people who had never once meant it.

"I spent four months," Isla said, "being the only one who knew. Four months in the bluffs, telling myself that was the price. That knowing was mine to carry alone, because carrying it alone was the only way to keep it safe." Her hand moved to the case, an old gesture, automatic. "And then in one day, five people heard everything, and not one of them ran."

"Give them time," Petra said. "The twins haven't thought it through yet."

"They've thought it through better than I have. Pip told me at supper that fear is just excitement with bad information, and the information's getting better every day we don't get shot." Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a laugh, but the ground where one might take root. "Your crew is insane, Captain."

"Certified. It's on the registry."

She looked out at the water a long moment. "My father used to say a survey is just a promise made in numbers. That the map is a way of telling the truth so precisely nobody can argue with it." Her voice went quiet. "I thought I was guarding the instruments. I think maybe I was waiting for people who'd want to keep the promise."

Petra didn't say anything to that. There wasn't anything to say that the river wasn't already saying, sliding silver and patient past the hull.

* * *

It was Fen on the midnight watch who saw it first — or rather, who heard it, because the eyes were useless in the dark and the boy had ears like a fox.

A sound from downstream, carried up the slough on the flood wind. Not the gunboat's engine; he knew that throb by heart now, had catalogued it in his head like a hymn. This was different. This was a low, rolling mutter from the gorge below them, the sound the river made when it stood up out of its bed.

He woke Pip. Pip woke Petra. By the time they reached the bow, the mutter had become a voice, and the voice was saying one word, over and over, in the language of water on the move.

Old Sable came up from the engine room with his lamp held high, and the light showed the river downstream of the slough's mouth running — not high, not fast, but *full*, bank to bank, with a white seam down its middle that had not been there at dusk.

"Flood," Sable said. "Early. Weeks early."

Petra looked at the water, and the water looked back, and he understood that the choice he'd been dreading — the gunboat, the clerk, the whole war of men — had just been overtaken by a much older adversary with no interest in bargains.

"Get us out of the slough," Isla said from the wheelhouse door, the case clutched to her chest. "Before it takes the choice away."

"The flood's already taken it," Petra said, and rang the engine bell.
