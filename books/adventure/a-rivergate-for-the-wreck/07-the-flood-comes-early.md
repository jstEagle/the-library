## 7. The Flood Comes Early

The river told them first, the way it always did, if you knew how to listen. Old Sable knew. He'd been listening to the Rivergate since before Petra was born, back when the Wreck's Fortune was young and her paddles bit clean water.

"Smell that?" he said, standing in the engine room with his head cocked like a heron's. He was elbow-deep in the guts of the boiler, grease to the shoulder, and he didn't even turn around. "Smell it?"

Petra sniffed. Mud. Pine. Marisol's beans, which were doing their level best to smell like nothing at all. "I smell dinner."

"You smell *silt*," Sable said. "Fresh silt. Off the high banks. That means water's moving up there that's got no business moving." He wiped his hands on a rag that was dirtier than his hands. "River's chewing on country it ain't chewed on in years."

Petra had been chewing on the same worry himself. Three days since they'd slipped the gunboat into the side slough, three days of creeping upriver by fits and starts, and the whole time the Rivergate had been behaving like a man with a secret. Running high when the season said low. Brown when it ought to run green. And the current — the current had teeth in it now, a pull you felt in the wheel ropes like something tugging your sleeve, asking, asking, *asking.*

"Diversions," Isla said from the doorway. She had her father's charts spread across the engineer's bench, weighted at the corners with wrenches. Her finger traced the lines of the government's secret works upstream — the coffer dams, the cuts, the gates that had been bleeding the Rivergate into channels it never agreed to. "They've been holding water back for months. All of it, behind their dams. And when the gates fail—"

"When, not if," Petra said.

"When," she agreed. "There's no spillway big enough. They built it cheap and built it quiet, and the river doesn't care about quiet." She looked up at him, and Petra saw the thing in her face that had been there since the bluffs — that hard, hungry need to be *believed.* "It's coming down the gorge. All of it at once."

* * *

They held a council on the texas deck that evening, which was to say Marisol brought up a pot of beans and everyone argued over them. The twins sat cross-legged on the roof of the pilothouse, Pip and Fen, identical as two nails and just as hard to tell apart, passing a biscuit back and forth.

"So we turn around," Fen said. "Run back down past the gunboat. Simple."

"Simple as a knife fight," Petra said. "That boat's still sitting at the slough mouth. I'd stake my hat on it."

"You don't have a hat."

"That's how serious it is."

Pip raised a hand like they were in school. "What about the gorge? The rapids. We're above it, right? Can't we just — go through? Isn't that what boats do?"

The deck went quiet. Marisol stopped stirring. Old Sable looked at Petra the way a man looks at a doctor before the diagnosis.

"The Gorge of the Nine Sisters," Petra said, "has killed every boat that ever tried it at flood stage. There's a reason the Rivergate Company routes around it. There's a reason the reason is carved on three headstones in Terrapin Landing." He turned his cup in his hands. "At normal water it's a hard run. At flood water it's a rumor. Nobody's ever come back to confirm it."

"Nobody's ever had to," Isla said quietly.

And that was the whole of it, laid out on the texas deck between the beans and the biscuits: behind them, soldiers and a gunboat and a clerk with a smile like a paper cut. Ahead, the gorge, running wild. And above them all, somewhere up in the dark hills, a river gathering itself to come down.

"We'll hold here tonight," Petra said at last. "Below the gorge, above the worst of the current. If the flood comes, we'll see it coming. Rivers don't sneak."

Old Sable snorted. "This one's learning."

* * *

It came at moonrise, and it did not sneak.

Petra heard it before he understood it — a sound like a freight train that had lost its rails, rolling down the canyon, getting louder. He was on the hurricane deck with a lantern, and the first thing he noticed was wrongness: the water in the channel below them was *falling,* dropping away like something exhaling, leaving mud banks naked and gleaming. The river pulling back its skirts before the dance.

"GET HER OFF THE BANK!" he roared, and the boat came alive. Marisol hauling on the spring line, the twins scrambling to take in the gangplank, Sable below feeding the boiler like a man stoking a furnace in hell. The *Wreck's Fortune* groaned off the mud with her old bones cracking, and Petra drove her out into the channel just as the flood came around the bend.

It did not look like water. It looked like the land itself changing its mind. A brown wall eight feet high and moving faster than anything that size had a right to move, carrying whole trees in its teeth — pines spinning like matchsticks, a section of fence line, a drowned deer riding the crest with its legs stiff as a figurehead. The wave hit the boat broadside and Petra went down on one knee, lantern spinning, and the *Wreck's Fortune* rose — actually *rose,* lifted on the shoulder of the flood like a child on a father's hands — and settled, wallowing, into water that was suddenly ten feet deeper than the charts said it could ever be.

Then the river simply ran. Fast. Terribly, gloriously fast, a full-grown flood in a canyon built for a creek, and every sandbar, every snag, every landmark Petra had memorized in thirty years on this water was gone or moving.

He got to the pilothouse and rang for Sable, and the old engine answered with a heartbeat he could feel through the deck plates. The twins appeared beside him without being called, the way they always did, one at the wheel lines and one at the window calling out what little there was to see, which was mostly brown fury and the occasional tree coming at them like a lance.

"Tree!" Fen shouted.

"I see it."

"Another tree!"

"I SEE IT."

Marisol came up the stairs with a pot of coffee in one hand and absolute contempt on her face, as if the end of the world were a scheduling problem she intended to have words about. "Nobody's eaten," she announced, and set the pot on the chart table, where it slid two feet and was caught by Isla, who had appeared in the doorway white as paper with her father's instrument case strapped to her back like a soldier's pack.

"Petra." Her voice was steady. He'd give her that; it was steady. "How bad?"

He took stock. The channel below them was a chute now, funneling toward the gorge, and the current was doing the steering whether he liked it or not. The gorge mouth lay two miles down, black in the dark, and the flood was carrying them toward it with the gentle insistence of a hand on the small of the back.

"Bad," he said. "But honest."

She frowned. "Honest?"

"Soldiers lie. Clerks lie. Governments lie in triplicate and file it." He nodded at the river raging past the windows, at the drowned trees going by, at the whole enormous indifferent violence of it. "The river never lies. It tells you exactly what it is and exactly what it'll do, and then it does it. No tricks. No bargains. You just have to be listening." He put his hands on the wheel. "It's the most honest thing I know. That's why I trust it more than the men who pay me."

The gorge mouth came up out of the dark like a held breath. The Nine Sisters — nine rock teeth down the center channel, white water combing off each one even in the old low-water charts — were invisible now, somewhere under all that brown, and the flood was pouring into the canyon with a roar that made the windows buzz.

Petra rang the engine to slow. Sable rang back, which meant: *I heard you, and I think you're wrong,* which was Sable's way of saying he loved him.

"Options," Petra said to the pilothouse, to all of them, because they were all in it now and they deserved to hear it plain. "We can try to make the bank above the gorge. That water's over the willows. We'd beach her high, maybe, and lose her to the flood anyway when it rises another three feet tonight. Or—"

"Or we run it," Pip said.

"Or we run it," Petra agreed. "At night. In flood. Through rocks nobody's ever mapped at this stage of water."

Silence. The river roared. The coffee pot slid and was caught again.

Isla stepped forward, the case on her back, her father's truth in her hands. "My father surveyed that gorge," she said. "Every rock. It's in the case. It's in *me.* I can read the water from the soundings he took. If anyone can put a boat through that, it's this boat, with this pilot, and—" her voice cracked, just once, and she hated it, he could see her hating it, "—and I am not letting the river have the last word. It's had it long enough."

Petra looked around the pilothouse. The twins, grinning like it was a fair. Marisol, arms crossed, who had not asked to be here and had not once suggested leaving. Old Sable's engine, beating below like a heart that refused to quit.

"Well," Petra said, and put the wheel over toward the black mouth of the gorge. "The river's honest. Let's go be honest right back."

Behind them, far downstream, a searchlight swept the water — the gunboat, coming up to see what the flood had dragged down to it. Ahead, the Nine Sisters waited under the brown water, patient as headstones.

"Full ahead," Petra said. "Sable — give me everything she's got. And somebody hold that coffee pot."
