## 8. The Clerk's Bargain

I learned to count heartbeats the way my father taught me to count seconds — by feel, without a watch, because out in the field your instruments fail you and all you have left is what your body knows. So I counted. The gunboat had come upriver at dusk and anchored in the channel below our slough, her lamps burning like a string of insolent lanterns hung on the dark. And now, at first light, there was a man standing on the Wreck's Fortune's forward deck under a white flag tied to an oar, and I counted forty of my own heartbeats before anyone spoke.

Forty heartbeats is a long time when everyone on deck is deciding whether to shoot.

"Mr. Quill," Petra said at last, and his voice was flat as a becalmed river. "You've got some nerve coming up here waving laundry."

"Flag of truce, Captain Brightwater." The clerk's voice carried better than I expected from such a narrow man. He was smaller than I remembered from the office in Terrapin Landing — I'd seen him only once, through a doorway, taking notes while men with better coats talked about my father as though he were a shipment gone astray. Up close he had the look of a person assembled from paperwork: pale, creased, stamped with worry. "You're obliged by custom to receive it."

"I'm obliged by nothing. You hired me with money that smelled like a dead fish, pointed me upriver like a hound, and now you show up behind me with a warship." Petra didn't move from the pilothouse door. Old Sable stood below him with a wrench the size of a forearm, which was not subtle, but Sable had never been subtle in his life. "Say what you came to say."

Quill's eyes found mine. That was the thing about clerks — they always knew who the cargo was.

* * *

They let him aboard, because that was the custom too, and because Marisol said if we were going to hang a man we ought at least feed him first, and she said it in the tone that meant she was joking and also entirely not. She put coffee in front of him on the main deck, on an overturned crate, and he wrapped both hands around the cup like a drowning man around driftwood. Pip and Fen flanked him with the studied casualness of cats pretending not to watch a bird.

"You're Isla Vane," he said.

"I'm nobody," I said. "You're drinking my father's coffee."

He winced. Good. I wanted him wincing.

"I knew your father," Quill said. "By reputation. Dr. Aldous Vane, chief surveyor, the best instrument man the office ever employed. When he stopped reporting, I—" He stopped. Recalibrated. A clerk's whole life is recalibration. "When he stopped reporting, I was the one who argued for sending someone."

"You sent a broke boat captain with a generous contract and no questions asked," Petra said from the rail. "That's not sending someone. That's sending bait with a boiler."

"And yet here she is," Quill said quietly. "Alive. Which is more than the last three parties can claim."

The deck went still. Even the river seemed to lean in.

"Three parties?" I said.

Quill set down the cup. His hands weren't steady; I'll grant him that much honesty. Whatever else he was, he hadn't slept in days, and fear had been eating him from the inside like rust eats a hull.

"Two recovery teams and one private inquiry funded by a judge whose brother was on the survey. None reported back. The office has concluded they deserted. All eleven men, plus two recovery crews, deserted into the wilderness simultaneously, leaving their boots, their rifles, and their paybooks." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as old ink. "I wrote the report myself. I used the word 'deserted' nine times. Do you know what happens to a clerk who writes a report that bad? Nothing. Because the men who ordered the report are the men who made the truth impossible."

"Why are you telling us this?" Petra asked. But I noticed he'd come down off the rail. He was standing closer now, arms crossed, listening the way rivermen listen — like the water might change its mind mid-sentence.

"Because I'm the one they sent to clean it up," Quill said. "And I'm not going to."

* * *

Here is what I knew about Mr. Quill before that morning: almost nothing, and all of it bad. Here is what I learned watching him talk with his hands shaking around Marisol's coffee: that he had spent eleven years in the Survey Office believing the work mattered, that maps kept people safe, that ink could be a kind of honor. And that somewhere between Terrapin Landing and this slough, he had opened a file he wasn't supposed to open, and the file had names in it. Towns' names. My father's name.

"The diversions," I said. "The coffer dams above Stonebridge Narrows. The gauge records my father hid."

Quill looked at me the way a man looks at a door he hoped was locked. "You know."

"My father spent four months measuring a river that was being murdered upstream of where anyone was allowed to look. Yes. I know."

"Then you know what's coming. Three towns. Rivergate proper, Millbrook, and the fishing villages below the Narrows. The spring drawdown will strand them, and the summer release will drown what's left. The office calls it 'hydrological optimization.'" He pronounced the words like he was spitting out seeds. "There's a file with the projected casualty figures. They rounded down."

Old Sable made a sound low in his chest, like a boiler about to complain. Fen put a hand on his arm.

"So burn it," Petra said. "Burn the file. Tell the towns yourself."

"They'd call me mad. Or they'd call me a traitor, which is faster." Quill turned to face me fully, and I saw that beneath the pallor and the tremble there was something harder, something that had survived its own collapse. "But a sealed instrument case, recovered from the lost survey, with Dr. Vane's own calibration logs and chain of custody? That's not a madman's tale. That's evidence. Evidence needs a courier it can't discredit. It needs—" he gestured, helplessly, at the battered, beautiful wreck of a boat around us— "this."

"No," I said.

"It hasn't been offered yet," Petra murmured.

"It doesn't need to be. I know how these conversations go." I stood up. My legs had gotten strong that winter in the bluffs, eating snared rabbit and boiled cattail root, and I was glad of it now, glad to be standing over a seated man from the Office of Rivers and Roads. "You want the case. Say it plainly."

"I want you to give me the case," Quill said, "and in exchange, I have authority — real authority, signed — to offer full amnesty. To every soul aboard this vessel. Captain Brightwater's debts, voided. The Wreck's Fortune released from lien, papers clean, free and clear. No charges for obstruction, for flight, for anything. Amnesty for the crew, for the twins, for the engineer, for Mrs. Marisol. Everyone walks away whole." He paused, and then, because he was honest in the way of men who have already lost everything worth lying about: "And protection. There are people downstream who would rather this evidence sank than surfaced. If you keep it, you're carrying a lit fuse through a powder store."

* * *

Amnesty. I watched the word land on each of them like weather.

On Pip and Fen, sixteen years old and already twice arrested for running goods they hadn't known were stolen — I watched them glance at each other, and I watched hope do a small, guilty dance. On Sable, whose bad knee and worse pension had him one winter from the poorhouse. On Marisol, who had a sister in Millbrook, one of the three towns, which meant she had more stake in the evidence than any of us and also more reason to take the deal and live to see the fight won another way.

And on Petra.

Petra Brightwater stood very still, the way he does when the current gets tricky, hands loose at his sides, eyes somewhere in the middle distance where pilots keep their rivers. I thought about the lien on his boat. I thought about the night he'd told me, in the low voice he uses for true things, that the Wreck's Fortune was the only family inheritance he'd ever gotten and the bank owned most of her. I thought about what amnesty would mean: his boat back, whole, his name clean, his crew safe.

All he had to do was stay quiet. All he had to do was let me decide, and whatever I decided, he'd never have to carry it.

That was the cruelest kindness anyone had ever done me.

"Well?" Quill prompted, when the silence had gone on too long. "Captain?"

"She's not my cargo," Petra said. "She's my crew. Crew decides their own heading when it's their neck on the block." He looked at me then, straight on, and his eyes said what his mouth wouldn't: *either way, I'm not handing you to him.* "Take your time, Miss Vane. Tide's not going anywhere."

But that was a pilot's lie and we both knew it. The flood was coming — you could smell it on the water, that green, mineral breath of snowmelt and broken banks. The gorge below us would be suicide by tomorrow night. The gunboat sat in the channel like a cork in a bottle. Every hour we held the case was an hour his family — *our* family, I corrected myself, and my chest hurt doing it — stood in the open with a target painted on them.

If I gave Quill the instruments, we might all live. We might also live to watch the towns die anyway, because files burned, witnesses vanished, and clerks got reassigned. My father had died — or worse than died, whatever it was — so that the numbers would survive. Eleven men. Two recovery parties. A judge's brother. All of it weighed against six lives on a sternwheeler, and one of those lives had laugh lines around his eyes from laughing at his own jokes, and one of them cooked like an angel and hummed while she did it, and two of them were children, practically, and one of them had a knee that sang ballads when it rained.

Staying meant risking them all. Leaving meant betraying everything.

My father's voice, in memory, steady as a level bubble: *An instrument is only honest if the hand holding it refuses to flinch.*

"I have a counteroffer," I said.

Quill blinked. Petra's mouth twitched — the ghost of a grin, quickly strangled.

"The case stays aboard this boat," I said. "With me. With all of us. What you can have, Mr. Quill, is a berth, a shovel of coal, and the chance to testify alongside the evidence instead of against it. You wrote 'deserted' nine times. Come write the correction."

For a long moment Quill just looked at me. Then he looked at the gunboat riding at anchor below, and at the green-smelling water rising past the second snag marker, and at the ragged, armed, unimpressive family surrounding him on this splintered deck — and something in his shoulders came undone, ten years of desk work collapsing all at once.

"The flood," he said hoarsely. "You can't mean to run the gorge in this."

Petra finally smiled, and it was the smile of a man about to do something magnificent and unforgivable.

"Mr. Quill," he said, "welcome aboard. Sable — get us steam. We run tonight."
