## 9. Running the Gorge

The river came up four feet in an afternoon, and by dark it had stopped pretending to be a river at all.

Petra stood on the hurricane deck of the *Wreck's Fortune* and watched the gorge swallow the last of the daylight. The water below was no longer water in any sense he'd grown up trusting. It was brown muscle, rolling in ropes, carrying whole trees sideways like a man carries firewood. A cabin — an actual cabin, door still hanging from one hinge — went past stern-first, spun once in the boil below the point, and vanished. He'd been piloting this stretch since he was nine years old, standing on a flour barrel so his father could see over his head. He knew every rock in it by name. Tonight every one of those names was a lie.

"Sandbars have moved," Fen said beside him, glasses pressed to her eyes. She said it the way you'd report weather. Fen reported everything the way you'd report weather, which Petra had long ago decided was either courage or a defect he'd learned to love.

"I can see that."

"The old middle bar is gone. There's a new one where the lower chute used to be. And there's something big and white just above the Narrows that I'm choosing to believe is foam."

"Don't choose to believe anything," Petra said. "Not tonight."

Below them, around the bend, the gunboat sat at anchor with two lamps burning, patient as a spider. *The Coriander*, though nobody aboard called her that anymore; the crew just said *the government boat* the way you'd say *the fever*. She couldn't come up into the gorge at flood stage — even soldiers weren't paid enough for that — but she didn't need to. She only needed to sit below the Narrows and wait for them to come down, because come down they must. There was no other way to Terrapin Landing. There was no other way anywhere.

Old Sable came up the stairs with the particular slowness of a man who has decided not to say what he's thinking until asked twice. He was seventy if he was a day, built like a boiler, and he'd kept the *Fortune*'s heart beating through two sinkings, one bankruptcy, and a decade of Petra's ownership, which Sable maintained was worse than both sinkings combined.

"She'll take it," Sable said. "The boat. If that's what you're asking."

"I wasn't asking."

"You were standing on your boat looking at her like a man looks at a coffin he's about to build. That's asking." He spat over the rail, ceremonially. "Paddleshaft's true. Boilers are sound. But she draws five feet loaded, Captain, and that river down there is running maybe eight over bars that used to show their teeth at three. You can't thread it. All you can do is pick which rocks you'd rather kiss."

"That's what threading is," Petra said.

Sable looked at him a long moment. "Your daddy would've waited for the drop."

"My daddy also died on this river."

"He did," Sable agreed. "And he'd be the first to tell you the river doesn't care whose child you are. Flood water's honest, though. It don't hate you. It just moves everything you were counting on and dares you to count again."

Down on the main deck, lamplight spilled from the galley, and Marisol's voice carried up through the night air, doing what it always did: making the end of the world sound like a scheduling problem. She was feeding people. It was the last night anybody might get fed, and she was going to see to it that it was a good meal, and Pip was stealing from the pot, and she was hitting him with a wooden spoon in the manner of a woman who loved him and would deny it under oath.

Isla Vane stood at the rail near the instrument case, wrapped in a blanket that had once belonged to Petra's mother. She hadn't let the case out of arm's reach in six days. She wasn't holding it now, but her hand rested on it the way you rest a hand on a sleeping animal you don't entirely trust.

She felt him looking and turned. In the dark her face was mostly eyes.

"You're going tonight," she said. Not a question.

"Flood won't crest till morning. After that we've got maybe a day before the whole gorge becomes one rapid. Tonight the water's high but the channels are still channels. Barely."

"And the gunboat?"

"Is asleep, or pretending to be. Either way, she's anchored below the Narrows with her searchlight cold, because her captain figures nothing alive comes down the gorge at night in flood. Which means when we come down the gorge at night in flood, we'll already be past her before her coffee's poured."

"Or we'll be on the rocks."

"There's that," Petra admitted. "There's generally an or."

* * *

They gathered in the saloon at midnight, all six of them, and Petra told them the truth, because they'd earned truth and because lying to your crew before a run like this is bad luck in a way that has nothing to do with superstition.

"Here's how it sits," he said. "We go down tonight. Chances are better than even we make it. Chances are worse than even the boat makes it. If she grounds on the shoals below the Narrows, we walk out, we carry the instruments, and we're in Terrapin Landing inside two days with everything Isla's father died protecting. If she breaks up in the gorge—" He stopped. Started again. "If she breaks up, same plan, wetter."

Nobody laughed. Then Pip, fifteen years old and incapable of silence longer than a sermon, raised his hand.

"What's the boat worth? Money-wise."

"Pip," Fen said.

"No, I want to know. Because I've been doing sums." Pip counted on his fingers, which he could do faster than most men could count on paper. "Boat's worth maybe eight hundred if she sold clean. Clerk's contract pays four hundred whether we find anything or not. Amnesty offer was open-ended. So the honest question is: what number makes walking away smarter than this?"

Marisol hit him with the spoon.

"It's a fair question!" Pip said, rubbing his arm. "It's the only fair question!"

"It is," Petra said, and the room went quiet. "And here's my answer. I've owned this boat eleven years. She's taken me up every mile of the Rivergate there is to take. And I will tell you the exact figure she's worth tonight." He looked around the saloon, at the twins, at Sable, at Marisol, at the girl clutching a dead man's instruments. "She's worth exactly as much as what she's carrying. And tonight she's carrying the only thing standing between three towns and a government that's decided they're cheaper drowned than heard. So we're not gambling a boat. We're spending one. There's a difference, and the difference is we get to choose."

Sable nodded slowly, like a man confirming a figure. Marisol put the spoon down and started clearing plates, which was her way of voting. Fen said, "Obviously," in a tone that suggested the vote had been held days ago without Petra.

Isla spoke last, from the doorway, quiet.

"My father used to say the river keeps honest books." Her knuckles were white on the case handle. "Everything you take, it bills you. Everything you give, it remembers. I've spent six months hating it for taking him." She lifted the case. "Tonight I'm asking it to take something else instead. Take the boat. Leave us the truth."

"Amen," said Old Sable, who was not a religious man, and rang the engine bell.

* * *

They cast off at one in the morning, black water, black sky, no moon worth mentioning. Petra took the wheel himself. Nobody argued; there was nobody alive who knew the gorge better, and everybody knew it.

The first mile was almost kind. High water buried the small rocks and smoothed the riffles, and the *Fortune* ran light and eager, paddles biting, boilers singing. Petra steered by memory against the current's argument, feeling the wheel speak to him through his palms — the tug of an eddy behind the old fish camp, the shove of the channel bending hard to starboard where the bluff shouldered out. The river had moved its furniture, but the walls of the gorge hadn't moved, and walls were half the navigation.

Then the Narrows.

He heard it before he saw it — a sound like continuous thunder with no lightning in it, the roar of the whole Rivergate squeezing itself through a gap it suddenly hated. The new sandbar Fen had spotted lay square across the upper approach, a long pale shoulder just breaking the surface, and the channel split around it into two chutes, left and right, both of them wrong. Both of them had been wrong yesterday. One of them had to be less wrong tonight.

"Left chute's wider," Fen said at his elbow, glasses useless in the dark, reading the water by its sound and its shine.

"Wider means deeper or means wider," Petra said. "Right chute's where the old channel ran."

"The old channel ran where the old bar was."

"Thank you, Fen."

"Anytime, Captain."

He chose right, because the right chute had the old channel's bones under it, and bones you can trust even when flesh has changed. He rang for full speed ahead and took the *Wreck's Fortune* at the gap with the flood lifting her stern and the spray coming over the texas in sheets, and for ninety seconds there was no boat and no crew and no war, there was only the wheel and the water and a man's whole life reduced to keeping a hull pointed between two noises.

Something struck the hull — a log, a rock, a hand of the river itself — a great grinding blow along the starboard side that threw everyone off their feet, and then they were through, into the wide water below, and the gorge was behind them, and the *Fortune* was still moving.

Still moving. But wrong. He felt it in the wheel before Sable's voice came up the speaking tube, calm as Sunday.

"We're making water, Captain. Starboard bow. I'll keep her ahead as long as she'll listen."

"How long?"

"Long enough," Sable said, "if you get us where we're going."

Below the Narrows, a quarter mile on, the searchlight bloomed — a white eye swinging up the river, finding them, fixing them. A voice came across the water through a speaking trumpet, official and absurd: *Heave to! This is a government vessel!*

"They always say it like that'll work," Marisol said, appearing on the hurricane deck with a lantern, because someone had to witness this properly.

Petra didn't answer the trumpet. He answered with the whistle — three short blasts, the pilot's oldest insult, *get out of my way* — and drove straight down the beam of the searchlight, because the only thing more frightening to a gunboat captain than a smuggler in the dark is a smuggler in the dark who clearly knows something you don't.

What Petra knew was the shoals.

Two miles below the Narrows the river spread wide and went shallow over a gravel field the charts called the Apron, and at normal water no pilot living would cross it. At flood stage, with four extra feet beneath his keel and a hull already opening along the starboard bow, it was exactly where he wanted to be. He ran the *Fortune* up onto the Apron at full speed, ground her along fifty yards of shrieking gravel with the searchlight blazing and the government boat blowing its own whistle in outrage, and brought her to rest in eighteen inches of water, settled, listing, done — with the deep channel and Terrapin Landing a hundred yards beyond, reachable by the small boat, by wading, by anything with legs.

"Everybody off," Petra said. "Instruments first. Then people. Then nothing else."

They went over the side into knee-deep water, Isla clutching the case overhead, the twins hauling the small boat, Marisol carrying — because she refused to be argued with — the galley's coffee pot. Sable came last, pausing at the rail to pat the deck beams the way you'd pat an old horse.

They stood on the far shore, soaked and shivering, and watched the searchlight play over the stranded sternwheeler, and heard the gunboat's crew shouting about salvage and arrest, and none of it mattered. The evidence was on free ground. The story would reach Terrapin Landing by morning.

Petra looked back once. The *Wreck's Fortune* lay heeled over in the shallows with the flood combing through her paddlewheel, and the river that had raised him was already beginning, patiently, to take her apart.

"Well," said Fen. "Now we're poor again."

"Now we're rich," Petra said, and found, to his enormous surprise, that he meant it.

Behind them, downstream, the lights of Terrapin Landing were coming up out of the dark — and with them, already visible on the levee, a crowd, and a newspaperman with a lantern, running.
