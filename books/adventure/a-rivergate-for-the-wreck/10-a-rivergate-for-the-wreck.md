## 10. A Rivergate for the Wreck

**Petra**

The Wreck's Fortune lay on her side in the shallows below Terrapin Landing, one paddle wheel raised to the sky like a hand asking a question nobody wanted to answer. The river had set her down there gently, in the end — almost tenderly, the way an old dog will carry a stick it has no intention of giving back.

Petra stood on the mudbank at first light, coffee in hand, and looked at her.

She was done. He'd known it before the water even finished draining out of her, known it somewhere in his boots when she struck the shoal and he felt her spine give — not with violence but with something closer to relief, as if she'd been waiting all these years for permission to stop. Twenty-two years of debt and patch-iron and Marisol's curses aimed at her boiler, and now here she was, listing in three feet of water with her name half-scraped off the bow.

*Wreck's For—*

The river had taken the *tune* out of her. Petra found that funny in a way he suspected he'd be laughing about properly in a week or two.

"You're up early," said Old Sable, coming down the bank with his toolbag over his shoulder and his bad knee announcing every step. "Or you never went to bed."

"Both."

Sable stopped beside him and considered the boat. He didn't say anything for a long while. That was one of the things Petra loved about the old man — Sable had never once in eleven years filled a silence just because it was there.

"She's not sunk," Sable said finally. "She's beached. There's a difference. A sunk boat is dead. A beached boat is resting."

"Sable, her hull's opened up like a Sunday oyster."

"Mm." Sable sipped his own coffee. "Still. I'd want somebody to say it about me."

**Isla**

Isla Vane had spent four months learning how small a person could make herself. In the bluffs you ate little, moved slow, spoke in whispers if you spoke at all. She had gotten so good at being small that walking into the *Rivergate Herald*'s printing office in Terrapin Landing felt like stepping into a furnace — all that noise, all those people, all that ink-smell and shouting, and her father's instrument case sitting on the editor's desk like a lit lantern in a room full of dry straw.

Editor Cobb was a barrel of a man with spectacles held together by wire and a voice that could carry across a levee. He had read the readings twice. Then he'd read them again, moving his lips, and Isla had watched him go from skeptical to pale to quietly furious in the space of twenty minutes.

"This is dated," he said, tapping the page. "This whole diversion scheme. Dated eight months ago."

"Yes," Isla said.

"And they flooded the gorge last week trying to drown this."

"The flood wasn't trying to do anything," Isla said. "That's what my father always said about the river. It doesn't try. But the men who moved its water knew exactly what they were doing."

Cobb took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve with hands that weren't quite steady, and put them back on.

"Miss Vane," he said, "I have been waiting nine years for paper like this. Do you know what happens tomorrow?"

"I imagine you print it."

"I print it," Cobb agreed, "and then I get sued, threatened, possibly arrested, and very likely run out of town on a rail. And I will enjoy every minute of it." He smiled then, wide and unguarded, and Isla realized she'd been bracing for betrayal so long that kindness caught her off balance worse than any threat could have. "Where's your captain? The one who ran the gorge at night in flood water?"

"He's looking at his boat."

Cobb nodded slowly. "A man should look at his boat," he said. "Once it's done looking back at him."

**Marisol**

Marisol had fed an army that morning, which was to say: six people, two newspapermen, and a clerk who kept hovering near the kitchen door of the boarding house like a cat that had decided, against all evidence, that this household might keep him.

Hollis Fenwick — the clerk, the trembling man, the bait — sat at the end of her table looking like a man who had been wrung out and hung up to dry. He hadn't slept since the gorge. None of them really had, but the others wore their sleeplessness like weather; Hollis wore his like a sentence.

"He hasn't eaten," Marisol told Pip, sliding past with a platter of eggs.

"He's scared of us," Pip said.

"He should be," said Fen, appearing from nowhere the way the twins did. "We're terrifying."

"You're seventeen," Marisol said.

"We're *seasoned,*" both twins said together, which was their way of ending arguments before they started.

Marisol set a plate down in front of Hollis harder than strictly necessary. He flinched. Then he looked up at her with red-rimmed eyes and said, "Mrs. Marisol, I want you to know that when I hired your captain, I didn't know. I swear it. They gave me a contract and a story about a lost survey team, and I was so frightened of losing my position that I didn't ask what the position was for."

Marisol studied him. She was a good judge of men — you had to be, feeding river people — and what she saw was not a villain. Villains ate better.

"Eat," she said. "Then go say it to him, not to me. I only cook the forgiveness. Somebody else has to serve it."

* * *

**Petra**

Hollis came down to the mudbank around noon, picking his way through the reeds with his city shoes ruined beyond salvation. Petra let him stand there a while. Let him look at the boat. A man ought to see what his paperwork cost.

"They're calling it the Brightwater Papers," Hollis said. "In town. Already. The Herald goes out tomorrow morning and every press in the territory will have it copied by week's end. The diversions are halted — there were soldiers at the works this morning taking orders from somebody new. Three towns are going to stay three towns." He swallowed. "Wickford. Lowbridge. Salt Lick. They'll never know how close they came."

"They'll know," Petra said. "Cobb prints names. Cobb always prints names."

"And mine's in it." Hollis laughed — a small, cracked sound. "Not as a villain, somehow. As a fool. Which is worse, I think, and fairer." He turned to face Petra fully. "Captain Brightwater. I owe you more than money, though the money is real — the contract pays out complete, wreck or no wreck. You delivered. More than delivered."

Petra looked at the Wreck's Fortune. Her deck was green with new algae already. A heron was standing on her raised paddle wheel as if it had been built for him personally.

"Hollis," Petra said, "do you know what I was going to do with the contract money?"

"Pay off the boat."

"Pay off the boat." Petra nodded. "One payment left. One. I've been one payment away from owning her outright for six years. Something always came up — ice season, a busted stern bearing, a doctor for Sable's knee. The river kept handing me the money and then taking it back out of my pocket like a pickpocket with a schedule." He shook his head. "And now she's a reef with a name, and I'm paid in full, and I can't decide whether to laugh or sit down in the mud."

"What are you going to do?"

Petra considered it honestly. The grief he'd braced for — the big black wave he'd assumed would come when he finally let himself look straight at her — hadn't arrived. What had arrived instead was something quieter. Gratitude, maybe. The feeling of a debt settled between old friends.

"I think," he said slowly, "she was never really mine. Boats like her belong to the river. We just borrow them, and the rent comes due eventually, and if you're lucky the river takes the boat instead of the crew." He looked at Hollis. "I got lucky. Don't tell her I said that. She'd argue."

**Isla**

They buried her father's instruments properly, which is to say Cobb published them.

Isla stood in the doorway of the Herald office on publication morning and heard her father's numbers read aloud in a stranger's voice, clear and undeniable, marching down the column like soldiers finally given honest orders. *Readings at Station Nine. Gauge heights. Diversion volumes.* His careful, patient handwriting reproduced in ink for the whole valley to see.

He would have hated the fuss, she thought. He would have pushed his spectacles up and said, *Well, the data speaks for itself, Isla, no need for drama.* And then he would have read every copy twice and secretly been pleased.

"You alright?" Petra asked. He'd appeared beside her the way he did — quiet, unhurried, smelling faintly of river and woodsmoke.

"No," Isla said. "Yes. Both. I don't know yet."

"That's the correct answer. The wrong answer is 'fine.' Only liars and drowning men say fine."

She almost smiled. Four months alone in the bluffs had taught her every shape of fear; the last three weeks had taught her, reluctantly, the shapes of everything else. A cook who fed her like she was underweight, which she had been. Twins who had appointed themselves her bodyguards without asking and could not be dismissed. An engineer who had shown her how the boiler worked and listened, actually listened, while she explained what a theodolite did.

And a captain who had stood silent on his own deck and let her choose — knowing either choice might sink him.

"Cobb says there's work," Isla said. "Surveying. Honest surveying, for the towns, mapping the channel so the levees get rebuilt right. He says I'd be wasted anywhere else."

"He's right."

"I'd need a boat."

Petra said nothing. Isla looked at him. He was watching the Wreck's Fortune with an expression she couldn't read — or could, now, better than she'd admit.

"There's a hull downriver," he said at last. "Half-sunk. Sternwheeler. Badly treated by her previous owner, but sound bones, or bones anyway." He glanced sideways at her, and there was something in his face she'd never seen there before — hope, unarmored, standing out in the open where anything could happen to it. "Might take a year to raise her. Two, maybe. Be dirty work. Cold work. Pay nothing for a long time."

"Who'd be fool enough to sign on for that?"

"Oh," Petra said, "I hear there's a crew of the willing lying around somewhere."

* * *

**Petra**

Evening came down gold over Terrapin Landing. On the mudbank below the town, the whole strange family of them gathered — Sable with his toolbag, already planning; Marisol with a basket of food enough for ten; Pip and Fen racing each other along the waterline; Hollis, awkward and earnest, holding papers he kept almost handing to Petra and then putting away again; Isla, standing closest, saying nothing, needing to say nothing.

Out in the shallows the Wreck's Fortune settled another inch, patient as stone. The heron had been joined by a second heron. Petra suspected a conspiracy.

"Fen thinks we should name the new boat after her," Pip said.

"The *new* boat," Sable said. "There is no new boat. There is a drowned hull and a great deal of work."

"Same thing," said Fen.

"It needs a name eventually," Isla said. "Something honest."

Petra looked at the sunset, at the boat, at the people. Somewhere downstream, three towns were lighting lamps that would still be lit next year, and the year after, because a girl had guarded a case and a clerk had grown a spine and a crew had refused, one after another, to jump ship.

"A Rivergate for the Wreck," he said.

They all looked at him.

"For the new boat," he said. "She carried the wreck's fortune as far as she could. The next one carries what's left of it. Us." He shrugged, embarrassed, and drank his coffee to hide it. "It's a working title. The twins will ruin it by morning."

Too late — they were already carving it into a driftwood plank with Sable's knife, arguing over spelling.

Petra stood on the bank a while longer as the others drifted toward the lamplight and the smell of Marisol's supper, and he let himself look at his boat one last time. No mourning in it. Just thanks, plain and simple, the kind you give and don't expect returned.

The river took her the way it took everything — without malice, without apology, keeping only what it truly needed.

It hadn't needed him. It hadn't needed any of them.

That, he decided, was the finest trade he'd ever made.
