## 1. The Debt Collector's Weather

The rain in Harrowgate didn't fall so much as settle, a gray suspension that made the whole town feel like something left soaking too long. Jude Marchetti stood under the awning of the Salt & Anchor with a coffee going cold in one hand and a phone buzzing against their ribs like an insect they'd learned to stop swatting.

The contract had come by courier. Actual paper, actual wax — Elias Voss was that kind of man, the kind who believed a signature meant more when it bled. *Salvage expedition. Coastal site. Ten days. Fee negotiable upward for discretion.* And beneath it, in ink that had dried crooked: *We need someone the sea doesn't argue with.*

Jude had read that line four times. It was flattery shaped like a warning, and it was also, undeniably, true. Ten years of guiding, two rescues written up in journals nobody outside alpine circles read, and then the Kestrel Traverse — the storm, the client who hadn't listened, the inquiry that had stripped Jude's certification like bark off a branch. Blacklisted. The word followed them through every port town on the coast like a dog that knew its owner.

The fee was triple what the work deserved. That was the part that should have scared them.

Jude signed anyway.

* * *

The Voss offices occupied the top floor of a building that had once been a cannery, all exposed brick and glass walls that looked out on a harbor full of boats nobody in this economy could afford. Maren Voss met them at the elevator. She was younger than Jude expected — mid-thirties, close-cropped hair, a handshake like she was testing whether your bones were real.

"Marchetti." She said the name the way you'd read a label on suspect produce. "My father's choice. Not mine."

"I've heard worse recommendations."

"You haven't heard mine yet." She walked, and Jude fell into step because there was nothing else to do. "We're going to a cave. The tide opens it maybe once a decade, and the window is in nine days. My father has been planning this for longer than I've been alive, which he'll tell you himself if you give him ten minutes."

"And you think it's a waste?"

Maren stopped at a glass door and turned. Up close her eyes were the flat gray of the harbor in February. "I think my father calls it salvage," she said, "and I've never once heard him use that word for anything he intended to give back." She pushed the door open. "But you're not here for my opinion. You're here because you're cheap and desperate and good. Two out of three is more than most people manage."

Inside, the room was dominated by a table, and the table was dominated by charts. Bathymetric surveys, tide tables going back sixty years, photographs so old they'd gone sepia at the edges. Jude's eyes caught on one — a black-and-white shot of men in wool and canvas standing at a cave mouth, grinning like they'd found the entrance to the world. A date penciled on the border: *1934.*

"Aldridge," Jude said, before they could stop themselves.

Elias Voss looked up from the far end of the table. He was seventy if he was a day, built like driftwood — hard, bleached, lighter than he appeared. "You know the name."

"My grandfather talked about him. Everyone on this coast did, back then. Aldridge went down with his ship in '34. Whole expedition lost. They never found bodies." Jude kept their voice level. "It's a story people tell children to keep them off the rocks."

"It's a story people tell because it's tidy," Elias said. He rose, joints cracking like rigging, and came around the table with a rolling gait that suggested he'd spent his life on decks. "Tidy stories are how the coast keeps its secrets. We're going to untell one." He tapped the photograph. "The cave opens on the spring low. Nine days. I want you to get my team across the seabed, into that cave, and back out before the water remembers us. Can you do it?"

Jude studied the tide tables instead of answering immediately. Professionalism was mostly theater performed with confidence, and Jude had gotten very good at theater. "The window's narrow. Four hours, maybe five if the pressure system holds. I'll need final say on turnaround times. When I call it, we go. No debate."

"Agreed."

"No debate means no debate. Not from you, not from your daughter, not from whoever else is coming."

Something moved behind Elias's eyes — not anger, but the particular satisfaction of a man watching a purchase behave as advertised. "Done. You'll meet the rest of them at the coast. Dr. Okafor, our geologist. Tomas Reyes, photography." A pause, fractional, deliberate. "And me."

"You're coming yourself. At your age. Across wet rock with a flooding tide behind you."

"I'm coming," Elias said, "because what's down there belongs to me. I intend to be present when I take possession of it."

There it was again — that word, *salvage*, doing work it wasn't built for. Jude filed it away next to everything else about this job that rang slightly wrong: the wax seal, the triple fee, the photograph of an expedition that had died before Elias Voss could possibly have cared about it, laid out on his table like evidence in a trial he'd already won.

* * *

The man was waiting outside Jude's apartment that evening, sitting on the seawall like he owned the tide itself. He wore a coat too good for the weather and no expression at all, and when Jude saw him, the cold coffee feeling came back — the specific nausea of knowing exactly who someone is before they speak.

"Marchetti." He didn't stand. "Corven. We spoke on the phone. Briefly."

"We spoke once. You said you'd call again."

"I'm efficient." Corven patted the seawall beside him. Jude didn't sit. "You took the Voss job today. Congratulations. Triple rate. Must feel like the first good news in a while."

"What do you want, Corven?"

"The same thing I wanted last month. Which is now easier, thanks to you." He finally looked up, and his face was pleasant the way a locked door is pleasant. "You owe my employers forty thousand. Interest has been accruing while you've been unemployed, which is a word I use generously. But now you're employed, and employed people can deliver."

"Deliver what? The fee goes straight to—"

"Not money." Corven stood, unhurried, brushing rain off his coat. "Whatever's in that cave. Voss wants it, which means it's worth something, which means my employers want it more. You'll be inside. You'll have hands on it before anyone else does. Bring me one item — small, portable, valuable. You'll know it when you see it. Do that, and the debt dies. Clean slate. You walk away."

"And if I don't?"

Corven smiled without any teeth involved. "Then when you come home, there won't be a home. There won't be much of anything. My employers are patient about interest and impatient about everything else." He stepped closer, and up close he smelled like clean cotton and nothing human. "You're a guide, Marchetti. You know all about windows. This one closes when the tide comes back in."

He walked off into the settling rain, and Jude stood on the seawall a long time, watching the harbor lights smear and double in the wet dark.

One item. Small, portable, valuable. Steal from the man paying triple rate, or lose everything — and *everything*, Jude knew, was a smaller inventory than it sounded. A room above a bait shop. A grandfather's brass compass in a drawer, engraved with initials Jude had never asked enough questions about. A reputation already dead. What was there left to lose that losing wouldn't almost be a relief?

That was the trap of it. Corven hadn't threatened Jude's life. He'd offered to end it politely, financially, and let Jude do the math.

Jude went upstairs and opened the drawer. The compass sat where it always sat, brass worn bright at the edges from a century of thumbs. J.M., the engraving said. Jude's grandfather had carried it through the war, or so the family story went; had carried it up every mountain and along every coastline until the day he handed it over and said, *It points where it points, kid. Don't argue with it.*

Jude held it now and felt the needle swing, restless, like something waiting.

Nine days. One cave. One item for Corven, one payday for Voss, and somewhere in between, whatever it was that Elias Voss had been hunting for fifty years under the cover of a word like *salvage*.

Outside, the rain kept settling, patient as debt.
