## 2. Low Tide, High Stakes

The tide was wrong before we ever saw the water.

I knew it from the smell first — that low-tide reek of kelp and iodine and old stone arriving hours ahead of schedule, rolling up the cliff road while it was still dark. Then the gulls. They'd moved out past the sandbar in a ragged line, standing on ground that should have been ten feet under, screaming at nothing.

By the time we crested the last headland, the sea had pulled back almost a quarter mile. It lay out there like a held breath, a vast wet plain of rock and weed glinting under a sky the color of dishwater.

"Tell me that's normal," Maren Voss said beside me. She hadn't slept either. I could tell by the way she held her coffee cup — two-handed, like a small animal she was afraid of dropping.

"It's not."

"Then what is it?"

"A once-a-decade thing, if you believe the charts. A never thing, if you believe anyone who's actually stood here." I shouldered my pack. "Which is why we're walking now and not in six hours."

Behind us, the geologist — Dr. Okafor, hungover into some altered state of being — groaned from the back of the Land Rover. "Walking where, exactly? Across the ocean?"

"Across where the ocean was," Elias Voss said. He said it like a man announcing a purchase. Seventy years old, silver-haired, dressed for an expedition the way other men dress for board meetings: everything correct, nothing worn in. He'd been awake since four. I'd seen his tent light at the camp above the beach, burning steady, patient. A man who didn't waste a minute of a decade.

The photographer came last. Name of Ren, hired through an agency in Bergen, quiet as ballast. They carried two camera bodies and never talked about the third bag they kept locked to their wrist. I'd stopped asking questions about clients a long time ago; asking questions is how guides end up blacklisted. But I noticed things. Noticing is free.

We went down the goat track single file, boots skidding on wet turf, and then the seabed opened under us and there was nowhere to go but forward.

* * *

Walking on the ocean floor is a feeling I don't have a clean word for. The rocks were furred with weed that dripped and shone, pools everywhere holding stranded fish and moon jellyfish like dropped lenses. Every few steps something small fled our boots. The silence was enormous — no surf, no wash, just our breathing and the tick of cooling stone and somewhere far off the sea itself, waiting, a gray line on the horizon that did not look like it intended to stay there.

Elias walked fast for his age. Maren matched him stride for stride, though her eyes kept going to that gray line too.

"How long do we have?" she asked me.

"The chart says slack ends at 11:40. My grandfather's notes say the water comes back early here. Always early." I tapped my watch. "So we treat 10:30 as 11:40. Anything we're not back across the flats with by half ten, we leave behind."

"And if someone's still inside at half ten?"

"Then they're swimming," Okafor said darkly, and retched quietly into a tide pool.

Ren said nothing. Ren photographed everything — the weed, the pools, the way Elias checked his own watch against mine. I caught them shooting me twice when they thought I wasn't looking. Once I'd have found that flattering. Now it just felt like inventory.

The cave mouth appeared out of the seabed the way a shipwreck does: gradually, then all at once. First a shadow in the rock face where the cliffs met the old seabed, then a black seam, then an opening tall as a church door, its lip draped in dead barnacles ten feet above anything the living ones could reach.

Nobody spoke for a moment. Even Okafor straightened up.

"That's not tidal," he said finally, professionally despite himself. "That opening hasn't been underwater in — the barnacles alone — that's not a decade cycle. That's centuries."

"Good," Elias said.

It was such a flat word for such a strange place that Maren turned to look at her father. So did I. He was smiling. Not wonder — satisfaction. A man looking at property.

"You've been here before," Maren said.

"I've read about here before." He adjusted his gloves. "There's a difference, Maren, and it's called preparation. Mr. Marchetti — lead on."

* * *

Inside, the cave swallowed our headlamps whole. The floor sloped down smooth as a throat, polished by ten thousand years of tides, and the walls ran with mineral stain — reds and greens and a white so deep it looked deliberate. Our lights made moving shadows of each other. Six people became twelve, became twenty.

About two hundred meters in, the passage forked, and my compass told me which fork was mine.

Except it didn't.

I'd been checking it out of habit — grandfather's brass compass, the one thing I owned that had never let me down, the needle steady as a heartbeat even when everything else in my life spun. Now I raised it and the needle was turning. Slowly. Deliberately. Like a dog circling before lying down.

I stopped walking.

"Problem?" Elias asked, without turning around.

"Just taking a bearing."

The needle swung left, right, left again, then settled — pointing not north but deeper into the cave, into the dark beyond our lamps, as if there were something down there worth finding and the compass had opinions about it.

I put it away. Some instruments you don't argue with in front of clients.

"Marchetti." Elias had stopped. His lamp swung to me, and for a second he was all light and shadow, a silhouette with a voice. "My grandfather funded an expedition to this coast in 1934. Aldridge expedition. Four men went inland from this shore. One came back, and he came back wrong — wouldn't speak, wouldn't eat, died within the year raving about a door in the rock. The official record says the others drowned. The official record is wrong, Mr. Marchetti, because the official record was written by a man who needed it to be." He paused. "What's down here belonged to my family before it belonged to the sea. We're just collecting."

Maren's lamp came around sharply. "Collecting *what*, exactly? Because the contract says salvage assessment. Nobody said anything about 1934."

"The contract also says I'm the client," Elias said pleasantly, "and you're my daughter, and Mr. Marchetti is the man I pay to know the way. Let's let him do that."

They both looked at me. Behind them, Ren had their camera up, filming — I couldn't tell whether the family or the dark.

I looked at the fork. Left passage climbed toward daylight cracks and probably nothing. Right passage went down, toward the cold breath of air moving out of the rock, air that smelled impossibly of old canvas and paraffin and — faintly, absurdly — tobacco.

A cave sealed under the sea for a decade shouldn't breathe smoke.

"Right fork," I heard myself say. "Stay between the markers. Touch nothing until I clear it."

We went down. And the whole way, with every step, I had the feeling I couldn't shake — the one I get sometimes on mountains when the weather's about to turn, except inverted, interior. The feeling of being watched. Not by the team. By the cave. Or by whatever the cave had been keeping safe all these years, patient as the tide, waiting for someone to come collect it.

The needle in my pocket pressed against my hip like a second pulse, still pointing down.

Still pointing home.
