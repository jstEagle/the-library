## 3. What the Sea Kept

The cold inside the cave was different from the cold outside. Outside it had been wind and salt and the raw slap of the North Atlantic; in here it was stillness, a cold that had been waiting, patient as a debt. Maren Voss stood just past the entrance with her headlamp cutting a cone through the dark and felt the temperature of the air change against her face like walking into a room that someone had only just left.

"Watch your footing," Jude Marchetti said from ahead, voice low, almost reverent. "The floor's slick for another twenty meters, then it levels out."

Maren followed the guide's silhouette over wet stone, her father's breathing loud behind her. The geologist — Okonkwo, hungover and gray-faced since dawn — muttered something about karst formations and hypothermia in the same breath. The photographer, Reyes, said nothing at all, which Maren had noticed was their habit. They moved like people who didn't want to be remembered being here.

Then the passage opened, and the light found it.

A camp.

Not wreckage. Not debris scattered by ninety years of tide. A camp — intact, ordered, almost tidy. Two canvas tents slumped against the far wall like sleeping animals, their poles rotted but their fabric somehow whole, darkened with mold but holding shape. Wooden crates stacked in a careful row. A cast-iron stove, its pipe still angled up toward a crack in the ceiling that must have served as a chimney. Tin plates on a flat stone that could only be called a table. A lantern hanging from a piton driven into rock, glass clouded but unbroken.

"Oh my God," Okonkwo whispered. "Oh my God, it's *intact*."

"The cave seals at high tide," Jude said. "Every day, twice a day, for ninety years. Nothing gets in. Nothing gets out."

Maren stepped closer to the nearest crate. Her gloves left prints in the frost-slick wood. Stenciled letters, faded to ghosts: *ALDRIDGE EXPEDITION — 1934*. Beneath that, smaller: *R. ALDRIDGE, LEADER*.

Rowan Aldridge. The name every schoolchild in three countries knew, the way they knew Scott of the Antarctic or Mallory on Everest. The man who led six souls onto this coast in the summer of '34 to chart a sea cave that appeared once a decade, and who never came out. The bodies of two of his team had washed ashore that autumn. Aldridge himself was listed as lost with the cave. There was a memorial plaque in a church two hundred miles south. Maren's grandfather had donated to its restoration.

She turned. "Dad."

Elias Voss stood at the edge of the camp with his hands clasped behind his back, and he was not looking at the tents, or the stove, or the crates. He was looking at the far wall, where a row of objects sat on a stone shelf: brass instruments, a sextant, a coil of rope, leather-bound books lined up spine-out like soldiers.

He was not surprised.

"Dad." She said it harder this time.

He looked at her then, and his face did what it always did — arranged itself into calm, into authority, into the expression he wore across boardroom tables when a number wasn't what he'd expected. "Remarkable," he said. "Better preserved than I'd hoped."

*I'd hoped.* Not than anyone hoped. Than *I* hoped.

"When did you first hope?" Maren asked. Her voice sounded strange in her own ears, thin under all that stone.

"Maren—"

"We're standing in a grave that's been sealed since 1934, and you said *than I'd hoped*. That's not the grammar of a man seeing something for the first time."

Jude Marchetti had gone very still by the stove, headlamp tilted down, pretending to examine the firebox. Reyes had drifted back toward the entrance, camera hanging unused at their chest. Even Okonkwo stopped rummaging. The silence stretched until it hurt.

"I've had an interest in the Aldridge expedition for some years," Elias said finally. "As has a great many people. It's one of mountaineering's great mysteries."

"This isn't mountaineering. This is a sea cave."

"A mystery doesn't care about its category, Maren." He smiled, and she hated how well the smile worked, how it had worked on her for forty years. "Look around you. We are the first living people to stand here since before either of us was born. Can we not simply *be* here?"

But she was already moving, because there was a thing she needed to check, and she needed to check it before her father could stop her, though she couldn't have said why she thought he would stop her. The crates. She pried the nearest lid — the wood gave with a soft sigh, hinges long dead — and inside, packed in straw gone to dust: ration tins, labels legible. *Pemmican. Bovril. Ship's biscuit.* A tin opener. A folding table. A chess set, pieces still mid-game on a board folded open beside them, a white knight advanced into enemy territory, the game abandoned eighty-nine years ago.

Abandoned, not ended. That distinction lodged in her chest like a fishhook.

Nobody plays chess and leaves the board unless they expect to come back.

* * *

The body was in the second tent.

Okonkwo found him — or her, at first none of them could tell — and made a sound Maren had never heard a grown man make, a short punched-out syllable, and stumbled backward into the crate stack. Jude got there first. Maren got there second, and wished she hadn't, and could not look away.

The cold had done what the cold does. The figure lay on a bedroll against the tent wall, curled slightly, hands tucked up near the chin as if asleep in genuine winter. Frost had silvered the sleeping bag and the hair and the edges of the clothing. The face was turned away, mercifully, toward the canvas. On the ground beside the bedroll, placed with intention — placed, Maren understood with a lurch, the way you place flowers — sat a leather journal, closed, weighted down with a smooth round stone.

"He didn't die running," Jude said quietly. "Look at the bedroll. Look at the position. He lay down. He knew what was happening and he lay down and he put his journal where someone would find it."

"How can you know that?" Elias asked.

"Because I've seen people die in the cold," Jude said, without turning around. "You arrange yourself. It's the last thing you get to do."

Maren crouched, keeping her distance, and read the cover of the journal by headlamp. No name on the outside. Inside the front cover, in ink gone brown: *Property of T. Calloway, Second Surveyor, Aldridge Expedition.*

Thomas Calloway. Third man lost. The one whose body was never recovered, whose family had waited, whose granddaughter — Maren remembered now, remembered a newspaper clipping her father had once circled in red pen and left on her desk like a test she was supposed to pass — had died only four years ago, still asking questions.

Her father had circled it. In red pen. Years ago.

She stood up slowly and looked at Elias across the dead man's tent, and the awe she'd been carrying since the entrance — the pure, childlike awe of standing inside a miracle — curdled in her stomach and turned into something with teeth.

"You knew," she said. "You knew there was a body here. You knew about the camp. How long?"

"Maren." Her father's voice dropped into the register he used for closing arguments. "Whatever you think you're constructing right now, dismantle it. We have perhaps five hours of low tide and a site of incalculable historical value. Every hour we spend on your suspicions is an hour stolen from the work."

"The work." She laughed, and the laugh bounced off the stone and came back wrong. "What is the work, Dad? Because I signed off on a salvage survey. Coastal anomaly, possible wreck timbers, insurance valuation. That's what the contract said. Nobody said anything about Rowan Aldridge. Nobody said anything about—" she gestured at the tent, at the frost-silvered dead man, at the chess game frozen mid-move "—*this*."

For a moment — half a second, maybe less — something moved behind her father's eyes. Not guilt. Guilt she could have handled. It was calculation. She watched him weigh her, watch him decide how much truth the moment required, and she watched him arrive at a number.

And then Jude Marchetti, over by the stone shelf, said: "Mr. Voss. You want to explain why your manifest lists archival recovery equipment? Acid-free boxes, document tubes, desiccant packs?" The guide lifted a canvas satchel from the pile of gear they'd hauled across the seabed. "Because I packed this expedition's kit myself, and none of that was on it. This was added after we left the harbor. Someone repacked our bags."

Reyes, by the entrance, went very quiet.

Elias Voss looked at his daughter, at the guide, at the dead man in his tent, and Maren watched her father — the man who had taught her to sail, to read a balance sheet, to hold her nerve — make a decision she had never once seen him make in forty years.

He decided to lie badly.

"There will be time for explanations," he said, "when the work is done."

And behind him, on the stone shelf, in the full glare of four headlamps, the light caught the spines of the leather journals — a dozen of them, maybe more, a complete record, a man's entire hidden life in row — and Maren saw, stenciled small on the end of the shelf itself, half worn away:

*R.A. — FOR WHOEVER THE SEA CHOOSES.*

The sea had chosen them. And her father had known it would.
