## 4. The Second Compass

The body was not the worst of it.

I want to say that plainly, because everything after — the journals, the initials, the price tag Elias Voss hung on my inheritance without ever speaking a number — all of it started with the body, and the body was almost gentle. A man folded into a sleeping bag in the back alcove, mummified by forty years of dry salt air, his face turned toward the wall as if he'd died annoyed at something. His boots were still laced. His coat was buttoned to the throat. Whoever he was, he had prepared for death the way a careful man prepares for weather.

The geologist, Okafor, stood over him with his headlamp shaking and said, "That's Aldridge. That's — that's supposed to be Aldridge."

"Supposed to be," Maren said.

She'd been saying things like that since the cave mouth. Short sentences with the bottom cut out of them. She crouched beside the sleeping bag without touching it and read the name tape stitched inside the collar: *A. ALDRIDGE*. Then she looked at her father, who was standing at the center of the camp with his hands clasped behind his back like a man inspecting a property line, and she waited. He didn't turn around.

Elias Voss had known. I understood it the way you understand a current under calm water — not from seeing it, but from the way everything else bent around it. The decade-old tide tables he'd had me verify twice. The way he'd walked into this chamber without hesitating at any fork, past shelves of gear that should have stopped him cold, straight to the crates stacked against the far wall as if he'd packed them himself.

"Maren," he said mildly, "catalogue the instruments. Mr. Marchetti — the journals, if you please. Top crate."

*If you please.* As if I were staff. Which, I reminded myself, watching the interest compound on my debt with every hour I breathed his air, I was.

The top crate held eleven journals in oilcloth, their spines gone soft as leather gloves. I carried them to the camp table — a folding table, aluminum, absurdly intact — and began where a professional begins: at the beginning.

*14 June 1934. Tide out further than any chart allows. We are through.*

Aldridge wrote like a man talking fast to keep from thinking. The early entries were expedition business: soundings, rations, the usual griping about a sponsor named Voss — and there it was, the family name, eighty years before Elias was born, funding the same hunt. I felt Maren reading over my shoulder and didn't move away. Some discoveries you don't want to make alone.

*19 June. Ransome's lungs are bad. He says the air in here is older than the air outside. Poetic nonsense, but the barometer agrees with him.*

*23 June. Found the gallery. God forgive us for what we've found. Not treasure. Better than treasure. Worse than treasure. The others want to go home and say nothing. I am beginning to think I will never go home at all.*

Then the handwriting changed. Not gradually — between one entry and the next, the letters went narrow and careful, a man writing for a reader who wasn't himself.

*2 July. If you are reading this, then the sea has let you in, which means the sea intends something. Listen. What we found cannot be sold, though men will try, and the trying will kill them. I have watched it begin already. So I have made an arrangement with the water. Tomorrow the tide takes our camp, our boat, and a dead man wearing my coat, and the world will grieve Edmund Aldridge, lost explorer, and stop looking.*

*I will walk inland.*

*The secret stays with me because a secret kept by one man can be buried with him. A secret sold becomes a wound that never closes. Ask the Vosses. They have been bleeding this coast for two generations.*

I set the journal down. My hands were steady, which surprised me, because nothing else was.

Behind us, Okafor made a small sound. The photographer — Reyes, who'd been shooting everything including, I now realized, everyone — had lowered their camera entirely. Even Elias had come closer, drawn despite himself, standing at the table's edge with his fingertips resting on the wood like a man at a graveside he'd paid for.

"He faked his own death," Maren said. "With a body." Her voice was very flat. "Whose body, Father?"

"The expedition's doctor," Elias said quietly. "Ransome. Dying anyway, by his own account. It's in the later entries." He looked at me. "Keep reading, Mr. Marchetti. There's a matter of some importance toward the end."

There was. But I found it sideways first.

Because when I reached for the next journal, my sleeve caught on the crate's edge, and my grandfather's compass swung out of my breast pocket on its cord — the brass case worn bright at the hinge, the initials *J.M.* engraved on the lid in a script I'd traced with my thumb ten thousand times since I was nine years old and a dying man put it in my hand and said, *It always finds what matters. Don't ask it for directions.*

And lying three inches away in the packing straw, face up, was another compass.

Brass. Same size. Same worn-bright hinge. Same engraving, same script, same initials.

*J.M.*

The cave got very quiet. Or I did. Hard to say which.

"That's impossible," Okafor said, uselessly, the way people do.

I picked mine up. I picked the other one up. I held them side by side under my headlamp and they were twins down to the scratch pattern on the cases, down to the maker's mark on the reverse — a small anchor over a crescent, a firm out of Bristol that closed in 1931. Two compasses. One hand had engraved them both. One pair of initials belonged to both.

My grandfather was Jude Marchetti too. Named for him, named after him, the whole chain of it. And somewhere inland, walking away from a staged drowning, a man named Aldridge had carried a compass marked with my name — or given it to someone who did — and that compass had ended up in the pocket of a grandfather who told me it always finds what matters.

"You didn't know," Elias said. It wasn't a question. He was watching me the way he'd watched the tide tables: measuring. "Look at your face, Mr. Marchetti. No. You genuinely didn't know." And something moved across his features that I would spend days understanding — not surprise. *Satisfaction.* The look of a man who has just learned that the thing he wants is even rarer than advertised.

"What is it?" Maren asked. "What does he know?"

"Read the last entry," Elias said.

I read it. My voice came out level, which I count among my life's small miracles.

*Final entry, undated. To whoever carries the other compass: you are family whether you know it or not. I could not go back — going back meant the secret dies wrong, in ledgers and lawsuits, bought and bled for. So I sent my compass ahead instead of myself. If it reached you, then the line held. Keep it moving. Keep it unpriced. The moment you sell what matters, it stops pointing.*

*— E.A., for J.M., always*

Reyes raised the camera. Lowered it again. Even they knew.

"So," Elias said, and his voice had changed — warmer, more reasonable, the voice of a man about to do you a favor. "You understand my position. This site, these documents, that instrument — they represent a claim my family has pursued for ninety years. A legitimate claim. And you, Mr. Marchetti, arrive holding the other half of it in your pocket." He smiled. It was a good smile. It was the smile of a man who mistakes ownership for belonging, and I recognized it, God help me, because I'd been wearing a version of it my whole blacklisted life.

"I'm not selling anything," I said.

"No one said selling." He spread his hands. "Consider it a partnership. Consider it debt relief."

Maren's head came up sharply. "Father."

"The tide turns in six hours," Elias said pleasantly, checking his watch. "We have time to discuss terms."

Outside, faint through the rock, the sea made a sound I didn't like — a long, low pull, like something drawing breath.

* * *

They gave me an hour alone with the journals, which I suspect was Elias's idea of generosity and Maren's idea of a test. I sat at the folding table with the twin compasses side by side in front of me and read every page of Aldridge's inland years, and what I found there broke something open in my chest that I'm still deciding what to do with.

He hadn't vanished to be free. He'd vanished to be *responsible* — moving every few years, leaving money anonymously for Ransome's daughter, for the widow of the radio operator, paying debts no court would ever enforce. The last third of the final journal was a ledger of quiet payments, and against each name, in the margin, a date. Dates of births. Descendants. Children and grandchildren of the men who'd died keeping his secret, tracked across decades by a ghost who never dared knock.

One name appeared more than any other. *Marchetti.* Payments stretching from 1936 to 1961. The year my grandfather died.

He'd known. My grandfather had known exactly who his compass came from, and he'd carried it thirty years without telling me, and the last thing he'd said about it — *don't ask it for directions* — rearranged itself in my memory until it sounded like a confession.

I closed the journal. Both compasses lay on the table, needles swinging, settling, refusing to agree on north. They pointed at each other. They had been pointing at each other since I took them out of the straw, and I told myself it was the iron in the crates, the salt in the stone, anything, everything, except the thing it obviously was.

When I looked up, Elias Voss was standing at the mouth of the alcove, watching me with the patience of a man who has already won and is simply waiting for the paperwork.

"Now," he said, "about your debt."
