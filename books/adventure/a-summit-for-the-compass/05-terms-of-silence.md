## 5. Terms of Silence

Elias Voss had spent sixty-one years learning that everything could be bought, and the last three days discovering the one exception.

He stood at the mouth of the inner chamber, where the lantern light gave out and the dark of the cave began, and he watched Jude Marchetti hold the second compass the way a man holds a hand grenade with the pin already out. Not afraid of it. Afraid of what it would cost to let go.

The boy — no, not a boy, a ruined man of thirty-four with his grandfather's jaw and his grandfather's compass and no idea how much both were worth — turned it over in his gloved fingers. The brass caught the light. Elias had seen that same instrument forty years ago, in a photograph he was never supposed to have, and he had built half his fortune on the certainty that it existed somewhere in the world, waiting.

Now there were two of them. Identical down to the initials stamped into the case. *J.M.* and *J.M.*

"You knew," Jude said. It wasn't a question. It was an accusation wearing a question's clothes, and Elias respected the craftsmanship.

"I suspected," Elias said. "There's a difference. Knowing is for people who can afford to be right."

Behind them, deeper in the chamber, Maren was cataloguing artifacts with the methodical fury she'd inherited from her mother — journals stacked by date, ration tins aligned, the frozen body of Aldridge's navigator covered with a groundsheet as if decency could be retroactive. The geologist sat against the wall nursing a headache and a thermos. The photographer, Halloran, hovered near the entrance with a camera he hadn't raised in an hour, which told Elias more about Halloran than any photograph ever had.

Elias gestured toward the passage leading back toward the sea cave's antechamber. "Walk with me."

"I'm fine here."

"It wasn't a request." He kept his voice level, almost gentle. He'd learned gentleness from his father, who had used it the way other men used crowbars. "Ten minutes. Then you can come back and glare at me in better lighting."

* * *

The antechamber was a cathedral of wet stone, the ceiling lost in black above them, the tide pools along the floor holding their own small skies. The sea was still out — unnaturally far out, the way it had been all day, as if the ocean itself had been paid to look away. Elias had read the tidal charts for this coast for eleven years. He knew what the receding meant. He knew what it meant when the water came back early, too, though he hadn't shared that particular knowledge with the team.

"Your grandfather," Elias said, "was Thomas Marchetti. Deckhand on the *Corvid*, 1934. Aldridge's supply ship."

Jude stopped walking. "That's not public record."

"No. It isn't." Elias picked his way to the edge of the largest tide pool and crouched, the way he crouched before acquisitions, before negotiations, before every moment in his life where someone else held something he wanted. "Aldridge faked his death in the ice fields and walked inland with a secret worth killing for. His crew scattered. Most of them died poor. One of them — yours — came home with a brass compass he had no business owning, and passed it down like a confession."

"And you've been looking for it."

"I've been looking for *him*. Or what he took." Elias straightened. "The consortium I'm competing with — Meridian Salvage, if you want the name — has been hunting this site since the nineties. They have better ships, better lawyers, worse instincts. If they reach these artifacts first, they'll strip the cave in a week and auction the journals in Geneva, and whatever Aldridge carried inland will be a footnote in somebody's hedge fund memo. I funded this expedition to get here first. That's the whole truth. You can check it against anything else I've said on this trip and find it holds."

Jude leaned against a rock formation crusted white with salt. In the lantern light their face did something complicated — the exhaustion of a man doing arithmetic he already knows the answer to.

"So what happens now," Jude said. "You've got your site. You've got your journals. What do you need me for?"

"The compass."

The word dropped between them like a stone into one of the tide pools, rings spreading outward through everything.

"It's mine," Jude said.

"It's evidence." Elias let the word sit. "It ties your family to Aldridge's disappearance. Which means it ties my family to it too, the moment anyone looks closely at who funded this trip. I need it off the table. And I need—" he paused, choosing the phrasing the way he chose investments, "—your discretion. About what we found here. About the condition of the site. About the fact that I've known its coordinates for longer than I admitted in the contract."

"You lied in the contract."

"I *abbreviated* in the contract. There's a difference, and it's measured in millions." Elias took a breath. This was the part he'd rehearsed on the boat, in the dark, while the others slept. "Here is my offer. Your debt — the full amount, the interest, the men who hold it. Gone. Today. I'll buy the paper myself and burn it. You walk out of this cave owing nothing to anyone. Clean slate, Marchetti. A name with nothing written on it. How long has it been since you had one of those?"

He watched it land. He always watched; it was the only honest thing about him, maybe. He watched the offer move through Jude's face like weather — the flinch of hope first, then the shame of hoping, then the cold arithmetic underneath. Elias knew that sequence intimately. He'd performed it himself, once, in a lawyer's office in Bergen, when he was twenty-six and someone offered him his father's company for a signature and a silence.

He'd signed. He'd never regretted it, because regret was a luxury for people who couldn't afford yachts.

But he watched Jude's face do something his own hadn't done. Something slower. Something that looked, horribly, like *thinking about other people*.

"Maren," Jude said. "Does she know? About the coordinates, the timeline, any of it?"

"My daughter is on a need-to-know basis."

"She found a body in there, Elias. She's been cataloguing a dead man's life for six hours. She deserves—"

"What my daughter deserves," Elias said, and heard his voice go flat and hard, the old man's voice, the father's voice, "is a company to inherit that isn't in receivership. She deserves options. She deserves to not spend her forties clawing back what her father gambled away chasing ghosts on a coastline nobody maps." He stopped. Reeled it back in. Gentleness again, or the shape of it. "I'm not asking you to betray her. I'm asking you to keep a secret. There's a difference, and it's measured in families."

Jude was quiet for a long moment. The tide pools breathed around them — that slow exhalation of trapped air that caves make, like the whole structure was sleeping.

"The compass stays with me," Jude said finally. "For now. I want to read the rest of the journals. All of them. Before I decide anything."

"That wasn't the offer."

"I know what the offer was." Jude pushed off the rock and stood straight, and for a moment Elias saw the guide the man must have been before the blacklist, before the debt — someone steady, someone whose judgment people trusted on ice. "You're buying my silence. Fine. But I don't sell things I haven't read. Call it professional habit."

Elias felt the familiar irritation rise — the one he felt whenever an asset displayed preferences — and beneath it, unwelcome, something else. Something that looked almost like respect, and that he filed away to examine never.

"Read them," he said. "Then we talk terms."

"There's one more thing." Jude's eyes went past him, up toward the ceiling of the antechamber, where the water stains ran down the stone like old tears. "The tide. It's coming back early. I've guided this coast for fifteen years and I know what that pull in the air means. We should be moving the artifacts to high ground tonight, not negotiating."

"We have until morning."

"We have less than that, and you know it." Jude met his eyes. "What else didn't you abbreviate?"

And Elias Voss, who had built a life on the distance between what he knew and what he said, opened his mouth to answer —

— and from the inner chamber, Maren's voice cracked across the stone, sharp with alarm:

"Dad! Jude! The water's coming back — it's in the lower gallery *now*."
