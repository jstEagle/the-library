## 6. The Turn

I felt the sea change before anyone else did. That's the thing about a decade of guiding — you don't hear the water coming, you feel it in the floor of you, some animal ledger that never stops counting.

We were still in the main chamber, surrounded by the Aldridge camp, when I stood up too fast and said, "Everybody stop talking."

Maren looked at me like I'd lost my mind. Her father didn't look up at all; he was crouched over the journals like a man at a bank window. The geologist — Okonkwo, hungover and reverent — was photographing ration tins with his phone, and Priya the photographer was shooting frames that had nothing to do with tins.

"Jude," Elias said, not looking up. "We have hours yet. The tide tables—"

"The tide tables are wrong."

That got him. He rose, slow, joints cracking, and I watched the calculation happen behind his eyes — the same calculation I'd seen in every rich man who'd ever hired me, the one where my expertise was weighed against his schedule.

"Explain."

"The water's coming back early. It does that sometimes. Wind shear, pressure drop, a swell from a storm we can't see. The sea doesn't read the almanac, Mr. Voss." I was already moving, checking the cave mouth, and what I saw there made my mouth go dry. The gap we'd come through — a generous twelve feet of air above black water an hour ago — was now maybe six. And shrinking.

I could hear it now. Not a roar. Worse. A long, patient hiss, like something breathing through its teeth.

"Everyone up. Now. We're leaving."

* * *

We almost made it.

That's the sentence that's going to live in my head for the rest of my life, however long that turns out to be. We almost made it. We were fifty yards from the cave mouth when the water came for us — not gradually, not the polite creeping tide of the tables, but a shove, a whole ocean deciding it was done waiting. It came through the entrance as a brown wall, and I got Maren by the collar and Okonkwo by the pack strap and dragged them both up onto a ledge six feet above the passage floor, and the sea took the space where we'd been standing and filled it like it had always been there.

Elias was higher already — of course he was, he'd been angling for the high ground since we entered. Priya scrambled up beside him, camera bag held over her head like an offering.

And then it kept rising.

That's the part I hadn't planned for, the part the tables said was impossible. The water didn't stop at the flood line. It climbed. The chamber we'd camped in — the Aldridge camp, the journals, the body in its sleeping bag, ninety years of perfectly preserved silence — began to drown. I watched the water take the ration tins first, then the table, then the journals Elias had stacked, and he made a sound I'll never forget, a small wet noise, grief and greed in the same breath.

"Higher," I said. "There's a shelf. Move."

We climbed. The cave narrowed above the main chamber into a chimney of rock, and near its top, maybe thirty feet up, a shelf ran back into the dark — dry, barely. We hauled ourselves onto it one at a time, and below us the sea finished its work, rising until the ceiling of the lower chamber was underwater, until the only evidence that the Aldridge expedition had ever existed was a drowned room behind a wall of black water.

The air on the shelf was close and salt and getting worse. Six people — no. Five. Five of us, one ledge, one way in, and the way in was gone.

Okonkwo started to laugh. Then he stopped laughing and started the other thing, the hyperventilating, high-pitched thing, and he said, "The entrance, the entrance was right there, we were fifty yards, you said we had hours, you said—"

"The tables were wrong," I said. "It happens. Breathe."

"You said—"

"Okonkwo. Look at me." I took his face in my hands. His skin was cold and clammy and his eyes had the specific wildness of a man doing arithmetic on his own drowning. "Breathe with me. In. Out. In. You're a geologist. You're standing on rock. Rock is your whole life. Tell me about the rock."

He blinked. "It's — limestone. Karst. The whole headland is karst, it's full of galleries, it's — " His breathing slowed a notch. "It's full of holes. The water's coming up through the system, that's why it's early, the pressure — oh God, the pressure means the whole system is flooding, not just this—"

"Okonkwo."

"Sorry. Sorry." He put his head between his knees.

I turned to the group. Elias was wringing seawater out of his cuffs with the composure of a man who believed money would eventually reach him even here. Maren was pressed against the wall, staring at the black water below us. And Priya was — I want to say this exactly right — Priya was too calm. She'd been too calm since the wave hit. Everyone else had panicked or gone quiet with fear, and she had simply gotten very still, like an animal that hopes stillness will make it invisible.

I'd seen that stillness before. In myself, mostly.

"Priya," I said. "Who hired you?"

The shelf went quiet except for the water, breathing below us.

"Jude," Maren said. "What are you—"

"Her gear's wrong. Her camera bag is dry-bagged to expedition spec, better than ours. Her boots are three weeks old and already broken in on scree, not beach. And she's the only one of us who wasn't scared when the water came, because scared people don't check their escape routes first." I hadn't consciously catalogued any of it until that moment, but it was all true, and I watched it land on her face. "So. Who hired you?"

Priya looked at Elias. That was the answer, really, before she said a word — she looked at Elias, and Elias didn't look back.

"Corrigan Salt," she said. "The consortium. They've been tracking this site for two years. When Voss chartered his expedition, they hired me to ride along and document everything. Location, artifacts, condition of the — of the remains." She swallowed. "I was supposed to transmit coordinates once we confirmed the find."

"And did you?"

"No." Her voice cracked. "The cave kills the signal, and then the water came, and — no. Nothing's been sent."

"Nothing that matters," Elias said.

We all turned to him. He was standing at the back of the shelf with his hands folded, and I realized with a lurch that he wasn't surprised. Not by the tide, not by Priya, not by any of it. He'd hired a spy and let her come anyway, or maybe he'd known exactly what she was from the first dockside handshake, because a spy in your camp is intelligence about your enemy, and Elias Voss collected intelligence the way other men collected stamps.

"You knew," Maren said. It wasn't a question.

"I suspected. I confirmed it in the cave, going through her spare battery case, where she kept her burner phone." He said it the way he said everything, like he was reading terms aloud. "I let her stay because a known channel is more useful than a closed one. And because Corrigan Salt's interest confirms the site's value. We are not lost, children. We are inconvenienced."

"We're entombed," Maren said.

"We are temporarily detained by the sea." He turned to me, and here it was, the look I'd been dreading since the cave mouth closed, the look of a man about to spend his last asset. "Marchetti. The journals. Aldridge's route out. You read them in the chamber. Tell me you remember."

I had read them. I had read them the way you read anything when your debt is due — fast, greedy, looking for the parts that were about you. But one passage had stuck, because it was written in a hand that had gone shaky, by lantern light, by a man who knew the sea was coming back early for him too:

*The lower gallery floods but does not close. Forty paces of water, then air. Rourke swam it on the third of June and came back white as a sheet but grinning. There is a chimney at the far end. It breathes.*

Forty paces of water. Then air. A chimney that breathes.

"There's a way," I said. "Through the flooded lower gallery. It comes out in the karst system — Okonkwo, the chimney Aldridge describes, does that track with the geology?"

Okonkwo lifted his head. I watched him do the thing he was actually good at, the thing the hangover had buried — he looked at the rock around us, really looked, and I saw his breathing steady as the rock gave him back to himself.

"There's airflow," he said slowly. "I've been feeling it. Left side of the shelf, there's a draft, it's been bugging me. Karst chimneys breathe with the tide — when the water rises, the air gets pushed up and out. If there's a shaft connecting to the gallery below..." He trailed off, then nodded, and it was the first honest thing anyone on that shelf had given me all day. "It tracks. It tracks."

"Forty paces underwater in the dark," Maren said flatly. "Through a passage described by a dead man."

"By a man who wasn't dead," I said, and didn't elaborate, because that was a different conversation and we weren't going to have it on a shelf above a drowning room. "We do it in relays. Two swimmers at a time, on a line. I go first with the rope, I tie off at the far side, and you follow the rope. Nobody swims alone. Nobody swims without a light. And nobody—" I looked at each of them in turn, and what I felt in that moment was so far past fear it came out the other side as something almost like calm. "Nobody holds anything back. Not from me. Not anymore. Not one more secret between any of us, or the sea will find it and use it. Priya — everything Corrigan knows. Now. Elias — everything you know. Now. Because in about ten minutes the only equipment that matters is whether we trust each other, and I need to know what I'm diving with."

Silence. The water below us hissed and climbed another inch.

Priya spoke first, low and fast, spilling it like a bag of coins — the consortium's timeline, the survey vessel waiting past the headland, the name of her handler, the fact that they had a second team on standby to hit the site the moment she confirmed it. Elias gave up less, but he gave up something: a letter of intent, a rival's name, a number that made Maren close her eyes.

And then it was my turn, and I stood there on the shelf with the sea rising and everyone looking at me, and I thought about a brass compass in my jacket pocket, twin to one in a drowned chamber below, and a debt with a man's name on it, and a grandfather I'd built a whole self out of.

"Jude?" Maren said.

"Rope's in my pack," I said. "Okonkwo, with me. We're first."

The chimney at the back of the shelf was narrow, and when I put my hand into it I felt the draft — cold, wet, alive, the cave breathing out. Somewhere below it, forty paces of black water waited between us and the sky.

"Everyone tie in," I said. "We're going through the drowned gallery."
