## 7. Through the Drowned Gallery

The water came in the way water always does — politely at first, then all at once.

Maren stood at the mouth of the lower gallery with the lantern held high and watched the sea reclaim the floor of the cave, inch by patient inch, as if it had never agreed to leave. The camp of the Aldridge expedition was already gone under — journals, tins, the frozen man in his leather coat, all of it dissolving into green shadow. Her father had spent eleven years and most of his liquidity getting to this place, and the sea was undoing it in twenty minutes.

"The passage," Jude said. They were crouched at the waterline, sleeves rolled, the twin compasses side by side on a flat rock between them. "It runs under the eastern wall, maybe forty meters, comes up in the outer chamber. Aldridge mapped it. It's our only way out."

"It's flooded."

"It's *flooded now.* In an hour it'll be flooded worse, and the air pocket in the outer chamber will be gone with it." Jude looked up. "So we go now, or we don't go."

The geologist, Okonkwo, made a sound that wasn't quite a word. He'd been holding it together since the tide turned, but barely, and the last of it went when he saw the water slide over the threshold of the lower gallery. "I can't — I don't dive. I'm a *sedimentologist.* I study things that are *dry.*"

"You can hold your breath," Maren said. "Everyone can hold their breath."

"Not for forty meters of black tunnel."

"No," Jude agreed quietly. "Not alone."

Maren looked at the compasses. They'd stopped making sense an hour ago, and they still hadn't started again. Both needles — her father's salvage brass and Jude's heirloom, identical down to the hairline crack in the crystal — lay pointing at each other, trembling, like two magnets arguing. Not north. Never north. Jude had tested them against the cave walls, against the seam of the tide, against each other, and the needles only did one thing: leaned toward their twin.

"That's not how compasses work," Maren said.

"No," Jude said. "It isn't."

* * *

They went in relays. That was Jude's plan, delivered in the flat, unhurried voice of someone who had talked people through worse and lost some of them anyway. The passage was a tube, mostly, wide enough for one, with a rope line Jude fixed at the entrance and paid out hand over hand. Two divers at a time. Follow the line. When your lungs burn, follow it faster. The compasses went first, lashed to Jude's wrist, because — and Jude said this without a trace of doubt, which frightened Maren more than the water did — "they'll find the way if we can't."

Her father refused to go in the first relay. He stood at the edge with his arms crossed, watching the water rise up the wall of the gallery, and said, "I'll go with the equipment. The artifacts go with me or not at all."

"You'll go when it's your turn," Maren said.

"I'm not leaving the site."

"The site is leaving you, Papa."

He didn't answer that. He was already turning back toward the drowned camp, and Maren understood with a cold, clarifying shock that he would drown down there before he'd surface without what he'd come for. Eleven years of planning. A rival consortium circling. A daughter he'd never once asked what *she* wanted out of this. Elias Voss stood in the rising water like a man guarding a house that was already on fire.

"First relay," Jude called. "Okonkwo, with me. Maren — you and the photographer next. Rope's anchored. Count your strokes, don't fight the line, and if you feel the tunnel narrow, exhale a little and slide through. Panic is just breath you're spending wrong."

Okonkwo went white as bone and went anyway. That, Maren thought, was the bravest thing she'd seen all day, and she'd watched a man dive into black water on the word of two haunted compasses.

Jude took his hand, took a breath, and pulled him under.

* * *

The water in the gallery was the temperature of a held grudge. Maren stood chest-deep at the entrance with the photographer, Reyes, who had not stopped shaking since they'd confessed — ten minutes ago, breathless, half-drowned in shame — that they'd been hired by the consortium. That the camera case was full of more than cameras. That they'd been paid to document the site and, if the opportunity arose, to mark it for salvage.

"You could have told us on the beach," Maren said.

"You would have left me on the beach."

"Yes."

Reyes laughed — a small, broken sound — and Maren found she was laughing too, or something adjacent to it, because the alternative was screaming, and screaming spent breath you might need in a flooded tunnel.

The rope twitched. Twice. Jude's signal: *clear, come on.*

"Go," Maren said.

Reyes went. Maren counted — one, two, three — and then the rope went slack in a way that was wrong, a sudden lightness, and the count died in her throat. She dove.

The tunnel was exactly what Jude had promised: black, close, a throat of stone. The rope ran through her fist like a lifeline and then like a lie. Twenty strokes in, her shoulder hit something soft and she understood it was Reyes, tangled, the rope looped twice around their ankle where the line had snagged on a lip of rock, and Reyes was pulling against it in the blind animal way that spends air like a drunk spends wages.

Maren got a hand on the back of their jacket. Reyes thrashed. She got her other hand on their jaw, held their face, and shook them — *stop, stop, stop* — and felt the thrashing falter into something worse: stillness. The terrible cooperation of a body running out of air.

She couldn't see. She couldn't see anything. But her hand found the rope where it snagged, found the loop, and she worked it — one twist, two — while her lungs began to write their own demands, while the dark pressed in like a thumb on the back of her neck. Her father was somewhere behind her in a drowning cave, guarding brass. Okonkwo and Jude were somewhere ahead, in the dark, trusting her to come through. And the person in her hands was a stranger who had sold them all, and she was going to die here for a stranger, and she did it anyway, a third twist, and the loop gave.

She shoved Reyes up and along the rope and followed, and the tunnel — mercifully, impossibly — began to rise, and then the water broke over her head and there was air, foul and real, and hands hauling her onto stone.

Jude's hands. Jude's voice, saying her name like a question.

* * *

They lay on the sloping shelf of the outer chamber, five of them, gasping like landed fish. The air pocket was smaller than it should have been — the tide was still coming, pressing up through cracks, and the ceiling here was maybe a meter of breathing space and shrinking. Through the mouth of the outer chamber, forty meters of churning green, the sea had fully returned. No one said it out loud. Everyone did the arithmetic.

Reyes was coughing seawater in long, racking spasms, curled on their side. Maren knelt beside them with a hand on their back and did not move it.

"You went back," Reyes rasped.

"You were on the rope."

"I sold you out. I was *paid* to—"

"I know what you were paid to do." Maren's voice came out steadier than she felt. "And I know what you did in that tunnel, which was hold the line and not fight me. So we're even, or we're close enough."

Across the shelf, her father surfaced last — hauled through by Jude on the final relay, the artifact case strapped to his back like a drowned man's soul. He came up coughing, furious, alive, and Maren watched him take inventory the way he always did: case first, compasses second, people a distant third.

"The journals," he said. "The body. The site—"

"Is under the sea," Maren said. "Papa. It's under the sea. It was always going to be under the sea. That's the whole nature of the thing you found."

"We had *documentation.* Photographs—"

"Ask Reyes about the photographs."

The silence that followed had a shape to it. Elias looked at Reyes, at the waterlogged camera case, at his daughter's hand still resting on a stranger's back, and Maren watched him arrive at the arithmetic — the consortium, the leak, the trap he'd walked them all into — and watched him, in the same breath, begin to arrange it so that none of it was his fault. She'd seen him do it her whole life. She'd just never been on the receiving end of the rearrangement.

"We're not out yet," Elias said finally. "There will be time for—"

"Papa." Maren stood. Her legs shook and she let them. "I'm done. Whatever this is — the claim, the consortium, the site — I'm out of it. I'm getting these people to the surface. You can come with us or you can stay here with your case, but I'm not choosing the mission anymore. I'm choosing the team."

The word *team* sounded small in the cave, and right. Elias stared at her as if she'd spoken in a language he'd once known. Behind him, Jude said nothing at all, but when Maren glanced over, Jude gave her the smallest nod — the kind of nod you give someone crossing a border.

"The tide's still rising," Jude said into the quiet. "There's a chimney in the roof of this chamber. Aldridge marked it. It goes up through the rock — a blowhole, probably — and if the swell outside isn't driving water down it, it's our way out. It's a climb, not a swim. Rope work. Everyone goes, everyone gets talked up the hard bits. Nobody climbs alone."

Okonkwo laughed — high, unhinged, real. "Nobody climbs alone," he repeated, like a prayer.

Maren looked up. The ceiling was black stone and a thread of gray light, thin as wire, maybe eight meters up, and the light was moving — which meant the sea outside was moving, which meant everything was moving, and the only fixed points left in the world were the people on this shelf.

The twin compasses lay on the rock where Jude had set them down, needles still leaning toward each other across the dark.

"Jude," Maren said quietly. "Which way do they point when there's nothing left to find?"

Jude picked them up, held them a moment, and looked at the thread of light above.

"Up," Jude said. "They'd point up. Come on — nobody climbs alone."
