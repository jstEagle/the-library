## 8. The Debt Comes Due

The light on the surface was wrong.

That was the first thing Jude noticed, hauling themselves out of the flooded gallery's exit onto the shingle beach above the tideline — the light had gone from silver to brass, the afternoon burning down toward evening, and they'd been inside longer than any of them had reckoned. The tide was already climbing back across the seabed they'd crossed that morning, black water sliding over stone with the patience of something that had done this ten thousand times and would do it ten thousand more.

Behind them: Maren, soaked to the collarbone, her arm still around the photographer — Wren, she'd finally given a name in the dark, though whether it was real was anyone's guess. Behind them: the geologist, Okafor, gray-faced and shaking, alive because Jude had counted him through the last relay like a rosary. Behind them: no Elias Voss. Elias had gone through first, alone, before anyone could argue, and had not waited at the far end of the drowned passage. Jude should have read that for what it was.

They read it now.

There were two vehicles parked up on the coast road where no vehicles had been that morning. A gray SUV and a black one, and between them stood four men in expensive rain gear that had never seen honest weather, and one man Jude knew by his silhouette alone, the way you know a scar.

Corben. The debt collector. The man who held every scrap of paper with Jude Marchetti's name on it.

"No," Maren said, beside them. Not a question. She'd understood faster than Jude had. "No, he wouldn't—"

"He would," Jude said. Their voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's. "He already did."

Because there was Elias Voss, standing apart from the others, dry under a borrowed coat, hands in his pockets, watching his daughter climb out of the sea like a man watching cargo come ashore. He wasn't restrained. He wasn't afraid. He'd made a call from somewhere — maybe there'd been signal at the cave mouth, maybe he'd carried a satellite phone the whole way through the dark — and he'd sold the coordinates, the story, and Jude Marchetti all in one transaction, held in escrow against whatever happened down there. Insurance. That was the word Corben would use. Elias Voss had insured himself against his own expedition.

Maren saw it too. Jude watched her see it — watched her father's face do nothing at all as she came up the shingle, and watched that nothing land on her like a blow. She stopped ten feet away. Water ran off her in ropes.

"Dad."

"Maren." Elias's voice was level, reasonable, the voice of boardrooms. "You're safe. Good. That's what matters."

"Is it?" She laughed, and it was an awful sound. "What did you give them?"

"What I had to." He glanced at Jude then, and for one moment something almost human crossed his face — not guilt, exactly. Regret of the practical kind, the kind a man feels about weather. "I'm sorry it went this way, Marchetti. But you were always going to be a liability. You must have known that when you signed."

Jude had known. That was the worst of it. They'd known and signed anyway, because desperation doesn't read contracts, it just signs them.

* * *

Corben came down the shingle with the unhurried gait of a man who had never once been late for anything that mattered. Up close he smelled of coffee and cologne, same as always. He smiled with his mouth.

"Marchetti." He looked Jude over — the salt, the rope burns, the wetsuit half-stripped and tied at the waist. "You look terrible. You look, actually, like money well spent."

"Corben."

"I'm going to be quick, because the tide's coming in and frankly so is my patience." He nodded back toward the black SUV, where two of the rain-gear men had begun, without hurry, to walk Elias Voss toward it. Elias went without resistance, which told Jude everything about how prearranged this all was. "Your friend Mr. Voss made certain arrangements with us. Site location, survey data, the full provenance of the Aldridge material. In exchange for certain considerations regarding his own... exposure. Standard stuff. But here's my problem, Marchetti." He spread his hands. "Voss can sell me what he knows. What he knows isn't everything. There are things down in that cave only you've touched. Only you've read. And there's—" his eyes dropped, deliberate, to the waterproof bag slung across Jude's chest, where the twin compasses rode together like two halves of a sentence, "—that."

The wind came off the water. Somewhere below them, the sea was closing over the cave mouth, patient as arithmetic.

"You want the compasses," Jude said.

"I want everything. The compasses, the journals, your testimony, your signature on a statement saying you found nothing personal in that site worth keeping. And in exchange—" Corben let the pause stretch, savoring it, a man who believed silence was a currency too, "—I burn every document I hold on Jude Marchetti. Every debt. Every judgment. You walk out of this a free person. Clean slate. New name in the industry, if you want it. Hell, I'll even make a couple of calls. You'd be surprised how fast a blacklist evaporates when the right people stop holding the pen."

Behind Jude, Maren said quietly: "Don't."

And behind her, Okafor said nothing at all, because Okafor was a geologist and had learned long ago which forces in a room were tectonic and which were merely loud.

Wren said nothing either. But Wren was looking at Jude with an expression that wasn't pleading. It was, of all things, trust — raw, unearned, absurd trust, from a person who'd spent the whole expedition lying about who paid them. As if the dive through the drowned gallery had burned away everyone's masks except the ones they'd chosen to keep.

Jude looked at the bag. Inside it, the two compasses lay against each other, brass on brass. One had been their grandfather's. One had belonged to a man who'd faked his death in 1934 and walked inland carrying a secret that four other men died keeping — a man whose journals, in the end, weren't about treasure at all. They were about the family he'd left behind. The descendants he'd watched from a distance. The name he'd protected by erasing himself.

*The pages naming the descendants.* Those were in the bag too. Jude had thought about them all the way up through the flooded dark, turning them over like stones.

"Here's what I don't understand," Jude said, buying seconds, watching Corben's face. "Elias sold me out. Sold the site. Why do you need me at all?"

"Insurance cuts both ways." Corben shrugged. "Voss is our problem now, not yours. But consortiums have long memories and shorter tempers, and if there's anything left unclaimed down there — anything you're sitting on — I'd rather have it today than spend three years litigating with a washed-out guide who has nothing left to lose." He smiled again. "Nothing left to lose people are unpredictable, Marchetti. I prefer predictable. Hence the offer."

"And if I say no?"

Corben's smile didn't move but something behind it did, the way ice shifts on a lake. "Then we take the bag, we take your testimony, and we take our time about the rest. And Ms. Voss and her friends become witnesses to a salvage dispute that got messy. Accidents happen on this coast. Ask any insurer."

The tide slid another yard up the shingle. Cold water found Jude's boots.

* * *

And here was the thing — the clarity of it, arriving all at once, cold and clean as the water in the drowned gallery:

Jude Marchetti had been becoming this person for years. The guide who took the contract without asking why the Voss family wanted a cave nobody had mapped. The debtor who listened to Corben's terms on the dock and felt relief instead of revulsion. The person who, standing in that cave with Aldridge's journals open in their hands, had thought — honestly, briefly, shamefully — *what's this worth?*

Not *what does this mean?* Not *who did this belong to?* Just: what's the number. What clears the ledger. They had been learning to price everything, because everything they loved had been priced and repossessed, and the lesson poverty teaches best is that value is just whatever someone will pay.

Elias Voss had looked at a miracle in a cave and seen an asset. Corben looked at the same miracle and saw inventory. And Jude had stood between them thinking the choice was between two buyers.

It wasn't. It never had been.

The compasses pointed at each other in the dark of the bag. Jude had felt it down there — the needles swinging off north, off anything, finding each other through forty feet of flooded rock like two people calling names into fog. Aldridge had built or bought or been given a twin, and carried one, and sent the other home to the family he couldn't face. Two instruments that didn't point north. They pointed at each other. That was the whole secret. That was what the old man had understood and Elias Voss never would and Corben couldn't buy with a warehouse full of ledgers: some things aren't meant to find true north. They're meant to find each other.

Jude's grandfather had carried one of these across three continents and never once sold it, though there had been years — Jude knew the stories — when selling it would have fed the family for a winter.

"Okay," Jude said.

Corben's eyebrows rose, pleased. Maren made a small sound beside them, and Jude raised one hand — *wait* — without taking their eyes off the collector.

"Here's my counteroffer," Jude said, and unslung the bag, and held it out — but didn't let go. "You take the compasses. Both of them. Brass, 1930s, provable, the crown jewels of whatever your consortium wants to put in a glass case. Take them, and in exchange you don't just burn my paper. You walk away from the Voss family. All of them. Maren, Okafor, Wren — no statements, no witnesses, no accidents on this coast. Their names never appear in anything you file. And you leave the journals in my possession, because as far as the world needs to know, the Aldridge site contained rusted tins and a dead man's boots and nothing else worth reading."

Corben's eyes narrowed. "The journals are the provenance. Without them the compasses are just—"

"Just antiques. Yes." Jude tightened their grip on the bag. "Which means you'll have to decide, right now, in front of everyone, whether you want the story or the objects. Because I promise you, Corben — I have been underwater today, twice, and I am not afraid of you anymore, and if you reach for this bag I will put both compasses into that sea before you finish your second step, and you can explain to your consortium how you lost the entire discovery to the tide and a disgraced guide."

The wind moved. Below them the water climbed, covering the last of the morning's footprints, erasing the path to the cave mouth as if it had never existed.

Corben looked at the bag. Looked at the sea. Did the arithmetic — Jude could see him doing it, the same cold ledger Elias lived inside — and for the first time since the dock, since the threats, since the whole long machinery of debt had ground into Jude's life, Corben blinked first.

"The journals stay with you," he said slowly. "Unread. And the Voss name stays out of it. And you disappear, Marchetti. No interviews, no book deals, no deathbed confessions. You were never here."

"I was never anywhere," Jude agreed. "I'm very good at that. You taught me."

Corben signaled. The rain-gear men released Elias — pushed him, actually, stumbling forward onto the shingle, a commodity returned damaged — and the black SUV door slammed, and engines started, and just like that it was over, the whole vast weight of it lifting off Jude's chest so fast they nearly staggered.

But at the SUV's window, Corben paused. Rolled the glass down. His face had gone thoughtful, almost gentle, which from Corben was the most frightening expression yet.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the consortium already has photographs. Divers, three years ago, before the tide cycles changed. They got the outer gallery. Some of the camp." He let that sit. "What you're protecting in those journals — it may already be too late to protect it. Think about that on your way home."

The window rose. The vehicles pulled away up the coast road, taillights shrinking through the sea haze, and the sound of them faded until there was nothing left but wind, water, and five people standing on a beach that would not exist again for ten years.

Elias Voss picked himself up, straightened his borrowed coat, and opened his mouth —

"Don't," Maren said.

He didn't. He looked at his daughter, and at Jude, and at the bag on Jude's shoulder that now held everything he'd crossed an ocean to own — and understood, finally, visibly, that he had lost it not to the consortium or the sea but to a broke guide's refusal to think the way he thought. He turned and walked alone up the beach toward the gray SUV, and no one followed him, and no one called after him, and that silence was the loudest thing Jude had ever heard.

Jude's hands were shaking now that it was safe to shake. They opened the bag. The two compasses lay together, needles trembling, pointing at each other across an inch of salt-damp air.

One of them was going to cost everything. Jude had just promised it away.

The other — the grandfather's, the inheritance, the one thing in the world that had always pointed somewhere true — sat warm against their palm, and Jude closed their fingers over it, and looked east toward the inland hills where a man named Aldridge had once walked away from his own grave, and understood that the hardest part hadn't even started yet.

The journals had to burn before morning.
