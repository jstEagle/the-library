## 9. What Jude Marchetti Carries

The collector's name was Rennick, and he had the patience of a man who'd never once been cold.

He stood on the shingle above the tideline with his hands in his coat pockets, flanked by two of the consortium's people — a woman with a clipboard and a man who kept looking at the cave mouth like it owed him money. Behind them, Elias Voss sat in the back of a grey van, wrists zip-tied, watching Jude through the glass with an expression that wasn't apology. It was arithmetic. *You were always the cheapest thing I could spend.*

Jude climbed the last of the rocks with the pack heavy on one shoulder and Maren a step behind, soaked to the ribs, salt-crusted, alive. The geologist was already in the second van, wrapped in foil blankets, talking too fast to no one. The photographer sat apart on a boulder, staring at their hands.

"Marchetti." Rennick smiled like a door opening onto nothing. "Heard you got them out. Impressive. The flooded gallery especially. My colleagues are very interested in how you did it."

"The passage is underwater now," Jude said. "It'll be underwater for ten years."

"Then we're all lucky you came up when you did." He extended a hand, palm up, casual as asking for a light. "The compass. And the journals. And everything the team saw down there — locations, depths, the body, the lot. In exchange, your debt dies today. All of it. You walk away clean."

Clean. Jude turned the word over. It had a shape, and the shape was a room with no one in it.

* * *

Here is what Jude Marchetti knew about their grandfather: almost nothing, and all of it secondhand. A man who died before Jude was born, who left a brass compass in a shoebox with a note in pencil — *For when you're lost, and for when you're not* — and whose only other legacy was a story Jude's mother told exactly twice: that he'd been saved once, on a mountain, by a stranger who then walked out of his life forever and was never heard from again.

Aldridge. It had taken four days inside that cave to make the connection, and thirty seconds holding the twin compass to feel it land like a dropped anchor. Same maker. Same wear pattern on the hinge. Same initials scratched into the case — *E.A.* — and beneath them, smaller, newer, in a different hand: *J.M.*

Two compasses. One given, one kept. A debt across ninety years that nobody had ever called in until now.

And here was what the journals said, in Aldridge's tight, slanted script: that he hadn't died on the ice in '34. That he'd staged the crevasse fall himself because men were hunting what he'd found, and the only way to protect it was to become a dead man. That three of his team had stayed behind to sell the lie with their lives, and he had carried their names inland like stones. That he'd lived out his years under another name, sending money in plain envelopes to the families of the dead — including, in the final pages, a single line that had stopped Jude's breath:

*The boy on the ridge owes me nothing. I owe him everything. Let him keep the compass and think well of me.*

Grandfather. The stranger on the mountain. The circle closing so quietly Jude could have missed it entirely.

That was what Rennick wanted. Not the compass — the proof. The journals were a confession, an asset, leverage against every family that had taken Aldridge's envelopes and built lives on them. The consortium didn't want treasure. They wanted witnesses.

* * *

"You're hesitating," Rennick said. "I understand. Sentiment. But let me be clear about your position." He nodded at the van, at Elias. "Your employer sold you to us this morning. Called it insurance. So whatever loyalty you think you owe these people — they've already priced you. The only question left is what you price yourself at."

Maren made a sound beside him, half-step forward, and Jude put a hand on her arm without looking away from Rennick.

"The compass," Jude said. "You get the compass."

Rennick's smile widened. "And the journals."

"The journals burned in the cave."

"They did not. Our diver's notes say otherwise. Waterproof tins, Marchetti. We've done this before."

The tide was coming back in. Jude could hear it behind them, filling the cave mouth with that low swallowed roar, erasing the site for another decade. Somewhere under that water was a camp frozen in 1934, a body that had waited ninety years to be found, and a story that belonged — not to the consortium, not to Elias Voss, not even to history — but to a handful of families who had never known why the envelopes kept coming.

Jude shrugged the pack off and knelt with it between them, unhurried, the way you move when the ground is unstable. Took out the compass first. Held it a moment longer than necessary, thumb over the initials, feeling the needle swing inside toward something that wasn't north and never had been.

"For when you're lost," Jude said quietly, "and for when you're not."

Then set it in Rennick's palm.

Rennick weighed it, satisfied, and held out his other hand. "The tins."

"There are no tins." Jude stood. "There's ash. Ask your divers — the lower gallery flooded before we came up. Everything paper went into the brazier Aldridge left, and the brazier went into the pool. You can send men down in ten years if you like. You'll find rust."

It was a gamble and they both knew it. Rennick studied Jude the way he'd study a contract looking for the clause written small. Behind him, the woman with the clipboard said something low, and the man who kept looking at the cave shook his head slightly — *nothing to salvage, not worth the tide.*

"Everything?" Rennick said.

"Almost."

* * *

Because there was one thing Jude had pulled from the fire, and it was burning a hole in the inside pocket now, against the heart, where a compass used to sit.

Three pages. Torn out before the rest went into the flames. The pages with the names — the families Aldridge had paid, the children he'd watched from a distance, the last line about the boy on the ridge. Jude had read them by headlamp while the others slept off the dive, and had understood, finally, what Aldridge had actually faked his death to protect. Not a discovery. People. Specific, ordinary, living people who deserved to stay unowned.

The consortium couldn't leverage what it couldn't read. So Jude had fed ninety years of confession to the fire page by page, and kept only the names, because some debts aren't meant to be collected. They're meant to be delivered.

"Then we're done," Rennick said at last, and something in his face suggested he knew he'd been beaten and couldn't prove it or even say how. He tucked the compass away. "Debt's cleared. Don't come near our interests again, Marchetti. You're worth more to everyone as a rumor."

The vans pulled away up the coast road. Elias looked back once through the rear window — not at Jude, but at the sea, at the site he'd spent years and other people's money trying to own — and then the road bent and took him.

* * *

They walked back along the shingle in the long gold light, five people who shouldn't have survived the morning, carrying nothing worth stealing.

Maren fell into step. "You gave it up. Your grandfather's compass."

"I traded a thing for the people it pointed at." Jude touched the pocket where the folded pages rode. "I think that's what it was for."

"And the journals?"

"Ash." Jude looked out at the water closing over the cave mouth, smooth and indifferent, keeping its secret for another decade. "Some stories should only be handed down, not sold."

Ahead, the photographer laughed at something the geologist said — a real laugh, cracked and human — and Maren slowed, waiting, until Jude caught up, and they walked on together.

There were still three pages in Jude's pocket with names on them, and one name at the bottom of the last page that wasn't Aldridge's at all.

It was time to go find out who else was still alive.
