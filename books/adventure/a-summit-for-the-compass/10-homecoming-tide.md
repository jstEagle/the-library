## 10. Homecoming Tide

The envelope had no return address because it didn't need one. I'd written the name on the front in block capitals, the way you'd write anything you were afraid to look at too closely: **E. ALDRIDGE-MOSS, 14 HARROW LANE**.

The house sat at the end of a street where every garden was trimmed into apology. Roses, hedges, a birdbath scrubbed white. The kind of place where people kept their histories in albums instead of caves.

I stood at the gate for a long time. Long enough that a woman two doors down stopped pretending to water her hydrangeas and just watched me. I couldn't blame her. I looked like what I was — a man with salt still in his jacket seams and a week of not-sleeping behind his eyes, holding an envelope that weighed nothing and everything.

The pages inside were the ones I'd pulled from the fire. Aldridge's last entries, the ones naming the daughter he'd left behind in Inverness, the granddaughter he'd watched from a distance through two wars and a wedding he hadn't been invited to. The handwriting got shakier toward the end, but the sentences got clearer, like the man had finally burned off everything that wasn't true. *Tell her I chose this,* he'd written. *And tell her choosing isn't the same as leaving.*

I hadn't told her. Not yet. Maybe never. Some letters are meant to be found, not delivered — and some are meant to be left on a doorstep like a stone you set down so you can walk away lighter.

I slid the envelope through the mail slot. It landed on the parquet floor of the hall with a sound like a single handclap, and before anyone could come to the door, I turned and went.

Halfway down the street I heard the front door open. Footsteps. A voice — older, careful: "Hello? Who's there?"

I didn't stop. But I did slow, just for a second, long enough to feel it land somewhere under my ribs: *someone is reading those words tonight.* Someone who'd spent seventy years thinking their grandfather died a hero on a rock in the North Atlantic was about to learn he'd lived forty more years alone on purpose, loving them from a distance he thought was kindness.

Whether that broke her heart or mended it, I'd never know. That was the trade. That was always the trade.

* * *

The tide came back in on schedule that evening, which felt like a small mercy from a sea that had shown so little all month.

I drove north out of the city with the windows down, past the salvage yards and the fish processing plants, past the turnoff for Harrow Lane, until the road narrowed and the hedges gave way to gorse and the air went briny and honest. The Voss property sat on a headland that Elias had bought decades ago and rarely visited — Maren told me later it was the only thing he'd ever owned that he hadn't tried to turn into something else. Just land. Just sea. Just a view.

There were four cars in the gravel drive when I arrived. Mine made five.

Maren met me at the door. She'd cut her hair since the cave — shorter, practical, like she'd decided something about herself and executed it without ceremony. She looked at me the way she'd looked at me across that flooded gallery, when we were passing the photographer's weight between us in the dark: measuring whether I'd hold.

"You came," she said.

"You asked."

"I half expected you to send regrets and disappear." She stepped aside to let me in. "Georg says you're a ghost now. Professionally speaking."

"Georg's hungover."

"Georg is *always* hungover." She almost smiled. "But he's right."

Inside, the house smelled of bread and woodsmoke and something with rosemary in it. The photographer — Priya, though I'd only learned her real name after the consortium men were gone, after she'd sat on the headland rocks and told us the whole shape of her betrayal with her hands shaking around a thermos — was setting a table by the big window. Georg was already seated, hair damp, looking human for maybe the first time since I'd met him. There was a fifth chair. Empty. Facing the sea.

Nobody said Elias's name. Nobody needed to. The empty chair said it for us — said it the way the cave had said it, the way the compasses had said it, pointing not north but toward each other, as if they'd known something about belonging that none of us had figured out yet.

Elias was alive. That was the strange part. The consortium had taken him in exchange for dropping the claims, and whatever that meant — negotiations, leverage, a slow unraveling of the Voss name in rooms Jude Marchetti would never see — it wasn't a grave. Maren had hired lawyers. Maren would keep hiring lawyers. But she'd also stopped pretending her father was a man who could be rescued from himself. You don't pull someone out of water they've decided is dry land.

"Sit," Maren said. "Before Georg eats your portion."

"I would never," Georg said, eating my portion.

We ate. Bread, fish, roasted things, a bottle of wine Georg produced from somewhere with the sheepishness of a man returning stolen goods. Priya told a story about the dive — about the moment her regulator snagged in the flooded gallery and the world went green and silent, and Maren's hands found her harness in the dark like they'd done it a thousand times. She told it lightly, the way you tell a story once you've survived it, and Maren let her, and nobody mentioned that three weeks ago Priya had been reporting our position to the people trying to bury us.

That was the thing about nearly drowning together. It doesn't erase what came before. It just makes what comes after simpler.

Afterward, we carried our glasses out onto the terrace. The sun was going down over the water in that slow, extravagant way it does in late summer, laying gold across the swell, and below the headland — invisible, unmarked, exactly where the charts said nothing was — the sea was closing over the cave mouth for another decade.

I felt it happen more than saw it. A shift in the light. A change in the sound of the water against the rocks. Somewhere under all that blue, the camp was going dark again: the ration tins, the journals' ashes' twin, the cot where a man had slept for years beside a secret he'd chosen over a family. The sea taking it back the way it takes everything, patiently, without judgment.

"My grandfather used to bring me here," Maren said quietly. We were standing apart from the others, at the rail. "Before the blacklist, before any of it. He'd point at that stretch of water and say, *that's where the world keeps its receipts.*" She shook her head. "I thought it was a joke. He was serious. He'd known about the site for eleven years, Jude. Eleven. And he never once said a word to me."

"He thought owning it was the same as understanding it."

"He thought owning it was the same as *deserving* it." She turned her glass in her fingers. "You understand it. You walked in there with a compass your grandfather gave you and you understood it in ten minutes. Took him a decade and he still missed the whole point."

"What's the point?"

She looked at me. "That some things aren't treasure. They're instructions."

* * *

I stayed at the headland that night, in a guest room with a bed too soft for someone who'd spent the last year sleeping in truck cabs. I lay awake listening to the tide and doing the arithmetic I'd been avoiding for weeks.

The debt was gone. Paid in full, wiped clean, the collector's number deleted from my phone by a man who'd shaken my hand on the headland with genuine respect and said, *you're either the dumbest guide on this coast or the only honest one, and honestly I can't tell.* The consortium had the compass now — my grandfather's compass, Aldridge's compass, the twin brass hearts of the whole rotten beautiful story — and in exchange they had nothing else. No site coordinates that mattered. No journals worth reading. No claim the lawyers could make stick. They had a paperweight and a grudge.

My name was still blacklisted. That hadn't changed. The mountaineering council hadn't returned my calls; the outfitters still routed around me; the word *Marchetti* still opened certain doors and closed most others. I'd traded vindication for four lives and a dead man's privacy, and the ledger didn't balance anywhere except in the one place ledgers don't reach.

And the strangest part — the part I kept turning over like a stone — was that my chest didn't feel empty where the compass used to be. It felt *full*. Like the absence had a shape, and the shape was everything it had ever pointed at: my grandfather's kitchen table, the smell of pipe smoke and waxed canvas, the day he put it in my hand and said *it doesn't point north, boy, it points home, you just have to be standing in the right place.*

I'd spent thirty years standing in the wrong place. Standing in debt, in disgrace, in the belief that clearing my name was the same as earning my life back. The cave had corrected my bearing. It cost me the instrument. Fine. Instruments are replaceable. Bearings aren't.

Toward morning I heard movement downstairs and found Maren on the terrace in a blanket, watching the water go silver.

"Couldn't sleep either?" she said.

"I sleep fine. I just like watching the tide come in."

"Liar." She shifted to make room on the bench. We sat. For a while neither of us said anything, because there was a version of this morning where we said everything — where I told her what I'd left on the doorstep in Harrow Lane, where she told me what the lawyers had found in her father's accounts, where we mapped the whole wreckage between us like survivors walking a beach after a storm.

Instead she said: "I'm putting together a small outfit. Coastal work. Survey, rescue, the odd expedition nobody respectable will touch." She glanced sideways at me. "I need a lead guide. The name on the paperwork will be Voss, because nobody will hire yours. But everyone on the boat will know whose judgment we're following."

"The council will come after you for it."

"The council can swim."

I laughed — actually laughed, first time in longer than I could remember — and the sound went out over the water and didn't come back, and that felt right too. Some things you give to the sea and it keeps them.

Below us, the swell rolled in clean and endless. Somewhere beneath it, a cave mouth sealed itself shut for another ten years, keeping its dead man's peace, keeping its instructions. Somewhere inland, an old woman was reading words her grandfather had written for her across a distance of seventy years, and I hoped — I genuinely hoped — that they read like love instead of abandonment, because that's what they were, in the end. Both things. The way leaving and staying are both things. The way a compass that doesn't point north can still get you home.

"I'll do it," I said. "On one condition."

"Name it."

"When the tide drops again — ten years from now — we don't go back."

Maren considered that. Out on the water, the light was coming up the color of hammered tin, and the sea was doing what the sea does, which is forget, so that we don't have to.

"Deal," she said.

And we stayed on the bench, two people with ruined names and full hands, watching the tide close over everything — not as an ending, but as the oldest promise there is: *the water remembers, so you don't have to. Go home.*
