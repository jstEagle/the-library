## 1. The Job That Was Sworn Off

There are three rules I run my boat by. Never haul anything that breathes fire, never take a charter from a man who wears his watch on the inside of his wrist, and never — under any circumstances, in any weather, for any sum — go back up the Vell.

I'd kept all three for ten years. It was a personal record, and like most personal records, it was broken by someone who knew exactly where to hit.

Cass Merrow found me at the Saltline docks on a morning the fog was so thick the gulls sounded like they were laughing at a joke I hadn't been told. I was doing inventory, which is to say I was standing in the hold of the *Marrow's Mercy* — yes, I know, the name predates me and no, I don't want to talk about it — counting crates of dried fish that were going to taste like regret by the time they reached Port Anselm.

"Still naming boats after your feelings, Sandoval?"

I didn't turn around. You don't give Cass Merrow the satisfaction of a flinch; it's like feeding a gull once. You'll never be rid of them.

"That's not her name," I said. "And you're not welcome on my deck."

"Your deck is a public dock, and I'm holding a contract." She let the paper do the talking, the way she always did. Cass had a voice like a door closing in a warm house — you heard it from outside and you wanted in, and you hated yourself for wanting it. "Ten pages. You can read. I remember that about you."

I remember that about you. As if we'd been colleagues. As if the last time we'd stood face to face — the inquiry, eleven years back, both of us under oath about a woman neither of us could claim — had been a seminar.

I turned around. Fog, gray water, and Cass in a coat too good for the Saltline, watch strapped inside her wrist like a smuggler's, like someone who'd learned early that the world takes things off you if it can see them.

"I don't do river work," I said. "You know why."

"I know what you *say*." She stepped closer, and the fog made it feel conspiratorial, which was her whole method. People thought Cass Merrow's weapon was money, and she let them, because it was cheaper than them knowing the truth: her weapon was that she was usually right. "The Vell's closing. Calving season starts in six weeks, maybe five if the summer runs hot. After that, the upper valley's sealed under ice and water until next year — and next year, Sandoval, there may not be an *upper valley*. The glacier's coming down faster than anyone charted. Your mother's last camp is up there right now, with her final season's records in it, and in six weeks it's at the bottom of a lake that didn't exist when she died."

"Then it can stay there."

"Ten thousand down. Forty on delivery."

The number did what numbers do. I hated it for that.

"Cass." I made my voice flat as the harbor. "I've spent ten years building a life that doesn't have the Vell in it. You're not going to stand on my dock and sell me back the worst night of my childhood with a payment schedule."

"No," she agreed, pleasantly. "I'm going to stand on your dock and tell you that I've already hired your crew."

The fog got very loud after that. Or I did, inside.

"You what?"

"Tomas signed yesterday. Priya and the twins signed this morning. Okafor's thinking it over, but Okafor's daughter needs a surgery, so we both know how he's thinking." She said it gently, which was worse. Cass doing gentle was a scalpel apologizing on the way in. "They're good people. They said yes because they trust you, and because they need the money, and because they believe — and I quote Tomas — 'if the boss says it's safe, it's safe.' I never told them it was safe. I told them the boss hadn't said yes yet. That's the hole in the hull, Lior, and it's your name on it."

I want to tell you I threw her off the dock. I want to tell you I said something with teeth in it. What I actually did was look downriver — toward the sea, toward the honest work, toward ten years of cargo runs and small payments and never once saying her name out loud — and then upriver, where the fog thickened into the hills, into the green dark, into the country I'd sworn off in ink and in blood.

Tomas. Twenty-three years old, hands like a boy's still, who'd told me once — drunk, on a night I'd pretended not to remember — that working for me was the first thing he'd ever been proud of. Okafor's daughter had a hole in her heart the surgeons in Anselm wanted four thousand to close. Priya had been sending money home for nine years without once complaining about it where I could hear.

And me, standing in the fog with the worst person I knew, being told the truth in the shape of a trap.

"You did this on purpose," I said.

"Of course I did this on purpose." Cass looked almost offended that I'd bother saying it. "I've watched you run from that river for a decade. I wasn't going to ask you. I was going to make refusing cost more than going. It's not manipulation, Sandoval. It's arithmetic."

"There's a difference?"

"Only in how you feel about it after."

* * *

I found the crew in the back room of the Anchorline, which is the kind of establishment that calls itself a tavern the way a leak calls itself a skylight. Tomas was there with a beer he wasn't drinking, turning the contract over in his hands like it might be rigged. When he saw me his face did three things at once — relief, guilt, and hope — and I understood that Cass hadn't lied about a single detail, because she hadn't needed to.

"Boss." He stood up. "I was going to tell you. I was coming to tell you tonight, I swear —"

"You signed a contract to take my boat up the Vell."

"Your boat that I've patched twice this season." He said it quietly, which was how Tomas fought — he didn't raise his voice, he raised facts. "And your boat that's three months behind on the hull work because the cargo rates are garbage and you keep taking the cheap runs so you can pay us through the winter. Boss, I need this. You *know* I need this. And I know —" he stopped, and his ears went red, and he said it anyway, because that was Tomas, "— I know what the river is. To you. I know. But you always say a crew's a family, and families don't let each other drown on dry land."

The twins were playing cards in the corner and pretending not to listen, which meant they were listening with their whole bodies. Priya was at the bar with her back to me, and her shoulders said everything her mouth was too loyal to say.

Ten years. Ten years of never saying her name, of routing around the Vell watershed even when it cost me a week, of keeping my mother a closed box in a closed room in a country I never visited. And in one morning Cass Merrow had pried the lid off with arithmetic and a crew who loved me.

That's the thing about resentment. It curdles. And what it curdles into, if you let it sit long enough, is something that looks a lot like commitment, because it's easier to steer toward the thing you hate than to admit you were never steering at all.

"Six weeks," I said. "Not a day more. We go up, we get the records, we come down, and nobody on this boat dies, gets rich, or asks me about my mother. Those are the terms. Cass can take them or leave them."

Tomas exhaled like a man surfacing. "She said you'd say that."

"Of course she did." I picked the contract off the table and read it, all ten pages, because I remembered that about myself too: I read everything, and I still got caught. "One more thing. Who's her guide?"

Tomas blinked. "Some local. River-born. Came recommended by —" he checked the contract, and his voice went strange, "— by the expedition. Ten years ago. Name's Wren. Wren Halawi."

I put the contract down.

The fog outside had lifted, and through the Anchorline's one clean window I could see the river mouth, brown and patient, running toward the sea like it had all the time in the world. Like it had been waiting.

"Get the twins," I said. "We provision tonight. We run the tide at dawn."

"Boss — the Vell's a bad river in monsoon season. Everyone says so."

"Everyone's right," I said, and signed my name.
