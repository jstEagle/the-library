## 2. Monsoon Water Rising

The rain started as a rumor on the third day and became a religion by the fifth.

I'd run this river in dry seasons, when it was a green ribbon you could read like a ledger. In monsoon it was something else entirely — brown, muscular, carrying whole trees sideways like it was late for an appointment. The *Marta Cee* didn't like it. Neither did I. She was forty years old, held together by paint and my stubbornness, and she was taking water over her rails in quantities that suggested she'd developed opinions about our cargo.

"Leak," Tomas said, appearing at my elbow with the particular calm he reserved for catastrophes. "Bilge pump's running every four minutes now."

"Every four minutes is fine."

"It was every ten yesterday."

Everything was worse than yesterday. That was the monsoon's whole business model.

Cass Merrow stood at the bow under a tarp that had given up any pretense of keeping her dry, studying the river through rain-beaded glasses like a woman reading a menu. "We're making good time," she called back to me, and there it was again — that note in her voice, bright and brittle, the sound of someone who'd decided the schedule mattered more than the weather. She'd been pushing since dawn. Pushing me to run sections I'd have portaged in daylight, pushing the crew to load faster at the fuel stop, pushing, always, toward the upper valley before the calving season shut it like a door.

"Cass." I came up beside her, shouting over the drum of rain on canvas. "The boat's taking water. We shelter tonight."

"We're two days from the valley mouth."

"And the valley will still be there tomorrow. It's been there for ten thousand years. It can wait one more."

She turned and looked at me, and for a second I saw something underneath the expedition-leader polish — something hungry and frightened. "You don't understand what's up there," she said. "What's *left* up there."

"No," I said. "And neither do you, apparently, because you won't tell me what's so urgent about my mother's paperwork that we should drown for it."

She smiled then, and it was worse than the hunger. "You signed the contract, Lior. You could have read it more carefully."

* * *

We put in at dusk at a village I remembered from another life — stilt houses leaning over the flood-swollen water, smoke bleeding sideways from cooking fires, dogs conducting their eternal border disputes on the mud flats. The villagers took us in with the practiced patience of people who'd been rescuing river idiots for generations. A woman named Ama pressed hot tea into our hands and asked no questions, which told me she already knew the answers.

Wren Halawi stood apart from all of it, at the end of the dock, watching the current with an expression I couldn't file anywhere.

I'd hired her in the coast town because Cass insisted we needed a local guide and because Wren was the only one who hadn't flinched when I said the destination out loud. She was maybe thirty, built like rope, spoke maybe six words a day, and none of them wasted. But there was something else. The way she looked at the river wasn't the way guides look at rivers. It was the way people look at graves.

"You knew her," I said, coming up beside her. No preamble. Ten days of not asking had curdled into needing to know.

Wren didn't pretend to misunderstand. That was one thing I liked about her; she never spent words pretending. "Yes."

"On the last expedition."

"Yes."

The rain came down between us like a curtain neither of us wanted to part. Somewhere behind us, Tomas was arguing with Cass about the bilge pump, his voice rising in a register I almost never heard from him.

"Ines Sandoval sent me away that night," Wren said, still watching the water. "The night she died. She put me in a boat and told me to warn my mother's village downstream. Told me the channel was going to blow." Her jaw worked. "I told her to come with me. She said someone had to hold the diversion open long enough for the water to go where it wouldn't kill anyone."

My hands were shaking, and I gripped the dock rail to hide it. "Diversion. What diversion?"

"The dig cut into a meltwater channel. Dammed it without meaning to. Your mother found out what she'd done and stayed to fix it." Wren finally turned to look at me, and her eyes were flat and old. "Everyone downstream lived. Ask your friend Merrow why the official report says flash flood, act of God, no fault assigned."

Behind us, the argument stopped. Cass was standing in the lamplight at the top of the dock, and she had heard every word, and she did not look surprised.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat in the wheelhouse with the lantern down and made a list, because lists are how I keep from drowning.

Tomas needed the payout — that much was true, his sister's medical bills were real, I'd seen them. But he'd taken Cass's offer before telling me, before even *telling* me the job existed, and loyal men don't do that unless they've been promised something extra or threatened with something worse. He'd been avoiding my eyes since the coast.

Cass wanted the records destroyed or buried — I'd known that since the dock, but knowing it and understanding it were different animals. The expedition's legacy fund paid for half the archaeology program at her institute. If Ines's final findings said what Wren just said they said, the fund was built on a drowned village.

And Wren. Wren who knew my mother better than I did. Wren who had been sent away and lived, and had spent ten years — I would have bet anything on it — carrying the same question I carried. Not *why did she leave me*. Something worse: *was I worth staying for, and she decided I wasn't.*

Everyone on this boat was keeping a secret, and mine was the smallest: that I hadn't come for the money either.

* * *

Wren couldn't sleep either. This is what she told me later, though at the time I only saw her sitting alone on the dock in the small hours, and I only know the rest because grief makes strange confidantes of strangers, and because she chose, finally, to tell me.

She sat with her feet above the black water and thought about the night Ines Sandoval pushed her boat off the bank.

She had argued. She remembered arguing — young and furious, twenty years old, saying *you cut the channel, you call the crew, we all go together.* And Ines had put both hands on her shoulders, rain streaming off her own face, and said, in that voice she used for facts too large for ordinary grammar: *"There are three hundred people asleep downstream, Wren, and one of me up here. Go. Counting lives is arithmetic, and I was always better at arithmetic than you were."*

It was a joke. Ines joked at the edge of things. Wren had gone, and blown the warning horn until her arms gave out, and the village had climbed to the high ground in time, and the water had come through the valley below like the end of the world, and Ines Sandoval had not come down after.

For ten years Wren had told herself she'd been sent away because she was the fastest boat-handler. For ten years she had also, in the honest hour before sleep, believed something smaller and crueler: that Ines had sent away the person whose death she couldn't bear to cause, and kept for herself the death she could carry.

Sitting on the dock, watching the same river that had swallowed her teacher, Wren realized the truth was neither. Ines had sent her away because Wren was the only one who could make the village listen in time — and because Ines had already seen, in the way the channel wall was weeping water, that whoever held the diversion would not walk away from it. She had chosen the arithmetic. She had simply lied about how easy the sum was.

Wren looked up at the black shape of the *Marta Cee*, at the light still burning in the wheelhouse where Lior Sandoval sat awake, and understood that tomorrow they would reach the valley mouth together, and that whatever was sealed up there in the ice — the strongbox, the journal, the truth Cass Merrow had paid to bury — belonged to both of them equally.

She went below to sleep, or to lie down near it.

In the wheelhouse, I watched the river and thought: *two days.* Two days to the camp, if the weather held, and the weather was not holding. And somewhere upstream, past the last village, past the tree line, the glacier was groaning through the dark with a sound the villagers said meant the mountain was clearing its throat.

Tomas knocked once and stuck his head in, rain running off his hood. "Boss. You need to see what Cass is loading into the skiff. She thinks everyone's asleep."

I killed the lantern and followed him out into the rain.
