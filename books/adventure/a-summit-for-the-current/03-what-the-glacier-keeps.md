## 3. What the Glacier Keeps

The river stopped being a river somewhere below the moraine and became, instead, a rumor of one — gray water braiding itself thin around boulders the size of drowned cathedrals. Wren took us up the last navigable channel at dawn, hands steady on the tiller, face doing nothing at all, which by now I understood to be her version of screaming.

"Calving season starts when the melt peaks," she'd said the night before, in the flat voice she used for facts that had teeth. "Three days. Maybe four. After that, nobody goes near the face."

"And nobody's been near it since my mother," I said.

"No," Wren said. "Nobody's been near it since your mother."

Cass stood at the bow with her binoculars like a figurehead who'd read too much Ayn Rand, and Tomas bailed the bilge with the grim rhythm of a man who had stopped believing the leak was temporary around the second day. Priya checked the climbing gear for the fourth time. Everyone was very busy not looking at anyone else, which is what a boat full of secrets looks like from the inside: a lot of competent people staring at the horizon.

The sealed camp sat on a gravel shelf above the waterline, and it looked like the glacier had been trying to swallow it for a decade and kept getting interrupted. Tent frames rusted into the shape of their own collapse. A supply cache with the lid pried open by bears or weather or both. And there, half-buried against the rock wall where the meltwater couldn't reach, a cairn of stones stacked with an archaeologist's particular stubbornness — each one fitted, not thrown.

My mother's handwriting was everywhere. On the cache. On a strip of aluminum flashing nailed to a post: *DO NOT DISTURB — INES SANDOVAL, SURVEY IN PROGRESS*. Ten years dead and still leaving instructions. I'd spent most of my adult life obeying that sign without knowing it existed.

"Lior." Cass's voice had gone soft, which on Cass was worse than shouting. "The strongbox. If it's here, we take it, we document nothing, and we're off this ice before the melt peaks. That was the contract."

"That was *your* contract," I said. "The one my crew signed before I did. Remind me — how did that conversation go, exactly?"

Tomas had the grace to look at his boots. Priya didn't look at anything.

The cairn came apart in twenty minutes. Under it: a steel strongbox, orange paint gone chalky, latches fused with corrosion that ten years of freeze-thaw had welded shut like a promise. Priya worked the hinges with a pry bar while I stood there holding my own breath like an amateur.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job heroically: a field journal. Cloth-bound, water-stained at the corners, my mother's initials tooled into the cover. And beneath it, a second object that made Wren, who had come up the bank silent as weather, make a sound I'd never heard from her — a small, punched-out breath.

A child's carving. A little wooden boat, no bigger than my hand, the kind you make for a kid who won't stop asking when you're coming home.

"That's mine," I said. My voice came out strange. "I made — no. She made it. She was carving it the summer she left. She said it was for the river."

I opened the journal because my hands decided before I did.

* * *

The early entries were my mother exactly: sediment cores and channel surveys, jokes in the margins, a sketch of Wren at twenty looking furious about being sketched. I read them standing in the wind with the glacier groaning somewhere above us like a giant rolling over in its sleep, and I let myself have four pages of her voice before the voice changed.

It changed on the ninth of the flood month.

*The channel is wrong.* Her handwriting, always so certain, had begun to lean. *The excavation cut into the lateral moraine and we've exposed a meltwater channel the survey missed. It's dammed with our own tailings. I've run the numbers three times. If the melt peaks early, the impoundment fails and everything goes downvalley.*

Downvalley. I looked up. Wren was watching me with the expression of someone who had already read every word of this and had carried it alone for ten years, which, I was beginning to understand, she had.

*Halawi's village sits in the path,* the journal went on. *Four hundred people. I've told the crew we break camp early — logistics, I said. Merrow wants the season's finds secured first. Merrow always wants the finds first. I've decided Merrow doesn't need to know how bad the numbers are. If I say it out loud, the fund gets pulled, the work stops, and the dam still fails with no one watching it.*

*I can breach the channel manually. It means staying. It means doing it alone, at night, in the melt. I've sent Halawi downvalley with the warning and the survey notes. She argued. She's the best person I've ever worked with and I sent her away to save her life and I lied to her to do it. I hope she never reads this. I hope she never has a reason to.*

The wind picked up. Somewhere above us the glacier made a sound like a door opening in a house with no one in it.

*If anyone finds this: the failure was ours. Mine. The dig did this. Write it down. Don't let them make it a mystery. Mysteries are how institutions launder guilt.*

I stood there holding my mother's last instruction, which was to tell the truth, and Cass Merrow stepped into my field of view holding out her hand like we were at a garden party.

"Give it to me, Lior."

* * *

"The legacy fund employs two hundred people," Cass said. She'd positioned herself between me and the boat, which she probably thought was tactical. "Conservation grants, site protection, the whole upper valley survey. One bad page and it all goes. Sponsors pull out. The institute dissolves. And for what? Everyone involved is dead except us."

"Wren isn't dead," I said.

"Wren signed a confidentiality clause like everyone else."

"Did she." I looked at Wren. "Did you sign anything, Wren?"

"I signed what they put in front of me at nineteen," Wren said, "so they'd pay for my mother's roof after the flood took the old one. It's been ten years, Merrow. The clause has done its work. It kept me quiet while the village rebuilt on charity instead of compensation."

"That's not—" Cass started.

"The journal says the excavation caused it," I said. "It says your fund's founding money is downstream of a flood your own survey data predicted and buried. That's not one bad page, Cass. That's the whole ledger."

The glacier chose that moment to clear its throat. A crack ran up the face of it, long and white and unhurried, and the sound arrived a full second after the sight, the way thunder does, and everyone on that gravel shelf stopped performing and became animals for a moment.

"We have maybe an hour," Priya said. "Maybe less. Lior — we need to move."

"Then move," Cass said. "But the journal stays with me. It's expedition property. It's in the contract."

"It's my mother's," I said.

"It's *evidence*," Cass said, and then, because Cass Merrow could always find the knife's handle, she turned to the crew. "Tomas. Your sister's clinic. Priya, your visa sponsorship. You want to know who pays for all of it? The fund. You burn the fund, you burn them. Lior knows this. Lior's asking you to choose her grief over your paychecks."

And there it was, the thing I'd felt rotting under the deck since the coast: Cass hadn't just hired my crew. She'd bought them, and she was calling in the paper in front of me, on a melting gravel bar, with the ice coming down.

Tomas looked at me. Priya looked at the glacier. And Wren said, quietly, "The village has been choosing between truth and survival for ten years. It's a bad trade. I don't recommend it."

Another crack. Closer. The face of the glacier shivered like something waking up.

I looked down at the journal in my hands — at the last line, the one I hadn't read aloud, the one my mother had written in a hand gone shaky and small: *Tell Lior I wasn't choosing the river over them. Tell Lior the river was going to take everything, and I stood in its way.*

Ten years I'd believed she'd chosen the dig. She'd chosen the village. She'd chosen Wren's people, and died for it, and the only inheritance she'd left me was a box of truth that one very rich woman was asking me to burn.

The glacier began to calve.

Not the warning cracks — the real thing, a section of the face the size of a cathedral leaning out over its own shadow, and Wren was already shouting for the boat, and I had the journal under my arm and Cass's hand closing on my sleeve, and I had about four seconds to decide what my mother's daughter was made of.

I twisted free.

"Cass," I said, over the roar, "you can have the strongbox."

I left her holding the empty orange shell as the ice came down, and I ran for the water with the truth under my arm and my crew — all of them, even Tomas, even Priya — running beside me.
