## 4. A Summit for the Current

The glacier announced itself the way Cass Merrow announced everything: loudly, and with no regard for anyone else's schedule.

We were still on the moraine when the first crack came — a sound like the sky tearing along a seam nobody had bothered to stitch. The ice face above the camp groaned, shifted, and shed a chunk of itself the size of a chapel into the meltwater channel below. The spray reached us as a cold, mineral-smelling mist.

"Move," I said, and for once nobody argued with me.

Tomas had the strongbox slung across his back like it weighed nothing, which was a lie we both agreed to keep telling ourselves. Wren was already halfway down the slope, moving with that economy of motion she never wasted on words. And Cass — Cass stood at the edge of the calving field with her hand out, palm up, in the universal gesture of a woman who believed the world owed her one more transaction.

"Lior." She had to raise her voice over the ice. "The journal stays with the fund. That was the contract."

"The contract," I said, "was to recover Ines Sandoval's findings. All of them. Intact." I held out my hand for the strongbox. "You're welcome to read what we publish."

Cass's face did something complicated — grief and greed fighting for the same real estate. "Ten years of legacy funding. Scholarships. The named wing at the institute. You burn all of that because—"

"Because my mother drowned a valley to save a village, and then let everyone believe she'd abandoned her kid instead." The second crack came, closer, and I felt it in my teeth. "That's not a scandal, Cass. That's the only honest thing anyone's done on this river in a decade. I'm not letting you bury it twice."

She looked at Tomas. Tomas looked at the glacier. Even he could do that math.

"You'll be finished," she said. "Blacklisted. No charter will touch you."

"Yeah," I said. "I've been finished before. It turns out you can live there."

The third crack didn't announce itself at all. It just happened — a whole shoulder of the icefield letting go, sliding, the channel below surging white. We ran. Wren grabbed my sleeve at the switchback and hauled me left where my instincts said right, and that was the whole marriage of our next ten minutes: her hands, my feet, Tomas swearing beautifully in two languages behind us, Cass's footsteps fading until they weren't there at all.

I didn't look back to see if she followed. That, too, felt like inheritance.

* * *

We made the boat by dark, or what passed for dark under monsoon cloud — a bruised half-light that turned the river the color of pewter. The leak had been patched with Tomas's last epoxy and Wren's considerable contempt, and she sat at the stern with a bilge pump between her knees like it was a sleeping animal she'd agreed to babysit.

Tomas counted out his payout on the chart table, then pushed half of it back toward me without being asked.

"Cass paid for silence," he said. "I worked for you."

"I'm dissolving the contract anyway. Full disclosure, everyone walks, no penalties."

"You're dissolving your own payday?"

"I'm dissolving the part where any of us owes her anything." I lashed the strongbox to the rail. "What's left is ours."

He studied me the way he studied weather — skeptically, but with respect for its capacity to surprise him. Then he nodded once and went forward to check the lines, which was how Tomas said things that mattered.

Wren hadn't moved. Rain needled the river around us, and the pump wheezed its slow rhythm, and somewhere upstream the glacier kept talking to itself in the language of collapse.

"She made me leave," Wren said, finally. "That night. She put me in the survey skiff and told me to warn the village, and she stayed with the diversion cut because someone had to hold the spillway open while the water went around instead of through." Her thumb moved over the pump handle, back and forth. "I warned them. Forty-one people walked uphill in the rain because Ines Sandoval sent a stranger ahead of herself. And then I spent ten years not telling anyone, because who would believe me? Who would believe her daughter wanted to hear it?"

"I want to hear it," I said. "All of it. Every night, if you'll tell it. But start with the parts about her that aren't in the journal."

So she did. Under the rain, with the river pulling us downcurrent, Wren Halawi told me about a woman who sang off-key to scare off river birds, who learned eleven words of a language she refused to mangle, who kept a photograph of a child folded so many times it had gone soft as cloth. I knew that photograph. It had lived in my mother's breast pocket my entire remembered childhood. I had assumed, for ten bitter years, that it had been left behind on purpose.

It had been in her pocket. On the last night. Facing the flood.

Grief, it turns out, doesn't answer questions. It just changes which questions you can stand to ask.

* * *

The confluence came up on the third morning, where the grey glacier-fed current met the brown of the lowland river and ran side by side for a mile before agreeing to mix. Ines had written about it in her early journals — the ones I'd kept, the ones that never mentioned me — called it *the summit*, where two waters meet and neither has to stop being itself.

I'd brought the ashes from the coast in a tin that had once held engine gaskets, because my mother would have found that funnier than an urn.

Tomas cut the engine. Wren held us steady against the seam of the currents, and the boat rocked in that strange doubled pull, and I knelt at the rail with the tin in both hands.

"I spent ten years deciding what you'd done to me," I told her. "Turns out I should've been asking what you'd done for everybody else. Sorry I was late."

I opened the tin. The wind took her gently, unceremoniously, the way she'd have hated from anyone else and permitted from the river. Grey ash met grey water at the seam of the confluence and drifted downstream in two directions at once, which felt right. Which felt like her.

Wren put her hand on my shoulder and left it there. Tomas, at the bow, very quietly pretended to be fascinated by a knot.

* * *

We published everything in the spring. The journal, the survey data, Wren's account, sworn and witnessed by every villager whose family had walked uphill in the rain. The institute's named wing got renamed; the fund got restructured with the village holding the majority stake, which Cass fought for eight months before settling, because even Cass eventually does arithmetic.

The crew stayed. Not because I asked — because nobody wants to work for a captain with a clean reputation and no future, apparently, except every single person I'd ever hired. We run cargo now, mostly, and survey work for communities upstream who'd like to know where the water's going before it goes there. Wren navigates. Tomas still swears in two languages. The leak comes back every monsoon like a relative with opinions, and we patch it, and go anyway.

Last week a letter came upriver, forwarded through three ports and smelling faintly of diesel. Handwriting I didn't know. A young archaeologist, maybe — the letterhead said a university I'd only ever seen on rejection slips. She wrote that she'd read the Sandoval report, all of it, including the appendix where I'd printed my mother's last journal entry verbatim, the one that ends mid-sentence because the flood took the pen out of her hand.

She had one question, she said. About the diversion cut. About whether the original survey maps still existed, because there was a second channel on the old charts, unlabeled, and she thought — she hoped — someone might want to know what else the glacier is keeping.

I read it twice. Then I carried it up to the wheelhouse, where Wren was steering us into the current, and handed it to her without a word.

She read it. She looked at me. Somewhere north, a glacier was doing its slow, patient bookkeeping, and the river was already asking where we'd like to be taken.

"Well," Wren said, and turned the wheel.
