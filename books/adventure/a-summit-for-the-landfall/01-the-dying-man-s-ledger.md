## 1. The Dying Man's Ledger

The monsoon was three weeks out and Vellamoor already smelled of it — salt rot and river mud, the whole port city holding its breath. Edmund Montrose sat in the back room of the Kettle & Chain with a ledger open before him and a glass of arrack he hadn't paid for, doing arithmetic that refused to come out in his favor.

Four hundred and twelve rupees owed to Harl & Sons, factors. Ninety to his landlady, who had stopped knocking politely. Sixty to the man who supplied the chalk he used for surveying work he no longer got hired to do. And beneath all of it, like ballast, the name *Montrose* itself — a name that had once opened doors on the mountain lines and now closed them, quietly, one by one.

He was forty-one years old and he had been an engineer of some promise. That was the worst part. Not the debt. The memory.

The boy who brought the message couldn't have been more than ten, barefoot, breathing hard from the stairs. "There's a gentleman at the Sable Rest," he said. "Says you'll know why."

Edmund didn't know why. He knew no one at the Sable Rest, which was not a rest at all but a flophouse above a tannery on Cordwainer Lane, where the rooms went by the hour and the hours went by the smell. But the boy pressed a coin into Edmund's palm — a full rupee, silver, worn smooth — and said the gentleman had promised another if the message came quick.

A rupee was a rupee. Edmund pocketed it and hated himself for the speed with which he did.

* * *

The Sable Rest smelled exactly as advertised. The stairwell was lit by a single lamp burning whale oil gone sour, and the walls wept in the humidity, great blooms of damp climbing toward the ceiling like slow green fires. On the second floor, a door stood ajar, and behind it a man's voice — cracked, wet, urgent — said, "Shut it. Bolt it. They watch the lane."

Edmund should have left then. He would spend the rest of his life knowing precisely the moment he should have left, and knowing also that he hadn't, because the voice had said *they*, plural, and a coward's first instinct is always to learn how many.

The man in the bed was dying the way rivers die in the dry season: not all at once, but visibly, channel by channel. He was gaunt under a blanket gray with use, his beard streaked with white that might have been age or might have been frostbite long ago healed wrong. His eyes were the trouble. They were clear as tap water while everything else about him failed, and they fixed on Edmund the moment the door shut.

"You're Montrose," he said. "The engineer."

"Formerly."

"The compass man." The prospector — he could be nothing else; his hands were scarred the way hands get from rope and rock and cold — fumbled beneath his pillow and produced a brass compass, its case dented, its glass crazed but whole. He held it out. "You know this?"

Edmund took it because refusing seemed crueler than taking. He turned it over. On the case's reverse, scratched deep enough to survive decades of pockets, was a single word:

*ALDER.*

His own surname, once. His father's name. The floor tilted very slightly, the way floors do when a man has drunk too little and learned too much.

"Where did you get this?"

"Halden Alder gave it to me. Eleven years past. Up beyond the closed lines, where the Sarn comes down." The prospector coughed, a long tearing sound. "I've carried it ever since. Carried what came with it, too." His hand slid under the blanket again and emerged with a folded square of oilcloth, tied with waxed string. "Open it."

Edmund didn't move. "I'm not a courier. I'm not anything. I owe money in this city to men who—"

"I know what you owe." The clear eyes didn't blink. "I asked after you. I asked after you for six days. A man drowning in debt is a man nobody will miss for a fortnight, and I needed a fortnight's worth of nobody." He pushed the oilcloth bundle against Edmund's chest. "Open it or don't. But listen first, because I haven't got the breath for twice."

Edmund listened. It was raining now, softly, the first spit of the season against the shutter, and the prospector spoke in the rhythm of a man conserving words like coins.

"There's a lake up there. Under the glacier, where every map says the Sarn begins in snowmelt and nothing else. It isn't nothing else. It's a living lake — it swells and shrinks, it breathes with the seasons, and the valley below lives by that breathing. Twelve villages. Maybe more. The company knows. The company has known since Halden Alder surveyed it, and they killed him for the knowing, and they've spent eleven years getting their papers in order to dam it and drain it and call the water *improvement*." Another cough. "This—" he tapped the oilcloth "—is everything. The true course. The villages. The dates of their surveys. Names. If it reaches the right courts, or even the wrong ones loudly enough, the works die. If it reaches no one, the works live, and the valley drowns on paper before the water ever touches it."

"Why me?" Edmund said. "Why not a newspaperman? A magistrate? Anyone with standing?"

"Because standing gets bought." The prospector smiled, and it was a terrible thing, that smile — the last asset of a bankrupt man. "Halden told me: find a man with nothing left, who still carries the tools of the trade. Nothing left means nothing to trade. Tools mean he remembers the mountains." His grip closed on Edmund's wrist, shockingly strong for a dying man. "Carry it upstream or burn it. Those were Halden's words to me and they're mine to you. Carry it up to the headwaters, put it in the hands of whoever keeps the lake — there are people up there, Montrose, keepers — or burn it here, tonight, and let the company win clean. But don't carry it *down*. Don't sell it. Whatever they offer. Because they'll offer."

"They?"

"They found me in Kalapore. I ran. I ran too slow." He let go of Edmund's wrist and lay back, spent. "Take the compass too. It was always going to come back to an Alder. That's the joke of it. Halden planned it that way, I think. He said the river remembers its engineers."

Edmund stood a long moment with the bundle in his hands. Outside, the rain thickened. Somewhere below, the tannery vats turned and turned.

"I'm not the man you want," he said finally. "I ran from a collapsed culvert on the Ghats line with two hundred men's wages in my strongbox and I never went back. I signed the report that blamed the surveyor. I know what I am."

"Yes," the prospector said. "That's rather the point. Halden knew too. He said: *give it to the coward. Cowards are careful.*"

Edmund laughed — one dry note, no humor in it — and untied the string.

Inside the oilcloth was a map, hand-drawn on survey linen, dense with elevations and annotations in two hands: one precise and academic, one rougher, added later, in the field. The upper reaches of the Sarn unspooled across it like a vein of blue fire, running up through gorges marked *CLOSED BY ORDER — PERMIT REQUIRED* into country no published chart admitted existed. At the top, drawn in the second hand, was a small blue shape like a lung. Beneath it, in his father's handwriting — he knew it instantly, the way you know a face in a crowd twenty years gone — were the words: *It breathes. Let it.*

* * *

He meant to burn it.

He told himself so all the way down Cordwainer Lane, all the way back through the wet streets to his rooms, the map sewn — he could not say afterward when he had decided this, only that the needle was in his hand and then the thread was through the coat lining, over his heart, and done — sewn into the shoulder seam where no porter or bailiff would think to look. The compass rode in his pocket, cold as a coin from a dead man's eye.

He had a lamp lit and the hearth half-laid for burning when the knock came.

Not his landlady's knock. This was three knocks, evenly spaced, patient as bookkeeping. Edmund stood very still. Through the window he saw, two streets down, a lantern moving — not walking, *searching*, swinging in slow arcs across doorways. And beside the lamplighter's post below, a man in a company rain-cloak stood with his collar up, reading the numbers on the houses the way a man reads a ledger, line by line.

Three knocks again.

"Mr. Montrose," said a voice, pleasant and unhurried. "Company business. We understand you may have recently visited the Sable Rest."

Edmund looked at the hearth. Dry kindling. One match would end it — the promise kept in the easiest way, the coward's way, the way that required nothing of him but a struck match and a lifetime of knowing he'd struck it.

Instead he pinched out the lamp, took the back stair he'd used to dodge his landlady a dozen times, and stepped down into the rain.

Behind him, the door gave way. Ahead, the dark streets ran downhill toward the river, and beyond the river, past the checkpoints and the closed lines, the mountains stood unseen under the storm, holding their water, waiting to see what he would do.

He walked. He did not look back. It was, he would think later, the first brave thing he had ever done, and he had done it mostly because the other option was worse.

Mostly.
