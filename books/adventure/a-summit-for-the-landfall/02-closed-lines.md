## 2. Closed Lines

The checkpoint at Kandapola Junction was a plank shack with a stove and two men, and Edmund had walked past a hundred like it in his working years. He still wore the coat with the railway buttons, the ones they'd never asked back when they struck his name from the rolls. Shame, it turned out, made a serviceable disguise.

He showed the passbook. The clerk — a boy with a cold and a stamp — turned it over, squinted at the embossed seal of the Vellamoor & Highland Railway, and looked at Edmund with something close to reverence. Nobody had come through this gate in eleven months. The line above the junction had been closed since the slide of '09, officially for repairs, unofficially because the company had stopped pretending the mountain line made money and started pretending it didn't exist.

"Survey party?" the boy asked.

"Hydrographic," Edmund said, which was even true in a way he didn't examine. "Cleared to the head of the gorge."

The stamp came down. The gate arm lifted. And Edmund Montrose walked back into the profession that had ruined him, carrying in his coat a dead man's map and a promise he already regretted.

The closed line ran up through country that got its teeth into you quickly. Tea gave way to jungle, jungle to scree. The rails were rusted orange at the joints, and in places the ballast had slumped and the whole roadbed hung like a loose thread off the mountainside. He walked the ties because the map said the gorge road was watched, and because a man walking rails looks like he belongs to them.

He heard the shed before he saw it — a small sound, rhythmic, wrong. A maintenance shed stood where the line crossed a stream on a low culvert, its door ajar, and inside it someone was breathing the way people breathe when they're trying very hard not to.

Edmund stood in the doorway with the afternoon behind him and let the shadow do the talking.

"I heard the handcar come through this morning," said a voice from behind a stack of creosote drums. "I heard it stop. If you're from the estate, I'm not going back. If you're from the company, I've got nothing worth taking. And if you're the police—" the voice wavered, steadied, "—then I'm nobody's daughter and you can write that down."

"I'm none of those," Edmund said. "I'm worse. I'm in debt."

A pause. Then a girl of perhaps sixteen worked herself out from behind the drums, small and wiry, with a bruise going yellow along her jaw and a canvas bundle clutched to her chest like a child. Her eyes did the arithmetic of him in about two seconds — coat, boots, the railway passbook still in his hand.

"You're running the closed line," she said.

"I'm walking it."

"Nobody walks it. Nobody comes up here at all. Which means you're either a fool or you're going where I'm going." She lifted her chin at his coat. "Upstream. Everyone who comes to the mountains ends up going upstream eventually."

"I'm going to the source of the Sarn."

Something moved behind her eyes — not fear. Recognition. "Then you'll need a boat," she said, "and there's only one man on this whole river who'd take one up there, and he's camped at the old ferry landing below the trestle, and he'll say no, and then he'll say yes, because he always does." She shouldered her bundle. "My name is Asha. I can pay my way in tea and silence. Which do you need more?"

* * *

Karun's boat was a long, shallow thing of lashed bamboo and blackened wood, pulled up among the reeds like a sleeping animal. The man himself sat mending a net with hands that had clearly been doing it for fifty years, and he looked at the two of them coming down the bank — the disgraced engineer and the runaway girl — with the flat patience of the river itself.

"I know you," he said to Edmund. "The railway man. The one they blamed for the Kandapola washout."

"The one they *found* to blame," Edmund said.

"Same thing, on this river." Karun bit a thread and tied it off. "You want the upper Sarn."

"I want the headwaters."

"Nobody wants the headwaters. The headwaters want *you*." He looked at Asha, and something in his face softened by a degree. "You're Devika's girl."

Asha went very still. "You knew my mother."

"I poled her down this river when she was your age, running from the same estate." He began, without appearing to decide anything, to gather his gear. "So. A dead man's errand, a runaway, and a disgraced engineer. The river will love this." He shoved the boat toward the water. "Get in before I change my mind."

They made six miles that evening, poling and paddling up a river gone glassy and gold in the last light, and camped on a gravel bar under the black shoulders of the gorge. Asha brewed tea from leaves she produced from her bundle like a conjurer. Karun told stories about the river's moods the way other men discussed weather — the Sarn sulking, the Sarn lying, the Sarn remembering. Edmund lay awake listening to the water and thinking about a promise made to a dying man, and about how a promise, once accepted, stops being a weight you carry and becomes a weight that carries you.

* * *

The ambush came at the trestle, because of course it did.

They'd taken to the line again above the ferry landing — the gorge road was washed out and the river ran through a slot too narrow to pole — and the trestle bridge stood a mile ahead, its timber legs black with age, spanning a ravine where the Sarn dropped forty feet in white noise. Edmund saw the men at the same instant Karun did. Four of them, spread along the far approach, moving with the unhurried confidence of professionals. And behind them, at the head of the ravine road, a fifth figure on horseback, still as a fencepost, watching through field glasses.

"Company," Karun said. "The one on the horse. That's the one who buys men."

"Rook," Edmund said. The name had come up twice in Vellamoor, always in the tone people used for weather that kills.

The killers started forward, not running. Herding.

Edmund's eye went to the handcar.

It sat on a siding stub near the near approach — the maintenance crews' old pump trolley, its handles folded, its wheels greased and waiting for men who would never come. He'd ridden them for six years. He knew exactly what a handcar weighed, exactly what a downgrade did to one, and exactly what happened when a handcar met a bridge at speed with its brake wedged and its crew gone.

"Get on the bridge," he said. "All the way to the middle. Don't stop."

Karun understood first — boatmen understand momentum the way priests understand sin. He took Asha's bundle, took Asha's arm, and they went out onto the trestle at a dead run, timbers booming under their feet, the river roaring below.

Edmund threw the brake wedge into the handcar's shoe, hauled the handles up, and put his shoulder to the frame. The car rolled. He let it gather speed down the siding's gentle grade, then leapt clear and watched it run — wheels singing, iron flashing — straight down the main line toward the bridge.

The killers saw it coming and scattered off the approach. But the handcar wasn't aimed at them. It hit the trestle's near abutment at full tilt, and the impact — Edmund had calculated it in the three seconds he'd had, and his calculations were the one thing about him that had never been disgraced — sheared the rusted tie-rods of the first span. The whole near section of the bridge sagged, groaned, and folded into the ravine with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat, taking the approach, the rails, and the route after it into the white water below.

The killers stopped at the new edge of the world. The horseman on the ridge did not move. Through the smoke of settling dust, Edmund felt the field glasses find him, hold him, *read* him — coat, build, the set of his shoulders — the way a clerk reads a manifest.

Then the figure turned the horse and was gone.

They went down the far side and up the gorge through the blue end of the day, Asha shaking, Karun silent, Edmund doing the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since Vellamoor. A bridge was a message. A bridge told a man like Rook exactly one thing: the map was real, the carrier was desperate, and the carrier was now on foot in closed country with a river for a road.

"He'll send men up the valley road," Edmund said. "And boats up from the delta. He knows where I'm going now, because I've just told him — I've burned everything behind me except the way forward."

Asha looked back once at the broken trestle, small in the dusk. "Was that the only way?"

"It was the way I'm best at," Edmund said. "Breaking things so they can't be used."

Karun poled them into the darkening gorge, and the river took them up into the mountains, toward the source, with the first far thunder of the building monsoon rolling down from the ice like a drum being carried into a war.
