## 3. Where the Water Remembers

The river changed its mind three times a day.

That was Karun's way of explaining it, poling them up the narrowing gorge while the walls closed in like a book being shut. In the mornings the Sarn ran green and reasonable. By noon it turned the color of milk-glass, swollen with glacier flour, and by dusk it could be brown and furious for no reason the sky admitted to. Edmund had spent eleven years designing grades and culverts for water he thought he understood. Three days in Karun's leaky boat had cured him of that.

"Company men came through here," Karun said, nodding at a fresh blaze cut into a cedar trunk — a surveyor's mark, paint still tacky under his thumb when he touched it. "Two weeks ago. They were not quiet about it."

Asha crouched in the bow with the map open across her knees, though she'd long since memorized it. She was fourteen and had run from an estate where the tea bushes went on forever and the wages did not, and she read terrain the way other children read picture books. "The lake should be past the second gorge," she said. "The X is drawn on flat ground. Nobody draws an X on flat ground unless they want you to think it's flat."

Edmund said nothing. He was watching the abandoned camp appear around the bend — canvas gone gray, poles leaning, a rusted theodolite still bolted to its tripod as if its owner meant to come back after lunch and never had.

He knew this camp.

Not from the map. From memory, from a childhood evening twenty-five years gone, when a tall man with sawdust in his hair had spread drawings across a kitchen table in Vellamoor and pointed at a place called the Sarn Head and said, *One day I'll show you where the river is born, Eddie.* Then the man had taken a government contract instead of a company one, and the company had ruined him for it, and Edmund had grown up learning that the surest way to hate a father was to become him.

* * *

They made the second gorge by nightfall and camped in the shell of a survey station that had been burned, deliberately, years ago — beams charcoaled, papers fused into black bricks. Asha found a strongbox pried open and empty. Karun found boot prints, recent, heading up-valley.

"Rook," Edmund said.

"He travels heavy," Karun said. "Twenty men, mules, blasting powder. We travel light." He smiled without much humor. "Light is faster."

They climbed through the next day, the air thinning, the trees giving way to scree and the first hard snowpatches. And it was Asha, scrambling ahead, who stopped so suddenly Edmund nearly walked into her.

The valley opened like a held breath.

The lake lay above them — not the blue of postcards but a deep, bruised turquoise, miles of it, held back by a lip of ancient ice and moraine. It moved. That was the thing no drawing conveyed: the whole surface breathed, slow swells crossing it like something turning over in sleep. Meltwater spilled from its lower edge in a silver braid and became, over ten yards, the Sarn.

"That's the source," Asha whispered. "It's alive."

"It's a reservoir," said a voice behind them, "waiting for management."

Rook stood on the ridge with six men and rifles, and he looked exactly as Edmund remembered him from the checkpoint — neat, patient, a man who filed people under headings. Behind him, half-hidden in the moraine, steel and timber rose against the sky: cofferdams, crane legs, the skeleton of a concrete face already poured halfway across the natural outlet channel.

"You've seen the works," Rook said pleasantly. "Then you understand there's no version of this where you walk away. The only question is what we do with the girl while we discuss it."

Two of his men dragged Asha forward. She didn't cry out. She looked at Edmund with a fury that was worse than tears, because it agreed with everything he'd ever believed about himself.

"The map," Rook said. "And the compass, Montrose. I know you have it."

* * *

They held him in the old survey hut above the works, alone, while Rook dealt with Asha somewhere out of earshot. Edmund sat on a bunk that had belonged to a dead man and turned the compass over in his hands for the hundredth time since Vellamoor.

Brass case, dented. A needle that had never once pointed true north in his experience, always a few degrees shy, as if apologizing. On the inside lid, worn almost smooth, an inscription he'd never bothered to read closely because it had come with a pawnshop ticket and a debt:

**E. HALLOWAY — THE SOURCE IS WORTH MORE THAN THE LINE.**

Halloway. His father's name. His own middle name, which he'd spent a career avoiding.

His hands began to shake. He pried the bezel wider with a thumbnail, and beneath the card, folded into a space no bigger than a stamp, was a scrap of tracing paper — a surveyor's hand, his father's hand, he knew it the way you know a voice through a wall. Elevations. Dates. And a line of arithmetic ending in a conclusion underlined twice:

*Impoundment will overtop the moraine in monsoon. Valley villages untenable. Company informed. Company proceeding.*

His father hadn't died in some accident on the mountain. He'd found this — the lake, the plan, the arithmetic of drowned villages — and he'd written it down, and the company had closed the line behind him. The prospector with the fever and the map had been his father's last assistant. Everything Edmund had been running from had been walking beside him the whole way north, ticking in his pocket, pointing a few degrees shy of true.

He laughed, once, and it came out wrong.

When Rook came in at dusk, Edmund was calm in the way of men with nothing left to protect except a decision.

"The map for the girl," Edmund said. "Unhurt, released downriver with Karun, and you get the original. My father's notes too. Every number."

Rook studied him. "You're giving up quickly."

"I'm an engineer," Edmund said. "I know when a structure's failed." What he didn't say was that the map in his coat was already worthless — that Asha had redrawn it from memory days ago, and the copy sewn into his lining was accurate in every detail except the one that mattered: the outlet channel, which his father's tracing showed would take the overflow naturally, if someone gave it help.

Rook smiled his filing-cabinet smile. "Done. But you'll stay until my engineers verify it. And Montrose — the girl waits at the works. Accidents happen on mountains."

They brought Asha to the hut at moonrise. She walked past Edmund without looking at him, and only when the guards withdrew did she turn, eyes blazing.

"You gave him the map."

"I gave him a lie with good handwriting." He unfolded his father's tracing on the bunk between them. "This is the real one. And it says the dam can't hold the monsoon — Rook's own numbers agree, he just doesn't care whose valley fills. So we're going to help it fail early, on our terms, before the storm comes and does it worse."

Asha stared at the paper, then at him. "With what?"

Edmund looked out the window, down at the half-built works, at the magazine shed where Rook's blasting powder was stored, guarded by four men who believed the fight was already won.

"With everything he brought up the mountain," Edmund said. "He just hasn't read the invoice yet."

Below them, far to the south, the horizon flickered — silent, sheet-white, again and again — the monsoon announcing itself like a drumroll before the curtain.

Karun's boat, Edmund thought, better be as good as the old man claimed.

Because in the morning, he intended to drown the only road home.
