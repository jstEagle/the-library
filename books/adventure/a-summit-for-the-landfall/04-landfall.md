## 4. Landfall

The storm arrived the way debts do — not all at once, but in a first cold drop that found the gap between Edmund's collar and his neck, and then a thousand more, and then the sky simply gave up pretending to hold.

He stood at the edge of the company's works with the map against his chest, sewn into the lining where it had ridden for three hundred miles of rail, gorge, and grief. Below him, half-built across the throat of the valley, the dam rose like a bad idea made concrete: scaffolding, coffer walls, crates of blasting charges stacked under tarps that snapped in the wind like trapped birds.

Rook waited on the timber staging above the sluice channel, one hand fisted in Asha's collar, the other holding a pistol he held the way accountants hold things — carefully, as if it might cost him.

"The map," Rook said over the wind.

"You have her."

"I have an asset. The map is the deed." He shook Asha once, hard, and she made no sound, which was worse than screaming. Her eyes found Edmund's over Rook's shoulder, and there was no plea in them. There was arithmetic. *Do what you came to do.*

Edmund climbed the staging. Rain sheeted off the planks. Up close, Rook smelled of wet wool and ledger ink, a man who had never once been rained on by accident.

"I'll give you the map," Edmund said. "But understand what you're buying. That lake isn't a reservoir. It's alive. It moves. Your engineers dammed a living thing because your survey was forty years old and wrong."

"My survey," Rook said, "is signed."

"So was mine." And he meant it more ways than one. He unpicked the coat seam with his knife, working fast, fingers numb, and drew out the prospector's map — the dead man's last argument, ink browned at the folds. He held it out.

Rook reached for it with the hand that wasn't on Asha.

Edmund let him take it. That was the trade; he honored trades. But as Rook's fingers closed on the paper, Edmund stepped back into the rain and said, "There's something you should know about the man who drew it. He died protecting this water. And so did the surveyor before him. My father carried that compass up here in 1889 and never came down. You're standing on why."

Something flickered in Rook's face — not fear, but recalculation, the accountant discovering a hidden liability. It lasted half a second. Then he shoved Asha toward Edmund, took two steps back along the staging, and raised the pistol.

Edmund caught Asha's arm and pulled her down behind the charge crates as the first shot went wide and singing into the storm.

* * *

The charges were Rook's own. That was the poetry of it, and Edmund had never had much use for poetry until now. Forty cases of gelignite, wired for the final breach of the coffer wall, sitting idle because the company's schedule had been set back by weather, by money, by men deserting downriver. All they needed was a detonator and a reason.

Edmund had both.

He'd spent eleven years as a railroad engineer. He knew how things failed — that was his profession, really: the study of failure, conducted from the safe side. He'd watched bridges fall and tunnels flood and always written it up afterward in neat columns, failure as data, failure as someone else's grief. Now he knelt in the rain among Rook's own wires and made failure his signature.

Karun appeared out of the white murk with a coil of fuse cord and a grin that had no joy in it, only readiness. "The boat is below the sluice," he shouted. "The river wants to go home, sahib. We are only giving it permission."

Asha tore the map from Rook's abandoned satchel — Rook had dropped everything to shoot — and held it out to Edmund. "Burn it or carry it?"

"Carry it," Edmund said. "Someone has to testify."

They wired the charges together while the storm leaned on them. Karun knew the old survey paths; Asha, quick-fingered from years of tea-leaf plucking, stripped wire faster than any sapper Edmund had trained. When the circuit was laid, Edmund looked once at the lake through the rain — grey-green, vast, patient, wearing its ice like a closed eye — and thought of a compass needle swinging north over a man's drowned bones.

"For the record," he said, to no one, to everyone, "the Sarn River Survey objects."

He threw the switch.

The world went white, then orange, then loud. The coffer wall did not crumble; it *exhaled*, a whole section of Rook's careful masonry lifting and folding downstream like wet paper, and behind it the lake stood up. Not a flood — a decision. Water found the natural channel, the one the river had cut for ten thousand years before men arrived with signatures, and poured itself home with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat.

The works went with it. Crates, staging, the company's brass claim-marker — all of it lifted, rolled, unmade. Somewhere in the roar Edmund heard or imagined Rook's voice, and did not look back to check. Some accounts you settle by closing the book.

* * *

They ran for the boat through water already at their knees.

Karun's craft was a long tea-estate transport skow, shallow and stubborn, built for exactly this kind of insolence. They piled in — Edmund at the bow, Asha bailing with a tin pan, Karun at the sweep oar — and the river took them the way a fist takes a thrown ball.

What followed was not navigation. It was negotiation. The Sarn ran brown and vertical in places, and the landfall storm met the released lake head-on, so that rain fell upward and the gorge walls wept waterfalls sideways. Twice the skow went airborne off standing waves. Once it struck a submerged scaffold beam and spun, and Asha went over the side, and Edmund went in after her without deciding to, and the river held them both for one long indifferent moment before Karun's oar found them like a parent's hand in a dark room.

They lost the tent. They lost the food. Edmund lost his coat, and with it nothing, because the map rode inside his shirt now, against skin, where promises live.

Near dawn — though dawn was a rumor under that sky — the gorge opened, the grade eased, and the river spread out into the flooded lowlands, carrying them past drowned paddy fields and rooftops where families waved them toward high ground. Karun read the current like scripture, choosing channels by instinct older than surveys, and somewhere in the grey hours Edmund realized he was laughing, and that Asha was too, and that neither of them could stop.

* * *

The delta received them at noon, when the storm had spent itself and the sky broke open into a washed, improbable blue.

They grounded the skow on a mudbank among fishing stakes, three people caked in river silt, possessing between them one knife, one tin pan, one compass with another man's name inside the lid, and a map worth nothing now except as evidence. Downstream, the port of Vellamoor glittered in the clearing light, unaware that its future had just been rewritten upstream. Somewhere behind them, a company would be counting losses and assigning blame, and Edmund Montrose's name would be at the top of every column.

"Broke," Asha said, spitting out delta mud. "Hunted. Soaked."

"Unbroken," Karun said, and it didn't sound like a boast. It sounded like a measurement.

Edmund opened the compass. The needle swung, settled, pointed not north but downriver — toward Vellamoor, toward the hearings and the handcuffs and whatever came after — and he found, to his surprise, that he wasn't afraid of it. His father had died pointing this way. He intended to arrive.

"We'll need a witness list," he said. "And a lawyer who hates the company more than he loves money."

Asha grinned. "I know a tea estate full of people with grievances."

"And I know every magistrate on the coast," Karun said, "and which ones can be bribed, and which ones can be *shamed*."

The tide turned. The river ran free behind them, all the way to the ice. Edmund Montrose, disgraced engineer, stood up in a borrowed skow with a dead man's map and a family made by water, and started rowing toward the trouble he had chosen.
