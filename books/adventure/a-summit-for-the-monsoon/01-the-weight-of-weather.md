## 1. The Weight of Weather

The rain began as a rumor at dawn and by mid-morning had become a fact, the way weather did here—announcing itself politely, then moving in without invitation. Felix Joubert stood on the flooded apron of her airstrip with her collar up and her hands in her pockets, watching the cloud deck lower itself over the valley like a lid being set on a pot.

The strip was hers in the way that anything was hers: she'd cut it herself, eleven years ago, from a shelf of old-growth alder above the river bend, and every season the forest tried to take it back. Alder shoots came up through the gravel between flights. Moss crept toward the centerline markers she'd painted from a can of white that had cost her three months of trading. She let it come so far and then she beat it back, and this negotiation—herself against the green insistence of the place—was the closest thing to a marriage she'd ever sustained.

"Joubert!" The shout came through the rain from the direction of the fuel shed, where Old Teodor stood under the eave with his shoulders hunched like a man expecting the sky to fall on him personally. "Radio says the Kestrel woman's fever's worse. They want her flown out today."

Today. Felix looked up at the ceiling, which was not a ceiling but a soft gray nothing that began two hundred meters over the water and went up forever. In the interior valleys, weather didn't give you days. It gave you windows, and windows closed.

"Tell them I'll be there by noon," she called back.

"They also want to know if you'll carry the mail."

"The mail comes with me or it waits for the next flood of blood and penicillin." She said it dryly enough that Teodor laughed, a wet bark of a sound, and turned back into the shed.

Her plane sat at the top of the ramp, a forty-year-old bush plane she'd rebuilt twice and named nothing, because naming things made them yours to lose. She walked around it the way she always did before a flight in bad air—hand trailing along the fabric skin, checking the tie-downs, tipping the tail to feel the weight of what the rain had already taken from her margins. Fuel was fuel. Weight was truth. Everything else was negotiable.

Inside, the cabin smelled of avgas and dried cedar and the particular mustiness of a man who had lived alone with his instruments for a decade. She ran the preflight by touch more than sight. Magnetos. Flaps. The altimeter, which lied by sixty feet and which she had long ago stopped correcting for, because knowing your instrument's lies was better than trusting an honest one.

The mail sacks went in first—eleven kilos of letters and seed catalogs and a pharmacy order for the upriver settlement, wrapped in oilcloth because everything here got wrapped in oilcloth eventually, the whole valley swaddled against its own climate. Then the medical crate: plasma, antibiotics, a splint kit someone downstream had requested with a note that said *dog, leg, do not tell my wife how much*. Then herself.

She keyed the radio out of habit more than hope. "Kestrel station, Joubert inbound, ETA one-two-hundred. Weather's coming down, so have someone at the meadow to meet me."

Static answered, then a voice crackling through it like something being torn. "*Copy, Joubert. Meadow's flooded ankle-deep but we'll stand in it.*"

They would, too. That was the thing about these people that she had never quite gotten used to, in eleven years—they would stand in a flooded meadow in the rain to meet a plane, because the plane was the difference between a fever and a funeral, and they knew it, and they were grateful in the specific, undramatic way of people who understood that gratitude was a form of currency and that she accepted payment in kind: firewood split and stacked, fish smoked and hung, silence about her comings and goings, and never—not once, not ever—a question asked twice.

The engine caught on the third try, coughed, caught again. Felix taxied up the flooded strip with water sheeting off the propeller in silver ropes, and took off into weather that a sane pilot would have waited out, except that waiting out weather in this country meant waiting out people, and people were dying at the rate of fevers, and fevers did not consult barometers.

* * *

She flew the river valley the way other people read a familiar sentence—eyes ahead, comprehension total, no need to look at individual letters. The river ran brown and swollen below her, dragging whole trees seaward like a crowd shoving toward an exit. The ridgelines on either side were gone, swallowed into cloud, so she flew by memory and by the shape of the water, which she knew the way a blind person knows a hallway: by count and by echo. Bend at the drowned spruce. Bend at the shale slide. Bend where the tributary came in loud and milky with glacier flour, though she couldn't see it today, only hear it in the changed pitch of the water's noise through the hull of the air.

Rain hit the windshield sideways. The wiper had died two seasons ago and she'd never replaced it, preferring instead to fly with her head cocked at an angle that had given her a permanent crick in the neck and, she suspected, a permanent tilt to her view of the world. Through the clear half of the glass, the valley unspooled: black-green slopes dripping, waterfalls everywhere the rock showed through, the whole landscape running like a struck bell that wouldn't stop ringing.

This was the part of flying she loved, if love was the word, and she had never found a better one. Not the takeoffs, which were work. Not the landings, which were arithmetic performed at speed. This—the moment when the machine became transparent and there was only her and the weather and the valley holding both of them, and nobody anywhere knew exactly where she was, and for a few minutes that anonymity felt less like loneliness than like freedom.

For a few minutes. She was honest enough with herself, in the privacy of her own skull, to know the difference. Freedom and loneliness were the same room seen from opposite doors, and she had been living in that room for eleven years, keeping it tidy, calling it by whichever name suited the hour.

The radio crackled. "Joubert, you still with us?"

"Never left," she said.

"Heard the pass is closing behind you. You'll want to be back over it before dark or you'll be sleeping in Kestrel."

"I don't sleep in Kestrel," she said, and heard the laugh come back at her down the static, and realized only after she'd said it how much it sounded like a boast, and how little of one it actually was. *I don't sleep in Kestrel* meant: *I have nowhere else to be.* It meant: *I will fly home through a closing pass rather than spend an evening making conversation with people who would feed me dinner and ask me questions.*

Because they would ask questions. They always did, gently, the way you offer food to a stray. *Where are you from originally, Felix? Family around? How'd a woman your age end up flying medicine into the wettest corner of the map?* And she had answers, polished ones, each one true in the way that a single frame of film is true: *I'm from south of here. No family to speak of. I liked airplanes and there was a shortage of pilots.* All true. None of them a window. She'd built herself out of true sentences the way you build a wall out of bricks, and the wall held, and behind the wall was a house she hadn't entered in years, and in the house was a name she hadn't spoken aloud since—

Since. She banked around the shoulder of the mountain where the cloud allowed it, and put the thought away with the practiced motion of long habit, the way you'd stow a loose tool before turbulence.

* * *

The meadow at Kestrel was exactly as promised: flooded, gray, ringed by people standing in the rain under umbrellas that the wind kept trying to confiscate. Felix brought the plane in low over the tree line, flared through a gust that shoved sideways at the wheels like a hand on the fuselage, and touched down in six hundred feet of grass and water, spraying a rooster tail that soaked the front row of well-wishers, who cheered anyway.

They always cheered. It embarrassed her every time.

Mrs. Amara Kestrel herself was waiting at the edge of the meadow, upright under an umbrella held by her grandson, her face the color of old tallow and her eyes still fierce. The fever had her, everyone said, and at seventy-nine the fever had a strong arm. But her eyes tracked the plane in, and when Felix climbed out she raised one finger off her walking stick and pointed it like a dueling pistol.

"You're late," Mrs. Kestrel said.

"By four minutes," Felix said. "I counted."

"In my day, four minutes late was still late." But the finger wavered, and the grandson steadied his grandmother, and Felix saw the truth underneath the performance, which was that the old woman had dressed for dying and was furious about it.

Felix crouched, letting the rain run off her hat brim between them. "Plasma's aboard. Doctor Voss sent instructions written plain, and I'll read them to whoever's nursing you, and I'll come back Thursday to see whether you've obeyed them."

"I've never obeyed anything in eighty years. Why start now?"

"Then I'll bring the good morphine Thursday and bribe you."

That got a laugh, which turned into a cough, which ended the audience. Felix handed the medical crate to the grandson, read the doctor's instructions aloud twice to the daughter-in-law while the rain drummed on the porch roof, drank one cup of tea she didn't want because refusing tea in this valley was a declaration of war, and answered—lightly, smoothly, with the ease of long practice—the three questions that came her way.

Where was she headed next? Upriver, mail and supplies, back before dark if the pass held.

Was it true she'd flown through the storm last month to reach the Halvorsen boy? It was true she'd flown when there was a reason to fly; storms were just weather, and weather was just the price of living here.

And—this one from a teenage girl who'd been hovering at the edge of the porch with the particular courage of the young—did she live all alone up there, at her airstrip, all by herself?

Felix smiled the smile she kept for exactly this question. It reached her eyes. It always reached her eyes; that was the trick of it, the craftsmanship. "I live with an airplane," she said. "Airplanes are excellent company. They never ask where you've been."

The girl laughed, satisfied, and Felix felt the small familiar victory and the small familiar loss of it, twin sensations, arriving together, as they always did. Another question deflected. Another door closed so smoothly the closer never even heard the latch.

* * *

She lifted out of Kestrel at 1600 with the mail delivered, the plasma working its quiet chemistry in an old woman's veins, and the sky behind her gone the color of wet slate. The pass was still open. She could see it ahead—a notch in the ridge line, streaming cloud like smoke from a chimney—and she could also see, with the professional eye that never entirely slept, that it was closing. The ceiling was dropping. The wind had backed around to the southeast, which in this country meant the real rain was still upstream of her, gathering itself, and would arrive within hours.

She flew home through the first of it.

The last twenty minutes were instrument work, hands and ears and the sixty-foot lie of her altimeter, flying a valley she could not see toward a strip she could not see, on faith and dead reckoning and eleven years of having done exactly this. The rain hit the airframe like thrown gravel. Somewhere below her, invisible, the river was rising over its banks, and somewhere below that, under all that brown water, the coast was doing whatever coasts did when the sky emptied itself onto them—giving things up, taking things away, rearranging the furniture of the shoreline according to rules older than any map.

She broke out of the cloud at four hundred feet, directly over her own strip, having aimed for it and hit it, and landed in a crosswind that tried to wrestle the plane into the alders, and won, and taxied in, and shut down, and sat for a moment in the sudden enormous silence with her hands still on the wheel.

The cabin, when she got to it, was cold and dark and exactly as she'd left it. She lit the lamp, fed the stove, hung her wet coat on its peg where it would drip onto the same spot on the floorboards it always dripped onto. From the shelf she took the tin where she kept the day's tally: one med-flight, one delivery, one life probably extended, zero conversations survived without deflection. She marked the day on the calendar nailed beside the door—a red X among red X's, going back years, a fence made of X's enclosing nothing.

Outside, the rain thickened. The river spoke to itself in the dark, louder than yesterday. Tomorrow, she thought, she'd fly the coastal route with the supply run, check the fuel cache at the tideline, maybe walk the beach below it if the tide was out far enough to allow it—which reminded her: the tide tables pinned above her desk had shown something odd for tomorrow, some anomaly she'd glanced at and dismissed, numbers lower than any she'd seen in eleven years of reading them.

She looked at the pinboard now. Read the figures again. Frowned.

The lowest tide in a generation, the table said. Tomorrow, mid-morning.

Felix stood in the lamplight with her wet coat dripping behind her and felt, without being able to name it, the sensation of a door she had nailed shut long ago beginning, very quietly, to rattle on its hinges.
