## 4. Monsoon Lockdown

The storm did not arrive so much as descend, the way a hand closes over something small.

Felix felt it first in her teeth—a pressure change she'd learned to read before any instrument confirmed it, back when she was still learning to fly and the old pilots called it "the ache." By dawn the barometer had fallen so far the needle seemed embarrassed, and the radio crackled with advisories in three languages, none of them good. The pass at Kessel Gap: closed. The southern corridor over the moraine: closed. The coastal route: closed until further notice, which in monsoon language meant *until we remember you exist*.

She stood on the cabin's narrow veranda with a tin mug going cold in her hands and watched the rain come down in sheets so dense they had texture, like grey velvet being dragged across the world. The airstrip below—her airstrip, six hundred meters of crushed coral and clay she'd graded herself over two seasons—was already standing in water. The drainage ditches she'd cut had surrendered around midnight. The Cessna sat at the far end, tied down triple, looking small and patient and doomed.

Behind her, inside the cabin, a stranger was drying his socks on her stove.

*Don't think about him,* she told herself, and thought about nothing else.

Idris Okonkwo—or whatever his name actually was; botanists did not carry altimeters and soil-core kits and a waterproof notebook full of coordinates that matched, coordinate for coordinate, the charts hidden in her grandmother's journals—had been aboard for two days now. Two days of careful conversation, of questions shaped like small talk, of watching each other across the width of her kitchen like two animals drinking from the same river who both knew the river was too low to hide anything.

The wind arrived an hour later. It came off the sea in a long exhalation that flattened the palms along the shoreline and then, finding the valley's throat, screamed down it. The stilt cabin swayed—not dangerously, not yet, but with a slow creaking rhythm like a ship at anchor, which was appropriate, because by noon Felix's whole world had become a ship: thirty square meters of ironwood floor, a woodstove, a cot, a hammock, shelves of tinned food and medical supplies, and one guest who would not be leaving.

The plane took its damage at half past one.

She heard it even over the storm—that particular hollow *bang* of aluminum struck hard—and she was out the door and down the ladder before Idris could finish saying her name. Rain hit her like a physical argument. She fought her way up the strip, boots sinking to the ankle, and found the damage exactly where she'd known it would be: a sheet of corrugated roofing, torn from the fuel shed, had cartwheeled the length of the field and wrapped itself around the Cessna's port wing like a lover with poor boundaries.

She got the metal off. That was the easy part. Underneath, the wing skin was creased inward in a long ugly smile, and when she pressed it, the aluminum gave with a sound like wet cardboard. Not structural failure—not yet—but a wing that had taken a hit like that needed inspection, rivets checked, possibly a panel replaced, and none of that was happening in this weather, and no parts were coming in through closed passes, and—

And she stood there in the drowning light with her hand flat against the wound in the wing and understood, with a clarity that felt almost peaceful, that she was not flying anywhere for days. Maybe a week. Maybe longer.

The stranger came slogging up the strip behind her, hooded, shouting something she couldn't hear. She didn't turn around. She let the rain have her face for another ten seconds, because the rain couldn't ask questions, and then she turned and shouted back:

"She'll live. Probably. If the hangar roof holds."

He nodded as if he'd heard every word, which meant he'd read her lips or simply decided agreement was safer than truth. They walked back down the flooded strip together, two dark shapes leaning into the wind, and Felix noticed—hated herself for noticing—that he walked the way her grandfather used to walk into weather: slightly forward, weight low, a man accustomed to decks that moved.

* * *

By evening the cabin had become a country with strict borders.

Felix claimed the stove corner, the medical shelf, and the window that faced the airstrip. Idris occupied the table, where his instruments lay arranged with a precision that irritated her precisely because it was so disciplined—the theodolite case closed but squared to the table edge, the notebook shut but within reach, everything positioned like a man ready to pack in ninety seconds. Between them, on the middle shelf where she'd put it without ceremony and without comment, sat the oilcloth bundle.

Her grandmother's journals. His coordinates. The reason they were both pretending to read in a room too small for reading.

The lamp hissed. Rain drummed the iron roof in long rolling volleys, paused, and began again, and between the volleys the silence in the cabin was louder than the storm. Felix turned a page of a month-old supply catalog without seeing it. Across the room, Idris sharpened a knife she hadn't known he carried, slow strokes on a whetstone, the sound threading under the rain like a second pulse.

"You could ask," he said finally.

"Could I."

"About the instruments. About what you found in my kit. You've been circling it for two days." He set the knife down. "I'd rather you asked than waited."

"And I'd rather you were a botanist," Felix said, not looking up. "We're both disappointed."

Something moved through his face—she caught it in the lamplight, quick and complicated. Not guilt exactly. Closer to relief wearing guilt's coat.

"The storm's turned your strip into a canal," he said instead. "Your wing is creased along the main spar line. And the river's come up past the second bend—I could hear it change pitch this afternoon. We're here for a while, Ms. Joubert. However long 'a while' turns out to be." He folded his hands. "So maybe we should decide what we are to each other before the weather decides for us."

Felix closed the catalog. Outside, the wind found a new register, a low moan through the gap beneath the door that made the flame in the lamp lean sideways. She watched it steady itself and thought about all the ways she'd spent ten years making sure no one ever had this conversation with her. Distance was the trick. Distance and weather and a schedule nobody else controlled. She had built a life the way you build a fire in the rain—one dry piece at a time, kept small, kept covered.

And now the rain had gotten under it anyway.

"Eat," she said, standing, because feeding a man was easier than answering him. "Then sleep. Tomorrow we bail out the boat shed and check the fuel drums. Whatever we are to each other, we can both carry buckets."

It wasn't peace. But it was a border both of them agreed to patrol, and in a room that small, that was diplomacy enough.

* * *

Idris woke in the grey hour before dawn to the sound of Felix talking in her sleep.

Not words—he gathered that much quickly, lying still in the hammock with the storm still working the roof above him. It was more a low, urgent murmur, names half-formed, a rhythm that rose and fell like someone reciting something learned young and never forgotten. A prayer, maybe. Or a list. He'd heard survivors talk like that in hospital wards after floods, keeping themselves anchored to the surface of consciousness by recitation.

He should close his eyes. He knew that. Instead he listened, and hated himself mildly, and listened.

The lamp had burned down to a bead. In its last light he could see her at the table—she'd fallen asleep sitting up, cheek on her forearm, the oilcloth bundle open in front of her. One journal lay exposed, its pages swollen with age, and even from across the room he could see the drawings: coastlines, cave mouths rendered in fine cross-hatch, depth soundings in a cramped, confident hand. The same hand, he was nearly certain, that had written the marginal notes in the survey fragments he'd been carrying for six months. The handwriting he'd flown eight thousand kilometers chasing.

*Amara Joubert.* The name had appeared once, in a colonial-era land registry file Meridian's archivists had flagged as irrelevant. Idris had flagged it differently. He'd never told anyone why—hadn't fully admitted it to himself until the skiff went down and the woman who pulled him from the surf turned out to have the same stubborn jawline as the woman in the forty-year-old photograph stapled to the file.

The bundle was right there. Three steps away. He was, after all, a thief of information by profession; Meridian paid him well for exactly this kind of proximity.

He looked at the sleeping woman instead—at the ink stain on her knuckle, at the way her hand rested near the open pages not guarding them but touching them, the way you touch a letter from someone dead—and felt the specific vertigo of a man realizing the thing he came to take has a face.

The lamp died. In the dark, the storm filled the whole world, and Idris lay awake doing arithmetic he didn't like: how many hours until the river peaked, how many days until Meridian's follow-up team stopped waiting politely, and how many lies he had left before he ran out of the ones that mattered.

* * *

The third day, the cabin shrank again.

Rain does that—it takes a space and folds it smaller each hour, until two people who started at opposite ends of a room end up passing each other in a doorway built for one, saying *excuse me* in voices gone hoarse with repetition. Felix bailed the boat shed twice. Idris re-tarred the veranda seams. They ate rice and tinned fish and argued about nothing—the correct way to strop a blade, whether the storm's third-day lull meant a break or a trap—with the intensity of people arguing about everything.

It was during the lull, that false amber calm late in the afternoon when the rain thinned to mist and the light came up strange and golden, that Felix opened the bundle in front of him.

She hadn't planned it. Or she had, and hadn't admitted it. Either way, she untied the oilcloth with hands that were steadier than she expected, laid out three journals and the rolled chart, and said, "You've seen these already. Don't insult us both by pretending otherwise."

Idris went very still. "Felix—"

"The handwriting." Her finger came down on the open page, on the looping, slanted script that had raised the hair on her arms the day the tide gave the cave back. "You recognized it. On the beach, when I showed you the first page—you breathed wrong. People breathe wrong when they've seen a ghost." She looked up. "Whose ghost, Idris?"

Outside, the mist hung motionless over the drowned airstrip. Somewhere downriver, water moved with the patience of something that intended to win.

He didn't answer immediately. He looked at the journals the way a man looks at a bridge he's about to cross knowing it might not hold—weighing, testing, deciding. And Felix realized, with a sensation like the floor tilting, that whatever he said next would either save them both weeks of siege warfare or start a war in a room too small to survive one.

"My employers sent me to find those," he said quietly. "And I'm no longer sure I want to give them to anyone."

The wind returned. It came down the valley like a verdict, slamming the mist flat, and the lamp guttered, and in the stuttering light Felix saw the journals lying open between them—her grandmother's secret, a stranger's confession, and the whole drowned valley holding its breath around the little stilt cabin where the truth had finally run out of places to hide.
