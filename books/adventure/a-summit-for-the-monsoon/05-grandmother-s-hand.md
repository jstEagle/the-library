## 5. Grandmother's Hand

The rain had found a new rhythm by the third night of the lockdown—a long, patient drumming on the cabin roof that sounded less like weather and more like someone reading aloud in a language Felix had forgotten she knew.

She had not opened the journals since hauling them up from the cave.

They sat on the kitchen table where she'd left them, wrapped in the oilcloth she'd used to keep them dry on the climb back, stacked like bricks of something volatile. Three of them. Waterproofed canvas covers gone stiff with salt and age, their spines stitched with waxed thread that had outlasted everything else in that drowned chamber except the stone itself. Beside them sat the lamp, its wick trimmed, its reservoir full—the one luxury she allowed herself, kerosene bought with money she didn't have, because flying by instrument light alone was a kind of loneliness she refused to add to the others.

Idris had retreated to the cot by the window hours ago, or pretended to. She could hear him not sleeping: the small betrayals of a body trying to be still, the creak of the frame when he turned, the careful silence of his breathing. He had asked once—only once—if he could look at the journals, and she had said no so flatly that even the rain seemed to flinch. He hadn't asked again. That restraint was either respect or strategy, and she was too tired to determine which, and too honest with herself to pretend it wasn't both.

Now the cabin was down to her and the lamp and the handwriting she'd spent ten years not thinking about.

Felix poured herself two fingers of the plum brandy Marta traded her every autumn and drank half of it standing up, looking at the stack. The other half she carried to the table like an offering.

*You're being ridiculous,* she told herself. *It's paper. It's dead woman's paper.*

But her hands weren't ridiculous. Her hands were shaking.

She unwrapped the oilcloth.

The smell came first—wax and brine and something older underneath, the mineral breath of the cave itself, as if the pages had absorbed the dark they'd been hidden in. She opened the top journal to its first page and there it was: her grandmother's hand. Odile Joubert, née whatever name she'd carried across whatever border she'd never spoken of, writing in the looping, slanted script that Felix knew from three surviving letters and a recipe card for cassava bread that no one had ever successfully followed.

*Entry the first. For whoever finds this, let it be someone who loves this coast enough to keep its mouth shut.*

Felix laughed—one short, involuntary bark—and then pressed her knuckles to her mouth because it had come out closer to a sob than she'd intended.

* * *

The lamp burned low and steady. Outside, the monsoon worked at the world with the diligence of a sculptor who had all the time in creation. Inside, Felix read.

Her grandmother had been a cartographer. Not the way Felix flew—point to point, fuel stop to fuel stop, the landscape a series of problems to be solved—but truly, obsessively, a maker of maps. The early entries were survey notes: tide tables, cave depths, water temperatures, sketches of rock formations annotated in a shorthand Felix couldn't yet read. Odile had measured the sea cave below the fuel cache with a sounding line and a lantern, over and over, season after season, building up a picture of the interior with the patience of someone who expected the work to matter to someone else someday.

*The second gallery floods at spring tides but drains within six hours. The passage beyond runs dry at neap. I have marked the safe windows in red.*

There were charts folded into the binding, drawn on treated paper that had kept its lines through decades of damp. Felix spread the first one under the lamp and felt the floor tilt.

She knew this coastline. Of course she did—she'd memorized it from the air before she could legally fly solo, every headland and river mouth, every sandbar that shifted with the storms. But this map showed what lay beneath and behind it. A network. Caves connected to caves, some above the tideline, some drowned at high water, passages threaded through the limestone like veins through a wrist. And along the margins, in red ink faded to the color of old blood, her grandmother had marked times. Windows. *Passable 0430–0900, March tides. Passable only by raft, April–May. Never attempt the lower route after heavy rain inland.*

These were not exploration notes. These were schedules.

Felix turned the page and found the entry that changed the shape of the room.

*Today I took the Ferreira children through. Eleven of them, the youngest still nursing. Their mother would not fit through the narrows and stayed behind with the others to wait for the boat. I will not write here what we all know is happening in the valley settlements. Let the records say what records say. But these children went out through the sea gate at dawn and by nightfall they were on open water, and I have written their names in the back of this book so that someone, someday, will know they did not simply vanish.*

Felix turned to the back of the book.

A list. Names in columns, dozens of them, each with a date and a destination rendered in code—initials, mostly, or place-names she half-recognized from ports two hundred kilometers down the coast. Some entries had small marks beside them. A star. A question mark. Once, devastatingly, a small x, and beside it nothing else, because what else was there to write.

She set the journal down. She stood up. She walked to the window and looked out at the rain hammering the flooded airstrip, at the shape of her plane under its tarp, at the black wall of the storm where the mountains should have been, and she breathed until the shaking stopped.

* * *

Idris made tea sometime past midnight without being asked. He set the mug at her elbow and withdrew to his cot without a word, and she registered dimly that this was the most fluent kindness anyone had shown her in years—the kindness of not requiring conversation—and then she picked the journal back up.

The purge, the valley called it, when it called it anything at all. It lived in the settlement the way old injuries live in a body: not discussed, but consulted constantly, in the things people wouldn't say, the surnames that had thinned to single families, the stretch of coastline north of the river mouth where no one built anymore even though the ground was good. Felix had grown up hearing fragments from her mother, who'd heard them from Odile secondhand and delivered them reluctantly, like contraband: soldiers in the mining towns. Lists of names. People who walked into the forest and did not walk out. Boats that left in the night.

She had thought those were stories. The way children think smoke means fire somewhere far away, in a country that doesn't concern them.

But here it was in her grandmother's hand, dated, methodical, furious in its calm. Odile Joubert had run an railroad through the bones of the coast. The sea cave was its central station. The family's fishing boats—her grandfather Rénald's boats, the ones Felix had learned to handle before she learned to ride a bicycle—hadn't all been fishing boats. The fuel cache above the cave mouth, the one Felix had used for years without questioning why her grandmother had insisted it be stocked there, *that exact spot*, in her letters: *keep the cache above the low gallery, Lix, you'll understand when you're older*—

She'd understood nothing. She'd flown medicine and mail over a network of escapes and never once looked down.

*Entry forty-one. The syndicate men came to the house again today asking about tide patterns. I gave them the fisherman's answer, which is that the sea does what it likes. Rénald laughed with them and poured rum and I watched him lie with such ease I nearly loved him anew. They are widening the quarry road in the spring. When they do, the upper entrance will be visible from it. We must move the stores.*

*We.* There had been others. Names surfaced through the entries like bodies rising slowly in deep water: Tomas the schoolteacher, who forged transit papers. Old Béatrice, whose house stood over the upper entrance and who baked bread for every crossing because, Odile wrote, *no one should travel on an empty stomach, least of all toward fear.* Marta's mother—Felix stopped breathing for a moment at that one—Marta's mother, listed as keeper of the valley end of the line, the one who decided who traveled and when, who read the mood of the settlements the way Felix read weather.

The debt Marta spoke of, whenever she spoke of Felix's grandmother at all, with that particular gravity she reserved for the dead—Felix had always assumed it was ordinary gratitude. A loan repaid in loyalty. Now she held the ledger in her hands.

* * *

The lamp guttered once around three in the morning, and Felix fed it more kerosene because stopping was unthinkable now. Sleep was for people whose grandmothers had merely been grandmothers.

The middle journal was darker. Later years—the purge's aftermath, when the boats still ran but the cargo changed. Not refugees now but evidence: documents carried out, testimony carried out, sometimes people who had stayed hidden in the caves for months and emerged thin as herons. Odile's handwriting tightened as the entries progressed, the loops compressing, as if she were rationing ink and feeling alike.

*They ask me why I do not leave with the rest. Because someone must hold the door. A door held is worth ten doors escaped through. Also I am too stubborn to give them the satisfaction of my absence. Tell Lix's mother that the coast remembers. Coasts remember better than people; people want to forget.*

*Lix.* Felix put her palm flat against the page, over the diminutive, as if she could feel the pressure of the pen fifty years gone.

No one had ever explained why her grandmother had taught her the tides before she taught her letters. Why every childhood visit had included walks along the shoreline at odd hours, Odile pointing at rocks and saying *remember this one, remember how it looks at low water, remember.* Why the family stories were full of doors and gates and thresholds and never once, not once, contained a single clear sentence about any of it.

Because a child who knows is a child who can be made to talk. Because Odile had buried the map inside her granddaughter the same way she'd buried the journals in the cave—instructions sealed against discovery, waiting for a tide strong enough to uncover them.

And the tide had come. Fifty years late, unprecedented, freakish—an ocean pulling back farther than any living person had seen, laying the cave mouth bare at the exact moment a stranger with false credentials was walking the beach below it.

Felix looked toward the window, toward the dark where Idris lay listening to his own heartbeat, and felt the cold arrive in her chest and settle there.

He had said he was a botanist. His instruments said otherwise. And now she knew what was at stake—not treasure, not minerals, though perhaps those too—but this: a list of names, a record of what had been done and who had defied it, a document that certain inheritances would very much prefer to remain drowned.

* * *

Dawn came gray and soundless behind the rain, the way it did at the storm's center, as if the sky were holding its breath between accusations. Felix had read all three journals cover to cover. She sat among them now like a woman sitting among the pieces of a dismantled engine, knowing every part, unable yet to see the machine whole.

The last entry of the last journal was short. Undated, or rather, dated with a season instead of a year: *Late rains.*

*I am old and the crossings are done and the young ones think me a strange old woman who talks to the sea. Good. Let them. To whoever of my blood finds this: I did not hide these books from you. I hid them from everyone else. There is a difference, and the difference is you. The valley kept our secrets when keeping them cost something. If you are reading this, the sea has decided it is time to pay that back. Do not pay it alone. And do not pay it in flight.*

*Do not pay it in flight.*

Felix closed the journal. Her eyes were dry, which surprised her; she felt wrung out, scoured, emptied in the specific way that comes not after crying but after something harder—after a story you believed about yourself turns out to have been a room in a much larger house.

She had thought she chose this valley. Ten years of believing it: that she'd landed here by accident, liked the rain, stayed for the work, kept her distance out of temperament. Self-sufficiency. Preference. A woman who simply preferred her own company and a horizon she controlled.

But she had been raised by a ghost with a flight plan. Every instinct she owned—never stay anywhere long, never let them know your face, keep the exits mapped, keep the caches stocked—had been inherited, installed, handed down through her mother like a recessive gene expressing itself a generation late. She hadn't chosen invisibility. She'd been fitted for it, by a woman who'd needed someone, someday, to be able to vanish if the world turned again.

And the world, judging by the surveyor asleep ten feet away, was turning.

Behind her, the cot creaked. Idris was awake—had probably been awake for hours, listening to pages turn, reading her posture the way he apparently read everything. She heard him sit up, heard the deliberate gentleness of his movements, a man deciding whether today was the day he'd ask again.

Felix squared the journals on the table. She looked at the lamp, at the rain, at the gray ghost of her plane under its tarp, and she understood—with the clarity that arrives only after a sleepless night—that the choice her grandmother had actually left her was not whether to protect the cave.

It was whether to finally stop being a hiding place herself.

"Idris," she said, and her voice came out steady, which felt like a small miracle, "make your tea. Then sit down."

A pause. The creak of him standing.

"I'm going to show you something," Felix said, "and then you're going to tell me exactly who sent you."
