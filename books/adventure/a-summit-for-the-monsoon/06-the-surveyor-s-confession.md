## 6. The Surveyor's Confession

The rain had a rhythm Idris could no longer ignore. Three days inside the stilt cabin and he'd learned to hear it the way Felix heard engines — not as noise but as language, a continuous low syllable against the tin roof, punctuated by the deeper grammar of water moving beneath the floorboards. The river under the cabin had risen past its banks sometime in the night. He knew because the sound had changed: the soft percussion on stilts replaced by a hollow rushing, as if the whole structure were a drum struck from below.

Felix sat across the room with her grandmother's journals open on her knees, and she had not looked up in an hour.

He'd been watching her read. Not deliberately — there was nowhere else to look. The cabin was small enough that their lives overlapped like weather fronts: her kettle on his burner ring, his drying socks draped over her wood box, the two of them orbiting the same iron stove while the monsoon pressed its thumb against every seam of the building. She read the way she flew, he was beginning to understand — total attention, nothing wasted, a stillness so complete it seemed almost hostile.

And she was reading *his* handwriting.

Not literally. But the instruments were his — the survey folio he'd carried out of the wrecked skiff, waterproofed in its case, containing six months of magnetometer readings, ground-penetrating radar strips, sediment cores. He'd told her they were botanical samples. He'd told her a great many things, and each lie had been a small stone laid carefully in a wall, and now the wall was between them and he could feel her reading through it, page by page, toward what he'd buried on the other side.

She turned a sheet over. Set it down. Turned another.

"Idris." Her voice was level. That was worse than anger. "What is anomalous mass at grid reference nine-forty?"

The rain filled the silence while he searched for an answer that wasn't the truth.

"It's a density reading," he said finally. "Subsurface."

"I know what it is. I'm asking why you have it." She didn't raise her voice. She didn't need to. The cabin was too small for escape and they both knew it; the question had been circling since the beach, since he'd come half-drowned out of the surf clutching a case that no botanist alive would guard the way he'd guarded that one. "You said you were cataloguing riparian flora. Riparian flora doesn't require a gravimeter. It doesn't require—" she lifted a page, "—borehole logs. There's a borehole log here, Idris. From March. Before the tide ever showed anyone that cave."

Outside, a branch came down somewhere in the dark with a crack like a rifle shot. Neither of them flinched. They were past flinching.

"Your grandmother's charts," he said. "The ones in the cave. Did you look at the margins?"

Her eyes narrowed. "Don't do that. Don't answer my question with yours."

"Because I did," he went on, and heard his own voice go quiet, quieter than he intended, as if the truth had weight and he was setting it down carefully, the way you set down something that might detonate. "I've been in that cave twice before your tide found it. Twice, in February. We entered from the seaward fissure at extreme low water — the same window your tide opened, just narrower. And the margins of those old charts are full of notations. Depths. Bearings. A woman's hand, very precise, correcting the survey lines." He swallowed. "I know that hand now. I've spent three days watching you write supply lists in it."

* * *

He should tell her all of it. He understood that with a clarity that felt almost physical, like cold water closing over the head. The lies had worked when they were load-bearing — when they kept him dry, fed, tolerated in this cabin where she could have put him back out into the surf and no one in the valley would ever have asked where he went. But the journals had changed the arithmetic. He'd seen her face change as she read them, night after night, lamplight moving on her features, grief arriving in her like weather. And last night he'd read over her shoulder — one page, only one, before shame made him look away — and the page had been a list. Names. Dates. A column marked simply *out*, and another marked *safe*.

He knew what those columns meant. He'd spent the last four days telling himself he didn't.

"The company I work for," he began, and stopped, because the sentence had a hundred endings and every one of them was a door closing.

Felix closed the journal on her finger and waited. She was good at waiting. It occurred to him that a woman who lived alone above a drowned valley, who flew into weather other pilots refused, had probably built her whole life out of waiting — waiting for storms to pass, for tides to turn, for people to show themselves. He was being shown. He could feel it happening, the way you feel a fever break.

"Meridian Extraction," he said.

The name dropped into the room and lay there. Rain on the roof. River under the floor. Somewhere in the walls, wind finding a gap and worrying it.

"The mineral survey division. I'm — I was — a senior field surveyor. Contracted eleven months ago to assess the coastal interior for extractable deposits. Chromite, mostly. Some indications of rare-earth mineralization in the basalt formations." He heard himself sliding into report-language, the flat professional cadence that had always protected him, and made himself stop. "That's the version I give at conferences. Here's the other one. Meridian has been buying up failed homesteads along this coast for two years through three shell companies. They've been running seismic surveys from boats that file false fishing manifests. And they've been looking for the cave system since before either of us knew it existed — because the same geology that folded these valleys also concentrated ore bodies, and someone in their Vancouver office noticed that a 1970s provincial survey flagged 'anomalous void structures' in exactly this stretch of coastline and then never followed up."

"Void structures," Felix repeated. Her voice had gone very soft. "You mean the tunnels. The ones my grandmother drew."

"The ones your grandmother *used*." He said it before he could decide whether to say it. "I read the journals, Felix. Last night. One page. I'm sorry — I know it was yours to read first, yours alone, and I took it, and I'll carry that. But I read it, and I know what I saw. And I need you to understand that when I found those records — when I understood what this system of caves actually *was* — everything about my job changed shape."

She hadn't moved. The lamp between them threw his shadow long against the far wall, a tall crooked thing that seemed to be listening harder than he was.

"What records?" she asked.

* * *

Here was the part he had rehearsed and abandoned and rehearsed again, lying awake on the narrow bunk while the rain counted hours he couldn't sleep through. Because the confession wasn't really about Meridian. Meridian was easy. Meridian was a villain with a letterhead, and villains with letterheads let a man off lightly — *they made me do it, I was a small gear, I didn't know.* What was hard was the part where none of that was true. Where he had known. Where knowing had been his job description from the first week.

"In January," he said, "I was given access to the historical land file. Due diligence — that's the term. Before you extract, you establish clean title, and to establish clean title you find out who held the land before, and whether anyone might contest it. Standard practice." His hands had gone still in his lap. He watched them as if they belonged to someone else. "The file covered the purge years. You know the period — the interior clearances, when the government relocated settlements it called irregular. Most of the documentation was routine. Relocation orders, compensation schedules, the usual bureaucratic furniture. But there was a supplementary annex. Field reports from the enforcement officers."

Felix's breath had gone shallow. He could hear it.

"One officer wrote extensively. Detailed reports — he was proud of his work, you could tell, he wanted commendation. In the third report he describes a settlement on this coast — the name won't mean anything to you, it was struck from later maps — and he describes how, over eighteen months, families kept disappearing from it ahead of the removal teams. Whole households, gone in the night. He suspected a network. He never found it." Idris looked up. "But he mapped where he *searched*. Every sea cave, every fissure, every sinkhole within forty kilometers. And his search map, Felix — his search map has gaps. Places he noted as inaccessible or flooded or unremarkable. And those gaps correspond exactly to the corrected bearings in your grandmother's margins."

The lamp flickered. Wind-driven spray hit the window in a long sighing gust, and for a moment the glass ran with moving water and the world outside ceased to exist — no valley, no river, no valley's worth of secrets, just the two of them sealed in lamplight with the dead.

"She moved them through the caves," Felix said. It wasn't a question. Her voice sounded scraped, like metal dragged over rock. "During the purge. My grandmother ran people out through the caves."

"Forty-one names in that journal," Idris said quietly. "I counted."

"You had no right—"

"No. None. And I've had five days to sit with that, and I don't expect it to balance out, ever. But you should know why I'm telling you this instead of—" he gestured vaguely eastward, toward the coast, toward the town where a floatplane would eventually come, toward the life he'd been living until the surf took his skiff apart, "—instead of reporting the find. Which is what I was supposed to do. Which is what I would have done, eight months ago, without losing a night's sleep."

"And what changed?"

The question hung there. He reached for the honest answer and found it already in his mouth, which frightened him more than any lie ever had.

"My grandfather," he said. "His name was Tomas Okonkwo. He came to this country in the clearance years as a contract surveyor — government work, the same program, the same purge. I grew up on his stories. He told them as adventures. Cutting sightlines through forest, blazing trails, *opening up* the interior — that was the phrase everyone used, opening up, as if the land were a locked room and they were doing it a favor." His voice caught on something and he pushed through it. "Last year, going through his papers after my mother died, I found his field notebooks from those years. And in one of them — one entry, dated October of the worst year — he notes assisting an enforcement detachment at a coastal settlement. He notes the number of structures. He notes the weather. And then there's a line I've read maybe four hundred times since. He wrote: *the people were gone before we arrived. The sea keeps its own doors.*"

Felix had gone white. Not dramatically — just a draining, a tide going out behind her eyes.

"He never found the network either," Idris said. "But he stood on this coast, in this rain, looking for the same gaps in the same shoreline. And I have spent eleven months standing where he stood, holding better instruments, working for men who would pour concrete into those caves by Christmas if it made the quarterly numbers." He spread his hands — empty, and wanting her to see that they were empty, that he had nothing left to hold except this. "I told myself for months that I was just gathering data. Data is neutral. Data doesn't choose. But data about a place where a woman hid forty-one human beings from men with rifles isn't neutral, Felix. It's ammunition. And I've been carrying it for them since February, and the only reason it hasn't been fired is that my skiff sank and the monsoon closed the passes and I ended up in the one house in this valley where someone would read those margins and understand them."

Silence. The stove ticked. Under the floor, the river ran high and brown and patient.

"So the anomalous mass," Felix said at last, and her voice was strange, controlled, a pilot's voice talking through turbulence. "Grid nine-forty. That's the main chamber. That's her — that's the big vault where the journals were sealed. You've known since February that the heart of it is directly under my fuel cache."

"Yes."

"And Meridian knows the general location. The coastline. The formation."

"They know the formation. They don't know the entrances. The entrances are the whole game — an unenterable cave system has no extraction value and no heritage claim value either. Without access, it's a line in a report." He hesitated. "Which is why I haven't transmitted since the wreck. My field unit is gone. Everything I collected is in that folio, and the folio is in this room, and as far as Meridian knows, Idris Okonkwo is missing, presumed drowned, along with six months of their most sensitive survey data."

Something moved through her face then — not forgiveness, nothing so warm, but a recalculation. He watched her do it, watch her weigh him the way she'd weigh cargo, shifting load until the aircraft balanced.

"You're a liability to them," she said slowly. "A drowned man whose body might wash up talking."

"I'm a man who burned his own boat," he said. "Yes."

"And if the storm lifts. If a plane comes. If Meridian sends people — and they will, Idris, they'll send people the moment that coastline clears — what do you do? Stand on the beach and tell them the cave is worthless? They'll bring their own surveyors. They'll bring divers. Men like you don't stay lost."

"No," he agreed. "They don't." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, close enough now that the lamp lit both their faces and he could see the exhaustion carved under her eyes, three days of journals and vigilance and a stranger in her house. "Which is why I'm telling you all of it tonight, while the rain still owns the sky. Because when they come — and they'll come fast, Felix, faster than you think, they've got a barge staged two hundred miles south and helicopters on retainer — you're going to need to know exactly what I am and exactly what I've cost you. And I'd rather you hate me with the facts than trust me on credit."

Felix was quiet a long moment. Outside, the storm shifted register, the rain thickening until the windows became solid grey and the cabin felt like the inside of a wave.

"There's a village," she said finally. "Upvalley. Marta's people. My grandmother's journals mention deliveries — medicine, letters, food left at certain cairns. For years. Decades." Her jaw tightened. "If Meridian's file has enforcement reports, it has that village in it. And if that village sits on land Meridian needs to clear—"

"It does," Idris said. "It's in the acquisition schedule. Phase two. I've seen the map."

The color left her face entirely this time, and he understood that he had just handed her the second half of a grief she'd only begun to carry — that the caves weren't just her grandmother's secret, they were a debt the valley was still paying, and the men he'd worked for had the payment scheduled in a spreadsheet.

"How long," she said, "do we have before phase two?"

He did the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do for days. Barge transit. Weather windows. Permit timelines that Meridian's lawyers could accelerate with a phone call.

"If the monsoon breaks early," he said, "three weeks. Maybe less."

Felix looked down at the journal in her lap, at her grandmother's handwriting sleeping under her palm, and when she raised her eyes again there was something in them he recognized from the mirror of his own worst nights — the look of a person realizing that hiding was over, that the flood had already found every door.

"Then we'd better hope," she said, "that my grandfather's boat floats better than your skiff did."
