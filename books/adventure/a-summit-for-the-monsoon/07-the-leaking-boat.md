## 7. The Leaking Boat

The rain had a rhythm by the fourth day of lockdown, and Felix had learned to hate it the way you hate a song stuck in your head—intimately, helplessly, note for note. It came off the ridge in long gray sheets, filled the gutters of the stilt cabin until they sang, and turned the world beyond the windows into something less than a rumor.

Her Cessna sat on the flooded airstrip like a drowned bird, its left wingtip dented where a wind-thrown branch had found it during the worst of it. She'd gone out in the eye of the storm—two hours of relative calm she'd spent ankle-deep in water, checking the cowling, draining the sump, wrapping the wing in tarpaulin and lashing it with more care than she'd ever given anything that wasn't an engine. The damage wasn't fatal. But there would be no flying until the strip dried and she could get parts upriver from the coast, and the coast was a hundred and forty kilometers away through country that was currently trying to drown itself.

Which left the boat.

She stood at the window with her coffee going cold and looked down at the boathouse below the cabin, half-swallowed by the swollen creek. Her grandfather's boat. *The Marisol.* Forty years old if she was a day, cedar over oak, built in a yard that didn't exist anymore by hands that were ash now. She hadn't opened the boathouse doors since the last time she'd needed to disappear by water, three years ago, and even then she'd only taken her out far enough to make sure the engine still turned over before beaching her again and letting the dark have her back.

"You're staring at it like it owes you money."

Idris's voice from the kitchen doorway. He'd been in her cabin six days now and had already developed the habit of narrating her silences, which she resented mostly because he was usually right.

"It does owe me money," she said. "Or it will, once I see what the rot's done."

He came to stand beside her, close enough that she could smell the woodsmoke on him—he'd taken over feeding the stove without being asked, another liberty she hadn't decided whether to forgive—and followed her gaze down to the boathouse roof, green with moss, sagging at one corner.

"That's our way out?"

"That's *a* way out. Whether it's ours depends on what we find under the tarp." She drained the coffee and set the mug down harder than necessary. "Get your boots on. If we're doing this, we do it before the next squall line comes off the ridge."

* * *

The boathouse smelled the way all old boats smell—tar and bilgewater and the sweet mushroom breath of rot—but underneath it, when she hauled open the doors and let the gray light fall across the hull, there was still the ghost of cedar oil, her grandfather's preservative of choice, applied so faithfully in his last years that the boat seemed to remember him better than she did.

He'd been dead eleven years. She'd scattered nothing, held no service, told no one. That was the year she'd started flying the med run full-time, the year she'd understood that grief was just another kind of cargo and the trick was not to look at it too often.

"Well," Idris said.

He crouched at the waterline where the hull met the black creek water, and ran his thumb along a seam that should have been tight caulk and was instead a soft gray groove you could press a fingernail into. Then he moved aft, methodical as a man reading a document, pressing every plank with the heel of his hand, listening. She watched him do it and hated how competent it looked. Botanists, in her experience, did not know to sound a hull the way you'd percuss a chest.

"Garboard strakes are soft," he said. "Both sides, aft of midships. And this—" he tapped a patch near the transom where the wood gave with a wet sigh, "—this is past soft. This is a hole waiting to be honest about itself."

"Rot from sitting," Felix said. "She was never meant to live in the water. My grandfather kept her beached most summers."

"Smart man."

"He had his moments."

Idris glanced back at her, and in the flat light of the boathouse she caught something in his face—not quite amusement, more like the careful neutrality of a man deciding which of his cards to keep face-down. Six days of monsoon confinement had taught her his tells: the way his jaw set when Meridian crossed his mind, the way his hands went quiet when he was calculating, the way he asked questions about her family only ever sideways, never head-on, as though approaching a animal he suspected might bite.

"Can she be made seaworthy?" he asked. "For river work?"

Felix crouched beside him and pressed the transom plank herself. It moved. Not much. But wood that moves is wood that's lying to you.

"For river work? Maybe. We'd need to cut out the bad planks, sister in new ones—I've got some quarter-sawn oak in the shed that might pass. Re-caulk everything we touch. New bilge pump, because the old one's a museum piece. And the engine—" she popped the hatch and peered into the dark throat of the engine bay, where her grandfather's old single-cylinder diesel sat under a fur of dust and mouse droppings, "—needs fuel lines flushed, filters, probably a new impeller. Two days' work if the weather holds. Four if it doesn't."

"And if we don't fix her?"

"Then we wait for the airstrip to dry and hope Meridian doesn't get curious about why their surveyor stopped answering his check-ins." She said it flatly, watching him. It was the first time either of them had named the thing they were both dancing around: that Idris Okonkwo was not simply stranded, he was *missing*, and missing men get searched for.

His jaw did the thing. "Right," he said. "Two days, then. Show me where the tools are."

* * *

They worked through the morning while the rain held off, as if the sky were also curious to see what they'd do.

Felix had repaired boats alone her whole adult life, and she had a system for it, and the system involved not talking. She expected to run the job the same way—her cutting, her fitting, her decisions—and she'd already composed, somewhere in the back of her mind, the speech she'd give Idris about holding the clamp and staying out of the way.

It lasted until the first plank came off.

The rot went deeper than she'd hoped. What should have been two garboard strakes turned into four, and one of them butted against a frame timber that was itself punky at the base, and she knelt in the bilge with the saw in her hand and the rain starting again on the boathouse roof and felt the particular despair of a repair that grows when you look at it—the arithmetic of materials and hours quietly doubling while the sky closes in.

"Frame three's compromised," Idris said. He was standing hip-deep in the shallows outside, holding the hull steady against the current, sleeves rolled, hair plastered to his forehead. He'd waded in without being asked. "You can't sister onto a rotten frame. You'll build strength onto weakness and it'll fail exactly where you don't want it to—in current, loaded."

"I know how frames work."

"I know you do." No apology in it, no flattery either. Just the level tone of a man stating weather. "But you're one pair of hands, and this is a two-person job from here. Laminate a new frame section. I've done it—river boats on the Niger delta, growing up. My uncle kept a ferry running twenty years past its funeral with laminated frames and prayer."

Felix sat back on her heels and looked at him. Water sheeting off his forearms, sawdust already in his hair from the plank she'd cut, absolutely unbothered. Somewhere behind his eyes was a man who worked for a company that wanted to grind this valley into tailings, and here he was knee-deep in her grandfather's creek offering to laminate a frame.

"The oak's in the shed," she said finally. "Green shed, blue door. There's a drawknife on the wall above the bench. Don't take the chisels down, they're—"

"Yours. I figured." A pause, and then, carefully: "Everything here is yours. I've noticed."

She didn't answer that. She handed him the saw instead, handle-first, and the transaction felt heavier than a tool exchange ought to feel, and she told herself it didn't mean anything, and knew she was lying, and got back to work.

* * *

By late afternoon they had the first new frame section laminated and clamped, five layers of oak epoxied into a curve that matched the original almost perfectly, and Felix had to admit—if only to herself, in the privacy of her own skull—that Idris's hands were good. Better than good. He planed a bevel freehand that she'd have jigged, and when she tested it against the hull it kissed the plank line without daylight showing anywhere along its length.

"Where did you actually learn this?" she asked. It came out more suspicious than she intended.

"University boat club, for the formal part. Oxford rowed in clinker eights when I was there—old boats, always breaking." He wiped his palms on his thighs. "And the informal part, my uncle's ferryyard, like I said. Lagos side. I spent every school holiday from age nine sanding bottoms. You learn a hull the way you learn a language. By immersion."

"A language," Felix repeated. She fitted the frame section into place and drove the first screw home, and the sound of it—oak biting into old cedar, solid, real—did something small and warm in her chest that she refused to examine. "That's a generous word for caulking seams."

"Caulking seams is grammar. Everything else is vocabulary." He smiled then, briefly, and it changed his whole face, and she looked away faster than she needed to.

They fell into a rhythm after that, and the rhythm was the thing she hadn't planned for. She'd braced herself for negotiation—for the push and pull of two people who distrusted each other sharing a workspace—but labor turned out to be its own treaty. He held; she fastened. She mixed epoxy; he laid it. When the light failed they hung a work lamp from the ridge beam and kept going, and the boathouse filled with the smell of oak shavings and marine glue and the sound of the creek rising, and Felix realized she had not spoken this many consecutive words to another human being in—she counted, and stopped counting, because the number embarrassed her.

At some point he started humming. Low, absent, something modal and minor that she didn't recognize. She found herself matching it without meaning to, a third above, and when he noticed he didn't say anything, just adjusted his key to hers, and they hummed at each other over a rotten boat in the rain like idiots, and she let it happen because stopping it would have required acknowledging it.

* * *

They broke at dusk for food—rice and tinned fish and the last of the good tea—and ate in the boathouse doorway with their boots in the shallows, watching the water climb the beach stones. The journals lay between them in their wax-sealed oilcloth, because Felix carried them everywhere now, even ten meters from the cabin. She'd stopped pretending otherwise.

"She used linseed and beeswax on this deck," Idris said suddenly, nodding at the boat. "Your grandmother. The recipe's in the third journal—page forty-something. She wrote it like a letter. *For the Marisol, spring maintenance, so the wood drinks before the sun takes.*"

Felix went still. "You've been reading them."

"I read everything. Occupational hazard." He turned the tin cup in his hands. "I wasn't prying. I was looking for chart references, for the cave system, and the maintenance notes are interleaved with everything else. She wrote about the boat the way she wrote about the caves. Like they were both alive and both keeping secrets."

"They were."

"Was she here? Your grandmother? On this creek?"

Felix looked out at the water instead of at him. The rain had softened to mist, and the far bank was dissolving into gray, and somewhere upstream a frog was testing its voice against the season.

"She grew up here. Before the purge, before everything. My grandfather built this boathouse for her as a wedding gift, and she kept the boat after he died, and then I kept it after she died, and none of us ever explained to anyone else why a family that lived inland needed a boat hidden below a cabin in a valley with one road." She heard her own voice go dry and careful, the voice she used for deflecting passengers who asked too many questions at altitude. "We're a family of exits, Idris. Boats, planes, caves. Always knowing which way out."

"And now you're teaching me the exits."

The mist drifted between them. Felix turned her cup slowly, watching the tea leaves settle.

"I'm teaching you how to fix a boat," she said. "The exits are extra credit."

He laughed—a short, surprised sound, genuine—and something in her chest loosened a fraction of a degree that she pretended not to notice.

* * *

They worked two more hours by lamplight, and by the time the second squall line rolled off the ridge they had three new frame sections in, both garboard strakes replaced, and the transom hole cut back to clean wood and battened over pending a proper patch in the morning. The *Marisol* sat on her cradle looking half-mummified, half-reborn, fresh pale oak glowing against the old gray cedar like new bone in an old body.

Felix stood back and wiped her hands and felt the specific exhaustion that only comes from good work—the kind that sleeps well.

"She'll float," Idris said. Not a question. He'd earned the right to state it.

"She'll float. Whether she'll survive the river gorge in flood season is a different exam." Felix looked at him—at the epoxy crusted in his eyebrows, the blisters forming on his palms despite her spare gloves, the way he was already coiling the lamp cord without being asked—and felt the strange vertigo of realizing she'd stopped watching him for tells. Somewhere in the last fourteen hours, suspicion had quietly put down tools and walked off the job, and she hadn't marked the hour.

"Don't get comfortable," she said, because someone had to say something.

"Too late," he said mildly, and ducked out into the rain toward the cabin, and she stood alone in the boathouse a moment longer, listening to the creek rise against the hull of her grandfather's boat, newly patched, newly seaworthy, carrying strangers' hands in its grain now alongside the family's.

Then, from the direction of the cabin, Idris's voice, sharp with a different register entirely:

"Felix. You need to see this. There's a light coming up the creek—and it's not a fisherman."
