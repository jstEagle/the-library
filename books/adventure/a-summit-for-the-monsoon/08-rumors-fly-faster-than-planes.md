## 8. Rumors Fly Faster Than Planes

Marta Okonkwo-Devi had lived long enough in the settlement to know that news traveled by three routes: the river, the radio, and the mouths of women who had nothing better to do while the rain came down sideways. The river was fastest this time of year, swollen and brown and carrying everything downstream—driftwood, drowned saplings, the occasional upturned skiff, and now, apparently, a story.

It arrived on a Tuesday, in the mouth of Ansel's youngest daughter, who had been out checking eel traps near the point and come back breathless with her hair plastered to her skull like kelp.

"There's strangers at the old cave," she said, dripping on Marta's doorstep, not waiting to be invited in because nobody waited in this weather. "Two men in a gray boat with an outboard too clean for this water. They had lights inside the cave. Big ones. And they were measuring."

"Measuring," Marta repeated.

"With tape. And one of them had a—" the girl's hands shaped something boxy and important in the air, "—a machine that beeped. Papa says it's how they look for metal under the ground. Papa says that's what they were doing at Kestrel's landing strip last month too, only then they said they were birdwatchers."

Birdwatchers. In monsoon season. Marta poured the girl a cup of ginger tea she didn't ask for and sent her home with a heel of bread, and stood a long time at her window afterward watching the rain stitch itself into the harbor.

The cave below Felix Joubert's fuel cache had been sealed within living memory—or so everyone had agreed to believe. The tide took it back twice a century, maybe, when the moon pulled hard enough and the sea forgot its manners, and every time it did, the elders made sure the story went around: unstable rock, dangerous currents, nothing in there but bat guano and bad air. Children were told it collapsed in the quake of '61. Fishermen were told it flooded without warning. Surveyors, when they ever came sniffing—and they came, once or twice a decade, men with clipboards and government letterhead—were told all of these things at once, with such overlapping conviction that they usually gave up and went back downcoast.

Because the cave was not empty. Marta knew what was in there. She had known since she was nineteen years old and half-dead with fever, carried through those passages in the dark by a woman whose hands smelled of lamp oil and salt, hidden in a dry chamber behind a false wall while soldiers walked the beach above asking questions about runaways. Three nights. A woman named Odile Joubert had sat with her in the dark and fed her sips of broth and never once asked where Marta had come from or what she was running from, because asking questions was how people got killed, and Odile Joubert had understood that the way bedrock understands weight.

The debt was fifty years old. Debts like that don't gather interest; they gather silence. But they come due all the same, usually at the worst possible hour.

* * *

The gray boat arrived in the settlement proper on Thursday, and by then Marta had already spent two days doing what she did best, which was listening.

She heard it from the net-menders first: the strangers had gone up to Kestrel Strip, walked the whole length of the flooded runway in their rubber boots, taken photographs of the hangar. She heard it from the postmistress, who heard it from the fuel depot man: they'd asked about flight schedules, about who flew in and out, about "the pilot." Not *a* pilot. *The* pilot, as if there were only one, as if Felix Joubert were a landmark rather than a woman. She heard it from Ansel himself, who'd shared his table with them because refusing hospitality in this valley was a sin older than any church: they were polite, well-funded, tired-looking. They called themselves an environmental assessment firm. They had cards. The cards said MERIDIAN in small silver letters, and beneath it, in smaller letters still, *Resource Stewardship Division*, which was the kind of phrase that meant exactly the opposite of what it said, the way a fisherman's "just one more cast" meant he'd be out until dark.

And they had asked, casually, over Ansel's stew, whether anyone knew anything about "historic coastal structures." Whether any families had kept papers. Old charts. Journals. Anything from before the purge years.

Ansel had shrugged the way the valley taught its sons to shrug—shoulders loose, eyes empty, the shrug that said *I am a simple man surrounded by rain*. He'd fed them seconds and sent them off toward the head of the inlet, and then he'd walked straight to Marta's kitchen, dripping, and said one word:

"Marta."

"I know," she said.

"They're going up the creek next. Toward her place."

"I know."

He looked at her across the table, this man she'd known since he was a boy stealing apples off her roof, and his face had the particular misery of a man caught between two loyalties he couldn't name aloud. "You should warn her."

"She has a radio."

"You know what I mean." He turned his cup in its saucer, around and around. "Fifty years, Marta. You think I don't remember who pulled my mother's family out? You think any of us forget?" He looked up. "But she won't listen to me. She barely listens to anyone. She'll listen to you, or she'll listen to no one, and if it's no one—" He didn't finish it. Outside, the rain filled the silence for him, drumming on the roof like impatient fingers.

* * *

The problem, as Marta saw it, was that you could not simply walk up to Felix Joubert and say *the men in the gray boat are coming, hide your grandmother's journals*. For one thing, Felix would want to know how Marta knew what journals were worth hiding, and that door had been locked from both sides for half a century. For another thing, Felix Joubert received warnings the way stone received water—all surface, nothing soaking in. Ten years of deflection had made her smooth as river glass. You could tell her a storm was coming and she'd thank you politely and keep flying into it, because flying into things was the only intimacy she trusted.

No. It had to be coded. It had to be in the old language, the one Odile had used, the one that sounded like nothing.

Marta sat down at her kitchen table with a sheet of paper and thought about the winter of '58, which she remembered only secondhand, through Odile's own telling, one night when the fever had broken and they'd both been too raw to sleep. Odile had described the signal system she and her husband had rigged along the coast: a lantern hung in a particular tree meant *soldiers on the road*. A blue cloth on the jetty meant *safe passage tonight*. And for the messages that had to travel farther than a lantern could reach, they'd used the supply orders themselves—the grocery lists, the medicine requisitions, the mundane paperwork of a remote trading post. Certain items, ordered in certain combinations, meant certain things. *Send flour and lamp oil* meant *come by night*. *Send rope, no flour* meant *stay away, we are watched*. Anyone intercepting the order saw only commerce. Only the family read the fear between the lines.

Felix ran the supply run now. Every week, sometimes twice, her plane came down on whatever strip wasn't flooded, and the settlement's orders went up with her, and Marta wrote half of them herself because she was the one who kept the collective's accounts. Which meant—

Which meant the channel was already open. Had been open for ten years. Marta just had never had anything to say in it.

She took out the current order form and looked at it a long time. Then she began, in her careful schoolteacher's hand, to write a list of supplies the settlement did not need.

* * *

She almost didn't send it. That was the truth of it, and she was old enough to be honest with herself about the shape of her own cowardice. Because sending the message meant choosing. It meant stepping out of fifty years of careful neutrality—the elders' great unspoken pact, *we owe the Jouberts everything and we will repay it by never speaking of it*—and into open motion. Meridian's men were polite and well-funded and patient, and patience was a weapon the valley had never learned to parry; you could outlast a storm, but not a survey. If Marta's handwriting ended up in some corporate file marked *persons of interest*, it wouldn't be her alone who paid. It would be Ansel's daughters. It would be the whole slow web of families who had survived the purge years precisely by being invisible.

But then, on Friday evening, the girl came back. Ansel's youngest, breathless again, rain-wrapped, standing on the step with her eyes too wide.

"They came back through the village," she said. "The gray boat men. They stopped at the jetty and one of them was talking on a satellite phone and I heard him say—" she frowned, concentrating, a child handling words too heavy for her, "—he said 'the pilot's plane is grounded anyway, we have until the weather breaks.' And the other one laughed and said something about 'the old woman's chart.' They didn't see me. I was behind the crab pots."

*The old woman's chart.*

Marta closed her eyes. Somewhere up the creek, in a stilt cabin with a leaking boat tied beneath it, a woman who had spent a decade being no one was sitting with her grandmother's handwriting spread out on a table, and she did not know that the net was drawing closed around her, that the weather was not her ally but merely a delay, that when the sky finally cleared it would clear for the hunters as surely as for the hunted.

There was no more room for cowardice. There was barely room for the night.

* * *

Marta could not go up the creek herself—that would be visible, and visible was fatal—but the message could go by water, and water had always kept the valley's secrets. Her nephew Tomas ran a fish trap line up past the fork of the creek, three nights out of five, and Tomas had been raised on the same silences as everyone else, and Tomas owed her the kind of favor that doesn't get discussed.

She found him mending floats under his tarp and told him only what he needed: take this packet, leave it in the split cedar at the third bend, the one with the red paint, the cache Felix used for mail drops when the strip was flooded. Don't speak to her. Don't be seen. Come back the long way.

"What is it?" he asked, weighing the oilcloth packet in his palm.

"A grocery list," Marta said, and let him make of that what he would. He was a good boy. He didn't ask twice.

Inside the oilcloth, folded twice, was the order form. To anyone who opened it—a customs man, a curious corporate clerk—it read as the settlement's weekly requisition, unremarkable except for its oddities: *flour, lamp oil, rope, candles, salt pork.* No flour had been requested in years; the collective ground its own. Lamp oil was redundant in a village wired for generator power. Rope and candles together appeared in no legitimate order Marta had ever written. But to a woman raised on Odile Joubert's stories—even a woman who claimed she'd forgotten them, even a woman who had spent ten years pretending the past was a country she'd emigrated from—those four items in that sequence said only one thing, in the old grammar, plain as a bell:

*They are coming for you. You are watched. Move what matters, and move it tonight.*

And beneath the list, because Marta was old enough to have earned one indulgence, she had added a single line in the margin, not code at all, just her own hand, the hand that had signed the settlement's accounts for thirty years, the hand Odile had once held across a fever:

*Some debts you pay forward. Go carefully, child.*

* * *

Tomas left at dusk, running the creek with his engine muffled by a blanket over the cowling, the way his father had taught him, the way someone had taught his father. Marta stood on her porch and watched the last gray light go off the water and listened to the rain settle in for the night, and she thought about Odile Joubert, dead eleven years now, buried up the hill under a stone that bore only her name and the dates, because even in death the family had practiced discretion. She thought about how strange inheritance was—how it skipped through blood like lightning through wire, striking where it pleased. Odile had given Marta her life in a dark cave and never asked for thanks. Now Marta was giving it back in the form of a grocery list, and the interest on fifty years was a single line of ink traveling up a black creek in the bottom of a boy's boat.

It would be enough. It had to be enough. Because tomorrow the gray boat would start up the creek in earnest, and the men aboard it would smile their funded smiles and ask their patient questions, and somewhere ahead of them a light would burn in a stilt cabin window, and either a woman who had spent ten years invisible would finally understand that invisibility was over—or she wouldn't, and everything Odile had built in the dark would be measured, taped, catalogued, and sold.

Marta went inside and took down the radio handset, though there was no one to call. She held it anyway, the way you hold a talisman, and listened to static breathe.

Up the creek, faint through the rain, so faint she might have invented it: an engine, running quiet, running fast, heading home.
