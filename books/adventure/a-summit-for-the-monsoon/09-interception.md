## 9. Interception

The rain had a rhythm to it that Felix had learned the way other people learned their own heartbeats—when it thickened, when it thinned, when it went quiet in that particular held-breath way that meant the sky was reloading. She was forty minutes out of the settlement, running the mail and med run down the coast toward Halfway Point, when the rhythm broke.

Not the weather. The radio.

"Felix Joubert." The voice came through crisp and wrong, too clean for the band, the accent polished somewhere far from any coast she knew. "This is the vessel *Cormorant*, anchored at your destination. We'd appreciate it if you'd hold your approach until we've spoken."

She checked her fuel gauge out of habit, as if the needle might have an opinion. The *Cormorant*. She knew every working boat on this stretch of water by name and engine-sound, and there was no *Cormorant* among them. Which meant it had come from outside, which meant money, which meant—given the week she'd been having—Meridian.

"Copy," she said, and let her voice flatten into the bored drawl she used with bureaucrats. "Holding."

She did not hold. Not exactly. She adjusted her line south, giving herself three extra miles of gray water to think in, and thought.

They had boarded her once already this season—a customs cutter, routine, two men who'd looked through her medical crates with the enthusiasm of men reading someone else's mail. That had been nothing. This would not be nothing. Marta's warning had come four days ago, folded inside a fish order: *strangers asking after caves, strangers with cameras.* And Idris Okonkwo was still sleeping under her roof, still eating her rice, still wearing one of her grandfather's sweaters like he'd been born into it, and every hour he spent under her roof was an hour she spent lying to herself about what he was.

The *Cormorant* resolved out of the drizzle as she came around the point: a white-hulled survey vessel, too new for these waters, its paint unscuffed, its radar mast bristling like something that had never known a storm. A launch was already in the water beside it, waiting. Of course it was waiting. They'd been watching her frequency.

She set down on the chop off Halfway Point's jetty because refusing would be worse than complying, and because her hands were steadier when they were doing something. By the time she'd tied off, the launch was alongside, and three people were climbing up onto her pontoons with the practiced ease of people who boarded small aircraft for a living.

The first was a woman, forties, silver-streaked hair cut short, a rain shell that probably cost more than Felix's annual fuel budget. The second was a big man with a clipboard and the patient face of someone paid to wait. The third stayed half in the launch, and Felix noted that without letting herself be seen noting it—the third one kept his hand near the boat's console, and his eyes never stopped moving across the shoreline, the cave country, the low black teeth of rock where the tide had done its unprecedented work.

"Felix Joubert." The woman smiled like a door opening onto a well-lit room. "Dana Voss. Regional operations, Meridian Extraction. Sorry to ambush you mid-run—we tried to catch you at the strip, but you're a hard woman to pin down."

"I fly when the weather lets me," Felix said. "Weather doesn't make appointments."

"No," Voss agreed pleasantly. "It doesn't. Mind if we take a look at your cargo? Standard procedure for charter operators in active survey zones. There's paperwork if you want it."

There was always paperwork. Paperwork was how companies made intrusion feel like weather—something that simply happened, that you signed for.

"Survey zones," Felix repeated. She leaned against her own fuselage, arms crossed, and made her posture say *I have all day*, which was a lie; the insulin in crate two had a temperature window and she was burning it. "I hadn't heard anything about a survey zone."

"You wouldn't have. It's preliminary." Voss produced a tablet, thumbed through it with the ease of long ownership. "Mineral assessment work. Nothing that touches your operation. But protocol requires us to verify that independent flights aren't carrying equipment that could interfere with our instruments—or, frankly, that could be used to poach data. You'd be surprised what turns up in bush planes."

"I would be," Felix said dryly. "Mostly what turns up is fish, insulin, and gossip."

Voss laughed, a real-sounding laugh, and Felix understood with cold clarity that this woman was good. Not corporate-bad good—actually good. Warm where warmth disarmed, precise where precision intimidated. Somewhere in a Meridian office someone had chosen her for exactly this coastline, exactly this pilot.

"Then this'll be quick." She nodded, and the big man moved past Felix into the hold with a courtesy so complete it was somehow more invasive than grabbing would have been.

Felix watched him work and made herself breathe. Crate one: dressings, sutures, the antibiotics Marta had ordered for old Tomas's chest. Crate two: insulin, packed in wax and wool, and she felt the man's gloved hands pause over it a half-second longer than necessary before moving on. Crate three: mail, oil filters, a child's bicycle disassembled for transport. Crate four—

Crate four was empty except for rope and a tarp, because the journals were not in the plane. The journals were wrapped in waxed canvas under the floorboards of her cabin, under a layer of dried fish that had been marinating since spring, and even so, watching a stranger's hands move through her aircraft, Felix's pulse ticked up into her throat and stayed there.

The man lifted the floor panel over the rear bay—the one that covered the emergency kit, the flare gun, the water purifier—and paused.

"Something?" Voss asked lightly.

"Flares," the man said. "Expired last month."

"I'll replace them," Felix said. "When someone sells me flares. Or does Meridian do that now too?"

Another laugh from Voss, warmer this time, and Felix hated how much skill it took to hate her properly. The panel slid shut. The clipboard man climbed out, shook his head almost imperceptibly at Voss—*nothing*—and Felix let one breath leave her body slowly, through her nose, the way she'd taught herself to breathe during engine-outs.

But Voss wasn't finished. Voss settled against the pontoon beside her, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, and looked out at the water with the expression of a woman sharing a confidence.

"Can I ask you something off the record, Ms. Joubert? Pilot to pilot—you've got more hours in this airspace than anyone alive."

"Than anyone willing to fly it," Felix said.

"Exactly." Voss turned, and her eyes were sharp now, the warmth banked down to coals. "We've been getting anomalous readings. Gravimetric, mostly. Magnetic anomalies along the coastal shelf that don't match the geology. Something big under there—cavern systems, possibly mineralized. Our remote sensing has been... inconsistent. Weather interference, maybe. Or maybe somebody's been in the field ahead of us." She smiled again, gently. "You see everything from up there. Caves exposed at low tide, washouts, anything unusual. Has anything caught your eye?"

And there it was. Not a question about cargo. A question about her eyes.

Felix had been lying since she was nineteen years old. She had lied to border officers and customs agents, to a husband in another country, to the valley itself for ten years, telling them with her silence that she was nobody, from nowhere, that her grandmother's name meant nothing. Lying was a craft like flying was a craft, and she was very, very good at it, and she drew on all of it now.

"Caves," she said, and laughed—a short, dismissive sound, the laugh of a woman with better things to think about. "This whole coast is hollow. Karst and sea-cut rock. My grandfather used to say you could drop a marble anywhere between here and the glacier and it'd find a hole to roll into. Half the old-timers sheltered in them during the big storm of '89." She shrugged. "If your instruments are surprised by holes in limestone, your instruments need recalibrating."

"Not limestone," Voss said softly. "That's the interesting part. The anomalies are in basalt. Basalt doesn't cave like that."

"Then your instruments are broken twice over." Felix pushed off the fuselage and straightened to her full height, and let a little frost into her voice—not guilt, never guilt, just the irritation of a working woman whose time was being eaten. "Look. I've got medicine going soft in this heat and a mail run to finish. If Meridian wants to buy me a coffee and argue geology, I'm at the settlement most Sundays. Otherwise—" she nodded at the crates being re-lashed, at the clipboard man climbing down into the launch— "we're done here?"

Voss studied her for a moment. Three seconds, five. Felix held her gaze the way she held a heading in crosswind: not rigid, just true.

"We're done," Voss said, and the warmth came back like a lamp switched on. "For now. Oh—one more thing." She reached into her jacket and produced a card, holding it out. Plain white. A phone number, nothing else. "If anything *does* catch your eye out there. Anything unusual. We pay finder's fees that would make your fuel bills disappear for a decade."

Felix took the card. That was the trick, she understood—refusing the card would be a statement, and statements were data. She took it, glanced at it, and tucked it into her breast pocket with the expression of a woman filing junk mail.

"I'll keep it with my lottery tickets," she said.

Voss laughed all the way down into the launch.

* * *

Felix sat in the cockpit for a full minute after the *Cormorant*'s wake had flattened, doing nothing but listening to the rain start again on the windscreen and feeling the shape of what had just happened settle over her like wet canvas.

They knew about the anomalies. They had gravimetric data precise enough to know the caverns ran under basalt—which meant they had surveys old or deep enough that *anomalous* was a polite word for *we know exactly what's down there and we want it.* They had a vessel stationed at her destination. They had her name, her route, her schedule. Dana Voss had recited Felix's flight hours back at her like a love letter.

Invisibility was over. That was the thing she made herself look at directly, sitting alone in the dripping cockpit with the expired flare gun under the panel and the card in her pocket like a splinter. For ten years she had survived by being no one—by being the weather-woman, the med-flyer, a fixture without a history, useful and unremarkable and beneath notice. She had told herself it was safety. Watching Voss's launch pull away, Felix admitted what she'd always half-known: it had also been hiding. And hiding only worked while no one was looking.

Someone was looking now. Had been looking for months—Idris had said as much, in his careful, guilty way, before she'd ever pulled him out of the surf. The cave had drawn them the way an open wound draws flies, and the tide that gave it back had given away *her* with it.

Either way, she was visible. Either way, the quiet life was gone. The only question left—the one that turned her fear over and found something harder underneath it, something with an edge—was whether she'd be visible as a woman who'd sold her grandmother's maps for a decade of fuel, or a woman who'd decided what they were worth.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed it with detached interest, then noticed she wasn't afraid anymore, or not only afraid. Under the fear was anger, and under the anger was something steadier, almost calm, the feeling she got at rotation speed when the runway was behind her and the only direction left was up.

She started the engine.

* * *

The flight back was short and ugly, low cloud pressing the coast flat, and Felix flew it on instruments and memory and let her mind work the problem the way her hands worked the yoke—continuously, without drama.

Voss would report back. *Cooperative, careless, clean aircraft.* Good. Let her. Every day Felix bought with blandness was a day for the journals, for the cipher Idris swore he could help crack, for whatever Marta was building in the settlement with her fish orders and her coded warnings. But days were not infinite. The *Cormorant* was anchored at Halfway Point, which meant it could be anchored anywhere by tomorrow. And Voss had said *for now* the way a chess player says *your move.*

She came in over the ridge above her cabin through a gap in the cloud she could have flown blind, dropped onto the flooded grass strip with more conviction than grace, and taxied up to the stilt house through water shin-deep on the wheels.

Idris was on the porch. He'd been watching the sky—she could tell by the way he stood, weight forward, the way a man stands when he's counting seconds on an approach—and when she killed the engine he read her face in about a second and a half, because he was smart, damn him, and getting smarter about her by the day.

"They were there," he said. Not a question.

"Three of them. A woman named Voss who laughs like she means it." Felix climbed out into the rain and stood below the porch steps, water running off her jacket, looking up at him. "They searched the plane. They asked about anomalous survey data. Basalt caverns." She pulled the white card from her pocket and held it up between two fingers. "And they offered me a finder's fee."

Idris went very still. "What did you tell them?"

"That the coast is hollow and their instruments are broken." She climbed the steps, soaked, tired, and more awake than she'd been in ten years. "Which buys us time. Not much. Voss is good, Idris. Better than you, maybe. And she'll be back, and next time she won't ask nicely, because they already know the data exists and they already know someone local can read what their machines can't." She stopped in front of him, close enough to see the rain beading in his lashes, and heard her own voice come out low and level and entirely unfamiliar to her. "So we stop reading those journals like they're a curiosity. Tonight we treat them like a countdown. You said your geology could crack the cipher. Prove it. Because as of this morning, I am officially visible—and I intend to decide what everyone sees."

Idris looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him nod slowly, once, like a man accepting terms.

Inside, on the table, under the lamp, the journals waited in their waxed canvas—her grandmother's hand, her grandmother's lies, her grandmother's map of a country hidden inside the country. Felix hung her jacket to drip, sat down, and opened the first one to the page with the impossible margin notes.

"Now," she said. "Show me what my grandmother didn't want anyone to read."
