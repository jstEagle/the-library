## 10. The Chart That Lies

### Felix

The rain came down like a verdict.

Felix sat at the kitchen table with the journals spread before her in a careful fan, the kerosene lamp turned up so high it hissed, and she could not stop looking at the third chart. It was beautiful. That was the thing that kept snagging her—how beautiful it was, how confident, the coastline drawn in a hand so steady it seemed to have been ruled by something other than a wrist. Her grandmother had drawn these caves the way some people prayed: completely, and with total conviction that someone was listening.

And the charts were wrong.

She had known it the way you know a face in a crowd that shouldn't be there—a wrongness felt before it was understood. The third chart showed the cave network branching inland from the sea mouth, a main gallery running northwest toward the ridge, with side passages marked in that same confident ink. Felix had flown over that ridge a hundred times. She had hiked it in the dry season, before the monsoon owned it. There was no gallery there. The rock didn't permit one. She'd have sworn it on her license.

Which meant her grandmother had lied on paper.

Felix turned the page. Another chart. The same lie, repeated with variations, the way a liar repeats a story and can't keep the details identical. Here the main gallery bent east. Here it forked. Here a passage was marked with a symbol she didn't recognize—a small circle with a line through it, like an eye closed by a blade.

"You've been staring at that page for an hour," Idris said from the doorway. He had a mug of the terrible coffee in each hand, and he set one down near her elbow without asking, which she was beginning to tolerate, which was itself alarming.

"The charts don't match the ground," she said.

He pulled out the opposite chair—the one that still creaked on its bad leg—and sat. He'd been in her cabin nine days now, and the cabin had rearranged itself around him the way water rearranges around a stone: reluctantly, and completely. His instruments were stacked by the window, the survey gear he'd stopped pretending wasn't survey gear. His hands were nicked from the repair work on the boat. He looked tired in a way that had stopped being about the storm.

"Show me," he said.

She turned the chart toward him and put her finger on the phantom gallery. "This passage doesn't exist. I know that ridge. There's a fault zone through there—my grandmother used to tell me about it, actually, when I was small. She said the mountain had a bad joint, like an old hip. Nobody builds a road through it, nobody mines it, nobody walks it in a hard rain." She stopped. Something cold walked down her spine. "She told me that story when I was eight. I thought it was about the mountain being old."

Idris leaned in. Rain drummed the tin roof hard enough to make conversation a lean-in affair, close and conspiratorial. "And the chart puts a major gallery exactly through the fault zone."

"Exactly through it. Where the ground would swallow anything that tried."

"Which is either a very stupid error," Idris said slowly, "or—"

"Or it's the point." Felix sat back. Her heart was doing something she didn't like, quick and bright, the way it did before a storm landing when the wind shear was bad and she had to fly it anyway. "She wasn't mapping the caves. She was mapping the *lie about* the caves. Anyone who took these charts at face value would walk straight into the fault and disappear."

"Anyone who took them at face value," Idris repeated. His eyes had gone sharp, the surveyor's look, the one that measured everything including her. "Including a mining company."

"Including a mining company," Felix said, and heard the silence between them fill up with everything they weren't saying about Meridian Extraction and the survey data Idris had been sent to find.

* * *

### Idris

Idris had spent eleven years reading ground for a living, and he had never once considered that a map could be a weapon until he sat in a stilt cabin above a drowned valley, holding a dead woman's ink against the memory of her granddaughter's voice, and understood.

The realization rearranged him. He thought of his own survey sheets back in Meridian's field office—neat, laminated, true. He thought of the anomaly his magnetometer had flagged near the sea cave three months ago, the reading he'd logged and then, in a fit of something he hadn't named, under-described in his report. He thought of how easily a company reads a map. How greedily. How a chart that promised a gallery through unstable rock would send Meridian's engineers to spend a season and a fortune drilling a fault zone, and how the fault would give them nothing but crushed equipment and, if they were stubborn, bodies.

The old woman had built a trap out of paper. It had been sitting in a sea cave for forty years, waiting.

"Look at the symbols," Felix said. She was leaning over the chart again, hair pushed back, lamplight catching the fine bones of her face. She had a way of disappearing into concentration that he recognized from himself and distrusted in her, because it meant she was somewhere he couldn't follow. "This one—the closed eye. It shows up eleven times. Always on the false passages. Never on anything I can verify."

"May I?" He held out his hand for the journal and she gave it to him, and he was careful with it, the way you're careful with something that isn't yours and never will be. The paper was waxed linen, dense and waterproofed, the ink iron-gall gone brown at the edges. The handwriting was small and upright and ruthless in its clarity.

He turned pages. The closed-eye symbol recurred, always at the head of a passage that led nowhere or into danger. And then, near the back, a different mark: a small open circle, drawn with a lighter hand, almost shy. It appeared three times, always beside a passage that Felix's own memory of the terrain could confirm as real.

"Open eye and closed eye," Idris said. "True passage, false passage."

"But why code it at all? If she wanted her family to find the real route—"

"Because it wasn't for family." He heard his own voice go quiet. "Felix. These journals were hidden in a sea cave behind a tide-dependent entrance. She waterproofed them. She drew them so a stranger would be led into a fault zone and a friend would be led home. She wasn't writing for you. She was writing for whoever came looking—anyone. And she wanted to decide, in advance, what they'd find."

Felix had gone very still. Outside, the rain shifted key, dropping from the steady drum of the afternoon into the lower, heavier register that meant the next front was loading itself over the ridge. "The purge," she said. "The people she moved through these caves. If the soldiers had found the journals—"

"They'd have found a map that killed them." He set the journal down. "Your grandmother didn't hide a map. She hid a minefield."

* * *

### Felix

The word *minefield* sat in the cabin with them while the storm built, and Felix found she couldn't look away from it.

She got up, mostly to move, and stood at the window where the world was a gray blur with the drowned airstrip somewhere beneath it. Her plane sat out there with a bent propeller blade and a wing spar full of river debris, grounded until the water dropped and parts came, and every day it sat there was a day the valley had no mail, no medicine, no her. She thought about the way she'd spent ten years being the only thread stitching that valley to the outside, and how her grandmother had spent her whole life being the opposite—a thread running *under* the valley, hidden, holding things together from below.

Two women. Same valley. Same instinct, turned in opposite directions.

"She used to tell me stories," Felix said, without turning around. "That's what I keep coming back to. The fault-zone story. And one about a river that ran backward under the hill—she said if you ever got lost in the caves, you followed the cold air, because cold air always came from the water. And one about—" She stopped, because the next one arrived in her memory all at once, complete, in her grandmother's voice, with the smell of the woodstove and the rain on the roof of a house that had burned down before Felix turned ten. "One about the two doors. She said every real door in the caves had a sister door that wasn't real, and the way to tell them apart was that the false door was always drawn *better*. More beautiful. Because lies are always more beautiful than the truth. People choose them."

The silence behind her had a texture. When she turned, Idris was looking at her with an expression she hadn't seen on him before—unguarded, almost undone.

"Say that again," he said.

"The false door is always drawn better."

He pulled the third chart back across the table and smoothed it flat with both hands, and Felix watched him look at it not as a surveyor now but as something else—a man reading a letter addressed to him by name. "The phantom gallery," he said. "It's the most detailed feature on the whole chart. Cross-hatched strata. Elevation marks to the decimeter. She spent more ink on the lie than on any truth in the book." He laughed once, short and disbelieving. "I would have drilled it. I'm telling you, if Meridian had put this chart in front of me in February, I'd have flagged it as a priority target by April. It's *gorgeous*."

"And the real passages?"

"Underdrawn. Sketchy. Almost apologetic." He tapped the small open circles. "These are the only marks of quality on the true routes. Everything else is deliberately mediocre. It's—" He shook his head. "It's the best cartographic deception I've ever seen, and she did it with iron-gall ink in a cave, probably by lamplight, probably with people's lives depending on getting it right."

Felix came back to the table. She sat. The lamp hissed. Somewhere under the floor, through the gaps in the boards, she could hear the floodwater moving through the trees, patient as a tenant.

"She raised me on those stories," Felix said. "I thought they were just—her. Old women tell stories. I never once thought they were a *key*." Her voice did something on the last word that she didn't authorize, a crack, and she pressed her lips together and waited it out. "She was teaching me the cipher my whole childhood and I didn't know it. I thought I was being entertained."

Idris was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, his voice was careful in a new way, as if he'd learned that she was load-bearing and he didn't want to find out what happened if he leaned wrong. "You know what I was raised on? Survey manuals. Drill logs. The Meridian method—*the ground is a resource, the resource is a number, the number is a target*." He rubbed a hand over his jaw. "I can read strata in my sleep. I can tell you the load-bearing capacity of that ridge to within a ton. But I can't read her. You can. You're the only person alive who can."

* * *

### Idris

Here was the thing Idris had learned about shame in the last nine days: it didn't fade. It just changed jobs. First it had been about the lie—*botanist*, he'd said, with a straight face, to a woman pulling him out of the surf. Then it had been about the confession, and the look on her face when he said *Meridian* out loud. Now it had mutated into something worse and stranger: shame at the poverty of what he knew, next to the wealth of what she'd been given without knowing it.

He had instruments worth four months of her mail-run income. He had a degree from a good university and a decade of field time on three continents. And none of it—none of it—could open this dead woman's book. Only a childhood full of stories could. Only being loved by someone paranoid and brilliant and afraid.

"Let me help," he said.

Felix looked up from the chart. Her eyes in the lamplight were the color of the floodwater, and about as easy to read.

"Before you say no," he went on, "hear the shape of it. You can tell me which stories map to which symbols. The closed eye, the open eye, whatever comes next—there'll be more, there always is, a cipher this careful doesn't run on two marks. You're the decoder ring. But once we know what's true and what's false, you'll need someone who can verify it against the ground. Strata, drainage, structural integrity—which passages are real *and survivable*, which are real and death traps, where the cold air would actually come from if there's water underneath. That's mine. That's the part I can do." He paused. "And when Meridian's people come back—and they will come back, Felix, I know how they think because I was how they think—what they'll want is these charts. And what they'll get is a map that eats them. But only if we know exactly which parts of it are bait. If we get it wrong, we hand them a weapon."

"Or they hand themselves one," Felix said.

"Only if we're sure." He turned the journal toward her, to the page with the closed-eye symbol, and made himself say the next part plainly, because he'd earned exactly zero right to hedge. "I'm offering because I owe this valley a debt I can't pay in money. My company is going to come here with drills and lawyers and a safety report full of fiction. The only thing standing between them and this ground is a dead woman's ink and two people in a flooded cabin. I'd like to be one of the two people."

The rain filled the pause. Felix studied him for a long time, and he let her, because he'd learned that about her too—she didn't trust words, she trusted watching. She'd watched him bail the boat for six hours in the cold. She'd watched him give back the journal every time she reached for it. She was watching now, and whatever she saw, it was enough, because she nodded once, a small economical motion, and turned the chart so it faced them both.

"All right," she said. "Decoder ring and geologist. But we do it my way. Every passage we verify gets cross-checked against what I've seen from the air. And nothing leaves this cabin—not the charts, not the cipher, not one word of it—until we both understand the whole thing. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"And Idris." She waited until he looked at her. "When we're done, if the real route is what I think it is—if it goes where the stories say it goes—then you're going to have to choose, again, on purpose, with your eyes open. Because a route like that doesn't just connect caves. It connects the coast to the interior, all the way through, under everything Meridian thinks it owns."

He held her gaze. Outside, the storm found its full voice, and the cabin's lamp flame leaned and steadied, leaned and steadied, like a thing deciding.

"I've already chosen," he said. "I'm just catching up."

* * *

### Felix

They worked through the night.

It went like this: Felix would close her eyes and reach for a story—the backward river, the cold air, the two doors, the singing stone her grandmother swore hummed near water, the one about the grandmother-of-all-pools where the cave drank the sky—and she'd say it aloud in her grandmother's cadence, and Idris would go still and listen the way he listened to rock, completely, and then they'd hunt the symbol together through forty years of ink.

The singing stone was a spiral. It appeared beside three passages, all of which, when Idris checked them against the drainage maps in his own instruments' memory, sat above limestone that would ring hollow under a hammer. The grandmother-of-all-pools was a triangle with a dot. The cold air was a wavy line, and it marked, without exception, passages running down toward the buried river—the real spine of the system, the one her grandmother had drawn faintly, almost invisibly, as if ashamed of how much it mattered.

"The false gallery through the fault zone," Idris said at some black hour, his voice sanded down by fatigue. "It's not just bait. Look." He laid the third chart beside the last one, and his blunt finger traveled from the phantom passage to a set of elevation marks on the buried river. "If someone drilled the fault, they'd breach the aquifer. The whole interior drainage. They wouldn't just fail—they'd flood their own workings and half the valley's water table with it." He looked up at her, and there was something almost reverent in his ruined tiredness. "She didn't just hide the route. She built a punishment into the lie. Anyone who comes here with greed instead of need drowns in it."

Felix sat back and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. Behind her eyelids: lamplight, a woodstove, a voice like smoke. *Lies are always more beautiful than the truth, little bird. Remember that when someone shows you something too good.*

She had thought it was a saying. It was a manual.

When she lowered her hands, Idris had turned to the final page of the final journal—the one she'd been avoiding, the one she'd been avoiding so obviously that he'd stopped pretending not to notice. He didn't touch it. He just looked at her, and then at it, and waited.

The page held a single symbol, larger than all the others, drawn with a hand that had shaken. A closed eye, and beneath it, an open one. And beneath that, in her grandmother's upright script, four words in a language Felix hadn't spoken since she was small, and had never once seen written down.

She read them. Her whole body went cold, and then hot.

"What is it?" Idris asked.

Felix looked at the words, and then out the black window, where the storm had begun, very faintly, to change—rain slackening by a single degree, the barometer in her bones ticking upward, the monsoon drawing one enormous breath.

"It's a date," she said. "It's the date the tide opens the low cave next. Three days from now." She swallowed. "And it says: *bring them down to the water*."
