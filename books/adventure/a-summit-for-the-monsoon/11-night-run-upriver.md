## 11. Night Run Upriver

The rain came down like a verdict.

Felix stood at the top of the bank with the last of the gear slung over her shoulder and watched the river that had been a river all her life become something else entirely—something with shoulders and intent. The Kestrel Fork had swallowed its banks two days ago and kept swallowing, brown water braided with foam, whole trees riding the current like drowned men giving up. Where it narrowed below the old ferry landing it ran maybe four meters higher than she'd ever seen it, and it made a sound she'd only heard once before, the year the dam road washed out: a low continuous roar, like the valley clearing its throat to say *no*.

"You're sure about this," Idris said behind her. Not a question. He'd learned in the past week which of his questions were welcome and which weren't, and he asked this one anyway, which meant he thought it mattered more than his welcome.

"No," Felix said. "But we're doing it."

She'd spent the afternoon on the boat's engine with her hands black to the wrist, coaxing the carburetor clean with wire and gasoline and profanity while Idris stitched the last seam of the hull patch under a tarp, his long fingers surprisingly patient with tarred canvas. The boat was her grandfather's—a flat-bottomed work skiff older than either of them, named *Marisol* for a woman nobody in the family would talk about—and she rode low in the water now with fuel drums lashed amidships and the journals sealed in wax and oilcloth packed inside an empty ammo can that had once held, according to the stencil on its side, detonators. Her grandmother would have appreciated the joke. Felix wasn't sure she did.

They launched from the ferry landing because it was the only place the bank dropped clean into deep water, and because Marta's warning had been specific: Meridian's second team had flown into the coastal strip at dusk, six men and a helicopter, asking after a woman pilot and a surveyor traveling together. Asking at the trading post. Asking at the mission. The monsoon grounded everything with wings, but not everything with hulls, and Felix had no intention of being found sitting at her own dock like bait in a trap.

So: upriver, at night, in flood, in a boat held together with canvas and hope.

"Get forward," she said. "Weight forward keeps the bow from stuffing when we hit a wave train. When I say *down*, you get flat on the deck and hold onto anything that doesn't move."

"And if you say nothing?"

"Then keep your head down anyway."

He climbed forward without argument, and she felt something loosen slightly in her chest—not trust, not yet, but the absence of resistance, which in her experience was where trust started.

She backed the throttle down until the propeller bit, and the *Marisol* slid off the landing into the dark.

* * *

Running a flooded river at night is less navigation than negotiation. You cannot see the water; you read it by sound and by the pale smear of foam, by the way current smooths over a submerged bar and boils up around a snag, by the smell of the land sliding past. Felix had done it perhaps a dozen times in ten years, always alone, always for reasons she hadn't examined too closely—medicine runs that couldn't wait for dawn, a woman in labor, a snakebite, once just a letter she'd promised to deliver and couldn't bear to sit on overnight. She knew the Fork the way she knew her own hands. But hands change when they're cold and shaking, and so do rivers.

The first hour was almost kind. The rain fell steady rather than violent, and the channel below the confluence ran wide enough that even in flood it moved as one body instead of many arguments. Felix stood at the tiller with the engine's vibration climbing her arms, and the world reduced itself to the circle of the running light, the white noise of water, and the shape of Idris crouched forward with his back to her, a darker darkness against the dark.

She talked to him because the silence was worse. Not about anything—the route, mostly, narrating hazards aloud so he could hear them coming—but underneath the narration she could feel the other thing pressing to get out, the thing she'd been holding behind her teeth since the cabin, since the journals, since the night she'd told him her real name and her real history in a whiteout on the ice and then pretended it hadn't happened. Trust, she was learning, was not a door you opened. It was a wall you stopped maintaining, one brick at a time, and every brick you stopped maintaining left you colder and lighter at once.

"Snag, right side," she called, and swung wide, and the dead tree went by close enough that she heard water hissing through its branches.

"How far to the narrows?" His voice carried back flattened by rain.

"Two hours if the river lets us. Three if it doesn't."

"What happens at the narrows?"

Felix watched the foam ahead go from scattered to banded, the current stacking itself against something invisible. "It stops negotiating," she said, "and starts dictating."

* * *

The trouble came an hour later, at the bend below the old mission, where the river shouldered against a bluff and turned hard left, and where in ordinary years there was a slow green eddy on the outside that let you swing wide and take the turn with dignity.

There was no eddy tonight. There was a standing wave the size of a church.

She heard it before she saw it—a deeper note under the roar, rhythmic, the sound of a great deal of water falling back into itself—and she cut the throttle to idle and let the current carry them toward the bend while she tried to read what the darkness wouldn't show her. The wave train came into view in pieces: first the crest, ghost-pale with foam, then the trough behind it, then the second wave stacked against the first, and beyond them, where the channel straightened, a chaos of white that might have been rapids or might have been the river simply losing its temper.

"We can't run that," Idris said. He'd come aft without being told, reading her stillness.

"We can't stop either." She pointed the tiller toward the inside of the bend, where the bluff's shadow met the water. "There's a chute along the rock. It'll be fast and it'll be ugly, but it's the only line that doesn't end with us swimming."

"And if the chute's blocked?"

"Then we find out together."

She put the throttle down.

The *Marisol* leapt like something released from a long captivity. The bow rose, slammed, rose again, and the rain turned horizontal in the wind of their own speed. Felix steered by the bluff—the one fixed thing in a world gone liquid—holding the boat in the seam between the rock's dead water and the main current's muscle, and for half a minute it worked, it actually worked, the skiff planing along the shadow of the cliff with the roar building on her left like applause from an audience she didn't want to meet.

Then the log came out of the dark.

It was running the chute with them—she understood that in the single strobe-flash instant the running light caught it, a whole cedar trunk, root ball upstream, surfing the same seam she'd chosen because of course it had chosen the only sane line too—and there was nowhere to go. Inside was rock. Outside was the wave train. Between them and the log were perhaps three seconds and no options, and Felix did the arithmetic of her life in that time: the journals in the ammo can, the man crouched behind her, the plane wrecked on the flooded strip, the cave full of her grandmother's handwriting waiting under the sea for someone to decide what it meant. All of it balanced on a piece of rotting cedar and a woman who had spent ten years making sure no one depended on her.

The log swung. The root ball caught the current wrong and pivoted, sweeping across the chute like a gate closing.

"*Down!*" she screamed, and hauled the tiller hard over, driving the bow straight at the gap between the root ball and the bluff—a gap the width of a coffin, a gap that existed for exactly as long as it took the log to finish turning—and the *Marisol* went in.

What happened next happened in pieces, each one complete and separate, the way catastrophes are generous with detail.

The port gunwale kissed the root ball with a crack like a rifle shot. The boat slewed. The starboard side lifted as the current got under it, and Felix felt the whole world tilt past forty degrees, felt her boots slide on wet decking, felt the tiller wrench in her grip with a force that had opinions about her continued ownership of it. Water came aboard in a single green sheet, cold as confession, and somewhere beneath her the patched seam gave a groan she felt in her teeth.

And Idris moved.

She saw it sideways, upside-down, in the strobing chaos—he'd ignored her command to get down, or obeyed it and then un-obeyed it, and he was up on his knees with both arms locked around the fuel drum lashings, throwing his weight to starboard, into the high side, into the lift, his whole body a counterweight strung between the drum cleat and the gunwale. "Tiller!" he roared. "Give me the tiller and steer!"

"I have the tiller—"

"Steer *out*, Felix! Downstream! Now!"

And here was the thing, the thing she would turn over for years afterward: she did it. With the boat on its beam ends and the log grinding along the hull and every instinct she owned screaming that the only person who got Felix Joubert out of trouble was Felix Joubert, she took direction from a man she'd known eleven days, a man who'd lied to her on a beach, and she steered downstream, into the main current, into the wave train, because he could see the water's angle from forward and she couldn't, because he was right, and because some brick in the wall had quietly come out without her permission.

The *Marisol* hit the first wave bow-on and went airborne.

For a moment they flew—actually flew, engine screaming, propeller clawing air, the whole boat and its cargo and its two soaked passengers suspended above a river that wanted very much to discuss terms—and then the bow came down into the trough and the world became water. It came over the transom, over the sides, everywhere at once, and Felix lost the tiller and found it again, lost her footing and found the deck cleat with her boot, and the second wave hit them quartering and spun them like a leaf, and then—

Then they were through.

The channel straightened. The waves fell away astern, still roaring, still furious, but behind them now, a country they'd left. The *Marisol* wallowed in the calmer water below, shipping the last of what she'd taken, riding low and listing slightly to port where the patch had softened, and the rain came down on all of it, indifferent, thorough, eternal.

Felix realized she was breathing in sobs. Small ones, swallowed, but sobs.

Idris was still on his knees with his arms around the fuel drum, shaking—she could see him shaking, though whether from cold or adrenaline or the memory of what nearly happened she couldn't tell. The root ball had torn a hand-length gash in the port gunwale and left a splintered furrow in the decking an inch from where his knee had been.

"Bail," she managed. "Start bailing. We're taking water."

He found the bucket without a word and began, methodically, rhythmically, throwing the river back into the river, and Felix stood at the tiller with her heart slamming and steered them toward the black shoulder of the far bank, where the current slackened against a gravel shelf, and where—if her mental chart still matched the drowned landscape—an abandoned homestead sat back among the alders, dry-ish, roofless in part, and invisible from the water unless you already knew it was there.

Her grandmother had known it was there. The journal charts marked it with a small symbol, a triangle with a dot, repeated at intervals all the way upriver. Safe houses, the cipher had finally admitted. Places to rest between the coast and the passes.

Places the family had used to move people who couldn't afford to be seen.

* * *

They grounded the boat on the shelf and dragged it up above the waterline by lantern light, both of them hip-deep and gasping, the *Marisol* suddenly weighing what a drowning thing weighs. The gash in the gunwale was cosmetic. The patch on the port seam had peeled at one corner and wept steadily the whole last mile; Felix re-tarred it by feel and by lantern, her hands steadier than she had any right to expect, while Idris bailed the bilge dry and then, without discussing it, began carrying gear up through the alders toward the shape of the homestead.

The house was a ruin that remembered being a house. Two walls still stood to shoulder height, roofed partially with corrugated tin that had rusted to the color of old blood; the other walls had folded outward into the brush decades ago and been claimed by it. But the floor was stone, raised above the flood line, and the corner where the tin still held was dry, and when Felix swept her lantern across the interior she saw, faint as breath on glass, the triangle-and-dot symbol scratched into the door lintel.

Someone had rested here. Many someones. People whose names had been erased from every record except these walls and a waterproofed journal in a sea cave.

Idris set the ammo can down inside the sheltered corner with exaggerated care, the way you set down something alive. Then he looked at her across the lantern light, water still streaming off him, and said nothing, and the nothing was enormous.

"You disobeyed a direct order," Felix said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended.

"You were going to die holding the tiller."

"I had it handled."

"The log had it handled." He wiped his face with a shaking hand. "You couldn't see the angle of the current from the stern. I could. That's all that was—that's all it was." A pause. The rain filled it. "I'm not trying to take the boat from you, Felix. I'm trying to help you keep it."

She stood there with the lantern dripping and her clothes plastered to her body and ten years of self-sufficiency sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone, and she understood that the stone had cracked, sometime between the root ball and the wave train, and that she could feel the water getting in.

"There's coffee," she said finally. "In the green bag. And a spirit stove. If you build a windbreak worth the name, I'll make some."

Something moved through his face—relief, maybe, or the beginning of laughter, strangled at birth by exhaustion. "That sounds," he said, "like the most important job I've ever been given."

"It is. Don't waste it."

While he worked—dragging driftwood and sheet tin into a rough lee against the standing wall—Felix sat on the stone floor with the ammo can between her knees and her palms flat on its lid, feeling the wax-sealed journals inside like a heartbeat. Through the broken wall she could see the river running past, huge and glittering under the lightning, still carrying its trees, still roaring its one long syllable. Somewhere behind them, Meridian's team was bedding down in the settlement, asking their questions, drawing their lines on their maps. Somewhere ahead, upriver and over the moraine, was the registry office and the only version of the future in which any of this mattered.

Between those two points: a ruined safe house, a patched boat, a cracked stone in her chest, and a man building a windbreak because she'd told him to.

"Felix." Idris crouched at the edge of the lantern light, a piece of tin in his hands. "The symbol on the lintel. It's the same one from the charts. Triangle, dot."

"I know."

"Do you know what it means?"

She looked at the scratch marks, decades old, cut by someone who had needed this place to be findable and hidden at the same time, and thought of her grandmother's careful script describing channels and stages of water and *the shelf below the alders will hold a boat in all but the highest flood*—written for a reader who hadn't been born yet, who was sitting right now on the floor of the house her grandmother had described, in the flood her grandmother had predicted, holding the journals her grandmother had sealed.

"It means *rest here*," Felix said. "It means somebody expected us."

Lightning whitewashed the ruin, and in that frozen instant she saw the room whole: the walls, the floor, the shadows of everyone who had ever waited out a storm exactly where she sat. Then the dark came back, and with it, far off downriver, thin but unmistakable, the sound of an engine that wasn't theirs.

Idris went still. "Tell me that's the river."

Felix listened, head tilted, heart dropping like a stone into the flood.

"That," she said, "is a boat."
