## 12. The Valley Keeps Its Dead

The homestead had a name once. Idris found it stenciled above the cold hearth in flaking white paint — *OKONKWO* was not there, of course; nothing here would ever be his — but the letters that *were* there had been worn to ghosts by decades of damp, and he stood a long time trying to read them while the rain came down like a verdict.

Felix had barred the door with an oar. She'd done it without ceremony, the way she did everything, wedging it under the latch and then turning to the room as if the room were a passenger who needed reassurance. The boat was tied below at the collapsed jetty, hull groaning against the pilings with every swell, and somewhere out on the black water that other engine had come and gone — passed them in the dark, or hadn't, neither of them sure enough to move again until dawn.

Now dawn had come, gray as dishwater, and the rain had settled into its long monsoon patience, and Idris sat on a stool that might have been older than both of them combined with his grandmother's handwriting — no. Not his grandmother's. He kept making the error in his own head, and each time it landed in his chest like a swallowed stone.

Felix's grandmother's handwriting. Waterproofed ink on waxed paper, the journals stacked in the dry corner where Felix had slept badly beside them, one hand tucked under her cheek like a child guarding a toy she didn't trust anyone else to hold.

He hadn't meant to read them. That was the thing he would tell himself later, in the years when he told himself many convenient things. He had only meant to look. To check the charts against his own instruments, because checking things was what he was for, because Meridian Extraction paid him — had paid him — to be a man who reduced country to numbers, and old habits died slower than people did.

But the journals were open on the floor where Felix had fallen asleep over them, and the lamplight — Felix's lamp, hoarded kerosene, a flame no bigger than a thumbnail — fell across a page that began:

*Third crossing this month. The Adeyemi family went through with the tide. Seven of them, the youngest still nursing. Marta's mother carried the baby because the mother's hands were too frozen to grip.*

Idris read it twice.

Then he looked up at the stenciled ghost above the hearth, and understood, all at once and completely, whose house this had been.

"Felix," he said quietly.

She woke the way she seemed to do everything — instantly, silently, one hand already finding the knife she kept lashed inside her boot. Her eyes went from him to the journals to the window, cataloguing threats. When she found none, she blinked the sleep away and said, "You're reading my family's mail."

"I'm reading your family's testimony." He turned the page toward her, but she was already looking past it, at the wall above the hearth, and her face did something complicated and closed. "Whose house is this?"

"You know whose house it is."

"I want to hear you say it."

She sat up, pulled her knees in, wrapped her arms around them. Outside, the river ran high and brown and loud, carrying whole trees toward the coast like a conveyor belt for the drowned. "It belonged to the Okoro family," she said. "Before."

"Before what?"

"Before your company needed the valley more than the valley needed itself." She said it flatly, without heat, which somehow made it worse. "Nineteen sixty-one. There was a purge — you'd call it a land consolidation, your reports probably do. The government wanted the interior 'secured' for extraction concessions. Anyone with claims, anyone with history, anyone who wouldn't sign — they were moved. Or they weren't moved, and then they weren't anything anymore."

"And this family?"

"The Okoros ran the river station. Every person who ever went through the caves came through this house first. My grandmother called it the front porch." Felix's mouth twisted around the word. "They lost everything for it. The man who owned this stool was hanged from his own jetty, according to the stories Marta tells. His wife made it to the coast with three children and a waterproof satchel full of maps."

Idris looked down at the journal in his hands. At the entry about the Adeyemi family, seven souls, the youngest still nursing. He thought about the word *concession*. He thought about how many times he had typed it himself, in triplicate, attached to satellite imagery, and never once wondered what it conceded, or from whom, or to what end.

"There's something else," he said, and his voice came out wrong, scraped. "There's something I should have told you on the beach."

Felix watched him with those eyes that gave away nothing except, lately, everything. "I know," she said. "I've known since the cabin. You hold your left wrist when you lie, and you haven't let go of it since we opened the second journal." She nodded at the stack. "So say it now, while the rain's doing the talking for you."

* * *

He told her.

Not the version he'd rehearsed on the beach — the botanist story, the cover, the careful half-truths he'd built like sandbags against exactly this moment. He told her the real architecture of it. Meridian Extraction. Mineral survey, phase two. The anomalous readings in the karst system, the copper and rare-earth signatures running through the cave network like veins through marble. His contract, his bonus structure, the clause about site acquisition support. The word *acquisition*, which he had used forty times in briefing documents without ever letting it mean what it actually meant.

"And your grandfather?" Felix asked, when he ran down. She said it gently, which was almost unbearable.

Idris took out the folded paper he carried the way other men carried photographs — his grandfather's service record, the only document the old man had ever been proud of, the one he'd pressed into fourteen-year-old Idris's hands and said, *this is who we are, boy, men who make the map.* Land clearance operations, 1961–63. Interior rivers division. Commendation for efficiency.

Efficiency.

"Marta's mother carried a nursing baby through a flooded cave because men like my grandfather were efficient," Idris said. "The Okoros hung from their own jetty because my grandfather got a commendation. And I flew into this valley with a company badge in my bag and a survey grid in my laptop, and I stood in that cave and touched those charts and felt—" He stopped. Started again. "I felt like I'd come home. That's the part I can't forgive. It felt like coming home, and it was a burglary."

The rain filled the silence. Down at the jetty the boat knocked against the pilings, patient as a dog.

Felix unfolded herself and crossed the room, and he braced for anger — had earned anger, had budgeted for it — but what she did was take the journal out of his hands, turn back several pages, and set it on his knees.

"Read this one," she said. "All of it. If you're going to carry your grandfather, carry him accurately."

* * *

The entry was dated in the old style, the year written out in words because numbers could be forged. It described a night in the second year of the clearances — a night when the boats came upriver with soldiers and paper, when the Okoro family loaded their own children into the cave route along with eleven strangers, when the grandmother — *A.M.J.*, the initials signed at the bottom of every page, Amara Mireille Joubert, and saying the name even silently felt like opening a sealed room — stayed behind to burn the surface records so the soldiers would find nothing but a fishing household and a bad roof.

*The valley keeps its dead,* the entry ended. *Not in graves. In routes. Every passage we cut, every cache we sank, every name we wrote in wax — these are the graves. Let the companies take the ground. The ground remembers us whether they permit it or not.*

Idris read it four times. On the fourth reading he noticed what Felix must have seen immediately: beneath the final line, in different ink, added later, the handwriting shakier —

*The young surveyor was decent to the children. I have written his name here so that someday someone will know that not every one of them was cruel. O. Okonkwo. God keep him, wherever the map takes him.*

The room tilted. Idris put his hand flat on the floorboards to steady himself against the spin of it, and the floorboards were warm where the lamp stood, and ordinary, and real, and none of it helped.

"That's why you stopped me on the beach," he said. "When I gave my name. You already knew it."

"I knew a name," Felix said. "Names repeat. I spent ten days deciding whether you were him." She crouched across from him, forearms on her knees, close enough that he could see the salt dried in her hair from the night run. "My grandmother forgave a Meridian surveyor in 1962 for being decent to frightened children. I decided I could wait ten days to find out if decency ran in families."

"And does it?"

"I don't know yet," she said. "That's the terrible answer. I don't know yet. But you're here, and you haven't radioed anyone, and last night you bailed water for six hours without being asked, so I'm inclined to run the experiment further." She stood, stretched until her spine cracked, and went to the shuttered window, easing it open a finger's width. Gray light and gray rain. The river. The drowned jetty. "Because here's what I've figured out, Idris, and you can take it or leave it. Your grandfather's name is in those journals twice — once as a commendation in your pocket and once as mercy in hers. Both are true. You get to pick which one you inherit."

* * *

They spent the day the way besieged people spend days: usefully.

Felix re-caulked the boat's seams with pine pitch she melted over the dead hearth, working with a focus Idris was beginning to understand as her native language — competence instead of conversation, labor instead of confession. He inventoried what remained of their supplies, spread on oilcloth: two days of food if they ate like monks, the lamp, the journals wrapped in waxed canvas, his instruments, her knife, one flare, and the chart with its deliberate lies that they were slowly learning to read.

And between tasks, when the rain thickened and there was nothing to do but wait, he read.

He read the whole afternoon. The journals were not one story but hundreds braided together — supply lists and tide tables and, threaded through them, names. Dozens of names. Families moved through the cave network during the purge, and after it, and in the quiet decades since, whenever the valley needed to make someone disappear safely. A midwife fleeing a debt. Two brothers on the run from a concession dispute. A girl pregnant by a company man, taken to the coast and given a new start under a new name. Each entry logged with the same unsentimental precision Idris used in his own field notes, and each ending with some variation of the same sentence: *the valley keeps its dead.*

By late afternoon he had filled six pages of his notebook with transcriptions, and he understood, with the clarity that comes only after the point where understanding can save you anything, what Meridian had sent him to find. Not copper. Not rare earths. They had sent him to find the evidence that the land was empty — that it had always been empty, that no one had ever lived here worth counting. The cave system wasn't an obstacle to their permits. It was the last living archive of everyone their permits had erased.

If the caves were registered as heritage, the permits died. He'd known that abstractly. Now he knew it in his wrists, in the place where he held them when he lied.

"Felix." He said it before he could talk himself out of it. "There's a clause in my contract. Site acquisition support. If I report the cave coordinates — just the coordinates, nothing else — the legal team handles the rest. Eminent claim, historical vacancy, the whole apparatus. Everything in these journals becomes inadmissible. Everything your grandmother buried gets dug up and sold."

She didn't stop working. The pitch knife moved through the seam in a slow, even stroke. "I know," she said. "Marta told me what happens to unregistered land when a company finds it first. Why do you think I'm taking the glacier road instead of the river? The registry office is the only door that locks from our side."

"It's five days' travel and Meridian has boats."

"Meridian has boats," Felix agreed. "And we have a grandmother who moved seven-person families through flooded caves at night with a lamp and a tide table." She finally looked up, and there was no softness in it, only iron with a shine on it. "We'll manage."

* * *

Dusk came early under the storm, the way it did in monsoon season — not falling so much as seeping, the gray deepening by degrees until the room's edges dissolved. Felix lit the lamp and rationed its light to the size of a coin, and in that coin of light they ate half rations and did not speak much, because speaking cost warmth and warmth cost calories and calories were arithmetic now.

Idris lay awake long after Felix's breathing slowed into sleep. The rain on the tin roof had a rhythm he could almost read, and the river spoke underneath it, and somewhere in the walls the house's old timbers ticked and settled like a body remembering its life.

His grandfather had stood in this room. That was the fact he could not put down. Sixty years ago, give or take, a young surveyor named Okonkwo had stood on these same boards, maybe sat on this same stool, had been *decent to the children* — and then had walked out of this valley and back into the machine, and had raised a son, and the son had raised another son, and handed down the service record like an heirloom and said, *this is who we are.*

Men who make the map.

Idris turned his head on the folded jacket that served as his pillow and looked at the journals in their waxed canvas, a shape no larger than a loaf of bread, holding more weight than any cargo he'd ever surveyed. Somewhere behind them, downriver, Meridian's second team was moving. Somewhere ahead, a glacier waited to seal itself for the season. And somewhere in his chest, in the space where his career used to live, a small fire was burning through the last of his excuses, and he found he did not want to put it out.

He was still awake when he heard it.

Not the rain. Not the river. Underneath both, threading up through the floorboards from the direction of the water — a sound he knew professionally, the way Felix knew engines, the way his grandfather had known the sound of villages going quiet.

An anchor chain, paying out.

Someone had stopped in the dark, close, very close, and was tying off for the night at the drowned jetty below the house.
