## 13. Sabotage at the Cache

The cache was a day's paddle upriver from the homestead where they'd sheltered, tucked into the root-cellar hollow beneath a dead cedar that had been falling for thirty years and never quite finished. Felix had built it herself, seven years ago, in the flat gray light of a February morning—planking scavenged from a wrecked fish boat, a tarp of waxed canvas, everything sealed against water because water was the one thing this coast never ran out of. She'd stocked it the way she stocked everything: redundantly. Fuel tablets, spare carburetor parts wrapped in oilcloth, a second radio battery, dried fish, rice in tins, medical kit duplicates, and—the part she never said out loud, not even to herself—a go-bag. Cash in three currencies. A chart with an airstrip marked on it two hundred miles south that appeared on no other map she'd ever filed.

She thought about that bag now as they rounded the last bend and the smell reached them before the sight did.

Smoke. Old smoke, days old, soaked into the wet air until it seemed to come from the river itself, from the mud, from the standing trees.

Idris smelled it too. He didn't say anything. He just shipped his paddle and let the current carry them the last hundred meters while Felix sat very still in the bow and watched the dead cedar resolve out of the mist, black and skeletal, and beneath it—

Nothing. That was the first thing. Not wreckage. Absence. The hollow where her cache had been was a clean-edged scar in the earth, charred at the rim, already greening at the edges with the obscene speed of rainforest recovery. Someone had burned it thoroughly and then, insultingly, neatly. The planking was ash. The tarp was a lace of melted polymer fused to the stones. The oilcloth bundles were gone entirely—not burned, she realized as she waded ashore and stood over the pit, but *taken*. You don't steal from a fire you set unless the fire is theater.

"They wanted us to see it," Idris said behind her. He'd beached the boat and come up without splashing, without sound, and she hated how grateful she was for that competence and hated that she'd started being grateful for things about him at all.

"No," Felix said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her, because nothing inside her was level. "They wanted us to *know*. Seeing is an accident. This—" She nudged a stone with her boot. Underneath, pressed into the cold mud, was a boot print. Deep-heeled, synthetic sole, a tread pattern she recognized because Meridian's field teams all wore the same contract-issue boots; she'd seen them on the men who boarded her plane, who smiled at her with their clipboards and their questions about anomalous survey data. "This is a message."

*We know where you keep things. We know where you'll go. We are patient.*

She crouched at the edge of the pit and made herself inventory the loss the way she inventoried everything: precisely, without flinching. Fuel tablets: gone. Carburetor kit: gone, and with it any realistic hope of getting the Cessna airborne if she ever got back to it and found it whole. Radio battery: gone. Food: gone. Medical kit: gone, and that one landed somewhere under her ribs and stayed there, because there were people up-valley whose insulin schedule did not care about her problems.

And the go-bag. She made herself look for it, made herself feel for its absence the way you probe a socket with your tongue. Gone. Of course gone. They hadn't known what it was—they'd taken everything—but they'd taken it, and the cash, and the chart, and the version of Felix Joubert that had always, always kept one door open onto a runway only she could find.

* * *

She was angry for two days.

Not the hot kind. The hot kind she could have used. This was the cold kind, the kind that lived in her hands and made her movements precise and terrible, the kind that had gotten her through her twenties when precision was all she had. She stripped the boat's remaining stores and repacked them in silence. She re-rigged the bilge pump with a length of hose Idris cannibalized from the engine cooling line. She ate without tasting, slept in ninety-minute shifts, and answered every question he asked with the minimum number of words required by physics.

On the second evening, moored in a side channel with the rain coming down like a verdict, Idris finally said it.

"You're planning something."

"I'm always planning something."

"You're planning something *alone*." He was sitting across the thwart from her, knees nearly touching in the narrow hull, and he didn't move away. "You've been doing the math since the cache. I can hear it. Distance to the coast, food per person, whether you could make better time without my weight in the boat."

Felix looked at him. Rain drummed on the canvas spray hood. Somewhere across the channel a kingfisher made its one dry rattling note and quit, as if even the birds had opinions about the weather.

"If I say yes," she said, "will you argue?"

"Probably."

"Then no." She turned away, toward the black wall of the far bank, and told herself the lie gently, the way you'd feed a dying animal. *There's still the plane. If the plane's intact. There's still the southern strip. There's still—*

But the lie wouldn't hold its shape anymore, and she knew why. It wasn't the burned cache. The cache was a symptom. The disease was simpler and older: Meridian had her name now. Not her alias, not her call sign—her actual history, or enough of it. The men who'd boarded her flight had asked questions with a specificity that meant files. And a woman with files on her cannot disappear. Disappearing requires being nobody, and she had spent ten years being nobody so successfully that she'd almost believed it, and the whole architecture of it had come down in one boarding, one search of her cargo, one clipboard smile.

Run, and they'd follow the paper trail to whatever new nobody she became. Run, and Marta's collective would face Meridian alone with half a claim and no witness. Run, and the journals in their wax-sealed wrapping would be evidence in someone else's case, or ash in someone else's cache.

"It's not about the weight," Idris said quietly. "Is it."

It wasn't a question either. She was beginning to understand that most of what Idris said wasn't. He had the surveyor's habit of stating observations and letting you do the arithmetic.

"My grandmother kept people alive in those caves," Felix said, to the rain, to the dark, to the part of herself that had been arguing since the tide went out. "She wrote it all down knowing what writing it down would cost her if the wrong hands found it. And I spent ten years treating her handwriting like contraband. Like something to be hidden better than she hid it." Her jaw ached; she realized she'd been clenching it. "I had a plan, Idris. I've always had a plan. Fly out alone, land someplace with no records, start over. I've done it before. I'm good at it. It's the one thing I'm best at."

"And now?"

"Now they've seen my face and my cargo manifest and my fuel logs, and the plan is worth exactly as much as that burned tarp." She heard her own voice crack on the last word and despised it, and let it crack anyway, because Idris had earned one crack, maybe. "The plan is dead. It died in a pit under a cedar tree and I wasn't even there to bury it."

Idris was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "My grandfather helped clear a village for your grandmother's valley to be emptied. Did I tell you that?"

"You told me."

"I've been carrying that fact like ballast my whole life without knowing what it was for." He shifted, and in the dimness of the hooded boat his face was mostly shadow and the wet gleam of his eyes. "You get to choose what you do with yours. That's the difference between us, and it's the only one that matters."

Felix didn't answer. But somewhere in the night, between one shift of sleep and the next, she noticed that the argument inside her had stopped. Not won—stopped. Retired. The fantasy of the lone plane climbing out over the coast into clean anonymous sky lay down somewhere in her chest and did not get up again, and what remained was harder and heavier and, God help her, more interesting: the boat, the moraine, the registry office, the collective's name on the claim. A future with witnesses.

* * *

They reached the secondary cache at dawn on the third day—a smaller stash, a barrel lashed in the roots of a fallen spruce, holding the boat's spare parts, a coil of rope, and the emergency transponder Felix had hidden from herself almost as carefully as from everyone else.

The spruce was still standing. That was the first good sign in three days, and she didn't trust it.

She was right not to. The barrel was there. The rope was there. The transponder was there, and when she thumbed its test switch it blinked its healthy green blink, and none of that mattered, because the spare parts were not. The waterproof bag that held the replacement shear pin, the spare propeller, the ignition coil, the gasket set—all of it lifted out light as a promise broken. Empty bag, carefully rezipped. Left behind so she'd know.

Idris checked the ground. Felix checked the waterline. Between them they read the same story: two people, maybe three, arrived by boat within the last day—the mud along the bank was cut by the same deep-heeled prints, and there was a cigarette butt stamped into the muck, filter down, the brand sold in every port town where Meridian kept offices. They'd found the barrel, taken what mattered, left what mocked. A boat with no spare propeller and no shear pin on a river full of submerged logs and glacial silt bars was a boat with a countdown clock in its hull.

"They're herding us," Felix said. She heard her own voice and it was calm again, the cold anger burned down to something else now, something with a floor under it. "Burn the big cache, gut the small one. They don't need to catch us. They just need us slow."

"Then we don't be slow."

"We have no shear pin, Idris."

"We have a river full of wrecks and I have hands." He was already wading toward a half-sunken skiff rotting against the far bank, turning it with his boot, running his fingers along its fittings with the same attention he gave rock cores. "Bronze doesn't rot. Someone, somewhere along this river, has a propeller in their shed and a grievance against the company that wants to dig up their valley. We trade. We beg. We make one."

Felix watched him work and felt the last of the old self-sufficiency loosen its grip—not because she was helpless, but because she was tired of helplessness being the price of solitude. Ten years of being the only one who could fix, fly, patch, and vanish, and every one of those years had been a corridor with one door. This—standing in the shallows while a former enemy salvaged a propeller shaft from a dead man's skiff—was a room with windows.

"All right," she said.

Idris looked up, water to his knees, hair plastered to his forehead.

"All right," she said again, louder, to the river, to the valley, to the grandmother whose handwriting rode in the wax-sealed tube lashed under the thwart. "No more exits. We go through. Registry, claim, collective's name on the line—and if Meridian wants to stop us, they can do it in front of witnesses."

Something moved upstream then—far off, at the limit of sight through the rain-haze: a boat wake, long and purposeful, running the channel they would need to run. An outboard, working hard against the current. Heading not toward them but toward the confluence ahead, the choke point, the place where the river narrowed and every route to the moraine funneled past a single bend.

Felix went cold in a way the rain couldn't account for.

"They're not herding us," she said. "They're ahead of us."
