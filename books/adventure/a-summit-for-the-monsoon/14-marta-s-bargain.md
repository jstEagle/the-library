## 14. Marta's Bargain

Marta Okoye had buried three husbands and one daughter in the valley's red clay, and she had learned from all four funerals the same lesson: grief does not ask permission before it arrives. It simply appears at your door, dripping wet, and you either let it in or spend the rest of your life pretending you didn't hear the knocking.

So when the knock came at her kitchen door an hour before dawn—three soft raps, spaced like a heartbeat trying to steady itself—she was already awake. She had been awake most of the night, in fact, listening to the monsoon work its fingers into the eaves of her house, and thinking about Felix Joubert.

"Come in before the river does," Marta said, and opened the door to a shape of oilskin and exhaustion that resolved itself, in the lamplight, into a woman who looked ten years older than the last time Marta had seen her on the airstrip. Which had been, what—nine days ago? Ten? Time moved strangely during lockdown weeks. The storm made every hour feel like a held breath.

"You got my note," Marta said.

Felix stepped inside, dripping. "Your note said *the old drying shed, when the rain forgets itself.* This isn't the drying shed."

"No. The drying shed has ears this week. Sit." Marta poured tea without asking—strong, bitter, cut with the wild mint that grew along the sluice gates—and pressed the tin cup into Felix's hands until the woman's fingers uncurled enough to take it. Up close, Felix Joubert smelled of kerosene and wet wool and something underneath both, something older: the particular must of paper that had been sealed away from air for a long time and then, suddenly, wasn't.

The journals. She'd brought them. Or some of them.

Marta had known she would. That was why she'd sent the warning in the first place—not only about Meridian's men asking their soft, polite questions at the co-op, but because a debt fifty years old had been sitting in her chest like a stone in a boot, and there were only so many more storms left in her body to carry it.

"You look terrible," Marta told her, settling into the chair across the stove. It was not unkind. In this valley, telling someone they looked terrible was a form of affection; it meant *I am looking at you closely enough to notice.*

"I've been reading," Felix said. Her voice was rough, scraped low. "All night. For three nights."

"The grandmother's books."

Felix's head came up sharply. Rain drummed the roof. Outside, somewhere down the slope, the river spoke in its sleep, that deep continuous mutter it kept even in flood.

"How do you know about—" Felix began.

"I helped her carry them down to the cave." Marta said it plainly, the way you'd say *I helped her bring in the harvest* or *I helped her bury her husband.* "Forty-one years ago. Two trips, because the wax and the oilcloth were heavy and I was younger then but not young. She didn't tell me everything that was in them. But I knew what they were for. I knew why they had to go where the tide could keep them better than any lock could."

Felix stared at her. And Marta watched something move behind those grey eyes—the same look she remembered on the girl's face when she was small and newly arrived, all elbows and silence, watching the valley children play as though play were a language she'd been taught wrong. Suspicion first. Then hunger. Then the fierce, furious effort to hide the hunger, because hiding was the only skill anyone had ever taught her that worked.

"She never told me about you," Felix said finally. "Any of you. About—this. Whatever this is."

"No," Marta agreed. "She wouldn't have. That was the whole point of her."

* * *

The stove ticked as the fire settled. Marta let the silence do its work. She had spent a lifetime learning that with people like Felix—with people who had been taught that words were hooks—you did not pull. You set the hook down on the table between you and waited to see if they would pick it up themselves.

Outside, the rain eased to that fine, hissing drizzle the old-timers called *the monsoon changing its shirt.* Through the one window, the valley was a darkness stitched with grey, the terraces running silver, the forest above them a black wall that breathed.

"My mother came here in the second purge year," Marta said at last. "You know the history they teach in the coastal schools—that it was a land dispute. A boundary adjustment. Resettlement for infrastructure." She smiled, and knew the smile was ugly. "My mother walked eleven days through the pass with me on her back and a cooking pot tied to her belt, because soldiers were burning the valley two ridges over and someone had to survive to remember it. Your grandmother's family took us in. Gave us terrace land nobody was farming. Never asked us to sign anything, never asked us to be grateful loudly. Just—made room."

Felix's cup had stopped halfway to her mouth. She lowered it slowly, as if afraid sudden movement might shatter whatever was happening in the room.

"The caves," Marta went on. "You've read by now what they were. Not treasure. Not mines. A road out. For people like my mother. For people worse off than her. Your grandmother kept the maps because a road out is worthless if you're the only one who knows where it goes—and dangerous if everyone knows. So she wrote it down in a way only the right people could read, and she hid it where the sea could guard it, and she carried the whole weight of it alone for forty years while this valley pretended not to notice her walking to the tideline with bundles under her arms." Marta's voice caught, just slightly, on the last of it. Fifty years, and still. "We owed her silence. We paid it. Every household in this valley paid it, generation after generation, without being asked. You want to know what kind of place raises people like that?"

"Yes," Felix whispered.

"So do I," Marta said. "Because I think you've been looking for it your whole life and nobody ever told you that you were already standing in it."

* * *

Felix set the cup down. Her hands were not quite steady. Marta noted it and said nothing, the way you say nothing about a wound that will close on its own if you don't poke it.

"There are men at the co-op," Felix said. Changing the subject, or maybe not—maybe just showing Marta the fear that was driving her, the way a driver shows you the crack in her windshield. "Two of them. Polite. They bought fuel they didn't need and asked about flight schedules and whether anyone had been 'surveying the coastline.' One of them had a Meridian lanyard he thought was tucked inside his jacket. I saw it when he leaned over the counter."

"They came to my house too," Marta said. "Tuesday. They called themselves environmental assessors. They asked if I knew of any 'heritage features' along the lower tideline." She laughed, short and hard. "Heritage features. My mother's eleven-day walk, reduced to a phrase for a permit application."

"What did you tell them?"

"That I'm old and my memory is a colander." Marta leaned forward. The firelight found the deep lines around her eyes, the silver threading her braids. "But listen to me now, girl, because I'm going to stop being a colander, and when I do, things are going to move fast, and you need to be standing in the right place when they do."

Felix went very still. It was a pilot's stillness, Marta thought—a stillness learned in cockpits, where moving wrong gets you killed.

"The collective," Marta said. "The valley council. The thing the coastal offices call a 'farming cooperative' because it's easier than saying what it actually is. We have held claim to these terraces since before the purge. Before the province existed on paper the way it does now. Our title is ancestral—recognized under the old settlement acts, the ones Meridian's lawyers pray nobody reads anymore. We have never filed it prominently. Do you understand why?"

"Because filing it makes it visible," Felix said slowly. "And visible means contestable."

"Visible means *attackable,*" Marta corrected. "For fifty years we survived by being too poor and too scattered and too unimportant to bother with. A claim nobody knows about is armor made of fog. But fog doesn't stop a syndicate with drilling permits and a survey budget. Meridian has been quietly buying up the coastal parcels for three years—I've watched the deeds change hands at the registry, little purchases, nothing alarming, each one legal. They're building a ring around us. When the ring closes, our fog won't matter. What matters is paper. Old paper. Recognized paper." She nodded at the oilcloth-wrapped bundle at Felix's feet. "Paper like that."

Felix looked down at the journals as though seeing them for the first time—as though until this moment they had been grief, and now they were suddenly ammunition.

"The charts document the cave system," Marta said. "The usage records—your grandmother logged every person who came through, dates, families, destinations. That's not just history, child. That's *continuous occupation and use.* That's testimony. Combined with our ancestral claim and the elders' affidavits, it's enough to file for protected heritage status. Enough to freeze extraction permits before the ring closes. Enough to make this valley permanent instead of provisional." She paused. "There's a registry office in Puerto Cabral. It takes a filing in person. With originals."

"And between here and Puerto Cabral," Felix said, "is a flooded river, a grounded plane, a leaking boat, and a mining company that already burned one of my caches."

"Yes," Marta said. "I know about the cache. I'm sorry. Tomas saw the smoke from the ridge and couldn't get there in time." She let that sit for a moment—the proof that the valley was watching, had always been watching, that Felix's solitude had never been as complete as she believed. Then she leaned forward and took the girl's hands in her own two weathered ones, and felt them flinch, and felt them not pull away.

"Now I'm going to ask you the hard thing," Marta said. "Harder than the boat. Harder than the glacier. Are you listening?"

Felix nodded once.

"Stop hiding." The words came out of Marta like something she'd been carrying uphill. "I don't mean stop being careful. Careful keeps you alive and I need you alive. I mean the other hiding. The one where you fly in, fix our generator, deliver our medicine, and vanish before anyone can learn your middle name. The one where you keep your grandmother's name locked in your throat like contraband. Child, I have watched you do it for ten years. We all have. We let you, because we understood—but the season for letting you is over. If we file that claim, your name goes on it. Your grandmother's name goes on it. The story comes out—the purge, the caves, all of it—and there is no version of events where you stay invisible afterward. Meridian will know exactly who beat them. The valley will know who you are. Everyone will know." Her grip tightened. "I am offering you belonging, Felix Joubert, and I need you to understand that it is not a gift. It is a bargain. Belonging costs visibility. There is no discount."

* * *

The rain returned, harder, as if the sky had been waiting for the question to be asked before it dared answer. Felix pulled her hands free—not rejecting, Marta saw, just needing them back so she could hold her own cup and have something to grip. She stood. Walked to the window. Stood there with her back to the room, a narrow silhouette against the grey, and Marta let her stand, because some decisions need the width of a room to turn around in.

"My grandmother spent her whole life making sure no one knew," Felix said at last, to the window. "That was her inheritance to me. Disappearance. She was good at it. I got good at it. It kept me safe for thirty years." A breath. "And she did it all to protect a valley she never let herself be part of. Isn't that—doesn't that mean something? Doesn't the hiding honor her?"

"It honored her *then,*" Marta said. "Hiding was the tool the moment required. But tools aren't heirlooms, child. You don't owe your grandmother her fear. You owe her the thing she was afraid *for.*" She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and crossed to stand beside Felix at the window. Below them, through the streaming glass, the valley lay in its rain-dark folds—terraces, rooftops, the co-op's tin roof flashing dull silver, the schoolhouse, the drying sheds, the graves on the hillside where Marta's dead slept in the clay. All of it provisional. All of it, until now, unspeakable.

"Look at it," Marta said. "Every roof down there owes something to your grandmother's silence. Mine included. My daughter's grave included. You have been flying over your own inheritance for ten years, treating it like scenery." She turned and looked up into Felix's face—into the exhaustion there, and the terror, and underneath the terror something rawer and more hopeful than either. "I'm not asking you to stop being careful. I'm asking you to come down out of the sky. Once. On purpose. With your name in your mouth."

Felix was quiet for a long time. The rain filled the silence, and the river beneath the rain, and somewhere far off a night bird cried twice and gave up.

"If I say yes," Felix said finally, "Meridian won't stop. They'll come after the claim. After the journals. After—" she gestured vaguely, at the cabin, the plane, the life she'd built out of altitude and anonymity. "After me."

"After us," Marta corrected. "Say it correctly, at least, if you're going to say it."

Something cracked in Felix's face then—not a breaking, Marta thought, but a thawing, ice shifting on a river that had been frozen so long everyone forgot there was water underneath. She looked down at the journals on the floor. She looked out at the valley. Marta watched her do the arithmetic that pilots do—fuel, weather, load, margin—except this time the numbers were names.

"The boat leaves tomorrow night," Felix said. "Tide window at the river mouth, if the storm breaks like the barometer says it will. Idris thinks the hull will hold if we baby it past the shallows." She swallowed. "Come to the cabin in the morning. Bring the elders who'll sign. If we're doing this, we do it before Meridian's next team arrives—they're due within days, Marta. I can feel it. The net's closing."

"Then we run ahead of it," Marta said. She put out her hand, palm open, an old woman's hand with dirt in its creases and fifty years of kept promises in its lines. "Do we have a bargain?"

Felix looked at the hand. Marta watched her look at it—watched her measure the distance the way she probably measured distances every day of her hidden life, the gap between the cockpit and the ground, between the sky she trusted and the earth she didn't.

Then Felix Joubert reached out and took it, and her grip was cold and strong and shaking, and she said, in a voice that hadn't been used in years and creaked like a door opening:

"We have a bargain."

Outside, the rain eased again, and far upriver—though neither of them could have known it yet—a boat with two figures aboard was already loading for the dark water, and the night it would have to outrun was closer than either of them thought.
