## 15. The Glacier Road

### Felix

The moraine announced itself before we saw it—a gray spine of broken stone running up the valley's throat like the backbone of something that had died on its knees. Felix stood at the tree line with her hands jammed in her pockets and studied it the way she'd once studied weather: not as scenery but as an opponent.

"That's the road?" Idris said behind her. He had the boat's bow line over his shoulder and the look of a man who'd been told a joke he hadn't finished understanding yet.

"That's the road." She pointed along the ridge where the ice fell away in blue-white terraces toward the valley floor. "Grandmother called it the Glacier Road in the journals. Everyone who ever left this valley in a hurry went over it. Before the airstrip, before the river was worth navigating—it was this or nothing."

"And now it's this *and* nothing."

"Now it's this or Meridian."

The morning was too beautiful for what they were about to do. The kind of clear, windless cold that made the whole world feel like glass—sunlight hammering off the icefield above, every serac lit up like a lamp, the air so clean it hurt to breathe it. Felix knew what that beauty meant. Beautiful cold up here meant warm nights down-valley, and warm nights meant the glacier was sweating, and a sweating glacier was a clock ticking toward calving season.

Marta's message had come two days ago, carried up by a boy on a borrowed horse, folded into a twist of waxed paper like everything important in this valley traveled: *Registry office closes for the season in eleven days. Ice bridge at the moraine head holds maybe eight. After that, no one crosses until autumn. Come soon or don't come.*

Eight days. Maybe.

They'd spent yesterday breaking camp at the burned cache site, salvaging what the fire had spared, and this morning they'd begun the work Felix had been dreading since Marta's bargain first took shape in her mind: getting a sixteen-foot boat up onto a glacier.

"Tell me again why we're bringing the boat," Idris said.

"Because past the moraine the meltwater channels run into the headwaters, and the headwaters run down to the coast faster than any trail. If we cross on foot and the claim gets contested, we're looking at three weeks of walking back through country Meridian will own by then. With the boat, it's four days downriver." She shrugged, feeling the weight of the pack straps already ghosting across her shoulders. "Also because Grandfather built it, and I'm not leaving it to be someone's firewood."

It wasn't the real reason and they both knew it. The real reason was that the boat was the last thing of hers that hadn't burned, been stolen, or been offered up to strangers. She was hauling it over a glacier because letting go of things had never once gone well for her.

They rigged the sledge from the cache's surviving runners and two lengths of spruce, lashed the hull upside-down atop it, and distributed the journals—sealed in wax, wrapped in oilcloth, split between their packs so that neither of them could lose the whole inheritance alone. Felix caught herself checking Idris's pack straps twice, then a third time, and made herself stop.

"Ready?" he asked.

"No," she said, and started walking.

* * *

### Idris

By noon Idris understood why the valley had kept its secrets so long. The land itself conspired in it.

The lower moraine was a chaos of stones the size of cars, deposited by decades of the glacier's slow indigestion, and every one of them was loose. He learned within the first hour to test each foothold with his weight before committing, to keep his hands free, to listen—to the grind and tick of rock settling under load, to the deeper sounds coming off the icefield above them, groans like whale song played through gravel. The boat on its sledge came apart twice on the scree, and both times they righted it in silence, breathing hard, saying nothing about turning back.

Felix moved through it like water finding its level. She didn't pick a line so much as *know* one, angling between boulders without appearing to look at them, calling out holds—"left foot high, trust the slab"—in a voice flat as a flight radio. He'd watched her fly, watched her read weather, watched her lie to Meridian's men with a smile. But this was different. Up here she wasn't performing competence. She simply had it, the way other people had eye color.

"You've done this before," he said, when they stopped to re-lash the sledge. Sweat had frozen at his hairline. His fingers wouldn't fully close around the knots.

"When I was nineteen. With my grandfather." She pulled the lashings tight with her teeth and one hand. "He said the glacier is honest. It only kills you if you're in a hurry or if you're stupid, and usually those are the same thing."

"Wise man."

"He was a smuggler, Idris. Wisdom was a side effect." But she said it softly, and he heard what lived underneath it—not bitterness anymore, or not only. Something closer to tenderness, still strange on her face, like a language she was relearning.

They gained the ice proper in the late afternoon, where the moraine's rubble gave way to a slope of ancient blue ice dented with melt pools, each one a perfect lens of turquoise holding a piece of sky. It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful. It was also a staircase with a countdown built into it.

Idris knelt at the edge of the largest pool and put his hand in the water. Cold enough to burn—but not glacial cold. Not yet. Meltwater, running *on* the ice instead of beneath it, which meant the sun was doing its work all the way up here.

"The forecast," he said. He'd checked the handheld radio that morning, huddled in the lee of the boat while Felix broke camp. The voice from the coastal station had been cheerful and useless: *unseasonably warm ridge parked over the interior, expect continued mild conditions through the week.* Mild. On a glacier, mild was a weapon.

"I heard it." Felix crouched beside him. In the reflected light her face was all angles and blue. "Warm ridge. No storms forecast."

"That's good, isn't it?"

"For flying, yes. For this—" she slapped her palm on the ice, and the sound rang out flat and dead across the slope, "—no. Storms slow the melt. Sun eats it. Every clear day is a day the bridges get thinner." She stood, shaded her eyes, and looked up-slope to where the ice steepened toward a saddle between two walls of seracs—towers of compressed snow leaning over the route like drunks propping each other up. "We want to be over that saddle in five days. Six at the outside. After that we're gambling with more than weather."

"Calving season."

"Calving season." She said it the way other people said *landslide* or *war*. "When the meltwater undermines the snout, the whole face lets go. You'll hear it from twenty miles away. From here you won't hear anything, because you'll be under it."

They made camp that night in a hollow below the serac wall, the boat overturned beside them as a windbreak, the stove hissing between them like a small tame animal. Idris took first watch because Felix had taken the last three and refused to discuss it. He sat with his back against the hull and watched the light die off the icefield, the blues going violet, then black, then giving way to stars so dense the sky looked bruised.

From somewhere deep in the glacier came a sound—a long, tectonic sigh, ice settling against ice—and he found himself thinking of his grandfather's hands. He'd written about them in his report to Meridian once, early on, some corporate-bonding exercise: *a man who worked the land.* He'd meant it fondly. He understood now that the same hands had helped clear a village out of the valley below this ice, and that the land his grandfather worked had been worked first by people who were removed from it, and that the journals sleeping in his pack contained their names.

The glacier sighed again. Idris pulled his collar up and let the cold hold him accountable.

* * *

### Felix

On the third day they reached the ice bridge, and Felix's stomach dropped through it just looking at the thing.

It spanned a crevasse where the route narrowed between two ridges of ice—a natural arch perhaps ten meters across, formed years ago when a serac fell and welded itself into the gap. In the journals, her grandmother had drawn it with a careful X and a note in her tight, slanted hand: *The Bridge. Cross early season only. Listen first.*

Felix listened.

The morning was silent except for wind and the drip-drip-drip of meltwater, and that was the problem. Drip. Running water. Three days ago there would have been none of it on the surface; now it threaded down the arch's flanks in bright veins, and at its base, where the crevasse yawned away into blue darkness, she could hear it falling—water hitting water, somewhere far below, hollowing the bridge out from inside like a moth in a beam.

"It's thinner than it looks," she said.

"Everything today has been thinner than it looks." Idris set down his end of the sledge rope and came up beside her. He'd changed in the weeks since the beach—harder through the shoulders, quieter in the way of men who had stopped explaining themselves. He studied the bridge with a surveyor's eye, and she watched him do the math she'd already done. "Could we go around?"

"Not without adding two days. We don't have two days." She chewed the inside of her cheek. "One at a time. Boat goes empty, slid across on the rope. Then the packs. Then us, spaced out. No talking on the ice—I want to hear it if it starts to sing."

"Sing?"

"Glacier ice sings when it's about to fail. High notes. Like wire under tension." She met his eyes. "If you hear singing, you run. Don't be polite. Don't finish your step. Run."

They crossed the boat first, easing it over the arch on the rope hand over hand, the hull whispering against ice, and Felix held her breath the entire way as though the bridge could hear her heart. Then the packs, dragged one by one. Then Idris, moving carefully, arms out, each boot placed like a word in a sentence he couldn't afford to get wrong. The ice held. He turned on the far side and gave her a thumbs-up, and she stepped onto the arch herself.

Halfway across, the glacier sang.

It was exactly as she'd described—one thin, rising note, almost musical, coming up through her boots—and her body understood it before her mind did. She ran. Four strides on ice that hummed under her, five, and then the note climbed past hearing and something *cracked*, deep and wet, beneath the arch, and she came off the far end of the bridge as a chunk of the near abutment calved inward and dropped into the dark. The sound it made hitting bottom came up a full second later, enormous and final.

She lay on the ice, chest heaving, staring at the new gap in the bridge's silhouette. Through it: sky.

"Felix." Idris's face above hers, his hands hovering, afraid to touch her. "Are you—"

"Fine." She sat up. Her legs felt like borrowed equipment. Behind her, the bridge stood with a hole punched in its shoulder, still spanning the crevasse—for now. For anyone behind them, which meant Meridian's teams, it was a coin flip. For anyone coming back—

Coming back. She looked at the gap and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that they were probably not coming back this way. That the road home, if there was one now, ran downriver, through the registry office, through whatever came after. There was no retreat off this glacier. Only forward, and the counting of days.

"We're committed now," Idris said quietly. He'd followed her gaze.

"We were committed on the beach." She got up, brushed the ice off her knees, and picked up the rope. Her hands were shaking. She let them shake for exactly as long as it took to walk to the sledge, and then she made them stop. "Five days to the saddle. Let's spend them well."

* * *

### Idris

That night the temperature stayed above freezing and Idris lay awake listening to the glacier die by inches.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the terrible part. Just water, everywhere, working. Trickling under the tent fly, murmuring in the crevasses, ticking off the hull of the overturned boat like rain on a roof. A whole watershed awake and busy, unbuilding a thousand years of ice. He thought about the reports he'd written for Meridian—the ones with phrases like *glacial recession presents expanded access corridors*—and wanted to be sick. They had known. All of them had known, and they'd written it down as opportunity.

Beside him, Felix slept badly, muttering once—his name, or her grandmother's, hard to tell—and he watched the faint starlight move on her face and understood that he would follow this woman anywhere the ice allowed, and a great deal of where it didn't.

Near dawn the radio crackled. He crawled out into the gray half-light to take it, cupping the handset, and the coastal station's voice was less cheerful now: *warm ridge strengthening, foehn winds possible over the interior passes by tomorrow evening, all parties on the ice advised to seek shelter.*

Foehn winds. Warm, dry, downslope—the kind of wind that could strip forty degrees off a glacier's surface integrity in a single night. He stood there a long moment with the handset dead in his hands, doing arithmetic against the sky, which had clouded in the east like smoke.

Felix emerged beside him, hair frozen flat, eyes already reading his face. "How bad?"

"Wind event tomorrow night. Real one." He handed her the radio. "And Felix—the station said a second advisory. Unrelated." He hesitated, hating the words even as he weighed them. "A survey charter filed a flight plan up this valley yesterday. Twin Otter. Company markings."

The silence between them lasted three of the glacier's small, patient sounds.

"They flew over us," she said.

"Or they will." He looked east, where the clouds were building, and felt the countdown tighten around his ribs like a rope. "Either way—we need to be over that saddle before the wind does. And I think we need to stop being findable."

Felix was quiet for a moment. Then she began striking the camp with quick, economical movements, and when she spoke her voice had gone flat and bright, the pilot's voice, the voice that lied fluently and navigated honestly.

"Then we travel white," she said. "No fire, no shine, no noise. And if that plane comes back—" she glanced up at the thickening sky, and something in her expression was almost, terribly calm, "—I hope it waits for the storm. Because the storm is the only cover we're going to get, and Idris—"

The first flakes came down even as she said it, fat and wrong for the season, drifting sideways on a wind that hadn't existed an hour ago.

"—it's starting early."
