## 16. Whiteout

The storm did not arrive so much as close around them, the way a hand closes around a moth.

Felix had seen weather do this before—seen a sky that looked merely sullen become, in forty minutes, a wall—but never this early in the season and never with such appetite. The snow came down not in flakes anymore but in sheets, in whole white provinces of it, and the wind found every seam of the tent they'd pitched against the moraine's shoulder and worried at them like teeth.

"Talk to me," she said, because she'd learned that rule years ago: on the ice, you talk. Silence is where people go wrong.

"I'm fine," Idris said, which was the first sign he wasn't.

They had made perhaps four kilometers before the light died. Four kilometers of hauling the boat's salvaged runner-sled over pressure ridges, of Felix navigating by feel and memory because the GPS had gone to static, of Idris stumbling twice and laughing about it the second time—a thin, unmoored laugh that she didn't like at all. Now they were zipped into the two-person tent with the stove hissing its rationed heat between them, and outside the world had been erased. Not darkened—erased. There was no up. There was no horizon. There was only the nylon shuddering overhead and the sound of the wind, which was not a sound so much as a condition, like being inside a lung.

"How long?" Idris asked.

"Two days if we're lucky. Three if we're not." She checked the fuel gauge by touch, tilting the canister to feel the weight of what remained. "We have enough to keep the stove alive at minimum burn for maybe thirty hours. After that we share body heat and pray."

"You say that very calmly."

"I've been saying worse things calmly my whole life."

He laughed again—that same thin laugh—and Felix turned toward him in the orange murmur of the stove-light and saw what she'd been afraid of seeing. His lips were pale. Not blue yet, but pale, drained of color from the inside out, and there was a glassy brightness to his eyes that had nothing to do with fever. He was shivering, but the shiver was wrong—too slow, too grand, the great rolling tremors of a body losing its argument with the cold rather than winning it.

"No," she said aloud, to herself as much as to him. "No, no. Not now."

"I'm all right."

"You're hypothermic. Mild stage, maybe—" She pressed the back of her hand to his neck. His skin felt like something kept in a cellar. "Idris. Listen to me. This is the part where people die. Not on the summit, not in the crevasse field. Here, in the tent, warm enough, fed enough, when they stop shivering properly and start feeling *fine*."

"That does seem unfair."

"It's extremely unfair." She was already moving, unpacking with hands that knew their work. Spare socks. The dry shirt from the bottom of her pack, the one she'd been saving. Her own sleeping bag, the good one, rated twenty degrees colder than his. "Get out of those layers. All of them. You're wearing your sweat and it's killing you."

"It's a bit intimate for a second acquaintance."

"Do it or die, surveyor."

He did it—clumsily, fingers stupid on the zippers, and she had to help him with the last one, peeling the frozen-damp fleece off his shoulders while he made a joke about botanists and modesty that trailed off into nothing. Then she bundled him into the dry layers, into her sleeping bag, zipped him up to the chin like a parcel, and sat back on her heels and made herself think.

The stove could not run hot; they didn't have the fuel. Movement would help but they couldn't move—the storm would take them apart out there, and even if it didn't, they'd lose the tent, and the tent was the only thing between them and a death measured in hours. Hot water: yes, she could melt snow, small amounts, make him drink until his stomach sloshed. Sugar. She had a block of piloncillo wrapped in wax paper and she broke off chunks and dropped them into the pot without measuring, an extravagance that would have scandalized her pilot's frugality, and did not care.

But she knew—I knew it in her bones, in the old knowledge passed down through women who lived at the edge of things—that warmth and sugar would not be enough. What he needed was for his body to remember it had a reason to keep burning. And bodies, Felix had learned across ten thousand solo hours in the air, did not run on fuel alone.

They ran on voice.

* * *

Her grandmother had told her that once, or almost told her—it had come sideways, the way everything important came from Amara Joubert, buried in a story about something else. They'd been mending nets on the stilt cabin's porch, or pretending to; Amara's hands had stopped working years before her mind did, and the net-mending was theater, an excuse to sit together in the salt wind. *When a man is freezing,* she'd said, apropos of nothing, apropos of the gulls, *you don't give him blankets first. You give him a reason. A name to answer to. Cold takes the ones who have already decided no one is waiting.*

Felix had been nineteen and had thought it was just another of her grandmother's oblique sermons. She had spent fifteen years understanding it was a survival instruction, and another decade refusing to understand why it had sounded so personal.

Now she filled the tin cup, pressed it into Idris's hands, and closed his fingers around it.

"Drink. All of it. Small sips, don't gulp." He drank. She refilled it. Outside, the wind rose through a register that made the tent poles sing, a high keening note like a struck wire. "Again."

"You're going to sit there watching me drink sugar water all night?"

"I'm going to talk to you all night. The drinking is incidental."

Something flickered in his eyes—the surveyor's wariness, the instinct that had catalogued her lies since the beach. "Talk about what?"

And here was the moment. Here was the door she had kept locked through ten years of deflecting questions, through Marta's knowing looks, through the entire slow siege of this man's honesty. She could talk about weather. She could talk about glacier mechanics, crevasse patterns, the calving forecast. She could fill the night with safe, technical noise and let him sleep and tell herself the lock still held.

But his lips were pale, and the wind was singing, and her grandmother's voice was in her ears like a hand on her shoulder.

*Cold takes the ones who have already decided no one is waiting.*

"My real name is Félicité," Felix said.

Idris went very still inside the sleeping bag.

"Félicité Amara Okoye-Joubert. My mother was Nigerian, second-generation, a hydrologist who died in a flood survey when I was four. My grandmother raised me after that, down on the coast, in a house that isn't on any map you'll find. Amara Joubert. The woman whose handwriting is all over those journals." She heard her own voice come out level, pilot-flat, and hated it, and pushed past it. "I changed the name when I was twenty-two. I got my license under Joubert only. I let everyone in the valley believe I blew in from nowhere with a rented Cessna and a talent for bad landings. Ten years, Idris. I built a person out of spare parts and flew her around until she seemed real."

"Why?" It came out slurred at the edges, the cold pulling at his consonants, but his eyes were sharp. Watching her the way he watched data.

"Because of what's in the journals. Because of what my family did." She fed the stove a measured splash of fuel and watched the flame shrink obediently. "You read some of it. The network. The routes. During the purge, my grandmother's family moved people through those caves—out of the valley, along the coast, onto boats. Dozens of them. Maybe hundreds. And afterward, when the companies came through buying silence, anyone connected to that network became a liability. People disappeared. Not dramatically. Just—filed away. My grandfather was one of the last pilots who flew that coast, and he taught me that the safest thing a person can be is unrecorded. So I made myself unrecorded. No surname that traces. No history that checks out. I answered every question about myself with a smaller question about someone else, and it worked, and I told myself it worked, for ten years."

"And now?"

"Now the tide gave my grandmother back." She laughed, short and unsteady, and was surprised to find her eyes burning. "Do you know how that feels? To spend your whole adult life burying a woman, and then have the ocean dig her up and lay her at your feet with her handwriting intact? Every chart, every journal page, every careful coded error—it's her, Idris. It's her hand reaching out of the ground saying *you are mine, you were always mine, you don't get to be nobody.*"

The wind slammed the tent wall and the flame guttered and steadied. Idris had pulled one hand free of the bag to hold the cup, and she took it—his hand, cold as ballast stone—and pressed it between both of hers.

"Keep talking to me," she said. "That's the deal. You stay awake, you answer me, and I'll tell you everything. Everything I've never said out loud. That's the trade."

"What if I fall asleep anyway?"

"Then I'll be insulted, and I'll tell your secrets to the glacier."

A ghost of his usual crooked smile. "Fair terms."

* * *

She talked until the fuel ran low and then past that, into the hour when the stove ticked itself cold and the darkness went total except for the faint grey suggestion of the tent walls. She told him about learning to fly at fourteen, illegally, on a crop-duster her grandfather bribed a mechanic to leave unlocked. About the first time she landed on the sandbar strip and understood, with a violence that frightened her, that the sky was the only room big enough to hold her. About the letters she wrote to her dead mother for years, addressed to a post office box in Lagos that had been closed since before she was born, and how she'd finally stopped when she realized she was writing them in her grandmother's handwriting without meaning to.

She told him about the night Amara died. How Felix had been mid-flight, delivering antibiotics upriver, and had felt it happen—not known, *felt*, a sensation like a door closing gently somewhere behind her sternum—and had finished the delivery first because there was no one else to finish it, and had landed at midnight and walked into the stilt cabin and found her grandmother sitting upright in her chair with the tide charts spread across her lap, arranged, deliberately, into a pattern Felix hadn't been able to read until three weeks ago.

"She left me the cipher," Felix said. Her voice had gone rough hours ago; she'd stopped caring. "In the arrangement of the charts. I thought for years I'd failed her somehow, that she'd died disappointed in me, that I'd never learned what she wanted to teach. But she wasn't teaching me maps. She was waiting for me to need them. There's a difference, and it took me a decade and a stranger wrecking on my beach to see it."

Through all of it, Idris answered. Slower and slower, his responses shrinking to single words, to hums, to the grip of his hand tightening around hers when she paused too long—but answering. She fed him water when the cup was in reach and made him recite river confluences when it wasn't, made him name minerals, made him describe in detail the geology of a place he'd surveyed in another life, his voice dropping to a mutter, her voice pulling him back each time like a rope thrown into dark water.

Once, near what she judged was the deepest hour of the storm, he surfaced enough to say, "Your grandmother smuggled people out. And you smuggled yourself out. Same route, different direction."

Felix stared at him through the dark. "That's the cruelest true thing anyone's ever said to me."

"Mm. Keep talking. It's working."

It was working. Somewhere in the fourth hour his shivering had returned—proper shivering, fast and violent and *alive*, the body rejoining the fight—and she nearly wept over it, over the ugly beautiful chatter of his teeth, and covered him tighter and kept talking anyway, because stopping felt like tempting fate.

Near dawn—or what she guessed was dawn, the darkness achieving a slightly different quality of dark—he said, quietly, from deep inside the sleeping bag:

"The records I found. At Meridian. The village that was cleared—Okonkwo was on the clearance manifests. My grandfather signed one." A pause, long enough that she thought he'd gone under. "I've never said that out loud either."

"I know," Felix said. "Chapter twelve of your confession. I was listening."

His laugh was barely breath. But it was warm-adjacent, and it was human, and outside the wind screamed on like something cheated.

* * *

The storm held them for two days exactly, as if it had read the same forecast she had.

They spent them in a rhythm Felix came to think of as tending: melt, feed, talk, sleep in shifts pressed together for pooled warmth, talk again. By the second day Idris was pale but present, moving stiffly, drinking everything she put in front of him and complaining about it with recovering vigor, and Felix discovered she was hoarse from forty hours of continuous speech and couldn't remember ever having spoken so much in her life. Something had been emptied out of her. Something else had been installed in the space, and she didn't yet have a name for it, but it sat in her chest like ballast and she suspected it was permanent.

On the second evening the wind dropped—not gradually but all at once, the way storms on the ice sometimes quit, mid-shriek, like a singer cut off—and the silence rang so loudly that they both flinched.

Felix crawled out through the vestibule into a world remade. The moraine had vanished under a meter of fresh snow, smooth and blue-white and featureless, the stars out in brutal abundance, the glacier ahead of them glowing faintly like something lit from beneath. Cold enough to crack teeth. Clear enough to navigate by.

Behind them, far to the south, a light moved across the sky. Steady, methodical, sweeping. Not a star. Not a plane in transit.

A search pattern.

"Idris," she said softly, without turning. "How much do you want to bet Meridian doesn't wait for good weather either?"

Inside the tent, the rustle of him sitting up. When he spoke, his voice was steady now, fully his own, and it carried an edge she'd never heard in it before.

"They're not searching for the cave anymore, Felix. They're searching for us."

He was already reaching for his boots.
