## 17. Meridian Catches Up

The moraine announced itself before we saw it—a grinding, mineral smell on the wind, like a quarry left out in the rain. Idris felt it in his boots first: the ground going from ice to stone to that loose, shifting scree that remembered every footstep and gave none of them back.

He had been walking point for six hours, breaking trail through the slush while Felix hauled the sled-lashed boat behind them, and he'd learned her rhythms the way he'd once learned survey grids—by repetition, by error, by the slow accumulation of evidence. She stopped when the light went flat against the glacier face. She sang under her breath when she thought he couldn't hear, old coast songs in a language she claimed not to speak. And when she was afraid, she got quieter, not louder, folding herself down into some interior room where even the weather couldn't follow.

She was very quiet now.

"Smoke," she said.

He smelled it a moment later—woodsmoke and something chemical beneath it, generator exhaust, the particular petroleum sweetness of a field camp run by people with a budget. His stomach performed a slow, professional collapse. He knew that smell from three continents. He had helped build camps that smelled like that.

"How far?"

"Past the shoulder." Felix set down the tow rope with exaggerated care, the way you put down something you wanted to throw. "There's a bench of rock below the lateral moraine where they could pitch tents out of the wind. It's the only sensible campsite for eight kilometers. If I were staging an interception, that's where I'd stage it."

"If *you* were staging it."

"I've been hiding from people my whole life, Idris. I know the real estate."

He crouched and pulled the binoculars from the pack—the good ones, Meridian-issue, another piece of his former life he kept failing to discard. Through the lens the world jumped and steadied: grey rock, grey ice, and there, tucked into the lee of the shoulder, four dome tents the color of dried blood. A generator hut. An antenna mast with a guy-wire glittering like a stitch through the landscape. Two figures moving between the tents, small as punctuation marks.

And parked on the ice shelf beyond them, absurd and magnificent, a helicopter—white-bodied, no livery, rotors tied down. Meridian didn't paint its aircraft because Meridian didn't believe it needed to explain itself.

"Four, maybe five," he said. "Plus air support."

"Ferris?" Felix asked, using the team lead's name the way you'd spit.

"He'll be there. This is a Ferris operation. He doesn't send people to do things he can stand in front of personally and loom." Idris lowered the binoculars. His hands were steady, which surprised him. He'd expected shaking. What he felt instead was worse and better at once—a terrible clarity, like the air after lightning. "They beat us here. The forecast said the pass wouldn't hold."

"The forecast said a lot of things." Felix was already repacking, movements economical, furious. "The question is what we do in the next hour, because in two days the calving season opens and this whole route turns into a shooting gallery of falling ice, and if we're still on it when that happens—"

"We won't be. We'll be past it or we won't exist." He looked at the camp again, at the tiny figures, and felt the shape of the thing he was about to do settle over him like weather. He'd been circling it for weeks—the way you circle a decision the way you circle a crevasse field, testing, probing, telling yourself there's still a way around. There wasn't. There never had been. "I need to talk to them."

Felix turned slowly. In the flat glacier light her eyes were the color of deep water over rock, and they were looking at him the way you look at a bridge you're about to have to cross. "Talk."

"Stall. That's all. They can't touch us out here—not legally, not with the claim filing forty hours away and witnesses in the valley who know where we are. Marta made sure of that. But they can delay us, and delay is the same as defeat right now. Every hour they hold us is an hour off the glacier's clock." He tapped the wax-sealed tube strapped across his chest, the journals inside it wrapped in oilcloth like a heart in a rib cage. "So I give them a reason not to search us. Not to detain us. A reason to let us walk."

"Which is?"

"That the data's encrypted." He heard his own voice go flat and technical, the surveyor's register, and hated how easily it came. "Which it is, partially—I built the decryption schema myself, layered, keyed to biometric input I never uploaded anywhere. Without me, their geologists have six months of beautiful imagery and no coordinates. I tell them that. I tell them I'm still deciding whose side I'm on, that I want terms, that I'll trade the keys for a clean exit and a bigger cut. Greed is believable. Greed is what they understand."

"And when they call your bluff? When they realize you're lying about the rest of it?"

"It's not entirely a lie. The encryption is real. The greed—" He stopped. Looked at her. "The greed is the only part I have to perform."

Felix studied him for a long moment. Wind moved her hair across her face and she didn't bother pushing it back. "You understand what you're doing," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Burning the boat behind me."

"You've read the journals. You know what happens to people who burn boats on this coast."

"They get rescued by grandmothers with smugglers' charts," he said, "or they drown. I'm aware of the odds."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not a smile, nothing so generous, but its distant cousin. She pulled the tow rope taut again. "Then we do it your way. But Idris—if they take you, I'm not coming back for you. I can't. Not with these." Her hand rested briefly on the tube. "You know that."

"I know that." And he did know it, and knowing it felt like the truest thing anyone had said to him in years. That was the arithmetic of the last month distilled: everything expendable except the truth, and he was one of the things that might turn out to be expendable. He found, examining the feeling carefully, that he could live with it. That he even preferred it. For ten years he had been indispensable to Meridian—key man, closer, the one who could read a landscape and price it. Indispensable meant owned. It was a relief, almost, to become disposable.

They came down off the ice onto the bench of rock an hour later, walking openly, deliberately visible, because the worst thing you could do with men like Ferris's was look like you were sneaking. Idris had spent the hour rehearsing not the words but the posture—the slight slump of a man weighing an offer, the calculated reluctance, the hunger kept just barely leashed. He'd watched Ferris recruit a dozen times. He knew the choreography from the inside.

Ferris stood at the edge of the camp with his hands in the pockets of a jacket that cost more than Felix's plane, watching them come. He hadn't changed. Silver at the temples now, but the same stillness, the same way of looking at people as though appraising salvage. Behind him, two of the team drifted into casual positions that weren't casual at all—one by the generator hut, one near the helicopter, both with the loose readiness of men who did security work and enjoyed it.

"Okonkwo." Ferris's voice carried easily across the scree, warm as a handshake. "You're a hard man to find. Though I suppose that was rather the point."

"Vasquez and I got delayed." Idris stopped ten meters out—close enough for conversation, far enough that the two flanking men would have to commit visibly if they wanted to close it. "Weather. Then mechanical. Then more weather."

"Mm." Ferris's gaze slid past him to Felix, catalogued her—the rope-burned hands, the frost-nipped cheeks, the sealed tube across her chest—and returned. "And you've been busy. Our other assets in the valley report considerable activity at a certain sea cave. Journals, charts, documentation of a historical nature." He smiled without warmth. "Documentation can be so inconvenient."

"It can also be valuable," Idris said. "Depending on what's in it."

"Depending on who holds it." Ferris took his hands from his pockets. "Here's where we are, Idris, since you've always appreciated directness. The permits are frozen—that was clever, the heritage filing, though lawyers are a renewable resource and injunctions are cheap. What isn't renewable is time. The board wants certainty by quarter's end. So: you're going to hand over whatever's in that tube, and Ms. Vasquez is going to sign a access easement, and everyone goes home rich and dry. Or—" he gestured mildly at the glacier, the darkening sky, the first fat flakes of the evening's snow already starting down, "—you keep standing here, and the mountain makes the decision for us. I'd rather it didn't come to that. I've always liked you, Okonkwo. You were the best of us."

*The best of us.* Idris felt the phrase land somewhere old and bruised. Ten years ago he'd have glowed at it. Now it sounded like an epitaph.

"There's a problem with your plan," he said.

"There usually is."

"The tube doesn't open for you." He kept his voice level, bored almost, a technician discussing a fault. "The journals are scanned—every page—and the scans are encrypted under a schema I designed. Layered. The key material is split; part of it's in my head, part of it's biometric-bound to me specifically. Thumbprint, retinal pattern, and a passphrase that exists nowhere written down. Without all three, the archive is noise. Beautiful, high-resolution noise." He shrugged. "You could take the originals, burn them, sink them—it wouldn't matter. The data exists in three places you don't control, and only I can make any of them legible."

He watched Ferris absorb this. Watched the appraisal shift, recalculate. It was mostly true, which was why it worked—the scans existed, the encryption was real, the biometric layer was genuine. The lie lived only in the emphasis: that Idris was the sole key, and that Idris was for sale.

"You encrypted company-adjacent research data with a personal schema," Ferris said slowly. "That's a violation of about nine policies."

"I learned policy from watching how this company treats policies." Let a little bitterness show, calibrated. "I've been carrying this project for months alone in the field. Forgive me for wanting leverage."

Silence, except the wind and the tick of cooling metal from the generator hut. Snow thickened between them. One of the security men shifted his weight, and Idris read the movement the way he read slope failure—loading, stress, the geometry just short of going.

"So what do you want?" Ferris asked finally. Which was the sentence Idris had walked down a glacier to hear.

"A clean exit. Full severance, no litigation, no blacklisting—the kind where references survive. And a finder's percentage on anything extracted, because unlike the rest of you, I intend to live a long time and money is portable." He glanced back at Felix, let Ferris see him glance, let him draw his own conclusions about what she was being promised or threatened with. "She gets passage out and immunity. Whatever she signed or didn't sign stops mattering today."

"And the keys?"

"When I'm on a plane. Not before. You get the biometrics at the airport, in front of witnesses, with the transfer executed simultaneously. I'm sentimental, not stupid."

Ferris studied him for a long, long moment. Snow gathered on his silver shoulders and he didn't brush it off, and Idris understood suddenly that the stillness wasn't composure—it was appetite, held. Somewhere behind those pale eyes a ledger was updating itself: asset value, risk, alternatives. Idris had seen that ledger close on villages, on watersheds, on a grandfather's name. He waited to see it close on himself.

"Terms acceptable," Ferris said at last. "You'll come to camp. We have a satellite uplink; I'll draft the agreement tonight. Your—" a fractional pause, the word *accomplice* being sanded down in real time, "—colleague can shelter with the team. We move out together in the morning. No one has to freeze."

"No," Idris said. "We keep our own camp. Half a kilometer east, on the bench above the icefall. You can see our fire. We'll come for the signing at first light." He held Ferris's eyes. "You taught me that too, remember. Never sleep inside someone else's perimeter."

Something flickered across Ferris's face—annoyance, yes, but underneath it, reluctantly, respect. The currency Idris had spent ten years earning and was spending now in a single afternoon. "First light," Ferris agreed. "Don't be late. The mountain won't wait, and neither will I."

They walked away uphill, unhurried, two surveyors heading to their own camp, and only when the shoulder of the moraine cut off the camp from view did Felix's breath come out in a long white plume.

"They bought it," she said.

"For a night." Idris's legs felt strange, weightless, as though the scree had stopped holding him. "By morning he'll have run every check he can. He'll find the gaps in my story eventually—the biometric key is real, but the passphrase recovery protocol has a flaw I built in years ago and never reported. If he finds the flaw, he finds the lie."

"Then we're gone before he finds it." Felix was already scanning the upper route, reading the ice the way she read weather, and there was something in her face he hadn't seen before—or rather, something he had, in mirrors, in his own face years ago before he'd learned to bury it: the look of a person who has decided. "The icefall crossing is doable in the dark if we rope up. Four hours to the far side, then the descent to the registry road. We leave now, tonight, and by the time Ferris realizes his golden goose flew—"

"—we're a day ahead and the glacier is closing behind us." He looked back once, downhill, at the blood-colored tents and the white helicopter shrinking in the snowfall. Somewhere in that camp was a version of himself that signed the paper, took the money, went home rich and hollow and safe. He waved goodbye to it, internally, without ceremony.

His hands, he noticed, had finally started to shake—but only now, only after, only when it no longer mattered. That seemed right. Fear, he was learning, was a thing you scheduled, like maintenance. You did it after the crossing, not during.

Felix tossed him the tow rope. "Walk," she said. "Grieve later."

Above them the seracs caught the last grey light and burned like candles, and the mountain began, softly, to talk in its sleep—the deep percussive mutter of ice under strain, the sound of a countdown that had nothing to do with Meridian, or lawyers, or men with clean jackets and dirty ledgers.

They climbed into it, roped together, into the dark and the falling snow, while behind them the camp lit its generators and waited for a morning that was never going to come.
