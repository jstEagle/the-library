## 18. Calving Season Opens

The glacier told us before the radio did.

We'd been hauling since first light—Idris on the bow line, me at the stern, the boat riding on its sledge of split bamboo and salvaged runners like a wounded animal we were dragging uphill. The moraine had softened overnight; every third step punched through a crust of ice into slush that sucked at our boots and made the boat's weight feel doubled. My shoulders had stopped complaining somewhere around dawn. They'd simply gone quiet, the way a body does when it accepts that pain is now a condition rather than an event.

Then the sound came.

Not a crack—a *groan*, deep and structural, rising out of the ice beneath us as if the whole glacier were clearing its throat after a century of silence. It rolled up through my boot soles, through my knees, into my teeth. Idris froze mid-stride, the bow line snapping taut in his fists.

"That's not settling," he said.

"No," I said. "That's calving."

The forecast had given us until tomorrow. Marta's coded message—relayed twice through settlements that didn't ask questions—had been specific: warm front off the coast, forty-eight hours, maybe less on the ice. We'd gambled on the full two days. The glacier was calling in the debt early.

"Distance to the far side?" Idris asked. He'd learned by now not to ask how much time we had. On this route, distance was the only honest unit left.

"Four kilometers if the ice holds its manners." I looked up-valley toward the serac field, the blue-white towers where the glacier poured over a bedrock step, fractured into leaning pillars the size of church spires. In another week they'd be beautiful. Today they were loaded guns. "Less if it doesn't."

He nodded once and put his shoulder back into the harness. That was the thing about Idris I'd stopped being surprised by: he didn't waste breath on fear. He spent it on motion.

* * *

We crossed the first crevasse field with the boat lashed flat and ourselves roped together, probing with the ice axe, moving in the shuffle-step my grandfather's journals called *walking like an old man in a new house*. The crevasses here were hidden under snow bridges, and the bridges were rotting from below. Twice the axe went through into blue darkness and we rerouted along the moraine's spine, adding hours we didn't have.

It was during one of these detours that I saw the first collapse.

A serac on the eastern flank—the smallest of them, no bigger than a chapel—leaned, sighed, and came apart. It didn't fall so much as *dissolve upward*, a column of powder and house-sized blocks that hit the ice with a concussion I felt in my chest. The sound arrived seconds later, a boom followed by a long white roar, and then the fog of displaced snow rolled across the glacier and swallowed us whole.

I stood in the whiteness with my hand on the boat's gunwale and listened to the echo die against the valley walls.

"Felix." Idris's voice came out of the fog, closer than I expected. His hand found my sleeve. "Talk to me."

"I'm here." My voice sounded strange to me, thin. "I'm counting."

"Counting what?"

"How many more times I can almost die before it stops being interesting."

He huffed something that was almost a laugh. Through the thinning mist his face emerged, frost in his eyebrows, the scar across his cheekbone livid with cold. "Keep counting," he said. "But count faster."

* * *

The second collapse took the route behind us.

We heard it mid-afternoon: not one groan but a stuttering series of them, like a building coming down floor by floor. When the snow-fog cleared enough to see, the tongue of glacier we'd crossed that morning was shedding itself into the meltwater lake at its foot, whole acres of ice tilting and sliding with a slowness that made it worse somehow—majestic, unhurried, indifferent. Waves radiated out across the lake. Somewhere under that water was the low-tide cave, my grandmother's sea mouth, sealed now behind a wall of falling ice.

I didn't let myself look longer than a minute. Looking longer would have meant grieving, and grief was ballast we couldn't afford.

"The journals," Idris said quietly. He'd read the thought off my face.

I touched the wax-sealed bundle strapped inside my jacket, against my ribs. Three nights ago, sheltering in the homestead, we'd melted candle stubs and paraffin over the most fragile pages, dipping them the way my grandmother must once have done, sealing ink against water the way she'd sealed people against a purge. The bundle rode next to my heart like a second heartbeat. Everything else—the plane, the cabin, the boat's spare parts, the escape plan I'd kept folded in a drawer for ten years like a suicide note written in reverse—all of it was behind us or burning.

"We keep moving," I said.

* * *

The boat died at the last crevasse.

It was a wide one, six meters of blue emptiness spanned by a snow bridge that even I could see was ceremonial. There was no way around; the moraine funneled here between walls of ice, and behind us the calving had opened a maze of new fissures we couldn't retrace. We'd have to cross light and fast, one at a time, which meant the boat couldn't come.

I stood at the edge and looked at her. My grandfather's boat. Forty years of river runs, of medicine crates and mail sacks and once—I'd found the entry in the journals just last week—a family of five smuggled downriver in her hold under a false floor, breathing through gaps in the planks while soldiers walked the banks. She was soft-hulled, patched three times, ugly as sin. She had carried everything that mattered in this valley for longer than I'd been alive.

"She's done her runs," Idris said beside me. He wasn't looking at the boat. He was looking at me, and there was nothing clinical in his face, nothing of the surveyor measuring an asset. Just a man who understood what it cost to leave things behind.

"There's gear in her," I said. Rope, the stove, half our food. "We can strip her."

"We'll strip her." He was already unbuckling the harness. "And then we give her a burial worth the name."

We worked fast because we had to, but also because work was the only language either of us trusted anymore. Out came the rope, coiled and shouldered. Out came the stove, the food tin, the wax, the small iron pry-bar Idris had carried since the beach like a talisman. Last I pulled the false-floor plank—the one with the breathing holes drilled through it, five holes in a row, worn smooth at the edges by hands gripping them in the dark. I wrapped it in oilcloth and strapped it to my pack. Some things you don't bury. Some things you carry forward as testimony.

Then Idris took the axe and cut the boat loose from her sledge, and we walked her to the lip of the crevasse, and together—we did it together, his hands and mine on the gunwale—we tipped her over the edge.

She fell without sound for a long time. Then the blue dark took her, and the glacier closed over the place where she'd landed, and that was all. No marker. No ceremony. Just ice doing what ice does, keeping what it takes.

I stood there longer than was safe. Idris stood with me. Neither of us spoke, and neither of us moved, and the wind came down off the summit fields carrying the smell of deep cold and ancient air, and I thought: *she carried them out, and now she carries nothing, and that is what winning looks like sometimes.*

"Come on," I finally said. "The truth doesn't walk itself to town."

* * *

We crossed the bridge one at a time, crawling, spreading our weight like children on pond ice. I went first because I was lighter and because I refused, on principle, to ask anyone to test death before I did. The bridge held. It groaned, sagged a hand's width, shed a fine powder of crystals into the void below where they caught the low sun and glittered like a spill of stars going down—but it held.

Idris came across after me, and when he reached solid ice he lay there a moment on his back, chest heaving, frost steaming off him, and laughed—one short, disbelieving bark at the sky.

"What?" I said.

"My grandfather," he said, staring up, "cleared villages for Meridian. And his grandson just crawled across a glacier carrying a smuggler's floorboard." He turned his head and looked at me, and his eyes were wet and freezing at the same time. "There's a symmetry in that somewhere. I haven't found it yet. But it's there."

"It'll be there when we're off the ice," I said, and hauled him up.

* * *

The last kilometer was the worst, because hope is heavier than fear.

Behind us the glacier was giving itself away in pieces now—reports rolling across the ice every few minutes, some distant, some close enough to flinch at, the whole valley ringing like a struck bell that wouldn't stop. A serac field to our north let go in a chain reaction, tower after tower folding down in sequence, and the shockwave of snow dust rolled over us and turned the world monochrome and made us stop and hold each other's arms and wait to learn whether we were still alive in the same world we'd been alive in a moment before.

We were. We kept walking.

My legs had gone past pain into a kind of humming numbness. Idris was limping—the old hypothermia damage in his feet, never fully healed from the whiteout, flaring with every hour of cold—and I watched him shorten his stride to hide it and said nothing, because he knew I knew, and that was its own kind of care. We ate standing up, sharing a frozen block of dates and the last of the flatbread, chewing like it was a task assigned by someone hostile. The wax-sealed bundle pressed against my ribs with every step. *Everything expendable except the truth.* I'd been saying it to myself for days like a rosary, and only now, with the boat gone and the plane wrecked and the cabin a day's flood behind us, did I understand it wasn't a sacrifice. It was a relief. There was nothing left to lose, and nothing left to lose turns out to be the lightest thing a person can carry.

The glacier's edge announced itself as a change in the ground's voice—from the hollow drum of ice to the gritty crunch of rock, the terminal moraine rising ahead of us in a ridge of gray rubble stitched with the first stubborn green of the lower valley. Beyond it, faint through the haze, I could see the river, brown and swollen with melt, running hard toward the coast. Toward town. Toward the registry office and its dusty clerk and its stamp that could freeze a mining syndicate's permits with one piece of paper.

Marta would have sent word ahead. Marta always sent word ahead. But lawyers traveled faster than mules, and Meridian's people knew we were coming—that was the arithmetic we'd been outrunning since the burned cache.

Idris stopped at the moraine's crest and looked back, just once, at the glacier we'd crossed. It was calving in earnest now, the whole face of it sloughing into the lake in thunderous installments, icebergs the size of buildings rocking in the swell. It looked like the end of the world. It looked like applause.

"They'll have someone waiting in town," he said. "Meridian. Probably at the registry itself."

"Probably."

"And your plan is?"

I touched the wax-sealed bundle through my jacket. Five breathing holes in a board, strapped to my pack. A grandmother's handwriting, sealed against water, sealed against time. A valley full of people who had kept secrets for sixty years without ever asking what was in it for them.

"My plan," I said, "is to walk in the front door and say my name out loud. All of it. Every name I've got." I started down the rocky slope toward the river and the road and whatever was waiting. "Let them bring their lawyers. I'm bringing my dead."

Idris laughed again—tired, cracked, real—and followed me down.
