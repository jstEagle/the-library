## 19. The Registry Office

**FELIX**

The coastal town announced itself by smell before sight—diesel and brine and the sweet rot of fish offal, all of it carried on a wind that had followed them down from the ice like a debt collector. Felix stood at the rail of the ferry, watching the harbor resolve out of fog: cranes, tin roofs, the registry office squatting on its hill above the water with its flag snapping wet. Behind her, somewhere in the white chaos they'd crossed, a glacier was calving into the sea, swallowing the boat her grandfather had built, and she could not make herself mourn it.

Her hands still smelled of wax.

They'd sealed the journals themselves, the last night on the moraine, melting paraffin over a stove while Idris shivered through his recovery and Marta's instructions ran through Felix's head like a litany: *wax, cloth, wax again, and never let them out of your body's reach.* So now there were nine journals wrapped in oilcloth strapped against her ribs under her jacket, and every time she breathed she felt them rise and fall with her, a second heartbeat she'd carried for eleven days without once letting herself imagine what came after.

What came after was stairs. A great many stairs, up the hill through drizzle, past a shuttered cannery and a church whose bell had been replaced by a loudspeaker, to a door of green-painted wood with a brass plate worn nearly illegible: *PROVINCIAL REGISTRY OF LANDS, TITLES & HERITAGE INSTRUMENTS.*

Idris stopped beside her on the top step. He was thinner than when she'd hauled him out of the surf, gray around the mouth, moving like a man whose body had filed a formal complaint. But his eyes were clear, and he looked at the door the way he'd once looked at core samples—with the particular hunger of a man reading something for the first time.

"You can wait outside," Felix said. "If you want. After what this costs you."

"I've already paid," he said. "I'd like to see the receipt."

She almost smiled. It hurt her face, like using a muscle gone stiff. She pushed the door open.

Inside smelled of paper and mildew and decades of pipe smoke soaked into the walls. The clerk behind the counter was a woman in her sixties with reading glasses on a chain and the unhurried air of someone who had processed every kind of human desperation and found most of it repetitive. Her nameplate said *R. AMARAL.*

"We need to file a heritage claim," Felix said. "Under collective title. Ancestral use documentation, continuous occupation evidence, and—" she unwrapped the first journal from her chest, and the smell of cold wax rose between them like incense "—primary historical records."

The clerk looked at the journal. Looked at Felix's salt-cracked hands. Looked at Idris, who set down his field case and opened it to reveal geological surveys, Meridian letterhead, and a folder of internal documents he'd been carrying like a stone in his gut since the moraine.

"Sit down," said R. Amaral. "This is going to take forms."

* * *

**IDRIS**

There were forty-one forms. Idris counted. He'd spent twelve years in extractive geology and he had never encountered paperwork with this much gravity; every signature required a witness, every witness required identification, every page of the journals required cataloguing before it could be submitted as evidence, and the cataloguing required a certified archivist who would not arrive until Thursday because the ferry came Tuesdays and Fridays and today was Wednesday.

So they waited. And waiting, in a registry office above a fishing harbor, turned out to be its own kind of confession booth.

Amaral read the journals in the back room with cotton gloves and a desk lamp, and every hour or so she would emerge and ask a question that landed on Felix like a small detonation. *Who is E. Vasquez? Relation to claimant? Can you speak to the notation system used in the March 1961 entry?* And Felix would answer, voice level, hands folded, while Idris watched her give away pieces of herself one careful sentence at a time—the grandmother, the routes, the families moved through the caves during the purge years, the deliberate errors in the charts that were not errors at all but a cipher, a mercy, a way of keeping the map useless to anyone who hadn't earned it.

He recognized the shape of what she was doing. He'd done it himself, in deposition rooms, for Meridian: the controlled disclosure, the truth rationed out in doses too small to wound. Except Felix wasn't protecting a company. She was protecting the dead, and doing a worse job of it than she wanted, because the dead kept insisting on being known.

On the second afternoon Amaral emerged holding Journal Six open to a page Idris had never seen Felix read aloud—a list of names, dozens of them, in columns, with dates and destinations. Not cargo manifests. Passenger lists.

"These names," Amaral said. "Some of these families are still in the valley."

"Some of them are Marta's people," Felix said. "She'll testify. She's sending a statement with the next boat."

"The next boat," Amaral repeated, and made a note. "You understand that Meridian Extraction holds provisional survey permits covering portions of this grid. When this filing enters the public record, their legal team will have forty-eight hours to lodge an objection before interim protection attaches."

"We know," Idris said.

"Do you." Amaral looked at him over her glasses, and there was nothing sleepy in her gaze at all. "Because you're listed in their personnel files as senior field surveyor, Mr. Okonkwo. You're filing against your own employer. I've seen men do it. I've seen how it ends."

"It ended differently for me," Idris said. "I made sure of it."

* * *

**FELIX**

Meridian's lawyers arrived thirty-one hours after the filing, which meant they'd chartered a plane, which meant someone in the provincial office had sold them the timestamp, which meant the world worked exactly the way Felix had always believed it did and had spent ten years hiding from anyway.

They came up the hill at nine in the morning: three of them, two men and a woman, in clothes too clean for the town, carrying document cases with combination locks. The woman introduced herself as Counselor Voss, of Meridian Extraction's legal division, and shook hands with everyone including Amaral, who received the handshake the way a breakwater receives weather.

"Filing 4471-C," Voss said pleasantly, laying papers on the counter. "We're here regarding an objection to interim heritage protection. There are significant irregularities. The claimant collective's standing is unverified. The documentary evidence has no provenance chain. And the principal deponent—" a glance at Idris, cool as a scalpel "—is in breach of a binding confidentiality agreement, which renders his testimony not merely inadmissible but actionable."

"Then you'll want to be actioning him somewhere else," Amaral said, "because this office doesn't adjudicate employment contracts. Next window."

Voss's smile didn't move but something behind it recalculated. "The mineral rights in question represent a nine-figure development. Communities have misfiled heritage claims before, Ms.—"

"Joubert," Felix said.

Something flickered across Voss's face. Fast, but not fast enough. She knew the name. Which meant Meridian had known the name for longer than Felix had known theirs, and the thought arrived cold and complete: *they were never hunting a cave. They were hunting a family.* The anomalous survey data, the advance teams, the burned cache—they'd been pulling a thread that led to her grandmother, and she had walked the whole length of it into their hands wearing her own name like a lamp.

"You have family interests in this valley," Voss said carefully.

"I have a grandmother," Felix said. "She's dead. She left me some homework."

She untied the oilcloth right there at the counter, in front of the lawyers, and opened Journal One to the first page, and read aloud. Her grandmother's handwriting marched across the page like a procession: dates, tides, names, the words *we go by the low water and we tell no one but the water.* Felix's voice held. She'd expected it to break and it didn't, and that surprised her more than anything else that week.

"When my grandmother hid people in those caves," she said, closing the book, "your company's predecessor cleared the valley above them. The permits you're objecting with descend from that clearance. So yes—check our standing. Check it thoroughly. Bring your best people. The valley collective has been waiting sixty years for someone to check."

The room went very quiet. Rain ticked against the green window. Somewhere below, the harbor bell rang the hour, patient as tide.

Voss gathered her papers without another word about irregularities. At the door she paused. "Interim protection attaches at five o'clock tomorrow, if the objection fails. We'll be filing appeals in three jurisdictions by Friday."

"Then you'll be busy," Amaral said, stamping something, "and so will we."

* * *

**IDRIS**

They won the forty-eight hours the way exhausted people win things: barely, and without ceremony.

At 5:04 the following evening, Amaral emerged from the back room, removed her glasses, and rubbed her eyes with the heels of her hands like a woman coming up from deep water. Then she stamped the interim order—three blows of the seal, deliberate as hammer strikes—and slid the certificate across the counter.

"Extraction permits covering the cave grid are frozen pending full adjudication," she said. "Ninety days minimum. Probably a year. Meridian will appeal everything and lose slowly." She looked up, and for the first time she smiled, small and dry as driftwood. "Congratulations. The valley owns its own secrets again."

Felix took the certificate with both hands, the way you take something living. She didn't cheer. She didn't even exhale, not fully—Idris watched her hold the paper and understood that triumph, real triumph, doesn't feel like winning. It feels like setting down a weight you'd forgotten you were carrying, and discovering the ache where it used to be.

Outside, the rain had thinned to mist. The harbor lights came on one by one, gold smears on dark water. Felix stood on the registry steps and read the certificate twice, then folded it into the oilcloth with the journals, against her heartbeat.

"Marta will want to see this," she said.

"She'll want to frame it," Idris said. "Then argue about where to hang it."

That got a laugh out of her—short, rusty, real. And then she went quiet, looking down the hill toward the ferry landing, where a boat was tying up with the Tuesday mail, and a figure was coming up the street through the mist with a canvas bag and a walk that Idris recognized from a lifetime of watching people arrive to change things.

Marta climbed the registry steps like she owned them, which, as of 5:04 that afternoon, in every way that mattered, she did. She looked at the certificate, then at Felix, then at Idris, and her face did something complicated and old.

"Well," she said. "Now the hard part."

Felix blinked. "The hard part? We just—"

"You froze the permits," Marta said, shouldering past them through the green door. "Nobody's frozen Meridian yet. Their regional director landed an hour ago, girl, and he's asking at the hotel for the Joubert pilot by name."
