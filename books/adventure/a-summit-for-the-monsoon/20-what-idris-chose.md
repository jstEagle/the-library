## 20. What Idris Chose

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were the days Meridian had always sent its worst news—redundancy notices, site closures, the quiet emails that ended careers with a subject line and no body text. Idris had learned to dread them in Lagos, in Perth, in the windowless regional office above the hardware store in Puerto Cabral where he'd once spent eleven months mapping a watershed he would never be allowed to name. So when the courier boat puttered up to the collective's dock that morning and handed him an envelope heavy with corporate stationery, his hands knew what it was before his mind caught up.

He didn't open it on the dock. He carried it up through the town instead, past the fish sheds and the rope coiled on every available surface, past the church whose bell hadn't rung since the last flood, up the hill to the rented room above the chandlery where he'd been sleeping for three weeks while the lawyers worked. The envelope was cream-colored, embossed, absurdly expensive for a place where salt ate everything within a season. He set it on the desk beneath the single window and looked at it for a long time.

Through the glass, the harbor lay flat and pewter-colored under a sky still bruised from the monsoon's retreat. Somewhere out there was the valley. Somewhere beyond it, the cave mouth, now marked on public charts with a heritage designation pending—*pending*, the lawyer had said, *nothing is ever not pending until the ink is dry and the appeal window closes*. And somewhere much farther away, in a tower of glass in a city Idris had tried hard to forget, there were people who had decided that the cheapest way to make all of this go away was to make him go away first.

He opened the envelope.

The offer ran to four pages. Reinstatement, effective immediately. Senior Geosurvey Lead—a title two grades above anything he'd held, with the salary to match and a relocation package that read like a bribe dressed as generosity. Full legal indemnification. A clause, buried on page three in language so smooth it took him two readings to find its teeth, releasing Meridian from "any and all claims arising from activities conducted under prior survey authorizations," which meant the cave, which meant the journals, which meant everything he'd done in the last six months would become legally invisible. And at the bottom, a signature line waiting for his hand, with a deadline: fourteen days.

There was a second sheet, thinner, unembossed. A handwritten note from Odile Ferrant, his old director, the woman who had hired him out of graduate school because she said he had "an honest face, which is useful." The handwriting was her usual blade-slash cursive:

*Idris — Take this. Whatever you think you've found down there, it isn't worth what they'll do to you if you don't. I'm telling you this because someone once told me and I didn't listen. Don't be me. — O.*

He read it three times. Then he folded both documents along their original creases, set them on the desk, and went downstairs to make coffee he wouldn't drink.

* * *

The collective's lawyers worked out of a converted net loft near the north quay, a long room smelling of tar and old hemp, its rafters strung with the desiccated bodies of fishing floats that nobody had ever taken down. There were three of them: a sharp-faced woman named Beatriz Okafor who did most of the talking; a younger man, Tomás, who handled the document scans; and an elderly notary whose name changed depending on who was asking and who seemed to have been present at the founding of every institution in the province.

Idris had been coming here every day since they'd come down off the glacier, hauling boxes of scanned journal pages, cross-referencing coordinates, doing the one thing he was genuinely good at—reading the earth's paperwork—for people who weren't trying to strip-mine it. It was strange work. It was the best work he'd ever done.

Beatriz looked up when he came in, took one look at his face, and said, "They wrote to you."

"They wrote to me."

"How much?"

"Enough to buy the valley twice and the town as a bonus."

She nodded slowly, unsurprised. "And the indemnification clause?"

"Page three."

"They always put it on page three." She pushed back from the table, and the chair legs groaned against the floorboards. "Sit down, Okonkwo. Let's talk about what you're going to do, because I can see you've already decided, and my job is to make sure you decide it properly."

He sat. Outside the loft's open doors, the tide was coming in over the mudflats, filling the channels with a sound like slow breathing. He thought about Felix, who was up at the registry annex this morning with Marta, signing affidavits, putting her name on paper for the first time in ten years. He thought about how she'd looked when she'd finally told him her real history on the ice—how her voice had steadied the colder she got, as if truth were something her body could burn for fuel.

"I'm not taking it," he said.

"I know."

"You knew before I walked in."

"I've been doing this thirty years. Men like you come in carrying letters like that the way other men carry wounds—they hold them away from the body so nobody touches them." She laced her fingers on the table. "But refusing the money is the easy part. The easy part costs you nothing but pride. What I need to know is whether you're prepared for the rest of it, because the rest of it is not symbolic. The rest of it is your career, your professional registration, probably your ability to work in this industry on three continents. Meridian doesn't forgive, and they don't forget, and they own enough consultancies and licensing boards that 'senior geosurvey lead' will be the last job title anyone offers you for a very long time. Possibly forever."

"I know that too."

"Do you?" Her voice wasn't cruel. It was careful, the way a surgeon's voice is careful. "Because I've watched men sit where you're sitting and say those words, and then six months later, broke and blacklisted, they come back asking if the offer's still open. And it never is. That's the design. The offer exists to be refused. Its whole purpose is to make refusal feel like your choice, so that when they destroy you afterward, the record shows a man who walked away from a generous settlement. You understand? They are building your obituary right now, and they want you to write it yourself."

The room was quiet except for the tide. Idris looked at his hands—hands that had hauled a boat across ice, that had held Felix's shoulder in a whiteout, that had spent twenty years reading cores and contours for people who converted every contour into a price per tonne.

"My grandfather helped clear a village for them," he said. "You know that. I told you. He drove the survey stake himself. He got a bonus and a commendation and died comfortable in a house built on the proceeds, and nobody in my family ever said the village's name out loud, because saying it would have made us complicit retroactively, and silence was cheaper." He turned his hands over, palms up. "I spent my whole career telling myself I was different because I measured things honestly. As if accuracy were ethics. As if a man who maps a place precisely for the people who intend to erase it is any better than the ones holding the bulldozers. He's just better at finding them."

Beatriz said nothing. She was good at that too.

"So yes," Idris said. "I understand what refusal costs. I've been paying installments on it since I was nine years old and found my grandfather's commendation in a shoebox and asked my mother what it was for, and watched her face do the thing faces do when they're deciding whether to lie to a child." He exhaled. "What I have is more than the indemnity clause. That's what you actually called me in here to establish, isn't it? Whether I have anything worth trading besides testimony."

For the first time that morning, Beatriz smiled. It was not a warm smile. It was the smile of a woman watching a door she'd been pushing finally swing open.

"Tell me."

* * *

He had found them in March, back when he still believed he was hunting ore.

Meridian's internal archive lived on a segregated server accessible only from regional offices, and Idris had clearance—had always had clearance, one of the quiet privileges of being trusted, which is to say useful. He'd gone looking for historical seismic data on the coastal interior, trying to reconcile his own readings with surveys from decades earlier, and instead he'd found the project files. *Operation Clearwater.* The euphemism was so bland it took him a full minute to understand what he was reading: schedules, contractor invoices, equipment manifests for a forced relocation in the valley forty years ago. Village by village. Family by family. Compensation figures calculated per household, with columns for "resistance likelihood" and "recommended approach"—and in the recommended-approach column, words like *pressure*, *isolate*, and, twice, in different hands, *burn-out*.

His grandfather's name appeared in the correspondence. Not as a laborer. As a consultant.

Idris had downloaded everything that night, before he'd fully decided to, his heart hammering in a regional office at two in the morning while the security cameras watched him do it—or didn't watch; he'd checked; the archive terminal sat in a blind spot some previous employee had mapped and never shared. He'd copied the files to three drives and hidden them in places only a surveyor would think of: sealed in a waterproof cache under a cairn, taped inside the false bottom of his instrument case, and—the copy that mattered now—carried against his body through a whiteout and a glacier crossing in a wax-sealed pouch that had ridden next to the journals like a twin passenger.

He laid it all out for Beatriz that afternoon: the drive, the file index he'd built from memory, the chain of custody he could swear to. Internal memos acknowledging that the heritage claim, if upheld, would expose Clearwater's paper trail to public record. Emails between Meridian counsel and the provincial mining registrar attempting to fast-track permit approvals before the appeal window closed. A cost-benefit analysis, dated eight months ago, in which the phrase "community displacement contingency" appeared beside a budget line.

Tomás scanned the drive in silence, the reader's light strobing blue across his face. The old notary witnessed each file hash with the ceremony of a man burying treasure. And Beatriz, reviewing the index page by page, kept making a small sound low in her throat—not satisfaction, exactly. Recognition. The sound of a case assembling itself.

"This changes the shape of everything," she said finally. "Their injunction motion assumes we have testimony and tradition. Testimony can be discredited. Tradition can be called nostalgia. But this—" she tapped the drive "—this is their own handwriting. This is the purge, documented by the purgers, delivered by one of their own senior staff. When this enters evidence, the permit freeze stops being procedural and becomes political. Nobody in that ministry wants their name attached to what's in these files."

"When do you file it?"

"Tomorrow morning, if you sign the affidavit tonight." She slid a form across the table. "Full disclosure, voluntary production, waiver of confidentiality protections. Once you sign, there's no version of your life where Meridian employs you again. There's no version where you testify as an anonymous source. Your name goes on every page. Their lawyers will take you apart in cross-examination—they'll dig up Lagos, they'll dig up the cost overruns on the Cabral survey, they'll imply you fabricated all of it to save yourself after your skiff wrecked. It will be ugly, and it will be personal, and you will sit in that witness box for days while strangers explain to a judge who you really are." She held out a pen. "So. One last time, and then I never ask again. Are you sure?"

Idris thought about his grandfather's commendation in its shoebox. About the village no one named. About Felix, who had spent ten years making herself invisible and had chosen, on a sheet of ice with death sitting patient beside them, to speak her history aloud instead—and how the speaking had kept them both alive.

He thought about the honest face Odile Ferrant had once found useful, and understood, finally, what honesty actually cost, and why so few people paid it.

"Yes," he said, and signed.

The pen scratched. The notary stamped. Outside, the tide had turned and was running out through the harbor mouth, and somewhere beyond the headlands the monsoon clouds were rebuilding themselves over the interior, patient as debt.

* * *

He walked to the telegraph-and-mail office as the light went amber, because some things shouldn't travel by encrypted channel. Odile Ferrant deserved a reply written in his own hand.

He bought a single sheet of the office's cheap lined paper and stood at the counter by the rain-streaked window, and this is what he wrote:

*Odile — Thank you for the warning. I mean that. You were the only person at Meridian who ever told me the truth, and I know what it cost you to send it, and I won't forget. But I can't take it. I found the Clearwater files in March. I have them. As of tomorrow they belong to the valley's lawyers, and my name is on the transfer. I'm not doing this to hurt you or to perform virtue—I've had twenty years to notice that I'm not a good man, only a precise one, and precision without conscience is just a sharper tool for someone else's hand. But I've read what my grandfather helped erase, and I've read what your company plans to finish erasing, and I've decided I'd rather end my career than extend theirs. If there's a version of you, years from now, standing in front of your own shoebox—there will be; there always is—maybe these files will make it lighter. That's the only comfort I can offer. Tell them I said the data was worthless without decryption. It was the last true thing I told them. — I.*

He folded it, sealed it, addressed it, and paid the outrageous postage without flinching. The clerk dropped it into the outgoing sack, and Idris stood a moment watching it settle among the bills and love letters and government forms, one small rectangle of ending.

When he stepped outside, the first drops of the evening rain were spotting the boards of the quay. Down the street, lamplighters were working their way along the waterfront, and beyond them, at the far end of the harbor, he saw a figure he knew by silhouette alone—broad shoulders, hair tied back, standing at the chandlery window where the day's charts hung drying. Felix had seen the courier boat that morning too. She would know what the cream envelope meant. By now she might even know what he'd answered.

He started toward her through the fine beginning rain, his footsteps loud on the empty boards, and behind him, in the net loft above the north quay, Tomás was already feeding the first of the Clearwater files into the scanner, page by page, the company's own handwriting turning slowly into evidence—while out over the darkening water, thunder gathered in the hills like a verdict being read aloud.
