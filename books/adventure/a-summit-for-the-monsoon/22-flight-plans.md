## 22. Flight Plans

The monsoon had a smell, and Felix had spent ten years pretending she didn't love it.

Wet earth and kerosene, bruised leaves and river silt—the whole valley exhaling after the long rains, steaming in the first honest sunlight since March. She stood on the airstrip at dawn with her sleeves rolled and her boots sinking into mud that smelled like beginning, and she let herself feel it. All of it. That was the discipline now: feeling things all the way through instead of around them.

The Cessna sat where she'd left it, gleaming under a tarp she'd pulled off an hour ago. Nine months of work lived in that airframe—new wing strut, patched cowling, a propeller she'd traded two months of mail runs to get freighted upriver. Debris damage, the insurance form had said, as if the storm had been an accident of weather and not a siege. She'd written *act of nature* in the box marked CAUSE and then, after a long moment, crossed it out and written *act of men* instead. The adjustor had called to ask if that was a joke. She'd told him no, and he'd paid anyway, because even insurance companies could tell when a woman was telling the truth about something that mattered.

She ran her palm along the fuselage the way you'd greet an old horse. "Morning," she said. The plane said nothing, which was how she liked most conversations to start.

Behind her, the valley was already awake. Smoke from cook fires rose straight in the windless morning, a dozen gray threads stitching settlement to sky. Somewhere downslope, Marta's voice carried across the water as it always did, pitched to carry three villages or one stubborn pilot, whichever required more volume. And below the tideline—she looked without flinching now, she'd practiced until looking didn't cost anything—the cave mouth stood open above the new tide line, its entrance framed in the survey stakes and painted boundary markers the collective had installed in June. A heritage site. Her grandmother's handwriting, photogrammetry-scanned and archived, the journals under glass in the registry office two hundred miles south, certified copies bolted inside the cabin of this very plane.

*Protected.* The word still fit strangely in her mouth, like a shoe bought for a foot that had gone bare too long.

She heard footsteps on the gravel and didn't turn. She knew the walk by now—heavier on the left than the right, a hitch the glacier had given him that the doctors said would fade and Idris privately hoped wouldn't. A souvenir, he called it. Proof he'd been somewhere worth paying for.

"You're pre-flighting like you're going to war," he said, coming up beside her with two enamel mugs and steam rising off both.

"I'm pre-flighting like someone who has passengers."

"Passengers." He handed her a mug. "You say it like a diagnosis."

"It is one. Terminal sociability." She drank. The coffee was terrible, cut with chicory the way Marta made it, which meant Marta had made it, which meant Marta had decided Felix needed feeding before flight and had not asked permission. Some things in the valley operated on their own authority. Felix had learned to stop fighting them. It had taken forty years and one epilogue, but she'd learned.

Idris leaned against the strut and looked out at the water with her. His survey case sat at his feet—leather cracked, brass corners dented, the same case he'd carried the day she'd hauled him half-drowned out of the surf and hated herself for it. Inside were the Meridian documents. Internal memos, permit histories, the 1970s clearance orders with his grandfather's signature countersigning the eviction of a village that no longer appeared on any map except the ones hidden in a sea cave. He'd be handing them to the collective's lawyers in the coastal town by afternoon, testifying under oath by week's end. Meridian had offered him reinstatement first. Then a promotion. Then, through intermediaries who thought they were being subtle, they'd offered less pleasant things. He'd refused all three categories with what Felix considered admirable economy.

"Nervous?" she asked.

He considered it honestly, which was another thing the glacier had given him. "Yes. But it's the good kind. Like standing at the edge of cold water."

"You would know."

"I would know." He smiled into his mug. "Your grandmother's charts got me across that moraine. Least I can do is make sure nobody else gets erased off the maps she drew."

Below them, the tide was coming in over the flats, erasing and revealing in its slow patient way. Felix watched it and thought about how she'd once believed the opposite of hiding was exposure—nakedness, danger, the end of everything. Now she knew the opposite of hiding was simply being *in* the picture. Standing in the frame. Letting the light land on you and discovering it wasn't a searchlight after all. Just light.

* * *

They came up the path at seven-fifteen, in single file, the way children do when they've been coached.

Marta's grandchildren—three of them, aged somewhere between eight and thirteen, wearing their going-to-town clothes and expressions of tremendous gravity. The eldest, a girl named Sefu with her grandmother's chin and her great-great-grandmother's eyes, carried a parcel wrapped in banana leaf that she held like ordnance. Behind her came two researchers from the university, Dr. Amara Chen and a graduate student whose name Felix had already forgotten and would learn again on landing, because that was the deal now: she learned people's names. She kept them. She wrote them in the little book she kept in the door pocket, next to the fuel log and the list of frequencies.

"You're late," Felix said.

"We're early," said Sefu. "Grandmother says your clocks run fast because you're always trying to leave."

Felix barked a laugh before she could stop it. Ten years of deflection, of *just passing through*, of answering personal questions with weather reports—and a twelve-year-old had dismantled the whole architecture in one sentence. She crouched to the girl's height.

"What's in the parcel?"

"Dried fish. For the lawyers." Sefu's face went solemn. "Grandmother says lawyers argue better when they've eaten, and that we owe your grandmother's family a debt, and debts travel better than arguments."

"That," Felix said, "is the wisest thing anyone has ever brought onto my aircraft."

She loaded them in with the tenderness she usually reserved for engine components. Seatbelts checked twice. The researchers wedged between sample cases and the sealed journal copies. Idris in front, his bad leg stretched out with the careful geometry of long practice. Felix climbed in last, as always, and sat in the seat she'd sat in alone for a decade, and found it did not feel smaller with people in it. It felt, for the first time, like the correct size.

She ran the checklist aloud so the children could hear it. She explained the headsets, the sick bags, the meaning of the various alarming noises a Cessna makes on takeoff ("most of them are just the plane talking; ignore the gossip"). Sefu asked why the door didn't lock.

"It locks," Felix said. "I just don't lock it anymore."

"Why?"

Felix looked out the window—at the cabin on its stilts, door standing open to the morning because she'd walked out of it that way and intended to walk back into it the same way. At the smoke threads. At the cave mouth catching the sun.

"Because I spent a long time thinking locked doors kept me safe," she said. "Turns out they mostly just kept people out."

Sefu absorbed this with the terrifying efficiency of children, nodded once, and moved on to more pressing questions about whether the plane could do loops. (It could not. Officially.)

The engine caught on the second try. The valley tilted away beneath them—the settlement, the river running brown and fat with the season's last rains, the green ridgelines stacked like sleeping animals—and Felix flew the pattern she'd flown a thousand times, except that this time she banked deliberately over the tideline, tipping her wing toward the cave mouth so her passengers could see it. On the new charts—the public ones, the ones any sailor or pilot or child with a school atlas could buy—the cave was marked now. Not a blank space. Not a smuggler's secret. A name, in letters small but permanent:

*AMINA VASQUEZ CAVE — HERITAGE SITE — PROTECTED WATERS.*

Her grandmother's name, spoken in ink, on every chart in the territory. Felix had fought the registry office for four months to get it exactly right. They'd wanted to call it something neutral. Something geological. She'd told them, in writing, that geology was the least interesting thing about that cave, and that if they wanted the coordinates accurate they should understand what the coordinates had been *for*.

Below, the sea swallowed the shoreline, and Felix turned south.

* * *

Two hundred miles is nothing in a Cessna with company. It's a conversation, a nap, a parcel of dried fish passed hand to hand, a graduate student losing a battle with airsickness somewhere past the second ridge. Felix flew with the window cracked because the researchers wanted the smell of the country they were studying, and she understood that too—wanting to take a place in through the nose, not just the eyes.

Idris slept against the window, his case hugged to his chest like a child holds a thing they're afraid to set down. Once, waking briefly over the delta, he looked down at the braided channels glittering below and said, "My grandfather flew over this same water. Different direction, though. Coming to take, not—" He stopped, corrected himself. "Coming. Just coming."

"And you?"

"Going," he said. "It's a better verb."

Over the last range, the coastal town appeared the way it always did—white walls, tin roofs, the harbor crammed with boats that had never once wondered whether they belonged there. Felix radioed ahead, filed her flight plan openly, using her full name. *Joubert. Felix Joubert, operating out of the upper valley, carrying five souls.* Five souls. She'd counted herself among them without hesitation, which was either progress or a very slow recovery, and she'd decided it didn't matter which.

On final approach, Sefu leaned forward between the seats, the banana-leaf parcel clutched tight.

"Pilot Joubert," she said—Marta's coaching audible in every syllable—"when you come back, will you teach me to fly?"

Felix lined up on the runway. The wind was quartering, gentle, the kind of day the valley gave you when it wanted something.

"Come to the strip any Saturday," she said. "Door's unlocked."

The wheels touched. The town came up to meet them, and somewhere beyond the harbor, in a courthouse with bad coffee and good intentions, lawyers were waiting for dried fish and testimony and the truth—all of it, finally, in the open where it could do some work.

Felix shut down the engine and opened the door before anyone asked.

"After you," she said.
