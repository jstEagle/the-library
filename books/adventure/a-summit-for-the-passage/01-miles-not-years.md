## 1. Miles, Not Years

The Corvus lay at forty-one fathoms, and Sebastian Fairweather had learned to measure his life the way he measured a dive: in minutes of bottom time, in the slow arithmetic of air and depth, in the distance between one breath and the next.

He rolled off the gunwale of the *Magpie* at first light, before the wind could raise its argument with the sea, and let the cold take him the way it always did — a fist closing around his chest, then loosening into something almost like welcome. Below him the water went from green to blue to a darkness that had no name in any language he spoke. He equalized his ears, checked his gauges by feel, and began to descend along the shot line, hand over hand, into the place where his brother had learned the same lesson the sea teaches everyone eventually.

*Fourteen years ago*, he thought, and then corrected himself, because that was the old way of counting. *Four hundred and nine dives ago.* That was better. Miles, not years. Bottom time, not birthdays. A man could carry almost anything if he carried it in the right units.

The wreck resolved out of the gloom the way it always did — first the masts, furred with white anemones like the antlers of drowned deer, then the superstructure, then the long dark hull lying on its beam ends in the silt, as if the ship had simply lain down one day and decided to sleep. HMS *Corvus*, corvette of His Majesty's Navy, lost with all hands in the winter of '31, officially to a storm, unofficially to whatever the storm was covering for. The Navy had struck her from the rolls and left her to the fish. The fish had done a fair job. The fish always did.

Sebastian's job was less poetic and better paid. A salvage contract, three months old, to recover the brass fittings, the copper piping, the instruments — anything non-ferrous that the scrappers ashore would weigh and pay for. The Navy had signed off on it with a bureaucratic shrug. *Recover what you like, Mr. Fairweather. There is nothing aboard of any interest to His Majesty's Government.*

He had been diving her for eleven days.

He worked the aft section first, where the light from his lamp fell in a slow yellow cone across steel that had gone the color of old teeth. A school of rockfish scattered from a porthole like coins from a torn pocket. He moved through the corridor that had been the officers' mess, past a table still bolted to the deck, past a cabinet whose door hung open on shelves of nothing, and he felt the particular solitude of the deep-water diver settle over him — that clean, cold, absolute solitude that was the closest thing he had to a religion. Down here there were no letters to answer, no debts to dodge, no faces to remember how to arrange. There was only the work, and the work was honest. Metal was where you found it. You took it or you left it, and either way the sea did not lie to you about what it was.

His brother had loved this too. That was the joke of it, the ugly joke he never told anyone. Tom had loved it more.

Sebastian found the fitting at half past the twenty-minute mark.

He was working a corroded plate near the engine room hatch when his lamp caught a gleam that didn't belong — not the soft dull amber of corroded brass, but a hard, bright, machined shine, as if the thing had been polished yesterday. He worked it free with his pry bar, expecting another valve wheel, another length of pipe, another item for the manifest.

It was a fitting, all right. A threaded collar, brass, about the size of his fist, the kind used on high-pressure lines. But the thread pitch was wrong. He knew navy hardware the way a priest knows scripture — eleven years of salvage, three of them on naval contracts, and his hands could read a fitting's provenance the way a blind man reads a face. This was not a Corvus thread. This was not, as far as his hands could tell him, a navy thread at all. And stamped into the collar's face, small and deliberate, half-eaten by corrosion but unmistakable, was a maker's mark he had never seen: a stylized river, a wavy line running between two mountains, above a set of numbers.

*SR — 41.7*

He turned it in the cone of his lamplight. The silt drifted. Somewhere above him, faint as a thought, the surface swayed and shimmered, and forty-one fathoms of ocean pressed down on his shoulders like a patient hand.

He should have logged it. That was the discipline, the whole architecture of his life: log everything, manifest everything, let the paper do the remembering so a man didn't have to. He carried a slate for exactly this purpose. The fitting went on the slate, the fitting went in the crate, the fitting went ashore, the fitting got weighed and paid for and forgotten.

Instead, Sebastian Fairweather did something he had not done since he was a boy stealing apples: he put it in his suit pocket and told no one, not even the slate.

He wasn't sure why. That troubled him more than the fitting did. He was a man who trusted his reasons the way other men trusted their legs — they carried him where he needed to go, and he didn't ask them to explain themselves. But there was no reason here. Only a bright machined shine in a drowned ship, a thread that matched nothing, and a number that looked less like a part code and more like a coordinate.

*41.7.* The Corvus had gone down at roughly forty-one fathoms. Coincidence, probably. The sea was mostly coincidence with the volume turned down.

He finished the dive the way he finished every dive — methodically, working the manifest, filling the crate, watching his gauges with the devotion other men gave to prayer. But some part of his attention had gone quiet and separate, the way it did when a current shifted and he hadn't yet worked out which way it was taking him. On the ascent he paused at his fifteen-foot stop and hung in the green water, watching his own exhaust bubbles climb away from him toward the light, and he thought about Tom, who had also hung at stops, who had also watched his bubbles, who had also believed the sea would always give him one more breath because it always had.

The sea had changed its mind on a Tuesday, off a wreck shallower than this one. Sebastian had been topside. Sebastian had been the one who hauled the line, hand over hand, feeling the weight go slack at thirty feet, feeling the whole world go slack with it.

Bottom time, not birthdays. That was the trick. You counted the minutes you could survive and you didn't count the ones you couldn't, and eventually the numbers got large enough that they stood between you and the thing you were counting instead of.

* * *

He came up the ladder into a morning that smelled of diesel, kelp, and gull. The *Magpie* rode her moorings in the harbor roadstead with the patient ugliness of all working boats, and the town behind her — Port Meridian, they called it, though there was precious little meridian about it — was just beginning to stir. Tin roofs catching the light. Smoke from the cannery. The harbor bar opening its doors to the first of the fishermen, who drank the way they worked: early, steadily, and without discussion.

Sebastian hauled the crate aboard, hosed the silt off the day's take, and laid the fittings out on the deck to inventory. Brass and copper, dull and honest. He wrote each one into the manifest in his blocky, unlovely hand. Then he took the odd fitting from his pocket, set it apart from the others on an upturned bucket, and looked at it in the honest daylight.

The maker's mark was clearer now that it had dried. The wavy line between the mountains. *SR — 41.7.* The brass itself was a different alloy than navy stock — lighter, almost gold-colored, the kind of metal that came from ore bodies with a reputation.

He got out his reference book — a salvage man's bible of threads, gauges, and standards, twenty years of navy specifications between salt-swollen covers — and spent an hour with it over coffee that had gone the color of the harbor. Nothing matched. Not the thread, not the collar profile, not the stamp. It was as if the fitting had been made for a ship that existed nowhere in any registry, machined by hands that had never held a navy contract, and then installed aboard a navy vessel that had officially drowned of nothing but weather.

*There is nothing aboard of any interest to His Majesty's Government.*

Sebastian turned the fitting over and over. He was not a curious man by temperament — curiosity was a luxury, and he had spent his adult life learning to live without luxuries. But there was a difference between curiosity and discipline, and discipline said: *an anomaly unexamined is a hazard unlogged.* A diver who ignored an anomaly ended up like Tom. The sea didn't care which anomaly you skipped. It only kept the account.

He wrapped the fitting in an oily rag and stowed it in the chart locker, under the charts, which was where a man kept things he intended to think about and not things he intended to lose.

The day went on. It went on the way days went: a second dive on the forward hold, a run ashore for fuel and fresh water, an argument with the harbormaster about mooring fees that Sebastian won by losing — paying it, that is, without argument, because argument was a currency he'd stopped carrying years ago. He ate standing up at the harbor bar, a plate of fried fish and potatoes, and listened to the fishermen talk about weather, and prices, and weather again. Nobody talked about rivers. Nobody in Port Meridian ever talked about rivers. The sea had the town by the throat and everyone had agreed not to mention it.

He was on his second coffee, watching the light go long and amber across the water, when the barman nodded past him at the door.

"Evening, Mr. Fairweather. Your friend's back."

Sebastian turned.

The old man was in his usual corner, the one under the mounted marlin that some forgotten owner had nailed there a decade before anyone remembered why. Elias Vane. The prospectors and river men called him the Man Who Sold Rivers, though Sebastian had never asked why, and Vane had never offered. He came into the bar maybe twice a month, drank one glass of whatever was cheapest, and sat looking at the harbor with an expression Sebastian recognized from mirrors — the look of a man counting something that wasn't money.

Tonight he looked worse than usual. The skin over his cheekbones had gone the gray of wet ash, and his hands, wrapped around the glass, had a fine tremor that the glass amplified into a small continuous ringing against the bar. He was dressed as always in a canvas coat gone soft as cloth-of-gold with age, and he was watching the door, or watching the space just beside the door, in the way of a man who had learned that watching the door directly invited things through it.

Sebastian would have said they were acquaintances at best. They had shared a table twice. Vane had asked him once, out of nowhere, how deep a man could work with hard-hat gear and a tender above him, and Sebastian had answered, and Vane had nodded as if the answer confirmed something he'd already decided to lose. That was the whole of their history. A question about depth, and an answer.

But tonight Vane looked at him across the bar with an intensity that had weight to it, the way a crate has weight, the way a body has weight, and Sebastian understood, with the flat certainty of a man reading his own depth gauge, that the old man had been waiting for him. That the tremor in the hands was not entirely the drink. That the gray in the face was not entirely the years.

Vane lifted his glass a half inch. An invitation, or a summons. It was hard to tell the difference with a dying man, and Sebastian had the sudden, unaccountable certainty that Vane was dying — not in the slow way everyone was dying, but in the specific, scheduled, near-at-hand way that a tide is going out.

He should have finished his coffee and gone aboard. He was a man who had built his whole life on the principle that other people's emergencies were other people's. He had a crate to inventory, a manifest to close out, a fitting in the chart locker that didn't match any registry in the civilized world.

He picked up his coffee and carried it to the corner under the marlin.

"Mr. Vane."

"Fairweather." The voice was a dry riverbed of a voice, all gravel and memory of water. Vane looked at the empty chair across from him, then at Sebastian, and something moved behind his eyes — relief or dread, or the place where the two had worn through and become the same thing. "Sit down. I've been counting the days until you came in. And I haven't got many left to count."

Sebastian sat. Outside, the last light left the harbor, and the water went dark and patient, keeping, as always, everything it was ever given.

Vane leaned close, and Sebastian caught the smell of him — canvas and woodsmoke and something underneath, something chemical and wrong, the smell of a body already halfway to being an exhibit. The old man's hand came off the bar and closed around Sebastian's wrist with a strength that had no business surviving in it.

"You dive the Corvus," Vane said. It wasn't a question.

"I have the contract."

"You've been in her eleven days." Vane's eyes were pale, river-water eyes, and they did not blink enough. "Tell me what you found."

And Sebastian Fairweather, who had told the manifest everything and the slate everything and the sea everything, heard himself hesitate — heard the silence open in front of him like dark water, and the old man's question go down into it, falling and falling, toward something at the bottom that had a number stamped on it and a river between two mountains.

"Nothing," Sebastian said. "Nothing unusual."

Vane smiled. It was the worst thing Sebastian had seen all day, and he had spent the day at forty-one fathoms.

"Good," the old man said softly, and his grip tightened. "Then you're the only man in this town who hasn't. Which means you're the only man I can trust with it."
