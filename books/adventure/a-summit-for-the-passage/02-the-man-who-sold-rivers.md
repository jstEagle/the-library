## 2. The Man Who Sold Rivers

The cantina at Puerto Hondo had no name that anyone used, though a hand-painted sign above the door read *La Perla del Río* in letters the sun had eaten down to ghosts. The pearl of the river. There was no river within forty miles of it, only the harbor's brown slack water and the smell of diesel and drying nets, and Sebastian Fairweather had long suspected the name was a joke some dead man had played on the town and nobody had the heart to take down.

He came for three things: coffee strong enough to strip varnish, a table in the corner where his back touched wall, and an hour of not being anyone in particular. He got two out of three most nights.

The third thing failed him on a Tuesday in late October, when the dry season had burned the hills to the color of old bone and every boat in the harbor sat a hand-span higher on her waterline, waiting for rain that the radio promised and the sky withheld.

The stranger was already in the corner when Sebastian arrived — his corner, or near enough — slumped over a glass of something clear with a wet cloth pressed to his forehead like a compress for a wound no one could see. He was old the way driftwood is old, bleached and split and still stubbornly holding its shape. His hands were enormous, knuckles swollen into walnuts, and they trembled against the tabletop in small persistent rhythms, as if they were still panning something, still sifting, years after any river had let them go.

Sebastian took the next table over. It wasn't charity. It was proximity — the old man's breathing had a rattle in it that Sebastian recognized from salvage work, from pulling bodies off wrecks where the sea had kept them too long. That sound meant days, maybe less.

"You're the diver." The voice came out gravelled, half-swallowed. "The one on the Corvus."

"I'm a diver," Sebastian said, signaling Marta for coffee. "There are others."

"Not here there aren't." The old man lifted the cloth from his forehead and looked at him with eyes the pale washed-out blue of glacier melt. "Elias Vane."

"I know who you are." Everyone in Puerto Hondo knew who Elias Vane was, the way everyone knew which houses were empty and which dogs would bite. A prospector who'd gone up the Serrano country thirty years ago and come back with nothing but stories nobody bought and a limp he wouldn't explain. He drank alone. He talked to river miles the way other men talked to women. And lately he'd been dying in public, patiently, buying glasses he didn't finish and sitting until Marta turned the lamps down.

"Then you know I sold rivers once." Vane almost smiled. "Staked 'em, mapped 'em, named the ones nobody cared about. Now the rivers are selling me. Going up my price by the day." He coughed, deep and wet, and when it passed he gripped the table edge until the wood creaked. "Sit proper, diver. I've got something heavy and you're the only pair of shoulders left in this town I'd trust with it, and I don't even trust yours yet."

Marta brought the coffee and took Vane's glass away without being asked, and Sebastian understood she'd been managing this dying man for weeks the way dock towns manage their unclaimed dead — with efficiency and without ceremony.

"I'm not a priest," Sebastian said. "And I'm not a courier."

"No. You're worse. You're a man who keeps promises to drowned things." Vane's gaze went past him, through the fly-blown window, out to the harbor where the Corvus lay moored over her own grave, the salvage barge and its cranes dark against the last grey light. "I've watched you work. Three weeks now. You go down that wreck like you're visiting someone. Like you're counting."

Sebastian said nothing. The coffee was bitter and hot and he drank it anyway.

* * *

They talked for two hours, or rather Vane talked and Sebastian listened the way he listened to depth gauges — attentively, without committing to anything the instrument told him.

Vane had been a surveyor's man once, before the government maps went quiet about the upper Serrano. Before the dam, before the reservoir drowned the mining camps and the government redrew the country so neatly that a whole valley simply stopped existing on paper. He spoke in river miles the way other men spoke in years. Mile twelve, where the narrows ran green even in flood. Mile thirty-one, the gorge you portaged on the left unless you wanted your bones added to the canyon's collection. Mile forty, where an old friend lived who owed him nothing and would give everything.

"And past all that?" Sebastian asked, mostly to keep him talking, because a man talking wasn't rattling.

"The source." Vane said it quietly, the way men said the names of churches. "Where the Serrano is born. Spring-fed, out of a cavern in the limestone, cold enough to stop your heart if you swam it. I've been twice. Once at twenty-two, hungry and stupid. Once at fifty, hungry and honest." His hands had gone still on the table, which somehow was worse than the trembling. "The second time I found what I'd been looking for my whole life, and it wasn't gold."

"What was it?"

"A debt." He looked at Sebastian then, and behind the glacier-blue there was something burning low and steady, banked coals in a stove about to go cold forever. "Forty men went up that river in 1934 with a company survey crew. Forty came down. Do you understand me, diver? Not one. Not a body. Not a grave with a name on it. Forty men went into that canyon and the paperwork says they were never there at all."

Sebastian set his cup down. Outside, a patrol launch — navy grey, running lights already lit — slid across the harbor mouth, unhurried, and he felt the old instinct stir, the one that told him when a current had a hidden pull in it. "That's a story for a journalist. Or a judge. Not for a salvor."

"There are no judges upriver. And journalists drown." Vane laughed, and the laugh cost him; he folded forward over his arms and breathed like a bellows with a torn seam until it passed. When he straightened, his face had gone the color of tallow. "I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking you to carry something. Upstream. Before the dry season ends and the storms close the canyon for good. That's — " he counted on ruined fingers, lips moving — "nineteen days. Maybe twenty if the weather lies to us."

"I have a contract. The Corvus won't raise herself."

"The Corvus has been down sixty years. She'll keep." He leaned closer, and his breath smelled of the clear liquor and beneath it something medicinal and sweet. "I watched you dive today. Fourth time on the same section of hull. You found something. Don't tell me what. But you came up slow, and you sat on the deck after, and you held whatever it was in your fist like a man holding a question he can't put down." His eyes glittered. "I know that look. I wore it for thirty years. It doesn't wash off, diver. It just gets deeper."

Sebastian stood. "Good night, Mr. Vane."

"It isn't," Vane said mildly. "But thank you."

* * *

He refused him properly the next morning, because Sebastian Fairweather was a man who did things properly. He walked to the room over the chandlery where Vane lodged — four pesos a night, window facing away from the water, because even dying men have their superstitions — and he said the words plainly: *No. I'm sorry. Find someone else.*

Vane received it the way a rock receives weather. He was sitting on the bed's edge in his union suit, map cases and oilskin bundles spread around him on the floor like the wreckage of a life, sorting papers with those great trembling hands. On the sill, a brass compass caught the morning light, its needle swinging lazily, unable to settle.

"Course you won't," Vane said. "You've got your brother down there."

The words landed like a hook finding flesh. Sebastian kept his face still, but it took effort, real effort, the kind that shows in the jaw.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Danny Fairweather. Lost off Punta Reyes, eleven years back. Boat found, body never. You were nineteen." Vane didn't look up from his papers. "Marta talks. The harbormaster talks. Small towns, big mouths. I'm not throwing it at you, son. I'm telling you why I asked you and not some ferry rat with strong arms and no ballast." He finally raised his head. "You already carry a man who never came home. One more won't sink you. That's not a compliment. It's just arithmetic."

"That's none of your business."

"Nothing is anybody's business. That's the modern age." Vane gathered a bundle from the floor — oilskin-wrapped, tied with tarred twine, flat and rectangular and lighter than it looked, the way important things always are. He held it out. "Take it. Don't promise anything. Just hold it while I tell you what's inside, and if you still want to walk out after, walk."

Sebastian should have walked. He knew that even then, standing in that hot little room with dust motes turning in the light like something alive. Every instinct he'd built in eleven years of salvage — the discipline, the distance, the refusal to touch other people's cargo — all of it said *walk*. Divers who reached for things below stayed down. He'd learned that lesson on other people's graves.

He took the bundle.

It weighed less than a book. Through the oilskin he could feel the stiff grain of paper, the hard ridge of what might be a folded chart, corners worn round with handling. Thirty years of handling. A man's whole remaining life, pressed into a rectangle he could cover with both hands.

"Inside is a map," Vane said. "Hand-drawn, because the printed ones lie. River miles from the delta to the source, every camp, every rapid, every grave I know of. And underneath the map — " his voice dropped, went rough — "is a letter. Names. Dates. What happened at mile thirty-eight in the autumn of '34, and who signed the order, and who carried it out, and how the company bought the silence afterward with company scrip and company threats. It's addressed to no one, because everyone it belongs to is dead or scattered. Except it isn't mine to burn, and it isn't mine to bury, and God knows it isn't mine to give to the government, because the government is who wrote it in the first place."

"Then give it to a newspaper."

"A newspaper man came to see me in June." Vane smiled, and it was terrible. "Very sympathetic. Took notes. Left on the afternoon steamer. My guess is he reached the city and made a phone call, and my guess is the call was taken by a man in uniform, because since then I've been followed by a gentleman who shines his shoes more than is natural for this climate." He tapped his own chest, where the rattle lived. "This will kill me before they do, probably. Probably. But I'd rather not bet Ruth's future on *probably*."

"Ruth?"

"My daughter." Something moved through the old man's face then, quick and painful, a fish under ice. "Who hasn't spoken to me in nine years and lives in the capital and thinks I'm a drunk and a liar, and who is right on both counts, and who is going to inherit this whether she wants it or not. Unless you get it upstream first. Then she inherits the truth instead. Which is heavier, but it floats."

Outside, a gull screamed over the harbor, and somewhere a winch started up, and the ordinary world went on making its ordinary noise while an old man handed a stranger his confession across a four-peso room.

"Nineteen days," Vane said. "The dry season ends, the storms come, and the canyon closes till spring. If I'm dead by then — when I'm dead — there'll be men looking for this. Men with clean shoes. They'll search my room, my pockets, my coffin if they're thorough. They mustn't find it. And the people it belongs to — the families, the descendants, the ones upriver at the delta who still say the lost men's names at supper — they deserve to have it carried home, not confiscated." He pressed the bundle back toward Sebastian's chest. "Carry it up. That's all. You don't have to read it. You don't have to believe it. Just carry it, the way you'd carry a drowned man to shore. You're good at that. I could see it the first day I watched you come up the Corvus line with your burden hanging under you, gentle as a midwife."

"I haven't agreed."

"No," Vane agreed pleasantly. "Not yet."

* * *

Sebastian made it three steps toward the door.

He would remember that afterward — that he had actually refused, had said the word *no* a second time, had gotten his hand on the latch — when the sound came from behind him. Not loud. A soft, wet collapse, like a sack of grain going over, and the clatter of the compass hitting the floorboards, its needle spinning free.

Vane was on the floor between the bed and the wall, on his side, knees drawn up, one huge hand clawing at the oilskin bundle Sebastian still held. His face had gone grey-blue around the mouth and his breath came in shallow, tearing pulls, each one with that rattle deeper in it, closer to the end of the sounding line.

Sebastian had pulled eleven bodies out of the sea. He knew exactly what this was, and he knew there was nothing in his hands that could stop it, and he knelt anyway, rolling the old man flat, loosening the collar of the union suit, shouting down the stairs for Marta, for anyone, his voice cracking the dusty quiet of the chandlery.

Vane's hand closed on his wrist. The grip was astonishing — a drowning man's grip, a grip with no tomorrow in it, all of it spent now.

"Listen." Each word cost him visibly, coins paid out one at a time from a purse nearly empty. "Nineteen days. Mile forty. Old Tomas. He'll know the rest." A tear of pure effort rolled sideways into his ear. "Don't give it to anyone in uniform. Anyone. Not even — " the breath tore — "not even if they cry."

"Doctor's coming," Sebastian lied, because Marta's footsteps were on the stairs and she'd know what to do better than he would, and because lying to dying men was a mercy he'd performed before and would again.

Vane's eyes fixed on him, glacier-blue going distant, focusing on something past the ceiling, past the walls, maybe up a river only he could see. "You counted bottom time instead of birthdays," he whispered. "I heard you tell Marta. Boy — count miles instead. Miles you can make good. Years just... run out on you."

Then his eyes rolled, and the grip on Sebastian's wrist eased from drowning-man to sleeping-child, and Marta pushed through the door with her sleeves already rolled and began giving orders in a voice that brooked no argument, and Sebastian Fairweather knelt on the floorboards of a four-peso room holding an oilskin-wrapped confession he had refused twice and could not, somehow, put down.

* * *

They laid Vane in the bed. The doctor came within the hour, an aging naval reserve man with kind eyes and careful hands, who listened to the ruin of the old prospector's chest and gave Marta instructions and said, outside the door, in the corridor's bad light: "A day. Two, if he's stubborn. He's been stubborn his whole life, I hear."

Sebastian went down to the harbor because he couldn't stand still and couldn't sit either. The evening came on copper and violet, and the Corvus lay black against it, her masts like the ribs of something that had swallowed its own history. Somewhere out on the water a patrol launch idled, running lights doubled tonight, and he watched it longer than he meant to.

The bundle sat inside his jacket against his ribs, light as a sin, warm from his body heat, patient as ballast.

He hadn't promised. That was the thing he kept returning to, turning over like a stone in a current. He hadn't promised. A man couldn't be bound by what he hadn't sworn. And yet walking the length of the quay, past the net-menders and the fuel dock and the shuttered fish market, he felt it working into him the way a splinter works into a palm — small, foreign, invisible from the outside, and impossible to leave alone. Every time he forgot it, some motion of his hand or turn of his mind pressed it deeper.

At the foot of the gangplank he stopped. Below him, through the hull, the Corvus waited with her secrets, and his contract waited with her, and the neat arithmetic of his old life — dives logged, fittings catalogued, birthdays measured in bottom time — waited to resume as though nothing had happened in a four-peso room over the chandlery.

Against his ribs, the map rode silent. Nineteen days.

Behind him, up the street, a lamp was lit in the chandlery window where a dying man was being made comfortable, and further off, at the harbor mouth, the patrol launch turned slowly, its searchlight sweeping the black water once, twice, and coming to rest — briefly, casually, unmistakably — on the gangplank where Sebastian Fairweather stood.

He stood in the light for a long moment, a man holding something that wasn't his, watched by something patient.

Then he climbed aboard, and did not sleep.
