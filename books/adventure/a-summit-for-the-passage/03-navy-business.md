## 3. Navy Business

The fog came in off the harbor before dawn the way it always did in that season, thick as wool and smelling of kelp and cold iron, and I was under the Corvus again by first light because sleep had become a country I could visit but no longer live in.

I'd been on her three weeks. Three weeks of counting bottom time instead of birthdays, of descending through water the color of old bottle glass to a ship that lay on her beam ends in forty fathoms like a drunk who'd finally found a bed he liked. The navy had written her off as a loss after the grounding — hull breach, flooding, all hands ashore, no scandal, just bad luck and bad charts — and then they'd sold the salvage rights at auction for less than the value of her propeller bronze, and I'd bought them with nearly everything I had.

Which is another way of saying I had nothing left but the work.

That morning I was working the aft magazine passage, clearing silt from a bulkhead seam with a hand rake, moving slow, breathing slower. Down there you learn a kind of arithmetic. Your gauge tells you how many minutes you own; your body tells you what they cost. Everything else — the fog above, the cantina, the dying man's oilskin packet sewn into the lining of my spare jacket back aboard the *Pelican* — everything else belongs to the surface, and the surface can wait.

I wish it had waited longer.

* * *

I felt the boat before I saw anything wrong. A change in the slap of water against the *Pelican*'s hull, transmitted down my tether line like a word passed hand to hand. Someone had come alongside. My tender, Miguel, should have hailed them, and his silence told me more than any shout would have.

I came up the ladder slowly, letting the sea drain off me, giving myself time to arrange my face into the blankness that serves a man better than a smile does. The fog had thinned to rags. Standing on my deck, in a gray uniform pressed sharp enough to draw blood, was a man I'd never seen before and recognized immediately anyway, the way you recognize weather.

He was perhaps fifty, tall without being big, built along the lines of a mast — all spar and rigging, nothing wasted. Silver at the temples, cropped close. His cap sat square. His shoes were dry. That last detail I noted the way a sailor notes anything: automatically, and with suspicion. A man who boards a working salvage boat in harbor fog and leaves with dry shoes is a man who has never once been surprised by the world.

"Sebastian Fairweather." He said it not as a question but as an inventory item being checked off. "Lieutenant Commander Adair Hale. Naval Intelligence." He did not offer a hand, which saved us both the theater of shaking one. "You're up early."

"Bottom time doesn't keep office hours." I stripped my gloves and hung them over the rail to drip. "And this is my office."

"So it is." He turned a slow half-circle, taking in the deck: the compressor, the coiled umbilicals, the crates of recovered fittings lashed under canvas, Miguel standing very still by the wheelhouse with his arms crossed and his eyes doing all the talking his mouth knew better than to do. "The Corvus," Hale said. "How is she?"

"Broken. But honest about it."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning she's exactly what the survey says she is. A grounded cruiser with a split hull. Nothing down there but rust, silt, and forty years of things that swim."

Something moved behind his eyes — not a flinch, nothing so generous. A recalibration. "I'm glad to hear it," he said, "because as of yesterday morning, the Corvus is closed. Salvage rights suspended pending a records review. You'll receive the notice by post."

The words went into me like cold water into a boot. I kept my face still. "On what grounds? I hold clear title. Paid auction fees, posted bond—"

"You hold paper," Hale said pleasantly. "Paper is a courtesy the government extends itself. There are fittings aboard that vessel that don't appear in her manifest, Mr. Fairweather. Brasswork, mostly. Non-standard castings. We'd like to account for them before civilians go prying."

Brasswork. The word landed in me with a small, specific weight, because there was a brass fitting sitting in a coffee tin under my bunk at that moment — a valve collar, non-standard casting, stamped with a foundry mark that didn't match any record in the naval registry, the same anomaly I'd pocketed three days ago and told myself meant nothing, or meant money, or meant a story worth chasing once the salvage paid out. I thought of it sitting there in its tin like a coin on a dead man's eye.

"I haven't seen any brasswork out of the ordinary," I said.

"No," Hale agreed. "I imagine you haven't." He let that sit between us while a gull screamed somewhere in the fog and the tide sucked softly at the pilings. Then he took a folded paper from inside his coat — not a warrant, not a notice, just paper, folded in quarters, and he held it without offering it. "There's also the matter of Elias Vane."

The harbor went quiet. Not actually — the water still moved, the gulls still argued — but something in my chest pulled shut like a hatch.

"What about him?"

"He died Tuesday night. St. Brendan's ward, heart failure, though the attending physician notes he was lucid until near the end. He was seen speaking with several people in his last week." Hale's voice stayed level, unhurried, the voice of a man reading a shipping schedule aloud. "A priest. A nurse. And a salvage diver who buys drinks for old men in dockside cantinas and listens to their stories."

"I buy drinks for anyone with a story," I said. "It's cheaper than buying books."

"It is." He unfolded the paper halfway, enough that I could see it was a list, handwritten, names in a column. He looked at it, then folded it away again. "Vane was a prospector. Forty years ago he worked the upper Serrano country, before the government flooded the valley for the hydro scheme. In his last days he talked about rivers. About going home upstream. About a debt." Hale's pale eyes came up and settled on mine, and I understood that everything before this had been the approach run, and this was the summit. "Dying men talk, Mr. Fairweather. It's the living I'm interested in. Specifically, whether any of them accepted deliveries."

"If you think he gave me something, search the boat."

"Oh, I intend to know everything that comes off this boat," Hale said, "but no, I won't be searching it today." He smiled, and it was the worst thing I'd seen all week, because it was patient. It was the smile of a man who has decided the fish isn't going anywhere. "Confiscation creates martyrs, and paperwork, and questions. I don't want your property, Fairweather. I want your good judgment. The Corvus is closed. The Serrano headwaters are closed — have been since the dam went in, restricted military watershed, though I doubt the signs survive up there anymore. Whatever a dying prospector whispered to you over cheap rum, whatever map or confession or fairy tale he was peddling to pay his bar tab — it's worthless. Worse than worthless. It's a debt someone else incurred, and debts die with the debtor." He straightened his cap against a wind that hadn't reached us yet. "Carry nothing upstream. Ask nothing about rivers. Finish your grief over that shipwreck, collect your bronze, and go home to wherever home is."

"And if I don't?"

He considered the question honestly, which I'll grant him. He looked out at the fog lifting off the water, at the gray line where the harbor opened toward the sea, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped into something almost gentle, which frightened me more than the rest of it put together.

"Then I suppose we'll both find out what kind of man you are. I've made a long study of that question in other men. It's remarkable how few of them turn out to be heroes, and how many of them turn out to be tired." He stepped to the rail, paused, and added, without turning around: "Your brother drowned, I understand. Off Cape Merrow, when you were boys. I mention it only because men who've lost someone to water tend to develop superstitions about it. They think the river gives things back. It doesn't. It only lends."

Then he went down my ladder, stepped into a navy launch that materialized out of the last of the fog as if it had been waiting there the whole time — it had — and was gone, leaving nothing behind but a wake that rocked the *Pelican* gently, like a hand patting a shoulder.

* * *

Miguel spoke first, in the low Spanish he used when the subject required privacy. "You knew him?"

"Never saw him before."

"You lie like a man who has practiced."

"I'm not lying. I just already knew what he'd look like." I went below, past the coffee tin, not touching it, feeling its presence the way you feel a bruise. I sat on my bunk with the hatch open and watched the water slide past the port, and I made myself think it through the way I'd learned to think through a dive plan: hazards first, then depths, then air.

Hale knew about Vane. Not guessed — knew. Knew the cantina, knew the conversations, knew enough to fold a list of names into his pocket like a menu. Which meant either the hospital leaked, or the cantina did, or Vane had been watched long before he ever got sick, which raised a question I didn't like: why does Naval Intelligence keep a file on a broke old prospector forty years after the valley he worked went under the water?

Because of what's under the water. Because of what the flooding covered besides the river.

And if they'd watched Vane that long, they'd watched whoever he spoke to. Which meant the oilskin packet sewn into my spare jacket wasn't a secret. It was bait on a hook, and I was already swallowing.

I should have burned it that afternoon. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is the whole point of what came later. I stood on deck with the tin in one hand and the jacket in the other, and I calculated — coldly, the way I do everything — that a man who confiscates nothing is a man who fears evidence more than theft, and a man who fears evidence believes it's real. Vane hadn't been peddling a fairy tale. He'd been carrying proof of something worth forty years of surveillance and a lieutenant commander's patience, and he'd chosen me, a stranger, a rootless diver with no country and no family left, precisely because I was nobody. Because nobody gets overlooked.

He'd been wrong about that. Or maybe he'd known exactly what he was doing, and the watching was the point, and the packet was a fuse he'd lit knowing it would take weeks to burn upstream.

I put the jacket back. I put the tin back. I told Miguel the Corvus job was suspended and watched him curse the navy with real feeling, having no idea how close he'd come to sharing its attention.

That evening the mail launch brought the official notice, as promised — salvage rights suspended pending review, no date given, no appeal process named. Paper as a courtesy the government extends itself. I read it twice and burned it in the galley stove, which changed nothing and felt necessary anyway.

And near midnight, when the fog had come all the way back in and the harbor lamps were halos without faces, I heard footsteps on the pier outside the bunkhouse — unhurried, deliberate, stopping just long enough to make themselves heard, then moving on. When I looked out, the walkway was empty. On the planking where the steps had paused, the fog had left a ring of damp, round and neat, like a glass set down and taken away.

They weren't going to take anything from me. They were going to wait for me to carry it to them.

I went back inside and began, quietly, to plan a trip upriver I hadn't agreed to take — checking tide tables for a river two hundred miles north, pricing freight canoes, remembering rapids I'd run as a younger man with a brother still alive to argue with me about the lines. Somewhere in the middle of that night Ruth Vane would be standing in my doorway with her father's telegrams and her father's temper, though I didn't know it yet. All I knew was the chill of it, the particular chill of being watched by something patient, and the splinter of a promise working its way deeper under my skin the harder I tried to pretend it wasn't there.
