## 4. A Daughter Named Ruth

The knock came at half past six in the morning, and it was not a knock so much as a verdict.

Sebastian had been awake anyway — he was always awake by then, thirty years of diving having trained his body to surface before the light did — and he lay in his bunk listening to the sound repeat itself: three blows, hard enough to rattle the tin lamp hanging from the ceiling beam, hard enough to say *I know you're in there, and I am not going away.*

The bunkhouse smelled of kerosene and wet wool and the ghost of last night's coffee. Rain worked at the corrugated roof with small patient fingers. He could see, through the fogged window above the sink, a figure standing on the mud apron outside: a woman, hatless, her dark hair plastered flat by the drizzle, a canvas satchel clutched against her ribs like something she'd rescued from a fire.

He knew who she was before he opened the door. There was only one person left in the world who might come looking for Elias Vane.

He opened it anyway.

"You're Fairweather." Not a question. Her voice was low and even, the kind of voice that had learned not to waste itself. Up close she was younger than her posture suggested — mid-thirties maybe — with her father's same weathered brown complexion and none of his looseness. Elias Vane had moved through the world like a man wading upstream. This woman stood like someone who had already reached the far bank and found nothing there worth keeping.

"Sebastian Fairweather," he said.

"I know. I've been asking about you since Tuesday." She looked past him into the bunkhouse, cataloguing it the way he'd catalogue a wreck: bunks, stove, chart table, the oilskin bundle lying where he'd left it on the table, wrapped and re-wrapped a dozen times as though wrapping could make it less heavy. Her eyes stopped on it and stayed. "That's my father's."

"It's mine now."

Something moved behind her face — not surprise; she'd expected the refusal, had perhaps rehearsed against it on the walk over — but a kind of grim confirmation, the way a diver feels the hull give under his hand and knows the breach is worse than reported.

"He sold it to you, did he." Flat. "My father. Who wouldn't sell a day of labor without three signatures and a sermon."

"He didn't sell it. He traded it."

"For what?"

"A promise."

She laughed once, short and bloodless. "A promise. From a stranger. In a cantina. While he was dying." She stepped forward, and he stepped back, and that small retreat cost him something — he felt it go, some private territory ceded — and she came inside uninvited, setting her satchel on the chart table beside the oilskin with the deliberation of a surgeon laying out instruments. "I'm Ruth Vane. You'll have guessed. And you're going to tell me exactly what happened at the Anchorline Cantina on Thursday night, from the moment he sat down across from you, and you are going to leave nothing out, because everyone else who knew anything about my father's last week is either dead or lying, and I have run out of patience for both."

* * *

Ruth Vane had spent nine days learning how men lied about her father, and she had gotten good at hearing the seams.

The harbormaster had lied first, politely, with paperwork: no record of Elias Vane docking at the fuel pier, though she'd held the receipt for his last fuel purchase in her own hand. The coroner's clerk had lied second, lazily: heart failure, natural causes, signed by a doctor whose stamp was two years expired. The bartender at the Anchorline had lied best of all, fluently, with free drinks and a shrug — *old man drank himself into the ground, happens every week, love* — while his eyes kept sliding toward the door whenever a navy launch went by the window.

So when Sebastian Fairweather began to talk, she listened the way she'd learned to listen all week: for what he said, and for what he arranged around what he said, the way a man arranges furniture to hide a hole in the floor.

Fairweather talked like a man counting. That was her first impression, sitting across the chart table from him while rain ticked on the roof. He gave her times. He gave her distances. He described her father's hands — the tremor in the right one, the way Elias kept pressing it flat against the tabletop as if pinning down a fish — with a precision that made her throat tighten, because she hadn't seen those hands in eleven years and here was a stranger who knew their last night better than she ever would.

"He said he was dying," Fairweather said. "Said it the way you'd report a tide. No drama. He said the doctors gave him a season and the season was nearly spent. Then he put this—" he touched the oilskin, not opening it "—on the table between us and told me it was a map to the headwaters of the Serrano. Told me the government flooded the strike out of existence forty years ago and drowned every record of it. Told me he wanted it carried upstream before the dry season ended."

"And you said no."

"I said no."

"Why?"

"Because I don't take contracts from dying strangers in cantinas. Because I had a salvage claim on the Corvus that was eating everything I had. Because—" he paused, and she watched him decide whether to spend the next words or hold them back, and watched him spend them, "—because promises are how men drown. I've buried one brother already. I know the arithmetic."

"And then?"

"And then he stood up to leave, and he fell." Fairweather's voice did not change, but his hands did — they flattened on the table, both of them, pressing down the way Elias's hand had pressed. "Before he hit the floor he grabbed my wrist and said, *carry it up*. Twice. Then nothing. The doctor called it his heart. Maybe it was."

"Maybe."

"Maybe," he agreed, and met her eyes, and she understood that this was the closest thing to an oath he intended to offer her, and that it was, in its way, an honest one. He wasn't telling her it was murder. He wasn't telling her it wasn't. He was telling her he didn't know, and that the not-knowing sat in him like ballast.

She opened the satchel.

* * *

Sebastian watched her lay the telegrams out on his chart table one by one, squaring their edges, and thought: *she does grief the way I do salvage. Methodically. Everything logged.*

There were five of them, yellow paper gone soft at the folds, handled so many times the ink had begun to blur.

"Read them," she said. "Out loud. I want to hear how they sound in another voice."

He picked up the first. Dated fourteen months ago. **ARRIVED PORT SAFE STOP HAVE WHAT WE DISCUSSED STOP DO NOT TRUST THE MAN IN UNIFORM STOP WILL EXPLAIN WHEN HOME.** It was addressed to a Ruth Vane at an address in the capital, four hundred miles north.

"You never got these," he said.

"They were returned undelivered. All five. I found them in his effects when the shipping agent finally released them — forwarded twice, refused once, sitting in a depot drawer." Her finger came down on the third telegram, its paper worn nearly translucent. "This one. Read this one again."

**STOPPED AT MILE FORTY STOP OLD FRIEND STILL LIVING STOP HE REMEMBERS EVERYTHING STOP THE SURVEYOR'S OFFICE HAS NOT FORGOTTEN EITHER STOP COMING DOWN WITH PROOF STOP WATCH FOR A LAUNCH WITH NO NAME.**

"The surveyor's office," Sebastian said slowly.

"You recognize it."

"I recognize the name Hale gave me when he boarded my boat two days ago. Lieutenant Commander Adair Hale. Naval intelligence." He heard his own voice go flat and careful. "He came aboard the Corvus site. Confiscated nothing. Asked about rivers."

"Asked about rivers," Ruth repeated.

"Specifically whether I'd had recent contact with anyone connected to the Serrano headwaters." He looked down at the telegrams, at the phrase *a launch with no name*, and remembered the gray shape that had idled past his mooring buoy at dusk two nights running, running no lights, making no wake worth mentioning. "Miss Vane. When did your father die relative to sending these?"

"The last one is dated nineteen days ago. He died twelve days ago." She gathered the telegrams back into their stack with quick, precise movements, and for the first time her composure showed a hairline crack — just at the mouth, a tightening, quickly mastered. "Seven days. Seven days between *coming down with proof* and a dead man on a cantina floor. And in those seven days, according to the harbormaster, a naval officer made inquiries at the fuel office, the pilot registry, and the post. My father's inquiries. About my father."

The rain thickened against the roof. Somewhere down the dock a winch started up, groaning through its morning work, and neither of them looked toward the window.

"So you think your father was followed," Sebastian said. "From the headwaters. Down the river. Into port. And that someone in uniform made sure he never delivered whatever proof he was carrying."

"I think," Ruth Vane said, "that my father spent the last year of his life walking backward, watching the trail behind him. I think he trusted exactly one stranger enough to hand him a map and a dying wish. And I think that stranger has been sitting on that map for twelve days doing nothing, while the dry season runs out and whoever killed my father decides what to do about the man holding it." She stood. She was not tall, but standing, she filled the bunkhouse the way cold water fills a boot. "So here is what happens next, Mr. Fairweather. Either you tell me what's on that map, or I go to Hale myself and tell him you have it, and let you explain to naval intelligence why a civilian salvager is hoarding documents connected to a closed government operation."

It was a good threat. Well-built, load-bearing. He found himself almost admiring it.

"You'd be handing them exactly what they want," he said.

"Yes. And if you keep stonewalling me, I'll do it anyway, because a bad outcome I cause is better than a bad outcome I wait for." Something flickered across her face then — exhaustion, or its cousin — and she sat back down, abruptly, as though the standing had cost more than she'd budgeted. "I haven't slept in a proper bed in nine days. I've been sleeping in the ferry terminal. I ate half a roll yesterday. I am not threatening you because I enjoy it, Mr. Fairweather. I'm threatening you because it's the only tool I've got that works on men like you."

"Men like me."

"Men who count things instead of feeling them. My father was one. I grew up with one." She looked at the oilskin bundle, and her voice dropped, and for a moment she sounded very young and very tired. "He sent me telegrams for a year. Five of them. Every one returned. Do you know what that does to a person? To learn that your father was trying to reach you — trying, the whole last year of his life — and the world just... declined to deliver him? I could have had a year. Fourteen months. Whatever was between us, we could have spent it. Instead I get a shipping crate of his effects and a bartender's shrug."

Sebastian said nothing. There was nothing in his experience equal to it — no, that wasn't true. There was one thing. One telegram of his own, years ago, from a river authority in a country he'd been guiding in, informing him that his brother's boat had been recovered, that his brother had not been. He remembered reading it standing in a post office, and the peculiar silence of the room, and how the clerk had asked him twice if he wanted to send a reply.

"My brother drowned in a river," he heard himself say. "Fourteen years ago. Guiding trip. Flash flood in a canyon section nobody should have been running at that water level, but the client wanted his photographs and Tom wanted his fee." He turned the oilskin bundle over once beneath his hands, not unwrapping it. "I've measured my life in bottom time since. Minutes down, minutes owed. It keeps things simple. Your father offered me a contract that can't be measured, and I refused it, and then he died holding my wrist, and now the debt exists whether I signed for it or not. That's what's on the table. That's the whole of it."

Ruth studied him for a long moment. Outside, the rain eased, and the light through the fogged window turned the color of pewter.

"The map," she said. "Show me."

He unwrapped the oilskin. Inside, the paper was old and soft, drawn in Elias Vane's cramped, confident hand — river miles marked in a neat column, abandoned camps named in fading ink, and at the top of the page, where a title should be, just two words: *the source.* He spread it flat between them on the chart table, weighting the corners with a coffee cup, a lamp base, her satchel, his knife.

Ruth leaned over it, and Sebastian watched her father's face emerge in hers — the same concentration, the same hunger held on a short leash.

"Mile forty," she said, touching a mark. "Old Tomas. The telegram."

"And here—" his finger traced the river's line upward, through narrows he knew by reputation and a gorge he knew by dread, "—here's the portage around the falls. Three days' carry, if the weather holds. We've got maybe five weeks of dry season left. After that, the storms come and the river takes the upper canyon back, and nobody goes up or comes down until spring."

"We," she said.

He looked at her. She looked back, chin level, daring him to retract it.

"I need someone who can read a ledger and photograph a grave marker," he said. "You need someone who can read water. Neither of us trusts the other, and that's fine — trust is cargo, and cargo gets loaded later. But we leave within two days, ahead of whatever patrol schedule Hale is running, or we don't leave at all."

Ruth Vane put her hand flat on the map, over the words *the source*, the way a person lays a hand on a coffin lid.

"Two days," she said. "And Mr. Fairweather — if I find out you had any part in how my father died, the river won't be what kills you."

Outside, far down the harbor, a gray launch with no name slid out past the breakwater and turned north, toward the river.
