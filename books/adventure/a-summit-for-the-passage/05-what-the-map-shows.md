## 5. What the Map Shows

Sebastian spread the oilskin on the table in his bunkhouse and weighted its corners with four coffee mugs, a tin of pipe tobacco, and the brass fitting he'd pulled from the *Corvus* — the one that didn't match any record. Outside, the harbor slapped against pilings in a rhythm like a slow heart. Rain had come in off the strait at dusk, and the lamp above the table hissed and guttered every time the wind found a gap in the boards.

The map itself was drawn on the back of a survey chart so old the paper had gone the color of weak tea. Someone — Elias Vane, Sebastian assumed, though the hand was steady enough to be a draftsman's — had inked a river down its length in a fine, confident line. The Serrano. It began in the mountains as a forked thread and ended, far to the south, in the delta's braided fingers. Along the river's spine, small notations climbed like rungs on a ladder.

*RM 12 — old landing. RM 27 — camp, burned. RM 31 — falls, portage left. RM 44 — narrows. RM 58 — the source.*

That last one was circled. Not once but three times, the pen pressed so hard it had nearly torn the paper, as if the man drawing it had wanted to make certain that whatever happened to him — and something had happened to him, Sebastian was sure of that now — the circle would survive.

"He wrote river miles," Sebastian said. "Not dates, not landmarks. River miles."

"Is that strange?"

"It's what a boatman does." He turned the map a quarter turn, studying the contour shading beneath the ink. "A prospector thinks in claims and elevations. A surveyor thinks in coordinates. But a man who's run a river — who's walked it in low water and poled it in high — he counts miles. Twelve miles to the landing. Twenty-seven to the burned camp. You measure a river by how far your arms have carried you."

Ruth Vane sat across from him with her coat still on, damp at the shoulders, her hat dripping onto the floorboards. She hadn't taken it off. Taking it off would mean staying, and staying was a decision she hadn't made yet. Sebastian could see the decision sitting on her like a weight — the same way grief sits on people who haven't yet decided to let it change them.

"My father never taught me to read a river," she said.

Sebastian didn't answer. Some things you let lie where they fall.

He bent closer. The chart beneath the ink was a government survey — he could see the faint stamp of the Hydrographic Office, half worn away — and the river it depicted was not the river Elias had drawn. Or rather, it was, but the survey showed the lower reaches only, the wide navigable water, and stopped at a jagged line of hatching labeled in faded type: *CANYON COUNTRY — UNSURVEYED.*

"Your father drew the upper river from memory," Sebastian said. "There's no chart of it. There never was one made public." He tapped the hatched line. "The government surveyed to mile forty and then stopped. You don't stop surveying a river unless someone tells you to stop."

"Or unless there's nothing worth finding."

"Rivers always have something worth finding. That's why they put the stops in."

He traced the route with one finger, and the terrain rose in his mind the way it always did — not as lines on paper but as smell and sound and the particular ache of a portage pack cutting into his shoulders. He had guided on the Serrano's tributaries for three seasons in another life, before the salvage work, before his hands learned to prefer the cold patience of diving to the hot patience of paddling. He knew the canyon country the way you know a face you dreamed about once and never quite forgot: not the details, but the character of it. Limestone walls that funneled wind into a single screaming note. Gravel bars that shifted every spring flood, so that last year's landing was this year's rapids. Water the color of glacier milk, so opaque that a man could stand in three feet of it and not see his own boots.

"Here." His finger stopped at the notation for mile thirty-one. *Falls, portage left.* "I know this. Or I know what it used to be. There's a gorge above the narrows — the locals called it the Throat. Unrunnable. The portage trail, if it still exists, climbs the left bank through scree for the better part of two days." He looked up. "Your father marked it like a man who'd carried a boat over it himself."

"He was gone four years once," Ruth said quietly. "When I was eleven. My mother told me he was surveying. When he came back his hands were ruined — the skin cracked so deep it bled through his gloves. He never said where he'd been. After that he started drinking, and after that he started talking in his sleep about a river, and after that—" She stopped. Her jaw worked once. "After that we didn't talk much at all."

Sebastian straightened. The lamp flame steadied, and in its light the map seemed almost alive, the inked river pulsing faintly like a vein.

"There's something else," he said. He turned the map toward the light and tilted it. Beneath the ink, where the lamp shone through the thin paper, ghost lines appeared — faint pencil marks beneath the pen, corrections and second thoughts. At the circle marked *the source*, the pencil beneath read something the pen had covered.

Ruth leaned across the table. "Can you read it?"

He fetched a magnifying loupe from his chart drawer and held it over the spot. The pencil ghost was faint, hurried, the letters half-formed. But they were there.

*Not gold. Tell them it was never gold.*

He read it aloud, and the words dropped into the room like stones into still water.

Ruth sat back slowly. "Tell who?"

"I don't know." But even as he said it, his eyes went to the lower margin of the map, where Elias had inked a row of small marks — not river miles, he saw now, but something else. Tiny crosses. He counted them. Forty.

Forty crosses, strung along the river's lower reaches like beads on a string.

* * *

Ruth could not stop looking at the crosses.

Her father's handwriting was as familiar to her as her own reflection — the cramped, forward-leaning script he'd used on the backs of envelopes, on the margins of newspapers, on the eleven birthday cards she'd received over the years and the four she hadn't. She had spent her whole adult life telling herself she didn't miss him, that a man who disappeared into the mountains for four years and came back with ruined hands and a drinking habit had made his choices, and she had made hers, and the distance between them was a thing they had built together, stone by stone, and neither of them was more to blame than the other.

That was a lie. She had known it was a lie for six years, since the Christmas she hadn't gone home, and she had carried the lie the way you carry a stone in your boot — painfully at first, then numbly, then as if the stone were simply part of your foot.

Now the stone had a name. It was called *forty crosses*, and it sat in the margin of her dead father's map, and she understood — in the way you understand things at two in the morning by lamplight, with rain on the roof and a stranger across the table — that whatever Elias Vane had found up that river had eaten him alive for thirty years, and she had not been there when it started eating.

"Tell them it was never gold," she said aloud. "What does that even mean?"

"Maybe he found gold and it wasn't what he thought it was." Sebastian had taken off his boots — she'd noticed this with some distant, absurd part of her mind, the way you notice things when your mind is trying to protect you — and sat with his feet flat on the cold boards like a man preparing to be comfortable for a long time. "Or maybe he found something that looked like gold from a distance. Ore that glittered. A story that glittered." He was quiet a moment. "Men have killed for less than a glittering story."

"Killed." She heard how the word came out of her — flat, hard, a nail driven in. "You think someone killed him."

"I think a man who spent thirty years drawing a map in secret doesn't die of a bad heart in a dockside cantina three days after he finally shows it to someone." Sebastian's face was unreadable in the way of men who worked alone and liked it. "I think the heart gave out because something else was already working on it. Fear, maybe. Or poison. I'm not a doctor. But I've watched enough men die to know the difference between a body quitting and a body being quit."

She should have argued. Some part of her, the part that had spent years building walls of sensible explanations — he was old, he drank, he had a bad heart, the doctors said so — reached for the walls and found them gone. The telegram she'd shown him had cracked them: *TRUST NO ONE IN UNIFORM. COME BEFORE THE WATER DROPS. — E.V.* Her father had been afraid, and she had read the telegram, and she had told herself it was the drink talking, because believing it was the drink was easier than believing her father had spent his last months terrified and alone and reaching out to a daughter who hadn't written in two years.

"I could have gone to him," she said. She hadn't meant to say it. It came out the way water comes out of a cracked dam — all at once, with pressure behind it. "Last spring. I was in the city. He wrote and asked me to come, and I had a job interview, and I told myself I'd go after the interview, and then I told myself I'd go in the fall." Her hands were flat on the table, on either side of the map, and she watched them as if they belonged to someone else. "He died in September."

Sebastian said nothing. That was the thing she was beginning to notice about him — he didn't fill silences the way most men did, didn't rush to pour comfort or opinion into every empty moment. He let the silence be a place you could stand.

"He was my father," she said. "And I knew him the way you know a house you've moved out of. You remember where the windows were. You don't know what's in the rooms anymore."

"He knew you," Sebastian said. "That map took years to draw. Men don't spend years on something unless they mean it for someone." He tapped the margin, the forty crosses. "And they don't write *tell them* unless there's a *them*. He could have gone to a newspaper. He could have gone to a lawyer. He gave it to a stranger on a dock."

"Because a stranger could be trusted not to care," Ruth said, and heard the truth of it as it left her mouth. "That's why he chose you. You didn't know him. You didn't love him. You couldn't be hurt by whatever's on this map." She looked up, and her eyes were dry, which surprised her; she had expected, somewhere in the last hour, to cry, and the crying hadn't come, and she understood that it was waiting — downstream, like everything else. "But I can be hurt by it. And I'm going anyway."

"I didn't say I was going."

"You didn't say you weren't."

He almost smiled. It was a small thing, quickly gone, but she caught it — the flicker of a man who had already decided and was annoyed at himself for deciding. "The dry season ends in five weeks. After that, the canyon floods and nothing goes upstream until spring." He looked at the map, at the three-times-circled mark at its head. "Five weeks to cover sixty river miles of unsurveyed water, one unrunnable gorge, and whatever it was that killed your father."

"You know the way."

"I knew it fifteen years ago. Rivers move. Gravel bars move. The falls at mile thirty-one might be a mile higher or lower now." He was quiet, and she watched him do something she'd seen her father do a hundred times at this same hour of the night: measure a thing against himself, silently, the way a man measures a board before cutting. "There's a freight canoe at the chandlery. Old hull, but sound. I can have her patched and provisioned in two days."

Ruth felt something loosen in her chest — not relief exactly, and not hope, but the space where those things would go if they ever arrived. "Then we go in two days."

"We." He raised an eyebrow. "I don't remember agreeing to a *we*."

"My father gave you a map. He gave me a reason." She stood, and finally, without deciding to, took off her coat and hung it on the nail by the door, and the small surrender of it seemed to settle something between them more firmly than any handshake would have. "You need someone who can read a ledger, Mr. Fairweather, and someone who asks questions men like you don't think to ask. I need someone who can read a river. Neither of us trusts the other. That's fine. I don't need to trust you. I need to know you won't leave me on a gravel bar to save your own skin, and I think — from what my father saw in you in one afternoon — that you won't."

Sebastian looked at her for a long moment. The lamp hissed. Rain ran down the window in silver threads, and somewhere out in the harbor a ship's bell rang once, twice, and went quiet.

"Two days," he said finally. "We leave before dawn. There's a patrol launch that makes its rounds at first light, and I'd rather not be on the water when it passes."

"You've given this some thought."

"I've given Lieutenant Commander Hale some thought. That's different, and worse."

Ruth gathered her coat again, then stopped, her hand on the map's edge. The forty crosses ran along the margin in their silent row, and above them, in her father's cramped hand, the words she hadn't been able to read until tonight: *Not gold. Tell them it was never gold.*

"Tell them," she said softly. "Whoever *them* is — I think it might be me. I think it might have been me all along."

Sebastian blew out the lamp, and in the sudden dark the map seemed to glow faintly, the way old paper does, holding the last of the light. And outside, far down the harbor road, an engine turned over in the rain — patient, unhurried, the sound of something that had been waiting a long time and could wait a little longer.
