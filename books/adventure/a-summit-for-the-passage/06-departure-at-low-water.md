## 6. Departure at Low Water

The moon was a fingernail paring over the harbor, and the tide was going out of the world.

Sebastian Fairweather stood at the rail of his own boat — *Magpie*, forty feet of patched steel and stubbornness — and watched the water fall away from the pilings in long, silver ropes. Low tide, low water, low moon. Everything draining toward the sea. It was the hour he had learned to trust, back when he'd learned it at all: the hour when honest men were asleep and the dishonest ones thought they had already won.

Behind him, in the wheelhouse, Ruth Vane was wrapping the oilskin map in a wool shirt and stuffing it into the bottom of a flour sack, and then the flour sack into a biscuit tin, and then the tin into her rucksack, which she had lined with waxed canvas. It was a great deal of ceremony for a piece of paper. He understood it anyway. Some things you wrapped like a wound.

"They'll watch the river mouth," she said through the wheelhouse glass. Her voice was flat, the way it went flat when she was frightened. He had learned that about her in four days. "Hale's launch. It was tied at the customs pier this afternoon. I walked past it twice."

"Then we won't go out by the river mouth."

"Where else is there? The Serrano is the Serrano."

"The Serrano is a hundred and forty miles of water," Sebastian said, "and only the last sixty of it is the river anyone bothers to name. Above the gorge the map calls it the Vane Fork. Your father's joke, I expect." He let that sit a moment. "We go up the coast first. Twelve miles of open water, ugly if the wind comes around, then the old logging slip at Punta Ceniza. There's a trail from there to the head of navigation. We put in above the first rapids and we're behind Hale before he knows we've left the harbor at all."

Ruth came out onto the deck with the rucksack on her back, and the moonlight caught the line of her jaw, set hard as a cleat. "Open water. In that." She nodded at the freight canoe lashed upside-down across the *Magpie*'s deck — eighteen feet of riveted aluminum, dented like a dropped kettle, that he'd bought that morning from a fisherman who had stopped asking questions years ago.

"In that," Sebastian said.

"It leaks."

"All canoes leak. That's how they tell you they're alive."

She looked at him the way she had looked at him the first night, in the bunkhouse, with her father's telegrams spread across his table like a hand of losing cards. But there was something else in it now. Four days of decoding a dead man's handwriting will do that — will make two strangers into something with a shared weight, if not a shared trust.

"You've done this before," she said. Not a question.

"Guiding. Six years, before the salvage work. This river, some of it. Rivers like it." He checked the painter on the canoe, then checked it again, because his hands wanted something to do that wasn't remembering. "My brother and I ran the Serrano the summer we were nineteen and twenty-two. Came down it laughing the whole way."

"And now?"

"Now I know what's in it," he said, and left it there, and she was kind enough, or wary enough, not to pull the thread.

* * *

They left the *Magpie* at anchor with her riding light burning and her cabin door unlocked, which was the point — let anyone who came looking believe her skipper was sleeping aboard, or had gone ashore to drink. Sebastian rowed them out in the tender with muffled oars, the canoe towing behind on a long line, riding low and patient as a log. The harbor was full of sleeping sounds: halyards ticking against masts, the groan of moored hulls, somewhere a dog with opinions about the moon.

The customs pier came abeam, black and silent.

The patrol launch was not there.

Sebastian's arms kept their rhythm and his stomach did not. The berth where the gray-hulled launch had lain all afternoon was empty water, rocking gently, as if the boat had simply been a rumor the sea had decided against. He counted without wanting to: a launch like that burned eight gallons an hour at cruising speed. The harbor was nine miles from the mouth of the Serrano. If Hale had gone out at dusk—

"Sebastian." Ruth's whisper. She had seen it too. Of course she had. She'd walked past it twice; she'd been counting the same arithmetic all day.

"He's either ashore," Sebastian said quietly, "or he's already at the river."

"Which do you believe?"

He shipped the oars and let the tender drift, and the silence of the harbor came in around them like cold water. "I believe he confiscated nothing," Sebastian said. "That's what's been eating at me since he stood on my deck. A naval intelligence officer who finds a salvage diver holding a map to a drowned strike — and confiscates nothing. You don't leave a thing like that in a man's hands unless you want to see where he carries it."

"You think he let us keep it on purpose."

"I think a patient man fishes with the hook left in."

Ruth was silent for a long moment. The tide was still falling; the tender turned slowly on the slack like a leaf deciding. "My father said the river would tell us," she said at last. "The last thing he said that made any sense. He said, *the water remembers the shape of the country, girl, and it doesn't lie, it only repeats.*" She laughed, short and without any humor in it. "I thought he was raving. He was mostly raving, by then. But maybe that part was true."

"Then we'd better go give it something to repeat."

He took up the oars again, and they pulled for the open dark beyond the breakwater, and the harbor let them go without ceremony, the way a mouth lets go of a word.

* * *

The twelve miles to Punta Ceniza were the worst water Sebastian had chosen in years, which was exactly why he had chosen them.

The sea ran in a long confused swell, left over from a blow three days south of them, and the canoe — towed empty, light as a cork — leapt and slapped and tried its best to swap ends at every trough. He kept the tender's head at forty-five degrees to the swell and made them both hold on and hold course, and for two hours the world was the sound of water on aluminum and the smell of salt and kelp and the small, stubborn light of the Ceniza headland growing by inches out of the black.

Ruth did not complain. He kept waiting for it, the way you wait for a rope under strain to part. She sat in the stern sheets with her arms around the rucksack — the map, the tin, the flour sack, all of Elias Vane's last testament riding against her ribs — and she watched the dark water go by, and once, when the tender took a wave broadside and threw spray across them both, she wiped her face and said, "He fished these waters. When I was small. Before." And then said nothing else for an hour.

Sebastian understood that too. There were whole countries in a person that you crossed only by not asking directions.

The logging slip at Punta Ceniza was a rotted tooth jutting from the headland, all that remained of a company town that had been dead longer than Ruth had been alive. They grounded the tender in the shingle below it and hauled the canoe up above the tideline, and Sebastian stood a moment with the water pulling at his boots, looking north to where the coast bent away and the Serrano's sediment plume would be staining the sea brown in daylight — invisible now, but he could feel it, the way you feel a bruise through a sleeve. The river was out there, running in the dark, running the way it had run since before anyone had thought to name it, and every mile of it ran downhill toward everything they were trying to outrun.

"You're smiling," Ruth said. She was wringing out her sleeves, and she sounded accusatory about it.

"Am I."

"You are. In the moonlight. I can see your teeth."

He turned it over and found it was true. His face ached with it, faintly, the way a hand aches after being clenched too long. "I haven't pointed a canoe at moving water in nine years," he said. "I'd forgotten what it does to you. It's like — " He searched for it, and gave up on elegance. "It's like the first breath after you've been under too long. Even when you know there's every chance the next breath is water too."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to be true." He cinched the last lashing. "Comfort is for people whose fathers died in bed."

She flinched. He heard it land and did not take it back, because it was the true thing, and they had agreed — without ever saying so — to deal only in true things until the river made liars of them both. She shouldered the rucksack and picked up her end of the canoe without another word, and they carried it up the trail together through the smell of pine and salt rot, toward the sound of the river, which they could hear now, faint and constant under everything, like the sea's memory.

* * *

The head of navigation was an old freight landing at the foot of the Vane Fork, and it was exactly as the map promised: a crumbling crib of timbers, a rusted winch, and water moving fast and glassy over a gravel bar, the current braided and gleaming in the starlight like spilled mercury. Upstream, the canyon walls stood black against the stars, and the river went into them the way a thread goes into a needle's eye.

Sebastian knelt at the water's edge and put his hand in, and the cold of it went up his arm and into his chest and settled somewhere behind the sternum, where it had lived for nine years, waiting.

"Talk to me about the first rapids," Ruth said behind him. She had the biscuit tin out and was checking the map one more time by the shielded glow of a candle stub, though he knew she had it by heart already. He had watched her do it three times since the bunkhouse. It wasn't navigation. It was a vigil.

"Two miles of it. Grade two, maybe pushing three at this water." He stood, wiped his hand on his thigh. "The dry season's been kind. Low and clear, which means the rocks show themselves, and it also means the portages are longer. The map marks a camp at mile six — your father marked it *good camp, high bench, dry.*" He almost smiled. "His hand's steady there. Whatever was eating him hadn't reached his handwriting yet."

"And after mile six?"

"After mile six the canyon narrows, and after that I stop knowing it, and we start trusting a dead man's pencil." He looked at her. In the starlight her face was all planes and shadow, and older than it had been a week ago, and he thought: we are both carrying more than the map. "Last chance," he said. "The *Magpie* will hold at Ceniza. There's a coastal steamer Thursday. You could be home in three days and no one the wiser, and I could — "

"You could what?" she said. "Give the map to Hale?"

"No."

"Then finish the sentence or don't start it." She rolled the oilskin closed. "My father traded you a promise, Mr. Fairweather. He was a gambler and a liar and he left me at fourteen and I hated him for twenty years, and he still knew what he was doing when he picked you. I watched him pick you. I've read every word he ever wrote about rivers, and I've spent four days watching you read water, and you look at it the way he did. Like it's a text." She slung the rucksack. "So we're past last chances. Get in the boat."

For a moment Sebastian said nothing at all. The river spoke instead, patient and continuous, repeating the shape of the country the way Elias had promised, and he thought: *she's her father's daughter after all — the same iron in a different hand.*

"Ladies first," he said, and held the canoe steady while she climbed aboard.

* * *

They ran the first rapids in the last hour of the dark, and it was everything.

The freight canoe took the water like a thing born to it — the old aluminum flexing and singing under the current, the dents in her hull whistling faintly at speed, a sound like a distant choir of kazoos that Sebastian had missed for nine years without knowing it. He called the lines and Ruth ferried when he told her to, clumsy at first and then less clumsy, and the canyon opened its arms around them and the stars wheeled overhead and the cold spray came off the standing waves and hit them in the face like a blessing.

*Left side, past the green tongue — ferry right — hard right — brace —*

The river rose up and they went over it, and the world was noise and silver and the enormous black walls leaning close, and Sebastian was laughing — actually laughing, out loud, a sound he didn't recognize as his own — and when he glanced back Ruth was laughing too, wild and wet, and for half a mile neither of them was anyone's daughter or anyone's brother or anyone's dead man's courier. They were just two people in a boat on moving water, which was the oldest and cleanest condition there was.

Then the rapids ended, and the canyon closed, and the river went quiet, and the quiet was a different kind of loud.

Because there, far behind them — back through the walls, back down the twelve miles of coast and around the bend of the world — Sebastian heard it. Faint, arrhythmic, wrong. Not water. Not wind.

An engine.

Ruth heard it a heartbeat later. He saw her go still in the stern, paddle frozen mid-stroke, her head turning upstream toward the sound the way a deer turns toward a snapped twig.

"That's not a logging camp," she whispered.

"No," Sebastian said. He put his paddle in and drew a long, slow stroke, and the canoe slid on into the narrows where the map's pencil marks began, and the sound came again, closer now, or maybe only clearer — a launch working upriver against the current, patient as its owner, hunting the same thread of water they were. "No," he said again, to himself, to the river, to the dark. "That's exactly what it is."

The canyon walls took the sound and threw it back and forth until it seemed to come from everywhere at once, and the Serrano carried them on into the black, downhill, into the country where the water remembered everything.
