## 7. The Drowned Camp

Ruth Vane had learned to read her father's silences the way other children learned to read clocks, and so she recognized the shape of this one immediately: the way Sebastian Fairweather went quiet at the bow of the canoe, paddle resting across his knees, staring at a bend in the river as though he'd heard someone call his name from behind it.

"What is it?" she asked.

"Smoke smell," he said. "Old smoke. And iron." He dipped the paddle, corrected their line without seeming to think about it. "There's something around that point. Something man-made."

The Serrano had narrowed through the morning, its banks rising into walls of granite streaked white with bird lime, and the light came down in long slanted blades that turned the water the color of old bottle glass. They had been on the river two days now, and Ruth had begun to understand that her body was keeping a different kind of time out here — not hours but miles, not days but rapids survived. Her hands had blistered and then hardened. Her shoulders had stopped complaining and started simply working. She hated how much of it she was beginning to love.

They rounded the point and the camp revealed itself all at once, the way a face reveals itself when a veil is lifted.

It sat in a shallow basin where the river widened and slowed, half-drowned — that was the only word for it. A decade or more of high water had crept up the bank and swallowed the lower third of the settlement, so that the buildings nearest the channel stood knee-deep in green water, their timber frames gone soft and black, their roofs collapsed inward like chests caved by grief. Farther up the slope, on ground that stayed dry, more structures leaned against each other: a stamp mill with its flywheel seized in rust, a row of bunkhouses with their doors hanging open, an ore chute running down to the waterline like a wooden vein. Everything wore the same gray-brown skin of weathering, everything softened at every edge, as though the whole place were slowly being returned to the mountain it had been cut from.

"There's no record of this," Ruth said. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "I went through the survey archives twice. The mining registers. There's nothing on the upper Serrano after 1934."

"Governments don't keep records of things they want forgotten," Sebastian said. "They keep records of things they want found."

He brought the canoe in along a drowned fence line, the posts standing out of the water like the stubble of a drowned man's beard, and they stepped off onto a shelf of gravel that crunched underfoot with a sound too loud for the silence. Ruth stood a moment and let the place look at her.

She had spent six years as an archivist's assistant before the telegrams, before her father's death, before all of it, and she knew the difference between a ruin that had simply been abandoned and a ruin that had been *ended*. Abandonment left a sediment of ordinary life: buttons, bottle caps, the small debris of people leaving in no particular hurry. This was different. The bunkhouse doors hung open because no one had bothered to close them, which meant no one had expected to come back, which meant they had left in a hurry or been made to leave, and there was nothing scattered on the floors. No plates. No boots. No coats on nails. Someone had cleared this place the way you clear a table after a meal you don't want to remember eating.

"Stay close," Sebastian said. He had the crowbar he used for prying salvage loose, and he carried it low against his thigh. "Ground will be rotten. Test everything before you trust it."

"I've walked ruins before," Ruth said.

"Not like these." He glanced back at her, and in the flat canyon light she saw something in his face she hadn't seen before — not fear exactly, but recognition. "My brother and I explored places like this when we were young. Old wrecks, old camps. You learn that decay isn't honest. It looks peaceful. It lies."

* * *

They worked the camp the way she supposed divers worked a wreck: methodically, corner to corner, calling out finds in short flat voices. The stamp mill first. Its mortar box still held ore — crushed quartz threaded with a dull yellow gleam that made Sebastian go very still when he scraped it with the crowbar.

"That's gold," he said quietly. "Real gold. Not trace. Not color." He held a fragment up to the light and turned it. "This mill processed serious ore, Ruth. Tons of it. Somebody found something here worth building all this for, and then somebody else made them stop."

"Or made them pay for it," Ruth said.

She had brought her father's camera — a folding Kodak, older than she was, that Elias had carried on every prospecting trip of his life. It felt strange and holy in her hands, this object that had looked at so much country through its one glass eye. She set up the first exposure on the mill, framing the seized flywheel against the canyon wall, and Sebastian shifted his weight beside her.

"You should be careful with that," he said.

"It's careful with itself. It's fifty years old; it knows how to survive."

"That's not what I mean." He lowered his voice even though the canyon held no one but them and the wind. "If Hale is behind us — and he is — then photographs are evidence. Evidence makes you a target in a way souvenirs don't. A man can take a keepsake from you and feel nothing. Evidence, he has to destroy. Or destroy *you*."

Ruth wound the film advance and looked at him over the camera's leather-covered body. "Then let him come and try to explain to me why a mass grave needs protecting."

Sebastian didn't answer. But he didn't argue either, and she had learned in two days on the water that his silences were agreements he wasn't ready to say aloud.

* * *

They found the marker in the late afternoon, when the sun had dropped behind the western wall and the basin filled with blue shadow and the temperature fell as if the day itself had died.

It stood apart from the camp, upstream, where a side creek came down out of a fold in the rock. A concrete plinth, poured rough, maybe four feet tall, with a slab laid across the top. It had been a grave marker once — that was unmistakable. There was the shape of it, the placement facing the water the way her mother's people faced their dead toward the east, the worn path of feet that had once come here often and now came never.

But the face of it had been chiseled.

Ruth saw it from ten yards away and her legs carried her the rest of the distance without consulting her. The slab's surface had been dressed smooth and inscribed — she could see the ghost of it, shallow shadows where letters had been, the rhythm of lines, perhaps twenty names in columns — and then someone had come with hammers and cold chisels and beaten every letter out. The stone was pocked and scarred where the chisel work had slipped, gouges radiating from the erased text like shrapnel. At the bottom, alone, one line had been left untouched:

*THEY WERE HERE.*

Nothing else. No names. No dates. No explanation. Just that, surviving like a heartbeat after everything else had been silenced.

Ruth knelt. The stone was cold through her trousers. She put her palm flat against the chiseled scars and felt the violence in them, the fury and haste of whoever had done this work — and beneath the fury, faintly, the care of whoever had poured the concrete in the first place, who had mixed and leveled and troweled it, who had written twenty names in wet cement because those names mattered enough to make permanent.

Someone had tried to make them permanent. Someone else had made them disappear.

"Sebastian," she said. Her voice sounded like it was coming from underwater.

He came up the path and stopped when he saw it. For a long moment neither of them spoke. The creek murmured past the marker, indifferent, carrying snowmelt down toward the drowned camp and the river and eventually the sea, toward everywhere, as if words written in stone were just another kind of sediment.

"Count the lines," Sebastian said finally.

Ruth counted. "Twenty. Maybe twenty-two. The chisel damage — some rows might have had two names."

"Forty laborers," Sebastian said softly. "That's what my brother used to say the old-timers whispered about. Forty men who went up the Serrano with the survey expedition in '34 and never came down. Everyone said it was a flood year. Everyone said the river took them." He crouched beside her, and she watched his eyes move across the scarred stone the way they moved across a wreck's hull, reading damage as testimony. "The river didn't take them. The river's been holding them."

Ruth raised the camera. Her hands were shaking and she hated that, hated the weakness of it, and she made herself steady by breathing the way her father had taught her when she was small and frightened of thunder: in for four, hold for four, out for four. The shutter clicked. She moved around the marker and shot it again from the east, where the raking light caught the ghost-letters best, and again from above, standing on a boulder, looking straight down at the wounded face of the stone.

"Ruth." Sebastian's voice had an edge now. "Light's going. We should—"

"One more." She shot the base of the plinth, the foundation, the disturbed earth around it. Then she did something she hadn't planned: she took off her father's compass, the brass one she'd carried since the bunkhouse, and set it on top of the slab for scale, and photographed that too. Elias's needle trembled and settled, pointing north, pointing home, pointing at nothing and everything.

When she looked up, Sebastian was watching the trail downstream with the crowbar in both hands.

"What?"

"Thought I heard something." He listened. The canyon gave back wind and water and the tick of cooling stone. "Nothing. Probably nothing." But he didn't relax, and she noticed that he repositioned himself between her and the downstream trail for the rest of the evening without ever appearing to do it.

* * *

They made camp in the second bunkhouse, the one whose roof still held, and Ruth cooked rice and dried fish over a small flame while Sebastian nailed a board across the door from the inside — not to lock anyone out, she understood, but so the sound of it breaking would wake them.

Afterward, by candlelight, she developed nothing — there was no darkroom here, no chemicals — but she unwound the film canisters from her bag and counted them like a miser counting coins. Eleven exposures left on this roll. She thought about the arithmetic of evidence: twenty names on a stone, forty men in a ledger somewhere upstream, one government office that had signed off on all of it, one lieutenant commander paddling somewhere behind them with patient eyes.

"Do you think they were buried here?" she asked. "The forty. Or is the marker symbolic?"

Sebastian fed a stick into the fire. "If they were buried here, the ground would tell us. Graves settle differently than undisturbed earth. There'd be depressions." He paused. "There aren't any. Which means either they're buried somewhere else — upstream, maybe, closer to wherever it happened — or..."

"Or they were never buried at all."

"The river takes what it's given," Sebastian said. "Ask me how I know."

She wanted to ask. She had wanted to ask since the portage, since the night he'd talked in his sleep — just a word, *Tom*, just a name — and woken with salt tracks drying on his face and pretended it hadn't happened. But the fire cracked and the darkness outside the board-nailed door was absolute, and some questions, she sensed, were doors that opened onto rooms a person wasn't ready to show you.

Instead she said, "My father came through here. On his way out, in '34. He must have. The map shows this bend, this basin — there's a little symbol, a square with a cross in it. I thought it meant a chapel." She looked toward the black window, toward the marker she couldn't see but could feel, the way you feel a bruise. "It means this. He marked the grave. He was one of the ones who ran, and he carried the knowledge of this stone inside him for forty years, and he never once told me. Never once."

"He was waiting," Sebastian said.

"For what? For me to find it without him? What kind of gift is that?"

"The kind you give when you're ashamed." Sebastian's voice was quiet, and it had a texture she hadn't heard in it before, something worn smooth by use. "Shame doesn't make men liars, Ruth. It makes them cartographers. They draw you maps to the thing they can't say."

Ruth turned that over. Outside, the creek kept talking to the river, and the river kept talking to the sea, and somewhere in the dark the drowned camp creaked and settled, timbers shifting in the current like sleepers turning over.

She slept badly, and near dawn she dreamed of her father standing waist-deep in green water among the ruined bunkhouses, younger than she'd ever known him, waving to her with an urgency that made no sound. When she woke, gray light was seeping through the wall boards, and Sebastian was already up, already packed, standing at the door with his ear against the wood.

"Boat sounds," he said. "Downstream. One engine, small. It's been idling for ten minutes, not moving." He turned to her, and the candle had burned to nothing in the night, and his face in the gray light was all planes and shadow. "Either that's Hale, or it's someone who wants us to think it is. Either way — we're moving. Now. Upstream, before the fog burns off."

Ruth stuffed the film canisters deep into her shirt, against her skin, where they lay cold and precious as eggs. Through the wall, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of an engine changing pitch — and then, cutting across the water, a single blast of a launch whistle, deliberate and long, like something patient announcing that it had arrived.
