## 8. Hale on the Water

The engine note reached them before the boat did.

Sebastian heard it first as a change in the canyon's voice — a thin, metallic whine threaded through the river's constant thunder, wrong the way a false note in a familiar song is wrong. He shipped his paddle and held up a hand. Ruth, three strokes ahead, read the gesture and stopped, her canoe swinging lazily in the current beside his.

They had been on the water two days. Two days of braided channels and gravel bars the color of old bone, of canyon walls rising sheer and rust-red on either side until the sky was a ribbon of blue overhead and the sun touched the water only at midday. Two days of Ruth learning to read the current — where it smoothed over a submerged rock, where it wrinkled into warning — and of Sebastian teaching her without quite meaning to, calling out lines in a voice pitched low, as if the canyon might carry their words somewhere they didn't want them carried.

Now he knew why the instinct had been right.

"Listen," he said.

She cocked her head. "Engine?"

"Outboard. Twin screws, by the sound of it. Government pattern." He let the current take him alongside her canoe so they could speak without shouting. "There's no one on this river but us who'd be running twin screws."

Ruth's face went still. Not afraid — Sebastian was beginning to learn that fear came late with her, after calculation, after decision — but set, like a jaw tightening around something bitter. "Hale."

"Or someone working for him."

"Is there a difference?"

Sebastian didn't answer that. There wasn't, and they both knew it. He scanned upstream instead, past the bend where the canyon narrowed into a slot of white water, and saw it: a gray shape resolving out of the shadow of the walls, running against the current with the arrogant ease of machinery. A navy patrol launch, thirty feet if she was an inch, canvas dodger forward, a man at the wheel, another standing in the bow with binoculars raised.

They had perhaps four minutes.

* * *

Sebastian's mind worked the way it worked underwater — cold, sequential, unsentimental. Options. Run: pointless; a loaded freight canoe paddled by two people could not outrun a patrol launch with two hundred horsepower, not in this stretch of open water. Hide: there was nowhere to hide on a river; the banks were vertical stone for a mile in either direction. Fight: he had a diving knife and a flare gun, and Ruth had a revolver she'd produced from her father's effects with an expression that suggested she knew how to use it, but neither of those facts changed the arithmetic of thirty feet of armed government vessel.

Which left talk.

"Paddle for the gravel bar," he said. "Starboard side, past the dead willow. We meet them standing up, not swimming."

"You think that matters?"

"It matters to me." He dug his paddle in. "I've drowned once already. I don't intend to make a habit of it."

It was half a joke and entirely true, and Ruth took it the way she took most of what he said now — with a sharp glance that weighed it and filed it away. They ran the last hundred yards hard, ferrying across the current to the wide bar of pale gravel below the dead willow, and dragged the canoes up beyond the reach of any wake. Sebastian pulled the map from the waterproof cylinder lashed under his thwart — reflex, pure reflex — and then stopped himself, hands on the oilskin, and made himself leave it where it was. If Hale searched them, and Hale would search them, the hiding place needed to look like no hiding place at all.

The launch came around the bend trailing a wake that slapped the gravel in long, lazy pulses. Fifty yards out she throttled down, the twin screws churning the water to foam, and swung her bow toward the bar with the smooth authority of something that had never once been told no. The man in the bow lowered his binoculars. Even at that distance Sebastian could see the deliberate unhurriedness of the motion, the way the whole boat seemed to be performing patience.

Lieutenant Commander Adair Hale stood up through the dodger hatch as the launch grounded gently on the gravel, and stepped ashore without wetting his boots.

He was exactly as Sebastian remembered him from the dockside encounter — neat, gray-eyed, a man whose uniform fit him the way other men's opinions fit themselves, worn so long it had become indistinguishable from the body underneath. Two sailors remained aboard. Hale walked up the gravel alone, hands clasped behind his back, and stopped a polite six feet away, close enough to talk, far enough to suggest he considered conversation beneath him and was granting it as a courtesy.

"Mr. Fairweather." His voice was mild. That was the thing about Hale that had unsettled Sebastian from the first day he'd boarded the salvage barge — the mildness. Men who shouted were spending their anger; men like Hale banked it. "Miss Vane."

"Lieutenant Commander." Ruth's voice was flat as slate. "You know my name."

"I make it my business to know names." Hale's gaze moved over the canoes with the unhurried thoroughness of a customs officer. "Yours especially, Miss Vane. Your father was a well-known figure in certain circles. It grieved me to hear of his passing."

"Grieved you," Ruth said. "That's an interesting choice of word."

"I choose my words carefully. It's a professional habit." Hale turned to Sebastian. "You're a long way from your wreck, Mr. Fairweather. The Corvus sits in forty fathoms off Point Ansel, does she not? Salvage work is precise work. I'd have thought every hour away would cost you."

"She'll keep," Sebastian said. "Wrecks are patient."

"And rivers aren't. Is that the idea?" Hale smiled faintly, and it was the smile that did it — not the words, not the two sailors watching from the launch with their rifles slung loose and casual, but the smile, which held nothing of humor and everything of appetite. "Dry season ends in eleven days, Mr. Fairweather. After that, this canyon becomes a different animal. I'd hate to see a civilian drowned chasing scenery."

"We're sightseeing," Sebastian said.

"Of course you are." Hale strolled a slow half-circle around the canoes, and Sebastian watched his eyes do the work his hands didn't — cataloguing, weighing, pricing. The spare paddle. The fuel drums lashed amidships. The tarp-wrapped bundle of food. The cylinder under Sebastian's thwart, which Hale's gaze rested on for precisely one second too long before moving on. "Beautiful country. Terrible history, though. Floods, cave-ins, mine collapses. A man could disappear up here and the river would keep his secrets better than any archive." He paused by the dead willow and touched its bleached trunk almost fondly. "Did you know they tried to dam this gorge in 'thirty-eight? Survey crews, engineers, the whole apparatus. The river declined the invitation. Took three men with it, too. The report called it regrettable."

"The report would," Ruth said.

Hale looked at her then, really looked, and Sebastian saw something shift behind the gray eyes — not anger, but interest, the way a fisherman's interest sharpens when the line finally trembles. "Your father thought so too, Miss Vane. Elias Vane had strong opinions about reports. About what got written and what got left out." He turned back to Sebastian. "I'm going to ask you a question, and I'd like you to consider whether the answer serves you. Are you carrying anything for Elias Vane?"

* * *

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them a hawk rode the thermals, screaming once, twice, indifferent. The river kept talking in its gravel voice, saying nothing, saying everything.

"No," Sebastian said.

Hale nodded slowly, as if the answer confirmed something rather than settling anything. "No. Of course not. Because a dying man trades his life's obsession to a stranger on a dockside bench, and the stranger says no, and then — what? — takes a holiday upriver anyway. With the man's daughter. Whom he met, when, exactly?"

"That's our business."

"It is now," Hale agreed pleasantly. "Because your business has become my business, and here is why, Mr. Fairweather, and I'll say this only once, so attend." He stepped closer, and the mildness dropped away like a mask slipping, and underneath it was something older and colder and much more honest. "You think I'm here to protect something. A secret, a site, some embarrassment in an old file. You think if you stay away from whatever it is, I'll lose interest, go back to my office, stamp my forms." He shook his head. "I'm not protecting anything. I'm hunting. There's a difference, and the difference is that protection ends when the threat turns back. Hunting doesn't. Hunting follows the quarry to the end of the earth, because the end of the earth is where the quarry was always going." He glanced upriver, toward the narrows, toward the source neither of them had named aloud. "Elias Vane spent forty years walking toward that place. I intend to know what he found. And everyone who helps him — helped him — is going to stand still while I find out."

"You won't find anything," Ruth said. "He left nothing."

"He left you," Hale said softly. "He left a stranger with strong arms and a salvage boat. Dying men don't give gifts, Miss Vane. They give debts. And debts get carried." He straightened, and the mask came back up, mild and courteous and worse than the face beneath it. "Now. Some administrative matters. You're operating unregistered vessels on restricted waters. I'm obliged to impound your fuel reserves — safety regulations, you understand. You may keep enough for cooking. The rest comes with me."

"They'll starve us out," Ruth said. "That's the idea, isn't it? Make the river do your work."

"The river does everyone's work eventually," Hale said. "I just prefer to schedule it." He snapped his fingers once, and the two sailors came ashore with efficient, unhurried movements, unlashng the fuel drums, rolling them to the launch. One of them hesitated over the waterproof cylinder, glancing at Hale, and Sebastian felt his heartbeat slow deliberately, the way he slowed it at forty fathoms, everything narrowing to a single clean point.

Hale looked at the cylinder. Looked at Sebastian. And smiled again, that appetite wearing a smile's clothing.

"Leave the gentleman's luggage," he said. "We're not thieves, Petty Officer. We're the government."

They loaded the drums. The launch backed off the gravel, swung her bow downstream — downstream, Sebastian noted, toward the port they'd fled, as if Hale wanted them to watch him leave, wanted them to sit with the arithmetic of eleven days and no fuel — and gathered way. At fifty yards the stern gunner fired a single round high over the canyon, the crack of it slapping back off the walls a full second later, flat and enormous.

"A reminder," Hale called, though the wind nearly ate the words, "that the next ones won't be."

Then the gray shape dwindled around the bend, the engine note fading into the river's voice until Sebastian couldn't tell anymore whether he was hearing machinery or memory.

* * *

They stood on the gravel bar a long time without speaking. The hawk screamed again. The dead willow creaked.

"He knows about Tomas," Ruth said at last.

Sebastian turned. "What?"

"Mile Forty. The hermit. He said 'everyone who helps him.' Present company, the packer — and anyone else along the route." Her hands were fists at her sides. "He's not guessing. He has a list."

"He has more than a list." Sebastian walked to the waterline and crouched, letting the cold push up through his boots, thinking the way he thought at depth. "He knew about Vane before Vane died. He knew about the map before we opened it. He quoted the 'thirty-eight flood report from memory." He stood. "This isn't an officer covering an embarrassment, Ruth. This is a man finishing a job somebody started forty years ago. Hale isn't guarding the secret. Hale is the secret — or he's serving whoever is."

"His office signed the ledger," Ruth said quietly. "Tomas hasn't told us yet, but I've seen the way he looks at the surveyor's signature line when we talk about names. Like he's reading a death warrant and recognizing the handwriting."

Sebastian looked upriver. Eleven days of dry season. Three days' hard travel to Mile Forty, longer to the narrows, and somewhere beyond them the source, and somewhere between here and there a man with twin screws and all the fuel in the world, hunting.

"He took our petrol," he said, "but he left the canoes. Left the food. Left us." He turned to Ruth, and heard his own voice come out level and cold, diver's-cold. "That wasn't mercy. That was a man who wants us to keep moving — ahead of him, upriver, leading him to whatever Elias hid. We're not the quarry, Ruth. We're the dogs."

"Then we stop being useful," she said.

"No." He was already unlashing the tarp, already counting in his head — food, rope, the patch kit, the flare gun, the revolver, the map warm in its oilskin against his ribs where he'd moved it while Hale's eyes were on the drums. "We get faster. We travel nights where the water allows it, we skip Mile Forty if we have to, and we beat him to the source by days, not hours." He met her eyes. "And when he gets there, following our wake — there's going to be nothing left for him to hunt. Only proof, already gone downstream."

Ruth held his gaze a moment, then nodded once, and began breaking camp with quick, economical motions, and Sebastian dragged the first canoe back toward the water.

Behind them, far downstream, faint as a false note in a familiar song, he thought he heard the engine start again.

Heading upriver.
