## 9. Portage of Bones

The gorge announced itself the way a held breath announces a scream.

Sebastian heard it before he saw it — a low, grinding roar rising through the canyon walls, the sound of the Serrano compressing itself into a throat of granite no wider than a wagon road. He brought the canoe alongside a gravel shelf gone slick with spray and let the current swing the bow in, and for a long moment neither of them spoke. They just listened.

"Run it?" Ruth asked. She'd earned the question. Twelve days on the river had taught her when to ask it.

"Look at the water," Sebastian said. It wasn't water anymore, not really — not past the first bend. It was a brown-green chaos of colliding currents, whole trees pinned underwater and thrashing like drowned men, the hydraulic at the narrows chewing foam into a mist that hung over the gorge like a curtain of cold. "Look at it and tell me what you see."

She looked. Her jaw tightened. "I see a place where the map stops making promises."

"That's what I see too." He stepped out onto the shelf, boots crunching on shale, and shaded his eyes against the afternoon glare. The gorge ran for miles — he could see the canyon walls pinching tighter and tighter downstream until the river vanished entirely beneath an overhang of rock. Around it? There was no around it, not on the water side. Only up, over, and across the spine of the ridge, hauling everything they owned and everything they were on their backs.

"Three days," he said. "Maybe four. We carry the canoe in sections if we have to. We carry the gear in loads. We walk until the river comes back to us."

Ruth stood beside him, and he watched her do the arithmetic — the food, the fuel, the rifle, the film wrapped in oilskin, the memory of a map stolen from them at the confluence and rebuilt from an old man's voice and Sebastian's guiding years. She'd stopped being a passenger somewhere back in the fog narrows. He could see it in the way she looked at the portage: not with dread but with a kind of grim appetite, the look of a woman who had decided the mountain owed her nothing and intended to collect anyway.

"Then we walk," she said.

They cached the canoe's spare paddle and half the fuel under a cairn, marked it with red cloth, and began to climb.

* * *

The first day was the easy one, though it didn't feel easy at the time.

The trail — if it could be called that — was a miner's track abandoned for forty years, a shelf of cut stone stitched along the canyon's north wall, crumbling into switchbacks where the rock had shrugged it off. Sebastian went first with the heaviest load, the pack frame biting into his shoulders through two folded blankets, and Ruth came behind with the canoe turned on its side and dragged on skid poles they'd cut from alder. The drag sang against stone all day, a thin metallic whine that got into his teeth and stayed there.

By midafternoon the canyon had closed around them so completely that the sky was a ribbon of blue overhead, and the river below was a rumor of violence, a sound more than a sight. The heat rose off the rock in waves. Sweat salted his lips. His hands, wrecked from years of rope and cold water, split along old calluses and bled a little, and he wrapped them in strips of shirt without stopping, because stopping was a habit the mountains punished.

Ruth didn't complain. That was the thing about her that kept surprising him. He'd expected grief to make her brittle, and it had, at first — but somewhere between the drowned camp and the fog narrows the brittleness had burned off, the way frost burns off a windshield when the sun finally clears the ridge, and what was underneath was harder than he'd guessed. She sang once, low and tuneless, some hymn her mother must have taught her, and the canyon took the sound and gave it back from three directions at once, and for a quarter mile they walked inside her own voice.

They camped that night on a bench of flat stone above the roar, a place where a stand of wind-bent pines had clawed a living out of a crack in the cliff. Sebastian built a fire small enough to hide in his cupped hands. They ate beans and hard bread and a trout Ruth had snagged at the last pool with a patience that embarrassed him.

"Your hands," she said, nodding at the bloody wraps.

"Old story."

"You've said that about the river. About the navy wreck. About the scar on your forearm." She tore her bread and didn't look at him, which he understood was a kindness. "I'm not asking tonight. I'm just telling you I've noticed you keep a lot of stories in a small room."

He fed a twig to the fire and watched it curl. "Small rooms are safe," he said. "You can count what's in them."

"And what happens when the room fills up?"

He didn't answer. Below them the gorge ground on through the dark, patient as a millstone, and somewhere far upstream — or downstream, or behind them in the dark — he imagined he could hear the low diesel knock of a patrol launch, though he knew it was impossible. Sound didn't travel that far. Fear did.

* * *

RUTH

The second day, the trail died.

It happened without ceremony. One moment the shelf of cut stone ran along the wall, worn smooth by boots forty years gone, and the next it simply ended — a slab the size of a church roof had let go sometime in the decades since, and taken a hundred yards of track with it into the gorge. Below the break, the wall was sheer. Above it, the wall was worse.

"We go up," Sebastian said.

Up meant a chimney of broken rock, a gully choked with scree, climbing on all fours with the packs riding high and wanting to topple them backward at every step. Ruth learned quickly to move the way he moved — three points of contact, weight never committed until the stone under it had been tested, everything slow. The sun climbed. The gorge dropped away beneath them until the river was a brown thread stitched along the bottom of the world, and the sound of it changed, lost its menace, became almost gentle at that distance. A lie of distance. She knew better now.

It was in the gully, near the top, that the mountain moved.

She heard it first as a sound like a door being dragged open somewhere underground — a deep mineral groan, felt in the soles of her boots more than heard. Then Sebastian, ten feet above her, said one word, very quietly, the way a man speaks when shouting would waste the time shouting costs:

"Move."

She threw herself sideways against the gully wall as the slope above him let go.

It wasn't an avalanche — it was smaller than that, meaner, a slab of shale the size of a dining table breaking loose with a hundred smaller stones riding on it, and Sebastian was directly in its path with a sixty-pound frame on his back and nowhere to go. She saw him try to dive left, saw the pack frame catch on a knob of rock, saw the whole sliding weight of the mountain come down on the place where he'd been standing half a heartbeat before.

The dust took everything. The roar went on and on, stones rattling down the gully and over the lip, falling away into the gorge below, and Ruth was on her feet before she'd decided to stand, clawing at the settling haze, screaming his name into the whiteness.

"Here." A cough. A hand, gray with dust, thrust up out of the rubble like something surfacing from deep water. "Here — I'm here."

He was wedged behind a boulder that had held, half-buried, one leg pinned to the knee under a slab he could shift only a few inches at a time. His face was running red where the dust mixed with blood from a gash over his eyebrow. When she got her shoulder under the slab and her legs under her and *lifted* — a scream tearing out of her that had nothing to do with the weight and everything to do with the last year, with her father's body on a dock, with everything heavy that had ever come down without warning — the stone rolled free, and he dragged himself out, and for a moment they knelt together in the settling dust, both of them shaking, her hand gripping his wrist hard enough to leave marks.

"You came back for me," he said. It came out strange, almost accusing, as if she'd broken a rule he lived by.

"Of course I came back for you."

"People don't." He looked at her then, really looked, and the dust and the blood and the exhaustion had stripped something off his face that he'd kept lacquered over for twelve days. "In my experience, when the mountain moves, people count what's theirs and climb."

"Well." She sat back on her heels, breathing hard, her arms trembling. "Your experience is with the wrong people."

* * *

They made camp early that second night, both of them too spent to go on, in a hollow below the ridge crest where a seep of water dripped from a moss-lined crack. Sebastian's leg was bruised black from hip to ankle and his eyebrow had been closed with a strip of adhesive from the medical kit and a length of Ruth's hair ribbon, because he'd laughed — actually laughed, a dusty, disbelieving sound — when she offered it, and then let her.

They sat against the packs with the fire between them, and the gorge was far below now, its roar reduced to a whisper, and above them the stars came out in the hard, cold abundance that only high desert country can manage.

"Tomás asked me once," Sebastian said, "why a man who guides rivers for a living can't swim worth a damn."

Ruth turned her head. "You can't?"

"Bad swimmer. Always have been. There's a reason I learned to dive with air on my back and a reason I run water in a boat and a reason I've never once gone in on purpose." He turned a stone over in his fingers, turning it and turning it. "My brother taught me to row before he taught me anything else. Danny. Older by two years. He was the one who loved the water — I just loved being where he was."

The fire cracked. She didn't move. She'd learned that much about him: the story would come or it wouldn't, and reaching for it was like reaching for a wild bird.

"There was a flood year, when I was seventeen. The river came up four feet over the takeout bar, brown and fast, and we were running the last stretch home in the old wooden skiff, and Danny stood up to wave at the house — just stood up, grinning, showing off for Mother — and the boat hit a submerged fence post." Sebastian's voice had gone flat and even, the voice of a man reading a report about someone else's life. "The current took him under in about four seconds. I went in after him. I was in the water — I don't know. A minute. Two. It was the coldest thing I've ever felt, and I've felt the bottom of the Corvus. I never found him. They found him three days later, nine miles down."

Ruth's throat ached. "Sebastian—"

"I counted," he said. "Afterward. That's what I do instead of feeling things — I count. Miles, bottom time, loads carried, river miles to the source. Because numbers don't drown. You can hold a number underwater and it comes back up still a number." He looked into the fire. "That's why I didn't want your father's map. A promise like that — it isn't a number. It has weight, and you can't put it down, and it pulls you toward water."

"Is that what it's been? A weight?"

He was quiet a long moment. "No," he said finally. "Somewhere back at the drowned camp, it stopped being a weight and started being a direction. I haven't had one of those since Danny." He glanced at her, and in the firelight his face was open in a way she suspected it rarely was. "You pulled a mountain off me today. I figure a man owes a story for a thing like that."

"You owe more than a story," Ruth said, and her voice cracked despite herself, and she let it. "You owe the rest of the portage. You owe the source. You owe my father the truth delivered where he couldn't carry it himself." She wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist, angry at the tears and done pretending about them. "So don't you dare die under a rockslide with the job half done, Sebastian Fairweather. I'll dig you up and kill you."

He laughed — a real one this time, startled out of him — and winced, and laughed again, and the sound went up into the cold stars and came back changed.

* * *

SEBASTIAN

The third day they crossed the spine of the ridge and came down the far side into a different country.

The gorge released them gradually — first the roar fading behind, then the walls opening, then the river itself reappearing below them, broad and green and almost gentle, sliding between gravel bars where herons stood like gray sentinels. The portage trail, where it existed, was older here, older than the miners' cut on the north wall: a path worn into the earth itself, polished by generations of feet, and Sebastian stopped when he realized what that meant.

"Someone's been walking this a long time," Ruth said beside him.

"Not miners. Miners leave wheel ruts and rust." He crouched and touched the packed earth, the way the trail curved around the living trees instead of through them. "This is a path kept. Cleared. Someone walks it often enough to keep it open and rarely enough to leave no trace."

"Tomás said to watch for smoke at Mile Forty," Ruth said. "He said the old man who lives up here would know we were coming before we knew it ourselves."

They found the canoe where they'd left it below the last switchback, untouched, and the cache cairn undisturbed, and Sebastian felt something in his chest unknot for the first time in three days. They loaded in silence, working together now without discussion, her hands and his finding the same rhythm, and pushed out into the current as the afternoon light went long and gold across the water.

It was Ruth who saw it first. She said nothing — just touched his arm and nodded upstream.

On the left bank, where a side creek came down through the trees, there was a garden. Impossibly green, impossibly orderly, a quarter acre of beans and squash and corn laid out in rows along the creek bottom, and above it, half-hidden in the shadow of a great overhanging cliff, a cabin of driftwood and stone with smoke rising from its chimney — thin, blue, unhurried smoke.

And standing at the edge of the garden, a rifle held easily at his side, an old man with a white braid watching them come, as if he had been standing there all day. As if he had been standing there for forty years, waiting for exactly two people in exactly one canoe.

Sebastian shipped his paddle and let the current carry them in.

The old man spoke before the bow touched gravel, and his voice, though worn thin as river glass, carried clearly across the water:

"You're late," he said. "Elias told me you'd come before the dry season ended. He was always optimistic."
