## 10. The Hermit of Mile Forty

Ruth had imagined meeting her father's past a hundred ways since the telegram arrived — in courtrooms, in letters, in the flat voice of a naval officer reading charges. She had never imagined it as a garden.

But that was what it was: a garden, improbably green against the canyon's iron walls, terraced into the slope above the river on soil someone had carried up basket by basket over decades. Beans climbing dead branches staked with twine. Squash leaves broad as dinner plates. A row of something bitter-smelling she didn't recognize, and beyond it a small orchard of stunted apple trees, their trunks scarred by frost but stubbornly alive. Water came to it all through an aqueduct of split cedar planks that ran along the hillside like a wooden vein.

The old man watched them beach the canoe without moving. His rifle stayed at his side, pointed at nothing, which was somehow more unnerving than if it had been pointed at them. He wore canvas trousers gone soft as cloth from washing, a shirt of undyed wool, and his white hair was braided down his back in a rope thick as a ship's hawser. His face was brown and seamed and almost perfectly smooth of expression, the way rock is smooth where water has run over it for a very long time.

"You knew my father," Ruth said, before Sebastian could speak. Her voice came out harder than she intended. Forty miles of river and three days of portage had scraped her down to the quick, and here was a man who had known Elias Vane when he was still alive, still whole, still hers — and she found she could not be gentle about it.

"I knew him," the old man said. "I also buried him, in a manner of speaking. Come up out of the sun. There is coffee, if you don't mind it weak, and there are beans, if you don't mind them plain."

Sebastian was looking at the rifle. "We're not armed," he said.

"Then you're either honest or foolish." The old man turned and started up the path between the bean rows, not checking whether they followed. "In my experience the two look identical until it's too late."

* * *

The cabin sat half into the hillside, roofed with shakes gone silver, its front wall hung with drying herbs and fish split and pegged open like pages of a book. Inside it smelled of woodsmoke and rosemary and old paper — that last one hit Ruth strangely, the smell of her father's study, of the years before the silence grew between them. A single room. A stove of riveted iron. A bed, a table, two chairs, and shelves, shelves everywhere, holding tins and jars and books swollen with damp, and bundles of documents tied with cord, and maps rolled tight as scrolls.

He was a man who kept things, Ruth thought. Like Elias. Like everyone who had ever lost something and couldn't stop looking for it afterward.

"Tomas," he said, setting three cups on the table. "That's all anyone needs."

"Ruth Vane."

Something moved behind his eyes then — not surprise exactly. Confirmation. "Yes," he said quietly. "I can see it now that you say it. Around the mouth. He had that same way of holding his jaw, like he was biting down on a word he'd decided never to say." He poured water from a kettle onto grounds in each cup. "Sit. Both of you. You'll want to sit for this."

They sat. The stove ticked. Through the one window Ruth could see the river below, running gold in the late light, and the canyon wall opposite going purple at its base, shadow climbing it like rising water.

"Tell me how far you've come," Tomas said, "and what you think you're walking toward."

So Sebastian told him — the Corvus, the dying prospector in the cantina, the map drawn in Elias's shaking hand, Hale and the patrol launch, the drowned camp with its scrubbed grave marker, the portage, the packer who had stolen everything and vanished upstream or back downstream, they didn't know which. He told it plainly, in the clipped way he told everything, and Ruth filled in what he left out because he didn't know it was important: the telegrams, the fear in her father's last letters, the way he had written *if anything happens to me* and then crossed it out and written *when*.

Tomas listened without interrupting, turning his cup slowly on the table, and when they finished he was quiet for so long that Ruth thought he might have fallen asleep sitting up.

"The strike was real," he said at last.

The words landed in the room like a stone dropped into still water. Ruth felt them spread outward through her chest.

"Gold?" Sebastian asked carefully. "Actual gold?"

"Actual gold. Not a pocket, not a rumor. A reef." Tomas set the cup down. "I was twenty-three years old. I was hired as a packer and surveyor's assistant for the Serrano Consolidated expedition — nineteen thirty-four, spring flood year. Forty laborers besides the staff. Most of them men from the delta villages, some from over the mountains. Good men. Men who sang while they worked." His eyes went to the window, to the river. "We found it at mile fifty-one, where the river cuts through limestone. Quartz vein wide as a door, gold in it visible to the naked eye, running back into the hillside farther than we could follow. I held a piece of it in my hand. I watched the assay come back off the scale."

Hope rose in Ruth like floodwater — and then, in the same breath, horror, because Tomas's face did not change, and she understood that whatever came next would explain why a man who had stood on a fortune would spend forty years growing beans alone above a river.

"And then?"

"And then the surveyor, a man named Corliss, sent the laborers upriver ahead of us. Said there was a second showing higher up, said the strong backs should go first to cut trail." Tomas's voice stayed level, but his hands had stopped moving. "There was no second showing. There was a gorge with bad footing and a storm coming, and Corliss knew it, because Corliss had been up there the week before. Eleven men went over the edge in the slide. We heard it happen. We were a mile behind, and we heard it happen, and when we got there the gorge had taken them and Corliss stood at the rim writing in his notebook."

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside a bird called twice and fell silent.

"He wrote in his notebook," Ruth repeated.

"'Accidental loss of native labor during route reconnaissance. Expedition proceeds.' That's what it said. I saw it later, when it was too late for it to matter." Tomas looked at her directly for the first time since they'd sat down. "Your father saw it too. He was the one who made Corliss write it down instead of just saying it. He said, *put your name under it.* And Corliss did. Because Corliss believed no one would ever come up here to check, and because a signature meant the government owned the deaths, and the government was happy to own them."

"But the strike," Sebastian said. "If the gold was real, why bury the discovery? Why kill for silence instead of mining it?"

And here Tomas smiled, and it was the worst thing Ruth had seen yet, worse than the scrubbed gravestone, worse than the ledger word she hadn't read yet but already felt coming — because the smile was tired and knowing and forty years deep.

"Because of what else was in the quartz," he said. "Because the reef ran through ground where those eleven men lay, and through two older sites besides — places from before, other expeditions, other 'accidents,' other names scrubbed off markers. Your father figured it out first. He always was quicker than the rest of us. The gold wasn't the treasure, Mr. Fairweather. The gold was the bait. Every few years the office in the capital sends someone up to confirm the strike is still rich enough to justify keeping the valley closed. Closed to miners, closed to settlers, closed to anyone who might dig where the digging would turn up bones. The gold keeps the valley empty. The emptiness keeps the graves safe. It's the most profitable mine in the country and no one has ever taken an ounce out of it."

Ruth heard her own breathing. She heard the river. She thought of the mass grave marker at the drowned camp, its names chiseled away, and of her father's telegram — *they know I know* — and of every unanswered letter she had sent him across nine silent years, and she understood suddenly that he hadn't been hiding from her. He had been hiding her. Keeping her away from the thing that killed forty men whose only crime was carrying packs for men with notebooks.

"Elias took photographs," Tomas said. "Corliss let him keep a camera because a camera looked like science. Elias photographed the slide site. The bodies before they were buried. The marker before the names came off it. He photographed Corliss's notebook page. And then he hid the plates somewhere in this valley, and he told me where, and he made me swear that if he died before he could do it himself, I would wait for whoever came carrying his map." Tomas's gaze settled on Sebastian. "Forty years, I waited. Two months ago, a boy from downriver brought me word that Elias Vane was dead in a dockside cantina, and that a salvage diver had walked out of it carrying an oilskin packet."

"How did Elias die?" Ruth asked. Her voice sounded far away to her, small in the big quiet room. "The official record says his heart."

"The official record," Tomas said, "is written by the same office that wrote *expedition proceeds*." He rose, joints cracking like green wood, and went to the shelf beside the stove. From behind a row of swollen books he drew a bundle tied in oilcloth, and from the bundle he took a photograph, yellowed and curling, and laid it on the table between the three cups.

It showed a line of men on a canyon trail, laden with packs, laughing at something outside the frame. Ruth counted them without meaning to. Eleven. At the edge of the image, half-turned toward the camera, a young man with dark hair and her father's jaw was grinning too — and beside him, caught mid-stride, a lean figure with a braid already going grey at the temples.

"That's you," she said.

"That's me." Tomas touched the photograph once, lightly, the way you touch a wound to check whether it still hurts. "Elias sent it to me the winter before he died. On the back he wrote: *I'm going to finish it, old friend. If I don't, they will.*"

Sebastian leaned forward. "They being—"

"Hale's people. The office. The same patient hands that have been closing around this valley since before you were born." Tomas straightened and looked at each of them in turn, and Ruth saw that beneath the calm of his face there was something burning, banked low for decades and now, at last, fed. "You should know what you're carrying before you carry it another mile. Elias hid the plates at the source itself — inside the cavern where the river is born. He chose it because no one goes there. The delta people call it forbidden; the government calls it uncharted; and the water that guards it has drowned better boats than yours."

"The cataract," Sebastian said quietly. "Above the falls."

"Above the falls," Tomas agreed. "And I'll tell you the rest of what you need — the narrows, the fog banks, the eddy that will eat a canoe at mile sixty-two — but first you'll eat, and first you'll sleep, because dead men deliver no messages." He gathered the cups. Then he paused, his back to them, and said, so softly Ruth almost missed it:

"Your father loved you, Ruth Vane. He talked about you the way starving men talk about bread. Whatever silence grew between you, it wasn't absence of love. It was love, wearing armor."

Ruth did not trust herself to answer. She looked out the window at the river running gold toward the falls she would have to run, and pressed her knuckles against her mouth, and said nothing at all.

* * *

She woke once in the night, in the corner of the cabin where Tomas had spread blankets for them, and found him awake too, seated at the table with a candle, writing. Not writing — copying. His pen moved with terrible patience, line after line, and when she stirred he glanced over and, seeing her watching, turned the page toward her without a word.

It was testimony. Dated, signed, witnessed — his account of the slide, of Corliss's notebook, of the forty names, written out in a careful hand that had clearly done this many times before, waiting for a world ready to receive it.

"There are copies," he said. "Hidden in four places between here and the delta. In case the river takes you. In case the river takes me." He blew out the candle, and in the darkness his voice came steady as current: "Sleep, Ruth. Tomorrow I teach you the river your father died trying to give back to the world."

Outside, far upstream, thunder rolled — or perhaps it was the cataract, speaking in its sleep. And beneath it, fainter, from the direction of the narrows, came a sound that made Sebastian sit bolt upright in the dark beside her: the thin, carrying whine of an outboard motor, where no motor had any business being.

Hale had found Mile Forty.
