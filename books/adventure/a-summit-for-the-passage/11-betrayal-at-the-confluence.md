## 11. Betrayal at the Confluence

Sebastian woke to the sound of the river doing what rivers do — talking in its sleep, muttering over stones — and knew before he opened his eyes that something was wrong.

It was the quality of the silence around the silence. A camp at night is never truly quiet: there's the tick of cooling canvas, the small industrious sounds of mice in the packs, the breathing of other sleepers, each with its own rhythm like a signature. He had slept beside strangers on boats and beaches for twenty years and could read a camp the way a diver reads water. And this morning — because it *was* morning now, gray light leaking through the fly — the camp read wrong.

Ruth's breathing: steady, from her tarp under the alders. His own gear: where he'd left it. The packer's spot by the fire ring: empty.

Not just empty. *Swept.* No bedroll, no mule, no canvas pack-frame leaning against the log. The fire had been fed and banked carefully, the way you'd leave it if you wanted no one alarmed until well past dawn.

Sebastian lay still for a long moment, listening to the Serrano talk, and felt the cold arrive not in his skin but somewhere behind his sternum, where it would keep.

* * *

The confluence at Mile Fifty-One was a place the map had marked with two careful inked lines meeting like clasped hands — the Serrano proper coming down out of the gorge country, and a tributary called the Río Hondo sliding in from the west, milky with glacial flour even in dry season. They'd made camp on the gravel bar between them because Elias Vane's map said so: *good ground, high water line, willows for windbreak.* The old man had known every yard of this river the way Sebastian knew the inside of a dive helmet. It had felt like traveling with a ghost who still looked after them.

Now the ghost's map was gone.

He found himself standing before he remembered deciding to stand, crossing the gravel in six strides, pulling open the cache they'd dug beneath the willows — the spare rope, the tinned food, the paraffin, the medical kit, the oilskin bundle that had started all of it.

Gone. All of it. Even the tin cup. Whoever had done it hadn't rushed. They'd taken their time, packed properly, left the hole filled and raked smooth with a branch. Professional. Patient. The work of someone who'd been paid to be thorough, not desperate.

"Sebastian?" Ruth's voice, thick with sleep, then sharp as she saw his face. She came out of the tarp with her boots half-laced, hair loose, and stopped dead at the edge of the fire ring. Her eyes went where his had gone. "The mule."

"The mule."

"He took the *mule*." She said it the way you'd say a word in a language you were only learning — outrage not yet arrived, just the shape of it. Then she turned a slow circle, taking in the swept cache, the banked fire, the absence of everything they'd hired Tomás Quispe — no relation to Old Tomas, though the coincidence had made them laugh three nights ago — to carry. "We paid him half up front. Half more at the source."

"Yes."

"He shook my hand." Her voice cracked on it, and Sebastian understood that the betrayal of the money was nothing; it was the handshake. Ruth Vane did not give trust often or cheaply, and she had given this man a piece of it on a gravel bar in canyon country, and he had carried it off on a mule.

Sebastian crouched at the fire ring. The ashes were still warm underneath. An hour, maybe two. "The trail along the Hondo's rideable," he said. "We could catch him by midday if we push."

"And then what?" Ruth crouched opposite him, and in the gray light her face was her father's face — the same hard jaw, the same eyes that had learned early to expect disappointment and price it accordingly. "You'll fight him? He's got thirty years and a bad heart on you, but he also has a machete and a head start and no reason to stop for anything we say."

"I've taken knives off men in worse places than a riverbank."

"I believe you," she said, and the terrible thing was that she seemed to mean it, and the terrible thing after that was what it cost her to say it. "But while we're chasing him downstream, Hale is coming upstream. You think this wasn't the plan? Take our supplies, take the map, strand us fifty miles from anywhere with no food and no proof, and let the river do the rest? Nobody has to shoot anybody. The canyon handles it."

Sebastian looked at the fire ring a long time. The coals breathed their last heat against his knuckles when he reached in, an old habit, testing the world's temperature before trusting it.

She was right. That was the worst of it. Every mile they spent chasing Tomás Quispe was a mile Hale gained, and Hale had a launch, fuel, radios, and men. The packer wasn't the disease. He was the symptom. Somewhere below them, Adair Hale was sitting in a boat with a cup of coffee, watching the current, waiting for the river to deliver two exhausted travelers back down to him — or not deliver them at all, which would suit him fine too.

The rage came then, finally, all at once, hot and total — rage at the packer, at Hale, at the navy, at the government that had flooded a valley and drowned forty names, at Elias Vane for dying and handing a stranger a map and a war, at his brother Daniel for drowning first and teaching him that the water always collects its debts. It rose in him like floodwater, and he let it rise, and he let it crest, and he let it pass through him and out into the ground, because he had learned on wrecks and in decompression stops that panic spends air faster than any leak, and rage is just panic wearing a uniform.

What was left when it drained away was cold and clear and very quiet.

"We don't chase him," Sebastian said.

* * *

They inventoried what remained, because that was the discipline — the one thing salvage diving had burned into him deeper than any scar. When the ship goes down, you don't mourn the manifest. You count what's in your hands.

One canoe, the battered freight-hull they'd run out of port, sound but scarred. Two paddles and a spare shaft. One coil of climbing rope, sixty feet, because Sebastian didn't travel light on rope even when he traveled light on everything else. His knife. Ruth's camera, wrapped in oilcloth inside her jacket, thank God — she'd slept with it the way other people slept with weapons. A frying pan. Half a sack of flour, overlooked in the willow cache because Ruth had moved it to the canoe the night before to keep it from the mice. A tin of tea. Sixteen matches, counted twice. Sebastian's diving knife and weight belt, useless here except that the lead could anchor things. Two tarps. The clothes they stood in. And in Sebastian's breast pocket, flat against his heartbeat, the brass fitting from the Corvus — worthless, irreplaceable, the first anomaly of this whole long story.

No map. No food worth the name. No fuel for the spirit stove, so the stove itself was ballast now.

"That's it?" Ruth asked, when he'd laid it all out on the tarp like a surgeon laying out instruments.

"That's it."

She sat down hard on a stone. For a moment she looked young — younger than her years, younger than her grief — a woman in her thirties discovering that the world could reach into a camp at night and take the past and the future both. "My father walked this river with less," she said finally. Not a question. Testing the thought.

"With less," Sebastian agreed. "In 1936 he walked it with a notebook and somebody else's boots. Tomas told us. He said Elias came down out of the high country half-starved with the survey ledger sewn into his coat." He paused. "And the map — I have the map."

Ruth's head came up. "You said—"

"I said the oilskin's gone. The oilskin was the copy Elias drew for whoever carried it downstream." He tapped his own temple. "I've had it up here since Mile Twelve. River miles, camps, rapids, the portage line around the gorge, the falls, the source. I spent two nights going over it while you slept, because I don't trust paper. Paper burns, paper drowns, paper walks off on a mule in the night." He heard how it sounded and didn't apologize. Trust was a currency he'd never held much of, and Ruth had her own ledgers on that score; she only nodded slowly, and something passed between them that was almost respect, or the foundation course of it.

"Then we're not blind."

"We're short-sighted. There's a difference." He stood and walked to the water's edge, where the Serrano and the Hondo braided together, the clear green current and the white glacier-milk running side by side for a hundred yards before they married. The confluence. On the map, Elias had written a single word beside it, and Sebastian said it aloud now, tasting it: "*Choose.*"

"Choose what?"

"Which channel. The map shows two ways up from here. The main stem — longer, more rapids, but gravel bars most of the way, walking water if the canoe dies. Or the Hondo — steeper, shorter, a canyon with no banks worth the name." He crouched, picked up a flat stone, and skipped it across the seam of the two currents, watching it stutter and sink where they mixed. "Elias went up the main stem. So did the expedition. Everything that matters is on the main stem."

"But?"

"But Hale knows that too. If he's coming up, he's coming up the main stem, and every mile of it is a mile we spend in front of his rifle." Sebastian threw another stone, farther, watched it vanish into the white water. "The Hondo buys us time. Costs us everything else."

Ruth came and stood beside him. Up close he could see she'd been crying without noise, sometime in the last hour, and had finished with it, the way she seemed to finish with everything — completely, privately, leaving no evidence on the record. "My father chose the main stem," she said. "And he survived it, and he came back down with the truth, and it still ate forty years of his life and put him in a dockside cantina dying alone." She looked at the Hondo, at its narrow dark entrance where the canyon swallowed the light. "I'm done following his choices. They were good ones and they killed him anyway. Let's be faster instead."

* * *

They broke camp in twenty minutes, because there was barely a camp left to break. Sebastian sank what they couldn't carry — the stove, the weight belt's surplus lead, the empty tins — weighting them into deep water under the willow roots, a salvage man's instinct: never abandon what you might come back for. Then they loaded the canoe, and it rode high and wrong without the packer's four hundred pounds of outfit, skittish as a colt, and Sebastian spent ten minutes retrimming it until it sat honest on the water.

At the seam of the confluence he paused, paddles shipped, letting the current hold them in the eddy while he looked one last time down the main stem — the way they'd come, the way home, the way Hale would come — and then up into the Hondo's throat, where the canyon walls leaned together and the river ran white and loud and cold.

"You understand what we're doing," he said. It wasn't a question either. "Three days of this, maybe four. No resupply. If the canoe goes, we walk out or we don't. And when we come back down to the main stem above the falls, we may come out exactly where Hale is waiting."

"Then we'd better come out fast," Ruth said, and dug her paddle in, and the canoe swung into the white water of the Hondo like a leaf deciding, at the last possible moment, to be a blade.

Behind them, on the gravel bar, the banked fire had gone entirely cold. Ahead, the canyon narrowed until the sky was a ribbon of blue no wider than a road, and the river's voice rose from a mutter to a roar, and Sebastian Fairweather, stripped down to a knife, a rope, sixteen matches, and a map drawn on the inside of his own skull, found that he felt lighter than he had in years.

Loss did that, if you let it. Took everything that wasn't essential, and left you holding only the true.

The first rapid opened below them, long and broken and grinning, and he set his blade to meet it.
