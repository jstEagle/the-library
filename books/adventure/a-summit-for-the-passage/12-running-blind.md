## 12. Running Blind

The fog came off the water before dawn, and by the time the sun should have risen it had swallowed the sun entirely.

Ruth sat in the bow of the freight canoe with Tomas's directions folded into her memory like a letter she'd read too many times to need the paper. *Two miles of narrows. Keep the left wall close enough to touch. When you hear the river change its voice — a low hum under the rush — that's the first hydraulic. Ferry hard right before it, or it will take you.* She recited the words silently, over and over, the way her father used to recite psalms when he thought no one could hear him: not as prayer, exactly, but as ballast.

Behind her, Sebastian knelt amidships with his paddle buried to the throat in black water, reading currents she couldn't see.

"Talk to me," he said quietly. "What did Tomas say about the walls?"

"Limestone on the left, broken granite on the right. He said if we can hear water dripping from the left side but not the right, we're still in the true channel."

"Listen, then."

She listened. The fog made everything intimate and enormous at once. The river ran beneath them with a sound like a crowd murmuring in a language she almost knew, and somewhere off to the left — close, impossibly close — water dripped from stone in slow irregular counts. *One. Two. Three.* A pause. *One. Two.*

"Dripping," she said. "Left side only."

"Good." A pause, the paddle turning. "Then we're where we're supposed to be, which is the only comfort I've got this morning."

Ruth almost smiled. Three days ago — was it only three? — since the packer named Ibarra had slipped away into the dark with the map and most of their food, she would not have recognized the woman sitting in this canoe as herself. That woman would have been crying, or shouting, or both. This one was listening to limestone drip.

* * *

They had spent the night after the theft huddled under an overturned hull on a gravel bar, and Ruth had expected Sebastian to rage. He didn't. He sat with his back against the cold aluminum and reconstructed the route aloud, drawing on wet sand with a stick, and something in his calm had shamed her out of her own panic.

"Tomas walked us through it twice," he'd said. "He walked it for forty years before that. It's in your head whether you believe it is or not. So tell me — what comes after the narrows?"

And she had closed her eyes and seen her father's hands moving in the air, tracing the river's shape the night Old Tomas told them everything, and heard her own voice say: "The fog narrows. Then a long pool. Then the hydraulic at the bend, the one he called the Washerwoman because—" she'd stopped, embarrassed.

"Because?"

"Because it wrings boats out and hangs them up to dry. His words."

Sebastian had looked at her across the dying fire with an expression she couldn't name then and could name now: respect, arriving late and unannounced. "Then we run it blind," he said, "and we trust the dead man who taught you."

* * *

The canyon walls rose around them now like the inside of a throat. She could hear them more than see them — the fog flattened everything into grey velvet, and sound came strange and doubled off the stone. Once, far above, a bird cried out and its echo answered from three directions, and Ruth felt the vertigo of a world without edges.

"How deep?" she asked.

"Under the hull? Twelve feet, maybe fifteen. Cold enough to stop your heart in a minute." Sebastian's voice stayed level, conversational, the voice of a man discussing weather. She had learned that about him — the calmer he sounded, the worse the water ahead. "If we go over, get your feet downriver, on your back, paddle with your arms. Don't try to stand unless it's shin-deep. Foot entrapment kills more people than drowning does."

"You've said this before."

"I'll say it again. Repetition is how things stick."

"My father used to say that." The words were out before she could weigh them. In the fog, confessions came cheap; there was no one to see your face. "About everything. Knots, prayers, the names of stars. I used to think he just liked hearing himself talk."

"And now?"

"Now I think he was trying to leave things in me. Like seed grain. In case of a bad winter." Her throat tightened. "I thought I had years. You always think you have years."

Sebastian didn't answer right away. When he did, his voice was gentle in a way that cost him something; she could hear the effort of it. "My brother drowned when I was nineteen. Shallow water, a river you could wade across in August. He went in headfirst off a rope swing and hit a submerged log, and I was twenty feet away and did nothing useful for four full minutes. Four minutes is a long time, Ruth. I've counted them my whole life." The paddle dipped, pulled, dipped. "So I don't say these things to fill silence. I say them because someone once needed to hear them and didn't."

The fog pressed closer. Ruth turned her face into the damp air so he wouldn't see what it was doing, though she suspected he already knew, and was kind enough to keep paddling.

* * *

An hour in, the dripping stopped.

She noticed it the way you notice a clock stopping — an absence that rang louder than any presence. "Sebastian. The left wall's gone quiet."

His paddle paused mid-stroke. "How long?"

"A minute. Maybe two."

"Feel the current. Under the hull. Is it pushing us left or right?"

She put her palm flat against the water alongside the gunwale, the way Tomas had shown her, fingers spread to read the flow like braille. At first nothing. Then — subtle, insidious — a lean. The surface of the river was tilted, higher on their right, sliding them gently toward the left bank, and beneath the tilt she felt something else: a slow lateral pull, like a hand drawing them sideways into the grey.

"It's pulling left. There's a crosscurrent."

"That'll be the eddy behind the bend. Tomas said the channel dog-legs right before the Washerwoman." He was already turning the canoe, his strokes long and unhurried, but she saw the tendons standing in his forearms. "We need to be hard against the right wall before we hear the hum. Give me a count of anything you hear."

She cupped her ears with both hands, absurd, useless, and listened past the drip of her own hair, past the murmur of the river, down into the fog ahead.

At first: nothing. Stone and water and their own breathing.

Then, faint as a word spoken in another room — a hum. Low, resonant, felt in the hull before it reached her ears, a vibration like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing.

"There," she whispered. "Underneath. Like a hive."

"Ferry right. Now. Paddle with me — all of it, every stroke, don't hold anything back."

She dug in. The canoe swung its nose toward the right-hand darkness and the current took exception immediately, grabbing the stern and slewing them sideways, and for ten seconds they were fighting not the river but each other's rhythm — her strokes landing a half-beat behind his, the hull yawing, the fog whirling around them as if the whole grey world were spinning on a potter's wheel.

"Match me," Sebastian said through his teeth. "Watch my shoulder. Not the water. My shoulder."

She fixed her eyes on the pale blur of him behind her and matched him, stroke for stroke, breath for breath, and slowly — grudgingly, the way a mule gives in — the canoe came around and began to slide along the right-hand wall. Granite scraped past close enough to touch. She did touch it, once, her fingertips trailing over stone slick as skin, and the contact steadied her more than any prayer could have.

The hum grew. It filled the canyon now, rose up through the hull into her knees, her ribs, her teeth.

"Hold angle," Sebastian said. "Whatever happens, hold the angle. If we broach—"

The fog tore open.

It lasted perhaps three seconds — a seam splitting in the grey — and through it Ruth saw the Washerwoman entire: the river bending hard around a shoulder of rock, and below the bend a hole in the water the size of a barn door, green-black, breathing. Water stood up in the middle of it, a humped wave that broke upstream against the current, and foam wheeled around its edges in slow spirals, and the sound coming off it was the hum amplified into a roar that she felt in her back teeth.

Then the fog closed and it was gone, as if she'd dreamed it.

"Right side of the hole," Sebastian said, very fast and very calm. "There's a tongue of green water hugging the right bank — Tomas called it the seam. We thread between the seam and the boil line. Too far left, we're in the hole. Too far right, we're on the rocks. There is a boat-width of correct, and we are going to find it."

"Sebastian—"

"Ruth." And now his voice changed, dropped its armor. "I've run worse blind. I haven't run worse blind with someone I—" He stopped. Started again. "Stay with me. That's all. Stay with me and we come out the other side."

The roar swelled until it was the whole world.

* * *

Afterward, Ruth could never assemble those ninety seconds into a straight line. They came back to her as fragments, the way a wreck comes up from deep water in pieces.

The boil line appearing out of the fog like a stitched seam across the river, foam to their left churning in slow terrible circles. Sebastian's voice counting strokes — *four, five, six* — as if numbers could hold the hull together. The canoe lifting at the bow as they crossed the seam's edge, weightless, sickening, a heartbeat of pure falling that wasn't a fall. The hole itself, glimpsed off the port side: a pit of rotating green light with a half-submerged log turning in it like a bone in a socket. Her own paddle blade snapping back at her, stinging her knuckles, because she'd buried it too deep. Sebastian's shoulder ahead of her, rocking with each stroke, and her eyes locked on it, matching him, matching him, matching him.

And then the roar falling away behind them like a door closing, and the fog going soft again, and the river running smooth and dark and astonished-looking, as if nothing had happened.

They drifted. Neither of them spoke. Ruth became aware, distantly, that her hands were shaking so hard the paddle chattered against the gunwale, a small wooden rattle in the vast quiet.

"Ruth." Sebastian's voice came from behind her, roughened. "Check yourself. Hands, feet, everything attached?"

She flexed her fingers. Wiggled her boots against the thwart. "Attached."

"The camera case?"

Her hand flew to the dry bag lashed inside the hull — the photographs from the drowned camp, the grave marker scrubbed of names, all of it irreplaceable. Dry. Intact. She let out a breath that shook on the way out. "Dry."

"Then we're rich," he said, and she heard the grin in it, ragged and real. "By our current accounting."

The laugh that came out of her was half sob and entirely involuntary, and it seemed to break something loose. She turned in her seat to look at him — really look, for the first time since the fog had sealed them in. He was soaked to the shoulders, a cut on his cheekbone she hadn't seen him get, hair plastered flat, and he was looking at her with an expression she'd seen on no man's face directed at her before: open admiration, undisguised, as if she were something he hadn't known to expect from the world.

"You matched me," he said. "Every stroke, after the first ten. Do you understand how rare that is? Most people freeze. Most people become cargo. You weren't cargo back there."

"I was terrified."

"Of course you were. Fear's the raw material." He shipped his paddle and rested, letting the canoe swing lazily in the current. "Courage isn't the absence of it. Courage is fear with a job."

Ruth looked down at her own hands — shaking still, but shaking around a paddle grip, holding something, doing work. All her life she had been carried: by her father's silences, by her mother's people's patience, by her own careful anger that kept the world at arm's length. Even on this river she had told herself she was a passenger, along for another man's promise to another man's ghost.

But her arms burned. Her knuckles stung. She had put her paddle in the water and the water had obeyed, and somewhere in the last ninety seconds the passenger had drowned quietly in the fog and no one had marked the spot.

"What's next?" she asked. "In Tomas's directions."

Sebastian blinked, then smiled — a slow, tired, genuine thing. "A long pool. Then a gravel bar on the left where we can dry out and eat whatever Ibarra left us, which by my estimate is half a bag of flour and some bitterness." He glanced up at the featureless grey sky. "Fog usually burns off by midday. If it holds, we make the pool below the falls by tomorrow evening."

The falls. The place where the old expedition's steam launch lay rotted in the shallows, where Elias's map had pointed with a single word — *the source* — beyond it. Ruth's chest tightened with something that was no longer only dread.

They paddled on. The fog began, very gradually, to thin, and through it the canyon walls emerged in pale silhouette, and the river ran on toward whatever it intended to show them.

It was midday, in the widening light, that Ruth saw the smoke.

A thin grey thread rising from the left bank, half a mile downstream, where no fire should be — where, according to every direction Old Tomas had given them, no one at all should be.

"Sebastian," she said quietly. "Look."

He looked. And the ease went out of his body all at once, like a tide withdrawing.

"That's not a driftwood fire," he said. "That's cut wood. Someone's camped there, and someone wanted to be found."
