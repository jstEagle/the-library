## 13. The Wreck Above the Falls

We heard the cataract before we saw it, the way you hear a congregation before you find the church.

For two days since the confluence we had run on memory alone — Tomas's directions recited nightly like scripture, Ruth tracing the route in her notebook with a pencil worn to a stub. The river had narrowed, gathered itself, grown opinionated. The canyon walls leaned in until the sky was a blue ribbon overhead, and the water went from green to the color of hammered tin, quick and cold and full of intent. Twice we lined the canoe through water I would not have paddled for any money, and once we portaged a single boulder-choked chute that Tomas had named, simply, *the Grinder*.

Now, on the morning of the third day, the sound swelled up through the soles of my boots before it reached my ears. It was a sound with weight to it, a sound you felt in the sternum and the back teeth — the river falling somewhere ahead, falling hard and long, the way water falls when the earth has decided to make an argument.

"Tomas said the falls come at mile fifty-one," Ruth said. She stood in the bow, one hand on the gunwale, her hair tied back with a strip of red cloth she'd worn since the portage. "And he said above the falls, there's a place where the river goes quiet."

"He said more than that." I shipped my paddle and let the current carry us, listening. "He said *you'll know it when you see it. The old girl's still there. She never went anywhere.*"

"The old girl."

"That's what he called her. He wouldn't say her name. He said the name belonged to the dead."

Ruth turned to look at me, and in the flat silver light of the narrows her face was all angles and patience. Three weeks on the river had changed her. The woman who had stormed into my bunkhouse with telegrams in her fist had been all edges, a blade looking for something to cut. This woman had been tempered. The river did that. It took whatever you brought it and burned away everything that wasn't necessary.

We rounded a bend of black schist, and the roar doubled, and then — as Tomas had promised — the river went quiet.

It happened so suddenly it was like walking through a door. One moment the current was dragging us toward thunder; the next, the water spread out into a long, still pool, deep as a held breath, walled in by cliffs hung with ferns that drank the spray. Mist drifted across the surface in slow rags. The light came down green and diffused, filtered through the vapor that rose from the falls somewhere ahead, and everything — the rocks, the ferns, our own hands on the paddles — looked as though we had passed underwater without noticing.

"There," Ruth whispered.

I saw it before she finished pointing.

She lay against the far bank where a gravel shelf met the cliff, half out of the water, heeled over at fifteen degrees like a sleeping animal. A steam launch. Forty feet if she was an inch, riveted hull, the stub of a funnel still standing, and the whole of her furred with sixty years of moss and lichen so that she seemed not wrecked but *grown*, part of the canyon, something the river had decided to keep. Her name was still legible on the bow, the paint gone but the letters cut deep into the wood and picked out in shadow:

**SERRANO QUEEN**

I stopped paddling and just looked at her, and I felt something move in my chest that I hadn't felt since I was a boy — that specific awe, the kind that makes you forget you have a body. I have spent my life around wrecks. I've walked the decks of ships that went down with all hands, and I know the grammar of them: how a hull tells you whether she died fighting or died tired, how the rust runs in tears down a plating seam. But every wreck I'd known was a *sea* wreck, softened by salt, buried in sand, visited by fish. This was different. This ship had died in fresh water, in cold, in the dark of a canyon no one was permitted to visit, and sixty years of river mist had preserved her the way peat preserves a body. She looked less like a ruin than like a held moment. As if the crew had stepped ashore that morning and meant to come back.

"They never reported her lost," Ruth said. Her voice had gone strange, careful. "My father's papers — I went through all of them, Sebastian. There was no vessel listed. No loss, no salvage claim, nothing. She just... stopped existing."

"Ships that vanish from the record are ships somebody wanted to forget." I brought the canoe alongside her hull, and the sound of the paddle scraping moss off iron was obscenely loud in that quiet. "Hold us here. Mind the current past her stern — the pool will pull toward the falls."

I got a hand on her rail and swung up.

The deck gave under my boot with a soft, rotten sigh, but it held. Sixty years of moss came away in sheets under my hands, and beneath it the teak was black but sound. Her wheelhouse stood open to the sky, the wheel gone, the binnacle a stump of brass. I stood in the middle of that dead deck, in the green falling light, and listened. Water dripped somewhere in her belly. The falls thundered beyond the mist like a heart in the next room. Otherwise, nothing. Otherwise, sixty years of nothing.

"Sebastian." Ruth had tied the canoe off to the launch's anchor chain — chain still bright where it entered the water, I noticed; river-cold water doesn't eat iron the way the sea does. She was standing at an open hatch amidships, looking down into the hold, and her face had gone the color of the mist. "You need to see this."

* * *

The hold was dry.

That was the first thing, and the strangest. The launch lay heeled against the bank with her stern nearly ashore, and whatever engineer had beached her had known his trade, because the hold — reached by a ladder down through the forward hatch — sat above the waterline, ventilated through a rusted grate, and dry as a church. Dry and dark and cold, and full of crates.

They were stacked in rows, forty-some of them, stenciled in fading white letters: **SERRANO CONSOLIDATED MINING CO.** and beneath that, in smaller stencil, a number. I pried the lid off the nearest with my knife. Inside, packed in straw that had turned to brown lace, lay ore. Quartz, heavy with a dull yellow veining that I recognized the way a priest recognizes scripture. Gold. Not legend-gold, not rumor-gold. *Ore*, in quantity, crated and stacked and waiting for a smelter that had waited sixty years.

"God in heaven," Ruth breathed beside me. She had a candle lit — she'd carried candles since the drowned camp, since the grave marker with the names scrubbed off, and I understood now that she lit one not for light but for the dead, a habit she'd never explained and I'd never asked about. The flame threw our shadows huge against the crates. "Tomas was right. It was real. All of it — the strike, the vein, everything my father said. It was *real*, and they just—" She put her palm flat on a crate as if taking its pulse. "They left it. Who leaves *this*?"

"Men who found something worse than gold." I was already moving down the row, counting, because counting is what I do — bottom time, river miles, crates, the dead. "Ruth. Look at the numbers on these."

She looked. The stencils ran in sequence — 1 through 44 — but the sequence had gaps, and where the gaps were, the crates were missing entirely, and in their places, against the launch's inner hull, stood something else.

A sea chest. An iron strongbox with its lock broken. And a wooden document case, the kind surveyors use, its brass corners green with age, wedged upright between two crates of ore as deliberately as a bookend.

Ruth reached for the document case and stopped, her hand hanging in the candlelight.

"You open it," she said. "I can't. If it's nothing — if it's just survey notes — I don't think I could—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to. I had watched her carry her father's death up this whole river like a stone in a pack. If this case held nothing, the stone gained weight.

I opened it.

Inside were papers. Ledgers, mostly — cloth-bound books, their spines cracked, their pages foxed brown but legible, the ink of that era written with a pen that pressed hard enough to survive anything short of fire. I lifted the top one out and the candle flame shivered, and I read the cover:

**SERRANO CONSOLIDATED MINING CO. — PERSONNEL REGISTER — 1931–1934**

Personnel. Not payroll. Not crew. *Personnel*, the way you'd record livestock or ammunition.

"Read it," Ruth said. Her voice was very steady, which is how I knew she was shaking. "Read it to me. I want to hear it out loud. I want it in the air."

I opened the book. The candle guttered in the river-damp air and steadied, and I read.

The first pages were ordinary. Names, dates of engagement, wages — forty-four names, and I understood the crates now, the ore crated in the same number as the men. Most of the names were indigenous, drawn from the delta country downstream, from the very people Ruth's mother had come from. Some had Spanish names. A few were marked with small notations in the margin — *lame*, *fevered*, *returned downriver* — the mundane bookkeeping of any hard enterprise.

Then, beginning in the autumn of 1933, the notations changed.

*Deceased — rockfall, adit three. Remains interred on site.*

*Deceased — fever. Remains interred on site.*

*Deceased — "attempted passage downriver without leave." Remains not recovered.*

*Deceased — "attempted passage downriver without leave." Remains not recovered.*

*Deceased — "attempted passage downriver without leave." Remains not recovered.*

Three men in a single week, all of them, by the surveyor's neat hand, runaways who drowned in a river that in the dry season a child could wade.

"Read the signatures," Ruth said. "Read who signed."

I turned to the column at the foot of each entry. The same name, over and over, in a hand that grew more confident, more careless, as the deaths accumulated — a hand that had stopped pretending to grieve somewhere around the twelfth entry: **H. R. Calloway, Government Surveyor, Office of Territorial Resources.**

Office of Territorial Resources.

The office that still stood. The office whose seal I had seen, three weeks ago, embossed in gold on the card of a naval lieutenant commander who had sat in my cabin drinking my coffee and telling me, with a smile, that questions about rivers were unwelcome.

The candle flame leaned, and in its light I saw Ruth's face, and what was in it was not surprise. It was confirmation. It was the last door of a house she'd been wandering all her life finally swinging open onto a room she'd always known was there.

"Forty-four names," I said quietly. "And I count thirty-one entries marked deceased or not recovered. Thirty-one men, Ruth. They didn't die of rockfalls. They died of *leave-taking*. They found the gold, they worked the gold, and then they tried to go home, and the river — the surveyor's river — swallowed them." I closed the ledger, and the sound of it in that dry hold was like a coffin lid. "This isn't a personnel register. It's a death warrant with a column for signatures."

Ruth sat down slowly on a crate of gold worth more than the whole delta country, and put her face in her hands, and the candle threw her shadow against the hull of a ship that had been waiting sixty years for someone to read its cargo properly.

* * *

We came up into the green light like divers, too fast, blinking.

I stood at the rail and looked at the falls — a quarter mile upstream, the whole Serrano River pouring over a lip of black rock into a canyon of smoke and roar, the mist rising off it in columns that swayed like the ghosts of masts. Somewhere below that thunder, I thought, were thirty-one men who had tried to run this water in whatever they could find and had been given to the river as a solution. The drowned camp with its scrubbed grave marker. The launch that vanished from the record. The ledger with its tidy hand. It all fit together now with the terrible neatness of a well-planned crime, and I understood at last what Elias Vane had been carrying when he walked into that dockside cantina to die: not a map to gold. A map to *this*. To a dry hold full of paper that could hang a government office.

"He knew," Ruth said beside me. She was looking at the falls too, her knuckles white on the rail. "My father. He was one of the ones who ran — the ledger would have his name in the back, the ones who fled. He carried this his whole life. Sixty years, Sebastian. He knew where she lay all along, and he never came back for her, because coming back meant—" Her voice broke and she mastered it, the way she'd mastered everything since the confluence. "Because coming back meant admitting he'd left the others behind."

The river said nothing. The river had been saying nothing for sixty years.

I turned back to the hatch, and that was when I saw it — the thing I'd missed in the candle-dark of the hold, wedged in the corner behind the iron strongbox. A smaller book. Not the company's register. A private one, leather-bound, water-swollen and re-dried into a warped brick, with a pencil stub tied to it by a length of fishline.

I opened it with the care you'd use on a bird's egg.

The handwriting was Elias Vane's. I knew it now — I'd spent three weeks with his map, with his careful mile-marks and his patient, draftsman's capitals. But this was younger writing, harder, the hand of a man in his twenties, and the first entry was dated the week the deaths began.

*They are not accidents. I have counted. Calloway counts them like cordwood and the office sends him more men instead of fewer. Tonight I write down every name in the register in my own book, so that somewhere, in some hand besides his, they are written as men and not as losses. If I live I will come back. If I do not live, I have told my wife the river holds me, and the river is the only witness I trust.*

Below it, in the same book, in a later, older hand — the hand from the map, the hand of the dying man in the cantina — a single line had been added, dated the year I was born:

*Still counting. Still meaning to come back. Forgive me.*

I stood in the green light above the thunder with a dead man's diary open in my hands, and I heard Ruth's breath catch behind me, and I heard — no. Not heard. *Felt*, through the soles of my boots, through the rail under my palm, through sixty years of patient river.

Engines. Far upstream, around the bend above the falls, where no launch had any business being — the low, diesel heartbeat of a patrol boat, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable on the wind that came off the cataract, the sound of men shouting to one another on the water.

Hale had found the river's back door. And we were standing in the one place on the Serrano with no way down but the falls themselves.
