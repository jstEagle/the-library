## 14. The Ledger of the Lost

The steam launch's hold smelled of creosote and river rot, of eighty years of water breathing through wood. Ruth crouched over the crate Sebastian had pried open, her lantern held low, and tried not to think about how the darkness around them was not empty. It was full. It had been full since the day this boat went into the water, and everything it held had been waiting for her.

"Careful with the lid," Sebastian said. "The boards are soft as bread."

The crate was ironwood, though — harder than the rest, harder than it had any right to be — and when the lid finally gave, it came away with a sigh, like a man releasing a breath he'd held for a long time.

Inside, packed in tar paper gone brittle as autumn leaves, lay a ledger.

It was not what she had expected. She had expected ore, or gold, or the raw glitter of the legend her father had carried like a stone in his chest for fifty years. Instead: a book. Foolscap, bound in government-green cloth, stamped in faded gilt with the crest of the Department of Surveys and Interior Development. A serial number. A form code. The banality of it struck her before anything else did — before the weight of it, before the smell of old ink, before the understanding of what she was holding.

"That's not a prospector's book," she said. Her voice came out strange in the hold, flattened by the low ceiling, by the river's thunder bleeding through the hull above the falls. "That's an official one."

Sebastian didn't answer right away. He was looking at the crest the way he'd looked at the brass fitting on the Corvus — the way a man looks at something that doesn't match the records.

"Take it up top," he said. "I don't want to read that in the dark."

* * *

They carried it to the gravel bar above the cataract, where the river ran shallow and glass-green before it gathered itself to fall. The evening light came slantwise through the canyon, gilding the spray, and Ruth sat on a stone still warm from the day and opened the ledger across her knees.

The first pages were survey records. Elevations, flow rates, mineral assays — her father's world, the world he'd tried to teach her about when she was small enough to still want to learn, before the silence came between them like a second river. She read the assay numbers and felt a flicker of the old ache. The strike had been real. That was the first thing the ledger confirmed, in the dry language of parts per thousand and tons per claim. Elias Vane had not been a madman, or a drunk, or a dreamer. He had been right.

She had spent her whole adult life assuming he'd been wrong about everything.

"Read it out loud," Sebastian said. He had built a small fire against the base of a boulder, and the smoke rose straight in the windless canyon air. "If there's something in there someone wanted buried, I'd as soon it lived in two heads."

So she read.

The early entries were dates and names — the expedition roster, forty-one laborers hired out of the delta settlements, listed by name and village and daily wage. Ruth slowed when she reached them. The names were her mother's names. Not her mother's family specifically, but the names of her mother's people, the river families, the ones who lived where the Serrano braided out into the marshes. Quispe. Marañón. Achá. Names she had heard sung at funerals and festivals, names that belonged to boats and fishing weirs and long tables under fig trees.

Forty-one men. She counted twice, thinking she'd misread, but no. Forty-one.

Then the entries changed.

*June 9. Fever in the lower camp. Two men removed to base. Condition: grave.*

*June 14. Cave-in at the north adit. Three men recovered. Condition: expended.*

She stopped.

"Read it again," Sebastian said quietly.

"*Cave-in at the north adit. Three men recovered. Condition: expended.*" The word felt wrong in her mouth, a coin of the wrong currency. "Expended. Like — like powder. Like lamp oil."

"Keep going."

She kept going. It got worse the way water gets deeper — not all at once, but steadily, so that by the time she understood how far under she was, she could not have turned back if she'd tried. The ledger recorded the deaths of the laborers with the same uninflected hand that recorded ore tonnage. A fall. A drowning. A fever that took four men in a week and was described, in the margin, in a different ink, as *containment preferable to evacuation.* The signatures at the bottom of each entry belonged to the same man: *A. R. Collingwood, Government Surveyor, Office of Surveys and Interior Development.*

"Collingwood," Sebastian said. He said it the way you'd say the name of a snake you'd just noticed on the trail.

"You know it?"

"I know the office." He fed a stick to the fire without watching it. "Hale's commission comes out of Surveys and Interior Development. I saw the seal on his orders when he boarded my boat. Same crest. Same gilt."

Ruth looked down at the ledger, and the evening seemed to tilt. The office that had signed off on forty deaths — *expended*, the way you'd expend a fuse or a mule — was the office that had sent a lieutenant commander up a river after her father's map, eighty years later, to make sure it stayed buried.

"They didn't lose the records," she said. "They kept them. They've *always* kept them. Hale isn't trying to hide that this happened. He's trying to hide that they *know* it happened."

* * *

She read until the light failed and the fire was the only sun, and Sebastian fed the flames and said nothing, and the cataract roared its one long syllable in the dark.

Near the back of the ledger, the format changed again. A final section, titled in careful capitals: *DISPOSITION OF PERSONNEL — EXPEDITION TERMINATED BY ORDER.* And beneath it, a list of the survivors — the men who had not been expended, who had instead been *removed from the survey area* and *bound to confidentiality under provisions of the Interior Act.*

There were six names.

The third one was *E. Vane, guide and prospector.*

Ruth read it four times. The fire popped. Somewhere across the river, a night bird called and got no answer.

"He was here," she said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "He signed this. He was one of the six who walked out."

She turned the page. Beneath the list of survivors was a final entry, dated eleven days after the expedition's termination, written in a different hand — smaller, hurried, the letters leaning as if written on a moving surface. A boat, maybe. Written by a man on a boat, going downstream, going fast.

*The forty-one were not lost. They were spent. Collingwood ordered the adit sealed with the injured inside it to stop the fever spreading to the wage-men, and when I objected I was reminded of the provisions I had signed. I signed them. God forgive me, I signed them and I took my wage and I walked out past the graves and said nothing. The ore is real. The gold is real. It came out of the mountain on the backs of men whose names are in this book, and every grain of it is owed. I have kept my copy of the record. I will keep it until I can put it in the hands of the families. If I cannot, then whoever finds this — you are reading a confession, not a map.*

Ruth's hands were shaking. She set the ledger on her knees and pressed her palms flat against the cover to steady them, and looked across the fire at Sebastian, who was watching her with the careful stillness of a man who has learned not to move suddenly around grief.

"He didn't run from it," she said. "All those years I thought — I built my whole life on the idea that he ran. That he found something up here and it broke him and he chose the river over me. And he was *carrying* it. He was carrying it for fifty years, waiting for the right hands."

"He almost waited too long."

"He did wait too long." The anger came up in her before she could stop it, hot and useless. "He gave it to a stranger. A diver he met in a cantina. Not to me. Not to his daughter. He'd sooner trust a man whose name he'd known for an hour than the girl he raised for sixteen years, because — because what? Because I'd have asked too many questions? Because I'd have made him *say it out loud?*"

"Maybe," Sebastian said, "because saying it out loud to you would have meant watching your face when he told you what he'd done. And he couldn't have stood that. I've carried one death for twenty years, Ruth, and I can barely look at myself in still water. He was carrying forty-one."

The number landed between them. She hadn't said forty-one to him yet — she'd said forty, the way the legend said forty, the way everyone said it — and he'd caught it, because of course he had. He counted things. Miles, bottom time, bodies.

"Forty-one," she said. "Everyone always says forty."

"Everyone always rounds down," Sebastian said. "It's kinder."

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat by the fire with the ledger open on her knees and read the names again and again until they stopped being entries and started being men — young men, mostly, the ages written beside them clustering between nineteen and thirty-four, delta men, boat men, men who had signed on for a season's wages and been spent like lamp oil in a hole in the mountain while a government surveyor signed his tidy name beneath each one.

*Expended.*

She found herself mouthing the names, and it was her mother's voice she heard saying them, the cadence of the delta funeral songs, and she understood suddenly that this was why her father had never come home — not to the delta, not to her mother's people, not to her. Because you cannot carry a book like this into a village of grieving cousins and aunts and uncles. Because the confession was not his to give until it was *safe* to give, and it was never safe, and so he had kept it, alone, for fifty years, and called it penance, and let his daughter believe she was the thing he was hiding from.

Inheritance, she thought. She had thought she might inherit a map. A legend. Maybe, if the legends were true, a fortune.

Instead she had inherited a debt. Forty-one lives, signed away in her mother's people's names, and a father's fifty years of silence, and a word — *expended* — that she would carry now the way he had carried it, except that she would not carry it alone, and she would not carry it quietly.

The fire burned down to coals. Across the gravel bar, Sebastian slept sitting up against the canoe, one hand resting on the ledger's oilskin wrapping even in sleep, and Ruth watched the spray of the cataract turn silver in the moonrise and thought about the word *expended* until it lost its shape and became what it had always been: a lie so large it required a river to drown it.

She was still awake when the first grey light came, and still awake when Sebastian stirred, and she was folding the ledger back into its oilskin when she saw it — the thing she'd missed in the dark, missed through two full readings, missed because grief had narrowed her eyes.

Tucked into the binding at the back, a pocket. And in the pocket, a single loose page, newer than the rest. Her father's handwriting, but recent — the shaky, enlarged script of his last year.

*If you have come this far, the plates are at the source. Under the white stone where the water is born. Take them to the delta. Take them to Marañón's granddaughter — she will know the names. And tell her father's daughter that I did not leave because of her. Tell her I stayed away because of this.*

Ruth read it twice. Then a third time, her lips moving.

"Sebastian," she said.

He was already looking at her. He'd seen her face.

"The plates are at the source," she said. "He hid the proof at the source. And there's a name in here — Marañón. Marañón's granddaughter." She looked up at him, and the canyon light was growing, and the cataract was roaring, and she felt the whole journey pivot beneath her like a canoe in a hydraulic. "Sebastian — I know that name. My mother's sister married a Marañón. Their granddaughter still lives at the delta."

Sebastian was quiet a moment. Then he said, in the flat, careful voice he used for depths and distances:

"Then we'd better reach the source before Hale does. Because if that office has spent eighty years keeping this book buried, they'll burn the mountain itself to keep those plates from seeing daylight."

He stood, and began breaking down the camp with quick, economical movements, and Ruth wrapped the ledger in its oilskin and lashed it inside her shirt, against her skin, where it lay cold and heavy as a second heart.

Above the falls, the river ran green and innocent toward its long drop, and somewhere downstream — a day behind them, maybe less — she could almost hear the patient knock of a launch engine against the current, coming on.
