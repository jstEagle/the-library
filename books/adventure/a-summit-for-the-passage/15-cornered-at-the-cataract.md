## 15. Cornered at the Cataract

We heard the launch before we saw it — a flat, mechanical coughing that carried up the gorge like a rumor of the world we'd left behind.

Sebastian Fairweather had spent enough years on water to know engines the way other men knew voices. This one was laboring. The Serrano above the falls ran shallow and braided through the last mile, and whatever boat Hale had brought this far was dragging its hull over gravel bars, cursing the river with every revolution of its screw. That bought us minutes. Maybe ten. Maybe less.

"Ledger," I said, and Ruth was already moving.

She'd slept with the thing for three nights now, wrapped in oilcloth inside her jacket, and she moved toward it the way a mother moves toward a crying child in the dark — no thought in it, only gravity. She pulled it out and stood there holding it against her chest while the fog off the cataract beaded on the leather cover and ran down her wrists like the river itself trying to take it early.

The ledger. Forty names written in a surveyor's tidy hand, each one followed by the word *expended* as if they were fuse cord or blasting powder, used up in service of the strike. Elias Vane's name among the witnesses who fled. Ruth had read them aloud the night before, all forty, her voice never breaking until the last one, and then not breaking even then — just going quiet, the way a river goes quiet above a drop.

Now the sound below us grew teeth.

* * *

The cataract itself was a thing I will describe badly because language wasn't built for it. The Serrano came down through a notch in the canyon wall maybe forty feet wide and fell better than sixty feet into a churning cauldron of white, and the sound of it was not a sound so much as a condition — a pressure in the chest, a vibration in the fillings of your teeth. The spray hung in the air permanently, feeding a garden of wet moss and black lichen that made every rock within thirty yards of the lip slick as oiled glass.

Above the falls, wedged in an eddy behind a shelf of granite, sat the rotted hulk of the expedition's steam launch — our discovery, our grief, our evidence. Below the falls, around the bend, the trail we'd portaged was the only way in or out on foot.

Which meant Hale would come up the trail, and we would meet him on the rocks, and the ledger would be exactly between us.

"We run for it," Ruth said. "Downstream. The canoe's patched, we shot worse water at the narrows—"

"Not this water." I looked at the falls and felt my brother again, the way I always did at water like this — Daniel, nineteen years old, gone under in a flooded quarry on a dare, found two days later folded into the sluice gate like a letter nobody wanted to read. "That hydraulic at the base will hold a canoe forever. It'll hold us forever. We stand."

"Stand and do what?"

"Tell the truth loudly enough that he can't pretend he didn't hear it."

Ruth looked at me then with an expression I'd learned to read over nine hundred miles of river: she thought I was being naive, and she loved me a little for it, and she was going to follow me anyway. Her hand went to the camera bag slung at her hip — Tomas's testimony, the drowned camp photographs, everything she'd documented against my caution since Mile Twelve. "And if he takes it?"

"He can't take what the whole world will have by winter."

The engine sound died. In its absence the falls filled the canyon completely, and beneath that roar, faint but unmistakable, came the crunch of boots on gravel.

* * *

They came up the portage trail single file, six of them, and I counted them the way you count anything that might kill you.

Hale first, of course. Lieutenant Commander Adair Hale had traded his whites for field khaki somewhere south of the confluence, and without the uniform he looked smaller and more dangerous — a lean man with a surveyor's squint and hands that stayed very still at his sides. Behind him, four men I took for his detail: hard-faced, river-dirty, carrying themselves like men who'd been promised something. And last, head down, wrists bound loosely in front of him with a length of rope —

Marek. Our packer. The man who'd stolen our map and supplies at the confluence for whatever Hale had paid him, and who now walked at the end of a gun-line looking like a man being marched to his own funeral.

"Well," Hale said, when he reached the flat shelf of rock where we stood. He raised his voice to carry over the falls, conversational, almost pleasant. "Mr. Fairweather. Miss Vane. You've made remarkable time for amateurs."

"You've made terrible time for professionals," I said. "This river hates you."

"The river doesn't hate anyone. That's rather the point of rivers." He stepped closer, boots finding the wet rock with an ease that told me he'd grown up on water too, some other river, some other life before the navy taught him to drown things for a living. His eyes went past me to the steam launch, to the crates of ore still stacked in its rotted hold, and something flickered across his face that wasn't greed. It was older than greed. It was dread. "You've been aboard her."

"We've been through everything," Ruth said. She held the ledger slightly away from her body, and I saw Hale's eyes track it like a compass needle. "Every crate. Every page. We know about the forty men, Commander. We know who signed the requisitions. We know which office burned the survey reports." She let that land. "Your office."

Hale's stillness deepened. When he spoke again, the pleasantness was gone, and what remained was worse — patience. "Miss Vane. Your father was a good man who made one bad decision fifty years ago, and that decision has been poisoning everyone who touches it ever since. Elias understood that at the end. It's why he sent the map downstream instead of publishing it. He knew some truths are heavier than the people asked to carry them." He extended one hand, palm up, gentle as a priest. "Give me the book. Go home. Bury your father properly. Let these men rest."

"They've been resting," Ruth said, "underwater, unnamed, for fifty years. I think they'd like to try something else."

Behind Hale, one of the detail shifted his weight, and I saw the rifle come off his shoulder by degrees, casual as a man adjusting a pack strap, and I understood that the parley was already over and only the words hadn't caught up yet.

"Hale," I said. "Your man's reaching for his rifle."

"I know," Hale said. "Ivan. Wait."

Ivan waited. But the rifle stayed half-raised, and the geometry of the moment settled into place around us like stones settling into a riverbed: four armed men, two of us, one ledger, and sixty feet of falling water at our backs.

* * *

It was Marek who broke it.

I don't know what finally cracked in him — whether it was seeing Ruth's face, or hearing the names read aloud in his imagination, or simply the arithmetic of standing bound between a man who'd hired him for murder and the proof of the murder. But he stepped forward, ropes and all, past Ivan's rifle muzzle, and his voice came out cracked and raw and fighting the roar of the falls.

"It wasn't the fever," he shouted. "Your father — it wasn't the fever that killed him!"

Everything stopped. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"Marek," Hale said quietly. It wasn't a threat. It was something colder than a threat — a reminder of terms.

But Marek had passed the point of no return, and I recognized the country he'd entered; I'd lived there myself since a dockside cantina and a dying man's grip on my wrist. "He came to me in the spring," Marek said, to Ruth, to me, to the falls, to God if He was listening over the noise. "Elias. He said he was going upstream one last time, that he'd found the plates, that he was going to give them to the families. He asked me to guide him. And I — I sold him, God forgive me, I went to this man—" he jerked his bound hands toward Hale "—because he offered more than a season's wages, and I told myself it was just information, just talk—"

"Marek." Hale's voice didn't rise. "You are confessing to a capital crime in front of witnesses."

"Yes," Marek said, and turned to face him, and there was something almost peaceful in his ruined face. "Yes, I am."

"What happened to my father?" Ruth's voice was steel drawn thin. "Tell me exactly."

"He was supposed to die on the river. Accidental. Old man, bad heart, high water — nobody questions it." Marek swallowed. "But he was stronger than he looked, and when the boat swamped he grabbed the rocks, and he was shouting my name, Ruthie, he was shouting *help me, boy*, and I stood on the bank and I watched the river take him because that man—" the bound hands swung toward Hale again, shaking now "—that man was standing beside me with a pistol, and he said, *let the river do its work.*"

The sound Ruth made was not a scream. It was quieter and far worse — a single syllable punched out of her, her father's name, *Papa*, in the voice of a girl of perhaps eight years old, the age she must have been the last time she'd said it to his face. Then her face closed like a fist, and she was twenty years old and made of iron again, and she said:

"You gave the order."

Hale looked at her for a long moment. Whatever he was — and I believed by then he was a great many things, patriot and functionary and true believer in the cold arithmetic of the state — he did not deny it. "Your father chose to reopen a wound that cost this country its stability," he said. "Men died keeping that secret once. I declined to be the second massacre."

"There it is," I said. My own voice surprised me — level, almost gentle. "Say it plainly, Commander. Forty men murdered for gold, covered up by your office, and my friend Elias murdered for wanting to say their names. Say it, and then tell me again how the book is heavier than the people carrying it."

For one suspended heartbeat, I thought he might actually answer. Something moved behind his eyes — exhaustion, maybe, the weariness of a man who had carried the same stone for years and knew its exact weight. Then Ivan's rifle came fully up, and the moment shattered.

"Enough," Ivan said. "Sir, the book. Now, or I take it."

"Ivan—"

"She'll throw it in the river! Look at her!"

And she might have — I genuinely don't know what Ruth intended — because at the word *river* her arms tightened around the oilcloth bundle and she took one step back, and her heel found the moss-slick granite at the very lip of the shelf, the black glass rock where the spray landed, and her arms flew out for balance and the ledger tore free of her grip.

I saw it turn over in the air. Once. Twice. Oilcloth unwinding from leather, pages fanning open in the updraft off the falls like a bird deciding whether to fly, forty names flashing white in the mist — *expended, expended, expended* — and below it the cauldron waiting, the hydraulic that holds canoes forever, that holds everything forever, that had held my brother for two days before it gave him back—

I dove.

No thought in it. Gravity, again. My knees hit the wet rock and skated, my hands closed on the trailing end of the oilcloth, and the ledger's full weight came taut against my grip and dragged me toward the lip with the whole thundering weight of the Serrano pulling the air behind it into its throat. The world became spray and roar and the burning line of my shoulders. Somewhere very far away Ruth was screaming my name, and somewhere equally far away a rifle discharged — I felt the round crack off the granite above my head and rain stone chips across my knuckles — and I dug my boot toes into a seam in the rock and held.

The ledger hung over the void, turning slowly at the end of my arms, pages drinking spray, and I hung with it, half my body over nothing, looking straight down into the white chaos where the river ate itself.

*Daniel,* I thought, absurdly. *Not this way. Not twice.*

A hand closed on my ankle. Then another on my belt. Ruth, hauling with a strength I'd only seen her use on the oars through the narrows, walking herself backward down the slope with her heels gouging furrows in the moss, and inch by screaming inch the ledger and I came back over solid rock and collapsed together onto the granite, gasping, soaked, alive.

The book lay between us, dripping. The oilcloth had saved it. The ink had held. Forty names, still legible, still waiting.

I looked up. Hale stood ten feet away, and Ivan's rifle was leveled at my chest, and the whole canyon held its breath except for the falls, which never held anything.

"Pick it up," Hale said to Ruth, very quietly, "and this becomes a different kind of conversation."

"No," Ruth said.

She picked it up. She stood, dripping, holding it against her chest, and looked at Hale with an expression I will see for the rest of my life — grief and fury fused into something that was neither, something that had no name yet because no one had ever needed it before.

"But you're right that it's a different kind of conversation," she said. "Because now I know how he died. And so does Marek. And so does every man you brought up this trail — look at them, Commander. Look at their faces. Secrets need everyone to keep them. Truth only needs one person to tell it."

And behind Hale, one by one, his four men began to lower their rifles.

Hale turned to look at them, and I watched him understand, in real time, the exact thing Ruth had just said — watched the arithmetic fail behind his eyes, the count of loyal men dropping from four to three to two to none as Ivan himself let the muzzle drift toward the wet stone. The commander of the expedition stood alone on the rocks above the cataract, and the roar of the falls filled the silence where his authority used to be.

He looked at the ledger a long time. Then at the steam launch, the crates, the source somewhere above us in the dark of the canyon where the plates still waited.

"The spring," he said at last, hoarse. "There's more up there, isn't there. That's why you haven't turned back."

Neither of us answered. We didn't have to.

Hale closed his eyes. When he opened them, the patient man was gone, and something desperate had taken his place, and desperate men, I have learned, are the most dangerous creatures a river produces.

"Then I'm coming with you," he said. "All the way to the source."
