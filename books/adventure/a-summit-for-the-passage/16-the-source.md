## 16. The Source

### Sebastian

They climbed the last mile in the dark, because Ruth said her father had always spoken of arriving at the source before the sun did, and because neither of them could sleep anyway. The canyon walls had narrowed all afternoon until they were walking in a slot of blue shadow, boots slick on stone worn glassy by ten thousand years of water, and above them the sky was a ribbon going from iron to ash to the first bruised suggestion of gold.

Sebastian carried the canoe over his shoulder for the final portage — a hundred yards of polished rock where the river simply gave up and went underground, pouring itself into a black seam like thread through a needle's eye. He'd run water on three continents. He'd never seen a river die before it lived.

"Here," Ruth said.

She wasn't looking at the seam. She was looking up, at a shelf of limestone forty feet above the canyon floor, where a pale line of travertine curved like the rib of some buried leviathan. And behind it — he saw it only when she pointed, only when the first light found it — a darkness that wasn't shadow but opening.

"How do you know?"

"I don't." Her voice was hoarse from three days of fog and cold spray. "But he drew the ribs. On the back of the map, under everything else. I thought they were mountains."

They left the canoe lashed to a dead cedar and climbed. The rock took their weight grudgingly, offering holds like a miser offering coins, and twice Sebastian's foot skated on wet travertine and his heart went with it. But the shelf held, and the opening held, and when he pulled himself through the gap and stood up inside it, he forgot for a long moment how to breathe.

It was a cathedral. There was no other word his mind would accept. The cavern rose a hundred feet overhead, its ceiling hung with flowstone that the dawn light, entering through some hidden chimney far above, turned to the color of candle flame. Stalactites came down like the pipes of an organ. And across the floor of the place, welling up out of a fracture in the living rock, the river was being born.

Not born dramatically. Born quietly. That was what undid him. The water came up through the stone in a dozen spring-fed pools, clear as breath, so still that each pool held a perfect inverted image of the glowing ceiling, and then spilled over rims of white mineral into channels that braided together, gathered, quickened, and ran toward the dark seam where the river dropped into its canyon and began its long argument with gravity. The sound was not a roar. It was a murmur. A congregation murmuring before the service begins.

"It's warm," Ruth whispered beside him. She had crouched at the nearest pool and put her hand in. "The water's warm."

Spring-fed. Deep-sourced. Water that had been traveling through the bones of the mountain since before either of them was born, carrying the mountain's own heat up into the light. Sebastian knelt and drank, and it tasted of stone and iron and something older he had no name for, and he thought: *Elias Vane walked here. This dying man stood where I'm kneeling.* The thought arrived with a weight like a hand on the back of his neck.

The plates were where a dying prospector's confession said they would be — or rather, where the map's final mark said they would be, once you understood that *the source* had never meant gold.

Behind the second-largest flowstone column, in a cavity sealed with river clay and capped with a flat stone, they found an ammunition box gone to rust, and inside the rust, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed canvas and more oilcloth, forty photographic plates in wooden frames, each one dry as the day it was exposed. Forty plates. Sebastian counted them twice with hands that shook, though the air in the cavern was warm.

He didn't look at them yet. Some instinct told him the light was wrong, that these images deserved better than a cave and a shaking lantern. He rewrapped them, and lashed the bundle to his chest with the same cord he'd used for his salvage log for fifteen years, and felt the weight settle against his sternum like a second heartbeat.

Ruth had not come to look at the plates. She had gone to the far side of the cathedral, where the floor rose in a natural dais of white stone, and she was standing very still, holding something in both hands.

* * *

### Ruth

Her father's compass had not worked in years. She knew this because she had tried to fix it herself at eleven years old, prying off the back plate with a kitchen knife, and had watched the needle swing free of its pivot and lie there pointing at nothing, forever after. Elias had never scolded her. He had taken the broken compass, looked at it a long time, and put it in his coat pocket, and carried it that way for twenty years — a compass that pointed nowhere, in the pocket of a man who spent his life walking away from places.

She had found it in his effects after the funeral, along with the telegrams, along with the name of a salvage diver he'd trusted enough to die in front of. She had carried it up the whole river without letting herself think about why.

Now she knew why. She stood on the white dais in the cathedral where the Serrano River was born, and the light came down through the hidden chimney in a single slanted column, full of drifting motes like the dust of everything that had ever been said in this place and never repeated, and she understood that this was where the compass had been going all along. Not north. Here.

"Dad," she said. The word came out cracked. She hadn't called him anything but *my father*, coldly, formally, for eleven years, and now the childhood word broke her open like ice going out in spring.

She knelt on the dais. The stone was smooth and faintly warm. Below her, the pools held their upside-down cathedrals, and the young river went on murmuring its way toward the world.

"I'm sorry." She said it to the water, because the water was everywhere here, because the water was the closest thing to him. "I'm sorry about the eleven years. I'm sorry I let the telegram be the last thing between us instead of the first thing. You wrote that you'd found something worth coming home for, and I thought — I thought you meant money. I thought you'd finally found your strike, and I hated you for it, because I'd decided you loved gold more than us." Her voice steadied, which surprised her. Grief, she was learning, did not always arrive as trembling. Sometimes it arrived as clarity, cold and clean as the pools. "You meant this. You meant the truth. You meant bringing it home to Mama's people. And you couldn't do it yourself because they got to you first, and so you handed it to a stranger, because a stranger was the only kind of man left who might keep a promise."

She opened her hand. The compass lay in her palm, brass worn bright at the edges by decades of a thumb worrying it, the crystal clouded, the needle lying at rest against the case like a bird that had finally been allowed to land.

"You always said a man is measured in miles, not years." She almost smiled. "I don't believe that anymore. But I believe you walked every one of yours trying to get back here."

She dug with her hands. The earth at the edge of the dais was soft, river silt deposited over centuries, and it took her only minutes to make a hollow deep enough. She laid the compass in it, face up, needle pointing at nothing, pointing at everything, and covered it, and pressed the silt flat with both palms, and then she stayed there kneeling while the light moved slowly down the far wall and the river kept being born beneath her, patient as forgiveness.

She did not cry until Sebastian, without a word, sat down on the dais beside her — not touching her, just present, the way you stand watch over a grave — and it was his silence that undid her, the particular silence of a man who had lost someone to water himself and knew there was nothing to say that the water hadn't already heard.

* * *

### Sebastian

They waited for full daylight before opening the plates, because Ruth insisted — *they've waited fifty years; they can wait two hours* — and because Sebastian had learned on this river that Ruth's instincts were better than his plans. They ate cold rice and the last of Tomas's dried fish, sitting on the dais with their boots off, drying their socks in the slanted column of light like a pair of churchgoers who had wandered into the wrong sacrament. The bundle of plates lay between them. Neither touched it. It seemed to generate its own weather.

When the light reached the floor, Ruth unwrapped the oilcloth.

There were forty plates, and they were not photographs of gold.

The first ones were almost ordinary: survey shots, the expedition's steam launch moored at a timber landing, rows of tents, laborers' lines drawn up for pay — forty men in the frame, and Sebastian counted them twice again, because the number had already begun to mean something. Then the angles changed. Someone with a camera had started standing further away. Started shooting from cover. The next plate showed the pay line again, but this time there were armed men at the edges of the frame who wore no expedition insignia, and the surveyor's tent in the background had a flag on it that Sebastian recognized from Hale's launch, from Hale's collar, from the seal on the telegram Ruth had shown him in the bunkhouse a lifetime ago.

Then the plates stopped pretending to be survey work.

Ruth held each one up to the light with steady hands, and read the penciled captions on the masking paper, and her voice did not break, and Sebastian understood that she had decided, somewhere on the dais with her father's compass, that breaking was a luxury the forty could not afford her.

"*Camp dismissed at dusk. Shots from the ridge.*"

"*Landing burned. Men driven to the gorge.*"

"*The gorge. Morning.*"

And then the plate that made even Sebastian, who had spent his life looking at drowned things, look away and look back and look away again: the gorge below the unrunnable falls, shot from above, and the river running red-brown, and the caption in a different hand, smaller, pressed hard enough into the paper to tear it:

"*They signed it 'expended.' I will not sign it. E.V.*"

Elias Vane. A young man's handwriting, decades younger than the map, than the cantina, than the dying hands that had pressed oilskin into a stranger's grip. He had been the expedition's assistant surveyor. He had been the one who hid the camera's plates instead of destroying them. He had fled downstream with the knowledge and carried it for fifty years like ballast, unable to surface with it, unable to sink it either, until the weight finally brought him down to a dockside cantina and one last trade.

"He wasn't a prospector," Ruth said quietly. "He was a witness."

"He was both," Sebastian said. "That's why the map works. Only a man who'd surveyed the route could draw it true, and only a man running from it would draw it in code." He looked around the cathedral — the organ-pipe stalactites, the murmuring pools, the light coming down like something conferred rather than merely fallen. "And only a man who loved this river would hide the truth at its source. Where it couldn't be flooded. They drowned the mines, they drowned the camps, they put a lake over the whole story. But nobody floods the source. The source is where the river keeps its records."

Ruth was repacking the plates with a care that bordered on liturgy. When she finished, she looked up at him, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely level.

"They'll come," she said. "Hale. He's a day behind us at most, and the packer knows this landmark if anyone does. We have the proof, and we're standing at the one place on earth he can't burn down and can't flood." A ghost of something that was almost a smile crossed her face. "My father chose his ground well."

Sebastian rose, shouldered his pack, and settled the plate-bundle against his chest, cord biting his shoulders, forty exposures riding over his heartbeat. Through the cavern mouth he could see the canyon below, the dead cedar, the patched canoe — and beyond it, far downstream, a smudge of dust rising off the portage trail that might have been wind.

Might have been.

"We'd better be gone before it stops being wind," he said.

Above them, in the hidden chimney, the morning light strengthened, and the river went on being born, indifferent and inexorable, already on its way to the sea.
