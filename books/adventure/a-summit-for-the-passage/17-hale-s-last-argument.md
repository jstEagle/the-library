## 17. Hale's Last Argument

The storm came down the canyon ahead of schedule, and Hale came with it.

Sebastian heard him before he saw him — not the launch engine this time, which had died somewhere downstream days ago, but boots on wet stone, deliberate as a metronome, closing the last quarter mile of shoreline below the falls. The light was going out of the gorge in long gray increments. Rain had begun to fall in fat, scattered drops that struck the river like fingers testing a piano, and each drop seemed to announce the man's approach: *he is coming, he is coming, he is coming.*

Sebastian stood at the mouth of the overhang where they'd made camp, the ledger wrapped in oilskin against his chest, and watched Lieutenant Commander Adair Hale pick his way along the rocks with the unhurried precision of a man who had already decided how this evening would end.

He came alone.

That was the thing Sebastian kept returning to as he watched him come — *alone*. No Marlowe with his service automatic. No Quist with his coil of rope and his flat, obedient eyes. No packer slouching behind them all with a stolen map and a stolen conscience. Just Hale, in a rain-darkened uniform coat that had once been immaculate and now hung on him like something salvaged off a drowned man, climbing the shoreline of a canyon he had no business being in, toward two people he had tried very hard to bury.

"He's by himself," Ruth said quietly from inside the overhang. She'd been rewrapping the photographic plates in her spare shirt, and her hands went still when she heard the footsteps. "Where are his men?"

"Gone," Sebastian said. "Deserted him somewhere back at the narrows, I'd guess. Men like that follow an officer as long as he looks like he knows where he's going." He turned. "He doesn't anymore."

"You can tell that from here?"

"I can tell it from the way he walks." Sebastian had spent fifteen years reading water — the way a current lied about its depth, the way a smooth stretch of river could hide a strainer that would gut a boat. Men moved like water too, if you watched long enough. Hale was moving like a channel that had lost its current. All the force in him was still there, coiled and dangerous, but nothing was directing it forward anymore. He was walking upstream because there was nowhere left to walk.

Ruth came to stand beside him at the lip of the overhang. In the failing light her face was all angles and shadow, Elias Vane's face worn young again. "I should talk to him."

"No."

"My father—"

"Your father is dead," Sebastian said, and hated the flatness of it even as he said it, because it wasn't a correction, it was a warning, and she deserved better than warnings delivered like verdicts. He softened it the only way he knew how. "Whatever Hale has to say, he'll say it to me first. That's how men like him work. They go for whoever they think holds the keys." He touched the oilskin bundle at his chest without meaning to. "And then they go for everyone else."

She looked at him for a long moment. The rain thickened between one breath and the next, drumming on the stone, silvering the river below where it pooled slow and deep above the thunder of the cataract half a mile on. Somewhere up-canyon, lightning flickered inside a cloud like a lamp behind a curtain.

"Be careful," she said finally. Not *don't go*, which he would have argued with, and not *come back*, which neither of them quite believed in yet. Just *be careful* — the smallest possible unit of trust, offered across the whole distance they still had left to close.

"Always," Sebastian said, which was a lie, and they both knew it, and she almost smiled.

Then he stepped out into the rain to meet Adair Hale.

* * *

They met on a shelf of granite above the waterline, a natural parlor the river had furnished with driftwood benches and a view of nothing but gray water and grayer stone. Hale stopped ten feet away — close enough to speak without shouting, far enough to reach for a weapon, though Sebastian noted his hands stayed loose and empty at his sides. Old habit. Or new honesty. With Hale it was impossible to say.

"Fairweather."

"Lieutenant Commander." Sebastian didn't offer his hand. "You look like a man who's been swimming."

Hale glanced down at himself — the ruined coat, the mud to the knee, the raw scrape along one cheekbone where the canyon had taken its toll — and something moved through his face that might have been humor in a better-lit life. "My launch foundered at the second narrows. My men decided, in their wisdom, that the mission no longer justified the risk." He said it evenly, without bitterness, which was somehow worse. "Quist went first. Then Marlowe. Then the packer simply vanished in the night with what remained of our provisions. I woke up alone on a gravel bar with a rifle and no ammunition worth mentioning." His eyes came up, pale and steady. "You'll forgive the state of my presentation. It's been an instructive week."

"It usually is," Sebastian said, "up here."

For a moment neither of them spoke. The rain filled the silence, and beneath the rain the river spoke its own language, patient and enormous, the voice of something that had been saying the same thing for ten thousand years and would go on saying it after every man in this canyon was silt.

"You have it," Hale said. It wasn't a question. "Vane's map led you true. You found the launch. You found the ledger." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "And the plates."

Sebastian said nothing. Ten years of salvage work had taught him the value of silence — how it made other men fill the space, how confession flowed uphill toward whoever held still longest.

Hale filled the space. Of course he did. He had come all this way to fill it.

"I'm going to make you an offer," he said. "And before you refuse it out of hand — and you will want to refuse it, Fairweather, I can see it gathering in your face like weather — I want you to hear all of it. Every term. Because I don't think you understand yet what you're carrying, or what it's actually worth, or to whom."

"The ledger lists forty names," Sebastian said. "Laborers. Listed as *expended*. Signed off by a surveyor from your own office."

"Yes," Hale said simply. "It does."

No denial. No deflection. Sebastian had prepared for the lie the way a diver prepares for cold — braced, expecting the shock of it — and its absence knocked him sideways more thoroughly than any falsehood could have. He stood there in the rain and felt the ground shift under an argument that hadn't even started.

"You're not going to deny it," he said.

"What purpose would that serve?" Hale turned slightly, looking downriver, toward the country they'd both come from and neither of them could see anymore. "I've read the file, Fairweather. All of it. I've read it more times than any man should read a document like that. Forty men went into that canyon in 1934 to work a gold strike that officially never existed, and forty men did not come out, and the paperwork calls them *expended* the way a butcher's invoice calls a side of beef *dressed*." His jaw tightened. "I was twenty years old when I first read it. I thought I was joining a service. Instead I joined a janitorial staff."

"Then walk away."

"I did my walking away years ago," Hale said, "in the wrong direction. That's rather the point." He faced Sebastian fully again, and now Sebastian saw it — the thing under the ruin of the uniform, the thing that had driven him up a drowned canyon after two strangers. Not cruelty. Something colder and more human than cruelty: a man who had spent his whole career holding a lid down and could no longer remember whether he was keeping something in or keeping himself from floating free.

"Here are my terms," Hale said. "Listen to all of them. That's all I ask."

* * *

He laid it out the way he must have laid out operations a hundred times — clean, sequential, each element fitted to the next like brass fittings on a hull. Sebastian listened with his arms crossed and the rain running off his chin, and part of him, the professional part, the part that had spent a decade assessing wrecks for recoverable value, couldn't help admiring the construction. It was a good offer. That was the trap of it. It was built to be admired.

Salvage rights to the Corvus — full and uncontested, signed by whatever authority Hale could still reach, worth more money than Sebastian had earned in his entire working life. The wreck sat in ninety feet of cold water with her strongroom intact; Sebastian had seen the manifests; he knew what navy vessels of that class carried in specie and plate. Enough to buy a fleet of boats. Enough to stop counting bottom time and start counting years, the way normal men did. Enough to put a name on a berth and a roof over it and never again feel the particular loneliness of a bunkhouse at 3 a.m., listening to the tide.

In exchange: the plates. The ledger could burn — Hale was explicit about that, almost gentle. *Paper is paper. But the plates are evidence, and evidence in the wrong hands becomes an inquiry, and inquiries have a way of surfacing things that sink other things.* The government would handle the descendants quietly. Pensions, perhaps. A memorial plaque in some provincial capital, unveiled by a minister who would pronounce the forty names correctly because he'd rehearsed them. Closure, Hale called it. Administered closure. The kind that came with signatures and seals and changed nothing except the temperature of the shame.

"And Vane?" Sebastian asked, when Hale finished. "Elias Vane. Your man killed him on a dock in front of God and the harbor gulls. Does the package include a pension for his daughter?"

Something flickered behind Hale's eyes — the first crack in the masonry. "The circumstances of Mr. Vane's death were not authorized."

"That's a remarkable sentence," Sebastian said. "You just told me you've read the file. You just told me you've carried this thing for twenty years like a stone in your boot. And the best you can offer the daughter of the man who died carrying the truth out of this canyon is that his murder was *not authorized*?"

"The alternative," Hale said, and his voice dropped, and the rain suddenly seemed loud between them, "is that she spends the rest of her life being watched. Because that is what happens, Fairweather, when this surfaces outside a controlled channel. I don't say it as a threat. I say it as a forecast. There are men above me — there were men above me, until Tuesday, when I stopped believing they would ever let me resign — whose entire careers rest on that file staying closed. If those plates reach a newspaper, they will not send another lieutenant commander with a foundering launch. They will send accountants and lawyers and quiet men with quiet methods, and Ruth Vane will spend thirty years looking over her shoulder for a settlement that never comes." He spread his hands, and the gesture was almost humble. "I am offering you the only version of justice this machine dispenses. It isn't beautiful. But it's the one that lets people live."

The rain came harder. Lightning walked the rim of the canyon, and for one white instant the whole gorge stood revealed — the black water, the wet granite, the small stubborn figures of two men arguing on a rock shelf while a river ran past them toward a sea that would take anything, carry anything, keep nothing.

And Sebastian stood in that flash of light and measured the offer the only way he knew how.

Not in pounds. Not in salvage rights or pensions or the price of a name on a berth. In miles. It was the arithmetic his brother had taught him, years ago, on their first river together, when Tom was sixteen and full of theories: *everything's a distance, Seb. Every promise, every debt. You just have to ask how far it runs.* Tom had meant it about portages. Sebastian had spent his whole life since applying it to everything else, and standing there with the dead man's ledger against his chest, he applied it now.

Hale's offer ran about four miles. From this rock shelf to the Corvus, sitting pretty in her cold green dark, and no further. Four miles of fortune, and past the end of it — nothing. A blank chart. A life with the depth soundings all filled in and no unmarked water anywhere, which sounded like safety and was in fact the only thing Sebastian had ever truly feared.

Against that: a map drawn by a dying man in an oilskin wrap, traded for a promise that had run three hundred miles of canyon and wasn't finished yet. Against it: forty names in a ledger, and a woman in an overhang behind him who had crossed the same three hundred miles on legs that bled, photographing graves scrubbed of names so the names could be given back. Against it: a compass buried at the source of a river, and a father's confession finally traveling the distance it was always meant to travel — downstream, to the people it belonged to.

Four miles against three hundred. Sebastian had dived on wrecks shorter than that gap.

"No," he said.

Hale closed his eyes. For a moment he looked less like an intelligence officer than like a man hearing a diagnosis he had suspected for years. "You haven't heard the figure."

"I heard it. I've dived the Corvus eleven times, Commander. I know exactly what's in her strongroom, and I know exactly what it costs to sleep well, and I've done the conversion." Sebastian uncrossed his arms. "You're offering me a fortune to break a promise to a dead man. But here's what you haven't priced in. I watched Elias Vane die with his hand around my wrist, and the last thing he had strength to do was squeeze it. That squeeze is worth more than your strongroom. It's the only currency I've ever trusted." He took a breath, and the rain tasted like iron and coming storms. "And there's this. You say the plates in the wrong hands become an inquiry. You're right. But you keep calling these hands the wrong ones, and you're wrong about that. They're the right ones. They're the ones the dying man chose."

"They'll destroy her," Hale said quietly. "The girl. You think you're protecting her. You're sentencing her."

"I think," Sebastian said, "that she's already survived worse than being believed. We both have. That's rather the point of us."

For a long moment Hale said nothing at all. The rain drummed. Downstream the cataract muttered its one word over and over, and upstream the storm kept building in the dark like a debt coming due.

Then Adair Hale did the last thing Sebastian expected. He laughed — short, harsh, genuine, a sound with no command in it whatsoever, the laugh of a man stepping off a ledge and discovering the fall was relief.

"A promise to a dead man," he said, shaking his head, water flying from his cropped hair. "Twenty years I've served men who broke promises to living ones before breakfast. And the thing that finally stops me is a salvager with a sentimental streak and a dead prospector's handshake." He looked up, and his eyes in the last gray light were tired and clear at once, like water after sediment settles. "What happens now, Fairweather? You have paper and glass and a canoe. I have a rifle with six rounds and a canyon full of weather. One of us is armed and it isn't me."

"Now," Sebastian said, "you decide who you are. You've been deciding it for twenty years. This is the last chance to get it right." He nodded up-canyon, toward the source, toward the cathedral cavern where a dead man's truth slept in the dark. "There's room in this story for a witness, Commander. There always has been. Tomas has been one for forty years. Vane was one for fifty. The question was never whether you'd be welcome." He turned back toward the overhang, where a small fire glowed and a woman waited with her father's eyes. "The question is whether you can stand to be on the right side of something for the few days it takes to matter."

Behind him, up the black throat of the canyon, the storm arrived all at once — a wall of rain and wind rolling down from the high country, swallowing the stars, and Sebastian felt the river rise against his boots like a pulse quickening.

The dry season was over. The descent would begin at dawn, if dawn came at all.

And somewhere above them, in the dark, the river at the source was already rising.
