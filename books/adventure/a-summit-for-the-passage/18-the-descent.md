## 18. The Descent

The storm announced itself the way Hale announced himself — not with noise, but with a pressure, a change in the weight of the air that Sebastian felt in the old break of his collarbone before he heard a single thunderclap.

He stood above the cataract with the ledger wrapped in oilskin under one arm and the plates lashed flat against his chest beneath his shirt, forty glass negatives in a wooden case that rode his ribs like a second sternum. The river below them had changed its mind about everything. What had been a thundering white vein between black walls two days ago was now a brown ocean moving uphill in places, standing waves taller than the canoe, the whole gorge filled to the moss line with a churning, hissing violence that smelled of torn earth.

"It's a different river," Ruth said beside him. She had her father's compass open in her palm, though there was nothing left to navigate. "It's not even a river anymore. It's the canyon falling down."

The rain came sideways off the Andes, warm and enormous, drops the size of grapes that hit the water and raised a second rain upward. Above them the sky was the color of wet slate. Somewhere upstream, behind the fog, Hale's party was coming — they'd heard the packer's whistle an hour ago, three notes, the signal Sebastian had watched the man use to mark rapids on the way in. The packer, Quispe, who had stolen their map and their food and sold their route for a promise of amnesty, was now leading armed men down the portage trail at whatever pace the storm allowed.

"We can't portage," Ruth said. It wasn't a question. The trail on the left bank had already gone to soup; they'd watched a section of it slough into the gorge an hour past, taking a hundred years of canyon wall with it in a single groaning slide.

"We can't stay," Sebastian said.

He looked at the canoe. They had patched her that morning with canvas, pine pitch, and the last of their tarpaulin, sewing the torn gunwale with wire from the wreck of the old steam launch, and she sat in the eddy behind a split boulder looking like exactly what she was — a freight canoe built for calm water, overloaded, overpatched, and about to be asked to do something no boat her age should survive.

The plates rode his chest. Forty negatives. Forty men whose names Ruth had read aloud by candlelight in the rotted hold of the *Santa Elena*, their deaths recorded in a surveyor's tidy hand as *expended* — a word that had made Ruth stop reading and press her fist against her mouth for a long moment before she could go on. The ledger held the names. The plates held the faces, and the pits behind the camp, and the surveyor standing with the officers who had signed it all away. Elias Vane had carried copies of those plates downstream once, seventy years ago, and had been chased until he buried them at the source and spent the rest of his life dying slowly of the carrying.

Sebastian was not going to bury them. He was going to run this water.

"You don't have to come," he said.

Ruth looked at him the way she'd looked at him the first night in the bunkhouse — measuring, unimpressed, deciding. Then she laughed, short and sharp, a sound almost lost in the roar. "My father traded a river for a promise. I'm not going to be the one who breaks it. Get in the boat, Fairweather."

* * *

Sebastian's POV:

They ran the first half-mile of the approach in a brown fury that Sebastian could only read by feel.

The flood had erased every feature he'd memorized. The line he'd picked from the overlook — left of the mid-channel fang, thread the tongue, ferry across to the eddy above the drop — was gone, buried under twenty feet of new water. What remained was the deep grammar of the river, the things no flood could move: the canyon's lean, the way the current stacked against the outside walls, the smell of a hole before you hit it. He steered by that grammar, by the tilt of the water and the sound coming up through the hull, and he kept his weight forward and his paddle in the water and did not think about his brother.

His brother had drowned in flat water. That was the joke of it, the cruelty of it — a lake, a capsized dinghy, a boy who swam like an otter gone down in thirty feet of nothing. Sebastian had spent his whole life since learning moving water the way a penitent learns scripture, and here at the end it came down to this: the flood did not care what he knew. It only cared what he did in the next three seconds.

"Rock!" Ruth's voice, close behind his ear.

He saw it late — a fang breaking the surface where no rock had broken it before, the flood having pulled something loose from the walls. He pried the canoe over with everything he had, and they scraped past with a sound like a nail drawn down the hull, the patched gunwale singing, and then the river took them.

The drop came faster than he remembered from the overlook. From above it had been a staircase; from inside it was a wall. The canoe went weightless, hung, and landed nose-first into a trough of standing water that folded over the bow and filled the boat to the thwarts in one green-brown breath.

"Bail!" he shouted, and Ruth bailed, a dented tin pot in each hand, bailing with the rhythm of a woman who had decided that panic was a luxury, while Sebastian fought the canoe down the next tongue with water to his knees and the plates a cold weight against his chest, and thought, with a clarity that felt almost like calm: *if we swamp, I cut the lashing and let the ledger go, but the plates come out of this canyon on my body or not at all.*

He did not say it aloud. Some vows you keep by not speaking them.

* * *

Ruth's POV:

Ruth had spent her life believing her father had chosen the river over her.

She had built a whole architecture of grievance around it, load-bearing walls and all — the summers he didn't come down, the telegrams that said only *working, will write*, the silence that stretched from months into years into the permanent estrangement of two proud people who would rather bleed than dial a telephone. She had come up this canyon to accuse a stranger of theft and had found instead her father's whole heart, drawn in ink on oilskin, every river mile annotated in his small careful hand: *bad portage, good water, camp here — Tomas says the fish run at dusk.*

He had written *Tomas* the way she wrote her own name.

Now she knelt in six inches of freezing floodwater in the bottom of a patched canoe, bailing with tin pots while a man she'd known three weeks steered them into a cataract that had swallowed a steam launch, and she understood, with the terrible simplicity of things understood too late, that her father had not chosen the river over her. He had chosen it *for* her. He had spent fifty years keeping a confession safe, and he had passed it to the first stranger with honest hands because he could not bear to put that weight on his daughter — and in doing so had put it on her anyway, because she was his daughter, and the weight knew its own.

The canoe hit the second drop and the world went white.

Water closed over her head — the whole gorge, it seemed, folding down on top of her — and then the hull surged up and she was breathing again, coughing, one pot still somehow in her fist. Sebastian was shouting something. She couldn't hear it over the roar, but she read his shoulders, the set of his arms, and did what he did. That was the whole of their language now. She had stopped being a passenger somewhere in the fog-bound narrows, and here at the bottom of the flood there was no room left in her for anything but the next stroke.

The ledger lay in the bilge under her knee, wrapped in its oilskin, and she put her hand flat on it as the canoe slewed toward the left wall where the current stood up in black coils. Her father's name was in that book. Among the witnesses who fled. She had hated him for fleeing her; she would not let the river have the only proof of what he'd fled *from*.

"Left!" she screamed, and Sebastian was already leaning, and the wall slid past close enough to touch.

* * *

Sebastian's POV:

The last drop was the one that had killed the *Santa Elena*.

He knew it now, seeing it at eye level instead of from above — the rotted planks of her stern still wedged in the rocks on the right, sixty-year-old timber black with moss, and below her the hole: a back-breaking reversal where the whole river fell into itself and came up boiling. In low water there had been a thread on the far left. In flood there was no thread. There was only the choice between the wall and the hole, and the wall was a lie, because the current was setting hard for the hole the way water always sets for the deepest thing.

Sebastian did the arithmetic he'd spent his life avoiding. Speed, angle, the weight of a swamped boat. The odds came back the way they always came back: not good. But the packer's whistle sounded again through the rain, closer now, down on the rocks above them, and the arithmetic stopped mattering.

"Ruth." He turned his head, found her eyes. Rain, spray, her hair plastered black to her face. "If we go over — the ledger. Let it go. You hear me? Let it go."

She looked at him and did not answer, which was its own answer.

He put the canoe at the drop at the only angle that offered anything — square, committed, nose down like a diver entering water — and the gorge swallowed them.

The fall lasted longer than any fall should. Then impact, a detonation of white, the hull flexing around something with a groan he felt in his teeth, and they were inside the hole, in the washing machine, in the place where the river keeps what it takes. The canoe went sideways. Went under. Came up. Sebastian's paddle was gone. The world was a brown blur and a roar and Ruth's hand locked on the thwart beside him, her knuckles white, her eyes open, and he hauled on the gunwale wire with both hands and leaned his whole body into the current's throat and *prayed*, not to anything with a name, just to the water itself, the way he had as a boy: *let us go, let us go, we're not what you're owed.*

The river considered it.

Then, with the indifferent majesty of a thing that has all the time in the world, it spat them out.

They shot out of the boil into the tail waves, swamped but upright, the canoe riding low and steady as a barge, and the gorge opened around them into a long brawling straightaway and the rain began to ease and Sebastian realized he was laughing — a cracked, gasping, disbelieving sound — and that Ruth was laughing too, or crying, or both, bailing with her tin pots in a rhythm gone almost jaunty.

Then he looked at the space under the thwart where the ledger had been.

Empty. The oilskin gone. The bilge water running clean.

Ruth's hands went still in the water. He watched her look, watched her understand, watched her turn and scan the boiling water behind them as though the book might surface, might come up gasping like they had. Forty names. Her father's name. The surveyor's signature. All of it somewhere in the flood now, pressed into the canyon's keeping, and the canyon did not give things back.

"I'm sorry," Sebastian said, and meant it more than he had ever meant anything he'd said to anyone.

Ruth sat very still. Rain ran off her chin. Then she reached across the bilge and put her hand flat on his chest — on the wooden case lashed there, the plates, the forty faces — and pressed, once, hard.

"We have the faces," she said. "Names can be remembered. Faces can't be forged." She picked up her bail pot. "Row, Fairweather. My father didn't carry this thing seventy years so we could drown in sight of the finish."

* * *

They came off the water an hour later, into a widening valley where the river slowed and spread and the rain thinned to mist, and the canyon walls stepped back as if the land itself had exhaled. Sebastian dragged the canoe onto a gravel bar with arms that no longer belonged to him, and they stood in the fading light, two drowned creatures, and looked back up the gorge.

Far above, tiny against the black rock, a lantern swung. Voices, thin with distance. Hale's party, arrived at the cataract to find their quarry gone and the river between them and everything.

Ruth raised one arm out of pure, exhausted insolence and waved.

Sebastian put his hand inside his shirt, felt the case, counted the plates through the wood the way a miser counts coins. All forty. He thought of the ledger somewhere below in the dark, settling into the silt beside the bones of the *Santa Elena*, and found he could not mourn it. The river had taken the evidence and kept the secret — but secrets kept by rivers had a way of surfacing, and besides, the river had always been on the side of the drowned.

Ahead, down the widening valley, a thread of woodsmoke rose against the dusk. A settlement. Lights. People — and among them, if Elias's annotations were to be believed, the delta villages where the lost forty had come from, where the names were not history but family.

"Can you walk?" he asked.

Ruth shouldered her pack, and her face in the last light was the face of someone who had come all the way up a river and found out what she was carrying.

"I can walk," she said. "And when we get there, Fairweather — I'm going to say all forty names out loud. Every one."

They started downstream, toward the smoke, as the first stars came out over the water and the flood behind them roared on in the dark, carrying its secrets to the sea.
