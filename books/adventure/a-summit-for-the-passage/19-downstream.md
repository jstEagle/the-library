## 19. Downstream

The river did not spit us out so much as set us down, the way a tired mother sets down a child she has carried too far.

I came awake on my side in the shallows, cheek pressed to wet sand, one hand still knotted in the painter line. Sebastian's knot. He had lashed me to the canoe somewhere in the white chaos of the cataract — I remembered his hands, quick and certain, wrapping the rope twice around my waist while the world went to foam — and the knot had held when everything else had failed. The canoe lay beside me on its side, hull split along one seam like a split loaf, the last of our gear scattered downstream in the reeds. The plates were against my chest. I knew it before I moved, the way you know a heartbeat, because Sebastian had strapped the oilskin packet there with the same rope and told me, shouting over the roar: *You are the cargo now. Nothing else matters.*

I sat up and vomited river water, a shocking quantity of it, my whole body heaving as if it meant to return everything the Serrano had lent me. My hands shook so badly I had to hold one wrist with the other hand to unbuckle the straps. The oilskin was warm from my skin and dry inside. Forty glass plates, wrapped in Elias's old shirt, each one a window onto the dead.

Sebastian was twenty yards down the bank, facedown in the shallows, and for three full seconds — three seconds I will carry the rest of my life — I thought the river had finally collected its fee.

Then he coughed, rolled, and swore at the sky with feeling, and I laughed, a ragged sound with no joy in it, the laugh of someone who has come out the far side of terror and doesn't yet know what to do with the rest of the day.

"Ruth." He got to his knees, blinking water. "The plates."

"Here." I held the packet up. "All of them."

He closed his eyes and let out a breath that seemed to come from the bottom of the gorge we'd just survived. Then he looked at me — really looked, the inventory of a man checking his partner for broken things — and said, "You held on."

"You tied me on."

"Wouldn't have mattered if you'd panicked." He dragged himself up the sand and sat beside me, both of us shivering now that the adrenaline was draining out and the delta wind was coming in off the water, cool and smelling of silt and salt and something green. "I've run water like that twice. Never in a boat that old. Never with the ledger already gone."

The ledger. I had watched it go — a black rectangle tumbling end over end in the brown violence, swallowed whole, forty names given back to the river that had taken them. It had felt like a second drowning. But the plates were the proof that mattered, the photographs the surveyor's office never knew existed, and the plates were here, dry against my ribs.

"Where are we?" I asked.

Sebastian shaded his eyes and looked around at the country, and something in his face changed. The exhaustion stayed, but under it came a stillness, an attention, like a man hearing a song he'd half forgotten. The river had braided itself wide here, splitting into a dozen silver channels that wandered through reed beds and mudflats toward a distant glitter that was not river at all but sea. Herons stood in the shallows like sentries. And on the far bank, half-hidden by willows, stood the shapes of houses — low, thatched, built of reed and timber, with smoke rising thin and blue from two or three of them.

"I guided a survey crew near here, years back," he said slowly. "The delta settlements. River people. They keep to themselves." He paused, and I watched him arrive at the thought the same moment I did, watched it land in his eyes. "Ruth. Your mother's people. Vane said — that night in the cantina, he said she came from upriver stock, delta families. He said it like an apology."

I hadn't thought of it since the fog narrows, since there had been no room in my head for anything but water and rope and staying alive. But it was true. My father had told me almost nothing about my mother — a silence I had resented for years and now understood as its own kind of wound — but he had told me this much once, drunk, weeping, ashamed: *She came from the river, Ruthie. From the delta folk. They never forgave me for what I ran from, and I never forgave myself, and that was the one true thing between us.*

The delta folk. The lost forty had been recruited from the river settlements — that was in the ledger's own cold language, *local labor, forty heads, engaged at Serrano mouth*. The government had taken its workers from these reed houses and these smoke plumes, and had never sent word home.

"Sebastian," I said. "We didn't wash ashore. We were delivered."

* * *

They came to meet us before we could come to them.

We had dragged ourselves along the bank toward the willows, plates between us, when a boy of perhaps ten stepped out of the reeds directly in front of us — soundless, barefoot, a fish spear in one hand — and looked at us with the frank, unafraid stare of a child who has never been taught to fear strangers because his people have never had anything worth stealing. He looked at Sebastian first, then at me, and his stare changed. His brow creased. He said something in a language I did not know, soft and questioning, and then, in accented Spanish: "You have the river's look. Whose daughter are you?"

"My mother was born here," I said, and my voice cracked on the second word. "I think. Her name was — " and I gave it, the one name my father had ever spoken of her, and the boy's eyes went wide.

He turned and shouted into the reeds, a long rising call, and the reeds answered. People came — not running, but coming the way water comes, steadily, from every direction, as if the delta itself had decided to produce them. Men with fish traps on their shoulders. Women with reed baskets. Elders leaning on grandsons. And at their center, moving slowly, a woman so old she seemed carved from the same pale driftwood as the banks, her eyes milk-fogged but her face turned toward me with terrible accuracy, as if blindness had only sharpened whatever sense she used instead.

She stopped three paces away. She reached out and took my chin in a hand light as a bird's foot, and turned my face to the light, and studied it.

"Ana's jaw," she said. "Ana's stubborn mouth." Her voice was dry as reeds in winter. "You are Vane's girl. The one he sent away."

The world tilted. "You knew him."

"Knew him." Something moved through the old woman's face, weather across water. "We knew him. Forty years he stayed away, and every year the river rose we said, maybe this year the coward comes back. Every year he didn't." Her hand fell from my chin. "Why has he sent you now?"

I looked at Sebastian. He gave me the smallest nod. *Yours,* the nod said. *This was never mine.*

I knelt in the mud so that my eyes were level with hers, and I unwrapped the oilskin packet, and I held out the topmost plate — the first photograph, the one we had found in the launch's strongbox, the one that had made Sebastian sit down on a rotted crate and put his head in his hands.

The old woman could not see it. But the people around her could, and I watched the photograph move through the crowd like a spark through dry grass. It was taken on the day the camp died — I knew that now, had studied it through a dozen nights by candlelight until I knew every figure in it. The laborers' tents. The surveyor's flag. The men lined up at the edge of the excavation, and the overseers with rifles, and in the foreground, half-turned, a young man with a prospector's pack, caught mid-flight, looking back at the camera. My father, forty years younger, wearing his cowardice for all time.

"He took the plates when he ran," I said. "He hid them at the source. He made a map and spent his last months finding someone to carry it up. He died before he could come back himself." I heard my own voice go hard. "He didn't come back because he couldn't face you. I'm here to face you for him."

The silence that followed was the longest of my life. The delta wind moved the willows. Somewhere a bird cried, three notes, descending.

Then the old woman said, "Bring them to the long house. All of them. And build up the fire." She turned her blind face toward the crowd, and her voice, which had been soft with me, went out like a hammer. "Send runners to every channel. Every family. Tell them to come and see. Tell them — " and here her voice broke, just once, a single crack in forty years of stone — "tell them we have their names."

* * *

They laid the plates out one by one on a cloth before the fire, and the whole settlement filed past them, and I understood that I was watching something no archive and no courtroom could ever hold.

The long house was a great ribbed hall of reed and timber, smoke-blackened, old beyond guessing. The fire was built up until the heat pressed on our faces. And the people came — dozens at first, then more, poling in from the far channels through the evening, until the hall was packed shoulder to shoulder and people stood at the doors and peered in over the others' heads.

Sebastian held each plate to the firelight, tilting it so the images caught the flame, and called out what each one showed in his plain salvage-diver's Spanish, and I translated into the delta tongue as best I could with the old woman — her name was Marisol, and she was, I learned with a jolt, my mother's aunt — murmuring corrections at my elbow. And the people looked.

They looked at a photograph of the camp, and an old man said, "That is my father's tent. The blue one. He wove that canvas himself," and sat down on the earth and wept without sound.

They looked at a photograph of the forty men drawn up for the surveyor's records, two days before the end, and a woman cried out a name, and then another name, and then the hall filled with names the way a struck bell fills with sound — *Tomás, Esteban, the two Herrera brothers, old Faustino who could whistle birds down out of trees* — each name called by someone who had a right to it, each name answered by a sob or a silence or a child asking *who was that?* and being told *your great-uncle, your great-uncle, look, look at his face.*

They looked at the photograph of the morning after — I will not write down what it showed; it is enough that the government surveyor had photographed his own work as documentation, and that Elias had stolen the documentation along with the plates of everything else — and the hall went quiet in a way I had never heard a room go quiet, forty people breathing at once and none of them making any other sound.

And then they looked at the last plate. The one of my father, running.

I had dreaded this one. I had wanted to hide it, to turn it face down, to spare them and spare him and spare myself. But Marisol put her hand over mine when I reached for it and said, "Show it. We are not a people who look away. That is how they beat us the first time — because we were far away and did not see. We see now."

So I showed it. And the hall looked at the young man running, and no one cursed him. An old man near the fire — the one who had wept for his father's blue tent — said, "He carried it. Forty years he carried it, and it killed him slow, and in the end he sent it home." He looked at me. "The river took his map and gave us his daughter. That is a fair trade. That is more than fair."

I did not cry in the hall. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only here: I did not cry until later, until the fire had burned low and Marisol had led me by the hand to the water's edge and told me about my mother — how she sang, how she could read the delta channels in the dark, how she had waited three years for Elias Vane to come back down the river and then married a fisherman and raised four children and never once spoke the prospector's name aloud, and how she had died in the fever year, and how Marisol had held her hand at the end and my mother's last word had been *upriver*, and no one had known what she meant.

Now I knew. Now they all knew.

* * *

It was near midnight when the runner came.

I was sitting outside the long house with Sebastian, both of us wrapped in borrowed blankets, too tired to sleep, watching the channels silver over with moonlight. He had been quiet a long time — the particular quiet I had learned to read on the river, the quiet of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

"Say it," I said.

"Hale's alive." He said it flatly, the way you report a depth sounding. "The cataract didn't take him. Men like Hale don't die in accidents; they die of paperwork, and there's no paperwork on him yet." He turned the brass fitting over in his fingers — he still carried it, that odd non-navy piece from the Corvus, worrying it like a talisman. "And the packer's alive too, and the packer knows where we went after the confluence, and Hale knows the delta. If he wants the plates badly enough to follow us down that gorge, he wants them badly enough to come here."

"Then he'll have to come through forty families who just learned what was taken from them."

Sebastian almost smiled. "I'd pay to see that. But Ruth — " and the smile went away — "there are two ways this ends. The plates go to Hale's office, get sealed as evidence, and in ten years some clerk loses them and the forty stay a rumor. Or they go to the world, all at once, in a way no one can unsee." He looked at me. "That's not my call. It was never my call. But someone in that hall has to decide it before Hale arrives, and Hale arrives soon. A day. Maybe less."

I was still turning that over — the shape of it, the weight, the terrible ordinary question of *who do you trust with the dead?* — when the runner came up the bank at a dead sprint, a boy of sixteen, soaked to the waist, and skidded to a stop before Marisol's door.

He was shouting before he'd caught his breath. A boat, he said. A gray boat with an engine, running dark, working up the main channel against the current. And in the bow, a man in a uniform, and beside the man in the uniform, wrapped in a navy coat, limping, gray-faced, alive —

The packer. The man who had stolen the map, and who had confessed on the rocks above the falls to what he had done to my father.

He was coming to the delta. And he was coming in Hale's custody, or Hale's company, and no one in the gray boat could mean us any good.

Marisol came out onto the step, blind eyes toward the river, and the whole settlement seemed to wake at once, dogs barking, doors opening, the fire in the long house thrown up bright. She found my hand in the dark and gripped it with that bird-light, iron-core strength.

"Daughter of Ana," she said. "Decide fast."
