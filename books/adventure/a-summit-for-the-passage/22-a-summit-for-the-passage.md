## 22. A Summit for the Passage

The dry season came again, as it always did, and with it the smell of the river dropping — that green mineral breath rising off the exposed stones, the water going from brown to a clean, cold jade. Sebastian Fairweather stood on the gravel bar at the mouth of the Serrano and watched the first paying customers of the Passage Company unload their duffels from the supply truck, and he found, to his mild astonishment, that he was nervous.

Not river-nervous. He had run this water forty times since the descent, and his hands knew every eddy line between here and the cataract the way a priest knows his psalms. This was a different kind of nerves. The kind that came from having something to lose.

"Fairweather." Ruth Vane's voice carried across the bar the way it always did, flat and clear as slate. She was standing by the canoe rack in her patched canvas jacket, checking lashings with two fingers, not looking at him. "The Delgados brought their boy. He's eleven. He wants to know if the rapids have names."

Sebastian smiled. "Do they?"

"You tell me. You're the one who names things."

He walked over. The boy — small, sunburned, wearing a life vest three sizes too large so that his arms stuck out like a heron's wings — looked up at Sebastian with the particular gravity children reserve for men who work on water.

"They have names," Sebastian said, crouching to the boy's level. "But we don't say them until we've run them. It's bad luck otherwise."

"Why?"

"Because the river hasn't decided what it is yet today. Neither should we."

The boy considered this with enormous seriousness, then nodded once, satisfied, and went back to helping his mother with the waterproof bags. Ruth watched him go, and something in her face loosened — the look she got, Sebastian had learned, when the past sat quietly instead of shouting.

"The memorial crew finished the last panel yesterday," she said. "Tomas came down to see it. He cried." She said it without embarrassment; they had passed through embarrassment months ago, somewhere around the third trip upstream, somewhere in the fog narrows where she'd taken the stern and called the lines while he paddled until his shoulders screamed. "Forty names. Carved in granite from up-canyon. The elders chose the stone themselves."

"I know. I helped carry it."

"I know you did." She finally looked at him, and her eyes were the same gray-green as the high water in spring. "I just like hearing you say it."

* * *

They ran the first rapids at mid-morning, three canoes in formation, Sebastian leading in the bow of the freighter with the Delgado boy beside him, solemn as a deacon. The Serrano took them the way it always took them now — not gently, never that, but with something that had come to feel like recognition. The river knew their weight. It made room.

Sebastian had stopped counting bottom time years ago, after Tomás drowned — after the Corvus, after the brass fitting he'd carried in his pocket through everything and left, finally, on Elias Vane's cairn at the source. For a long time afterward he had measured his life in miles: miles of river, miles of salvage line, miles between himself and whatever waited behind him. Miles were honest. Miles didn't ask you to stay anywhere or answer to anyone.

Now, poling through the long slow bend below the drowned camp — where the water still covered the old timbers and the scrubbed grave marker, where the elders had insisted the site be left as it lay, a wound kept open on purpose — Sebastian caught himself measuring differently. The Delgado boy would be twelve next season. Ruth's mother's people were talking about a school field trip in the fall, the older children, the ones who could carry the story respectfully. Old Tomas had said he wanted to make one more trip up before winter, and Tomas was eighty-one, and so the company had agreed to run a special departure, slow, with extra porters, whenever Tomas said the word.

Years, he thought. He was learning to measure in years.

At the camp's edge, where the half-submerged buildings broke the surface like the ribs of some patient animal, Ruth raised her hand and the flotilla paused, drifting, while she told the story. She told it plainly, without ornament: the strike, the surveyor's ledger, the forty laborers listed as expended, the government flood that buried the evidence under a reservoir's dead water. She told it the way her father should have been allowed to tell it, decades ago, before a man in uniform put out his light on a dock far from home.

The boy listened with his mouth open. One of the Delgado women wept quietly and was not consoled, because she did not want to be.

Then Ruth raised her hand again, and they moved on, and the river closed over the camp the way it always did, keeping what it kept.

* * *

They portaged the gorge on the second day — the same route, the same switchbacks, the same stretch of trail where a rockslide had nearly taken Sebastian and where Ruth had hauled him back by the pack straps, and where afterward, shaking, he had told her about his brother for the first time. The trail was wider now. The company had paid for the blasting, and the elders' young men worked it each season, and there was a cairn at the slide point with no name on it, because Sebastian had asked for it that way. Some debts you marked without signing.

Ruth walked the portage at the rear, sweeping for stragglers, and found herself doing what she always did on this stretch: listening for her father.

Elias Vane was eleven months dead now. The inquiry had gone forward without him — the journalist's copies of the photographic plates, the surviving ledger pages, the packer's testimony given in exchange for a sentence he'd serve in a minimum-security facility where, Ruth had heard, he grew tomatoes and asked for forgiveness from anyone who would listen. Hale had resigned rather than face the board, and disappeared into whatever country took men like him. There had been no trial for Elias's murder, not properly; the state had settled, quietly, with money Ruth had used to buy the canoes and outfit the company and carve the granite. Justice outside any courtroom, the journalist had written. Ruth had cut the clipping out and burned it, because she didn't need paper to remember.

What she remembered instead was the map. Her father's hand-drawn map, oilskin-wrapped, pressed into a stranger's hands on a dock because he hadn't trusted anyone else — including, God forgive him, his own daughter. She had spent a long winter being angry about that. She had spent the spring letting it go. The map itself hung now in the company's office in town, framed behind glass, its ink fading year by year, and visitors sometimes asked why they didn't preserve it better, and Ruth always gave the same answer:

"It wasn't drawn to last. It was drawn to arrive."

On the high shoulder of the portage, where the whole canyon opened below them — the river a bright thread stitched through a thousand feet of gold and rust-colored stone — she caught up to the Delgado boy, who had stopped and was staring down at the water.

"Mister Fairweather says the river decides what it is," the boy said to her. "What is it today?"

Ruth looked. The Serrano ran green and certain through the gorge, born in the cathedral cavern two days upstream, headed without hesitation for the sea.

"Today," she said, "it's going home. Same as us."

* * *

They reached the source on the fourth morning, as they always tried to, timing the final approach for dawn.

There was no way to arrive at the cavern quietly; the river announced it, roaring up out of the rock face in a plume of spray, and the last hundred meters were climbed hand over hand on wet stone while the mist soaked everyone to the skin. But inside — inside the sound fell away into that vast resonant hush, and the lantern-light rose along walls ribbed like the throat of some enormous instrument, and the spring welled up out of the dark floor, cold and impossibly clear, and every visitor, every single time, went silent.

Sebastian had seen grown men kneel in here. He had seen the elders' grandmother stand in the shallows with her arms open and sing a welcome in a language older than the canyon, singing to the water as if greeting a relative returned from a long journey. He had seen Ruth bury her father's compass beneath a cairn at the cavern's edge, and he had laid the Corvus's brass fitting on top of it, and the two objects had stayed there together through rain and freeze, slowly going green, slowly becoming part of the mountain.

This morning the memorial stood waiting above the spring: the granite slab with its forty carved names, set into an alcove the masons had cut so the spray would reach it, so the water would touch the stone daily, forever. Below the names, in smaller letters, the words the elders had chosen:

THEY WERE EXPENDED BY MEN WHO KEPT COUNTS.
WE KEEP THEIR NAMES.

Tomas had made this trip after all — he rode in the lead canoe wrapped in wool blankets, refusing all help except at the climb, which he made on his own feet, slowly, leaning on his grandson's arm. Now he stood before the slab and read each name aloud, unhurried, and the visitors stood behind him in the dripping dark, and the boy Delgado mouthed the names along with him, learning them the way you learn a psalm.

When Tomas finished, he turned to Ruth.

"Your father," he said, "was the only one who came back down and could not forget. That is a kind of courage too. A harder kind." His old eyes moved to Sebastian. "And this one carried the confession up a river that did not want him. Also a kind of courage." He raised his voice slightly, so it filled the cavern the way the spring filled it. "Courage is not the same as glory. Remember that, all of you. Glory is what men write about themselves. Courage is what you do when no one is writing anything."

Then he touched the granite, once, and let his grandson help him back down to the boats.

* * *

They camped that night on the shelf above the falls, the expedition's traditional last night, and after the visitors slept Sebastian and Ruth sat up with tin cups of coffee watching the stars wheel over the canyon rim.

"Do you remember," Ruth said, "when I came to your bunkhouse and accused you of stealing my father's effects?"

"Vividly. You had a telegram in your fist like a warrant."

"I'd rehearsed it. On the bus. Three hours of rehearsal." She laughed softly. "I thought you'd be some salvage pirate. Half my courage that night was fear, and the other half was guilt — I hadn't spoken to him in six years, and then he was dead, and I needed someone to blame who wasn't me."

"And instead you got stuck with me."

"Instead," she said, "I got stuck with you."

Below them the cataract thundered, the same water that had nearly killed them both on the descent, that had swallowed the ledger but spared the plates lashed to Sebastian's chest. He thought of that morning sometimes — the gray water standing up in waves, Ruth screaming the lines at him over the roar, the two of them alive afterward on the bank, coughing, laughing, ruined and reborn in the space of ten minutes. Everything wagered on skill and luck. Everything, it had turned out, worth wagering.

"Next season," Ruth said, "the elders want to talk about expanding. Two more canoes. Maybe a base camp at Mile Forty, near Tomas's place — he says he'll advise, if we feed him and let him complain."

"He'll complain regardless. It's how he blesses things."

"And there's a letter." She reached into her jacket and handed it to him. "Came to the office last week. Addressed to 'The Passage Company, care of R. Vane.' No return address."

Sebastian turned it over. Heavy paper. Official watermark, faintly visible in the firelight. His stomach tightened with an old reflex — navy business, off-limits, questions unwelcome — but Ruth only sipped her coffee, unbothered.

"Open it," she said. "Whatever it is, it can't follow us up here. Nothing follows us up here anymore. That's the whole point of this place."

He opened it. Read it. Read it again.

"What?" Ruth said.

"It's from the naval hydrographic office." He looked up at her, and the firelight caught something in his face she couldn't name yet. "They're requesting a survey partnership. The upper Serrano was never properly charted — officially, it doesn't exist above the reservoir. They want our guides. They want *me*." He paused. "And there's a postscript. The request was authorized by—" he checked the signature block, and felt the miles and years of his life shift subtly beneath him, like ballast moving in a hull— "Lieutenant Commander Adair Hale."

Ruth set down her cup. "Hale resigned."

"Hale resigned from intelligence." Sebastian read the postscript once more, slowly. "This commission is civilian. Survey branch. And the last line says—" he almost laughed— "'I am told the river has names now. I would like to learn them honestly.'"

The cataract roared below them. Above, the stars kept their ancient, indifferent count.

Ruth reached over and took the letter from his hands, read it twice, and handed it back. "Well," she said. "The river decides what it is each day. I suppose men get to as well."

Sebastian folded the letter away and looked downstream, toward the sea, toward the drowned wreck of the Corvus and the harbor lights and everything they had carried up this water and brought back down. Then he looked upstream, toward the source, where the compass and the brass fitting slept under their cairn, where forty names wore the spray like a blessing.

"We'll sleep on it," he said. "We decide everything on the water. That's the rule."

"That's the rule," Ruth agreed, and poured the last of the coffee, and the two of them sat a while longer in the dark, measuring the silence not in miles, not in years, but in the sound of the river going home.
