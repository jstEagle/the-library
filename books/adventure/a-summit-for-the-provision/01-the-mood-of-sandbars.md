## 1. The Mood of Sandbars

The delta woke the way it always did: slowly, and in layers.

First came the birds — the sandpipers working the mudflats at first gray light, their cries thin as wire. Then the smell, brackish and green, the river's breath rising off the shallows where the Kestrel braided itself into a hundred channels and forgot, for a while, that it was one river at all. Last came the people, if you could call them that before coffee: Juno clattering up the gangplank with her hair still wet from the crew-house pump, old Ferro coughing his way through the engine check like a man arguing with a stubborn relative.

Rafael Ishikawa was already awake. He was always already awake, though he could not have told you why. Some part of him kept a watch no one had asked for, standing its post behind his eyes through the small hours, and by the time dawn touched the water he was on deck with tea going cold in his hands, listening to the Marrow breathe against her moorings.

She was a good boat. Not a beautiful one — the delta didn't make for beauty, it made for endurance — but honest. Sixty feet of riveted hull and salt-scoured deckhouse, her wheel painted the green of bottle glass, her name lettered on the bow in paint that had faded to the color of an old bruise. *Marrow.* His grandfather's joke, or maybe his father's: the boat was what was left when everything soft had been boiled away. She'd carried cargo, mail, the occasional coffin, and for nine years now she had carried Rafael too, up and down these patient channels, asking nothing of him except that he show up each morning and know the water.

And he did know it. That was the one thing about himself he trusted without reservation. He knew where the sandbars slept and where they wandered; knew which channels were lies in August and which were lies only in flood season; knew the way the current combed the reeds at the Second Narrows so that they all leaned east, like a congregation listening to something upstream. The delta forgave nothing but it also hid nothing, and there was a comfort in that which Rafael had never examined too closely. A man who has lost his memory learns to love what can be known completely.

He drank the cold tea anyway. It was that kind of morning — the kind the delta gave you two or three times a season, when the fog sat low and white over the flats and the whole world seemed to have agreed to wait.

* * *

Isela came aboard at seven, carrying bread wrapped in a cloth and an expression that meant she'd already been awake longer than he had.

"You're brooding," she said, setting the bread on the chart table. "I can hear it from the dock."

"I'm drinking tea."

"You're drinking tea *broodingly*. There's a difference. Ferro will tell you."

Ferro, elbow-deep in the engine room, raised one grease-blackened hand in solemn confirmation. Isela laughed, and the sound moved through the deckhouse the way sun moves through fog — not dispelling it, exactly, but warming it from within.

Rafael watched her the way he allowed himself to watch her: briefly, thoroughly, and never when she might turn around. She was pulling off her coat, shaking river damp from it, and her dark hair was escaping its braid in the way it always did by mid-morning, and there was flour on her knuckles from the bread, and none of this should have mattered to a man who ran a cargo boat, and all of it mattered more than the tide tables.

Nine years he'd known her. Four since she'd come aboard as first mate, after the ferry job dried up, and in four years he had said perhaps a tenth of what he thought about her, and every unspoken word had settled somewhere in his chest like silt, building up quietly, changing the channel of things without anyone marking the change on any chart.

"Juno says the fish buyer's offering double rates for a run to Corvel," she said, tying her braid back. "We could take it tomorrow, be back by the weekend."

"We're not due anywhere."

"That's my point. We're due nowhere, we owe everyone, and the purse is thin enough to see light through." She looked at him across the chart table, and her eyes were brown and level and saw entirely too much. "How much did you send the clerk this month?"

The question landed the way her questions always landed — gently, and exactly where she aimed them.

"The usual," Rafael said.

"The usual being?"

"A fair installment."

"On a debt nobody's ever explained to me." She said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. She wasn't accusing. She was simply stating the shape of the hole in their finances, the way you'd note a rot spot in a plank. "Four years I've kept your books, Rafa. I've never once seen a paper for it. No lender's mark, no interest schedule. Just you, walking down to the harbor office on the first of every month, coming back lighter."

"It's settled business."

"Nothing is settled that takes this long to settle." She let it go then — she always let it go, one step short of the edge — and turned to the day's work, and Rafael stood at the wheel pretending to study a channel he could steer blindfolded, and hated the small cowardly relief of having dodged her again.

Because here was the truth he kept below his waterline: he didn't fully know what the debt was either. He knew the clerk — Pellam, a dry stick of a man with ink-stained cuffs and a face like a closed ledger. He knew the amount, fixed and unchanging, year upon year. He knew that the first time he'd walked into the harbor office to pay it, nine years ago, he had done so with a bandage around his head and a gap in his memory wide enough to lose a brother in, and that Pellam had looked at him with something that was not quite pity and slid a receipt across the desk without a word of explanation, and that Rafael had signed because some instinct older than thought told him signing was safer than asking.

He remembered Tomas. He remembered Tomas the way you remember a photograph — vivid, complete, and flat. The laugh. The way he stood with his weight on his heels. The compass he'd carried, brass-cased, that their mother had given him. What Rafael did not remember was the night Tomas died, and the not-remembering had a texture to it, a smoothness, like river stone worn by water. Grief he could understand. This was different. This was absence with edges, and every month, walking down to pay Pellam, he felt those edges under the surface like a bar waiting for a keel.

* * *

The morning resolved into work, as mornings did. They took on a load of timber ties and salted fish for the middle-reach settlements, and the fog burned off by ten, and the delta showed its ordinary face: mudflats steaming, herons standing their one-legged vigils, a boy poling a skiff through the reeds with the unhurried arrogance of someone who owned every mosquito in the territory.

Juno worked the forward deck, coiling lines with the fierce precision of someone proving something. She was nineteen and had been with them two seasons, a delta kid with rope-burned palms and a habit of asking questions until even Ferro surrendered. She'd come aboard looking for wages and stayed, Rafael suspected, looking for something else — a shape for her life that the shore hadn't offered. He recognized the hunger. He'd just never figured out what he'd been hungry for instead.

"Captain," she called, testing a cleat with her boot. "Ferro says if we don't repack the stuffing box this winter he won't answer for the consequences."

"Ferro says that every winter."

"Ferro means it every winter." She grinned, and the grin was quick and bright as a fish turning. "He showed me the packing. I could do it. I've got smaller hands than him."

"You've got smaller hands than the winch drum," Ferro's voice floated up from below, "and half the sense."

"She'd do it better than you and you know it," Isela said, passing forward with the manifest, and Juno glowed like a lamp, and Rafael felt the familiar ache of watching his crew become a family he hadn't planned and couldn't imagine losing.

That was the thing about contentment, he thought, as the Marrow cast off at noon and nosed out into the main channel. It never announced itself. You only heard it afterward, in retrospect, the way you hear a door close. On the water that afternoon, with the sun warm on the deckhouse roof and Isela at the stern checking the wake and Juno singing something tuneless at the bow and the delta unrolling its slow green miles ahead — on that afternoon, if anyone had asked him, he would have said he was content. He would even have believed it.

It was a contentment built the way a house is built on pilings over deep water: sound enough, so long as you never looked straight down.

* * *

They made the harbor office at half past three, tied up at the customs berth, and Rafael walked the two blocks to see Pellam alone, as he always did.

The harbor office smelled of ink, tar, and old paper — the three smells, Rafael sometimes thought, that held the delta together. Pellam sat behind his counter beneath a wall of pigeonholes, each slot stuffed with manifests and mooring certificates and the small bureaucratic sediment of a thousand voyages. He was a narrow man, gray as driftwood, with spectacles that magnified his eyes into something startled and permanent.

"First of the month came early," Pellam observed, without greeting.

"Cargo came early."

Pellam opened a ledger — the ledger, the one kept apart from the others, bound in plain black cloth with no title on the spine. Rafael had never seen inside it. In nine years he had never once asked to see inside it, and the not-asking had become its own kind of discipline, like a tongue returning to a missing tooth.

He counted out the notes onto the counter. The same sum, always. He'd stopped wondering how the figure had been set; it had been set before he understood there was anything to wonder about, back in the white weeks after the accident, when men in sober coats had spoken to him kindly and slowly and he had agreed to whatever was put in front of him because agreeing required less of him than understanding.

Pellam counted it again, though they both knew the count would match. Then he wrote the receipt, tore it free, and slid it across.

"Balance remains," Pellam said. It was not quite a question and not quite a statement. It was the sentence they exchanged every month, a ritual worn smooth as a prayer bead.

"Balance remains," Rafael said.

And then — because the fog had lifted and the light was slanting gold through the dusty window and something in Rafael was tired in a way sleep didn't touch — he heard himself say: "How much longer, Pellam?"

The clerk's pen stopped moving.

It was the smallest thing. A hesitation of perhaps two seconds, the nib hovering above the ledger, a drop of ink gathering at its tip and falling back. But Rafael had spent nine years reading water — reading the lie in a smooth stretch of current, the bar hidden under a glassy surface — and he read that hesitation the way he read a river. Something underneath. Something moving against the visible flow.

"How much longer is the balance?" Rafael pressed. "What is it a balance *of*, Pellam? I sign your receipts every month and I've never once—"

"The terms were fixed," Pellam said quietly, "by parties other than myself. I am a clerk, Captain Ishikawa. I record. I do not originate." He blotted the receipt, aligned it with the counter's edge, and for a moment his magnified eyes came up and met Rafael's, and there was something in them that might have been apology, or warning, or both wearing the same coat. "You might ask yourself, instead, whether you truly want the account closed. Some balances, once settled, leave nothing between the parties. Nothing owed means nothing withheld. And what is withheld—" he tapped the black ledger once, softly, "—is often kinder than what would be revealed."

The words hung in the tar-and-ink air. Somewhere outside, a gull screamed once and fell silent, as if embarrassed.

Rafael took the receipt. His hand was steady, which surprised him. "One day I'll want to know."

"Yes," Pellam said, returning to his writing. "I imagine one day you will. The river brings many things back downstream, Captain. It always has. It's only a question of what condition they arrive in."

Rafael walked out into the late light. The delta lay gold and calm around him, the flats shining, the channels braiding away toward horizons that promised nothing and delivered exactly that. Behind him the harbor office door clicked shut, and ahead of him the Marrow waited at her berth with her running lights just lit, and Isela stood at the rail watching him come, and he raised the receipt in mock salute and made himself smile, and she smiled back, and neither of them knew — how could they — that the smile was built on the same pilings as everything else, over water far deeper than the delta's own.

That night, moored and quiet, he dreamed of ice.

Not the delta's soft mud and warm reeds but true ice, blue-white and groaning, and a voice calling his name across it in a tone he almost recognized, and his own hands — he saw them clearly, in the way of dreams — wrapped around a rope that went taut and then slack, taut and then slack, and he woke before he could learn which direction the rope was running, and lay in the dark listening to the river move against the hull, patient as anything that intends to arrive eventually.

In the morning, there was a stranger on the dock.

She stood at the foot of the gangplank in a traveling coat the color of wet slate, watching the Marrow with the stillness of a heron, and when Rafael came up on deck she looked up at him, and he knew her.

He knew her the way you know a scar — instantly, painfully, and without any memory of how it was made.

"Hello, Rafael," Sable Voss said. "It's been nine years."
