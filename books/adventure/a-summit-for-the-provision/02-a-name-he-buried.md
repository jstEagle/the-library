## 2. A Name He Buried

The morning Sable Voss came back up the Kestrel, the fog was doing what fog always did on the delta — lying about distances, making the moored boats look like ghosts of themselves, making the water look like something you could walk across if you believed hard enough. Rafael Ishikawa stood at the rail of the *Marrow* with a tin cup of chicory going cold in his hand and watched a shape resolve out of the white: a river skiff, black-hulled, running light and fast against the current, the way only someone who knew this water intimately could run it.

He knew the boat before he knew the person. That was the trouble with rivers. They kept everything. Hulls remembered their builders; wakes remembered their makers. And some part of him — the part that had spent nine years pretending to be a man with no history deeper than the last high-water mark — recognized that wake pattern the way a dog recognizes a whistle.

The skiff came alongside the dock below the *Marrow*'s berth with barely a kiss of fender against piling. The woman who stepped out wore a long coat the color of wet slate and boots that had seen real country, not just harbor stone. She was older. Of course she was older. But time had not softened Sable Voss so much as it had carved her — cheekbones sharper, mouth set in a line like a channel marker, eyes the flat gray-green of deep water over rock.

She looked up at the rail where he stood, and nine years collapsed into nothing at all.

"Hello, Rafe," she said.

The cup trembled. He set it down on the rail cap before she could see, or told himself he did it for that reason, though his hands were not entirely his own just then.

"That's not my name," he said.

"It's the name I knew." She hooked her elbow over a piling and regarded him with the unhurried patience of someone who had traveled a long way to have this conversation and intended to have all of it. "You can be whoever you like to everyone else. To me you're Rafael Ishikawa, captain of the *Marrow*, and the best pilot this river ever drowned and spat back."

"I don't know what you've heard—"

"I know what I saw." Her voice didn't rise. It never had. Voss argued the way water worked — constant pressure, finding every seam. "I saw you take a boat through the Lower Stair with your hands bleeding and your face gray as ash, and I saw you do it again the next season because nobody else could. Then I watched you bury yourself alive down here in the mudflats, paying off clerks and keeping your head down, and I let you do it, because I owed you that much. I'm done letting."

Rafael came down the ladder slowly, buying himself each rung of delay. Up close she smelled of woodsmoke and cold air and something faintly chemical — lamp oil, maybe, or the mineral tang of glacier country. Nine years, and his body still reacted to her presence like weather: a dropping of pressure, a tightening behind the sternum, the old animal knowledge that a storm was in the room.

"You shouldn't have come here," he said quietly.

"No," she agreed. "But here I am."

* * *

They did not talk on the dock. Dock talk was public talk, and whatever Voss had come to say, she said it low, under the creak of the pilings, while gulls screamed their idiot opinions overhead. She laid it out plainly, because plainness was her weapon when she chose to use it.

Triple pay. A run upstream — all the way upstream. Past the Lower Stair, past the Narrows, past the toll bridges and the braided channels and the halfway house rotting into its own foundations. Up through the calving fields to the glacier source itself, arriving before the ice sealed the season shut.

"The window's short," she said. "Three weeks, maybe less. The ministry's survey teams pulled out early this year. Nobody's running cargo that far up. Nobody sane."

"And you want me to do it anyway."

"I want *the Marrow* to do it. You at the wheel. Your crew on the lines. Triple pay, half up front, and the rest on delivery at the source."

Rafael laughed, and hated the sound of it — a dry, cracked thing, like ice shifting. "The source." He turned away from her and gripped the rail, looking out at the brown water sliding seaward, carrying everything it took from the mountains down to the delta and forgetting none of it. "Do you know what's up there, Voss?"

"I know exactly what's up there."

"Do you?" He spun back, and the words came out of him with a force that surprised them both. "Because I don't. That's the joke, isn't it? Nine years, and I still don't know what happened that night. I remember the fire. I remember shouting. I remember the cold coming up through the deck plates like it was rising out of the river itself. And then—" His hands opened and closed at his sides. "Then nothing. There's a hole where that night should be, and you were there, and you've never once told me what's in it."

Something moved behind Voss's eyes — quick, submerged, gone. A fish turning under ice.

"If I tell you what's in it," she said carefully, "you'll never make that run. And I need you to make that run."

"Then we have nothing to discuss."

"We have triple pay to discuss. We have a crew to discuss." She glanced up at the *Marrow*, at the patched hull plating visible even from here, at the rigging mended with line that should have been retired two seasons ago. "How much is in the purse right now, Rafe? After the clerk took his cut this morning?"

The question landed harder than any blow could have. He thought of the ledger page in the harbor office, the column of figures he'd been reducing installment by installment for years, a debt whose origin he kept deliberately vague even in his own mind. He thought of Juno's boots, split at the seams. He thought of Isela counting stores last night by lamplight, lips moving, and the expression she'd worn when she finished — not despair, exactly. Arithmetic.

"This conversation is over," he said.

"It hasn't started yet." Voss pushed off the piling and straightened her coat. "I'll be at the Salt Lantern until tomorrow's tide. Not longer. If the answer's no, I'll find another boat, and God help whichever crew signs on without you knowing the channel." She paused at the foot of the gangway, and for one unguarded moment the carving of the years fell away and she was just a tired woman on a dock, saying something true. "I'm sorry it has to be you, Rafe. If it could be anyone else, it would be."

Then she was gone into the fog, and the delta closed over her like water over a dropped stone.

* * *

He didn't go to the Salt Lantern. He went up instead, into the wheelhouse, and stood at the wheel he hadn't left in nine years except to sleep, and let the river talk to him. It talked in the language it always used — current against hull, the groan of the mooring lines, the far-off percussion of the dredge barges working the shipping lane. Ordinary sounds. Safe sounds. The soundtrack of a life built carefully on top of a cellar door he never opened.

Isela found him there an hour later. She came in without knocking, which was her privilege and always had been, carrying two cups of the chicory he'd abandoned earlier, now fresh and hot. She put one in his hand the way she did everything — without asking permission, without apology, with a competence that made the world feel briefly load-bearing.

"You met her," Isela said. Not a question. News traveled down a dock faster than floodwater.

"You knew?"

"I saw the skiff come in. Black hull, upstream lines, running against the current like she owned it." Isela leaned against the chart table and studied him with those dark, level eyes that missed nothing and forgave selectively. "I figured either the syndicate finally found you, or a ghost did. Voss's name came out of the harbormaster's mouth inside ten minutes, so. Ghost."

"She wants us to run the source route."

Isela went very still. It was a small stillness, controlled, the kind she'd learned growing up around unpredictable water — but Rafael had learned to read it, and it told him everything: she understood immediately what that meant, what it cost, what it dug up.

"And what did you tell her?"

"No."

"Of course you did." She sipped her coffee, watching him over the rim. "And how did the crew factor into that answer? Did you think about Juno's boots? About the fact that we're eleven days from being unable to pay the mooring fees, and after that we start losing the boat piece by piece to the harbor trust? Did you think about the fact that Okafor's been talking about jumping to a coastal freighter because we can't promise him a season?"

"I thought about the fact that the last time we ran that route, we lost four people," Rafael said. The words came out flatter than he intended, scraped raw underneath. "I thought about the fact that I came back down that river with four fewer crew and no memory of why, and that the woman offering me triple pay was standing next to me when it happened and has never told me the truth about it. That's what I thought about."

Isela set her cup down. When she spoke again, her voice was gentler, but gentleness in Isela was never softness — it was precision applied with care.

"Rafa. I know what that night took from you. I've watched you carry it for nine years like ballast you refuse to jettison. And I am not going to stand here and tell you grief is a budget item." She held up a hand before he could speak. "I'm not. But I will tell you what I told myself last night over the stores ledger, because somebody on this boat has to say it out loud. Pride doesn't float a hull. Grief doesn't pay mooring fees. And love—" her voice caught, just slightly, and she smoothed it flat again, "—love doesn't keep the lights burning on its own. We are broke, Captain. Not struggling. Broke. And the only offer on this river worth anything is standing at the Salt Lantern until tomorrow's tide, and she asked for *you*."

"She asked for the channel in my head. That's different."

"Maybe. Maybe not. But you won't know which unless you ask her, and you can't ask her anything from behind this wheel." Isela pushed off the table and crossed to the window, looking out at the fog thinning as the morning warmed, the delta emerging from its white lie into ordinary daylight. "I'm not telling you to say yes. I'm telling you that 'no' stopped being free the moment she walked onto this dock. Everything costs now. Even refusal."

He looked at her — at the set of her shoulders, the stubborn line of her jaw, the way her hand rested on the windowsill as if the whole boat were an extension of her body. He loved her. He had loved her for years, in the wordless, deferred way of a man who felt he had no right to want things, and he would have carried that silence to his grave rather than burden her with it. But in that moment, watching her argue for the crew's survival with the same ferocity she brought to everything, he understood that she was already carrying more of his weight than he'd ever acknowledged.

"If we take this," he said slowly, "it changes everything. The route, the boat, us. There's no coming back down that river the same people."

"I know." Isela turned to face him fully. "But Rafa — we were never coming back the same people anyway. Boats change. Rivers change. People change or they sink. At least this way we'd be choosing the current instead of drowning in the eddy."

* * *

That evening, the crew gathered in the mess — Okafor, broad and quiet, mending net by lamplight; Juno, seventeen and all elbows and hunger, perched on the bench with her knees drawn up; old Piet, who'd been on the river longer than the river had names, smoking his pipe by the stove. Isela laid out the numbers the way she always did: plainly, without drama, letting the arithmetic do the arguing.

Triple pay. Half up front. A three-week window. And the destination, spoken aloud in that small warm room for the first time in nine years: the glacier source.

Nobody spoke for a long moment. The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, a night bird called across the water, and the *Marrow* rocked gently at her moorings as if shifting her weight in her sleep.

"The source," Piet said at last, around his pipe stem. His voice was unreadable, ancient. "Huh. Thought that river was done with us."

"It's not done with us," Juno said, surprising everyone, herself included. Her voice cracked on the second word, and she pushed through it. "It's not done with us because we never finished anything. We just... stopped. There's a difference."

Okafor set down his netting needle. "What's the catch?" he asked Isela, not Rafael. Everyone knew who actually ran the business end of the *Marrow*. "Nobody offers triple pay out of charity. What's she not telling us?"

"I don't know yet," Isela admitted. "But I intend to find out before we sign anything. Terms first. Full terms. In writing, with escape clauses, and half the money in our account before the lines come off the dock." She glanced at Rafael, and something passed between them — an agreement, an apology, a promise. "If the terms aren't clean, we walk. Captain's veto stands. Agreed?"

There was a chorus of assent, ragged but real. Rafael sat at the head of the table and said nothing, because there was nothing left to say that the numbers hadn't already said for him. He had lost the vote before it was cast, and some traitorous part of him — the part that still dreamed in cold water, the part that woke some nights with Tomas's name dissolving on his lips — was not entirely sorry.

Later, alone on deck under a sky rinsed clean of cloud, he stood at the rail and let the river's voice wash over him. Tomorrow he would go to the Salt Lantern. Tomorrow he would sit across from Sable Voss and negotiate terms for a journey back into the worst year of his life, and he would do it because his crew needed feeding and his boat needed patching and because Isela was right, damn her, about everything, as usual.

But tonight, for a few minutes longer, he allowed himself the luxury of the old refusal. He let himself feel the weight of the name he'd buried — not Voss's name, but his own. Rafael Ishikawa. Pilot of the upper river. Brother of Tomas. Man who once swore, on the ice itself, that he would never again run water he couldn't see the bottom of.

Tomorrow he would dig that name up and put it back on, like a coat that no longer fit.

Tonight, the fog rolled in off the delta again, and somewhere out in the white, he could almost believe he heard a skiff's engine, patient as guilt, waiting for an answer.
