## 3. Terms in Salt Water

Isela had learned to read a room by its silences, and the Marrow's cabin that morning was louder than any argument.

Rafael stood at the head of the table with his arms crossed, the way he did when he was holding himself together with his own hands. Sable Voss sat across from him, hat off, rain still beading in the wool, and the space between the two men was the widest thing in the room — wider than the table, wider than the nine years they hadn't spoken, wide enough that Juno kept glancing into it and away again like a bird deciding whether to land.

Old Pell had lit the lamp even though it was barely past dawn, and the flame threw their shadows up the bulkhead, two long dark shapes that almost touched and never did.

"Triple pay," Voss said again, as if repetition were a kind of argument. "Upstream to the source, before calving. Six weeks, maybe seven. I supply the permits, the fuel, the charts."

"There are no charts," Rafael said. "That's the whole point of the route. That's why it's forbidden."

"I have charts."

The words dropped into the room like a stone into still water. Isela watched Rafael's face — watched the small muscle under his eye jump, watched him decide not to ask. That was Rafael all over. He'd built a whole life out of deciding not to ask. Nine years of it, and she'd loved him through most of those years without ever quite saying so, because saying so would have meant asking what he was carrying, and he'd made it plain the first winter she'd known him that some cargo he would not unload for anyone.

"Say the rest of it," Rafael said quietly. "Say why you came to me and not to any of the fifty other captains on this river."

Voss's jaw worked. "Because you know the channel."

"Nobody knows the channel. The channel changes every spring when the ice lets go. That's why they call it the Kestrel and not the road."

"You know how to *read* it, then. Don't make me flatter you, Ishikawa. We both know you're the only man alive who's run the upper river and lived, and we both know why."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, on the deck, the rain picked up, drumming on the planks, and the Marrow shifted at her moorings with that patient creak she made in her sleep, like an old dog dreaming.

Isela looked at the purse hanging from the nail by the galley door. It was flat. It had been flat since the spring floods closed the delta runs, and it would stay flat until the delta opened again, and flat meant no flour, no lamp oil, no pay for Juno, whose mother was sick downriver in Marsh Landing and whose letters had started arriving with the kind of handwriting that meant the news was getting worse.

She looked at Rafael's back, at the set of his shoulders, and she understood that what he was refusing wasn't a job. It was a door. And she understood, with the cold clarity that had made her a good first mate and a bad liar, that if she let him close it, she'd be choosing his ghosts over her crew's empty bowls.

"Rafael," she said.

He didn't turn. "No."

"You haven't heard the crew."

"I've heard enough of this man for one lifetime."

"Then hear me." She kept her voice level, the way you kept your hands level in rough water. "The purse is empty. Juno's mother needs medicine. Pell's roof went in the last storm and he's been sleeping in the hold and pretending he isn't. We can eat pride or we can eat, and I know which one fills the belly."

Rafael turned then, and the look he gave her wasn't anger. That was the worst of it. It was something more like pleading, a man on a riverbank watching someone wade into water he knew was deep. "Isela. You don't know what's up there."

"No," she agreed. "And neither do you. That's the trouble with the both of you — you remember it, and you won't say it out loud, so the rest of us get to be frightened of a thing nobody will name."

Voss made a sound — small, bitten off. Isela caught it and filed it away, the way she filed everything.

"The crew votes," she said. "That's the law of this boat. You wrote it yourself, Rafael, the first week I signed on. *The Marrow decides with all hands or she doesn't decide at all.*"

"I wrote that before I knew what I was asking them to vote on."

"You wrote it so you couldn't be a tyrant. Don't be one now just because it's convenient."

For a long moment he said nothing. Rain on the deck, lamp hissing, the river breathing against the hull. Then Rafael looked at Voss, and Isela saw something pass between the two men that had nothing to do with money or routes — some old accounting, some debt with interest still accruing — and Rafael said, "Fine. Vote. But I'm telling you all now, and I'll say it plain: that river took a crew from me once. It will not take another. If it comes to choosing, I'll turn this boat around with or without your pay."

"Then let's hope it doesn't come to that," Voss said, and Isela heard the crack in his voice, hairline-fine, and filed that away too.

* * *

They voted in the galley, standing up, because Pell said votes taken sitting down took too long and Juno said votes taken sitting down felt too much like church. Isela counted. Juno's hand went up first, fast, before fear could catch her — Isela loved her for that, and worried about her for it too. Pell raised his hand slow, the way he did everything, and said, "Triple pay buys a new roof," as if that settled the moral question, and maybe it did. Isela raised her own last, after Rafael, who raised his like a man signing something he'd regret, and she caught the look he gave her — not blame, exactly. Warning. The look of a man who had already seen the end of a story and was trying to tell it backward.

Four to one, with Voss abstaining, sitting in the cabin like a guest at his own trial.

"Then it's settled," Isela said, and her heart was pounding, because she'd just voted her crew onto a river that had drowned men she'd never met but heard about in the way you hear about weather — the disaster year, the boat that went up and didn't come down, the captain who came back with his eyes changed.

She found Voss on the aft deck afterward, standing at the rail with the rain coming down around him like he didn't notice it, watching the delta current carry silt and sticks and one drowned bird out toward the sea.

"Terms," she said. "You'll negotiate them with me, not him."

Voss turned. Up close, Isela could see what the hat and the coat were built to hide: a man worn down to the frame. Gray at the temples he hadn't earned honestly. Hands that were river hands, whatever the fine coat said — rope-scarred, knuckles like river stones. He'd been a boatman once. Whatever he was now, he'd been a boatman first, and that was its own kind of information.

"Rafael is the captain," Voss said.

"Rafael is the captain of the water. I'm the captain of everything else — the purse, the cargo, the crew's backs, and the contract. That's how the Marrow runs. You want her, you deal with me."

Something moved behind Voss's eyes that might have been respect. "Terms, then."

"Triple pay, half in advance. Advance today, before we cast off, in coin, not paper. Paper burns and banks fail and I've seen both."

"Done."

"Provisions and fuel are yours to supply, but we inspect everything that comes aboard. Every crate, every barrel. If I find something that sinks us, it goes over the side, and you don't argue."

Voss's mouth twitched. "And if it's something that floats?"

"Then I'll decide what floats." She let that sit. "Medical fund for the crew, separate from pay. If anyone's hurt on this run, their family is paid through the season, no questions, no conditions. In writing."

"In writing," Voss agreed, too easily. Isela made a note of that too — men who agreed too fast were either honest or rehearsed, and she'd met more of the second kind.

"And one more." She looked out at the river, gray under gray, the far bank a rumor. "Nobody dies for your cargo. Whatever's in those sealed crates, whatever's at the source you're so hungry for — the moment it costs a life, this contract is void, we turn around, and you pay in full anyway. That's the term that isn't negotiable, and if you won't sign it, we're done talking."

The rain filled the silence. Voss looked at the water for a long time, and when he spoke his voice had changed — lower, rougher, the fine-man polish worn through in one spot.

"You think I don't value life on this river." He turned to her. "You think I came up that dock after nine years because I wanted cargo delivered. Ask your captain what I've already paid for this route. Ask him what it cost the last time."

"I'm asking you."

"And I'm telling you the terms are agreed." He put out his hand, and she took it, and his grip was cold and strong and careful, the grip of a man who had learned to be careful with his strength. "Half in advance, coin. Inspection rights. Medical fund. Void on a death." A pause, so small she almost missed it. "Void on another death," he said, and corrected nothing, and Isela felt the hair rise on her arms.

*Another.* He'd said *another*.

She let go of his hand. "One question before I draw up the paper. The last time you ran this river — the crew that was lost. I know the captain. I don't know the rest. Who was on that boat?"

"Deckhands. A cook. A mate." Voss said it flat, reciting, a man reading a manifest of the drowned.

"Names?"

"Why does it matter?"

"Because I sign my crew onto your river, and I want to know what it's already taken."

Voss looked past her, at the cabin, where Rafael's shadow moved behind the glass of the lamp, and Isela watched his face do something complicated — grief and fear and something older than both, guilt with nine years of silt on it.

"There was a boy," Voss said. "Young. Came aboard as a deckhand and ended up running the bow line like he'd been born to it. Best of us, by the end." His voice caught on the last word, and he covered it with a cough, a small dry sound. "His name was Tomas."

The rain came down. The river breathed. And Sable Voss — the man who had sat through Rafael's hatred without blinking, who had offered triple pay like it was nothing, who had agreed to every term without a flinch — Sable Voss flinched at a dead boy's name. Not at the memory. Isela had seen men flinch at memories, and it looked different. This was a flinch like a man touching a wound he'd made himself and told everyone was someone else's.

"Thank you," Isela said, and her voice was steady, and inside she was already writing.

* * *

That night, after Voss had gone ashore to his hotel and the advance had been counted twice and locked in the strongbox, after Juno had gone to her bunk and Pell to his roofless corner of the hold, Isela sat in the galley with the lamp turned low and a blank ledger book open in front of her — one of the boat's spares, meant for cargo manifests, its ruled lines empty as still water.

She wrote a heading on the first page. Not *Cargo*. Not *Accounts*.

**Lies**, she wrote. And under it, the date.

Then, in her small careful hand, the way she recorded every barrel and crate that crossed the Marrow's deck:

*1. Voss says he came for the channel. A man doesn't wait nine years for a channel.*

*2. Voss agreed to every term too fast. Honest men haggle.*

*3. Voss said "another death." He counted before.*

*4. Voss flinched at the name Tomas. Not like grief. Like guilt.*

She sat back and read it over. It wasn't much. Four lines. But ledgers were like that — a thing started small and grew by entries, and the truth of a voyage was never in any single line but in the running total.

She thought about Rafael, alone in the wheelhouse, doing whatever he did up there in the evenings — she suspected he stood at the window and looked upriver, and she suspected she was the only person alive who knew that about him, and she suspected he didn't know she knew. She thought about telling him what Voss had said. *Another death.* She thought about how his face had looked when he'd said *that river took a crew from me once*, and how the word *Tomas* would land on a man like that, and she decided — not finally, not cleanly, but for tonight — that some entries belonged in her ledger before they belonged in his.

She was protecting him. Or she was managing him. She honestly couldn't tell the difference yet, and that frightened her more than the river did.

The lamp guttered. Isela dipped her pen again, and beneath the four entries she wrote one more, small, almost against her will:

*5. I voted them onto this river to fill the purse. If the ledger of lies ever has an entry with my name in it, let this be the first.*

She blew out the lamp and sat a while in the dark, listening to the Marrow breathe at her moorings, the river running north toward the ice, and somewhere out in the wet night, faint and far off, she thought she heard — though it might have been only wind in the rigging — the long low groan of a glacier letting go of something it had held a very long time.
