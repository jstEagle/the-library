## 4. Loading the Marrow

The morning came up gray and slow over the delta, the kind of light that made everything look like it was remembering something. Rafael stood on the aft deck of the *Marrow* with a mug of chicory going cold in his hand and watched the fog peel off the water in long, reluctant ribbons. The river was high with snowmelt. It carried things downstream — branches, silt, once, memorably, an entire floating boathouse with its doors still latched — and it took things too, if you let it.

He had spent nine years learning to let it.

Below him, the crew was already moving. He could hear Juno's voice carrying up through the deck boards, bright and argumentative, narrating her own labor as she always did, and beneath it the softer rhythm of Isela's instructions, clipped and precise, each word placed like a cleat. The *Marrow* groaned at her moorings, settling into the weight that would soon be hers. A hundred and ten feet of riverboat, shallow-drafted, paddle-wheeled at the stern, built by a shipwright who had died believing she was his masterpiece. She had been running cargo on the Kestrel for eleven years now, and she complained like an old woman about every ton they asked of her, but she had never once left them swimming.

Rafael drank the last of the chicory and went down to work.

* * *

Loading day had its own liturgy, and he followed it the way he followed all rituals — not because he believed in them, but because the shape of them kept other shapes out. Fuel drums first, rolled up the gangplank two men to a barrel, stowed low and amidships where the hull could forgive them. Then dry goods, rope, the spare paddleshaft wrapped in canvas like something wounded. Juno hauled crates with a wheelbarrow she'd repaired herself from salvage, singing under her breath, off-key, a dockside song about a girl who drowned her lover and felt fine about it.

"Captain." Isela appeared at his elbow with the manifest on a clipboard, pencil tucked behind her ear. "Voss's people are here."

"I know. I can hear them."

"You can hear Juno. Everyone can hear Juno."

That was true. Juno's narration of the fuel drums had escalated into a full aria.

Isela handed him the manifest, and he read it standing in the gray light with the river breathing against the pilings. Most of it was what he expected: provisions, coal oil, spare parts, medical stores. Voss had paid for all of it upfront, cash, no haggling — which itself was strange, because men who paid without haggling were either rich or frightened, and Sable Voss had never been rich in a way that showed.

But there were three line items that didn't sit right.

*Surveying instruments, one case.* Fine. You surveyed a route before you ran it heavy. Except they weren't surveying a route; Rafael knew this river the way he knew the backs of his own hands, every sandbar and snag from the delta to the Lower Stair, and above the Stair nobody surveyed because nobody went.

*Sealed crates, six.* No contents listed. Just *sealed*, as though the word were a description rather than an evasion.

*Permits, ministry-stamped, one folio.*

He looked up. At the foot of the gangplank stood two men he hadn't seen arrive — Voss's people, presumably, though they moved nothing and helped with nothing. They stood with their hands folded in front of them like ushers at a funeral, watching the loading with the particular attention of men counting what wasn't theirs yet. One was tall and gray, the other short and dark, and between them on a hand truck sat a wooden case about the size of a coffin for a child.

"The instruments," Isela said, following his eyes. "They want them stowed in the forward hold. Sealed section."

"Nothing goes in my sealed section without me opening it first."

"They said—"

"I don't care what they said."

He crossed the deck. The tall man saw him coming and straightened, and there was something in the way he did it — not deference, exactly, but readiness — that made Rafael slow down. Men who straightened like that had done time holding weapons or being near them.

"Captain Ishikawa." The tall man's voice was flat and pleasant. "We're told you're the best pilot on this stretch."

"Who told you?"

"Mr. Voss."

"Mm." Rafael stopped at the hand truck. Up close, the case was good work — brass corners, oiled wood, locks that had been waxed against weather. Stenciled along one side, half worn away, was a marking he almost recognized: a circle bisected by a line, and beneath it a string of characters in a bureaucratic alphabet he'd seen on customs forms and toll receipts his whole life. Ministry property. Except the ministry whose mark that resembled had been dissolved nine years ago, its functions scattered, its seals retired. He'd watched the announcement himself, read it twice, because Tomas had worked a season as a junior clerk in one of its satellite offices and had come home laughing about the paperwork.

The seal shouldn't exist. And yet here it was, stenciled on a case riding a hand truck up his gangplank.

"Open it," Rafael said.

The tall man didn't move. "It's sealed for calibration. Opening voids the warranty of accuracy."

"Instruments survive river travel or they don't. Open it."

A pause, long enough to have weight. Then the short man stepped forward, produced a key from inside his coat, and opened the case with the unhurried competence of someone who had done this many times and been asked about it never.

Inside, nested in wool, lay the instruments. A theodolite, gleaming. Levels, plumb lines, a rangefinder with a leather strap still stiff from disuse. And beneath them, occupying the bottom third of the case, a flat drawer that the short man did not open, locked with a mechanism too fine for anything containing maps.

Rafael looked at the drawer for a moment. "What's in there?"

"Benchmarks," the tall man said. "Reference coordinates. Mr. Voss wants the source position fixed precisely."

"For what purpose?"

"Mr. Voss's purposes."

Rafael closed the case himself, pressing the hasp until it clicked. Around them the loading continued, Juno's song drifting across the deck, the river sliding past below, ordinary as bread. But something had shifted in the morning's grain, some small wrongness, the way a current changes a quarter-mile before you ever see the bar that caused it.

"Stow it forward," he said finally. "Sealed section. I'll want the key."

The tall man hesitated, then handed it over — a small brass thing, warm from his pocket. Rafael took it and turned back toward the boat, and behind him he heard the two men exchange no words at all, which was somehow worse than any conversation.

* * *

The sealed crates came aboard through the afternoon, one at a time, carried by hired dockhands who were paid well enough not to look curious. Six of them, each about the size of a flour barrel laid on its side, each strapped in iron banding, each stamped with the same impossible seal. They weighed more than their size suggested; Rafael watched the dockhands' knees and backs tell him so. Whatever was inside was dense. Machinery, maybe. Or metal. Or paper — paper in quantity could weigh like sin.

Isela directed their stowage while Rafael handled the last one personally, walking it up the ramp with Juno steadying the far end, and it was as he crossed the main deck with the crate in his arms that his compass happened.

He wore it on a lanyard, as he always had — his father's compass, brass-cased, glass scratched nearly blind, the needle balanced on a pin so fine it responded to a heartbeat. He'd navigated by it through fog and night and one memorable whiteout on the upper braids, and in eleven years it had never lied to him, never wavered, never done anything but point north with the dumb faithful certainty of a dog.

As he passed the stack of crates already secured in the forward hold, the needle trembled.

Not swung — trembled. A shiver, quick and fine, like a struck tuning fork, and then it settled back to north as though nothing had happened. Rafael stopped. Held the crate. Watched the needle.

It trembled again, a stuttering hesitation, and this time when it settled it settled a degree off true, quivering at the edge of correction like a man glancing over his shoulder.

He walked the crate to its slot, set it down, and stood there in the dim of the hold with the dust motes turning around him. Then he took the compass off the lanyard and held it flat in his palm and walked the length of the stacked crates, slow, watching.

Near the second crate, the needle shivered. Near the fourth, it dragged, fighting its own bearing, and for a moment pointed not north but at the crate itself, dead-on, as though the thing inside were a pole star of its own. Then he stepped past and it released him, snapping back to true with a tiny audible tick he'd never heard it make before.

Magnetized cargo. That was the reasonable answer. Steel stock, instrument cores, something industrial. He said it to himself twice, the way you repeat a lie to make it hold its shape. Magnetized cargo, nothing stranger than that, and rivers were full of iron ore barges and compasses were delicate things and there was no reason in the world to feel the cold that had just walked up the back of his neck.

Except that his father's compass had sat in the hold of boats carrying steel cable, engine blocks, an entire disassembled dredge, and had never once flickered.

He climbed out of the hold into the late-afternoon light and found Isela checking lashings, and for a moment he considered telling her. The words assembled themselves somewhere behind his teeth — *the compass, the crates, the seal that shouldn't exist* — and then he looked at her face, set and competent in the slanting sun, and thought about the crew's empty purse, and the vote already taken, and how a captain's job was to carry dread so his crew didn't have to. That was the trade. That was always the trade. You swallowed the fear whole and gave them back routine, and routine was a kind of gift, and he had been giving it for nine years.

"Anything?" she asked.

"Cargo's heavy," he said. "Balance it low and forward."

She studied him a beat longer than the answer deserved. Isela missed nothing; that was her gift and her burden both. "You'll tell me if it's more than heavy."

"It's heavy," he said, and hated how easily it came.

* * *

By dusk the *Marrow* sat deep in the water and ready, lamps lit along her rail, the delta gone violet around her. Voss arrived at last light, alone this time, walking the dock with his coat collar up, and stopped at the gangplank to look over the loaded boat the way a man looks over a horse he's betting his life on.

"She rides heavy," Voss said.

"She rides true. Your crates are stowed. I want to know what's in them before we clear the Stair."

"You'll know what you need to know when you need to know it."

"That's not how I run a boat."

Voss smiled, and there was something tired underneath it, something old. "No. How you run a boat is you take on passengers you don't ask questions about, and you pay your harbor clerk every month like clockwork, and you keep your head down and your channel clean. I've watched you do it for nine years, Rafael. It's why I hired you." He put one boot on the gangplank, then paused. "One more thing comes aboard tonight. Small. You'll find it in the morning."

"What is it?"

"You'll find it in the morning," Voss said again, and went up the plank into the lamplight, leaving Rafael with the river talking against the pilings and the compass hanging at his chest, needle steady, pointing north, lying to him perfectly.

That night Rafael slept badly in the wheelhouse bunk, and woke before dawn to a sound he couldn't name — not a footstep, not water, just the sense of the boat having changed in the dark, of something added. He came out onto the main deck while the fog was still thick enough to drink, and there, lashed neatly beside the pilothouse door where anyone steering would pass it a hundred times a day, sat a new crate.

Small. Iron-banded. Stamped with the circle-and-line seal.

And as he stood looking at it, his father's compass trembled at his chest, once, twice, like a pulse counting down to something, and out on the black water a lamp appeared — far upstream, moving fast against the current, coming toward them out of the fog.
