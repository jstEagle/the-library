## 5. First Rapids

I learned the river's moods before I learned to read, and the Lower Stair had a mood like a drunk uncle — all laughter one minute, then grabbing your wrist hard enough to bruise. From my perch on the foredeck, coiling rope that didn't need coiling, I could hear it before I could see it: a long, grinding hush under the engine's chug, like the water holding its breath.

"Juno." Isela's voice came from behind me, clipped and calm, which was how she sounded when she was worried. "Stop making that line pretty. Check the pin on the port cleat. Then the starboard. Then do them again."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And Juno — when it starts, you don't think. You do what the captain says the first time he says it. Not the second. The first."

I nodded, though she'd already turned away. That was the thing about Isela: she never said *if* something went wrong. Only *when*.

The Marrow wasn't much to look at — a hundred feet of patched hull and stubbornness, her paddle wheel turning like an old man climbing stairs — but I'd been aboard six months now, longest I'd ever stayed anywhere, and I knew her sounds the way I knew my own heartbeat. This morning she sounded nervous. Boats don't get nervous. But crews do, and boats catch it like a fever.

Captain Ishikawa stood at the wheel in his usual silence, gray at the temples, hands resting on the spokes light as a pianist's. He hadn't said more than ten words since we cast off from the fishing village two days back, and half of those had been channel depths. There was a rumor below decks that he'd run this stretch of river before, years ago, and that the last crew who tried it with him hadn't come all the way home. Nobody said it where he could hear. Nobody needed to. You could see it in how he watched the water — not like a man reading a chart but like a man looking for something he'd lost in it.

And then there was Voss.

Sable Voss moved around my deck like she owned it, which irritated me more than I could say, because technically she did own the charter. She wore her coat open despite the cold coming off the glacier country upstream, and she handled lines with a crisp economy that made my own work look clumsy. Every so often I caught her watching the captain's back with an expression I couldn't file anywhere — not anger, not exactly. Something older than anger. Something that had been left out in the weather a long time.

The river narrowed ahead. The banks shouldered in close, granite walls streaked white with bird lime, and the water between them went from brown-green to a churning, muscular gray. The sound of it filled everything, the way a shout fills a small room.

* * *

"Lower Stair," the captain said, and his voice carried without effort, the way some men's voices do when they've spent their lives being listened to. "Three drops over half a mile. We take the left tongue at the top, ride the second chute center, and pull hard right before the hole at the bottom. If I say *all ahead full*, I mean it. If I say *back water*, I mean that too. Questions cost us nothing now. They'll cost us everything in ninety seconds."

Nobody had questions.

Isela came forward and took the bow line station beside me, checking my cleat pins herself with two sharp tugs. She smelled of tar and coffee. "You've got the heaving line if anyone goes in," she said quietly. "You throw, you don't jump. You hear me? You throw."

"What if they miss it?"

"They won't miss it. Make sure of that." Her hand squeezed my shoulder once, brief and hard, and then she was all business again, facing downstream.

Voss took the stern without being assigned there. I noticed that. Nobody told her where to stand; she just arrived at the place where the steering oar backup lived, sleeves rolled, and started paying out slack so the line would run free if it was called for. Her hands did it without looking. That bothered me too, in a way I couldn't name yet — the way she fed rope through her palms, thumb and forefinger, letting it breathe through the coils instead of fighting it. Deckhands learn to fight rope. Pilots learn to talk to it.

The first drop announced itself as a smooth green shoulder of water maybe forty yards wide, curling over into foam. Beyond it the river fell away in steps — I could see the spray of the second chute rising and falling like something breathing, and past that, a boil of white where the whole current slammed into a rock shelf and split.

"Ahead full," the captain said.

The Marrow leaned into it. The wheel dug deep and the deck shuddered, and then the bow lifted — actually lifted, I felt my stomach float — and we went over the first stair with a crash that soaked everything forward of the midships house. Cold hit me like a slap. My hands were on the heaving line coil before I even decided to put them there.

Through the streaming water I heard the captain's voice, level as a carpenter's level: "Left tongue. Hold her there. Isela — watch the rock, starboard side, tell me what you see."

"Submerged shelf, two feet down, thirty yards!" Isela called back, and I marveled, in the stupid way I marveled at things during disasters, at how she made information sound like comfort.

"Hold. Hold. Now ease right — *ease*, Juno, off the line, let it run—"

I hadn't realized I'd been strangling the heaving line. I opened my fists. Rope hissed through them, burning, and I let it.

The second chute took us sideways. That's the honest truth of it — no boat rides that water straight; the river decides your angle and you negotiate from there. The Marrow slewed, wallowed, shipped a ton of gray water across the cargo deck, and somewhere below I heard one of the sealed crates shift against its lashings with a knock like a knuckle on a door.

"Center! Give me center!" the captain roared, and the engineer gave him everything, the paddle wheel thrashing, and slowly, grudgingly, the old boat squared herself and dropped down the chute's throat.

For three seconds there was nothing in the world but noise and water and the tremendous animal strain of the hull. I remember thinking, absurdly, of my mother's washing tub, of the way water spiraled when you pulled the plug, and how the whole Kestrel River suddenly felt like that — like we were circling a drain the size of a cathedral.

Then the captain's voice again, sharper now, the first crack in the level: "*Hard right.* Hard right, all of you — Voss, the stern line, NOW—"

There was a hole at the bottom of the Stair. I'd heard about holes. Water that pours over a ledge and doesn't come up on the other side, just rolls back on itself, hungry. We were dropping straight toward it and the current wanted us dead center in its mouth.

Voss was already moving. I saw her take two steps down the wet deck, plant her boots, and haul the stern line across in one long, singing arc — not to cleat it, but to swing it wide and take a turn around the bollard, using her whole body as the lever, leaning back until her heels skidded. The line snapped taut and the Marrow's stern swung, just a few degrees, just enough—

"Hard right," the captain said again, quieter, and the wheel answered, and the bow came around, and we slid past the edge of the hole close enough that I could see down into it: a spinning green eye ringed with foam, deep as a well, patient as winter.

Then we were through. The river widened, gentled, went back to pretending it was harmless, and the Lower Stair fell astern roaring its disappointment.

* * *

Afterward nobody celebrated, exactly. Celebration comes later, after the shaking stops. What happens first is work — bailing, wringing out, re-lashing the crates that had knocked. I was sent below to check the hold, and that's where I found Voss, alone in the lamplight, standing over the sealed crates with her hand flat on the nearest lid. Resting there. Not opening it. Just resting, the way you'd rest your hand on a grave marker.

She heard me and pulled her hand back fast, too fast, and turned with the charter-owner face on. "Damage report?"

"Crates held. One shifted. Lashings are good."

"Good." She looked at me a moment longer than the report required. "You did well up there, Juno. The line work."

"Thank you, ma'am."

"It's Sable." She smiled, and it almost reached her eyes. Almost is the important word. "Your captain ran this water like he had it memorized."

"He says he doesn't remember it."

Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under a boat. "No," she said softly. "I expect he doesn't." And she climbed the ladder out of the hold, leaving me alone with the crates and the drip of riverwater off the beams.

I stood there a while. Six months I'd been aboard the Marrow, and in six months I'd learned that this crew kept its secrets the way the river keeps drowned things — down deep, in the cold and the dark, where the current can't reach them. I was starting to understand that I'd been hired onto a story that began long before I did, and that the sealed crates, and the captain's silences, and Sable Voss's hands that remembered ropes her mouth denied knowing — all of it was one story, and I was sailing straight up its throat.

Back on deck, the captain was at the rail, watching the water slide by. I brought him coffee, because that was my job on good days, and because I wanted to ask him something on bad ones.

"The Stair," I said. "You made it look easy."

He took the cup. For a moment I thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "It isn't easy. It only looks that way when you've already survived it once." He drank, watching the current. "The river forgives nothing, Juno. It just forgets slower than people do."

I didn't know what he meant yet. But that evening, moored in the eddy below the bluffs, I saw Voss coil a line at the stern — that same pilot's trick, thumb and forefinger, letting the rope breathe — and I saw the captain see it too. He went very still, the cup halfway to his mouth, staring at her hands like they were a language he used to speak.

Neither of them said a word. But the air between them pulled tight as a line coming taut, and I thought: whatever broke between these two didn't break on calm water.

Two days later we'd round the bend above the Narrows and find out what someone had done to our rudder pins in the night. But that evening I didn't know it yet. That evening I only knew the river's roar fading behind us, the crew laughing shakily over supper, Isela's hand ruffling my hair like I'd earned something — and the feeling, growing in me like frost on a window, that the hardest water we'd run on this river wouldn't be found on any chart at all.
