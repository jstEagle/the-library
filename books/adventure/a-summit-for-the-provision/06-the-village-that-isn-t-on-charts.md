## 6. The Village That Isn't on Charts

The fog came off the water before dawn, thick as milk, and Rafael steered the Marrow into it the way a man walks into a room where he knows something has died.

He'd been awake since three. Not from the nightmare — that had been mercifully absent — but from the compass. It sat in its brass housing on the wheel stand, and all night the needle had done a thing he'd never seen it do in twenty years on the Kestrel: it had wandered. Not spun, not stuck, just wandered, a slow drunken oscillation of a few degrees, as though the river itself had shifted its mind about which way was north. He'd checked it against the stars when the clouds broke around midnight and confirmed what he already knew. The compass was fine. The river was wrong.

Or the cargo was.

He didn't look at the sealed crates lashed amidships. He'd learned, in the four days since they'd loaded them, the particular discipline of not looking. It was the same discipline he used for the past — the year that had a hole in it, the brother whose face he could summon only in pieces, an ear, a laugh, the back of a head going under dark water. You kept your eyes on the channel ahead and you did not look at the thing in the hold, because looking was how it got its hooks into you.

"Sandbar off the port bow," Isela said, coming up beside him. She had a mug of chicory in each hand and she passed him one without being asked, which was its own small language between them. "You see it?"

"I see it."

"You always see it." She drank. Steam curled between them. "That's why Voss pays you triple."

"That's why Voss pays me triple," he agreed, and did not say the other thing, which was that Voss paid triple because Voss wanted something, and Rafael had not yet learned what, and every mile upstream the not-knowing sat heavier in his chest, like ballast loaded wrong.

The fog thinned as the sun came up behind them, and the river widened, and there — where no chart in his cabin said there should be anything but reeds and a drowned forest — smoke rose from the far bank.

* * *

Rafael had run the Kestrel for twenty years and he knew its moods the way other men knew their wives. He knew the delta's silt breath, the way the Lower Stair changed its voice with the season, the exact gray-green of the water when the glacier was grinding stone somewhere far above. He knew every charted village, every fuel dock, every toll post. And he knew — with the certainty of a man who had drawn half these charts himself, in the early years, when he and Tomas had surveyed the upper river with a theodolite and a skiff and more ambition than sense — that there was no village here.

But there it was. A crescent of stilt houses on the north bank, roofs of split cedar gone silver with weather, drying racks hung with fish that flashed like coins in the morning light. Nets. A stone jetty, old and well-built, the kind of jetty that outlives the people who built it. Smoke from cookfires, and under the woodsmoke something else — roasting fish, and beneath that, faintly, a smell that stopped Rafael's breath for half a second: pine resin and burnt sugar. Someone was boiling sap. His mother had boiled sap. He could not have said whose mother, or where, but the smell went through him like a fishhook finding flesh.

"Charts are wrong," Juno said, appearing at the rail with her binoculars. She was nineteen and treated the river as a puzzle that would eventually admit her. "Or the village is new."

"Villages aren't new," Rafael said. "Villages are old. That jetty's older than me."

"Then the charts are old too."

Isela said nothing, but Rafael caught the look she gave him — the one she thought he didn't notice, the one that had been on her face more and more since Voss came aboard. It was the look of a woman doing arithmetic and not liking the sum.

"Supply stop," Voss said from behind them, and Rafael hadn't heard him climb to the pilothouse, which was itself a small alarm; a man that big shouldn't move that quietly. Voss stood with his hands clasped behind his back, surveying the village with the expression of a man reading a document. "We need greens. Fresh water. And I want to know what the ice is doing upriver."

"You know this place," Rafael said. It wasn't a question.

"I know *of* it."

"You know *of* it," Rafael repeated, "the way you know *of* the route you swore you'd never run again."

Voss's jaw worked once. "The village is called Sennet. It doesn't put itself on charts because charts bring tax men and press gangs and worse. They trade with boats they trust and they're invisible to boats they don't." He turned, and for a moment his eyes had something in them that wasn't the flat merchant's calm he wore like a coat. "They'll trust you, Rafael. That's why we're stopping."

"Why would they trust me? I've never—"

He stopped. Because the sentence had a hole in it, the way so many of his sentences had holes in them, and the hole was shaped like nine years ago.

* * *

They came in slow, Rafael at the wheel, feeling the current's opinion of the jetty through the deck plates. The village watched them arrive without greeting. Faces at windows. A dog that did not bark. Children who stopped their games and stood perfectly still, which was worse than staring — it was the stillness of children who have been told to be still.

An old man came down the jetty to meet them. He walked with a staff of black wood and he was small and brown and wrinkled as a walnut, and when Rafael stepped onto the stone with the mooring line in his hands, the old man looked up at him and said:

"Rafi."

Just that. One word, in a voice like dry reeds, and the word went through Rafael like cold water through a tear in a hull.

"I'm sorry?" Rafael said.

"Rafi." The old man said it again, gently, the way you'd repeat a name to a man waking from a long sleep. "You came back. After all this time. We thought the river had you."

"My name is Rafael Ishikawa. I don't—" He looked back at the boat. Isela had come to the rail. Voss stood at the gangway and had gone very still, in the way of a man watching a fuse he'd lit himself. "I don't know you."

The old man's face did something complicated — sorrow and patience and a flicker of something like anger, all of it passing over his features like weather. "No," he said. "I suppose you don't. Come. Sit. The fish is good this morning and the sap is sweet, and I will tell you about a boy who used to mend nets on that jetty and argue with the tide."

"I'm not—"

"You are," the old man said, with terrible gentleness, "and you did. Come and eat, Rafi. The river brought you back for a reason. It always does."

* * *

They ate in the largest of the stilt houses, cross-legged on cedar mats, and the fish was indeed good, and the sap was indeed sweet, and Rafael tasted none of it. The old man was called Ede, or so Voss said — Voss, who sat slightly apart, who ate with his eyes down, who had not looked at Rafael since the jetty. The villagers drifted in and out, bringing water jars and bundles of greens, and every one of them looked at Rafael the way you look at a man you'd buried.

"You mended nets here," Ede said. He sat with his staff across his knees. "Two seasons. You and your brother. You came upriver after the — " he paused, choosing, " — after the bad year, and you stayed. You were quieter then. You'd lost something. But you laughed more, too. It's strange, the ones who've lost things. Sometimes they laugh the most."

"I don't remember this."

"No."

"I remember the upper river. I remember surveying it. I remember—" He stopped, because what he remembered next was not a thing he had ever remembered before, and it arrived not as a thought but as a sensation: the smell of pitch smoke and the sound of a woman shouting his name — not Rafael, not Ishikawa, but *Rafi, Rafi, get down* — and the world going orange.

The fire.

It came in pieces, the way wreckage comes up from a wreck. A fire on the water. A boat burning. Shouting — many voices, and one voice under the others, a young voice, his brother's voice, saying words he could not catch, the way you can't catch a word in a dream. Then cold. Cold so total it wasn't cold anymore, it was just *dark and pressure and the taste of the river going into him*, and hands on his collar, dragging, and a light above the surface that he had reached for and —

"Captain." Isela's hand was on his arm. The mat. The fish. The room. His own breath, loud in his ears. He was gripping the mat so hard his knuckles had gone white. "Rafael. You're here. You're here with me."

"I was—" He swallowed. The taste of river water was in his mouth, actual river water, nine years old. "There was a fire. On the water."

No one spoke. Voss looked at the floor. Ede looked at Rafael with an expression of such old, worn sadness that it was almost worse than pity.

"There is always a fire on the water, in this village's stories," Ede said at last. "The night the big boats came. You remember the big boats?"

"No."

"Three of them. Black hulls. They came upriver at night without lights, which boats don't do, not on this river, not past the shallows. They were looking for something. Or someone." The old man's eyes went, briefly, to Voss, then back. "There was an argument on the bank. A fire. A man went into the water. And a boy — a young man — went in after him, and the river took them both for a while, and gave one back."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere outside, the drying racks creaked. Rafael heard his own pulse.

"Which one came back?" he asked. His voice sounded like someone else's.

"You did," Ede said. "You came back, and you stayed two seasons, and you mended nets, and at night you sat on that jetty and asked the water to give him back, and the water did not, and in the spring you went downstream and you told us you would forget. You said it like a plan. *I will forget this.* And we said, Rafi, that is not how forgetting works. And you said—" the old man's voice cracked, just slightly, just enough, "—you said, *then I will make it how it works.*"

Rafael stood up. He didn't decide to; his legs simply did it. He walked to the window and looked out at the jetty, at the gray water, at the Marrow riding at her lines, and he thought: *I built a wall inside myself, brick by brick, and I lived on the downstream side of it, and I called the downstream side a life.*

"Who went into the water," he said. "The man. Who was it."

Ede was silent long enough that Rafael turned. The old man was looking at Voss. Voss was looking at his hands.

"You know," Rafael said to Voss. The fog in his head was burning off and what it revealed was terrible and clean. "You've known this village. You've known me. You knew that name. You knew all of it, and you hired me anyway, and you brought me here anyway, or you let the river bring me here, and you're sitting there looking at your hands like they belong to someone else."

"Rafael," Voss said quietly.

"Who was in the water?"

Voss raised his eyes. And Rafael saw — for the first time in nine years of rivalry and debt and silence — the thing underneath Sable Voss's merchant calm, the thing he'd paid a harbor clerk in installments to keep drowned. It wasn't guilt exactly. It was older and more frightened than guilt. It was the face of a man standing on ice and hearing it begin to speak.

"Ask me at the source," Voss said. "I'll tell you everything at the source. I swear it on—"

"Don't swear on anything," Rafael said. "You've spent nine years swearing on things."

* * *

They took on water and greens and a sack of smoked fish that Ede refused payment for, pressing it into Juno's arms with the instruction that the boy — the girl, Juno corrected, and Ede bowed as if the correction were a gift — should eat. The crew worked in near silence, and the villagers watched them load, and Rafael felt their eyes on him the whole time: the eyes of people who had known a version of him he had never met.

Isela found him at the wheel as they cast off. She didn't ask. She stood beside him with her shoulder almost touching his, and after a while she said, "You almost told me something, in there. Before you stood up."

"I don't know what it was."

"Yes you do."

He looked at her. Her face in the afternoon light, the small scar at her hairline he knew better than his own hands, and he thought about telling her everything — the fire, the cold, the name Rafi like a key turning in a lock he hadn't known was in him. He thought about it and the words came as far as his teeth and stopped.

"Not yet," he said.

"Then when?"

"When I know what I'm telling you."

She nodded slowly, and he saw her file it away, in whatever ledger she kept — he'd caught her at it twice now, the little notebook, the entries she thought he didn't see, and he wondered what she was writing about Voss, about the crates, about him.

The village fell behind. The river narrowed. And in the last bend, looking back, Rafael saw Ede standing alone on the jetty with his black staff raised — not waving, he understood suddenly. Pointing. Pointing upriver, toward the white line of the high country, toward the source, and holding the pose like a man who had been pointing at that same spot for nine years, waiting for the river to bring the boy back and send him on to the end of the story.

That night, moored in the reeds, Rafael took out his charts and found the stretch they'd passed through and looked at the blank space where Sennet should be. Then he took a pencil, and his hand shook, and he wrote a single word in the margin:

*Rafi.*

The compass needle trembled in its housing, and upriver, faint as a rumor, came the first far-off groan of the glacier, talking in its sleep.
