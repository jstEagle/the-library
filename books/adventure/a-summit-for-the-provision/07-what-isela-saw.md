## 7. What Isela Saw

Isela had learned to sleep in layers: the body's sleep on the deck cot beneath the awning, and above it a thinner watchfulness that never quite went under — the sailor's habit of listening to the river even while dreaming of it. So when the smell came, faint and wrong, threading through the smell of wet rope and coal smoke and the green breath of the banks, she surfaced from both sleeps at once.

Burning paper.

Not wood. Not pitch. Paper has its own voice when it burns — a dry whisper, almost like a man reading aloud very fast to himself — and beneath it a sweetness, ink and glue giving themselves up to flame. She lay still for a count of ten and listened. The Marrow was tied up at a gravel bar below the Narrows, banked for the night because Rafael would not run water he couldn't see the bottom of after dark, and the crew's breathing made its usual music: Juno's deep and unguarded, the cook Ovidio's broken by his old whistle of a snore, Rafael's so quiet she sometimes leaned close just to confirm it was there.

Rafael's breathing. Still quiet. Still there.

The burning was coming from aft.

She rose without sound, as she'd learned to do years ago on boats where rising with sound had consequences, and moved along the deck in her bare feet, the planks cold and gritty under her soles. The fog had come down off the hills again, thick as wool, and the lantern at the stern burned inside its little cage of glass like a held breath. Beyond it, at the rail near the cargo hatch, stood a shape she knew too well to mistake.

Sable Voss.

He had a tin basin balanced on the coaming of the hatch, and he was feeding papers into it one at a time, letting each catch fully before adding the next. He wasn't hurrying. That was what struck her first, before fear, before suspicion — the patience of it. A man destroying evidence in panic feeds the fire handfuls. A man destroying evidence with care feeds it pages, and watches each one go, and Isela understood she was looking at someone settling accounts with himself, not hiding from anyone.

Except she was hiding from him now, folded into the shadow between the winch housing and the rail, and the fog was on her side.

* * *

She had not trusted Sable Voss from the moment he stepped onto the dock at the delta with his city coat and his river eyes. There was a contradiction in him that itched at her the way a splinter itches under skin — you can leave it alone for hours, days, but your thumb keeps finding it. He talked like a man who had left the Kestrel behind and made something of himself elsewhere; he handled rope like a man who had never really left. When he'd said Tomas Ishikawa's name — no, when *she* had said it, testing him, watching — something had crossed his face too fast to name and too slow to miss. She had written it in the ledger she kept behind her eyes, the one no crew member ever saw: *Voss flinched at the brother's name. Reason unknown.*

Now here was more ink for the ledger.

He fed another page to the flame. The light of it climbed his face and she saw, for the first time since the delta, how tired he looked — not soft-tired but worn-through, the way a mooring line wears at the chafe point, still holding, still doing its work, but thinner than it used to be in one place where all the strain collects. He glanced over his shoulder once, toward the foredeck where the crew slept, and Isela pressed herself smaller against the cold iron of the winch.

He was looking at Rafael's cot. She was sure of it. The fog swallowed everything past twenty feet, but his head turned to exactly that spot and stayed there a moment longer than a man checks for eavesdroppers. It was the look of someone checking on a sleeping person. Or standing vigil over one.

Then he went back to the fire.

Isela weighed her options the way she weighed anything: quickly, honestly, and with an eye to what the river would tolerate. She could wake Rafael. The thought arrived first and loudest, because everything in her that loved the man wanted to carry every burden to him, split it down the middle, bear half. But Rafael had been strange since the village — stranger than strange, hollowed, walking around with fragments of something moving behind his eyes like fish under ice. He had told her about the elder calling him by a name he didn't remember using, and his hands had shaken saying it, and he had said, in a voice she'd never heard from him, *I feel like I'm standing on ice that's already cracked.* Waking him at midnight to say *Voss is burning papers* would put weight on that crack. And whatever Voss was burning, it would keep or it wouldn't — either way, knowing which required her to see it first.

So she waited, and the cold worked into her feet, and the river murmured against the hull, and Voss burned his documents page by patient page until the basin held nothing but a gray lace of ash.

Almost nothing.

A scrap caught on the rim, curled but unconsumed, and when Voss lifted the basin to tip the ash over the side, the scrap lifted with the heat and skittered across the hatch coaming and fell — of all the square feet of deck available to it — into the shadow at Isela's feet.

Voss set the basin down. He looked around once, a slow sweep of the fog, and Isela stopped breathing entirely, became a piece of the boat, part of the machinery, a woman-shaped fitting welded between winch and rail. He picked up the basin and carried it forward to dump the ash off the port side, away from where she crouched, and she heard the gray lace hiss into the water, and then his footsteps went aft to his cabin, and the door closed with the particular care of a man trying not to wake anyone.

She counted to two hundred before she moved.

* * *

The scrap was small, no bigger than her palm, torn from the corner of something larger. The heat had browned its edges and curled it like a dead leaf, but the center had survived, and in the lantern light she turned it over twice before her mind agreed to read what her eyes were telling her.

Numbers. Coordinates, written in a cramped, hard hand — latitude and longitude in the old surveyor's style, the style used on the Kestrel before the ministry standardized the charts. Below them, a date.

She knew the date the way you know your own scars. Ninth year back. The year of the disaster. The year the Marrow had gone upriver with a full crew and come back short three men and one brother, the year Rafael had returned with his memory of the final night gone as cleanly as if the river had reached into his skull and taken only that one thing, leaving everything else — the channels, the sandbars, the skill — untouched. The year before Isela had signed onto this boat, drawn to its silent captain the way driftwood is drawn to a snag.

And the coordinates. She read them three times, and each time they resolved to the same place, because she had spent nine years studying this river the way other women studied scripture. Upstream. Far upstream. Past the Halfway House, past the last charted reach, into the calving fields.

The source. The glacier lake. The place the route had been sworn off, the place the maps went blank, the place Rafael refused to speak of even in his sleep — though lately, she suspected, his sleep had been taking him there anyway.

Someone had written down the source's coordinates in the year of the disaster, and Sable Voss had tried to burn them nine years later, on Isela's boat, in the dark, while the man whose brother died getting there slept thirty feet away.

She sat down slowly on the hatch coaming, the scrap cupped in both hands like a moth she was afraid to crush and afraid to free.

* * *

The obvious reading was ugly. She gave it its due, because she was honest with herself the way she was honest with the river: maybe Voss had betrayed them nine years ago. Maybe the coordinates were proof — a plan, a claim, a stake driven into the source before anyone else knew it existed. Maybe the sealed crates in the hold, the trembling compass needle, the permit stamped with a seal that shouldn't exist, all of it pointed the same direction: Voss was using Rafael the way you use a key, and the lock was the glacier, and what waited behind the lock was worth enough to burn evidence over.

But the obvious reading had never sat right with her, and she prided herself on noticing what didn't sit right. A man who betrays a crew doesn't stand vigil over the betrayed man's cot. A man chasing profit doesn't feed a fire one page at a time, like a mourner laying flowers. And the flinch — she went back to it, the flinch at Tomas's name — a betrayer flinches at being caught. Voss hadn't flinched like that. He had flinched like a man who has taken a wound in an old place and forgotten until something presses it.

There was another reading. She turned it over carefully, the way you turn over a blade to see which edge is live. Maybe the coordinates weren't a claim. Maybe they were a grave marker. Maybe Voss had carried the exact spot of the disaster written down for nine years the way Rafael carried its absence, and tonight, upstream again at last, closing in on it, he had finally decided he couldn't carry it into that country and back out.

Maybe he wasn't hiding the truth from them.

Maybe he was trying to burn it out of himself, and failing, because the truth doesn't burn. It floats. It washes ashore. It lands at the feet of a barefoot woman in the fog who loves the wronged man enough to keep his wounds clean, and it waits.

Either way — the ugly reading or the merciful one — the conclusion was the same, and it settled over her with a weight she recognized from the worst nights of her life, the nights she'd spent bailing a boat alone while everyone else slept.

She couldn't give this to Rafael. Not yet.

Not because he couldn't bear it — he could; he'd borne worse, whatever worse had been, though he didn't remember it. But because he was already carrying a full load: the debt payments, the route he'd sworn off, the memories surfacing like drowned things coming up gasping. If she handed him *Voss knows the coordinates of the night you lost your brother* now, mid-river, mid-run, with the syndicate's shadow somewhere behind them and the rapids ahead, he would do one of two things, and she knew them both because she knew him. He would turn the Marrow around and surrender the job and the crew's wages and whatever chance they had of outrunning what followed them. Or he would drive upstream through the calving fields half-mad with grief, and the river would take its toll, and she would lose him to the same water that had taken Tomas.

So she said nothing. She folded the scrap small and sewed it into the hem of her oilskin jacket with three stitches from her mending kit, working by lantern light with her fingers steady and her heart anything but, and she told herself the lie that keepers tell: *I'm protecting him.*

And then, because she was honest with herself the way she was honest with the river, she told the rest of it: *Or I'm keeping it for myself. Because knowing something he doesn't is a kind of power, and power over the man you love is the most dangerous cargo there is, and you are carrying it in your hem, Isela, and you will have to answer for it.*

* * *

Dawn came up gray and unhurried. The fog thinned to gauze, then to memory. Isela fried eggs and hard bread in the galley and watched the crew assemble around the mess box, and she performed the ordinary morning so well that she frightened herself a little with how good she'd gotten at it.

Juno came yawning, hair wild, asking if they'd make the Narrows before noon. Ovidio complained about the fog getting into his flour. Rafael ate in silence, as always, but his eyes found hers across the mess box and stayed a beat longer than usual — checking on her, the way he checked on everyone without appearing to, the captain's habit of counting his people like a man counting coins he can't afford to lose.

"You sleep all right?" he asked.

"Like ballast," she said, and smiled, and the smile cost her more than any rapid they'd run yet.

Voss came last, coffee in hand, city coat traded for a river jacket that fit him better than it should have. He looked rested in the way of men who have finished a labor, however grim. His gaze swept the deck — foredeck, hatch coaming, the rail where the ash had gone over — with the thoroughness of a man searching a room for something lost, and Isela met his eyes over the rim of her cup and offered him nothing but a first mate's flat morning nod.

He nodded back. Held it a half-second too long. As if he were wondering.

As the anchor came up and the Marrow swung her bow upstream, Isela took the wheel while Rafael walked the decks checking lashings, and she felt the scrap against her ankle with every step, small and hard as a stone in a boot. Ahead, the river narrowed toward the Narrows, black water shouldering between rock walls, and beyond it, days upstream, the country where the maps went blank and the glacier groaned in its sleep.

She had spent her whole life believing that love meant carrying half of everything. Standing at the wheel with the current pulling and the secret stitched into her clothing, she understood she had signed on for something heavier: carrying all of it, alone, for a while longer — and praying the stitching held.

Behind them, far downstream, so faint it might have been wind, a horn sounded once across the water.

Isela's hands went still on the wheel spokes.

It sounded again. Long, low, deliberate. Not wind. Not geese. A boat horn, blown twice, the way boats signal when they want to be answered — and she did not answer, and neither did Rafael, who had gone motionless amidships with his head cocked, listening the way a man listens when a sound reaches all the way down to a place he doesn't talk about.

"What was that?" Juno asked from the bow.

"Foghorn," Isela said. "Leftover fog."

But the fog was gone, and they all knew it, and the river carried them upstream toward the Narrows with the sound of that second horn lying on the water behind them like something following.
