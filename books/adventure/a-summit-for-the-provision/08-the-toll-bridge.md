## 8. The Toll Bridge

Juno had learned to read the river the way other girls read faces, and the river had been telling them for two days that someone was waiting.

It said it in the driftwood, first — fresh-cut pine, pale as bone, riding the current in pieces too straight to have fallen naturally. It said it in the birds, or the lack of them: the herons that should have stood the gravel bars were gone, and the crows had moved downstream in a loose black procession, like a funeral that hadn't decided whose it was. And it said it loudest of all in the sound. For two mornings the Marrow had run through a silence that had weight to it, a held-breath silence, the kind the river made when it was being watched.

By the third day, Juno had stopped pretending she wasn't afraid.

She stood her usual watch at the bow rail, one hand hooked through the lifeline, the other holding a cup of tea gone cold an hour ago. Below her, the Kestrel ran green and glassy through a country of black spruce and leaning shale, so calm it looked like a painting of itself. That was the trouble. Upstream of the Lower Stair, the river had no business being this polite.

"You've got the face again," Isela said, coming up beside her with the logbook under her arm.

"What face?"

"The one where you're chewing on something you won't spit out."

Juno considered lying. She was getting better at it — nine days on a boat taught you that much, at least — but Isela had a way of waiting that made lying feel like a waste of good breath. "The driftwood," Juno said. "It's cut. And there's no herons. And the water's too quiet."

Isela looked at the water for a long moment. "Anything else?"

"There's smoke," Juno said. "Way up. Past the bend where the cliffs come together. It's not rising, it's hanging. Like a fire that's being fed slow."

Isela wrote something in the logbook without appearing to write anything. "You've got good eyes, Juno."

"I've got scared eyes. It's not the same thing."

"It's better." Isela closed the book. "Scared eyes live longer. Go tell the captain what you told me. Use the same words. He listens better when it's numbers and smoke and not feelings."

Juno found Rafael at the wheel, as she almost always did now, standing with his hands light on the spokes and his eyes doing that thing they did — fixed on the middle distance, on some channel only he could see, the one that lived behind his face instead of in front of it. Since the village, since the old woman had called him a name that wasn't his, he'd gone quieter, if that were possible. He steered like a man carrying something breakable in his chest.

"Captain." She gave it to him straight, the way Isela had told her: driftwood, herons, smoke. "Somebody's up there and they don't want to be surprised, and they don't want us surprised either. They want us to know they're there."

Rafael's hands went still on the wheel. For a moment he was somewhere else — she could see it happen, the way his gaze dropped to the deck as though reading water that wasn't there. Then he looked up, and he was back, and his voice was level.

"The Toll Bridge," he said, mostly to himself. "Of course. I'd forgotten they rebuilt it."

"You know it?"

"Everybody on the Kestrel knows it. Or used to." He turned the wheel a degree, two, easing the Marrow toward the outside of the bend. "It's a gorge about four miles up. The river pinches down to half its width and the only way through is under an old timber trestle that some company threw across it thirty years ago. There's a family that lives there — lived there — that collects passage. Toll keepers. They've got chains across the water and a very large crossbow and no sense of humor."

"Is it legal?"

Rafael almost smiled. "Nothing on this river is legal, Juno. Some things are just older than the law." He raised his voice. "Isela. Slow her to half. We'll want to arrive looking poor."

* * *

They arrived looking poor, and it did not help.

The gorge announced itself by sound before sight — the river's polite silence breaking all at once into a long, throat-deep roar as the water compressed between walls of wet black stone. Juno stood at the bow and felt the sound come up through the deck plates and into her teeth. Mist hung in the gorge like breath on a window. And there, spanning the gap a hundred feet up, was the trestle: gray timber, moss-eaten, its pilings furred with creeper vine, with a plank platform built out over the water at its center like a gallows with ambitions.

From the platform, a chain ran down into the river. And on the platform stood men.

Juno counted six before she stopped counting. They were big in the way of men who ate well off other people's fear, in oiled canvas coats, and one of them held the crossbow — not aimed, but held, which was its own kind of aim. Below the platform, moored to the gorge wall, rode a long black skiff with a covered midsection, and Juno's stomach did something complicated when she saw it, because she had seen a skiff like it before, or thought she had, in the fog behind them two nights ago, and had told herself she hadn't.

Rafael brought the Marrow in slow, letting the current carry her the last hundred yards, and rang the engine down to a bare idle. The chain came into view just under the surface, links as thick as Juno's wrist, strung between the pilings with floats to mark it. A toll gate, made of river and iron.

A man leaned over the platform rail. He was older than the others, with a beard gone to wire and a voice that carried down the gorge without effort, the way voices do when they've spent a lifetime shouting over water.

"Vessel! State your business and your name!"

"Passage!" Rafael called back. "Marrow, out of the delta. Six souls, light cargo. Bound for the head of navigation."

"The head of navigation." The man said it like a joke someone had told him once and he hadn't found funny since. "That's glacier water, captain. Nobody runs to glacier water this time of year."

"Nobody but fools," Rafael agreed. "We're the fools. What's the toll?"

The man was quiet for a moment, and in the quiet Juno heard the river working against the chain, the whole gorge groaning faintly with held-back water. Then the man laughed — a short, hard sound with no warmth in it.

"The toll is four hundred crowns."

The number landed on the deck like a dropped anchor. Juno heard Isela exhale behind her. Four hundred crowns was more than the Marrow's entire purse. It was more than the purse had been even before Voss's gold had gone into the fuel and the repairs and the hull work after the Narrows. Four hundred crowns was, very nearly, everything.

"Four hundred," Rafael repeated, without inflection. "That's a steep price for four hundred yards of calm water."

"That's the price for the *chain*, captain. The calm water's free." The man spread his hands. "You can pay it, or you can turn around, or you can try the north channel, which is a lie we tell people we don't like. Your choice."

Juno watched Rafael's back. She watched the set of his shoulders, and she thought: *he's going to turn us around.* And she thought, right behind it: *and maybe he should, and maybe I want him to, and maybe I'll hate him if he does.*

It was Voss who came forward.

He'd been standing at the rail by the engine housing the whole time, arms folded, watching the platform with an expression Juno couldn't read — not fear, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man seeing a room he'd been in before. Now he stepped past Rafael to the bow rail, and when he spoke, his voice carried up the gorge as easily as the toll keeper's carried down.

"Brannoch," he said. "You've got fat."

The crossbow moved. Not much. An inch. The bearded man's laugh came down again, and this time there was something in it that wasn't entirely unkind.

"Sable Voss." He said the name the way you'd spit a fishbone. "Nine years, and the river finally washes you back up. I'd heard you were dead."

"I've heard that too. It's been convenient." Voss rested his hands on the rail. "The toll's four hundred now?"

"The toll's whatever I say it is. And for you, Voss, it's four hundred, because the last time you came through here you left me holding a debt and a bad reputation, and I've had nine years to add interest."

"Fair." Voss said it simply, and Juno realized with a small cold shock that he meant it. "I don't have four hundred crowns. But I have something better."

"Voss—" Rafael started, low.

"One moment, Captain." Voss didn't turn. "Brannoch. How's the ministry's new survey office treating you? The one that opened in Kestrelmouth last spring? The one that's been sending its inspectors up and down the river, checking permits, impounding boats?"

The silence from the platform had a different texture now.

"Nobody likes the inspectors," Brannoch said at last, carefully.

"No," Voss agreed. "Nobody does. And a smart toll keeper — a man with a trestle to maintain and a family to feed — a smart man might find it useful to have something in his strongbox that an inspector looks at and then *leaves*. Something with a seal on it. Something that says: this man is protected. This man is working for people you don't want to meet."

He turned. "Juno. The third crate. The one lashed under the tarp by the mast. Bring it here."

Juno looked at Rafael. Rafael was looking at Voss with an expression she had never seen on him before — something between anger and a kind of grim, dawning understanding, the look of a man watching a door open onto a room he'd always known was there.

"Captain," Voss said, still not turning. "You hired on to run this river. This is how it's run."

Juno went and got the crate.

It was heavier than it looked, and cold, colder than the morning had any right to make it, and when she set it on the deck at Voss's feet, he broke the seal with his thumb — the wax cracking with a sound like a knuckle — and lifted the lid.

Inside, packed in oiled paper, were documents. Juno, standing closest, could see them: heavy stock, printed borders, and at the head of each page, stamped in red-black ink, the crest she'd been seeing in her nightmares since the Narrows — the mountain and the crossed hammers. The ministry seal. The one Rafael had said shouldn't exist.

But it wasn't the crest that made Juno's mouth go dry. It was the words beneath it. She could read them from where she stood, upside down, in the neat bureaucratic print:

*PERMIT FOR BLASTING OPERATIONS — RIVER WORKS, CLASS ONE. AUTHORIZED: FORTY CHARGES, GELIGNITE, WET-HOLE RATED.*

Gelignite. Forty charges of it, authorized, ministerial, wet-hole rated — rated for use *underwater*. Juno had grown up around blasting. Her father had drilled road cuts for six years before the rock took two of his fingers, and she knew what a permit like that was worth, and she knew what it meant. It meant someone, somewhere, had signed papers authorizing the destruction of the river itself. And it meant Voss was carrying a stack of them, signed and sealed and blank in every space that mattered, like a book of tickets to anywhere.

Voss took out one permit, rolled it, and tied it with the cord from his own sleeve. Then he held it up.

"One of these," he called to Brannoch, "is worth more than your chain. One of these says that anything I do on this river, I do for the ministry, and anyone who interferes answers to the ministry. An inspector sees this in your strongbox, he'll tip his hat and write you down as a friend of the survey office. No more questions about your trestle. No more talk about the tolls being illegal — because they won't be illegal, they'll be *contracted*." He let the permit catch the gray light. "I'm offering to make you legitimate, Brannoch. For four hundred crowns' worth of legitimate."

The gorge was very quiet. Even the river seemed to have gone still against the chain.

"Show me the seal," Brannoch said finally.

Voss threw the roll. It arced up through the mist, and the crossbow man caught it against his chest without seeming to move, and passed it up, and for a long moment the bearded man read it, his lips moving, his thumb rubbing the crest over and over like a man checking a coin for gold.

Then he laughed. It rolled down the gorge, huge and easy, and the crossbow came down off its half-aim, and somewhere above them a block creaked and the chain began to rise.

"Voss," Brannoch called down, "you are the worst man I have ever done business with, and I include the man who sold me this bridge."

"High praise."

"Chain's up. Pass under slow — there's a snag off the east piling, moved in the spring flood. And Voss." The bearded man leaned out over the rail, and for just a moment the humor was gone from his face, and what was underneath it looked almost like pity. "Whatever you're doing up there at the ice — whatever it is you bought those papers for — the river keeps accounts too. Same as me. Only it doesn't take permits."

* * *

They passed under the trestle in a silence that had nothing to do with the river.

The gorge swallowed them — walls rising sheer on either side, dripping, green-black, the trestle overhead cutting the sky into a single gray stripe — and the Marrow slid through the shadow of it with her engine at a murmur, and nobody spoke. Juno stood at the bow and watched the chain come up out of the water ahead of them, link by dripping link, hauled skyward by whatever winch the toll keepers used, and she thought: *that chain was down because of us. That chain comes up because of a piece of paper. And the piece of paper is a lie, and the lie has a seal on it, and the seal is real.*

That was the part that kept catching in her, like a burr in a sock. The seal was real. The permit was real. Somewhere, in some office, some clerk with good handwriting had stamped that document, and either that clerk had been deceived, or the clerk was part of it, or — and this was the thought that made her grip the rail — the whole thing was exactly what it looked like, and they were all of them, every soul aboard the Marrow, sailing up this river under the protection of a ministry that had authorized them to blow holes in it.

She heard boots behind her and didn't turn. She knew the weight of them. Voss.

"You handled that well," he said. Not to her — past her, to Rafael, who had come up on her left.

"Don't," Rafael said.

"Captain—"

"I said *don't*." Rafael's voice was low and very even, the way it got when it was most dangerous. "You put that crate on my deck and you never told me what was in it. You let me sign on six people to run a river under a forged—"

"The seal isn't forged."

"That's worse." Rafael turned then, and Juno saw his face, and it was worse than anger. It was tired. "If it were forged, you'd be a criminal. Since it's real, you're something with friends in high places, and I don't know which frightens me more."

"The permit is real because the work is real," Voss said. "There are channels up there in the ice that need opening, Captain. That's the survey office's story and it's even true, in its way. The permits cover us. That's all they're for. Protection."

"Protection." Rafael looked at him for a long moment. "From whom?"

And Voss — Juno was watching him, she was the only one watching him, because Rafael was watching Rafael's own hands and Isela had gone very still by the wheel — Voss hesitated. Just a beat. Just long enough.

"From whoever's following us," he said.

The words went into the quiet of the gorge and stayed there. Juno thought of the black skiff in the fog. She thought of the way Brannoch's man had caught the permit roll without looking. She thought: *he didn't hesitate because he didn't know the answer. He hesitated because he has three answers and doesn't know which one to give us.*

Rafael turned back to the wheel. "Juno," he said, and his voice was ordinary again, captain's voice, and somehow that was the most frightening thing yet. "Take the bow watch through the gorge. Watch for that snag off the east piling."

"Aye, Captain."

"And Juno." He didn't look at her. "Whatever you saw in that crate — it stays on this boat."

"Aye, Captain," she said again, and meant it, and hated that she meant it.

* * *

The gorge let them out an hour later into a country that didn't look like the same river at all.

The water up here ran colder — you could feel it through the deck plates, a faint chill rising like the memory of winter — and it ran a different color too, a pale glacial green, the green of bottle glass, of eyes in certain lights. The spruce gave way to birch, the birch to alder scrub, and the mountains came down closer to the banks, their upper slopes white now, genuinely white, the first snow of the season sitting on them like a held breath. And far, far upstream, where the river narrowed to a thread between two gray shoulders of stone, there was a brightness that wasn't sky.

Ice. The glacier's tongue, just visible, like a door left ajar.

Juno stood her watch and did her job — called the water, called the snags, kept her eyes moving the way Isela had taught her — but underneath the job, underneath the cold tea and the bow rail and the long gray afternoon, something had cracked, and she could feel the cold getting in.

She had come aboard the Marrow because it was the cleanest thing in her life. That was the truth of it, the truth she'd never said out loud, not even to herself in the dark of the forepeak. The delta had been debt and rot and her mother coughing in a room that smelled of pitch, and the Marrow had been a boat with an honest captain and a first mate who taught you to read water and a crew that ate together and a name — *Marrow* — that sounded like something that could be trusted with the inside of you. She had thought the worst thing she'd ever have to do on this boat was lie to a harbor clerk about cargo weight.

Now she stood on the deck of a boat running under ministry blasting permits toward a glacier, paid for by a man who lied the way other men breathed, captained by a man who was running from something so hard he'd paid nine years of hush money to a harbor clerk to keep it behind him — because she'd figured that out too, weeks ago, the installments, the ledger, the way Rafael went gray at the sight of the debt collector's office — and the chain had come up for them not because they were honest but because they could afford to look like the kind of people who break rivers for a living.

*The river keeps accounts too,* Brannoch had said. *Only it doesn't take permits.*

Juno looked at the ice-bright thread of the river ahead, and at the green water sliding cold under the hull, and she made herself a promise, small and private and scared: *I'll find out what we're carrying. All of it. Before we get there.* Because her father had a saying about blasting, one he'd said with his two-fingered hand: *you can pour the charge and tamp the hole and run the wire, but the rock decides when it goes.* And Juno was beginning to understand that the Marrow was tamped full of charges — the crates, the permits, the lies, the name Rafael had buried, the thing Voss wanted at the ice so badly he'd buy a toll bridge with a signature — and that somewhere ahead, the rock was going to decide.

The first groan came at dusk.

It rolled down the river out of the north, a long, deep, grinding moan, so low she felt it in her sternum before she heard it, and it went on and on, the ice speaking in a language older than tolls or ministries or any of them. The crew came up on deck one by one. Nobody spoke. Even Voss stood at the rail with his head slightly bowed, like a man in a church he'd been excommunicated from.

Rafael stood last, at the wheel, his hands still, listening. And Juno, watching him, saw something she would remember all her life: he wasn't afraid of the sound.

He was listening for it. Like a man hearing a voice he'd spent nine years pretending was silent.

The groan faded. The river ran on, cold and green, toward the ice. And Juno turned back to her watch with the crack in her innocence widening, one thought keeping time with the engine's slow beat: *whatever's waiting up there, it's already awake — and we're carrying the match.*
