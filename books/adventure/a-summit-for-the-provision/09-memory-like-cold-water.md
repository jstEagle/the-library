## 9. Memory Like Cold Water

The dream always began with the sound.

Not the crack of ice — that came later, that was the punctuation — but the sound before it: the wet, patient creak of the river working at its own bones. In the dream Rafael stood on white ice that stretched away in every direction, and the Kestrel beneath him was not water but a living thing, a long muscle flexing in its sleep, and he could feel it through the soles of his boots the way you feel a heartbeat through a floor.

*Tomas,* someone said, or he said it, or the river said it. *Tomas is out there.*

The ice was the color of old teeth. Smoke hung over it in ropes. Somewhere behind him there was shouting — his own voice, he would understand later, or half-understand, in the way you understand a language you spoke as a child — and somewhere ahead, far out on the white, a small dark figure in an orange oilskin that the wind kept trying to peel off him.

*Tomas.*

He ran. The ice sang under him, a high thin keening like a wire drawn tight, and he knew — the way you know things in dreams, completely and uselessly — that the singing was the sound of the ice deciding whether to hold him or not. It was deciding about Tomas too. It was deciding about everything.

The rope came out of nowhere. That was the cruelty of the dream, that the rope was always a surprise, though his hands knew its weight, knew the waxy bite of the hemp, knew the particular way a rescue line coils when it's been coiled a hundred times by the same hands. He threw it. He watched it unspool across the white like a sentence being written, and Tomas caught it — Tomas always caught it, Tomas had been a river man from the day he could walk, their mother used to say he'd learned to swim before he'd learned to fear — and Tomas began to come toward him across the failing ice, hand over hand, and Rafael leaned back against the pull and dug his heels in and the whole world was the rope and the weight at the end of it and the singing of the ice.

*Come on,* he heard himself say. *Come on, little brother. Come home.*

And then the rope went slack.

Not snapped — slack. Cut. He would see the ends in his mind for nine years: not frayed, not torn, not parted under strain the way honest rope dies, but cut, sheared through in a single clean stroke as if by a knife or a hatchet or a blade drawn deliberately, patiently, by someone standing close enough to touch him. The two halves of the line lay on the ice like a severed thing, and beyond them Tomas stood for one impossible moment on a plate of ice no bigger than a door, and he looked at Rafael — not at the rope, at Rafael — and his face in the dream was not afraid. It was puzzled. It was the face of a man doing arithmetic that won't come out.

Then the ice said its one word, and the river took him, and Rafael was running on white that opened behind his heels like a mouth, diving, hands first into water so cold it was not cold but hot, a scalding, a brand, and his hands — his hands were bleeding, he could see them in the green dark, ribboning red into the current, sliced open on the ice as he'd clawed at the hole, at the edges, at the place where his brother had stopped being in the world —

*Tomas.*

Nothing answered. The river never answered. That was the thing about the Kestrel that he had learned young and forgotten and learned again: it takes and it does not explain. It keeps its ledger in silence.

He was clawing at the ice edge. He was nine years old. He was twenty-six. He was on the deck of the *Marrow* and the deck was tilting and Isela was saying his name, except in the dream she was saying it from very far away, from the far side of the ice, from the far side of nine years, and her voice came down through the water like light —

*Captain. Captain. Rafael.*

* * *

He came up out of the dream the way a drowning man comes up — all at once, violently, with his hands already moving, already clawing, and they closed on something warm and solid and alive, and for one blind second he gripped it with all the strength the nightmare had left in him.

It was Isela's wrist.

He let go as if her skin had burned him. They were in the great cabin of the *Marrow*, moored in the lee of a gravel bar two days above the uncharted village, and the lamp had been turned low, and she was sitting in the chair beside his bunk with her knees drawn up and her arms wrapped around them, and she had been there long enough for the lamp to burn down that far. Long enough that she'd heard everything. Long enough that she'd heard whatever his mouth had done while his mind was under the ice.

He was soaked in sweat. The blanket was on the floor. His hands ached — actually ached, the palms of them, in a way that had nothing to do with rope work, and he made himself not look at them, made himself not turn them over in the lamplight to see whether the old white scars had opened, which was impossible, which was madness, and which he believed completely for the length of three heartbeats.

"You were calling him," Isela said.

The river talked against the hull. Somewhere out on the bar a night bird made its one dry note. He lay with his chest heaving and said nothing, because there was nothing in his mouth that wasn't either a lie or a wound.

"Tomas," she said. Not a question. She'd heard the name enough times now — from the elder in the village, from the way Voss's face had moved when the name passed near him, from the way Rafael himself went quiet as a closed door whenever anyone came close to it. "You were calling Tomas."

"It's an old dream." His voice came out gravelled, wrecked, a stranger's voice. "It's nothing."

"Rafael." She said his name the way she said it on deck — not softly, never softly, Isela did not have softness so much as precision, and her precision was its own kind of tenderness. "You were bleeding. You had your hands up like —" She stopped. She looked at his hands, lying open on the blanket, and he watched her decide not to say what she'd seen.

He sat up. The cabin swam and settled. The lamp flame leaned and righted itself, and in its light Isela's face was very steady and very tired and very much awake at an hour when no first mate has any honest business being awake in her captain's cabin, and he understood that she had been sitting there watching him drown for a long time, and that she would have sat there until morning, and that she would never, ever mention it to Juno or to anyone, because that was who Isela was: a keeper of things. A keeper of ledgers, he thought, and didn't know why the phrase came, and where it came from, and what it was she might be keeping in hers.

"How long," he said.

"Since the second watch." She unfolded herself from the chair and poured water from the jug into the tin cup and put it in his hands, wrapping her fingers around his for a moment to make sure he'd hold it, and her hands were warm and rope-hard and he wanted to say something with them, something true, and what came out instead was:

"You should be sleeping. We run the Narrows tomorrow."

"The Narrows will keep." She sat down on the edge of the bunk, not touching him, close enough that he could feel the warmth of her through the blanket. "The dream won't. It's been getting worse since the village. You think I haven't noticed. You steer like a man reading a chart that isn't there, Rafael. You stand at the wheel at night when it's my watch. You knew that bar was going to shoal out before the leadsman called it — you knew, and you didn't know how you knew, and it scared you. I saw it scare you."

He drank the water. It was the coldest thing in the world, and it tasted like the river, and for a moment he was nine years under it again, green dark and red ribboning, and he made himself stay in the cabin, in the lamplight, in the warmth of her nearness, because the other thing — the going under — was right there, patient as the river, waiting for him to relax his grip.

"Isela." He set the cup down. His hands were shaking and he let them be seen shaking, which was the hardest thing he had done since the dock at the delta, since Voss had stepped off that gangplank with nine years folded up in his coat. "If I told you something. If I told you something and it wasn't — if it turned out I didn't know what I was telling you. If it was just pieces. Broken pieces that don't make a picture."

"Then I'd hold the pieces," she said. "That's what I'm for."

And God, the almost. It rose in him like water in a hold. He almost told her everything, right there, in the low lamplight with the river talking against the hull: the fire and the shouting, the ice the color of old teeth, the rope lying cut on the white, his own hands ribboning red, the nine years of a debt paid in installments to a harbor clerk whose name he had never once asked because asking would have meant knowing what it purchased. He got as far as opening his mouth. He got as far as the first breath of it.

And then, from up forward, faint through the bulkhead, came the sound of Juno's voice — she was on first watch, talking to herself the way she did, running the channel aloud, *and round the gravel to the far side, mind the snag* — and the ordinary sound of it, the dear ordinary sound of his ship being kept by his crew, broke the moment the way a branch breaks a current. Because it wasn't only his to tell. That was the thought that closed the door, that had always closed the door: whatever had happened on the ice nine years ago, it had not happened to him alone. There had been a crew. There had been names. And if the pieces were broken, if his memory was a chart with whole reaches inked out, then anything he told Isela would be a guess wearing the clothes of a fact, and she would carry it, and she would act on it, and if he was wrong —

"I can't," he said. "Not yet. Not until I —"

"You don't have to explain the not." Isela stood. She picked up the blanket from the floor and shook it out and laid it back over him with the brisk, unceremonious care of a woman making up a bunk, which was its own language, and he understood it. "But I'm going to say one thing, Captain, and then I'm going to my own bunk and you're going to sleep or pretend to, and tomorrow we run the Narrows and nobody mentions any of this on deck. Here it is." She paused at the lamp, her hand on the wick key. "Whatever you drowned in — it wasn't just water. And Voss knows what it was. I've watched him watch you. He's not here for cargo, Rafael, whatever's in those sealed crates. He's here for *you*. And a man doesn't spend triple pay and nine years to find one specific river pilot for anything good."

The lamp went out. Her footsteps went up the companionway, unhurried, and the hatch closed soft behind her, and Rafael lay in the dark with his heart going like a lead line hitting bottom.

He raised his hands in the blackness and turned them over. He couldn't see the scars. He didn't need to. He had carried them so long they had become part of the map of him, two pale seams across each palm, and he had told himself for nine years that they came from ordinary work, ordinary rope, ordinary rivers — and the dream had torn that story open again tonight the way it did every few months, and tonight, for the first time, the story would not close back up. The edges stayed parted. He lay there and felt the cold coming off them, off his own hands, as if they remembered water his mind refused to.

*A rope cut clean.* Where had that thought come from, tonight, in the dream, with such certainty? Frayed rope was the river's work. Cut rope was a person's.

He did not sleep. Toward the gray end of the night he rose, quietly, and went up into the damp cold of the deck, and stood at the wheel though it was not his watch, and Isela came up an hour later and saw him there and, true to her word, said nothing at all, only took the mate's side and began running the channel aloud in a low voice for Juno's benefit — *and round the gravel to the far side, mind the snag* — while the fog came down the Narrows ahead of them like a held breath.

It was Juno, high in the bow with the leads, who called it first — a wrongness in the sound of the water, a hollowness under the hiss — and it was Isela who went aft to check the steering gear as a matter of routine, and it was Isela's voice, one octave flat and very quiet, that came back along the deck and put an end to the night's last ordinary moment:

"Captain. You'll want to see the rudder pins."

* * *

Rafael knelt in the stern lazarette with the lamp held low and looked at what she was looking at, and felt the dream's cold climb out of his hands and into his spine.

The rudder pins had not worn. They had not worked loose. Someone had backed them off — backed them off carefully, a few threads at a time, with a tool, in the dark, on a crew of four — so that they would hold through a calm night and let go precisely in the worst water on the river.

He looked up at Isela, and she looked down at him, and above them both, somewhere in the fog, Voss stood at the rail with his hands folded, watching the water, and Rafael understood that the river was not the only thing aboard the *Marrow* that kept its ledger in silence.
