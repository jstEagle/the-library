## 10. Sabotage at the Narrows

Sable Voss did not sleep well in the best of places, and the abandoned waystation at the head of the Narrows was not the best of places. It was a rotting crib of timber and moss, its roof sagging like a tired shoulder, its single stove breathing smoke through every seam. He lay in the loft with his coat for a blanket and listened to the river working below — that low, patient grinding of water against stone that never stopped, never slept, never forgave. Nine years since he'd heard it this far up. It sounded exactly the same. That was the cruelty of rivers. They kept nothing, so they lost nothing, and everything a man wanted to believe he'd buried was still down there, turning over and over in the dark like a stone in a current.

He had taken the loft because it gave him sightlines. Old habit. He could see the deck of the *Marrow* through the warped window, the lantern at the mast dimmed to a coal, the shapes of the crew below — Ishikawa in the wheelhouse, upright even in sleep, the man slept like a sentry; Isela curled on the cargo hatch with a blanket over her boots; the girl Juno in the lee of the pilothouse wall, her hat over her eyes, her hand even now resting on the coiled line beside her like a child holding a toy it refused to put down.

Four of them. Four, plus himself, and one of them had spent the evening on their knees at the stern with a wrench.

He had watched it happen and done nothing. That was the part that sat in his chest now like a swallowed hook. He'd seen the deckhand — no. He hadn't seen. That was the lie he kept reaching for, and the lie kept slipping. He had seen a shape. A shape at the rudder housing in the last grey hour before full dark, when the fog came off the water in sheets and the lantern light couldn't reach past the second cleat. A shape, and the small, intimate sound of metal turning metal, patient as a beetle boring into wood. And he had stood in the shadow of the waystation wall with his hand on his knife and his breath held, and he had let it happen.

Because he needed to know who it was. And because, in some cellar of himself he did not care to light, he had thought: *let the river test them. Let the river tell me the truth faster than I can.*

That was the kind of thought that used to get crews killed. He knew that better than anyone breathing.

He rolled onto his back and looked at the underside of the roof, where the rafters were furred with frost. The stove ticked. Somewhere below, in the black throat of the Narrows, a boulder the size of a chapel shifted under the current and made a sound like a door closing.

*You put Tomas on the ice.* The thought came the way it always came, at night, uninvited, wearing Rafael's voice. *You stood on the bank and you watched the rope and you did nothing fast enough.*

He closed his eyes. Nine years of practice closing them against that.

* * *

Dawn came up grey and sour, the fog so thick that the far bank was a rumor. Voss was on deck before anyone else, which was his custom and his camouflage — a man who looked busiest was a man nobody watched. He made coffee on the deck stove, bitter and scorched, and carried a cup up to the wheelhouse without being asked, because that too was camouflage, and because Rafael Ishikawa's face in the early morning was a thing Voss had never learned to look at without something turning over in him.

"Water's at four and a half feet on the gauge stone," Rafael said, taking the cup without a word of thanks, because thanks between the two of them had died somewhere upstream of the disaster and neither had held a funeral. "Fog'll burn off by midmorning. We run the Narrows at midmorning or we don't run them."

"Your river," Voss said.

"My river," Rafael agreed, and there was something in the flat way he said it — not pride, not quite, something more like a scar being touched — that made Voss look away and study the fog.

He had hired this man for exactly that flatness. That was the truth he'd told the syndicate's paymaster and the truth he'd told himself: Ishikawa was the only pilot alive who knew the channel through the calving fields, the only one who'd run the Kestrel to its source and come back with his memory in pieces, and a man with holes in his head didn't second-guess the channels that remained. That was the truth. It was also, Voss knew now, standing in the fog with his coffee going cold, a smaller truth than the one underneath it. The one underneath was simpler and worse: he had wanted to stand on the same deck as Rafael Ishikawa again. He had wanted the man who knew what he'd done to be in the same room when the river finally asked him about it.

He had told himself it was about the cache. The cache was real — crates of ministry records, pay ledgers, sealed testimony, the whole rotten archive the syndicate had sunk at the source nine years ago when the investigation got too warm, believing the glacier would keep their secrets until the ice itself went to the sea. Voss had spent nine years learning where it lay. But a man could want two things, and only one of them was money.

"You're up early," Isela said from the deck below, and Voss turned to find her standing at the base of the wheelhouse stairs with her arms folded, watching him with those level dark eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. "Couldn't sleep?"

"River's loud."

"It's always loud."

"Not here." He made himself smile, the easy merchant's smile, the one that had opened a hundred locked harbors. "You get used to the delta. Down there the river whispers. Up here it argues."

Isela didn't smile back. She looked at him a moment longer than was comfortable, then went forward to rouse Juno, and Voss stood with his cold coffee and understood that the first mate was keeping a ledger on him. Good. Let her. A crew that watched its paymaster was a crew that might watch each other, and one of them needed watching.

* * *

They warped off the waystation bank at midmorning, when the fog had thinned to rags and the sun was a pale coin behind it, and Rafael took the *Marrow* into the Narrows with the wheel in his hands and his whole body gone quiet in that way Voss remembered too well — the pilot's quiet, the listening quiet, as if the man had grown extra ears in his skin.

The Narrows were a half mile of river squeezed between walls of granite, where the Kestrel, having wandered lazily for two hundred miles, suddenly remembered it was falling off the spine of the world. The water came through in braided tongues, white over black stone, and the channel — Rafael's channel, the one that existed in his hands and nowhere on any chart — threaded between two midriver fangs that the old pilots had named the Sisters because they took boats the way sisters took toys: without malice and without giving them back.

"Stand by your lines," Isela called. Juno was forward with the boat hook, small and fierce, her knuckles white on the shaft. Voss took the stern, because the stern was where the rudder lived, and because he wanted to be standing next to the sabotage when it spoke.

He heard it before he saw it. A sound from the rudder housing like a knuckle cracking — a small, loose, metallic sound that had no business being there — and then the wheel in the wheelhouse turned and the *Marrow* did not.

"Rudder's soft," Rafael said. Not shouted. Said, in that terrible level voice, the way a man says *the floor is gone* while already reaching for the next handhold. "Voss. The pins. Check the pins."

Voss dropped to his knees at the housing and looked, and there it was, plain as a confession: the retaining nuts on the rudder pins backed off — not stripped, not sheared, not rusted through, but *backed off*, three and four turns, the washers loose as buttons, the pins riding in their sockets with a play in them like a tooth about to come out. A few more minutes of white water and they'd have walked. The rudder would have dropped its bite and the *Marrow* would have gone into the Sisters as a cork goes over a weir.

He could have said *worn*. He could have said *vibration, old metal, nobody's fault.* The words were right there. They were easy words and they would have bought them all an hour of peace.

"Backed off," Voss said instead, loud enough to carry to the wheelhouse. "Someone backed them off. Hand me the wrench."

The fog of the morning seemed to come down into the boat with them then. He felt the crew's silence change shape — Juno's head snapping around, Isela's eyes finding him over the cargo, and above all Rafael's stillness at the wheel, that stillness that was worse than any shouting.

"Tighten them," Rafael said. "Talk after."

There was no after, not really, not for the next six minutes, because the Narrows took them. Voss worked with the wrench in the spray and the thunder, the pins biting home one turn at a time while the *Marrow* bucked under him like a living thing, and the first Sister came up off the port bow — a fang of wet granite with the river peeling around it in a white shoulder — and Rafael did the thing that had made his name on this river nine years ago and had never left him, not even the night that took everything else: he leaned into the wheel and *read* the water. Voss, flat on the deck with his arms around the rudder housing, watched the pilot's shoulders and steered by them, and the *Marrow* slid past the first Sister with a hand's width of black rock to spare, and the second Sister rose and the river stood up on either side of the hull like walls, and Juno was suddenly beside him, small hands adding their strength to the wrench, her face sprayed white with foam and set like stone, and the last pin came home with two turns to spare as the stern swung and the rudder *caught* — caught like a hand catching a falling child — and Rafael laid the boat into the slack water below the Narrows as gently as a man laying down a sleeping animal.

The silence afterward was enormous.

* * *

Voss called the meeting himself, in the pilothouse, with the door shut and the *Marrow* tied to a seam of calm water below the rapids. He did it because a man who called the meeting controlled the shape of it, and because he had spent the last hour watching the four of them look at one another and had seen the poison start to work — the sidelong glances, the hands moving to check gear that had been checked an hour ago. A crew that suspected itself was a crew that would come apart at the next rapid, and the calving fields were ahead, and he needed them whole.

"One of you loosened those pins," he said, without preamble. "I'm not going to pretend I don't know it. I'm going to ask who, and why, and I'm going to ask once."

"Or what?" Juno said. She was the youngest and the angriest, her hair still damp with spray, her fists on her hips. "You'll dock our pay? You'll throw us to the syndicate?"

The word landed in the room and lay there.

"Where'd you hear that word?" Voss said, very quietly.

"I hear things," Juno said, chin up, and Voss saw — with the cold, clear sight that paranoia gives a man, that terrible gift — the flicker of her eyes toward the stern, toward the rudder housing, toward the dark hours before dawn. Not guilt. Fear. She was afraid of something, and it wasn't him, and it wasn't the river.

"Nobody's throwing anyone anywhere," Isela said, stepping between them, her voice the level one, the one that kept crews alive. "But somebody on this boat turned those pins, and they did it with skill and patience, and they did it so the failure would happen in the worst water on the river. That's not sabotage of a boat. That's sabotage of *us*. So before we go one more mile, we settle it. Voss. You hired us all. You tell me — who knew our route? Who knew we'd run the Narrows today?"

Voss looked at her and felt the trap close around him from the inside. Because the answer was: everyone on this deck, and a dozen people on the delta, and at least three men in the pay of the Ferrymen's Compact — the rival syndicate, the one that had been bleeding his employers' routes for two years — and he could not say any of that without explaining why a legitimate survey contract needed secrecy from a rival, and he could not explain that without explaining the crates, and he could not explain the crates without lying, and he was so tired of lying that for one naked moment the truth stood right behind his teeth like a man at a door.

"The route was quiet," he said. "I kept it quiet because quiet is worth money on this river. That's all any of you need to know today."

"Today," Isela repeated.

"Today."

Rafael had not spoken through any of it. He stood at the wheel with his back to them all, looking upriver at the fog that was already closing behind them, sealing the Narrows away, and when he finally turned his face was the old flat mask, and his eyes were the eyes of a man reading water that hadn't arrived yet.

"Juno, check the steering lines. Isela, log the damage. Voss —" he paused, and something moved behind the flatness, something old and cold, "— you and I will talk before we make the next bend. Not about pins."

* * *

That night, anchored in a backwater that smelled of pine and cold stone, Voss sat alone on the cargo hatch with the crates around him and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the delta.

Someone aboard was the Compact's. Or someone ashore had been paid to reach the boat at the waystation — but the waystation had been empty, he'd walked it, he'd *walked it*, and the only tracks on the bank besides their own had been a heron's and his own returning boots. Which left the boat. Which left four people, one of whom had knelt in the dark with a wrench, and three of whom had alibis made of nothing but his own need to believe them.

And underneath that arithmetic, as it always was, as it had been for nine years, the other sum. The one that never balanced.

Tomas Ishikawa, twenty-two years old, laughing on the ice with the rope coiled over his shoulder, saying *I'll take the far anchor, it's faster from my side* — and Voss, on the bank, holding the near end, watching the rope run through his gloves, watching the ice go grey under the young man's boots, watching the crack travel like a sentence being written, and reaching, and the rope — the rope had been cut. Clean. He had told the inquiry it frayed. He had told Rafael it frayed. He had told himself, for nine years, in the dark, in the cellar where he kept the things that didn't balance, that he did not know what a clean cut looked like.

He knew. He had always known. A frayed rope tells you about the river. A cut rope tells you about a man.

And the man it told him about might have been himself — might have been his own frozen hands, his own failure to reach, the knife at his belt that he'd used to cut Tomas free of the tangle after, after, after — or might have been the fourth member of that crew, the one who took the syndicate's money that same winter and was never seen on the Kestrel again, the one whose name Voss had spent nine years and a small fortune trying to find, because the cut rope was the one question the river had never answered and Voss had built his whole crooked life on the promise that someday, somewhere, it would.

He looked up. Rafael was at the wheelhouse rail in the last light, alone, looking north toward the calving fields, and the distance between them — six feet of deck, nine years of silence — had never seemed so small or so impassable.

Below, in the black water, something knocked against the hull. Once. Twice. Then the current took it, whatever it was, downstream, and Voss sat very still in the dark and listened to the river carry it away, and wondered, with a certainty that felt like ice forming, what else was following them up.
