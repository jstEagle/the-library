## 11. Halfway House

The waystation announced itself the way old grief does — gradually, then all at once. First a suggestion of timber through the fog, a gray vertical line that might have been a dead tree. Then the roofline, sagging like a tired shoulder. Then the whole of it: a two-story building of blackened spruce perched on a stone shelf above the river, its gallery rail draped in moss, its windows either boarded or blind with grime.

"Halfway House," Voss said from the bow, and there was something in her voice Isela hadn't heard before — not nostalgia exactly, but the careful flatness of someone naming a grave. "Halfway between the delta and the source. Crews used to winter here when the ice came early."

"Crews," Juno repeated, tying off the bow line to a rotted bollard. "Which crews?"

"The kind that don't anymore." Voss stepped onto the shelf, boots crunching on frost-killed sedge, and for a moment she stood looking up at the building as if it were looking back at her.

Isela watched her from the wheelhouse window. Nine days on the river now, and Isela had filled three pages of her ledger with small entries: *Voss handles a rope left-handed until she thinks no one's watching, then right-handed. Voss knew about the shallows below the Toll Bridge before Rafael called them. Voss flinched — actually flinched — when Juno asked what happened to the last crew that ran this water.* The entries had no conclusions yet. They were just weights on one side of a scale, and Isela was waiting to see which way it tipped.

Rafael brought the Marrow in himself, threading the channel with the unhurried precision that never stopped astonishing her — reading the water the way other men read faces. He hadn't spoken more than twenty words all morning. Since the nightmare four nights ago, since she'd sat on the edge of his bunk while he shook and said his brother's name into the dark, he'd been carrying something visibly heavier, and he carried it the way he carried everything: silently, close to the body, where no one could take it from him without taking him apart.

"We'll make camp ashore," he said now, killing the engine. The silence afterward rang. "The Marrow needs her seams looked at anyway. Juno, you're with me on the hull. Isela—" He paused, and she saw him choose the chore the way a man chooses which hand to offer. "See if there's anything dry in the station. Firewood, if we're lucky. Voss, you're with her."

*With her.* Not *with me*. Isela filed that away too.

* * *

Inside, the waystation smelled of mouse droppings and cold pitch and, beneath it, faintly, impossibly, woodsmoke — old smoke soaked so deep into the beams that nine years hadn't rinsed it out. Their lantern light moved across a long room: a stone hearth choked with ash, a table scarred by knives and heat rings, bunks along the far wall with blankets still folded where their owners had left them, as though the owners had only stepped out to check the lines and would be back any minute.

"They left in a hurry," Isela said.

"They left in the night," Voss corrected quietly. She ran her palm along the table's edge, gathering dust, and looked at it the way people look at ash. "After the disaster, nobody wanted to sleep upstream of what happened. This place emptied out. The charts dropped it because the chartmakers dropped everything they couldn't sell."

Isela set her lantern on the table and went to work, because work was how she kept her hands honest. She pried boards from a storage closet — nothing but rusted traps and a coil of tarred twine gone brittle. She checked the hearth for usable fuel and found birch logs stacked in the woodshed out back, still sound under their weathering. She was dragging the third armload inside when she noticed the wall.

It was behind the bunks, an interior partition of rough planking, and one plank sat proud of its neighbors — not warped, not loose from settling, but *proud*, the way a board sits when something behind it is pressing. Frost had gotten into the gap and swelled the wood, or time had, or the house itself had finally grown tired of keeping secrets. Isela ran her thumb along the edge. The plank rocked, very slightly, like a tooth.

She should have called Voss over. That was the sensible thing, the crew thing. Instead she glanced across the room — Voss had drifted to the window and stood with her back turned, watching the river, watching Rafael and Juno working the hull below — and Isela worked the plank free with the flat of her knife.

Behind it: a cavity the depth of her forearm, lined with oilcloth gone stiff as bark. And inside the oilcloth, a book.

Not a logbook. Logbooks were square, official, stamped. This was a private journal, cheap paperboard covers soft with damp, tied shut with a leather lace gone green. On the cover, in pencil, in handwriting Isela had seen a hundred times on the Marrow's cargo manifests and supply lists — blocky, patient, slightly left-leaning — was a single word:

*TOMAS.*

Her breath went out of her slowly, like air from a punctured hull.

She untied the lace with fingers that did not feel like her own. The pages were foxed and wavy but legible, the ink faded to brown, the pencil holding truer than the pen. She carried the journal to the table, set it beside her lantern, and told herself she would read only enough to know what it was. Whether it was worth anything. Whether it was dangerous.

She read the first page standing up. She read the next forty sitting down.

* * *

Tomas Ishikawa wrote the way he must have talked — plainly, warmly, with jokes in the margins and little sketches of knots and birds in the corners. The earliest entries were ordinary river life: fuel costs, a card game Juno's name appeared in (she'd have been a child then; Isela made a note to ask), a complaint about the coffee at Halfway House that made Isela laugh out loud alone in a dead man's room, and then stop laughing.

Then, seven pages in, the tone changed.

*V has a plan,* Tomas wrote. *A real one this time, not bar talk. There's a cache at the source — pre-Confederation ministry stores, sealed when the survey teams pulled out. Provisions, equipment, records. Records is the word she keeps using. She says whoever holds those records holds the syndicate's throat. She says we could be rich or we could be righteous, our choice.*

*I told Rafa. He laughed. Then he didn't laugh. He said the calving fields can't be run in a boat the size of ours and V said that's why we'd go before the season, thread the lake while the ice is still holding. He said no. I said maybe. That's where we are tonight. He said no and I said maybe, and V bought the second round.*

Isela turned pages with increasing care, as though the paper might bruise.

*Went over the charts with V again. She's got survey marks nobody should have — ministry marks, from before. She won't say how she got them. I'm starting to think there are two plans: the one she tells us and the one she tells herself.*

And then, near the end, the handwriting tightening, the sentences shortening, a man writing faster than he wanted to think:

*Rafa gave the order for the ice run. Not V — Rafa. He figured the load light and the ice thick enough. I keep thinking about that. He's the captain. It was his call. I agreed to it, which makes it mine too.*

*V cut the line.*

I read that sentence ten times. *V cut the line.* No context before, none after. Just that, alone on the page, underlined once, and then the final entry:

*Tomorrow we go up to the lake. If you're reading this, somebody tell my brother I wasn't scared. Tell him the ice was singing and I liked the song.*

The journal ended there. Forty-one pages of a living man, and then nothing, and the nothing pressed against Isela's chest like cold water.

*V cut the line.*

Cut it to do what? To save him? To drop him? The page didn't say, and Tomas never would, and somewhere downstream of that sentence Rafael had spent nine years drowning in the space between the words.

Isela sat in the abandoned waystation with her brother-in-everything-but-blood's dead brother's journal open in her lap, and understood that she was holding the answer to the question that had hollowed Rafael out — or half of it, or a fragment of it, or possibly a lie; she couldn't know which, not alone, not yet. *V cut the line* could mean murder. It could mean mercy. It could mean something she couldn't imagine. But it meant *something*, and it meant Voss had been at the center of it, and it meant Voss had walked onto their dock nine years later with triple pay and a smooth face and never once said *your brother wrote a journal and hid it in a wall and I may have known.*

Had she known? Isela looked across the room at Voss's silhouette against the window glass. Had Sable Voss known this book existed? Had she come back for it? Was that part of the plan too — the sealed crates, the false permit, the whole rigged voyage — a search for the one document that could hang her?

Or worse, gentler: had Voss hidden it herself, nine years ago, in this wall, unable to destroy it and unable to carry it?

Isela closed the journal. Tied the lace. Held it in both hands like something warm she was afraid to spill.

* * *

She didn't hide it again. That was the first decision, and it surprised her — she'd expected to hesitate longer. But she'd seen what secrets did to Rafael, how they grew teeth in the dark. She wrapped the journal in the oilcloth, tucked it inside her coat, and told herself the second decision could wait until morning.

It couldn't, of course. Decisions like this never waited; they just got heavier.

Supper was beans and smoked fish on the waystation's scarred table, the fire built from Tomas's own stacked birch, which Isela could not stop thinking about. Juno told a story about a sturgeon she'd supposedly wrestled at fourteen; even Rafael almost smiled. Voss ate little and watched Rafael more, and Isela watched Voss watching him, and the ledger in her head gained another entry: *She looks at him like a person looks at a debt. Not money. The other kind.*

Later, when Juno had rolled into her bedroll and Rafael stood outside on the gallery checking the night lines, Isela found Voss alone at the hearth, feeding the fire a stick at a time, slow, like a woman who needed her hands busy.

"The wall behind the bunks," Isela said. "Loose plank. Empty cavity behind it. Oilcloth lining."

Voss's hand stopped mid-motion. A single heartbeat of stillness — Isela counted it, she counted everything now — and then the stick went into the fire and Voss said, without turning, "Houses like this are full of cavities. People hid things during the syndicate wars. Coin. Papers. Sometimes people."

"Were you looking for something particular?"

"Why would I look for something in a wall I've never—"

"You wintered here." Isela kept her voice level, almost gentle. "You said so yourself. Crews used to winter here. You knew the roofline in the fog before any of us did. So I'll ask plainer. Did you know Tomas kept a journal?"

This time Voss did turn. The firelight did things to her face that Isela couldn't read and didn't trust. For a long moment the only sound was the river moving in the dark below, patient as a debt collector.

"If you found something," Voss said finally, "it belongs to Rafael. Whatever it says, whatever it doesn't say — it's his. Don't make it yours."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got that won't hurt anybody tonight." Voss stood, brushed off her knees, and paused at the doorway. Without looking back she added, "He's not ready. You know he's not ready. Some truths you have to carry to a man across calm water, Isela. Not in the rapids. Not now."

Then she was gone, up the stairs to the loft, and Isela sat alone by a fire built from a dead man's wood, holding a dead man's secret under her coat, having just been advised on timing by the woman the secret was about.

That was the thing about knowing, Isela thought. Everyone assumed knowledge was power. Nobody warned you it was ballast. It weighed nothing in your hands and everything in your chest, and every mile upstream it got heavier, and there was nowhere to set it down that didn't cost someone.

Through the window she could see Rafael on the gallery, lantern in hand, checking lines that didn't need checking — the way he did everything twice, the way he held the world at arm's length so it couldn't hurt him again. Tomorrow they'd cast off into the upper reaches, where the charts thinned to guesswork and the glacier's voice would start carrying downriver, and after that there would be no more waystations, no more walls with cavities, no more good places to stop and decide.

She took out the journal once more, opened it to the last written page, and read the line she'd already memorized: *Tell him the ice was singing and I liked the song.*

"Not yet," she whispered to Tomas, to Rafael, to the fire. "But soon. Before the source. I promise you both — before the source."

Above her, the loft floor creaked once, softly — Voss turning over, or listening, or both.

And far upriver, so distant it might have been thunder, the glacier cleared its throat.
