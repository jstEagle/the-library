## 12. The Debt Collector

The morning the skiff came, the river was the color of pewter and moving like it had somewhere to be.

I felt it before I saw it — a change in the water's grammar. Nine years on the Kestrel teaches you to read its moods the way a wife reads her husband's silences, and this morning the river was holding something back. The current ran fast and clean through the braided shallows, but beneath it there was a tremor, a vibration in the hull that had nothing to do with the engine. I stood at the wheel with my coffee going cold in my hand and watched the downstream haze, and I thought: *something is coming upriver against the current, and it is not tired.*

Nothing comes upstream without effort. That's the first law of the Kestrel. The river gives freely going down and takes everything going up, which is why the delta towns all face seaward, why the fishing fleets never bother with the upper reaches, why men like me settle at the bottom of a watershed and pretend we were always meant to be bottom-dwellers. Going upriver costs. So when I saw the wake — a long, disciplined V cutting against the grain, white as a scar — I knew whoever was making it had paid for the privilege.

"Captain." Juno's voice from the deck below, tight. "Boat astern. Been gaining on us since the narrows."

"I see it."

"It's not a fisherman."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

Isela came up the companionway two steps at a time, hair still damp from the morning wash, and stood beside me at the rail. She didn't ask what it was. She'd been keeping her own count of things wrong with this voyage — I could see the ledger behind her eyes every time she looked at Voss — and a black-hulled skiff running us down at forty miles above the last charted village fit neatly into her column.

Voss was already on deck. Of course he was. He'd slept in his clothes again, or maybe never slept at all; there was a quality to him lately, a wire-tautness, like a man listening to a sound no one else can hear. He raised a pair of brass binoculars to the skiff and lowered them slowly, and what crossed his face wasn't surprise.

It was recognition, and under the recognition, resignation.

"You know that boat," Isela said. It wasn't a question.

"I know the make." Voss set the binoculars down on the rail with exaggerated care, the way a man handles something when he doesn't trust his hands. "Kestrel Watercraft, Series Four. Fast, shallow draft, ugly as sin."

"That's not what she asked," I said.

He looked at me then, and for a moment — just a moment — I saw something in his face I hadn't seen in nine years, not since the night at the harbor office when he'd signed the papers that ended my old life and begun this one. It was pity. Pity, aimed at me, from Sable Voss, and I would rather have taken a hook through the hand.

"They'll want to talk," he said. "Let them talk."

* * *

They came alongside an hour later, where the river widened into a long brown lake between bluffs of grey shale. The Marrow was too heavy to outrun them and too deep-drafted to lose them in the shallows, and Voss didn't order evasive action, which told me everything before the skiff even touched our hull.

There were three of them aboard. The one who stepped across to our deck wore a plain oilskin the color of wet slate, unmarked, unadorned, the kind of coat bought by someone who never wanted to be described by what he wore. He was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, with the soft hands of a man who had never hauled a line in his life and the steady eyes of a man who had watched many other people haul lines for him. Behind him, two younger men stayed in the skiff, and they did nothing so obvious as touch their jackets, but they arranged themselves so that touching jackets would be easy.

"Captain Ishikawa." The man in the slate coat said my name the way a bank clerk reads a figure off a ledger — complete, final, correct. "My name is Corven. I'm here about your account."

"My account," I repeated.

"Your standing arrangement." He smiled without warmth, the smile of a man performing a social duty he found tedious. "You've been a reliable client for nine years. Regular payments, regular discretion. Frankly, you're one of our favorites. But your account has fallen into arrears."

Something cold moved through me, slow, like water finding a crack in a hull. "I paid the installment at Halloway's dock. Two weeks ago. The clerk confirmed—"

"The clerk confirmed receipt of the payment. Yes." Corven turned, unhurried, and looked out over the rail at the water sliding past, the shale bluffs, the thin white line of the glacier country far upstream, still invisible but present somehow in the light, in the cold coming down off the high country. "You misunderstand the nature of the arrangement, Captain. You always have, I think. Which is understandable. Most clients prefer not to understand what they're paying for. It makes the paying easier."

"Corven." Voss's voice, flat. "This is a chartered run. Ministry permits. Whatever ledger you think—"

"Ministry permits." Corven said the words like he was tasting spoiled milk. "Mr. Voss. We both know whose seal is on those papers, and we both know how much a dead ministry's stationery is worth. Please don't insult the crew. They seem like honest people. Well." A glance at Voss. "Most of them."

Juno had drifted closer along the rail, arms crossed, jaw set. Isela stood at my shoulder, close enough that I could feel her breathing. The engine idled beneath us, a heartbeat we were all pretending not to listen to.

"Say it plainly," I said to Corven. "What am I paying for?"

He turned back to me, and now the performance dropped away, and what was underneath was worse — patient, almost kind, the way a surgeon is kind.

"Silence, Captain. You are paying for silence. Nine years ago, a boat went up this river with eleven souls aboard. One boat came back with six. There was an inquiry — brief, because the syndicate's friends sat on the inquiry board — and it concluded that the loss of the *Pale Meridian* was attributable to ice conditions beyond the crew's control." He let the old name fall into the air between us, and I heard Isela's breath catch beside me. She'd never heard it. I'd made sure of that. "The inquiry asked certain questions of certain survivors. One survivor in particular. The helmsman. The brother of the drowned man. And that survivor, through his representative, purchased a settlement: his testimony would be limited, his memory would be — how shall we put it — unreliable. In exchange, the syndicate would ask nothing further, pursue no charges regarding certain irregularities in cargo manifests, and extend certain financial considerations to assist the survivor in establishing a new life. Downriver. Under a new name, if he liked."

The river kept moving. The engine kept idling. Somewhere above us a bird cried out, once, and was silent.

"I don't—" I started, and stopped, because my mouth had gone dry as ash.

"Every quarter," Corven went on, gently, horribly, "for nine years, you have walked into a harbor office and paid a clerk. And every quarter, that money has traveled up the chain to us. You believed you were servicing a debt. Captain, you *were* servicing a debt. Just not the one you imagined. You weren't borrowing money. You were renting your own silence. And Mr. Voss—" he inclined his head toward her, and there was something almost respectful in it, which made it filthier somehow, "—has been making the payments on your behalf whenever your purse ran short. Which, if the records are accurate, has been often. She's very loyal. Or very guilty. With clients of long standing, the two become indistinguishable."

I looked at Voss.

She didn't look away. That was the thing about Sable Voss — she never flinched, never softened, never gave you the mercy of a lie when the truth would cut deeper. Her face was pale and hard as the shale bluffs, and her eyes said: *yes. All of it. And I'd do it again.*

"You bought my memory," I said. My voice came out low and strange, like someone else's.

"I bought your safety," Voss said. "There's a difference."

"There were questions about that night," Corven said, "that the syndicate needed never answered in any forum. Questions about who ordered the crossing onto the ice shelf. Questions about the rope. If you had testified fully — if your memory had returned when the investigators pressed it — certain parties would have required a scapegoat, and the helmsman with the head wound and the dead brother was the most convenient article on the shelf. Your silence kept you free, Captain. And it kept the inquiry closed. And it kept—" he paused, delicately, "—certain other parties' involvement in the *Meridian*'s cargo buried with the boat. Everyone got what they needed. You got a riverboat and a quiet life. That's not nothing. Many men would call it a bargain."

"A bargain," Isela said. Her voice was quiet and it carried further than shouting. "Nine years. He paid every quarter. He counted coins out of a purse that couldn't cover the crew's boots, and you let him believe—"

"He preferred believing it," Corven said. "Belief is a service we provide. It's in the contract's spirit, if not the letter."

* * *

Afterward, I couldn't have said how long we stood there, the skiff pacing us, the bluffs sliding past, the glacier-light strengthening by degrees in the north. Time went strange, the way it does in cold water.

I thought about the harbor clerk at Halloway's dock — a soft-bellied man named Pell who always had tea going and always took my coin without counting it, because the amount never changed. Nine years of quarters. Nine years of walking home lighter by exactly the weight of my silence and calling it debt, calling it obligation, telling myself a man pays what he owes and asking no one what the principal had been. I had built a whole life on top of that payment schedule the way a shanty is built on pilings — and now Corven had shown me the waterline, and the waterline was so much lower than I'd ever let myself look.

Every contentment I owned had been mortgaged. The Marrow. The delta mornings. Isela's laugh in the galley. Juno learning knots on the foredeck. All of it floating on a silence I hadn't known I'd sold, purchased by the woman I hated most in the world, administered by men who filed my grief under *accounts receivable*.

And underneath that — colder, deeper, the thing I would carry longest — was the question Corven had planted with such surgical care: *questions about who ordered the crossing onto the ice shelf.* About the rope. About Tomas.

I remembered — I had always remembered, in the way you remember a room you grew up in, without knowing you remember it — bleeding hands. Rope. Ice going white and rotten underfoot. Tomas's face, young and laughing and then not laughing. And a hole where the middle of the night should have been, nine years wide and nine years deep, and I had called that hole my injury, my wound, my cross. It had never occurred to me that it might also be a purchase. That someone had looked at the ruin of my memory and seen not tragedy but *terms*.

"Turn back," Corven said.

We had moved to the rail, he and I, while Voss argued terms with his assistants in low voices that went nowhere. The skiff matched our speed ten feet off the starboard side, its engines purring, unhurried. Men who chase never look unhurried. Men who own the chase do.

"The source is closed," Corven said. "Calving season is early this year. The syndicate's interest in the provision cache predates all of you, and our claim will be honored whether you proceed or not. What varies is the cost. Turn back now, and your accounts are settled — all of them, Captain, including the ones you didn't know you held. You go back to your delta. You keep your boat, your crew, your quiet life. The silence stays purchased. Everyone walks away whole."

"And if we don't turn back?"

He looked at me for a long moment, and there was no cruelty in his face, which was the cruelest thing about it. Only arithmetic.

"Then the arrears come due," he said. "All of them. The old testimony. The reopened inquiry. The cargo manifests. The names of everyone who helped bury the *Meridian*, which includes a woman standing not four feet from you who has spent nine years paying for your silence out of guilt you can't imagine and wouldn't want to." He straightened his collar, a small precise gesture, a man closing a ledger. "Coin is the least of what can be forfeited, Captain Ishikawa. You of all people should understand that. You've been forfeiting things for years. You simply signed the receipts without reading them."

He stepped back across to the skiff. The gap widened. The slate-grey hull swung away downstream, riding the current it had fought so hard to climb, and within ten minutes the river had folded over its wake and there was nothing to show it had ever been there at all.

That's the Kestrel for you. It keeps what sinks and hides what passes.

* * *

We anchored that evening in the lee of a gravel bar, and nobody ate much.

Juno went forward and sat with the mooring lines, doing nothing, needing something to hold. Voss shut herself in the chartroom with the false permits and I heard paper tearing, slow and steady, a woman burning a bridge she'd already crossed. And Isela found me at the stern rail, where I'd gone to watch the last light die on the water, and stood beside me without speaking, because she knew — she always knew — that words would have to wait until I'd finished drowning.

"She bought it," I finally said. "Nine years. Every quarter. While I counted out coins like a penance, she was—" My throat closed. I made myself finish. "She let me believe I owed a debt. She *made* me owe it. And I built my whole life inside the cage and called it a river."

"Yes," Isela said. No comfort in it, no defense of Voss either. Just the truth, laid flat, because that was what she had decided to deal in somewhere back upriver, alone, in the dark, burning-paper nights I hadn't known about yet.

"Why?" I asked, and meant a hundred questions at once. Why buy my silence. Why hire me now. Why bring me back up this river, past every mile marker of the worst night of my life, unless—

Unless she needed me here. Unless there was something at the source that only I could reach, or only I could pay for.

Isela's hand found mine on the rail. Her fingers were cold from the evening air and they held on anyway.

"Rafael," she said quietly, "whatever she wanted you for — whatever this whole run is — you need to decide what *you're* going up this river for. Because after tonight, turning back won't buy you anything either. He told you that himself. The silence is already spent."

Ahead of us, far up the darkening channel, the sky held a faint bruised glow where the glacier country breathed its cold down onto the world. Closer than it had been yesterday. Closer every hour.

And beneath the idle of the engine, beneath the lap of water on the hull, I heard it — faint, rhythmic, patient — a sound like a great door being tested on its hinges, somewhere in the ice.

The calving season, coming early. Coming for all of us.

I turned the wheel mark over in my mind, and pointed the Marrow's bow upstream, into the dark.
