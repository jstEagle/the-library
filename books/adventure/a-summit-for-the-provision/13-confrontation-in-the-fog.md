## 13. Confrontation in the Fog

The fog came up the river at dusk, the way it always did in the narrows above the Toll Bridge — not drifting in but *rising*, as if the Kestrel itself were exhaling after a long day of carrying us. I had seen fog like this only once before, nine years ago, on a night I still couldn't fully see. It came off the water in slow gray tongues and swallowed the banks, the trees, the pale smear of the moon, until the world was the width of the Marrow and nothing else.

I should have anchored. Every instinct I owned said anchor, wait it out, let the river keep its secrets till morning. But the syndicate skiff had been behind us since the Debt Collector's warning — I could feel it the way you feel a hand on the back of your neck — and every hour we sat still was an hour it gained. So I stood at the wheel and ran on sound: the hiss of current against rock, the groan of the channel markers, Isela's voice from the bow calling depths in a low, steady rhythm that was the only thing keeping my heart at a walking pace.

"Twelve feet," she called. "Nine. Nine and a half. Rafael — there's a shape off the port side. Big."

"Bank," I said. "Fog lies about size."

"Fog isn't lying about this one."

I eased the wheel over. The Marrow answered with the sluggish grace of a loaded woman, and the shape slid past us — a drowned tree, maybe, or a boulder with ambitions. Behind us, somewhere in all that gray, the foghorn sounded again. Two long notes, low and deliberate. Not a navigation signal. A knock on a door.

Voss came up from below deck without a lamp, moving through the fog like she'd been manufactured from it. She had a way of appearing that I'd hated for twenty years — no footsteps, no warning, just presence, the way weather arrives. She stopped at the rail a yard from me and looked back the way we'd come, toward the sound.

"They're not trying to catch us tonight," she said.

"No?"

"No. They're letting us know they *can*."

"That's a lot of certainty for a woman who can't see her own hands."

"I paid that horn's crew for three years, Rafael. I know their habits." She turned her head. In the fog her face was half suggestion, half scar. "I know yours, too. That's why you're at that wheel."

The words went into me like a splinter, small and deep, and I kept steering because steering was the only honest thing left in my body. "Say what you mean."

"You know I mean." Her voice stayed level, which was its own kind of violence. "The calving fields ahead — the braided channels, the ice-choked narrows where the charts give up and the river makes up its own mind every morning. There are maybe four pilots alive who can read that water. Two of them are dead. One is Tomas." She let that sit between us, cold as river stone. "The last one is you. I didn't hire a crew, Rafael. I hired a memory I was hoping you'd lost."

The wheel was slick under my hands. I realized I was gripping it hard enough to hurt, and that the pain was the only thing keeping me from letting go of everything else.

"You bought my silence for nine years," I said. "Installments. Through a clerk. Through a *debt* I thought I owed. And the whole time, the money wasn't the point — the silence was. You kept me quiet and small and half-blind so that one day you could walk back into my life and spend me like a chart."

"Yes."

Just *yes*. No flinch, no ornament. I almost admired it. I almost broke her jaw for it. The two impulses arrived together and I chose the wheel again, because if I let go of it I was going to do something the fog would keep forever.

"Isela knows," I said. "About the journal. About the coordinates. She's been carrying it for weeks because she was afraid of what it would do to me, and that's a second thing you stole, Voss. You stole the truth between us."

"I stole nothing you weren't already hiding from yourself."

The foghorn sounded again — closer now, or the fog was thinner, or my ears had decided to betray me. And something in me went past anger into that flat, quiet country where my hands do things before I've agreed to them.

I let go of the wheel.

Isela's voice came sharp from the bow — "Rafael!" — and Juno was there in a heartbeat, hands on the spokes, holding our heading the way I'd taught her, God help her. And I crossed the deck and put both hands in Voss's collar and drove her back against the rail, and the fog closed around us like a room with no door.

"You want to talk about what I'm hiding from?" My voice came out lower than I meant it to, almost gentle, which frightened me more than shouting would have. "I have spent nine years not remembering the worst night of my life. I have paid for the not-remembering. I have built a whole quiet life on top of the hole, and you just told me you *dug the hole*. So before this river takes anything else from me, you are going to stand here and tell me what happened to my brother. All of it. No more installments."

She didn't struggle. That was the worst part — she let me hold her there, her back against the rail, the fog beading on both our faces, and she looked at me with something I refused to call pity.

"You'll hate me either way," she said. "That's the one thing I know about this river. It always makes you choose which hate you can live with."

"Tell me."

"The rope," she said. "That night. You remember the rope."

I did — a fragment, white and screaming: a rope, taut, singing under load. Tomas somewhere beyond it. My hands.

"I was there," Voss said. "I was the one holding the other end."

The river hissed against the hull. Isela had stopped calling depths. The whole crew, I think, had gone silent, listening through the deck.

"You're lying," I said.

"I've lied to you for nine years, Rafael. Let me have one true sentence." Her voice cracked, just once, just at the seam. "The rope was in my hands. What I did with it — that's yours to hate me for, and you will, and I deserve every hour of it. But I did not stand on that ice and watch him go under and feel *nothing*. I have felt nothing else for nine years. Every coin I paid you, I paid to both of us. To keep you from remembering, and to keep me from having to say it out loud."

My hands were shaking. I let go of her collar because holding on had started to feel like holding on to the night itself, and I wasn't ready for what was under it. I stepped back. The fog took the space between us and made it enormous.

"You could have told me," I said. "Any time. Any one of a thousand mornings at that harbor clerk's window."

"And you'd have remembered. All at once. On a dock, in daylight, with no river under you to hold you up." She straightened her collar with two fingers, unhurried, and that small vanity of hers was so absurdly human that it hurt. "I wanted you to remember here. On the water. Where he — " She stopped. "Where it happened. Because the river is the only witness either of us ever trusted."

"That's not mercy. That's choreography."

"Yes," she said again. "It's what I'm good at."

* * *

We ran on through the fog after that, and neither of us spoke, and the silence between us was worse than the fight because it was *full* — full of rope and ice and a name I hadn't let myself say aloud in nine years. Tomas. I said it once, inside my own skull, and it went through me like cold water through a cracked hull.

Juno held the wheel for an hour and never once asked what she'd walked into. When I took it back from her, she looked at my hands — they were still trembling — and then at my face, and she just nodded and went forward to spell Isela, and I understood that the girl was learning the other half of river work: when to be a witness and when to be a wall.

Isela came aft near midnight. She didn't touch me. She stood at the rail beside me, close enough that our sleeves touched, and we watched the fog eat our wake.

"You fought," she said. It wasn't a question. The whole river had heard it.

"He told me he was holding the rope."

"I know." A pause. "I found the journal, Rafael. Weeks ago. I've been — " Her breath went out slow. "I've been deciding. Every day, deciding. Whether knowing would drown you or save you."

"And?"

"And I still don't know. That's the truth. I love you enough to not pretend I had a plan." She turned her face to me, and in the fog her eyes were just two dark points of attention. "But I'm done deciding alone. Whatever's in that journal, whatever Voss is carrying — you get it when we anchor. Both of them. At once. I won't hand you the truth in installments. That's *her* currency, not mine."

I looked at her and felt something in my chest come loose — not relief, nothing so kind. More like a knot cut rather than untied. She had been carrying my worst night around in her pocket for weeks, weighing it every morning against my face at breakfast, and she had done it out of love, and love like that is heavy enough to bend a person, and she had bent and not broken.

"Isela," I said.

"Don't." Her voice went rough. "Not yet. Not in the fog. When you say what I think you're going to say, I want to be able to see your whole face, and I want the river to be quiet, and I want it to be *after* — after the journal, after the source, after whatever's coming up that river for us. I want it in daylight, Rafael. I've earned daylight."

The foghorn sounded again. Three notes this time, and closer, and wrong — the pitch of a horn I knew, from a boat I'd been shown in a collector's lantern. The syndicate wasn't following anymore. They were *escorting*.

Voss appeared at the rail again, and this time she had a lantern, and she held it up and answered the horn with two short blasts of our own — a signal I didn't know, in a code I'd never learned, and the answering horn went silent.

"What did you just tell them?" Isela asked.

"I told them we see them." Voss set the lantern down. Her face in its little circle of light was calm the way ice is calm. "And I told them we're not stopping. Which means, gentlemen and ladies of the syndicate, that everything from here to the calving fields happens fast, in the dark, in water only one man alive can read." She looked at me across the lantern light, and for one unguarded second I saw what was actually underneath all that composure — not greed, not ambition. Terror. Nine years of it, worn smooth. "So, Captain. You can hate me and put us ashore. You can hate me and run us aground on purpose. Or you can hate me and get us through — because I am the only reason they haven't boarded us tonight, and you are the only reason any of us sees morning on the other side of the ice."

The fog pressed in. The river ran. Somewhere behind us, oars or screws or something turned in the dark, patient as interest on a debt.

I put my hands on the wheel.

"Juno," I said. "Wake the engine room. Isela — forward, sound the channel. Voss." I looked at her, and I let her see that the hatred wasn't going anywhere, and neither was I. "You're going to stand next to me at this wheel and tell me everything you remember about that night, every word, all the way to the ice. You bought the silence. Now you can pay for the noise."

She held my eyes. "That's the fare?"

"That's the fare."

The Marrow leaned into the current, and the fog closed behind us like a door, and ahead — faint, felt more than heard, a vibration coming up through the hull and into my hands and my ribs and the oldest part of my memory — the glacier began to groan.
