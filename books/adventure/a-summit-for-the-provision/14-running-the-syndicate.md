## 14. Running the Syndicate

Juno heard the engine before anyone else did.

She was up in the crow's nest they'd rigged from an old cargo boom — not because the Marrow needed a lookout in the braids, but because Juno liked it up there, where the wind came off the water clean and she could pretend, for a few minutes at a stretch, that she was somewhere the past couldn't reach. The channel below unspooled silver and green, splitting and rejoining like a braid of dark hair. Sandbars showed their spines at low water, pale as fish bones.

And behind them, maybe two miles back through the haze, a black skiff was eating the distance between them.

"Skiff!" she shouted, and her voice cracked on the second syllable, so she shouted it again. "Skiff astern! Black hull, twin engines!"

The deck came alive beneath her feet. She scrambled down the boom ladder fast enough to tear her palms, dropped onto the planking, and found Captain Ishikawa already at the wheel, his hands resting on the spokes as if he'd been waiting for this all morning. Maybe he had. He'd barely slept since the collector's skiff had pulled alongside two days ago, since the man with the gray eyes had told them to turn back or forfeit more than coin. Since Rafael had learned that his debt — nine years of installments paid to a harbor clerk, nine years of quiet — had been purchased by Sable Voss before Juno ever signed aboard.

Voss stood amidships with his arms crossed, watching the wake. His face was doing something complicated. Fear, maybe. Or arithmetic.

"How many?" Isela called up to Juno.

"One skiff. Fast one." Juno shaded her eyes. "They're not even trying to hide."

"They don't need to," Voss said quietly. "In open water, nothing we have outruns them."

Rafael turned the wheel a spoke's width. "Then we don't run in open water."

* * *

Juno had been on the river three years now, long enough to know that the braided channels above the Toll Bridge were less a river than an argument the water was having with itself. The Kestrel split into a dozen threads here, weaving between sandbars that moved every flood season, some of them growing, some of them drowning, none of them where any chart said they should be. The syndicate pilots ran these waters with ministry charts and depth sounders and the arrogance of men who'd never been wrong enough to learn humility.

Rafael ran them by memory. By the look of the light on the water, by the color of the foam, by the way a current bent a stand of reeds. Juno had watched him do it for three years without understanding what she was seeing. Now, with a black skiff closing behind them, she understood: the captain wasn't navigating. He was remembering.

"Juno." His voice was flat and calm, which she'd learned meant the opposite. "Bow watch. Call me everything you see. Every bar, every snag, every color change. Don't think about what it means. Just say it."

"Aye, Captain."

She went forward to the prow, gripped the rail, and made herself into an instrument.

The first bar appeared off the starboard bow within minutes — a pale smear under the surface, no more than a hand's width deep. "Bar, starboard, shallow!" The Marrow swung left, close enough that Juno felt the sand whisper against the hull, a soft grinding kiss that would have torn out the bottom of a lesser boat. Behind them, the skiff's roar grew louder. They weren't slowing for the shallows. They didn't know them.

"Split ahead," Juno called. "Three channels. Left looks widest."

"Left is widest," Rafael said. "We're taking the middle."

Isela appeared beside him at the wheel, and Juno saw the two of them exchange something — not words, just a look, the kind of silent conversation people have when they've steered together long enough. Isela nodded once. The Marrow threaded the narrow channel, so tight that willow branches raked both rails, and Juno heard the skiff behind them swing wide toward the safe water, losing half a mile in the detour.

A cheer went up from the deck. Voss didn't join it. He was looking at Rafael with an expression Juno couldn't name — something between awe and grief, as though the captain's gift were a wound he recognized.

"Don't celebrate yet," Rafael said. "They've got charts. Charts get you through the easy parts. We're going where the charts end."

* * *

The syndicate skiff caught up again an hour later, at the place the crew called the Needle's Eye — a channel barely wider than the Marrow herself, cutting between two bars that met below the surface in a submerged ridge. Juno saw it before Rafael asked: the ridge showed itself as a line of broken water, white stitching across the brown current, like the seam of a garment.

"Seam dead ahead," she called. "Running water over the bar."

"I see it." Rafael's knuckles were white on the wheel. "Isela. If we ground on that ridge, we're done. If we go around, we're done slower. So we go over."

"Over?" Juno said, before she could stop herself.

"At speed, we plane across. Six inches of water under the keel for maybe thirty yards." He glanced back at her, and there was something almost like a smile in his eyes, tired and fierce. "You wanted adventure when you signed on, Juno?"

"I wanted wages," she said. "This better count double."

He opened the throttle.

The Marrow leapt. Juno had never felt the old boat move like that — the whole hull rising, shuddering, the engine howling a note she'd never heard it hit, spray coming over the bow in sheets that soaked her to the skin in a heartbeat. The seam of white water rushed at her face. She braced, closed her eyes, thought absurdly of her mother's kitchen, of bread dough being kneaded —

— and then the grinding came, a terrible shudder through the whole ship, wood on sand, and Juno's stomach dropped, and then the shudder released and the Marrow was through, wallowing into deep water on the far side, listing hard to port.

"Report!" Isela shouted.

Juno ran aft along the rail, looking down. Water was coming in through a seam near the waterline, not fast, but steady — a thin silver thread welling up between the planks. "Hull breach, port side, forward of the midship frame! Slow leak!"

"Bilge pump," Rafael ordered, and Juno was already moving, cranking the old brass handle while Isela took the wheel and Rafael swung around the next bend. Behind them, the skiff's engine note changed — hesitation, then a frustrated snarl as it swung wide to find another way across the ridge. They'd bought time. Juno pumped and pumped, her arms burning, watching the bilge water swirl and thinking: *we won, we won* — and then noticing the fuel gauge through the engine room hatch, the needle sitting a third lower than it should have been, the tank holed or leaking somewhere she couldn't see.

Triumph, she was learning, always arrived with a bill attached.

* * *

They hid through the afternoon in a slough so narrow the Marrow's rails brushed cattails on both sides, engines cold, everyone whispering as if the river itself might carry their voices to the syndicate. Juno took the first watch at the stern with a borrowed rifle she hoped she'd never use, and Voss came and sat near her, which surprised her. He rarely spoke to the deckhands. He spoke to Rafael, or to Isela in that careful negotiating voice, or to himself.

"You did well today," he said. "Calling the water. Most greenhands freeze."

"I didn't freeze because I didn't have time to think."

"That's most of courage," Voss said. "The rest is pretending afterward that you meant it."

Juno studied him. Up close, in the slough's green shade, he looked older than he had at the dock nine days ago. There were lines around his eyes that hadn't been there, or that she hadn't looked hard enough to see. "You knew the captain before," she said. "Before all this. You knew him on this river."

Voss was quiet for a long moment. A kingfisher went down the slough like a thrown blue dart.

"Yes," he said finally.

"And whatever happened to make him forget — you were there."

The silence stretched until Juno thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "Ask him. When he's ready. Not before." He stood, joints cracking, and paused. "And Juno — if he ever tells you he was the one who failed? Don't believe it whole. There were three people on that ice, and only one of them has spent nine years paying for it, and it isn't the one you'd guess."

He walked forward into the shadows, leaving Juno with her rifle and her racing heart and a new ledger entry of her own, because she'd started keeping one too, ever since she'd seen Isela burning-question eyes at supper. *Voss knows about the ice. Voss knows who pays. Voss is afraid of something worse than the syndicate.*

The skiff's engine growled somewhere far downstream, patrolling, patient. Juno settled the rifle across her knees and watched the cattails sway and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that the Marrow wasn't running from the syndicate anymore.

The syndicate was herding them.

* * *

They ran again at dusk, when the low sun put the pursuers' eyes into the glare and turned every channel west of them into hammered gold. Rafael took the wheel himself, refusing relief, and Juno stayed at the bow because he asked her to, because her eyes were young and her mouth was quick and because, she suspected, calling the water kept her from being afraid of it.

They lost the skiff twice. Once in a maze of bars where Rafael followed a channel that existed only as a memory — "there was an island here before the flood year, the channel runs where its lee shore used to be" — and once by killing the engine entirely and drifting, silent as a log, while the syndicate boat roared past a quarter mile east, hunting the wrong braid.

Each time, the crew's spirits rose. Each time, Rafael's face got grayer.

"It's not enough," he said quietly to Isela, but the evening air carried it to Juno's ears at the bow. "They'll call the other boats. By tomorrow there'll be three skiffs, maybe four, covering every exit from the braids. And we can't keep running flat-out. Look at the fuel."

Juno looked. The gauge needle had fallen again. A third gone, maybe more, leaked away through whatever wound the ridge had given them, and the nearest fuel cache was at the Halfway House, a day upstream, if it still stood, if anything was still in its tanks after nine abandoned years.

"We make the Halfway House," Isela said. It wasn't a question or a hope; it was a decision, delivered in the voice she used when the vote had already happened inside her. "We patch the hull there, we beg, borrow, or distill fuel, and we rest the crew. And Rafael—" her voice softened by a degree, the only concession she allowed, "—you sleep. Actual sleep. In a bunk. I'll stand your watch."

"I don't dream well lately."

"I know," Isela said. "I'll be there anyway."

Juno stared very hard at the golden water and pretended she hadn't heard, and felt something twist in her chest that was happiness and dread braided together, like the channels themselves.

* * *

Night fell, and with it the fog — river fog, thick as wool, rolling off the cooling water in banks that swallowed the bow from the wheelhouse. Running blind in fog through moving sandbars should have been impossible. But Rafael slowed to a crawl and navigated by ear, by the sound of current against the hull, by the faint hiss of a bar breaking water to port, the hollow gurgle of a cutbank to starboard. Juno hung over the bow rail with a lantern hooded to a coin of light, calling depths by the color of the water under the glow, and it was like being inside a bell, inside a lung, inside the river's own breathing.

It was, she would remember later, almost peaceful. That was the trap of it.

The impact came without warning — a jolt, a crunch, a lurch that threw her against the rail and knocked the lantern spinning. Not a sandbar. Sandbars whispered. This hit like a fist.

"Report!" Isela's voice, sharp with fear.

Juno leaned out, lantern swinging. "Prow's stove in — planking sprung above the waterline, port bow! We hit something solid—" She raised the light higher and her stomach clenched. "Captain. It's a piling. An old dock piling. There's a structure here — right in the channel."

Through a tear in the fog, ghost-gray and sudden, rose the silhouette of a building on stilts at the channel's edge, its windows dark, its roofline sagging, a rusted sign hanging by one chain. Juno had never seen it on any chart. But she saw Rafael come out of the wheelhouse and stop dead, staring up at it, and she saw his face go the color of the fog.

"Halfway House," Isela breathed. "But the channel's moved. It shouldn't be *on* the water anymore—"

"The river remembers differently than the charts do," Rafael said. His voice sounded strange, scraped thin. "So do I."

Behind them, far off through the fog, a horn sounded — once, twice, answered by another, farther east. Three skiffs at least, converging on the last known position, closing the net while the Marrow sat bleeding against a drowned waystation with a third of her fuel gone and a hole in her bow.

Juno looked at the dark building, at the captain's frozen face, at Isela's hand finding Rafael's sleeve, and she reached for the mooring line, because someone had to, because that was what she'd learned on this river: courage was mostly not having time to think.

The rest, Voss had said, was pretending you meant it.

She threw the line over the piling and hauled, and the Marrow groaned against the old dock of a place that had no business existing, and the horns sounded again in the fog, closer now, patient as the tide.

Whatever waited inside the Halfway House, they had no choice but to go in.
