## 15. Isela's Choice

The Marrow limped.

Isela felt it in her boots before she heard it in the engine — a shudder in the deck plates, a hitch in the old girl's stride, like a woman walking on a sprained ankle and pretending she wasn't. The hull had taken a beating in the braided channels, and the port shaft was running rough, a low grinding complaint beneath the diesel's growl that Juno swore she could fix and Rafael hadn't yet let her try.

They had tied up in a fold of the river where the current slackened behind a gravel bar, a pocket of brown water the color of steeped tea. The banks rose steep and gray on either side, birch and alder clinging to the scree. No smoke from any chimney. No lights. The syndicate skiff was somewhere behind them, maybe, or ahead, or watching from a ridgeline with optics — Isela had stopped trying to guess. She had started, instead, doing the one thing she knew how to do when a ship was hurt and a crew was scared: she counted.

She counted the fuel. A third gone, burned in the chase or spilled through the cracked line Juno had jury-rigged with hose clamps and prayer. She counted the food, the water, the spare pins for the rudder, the patches for the hull. She counted the hours of daylight, which were shortening the way they always did this far north, the sun sliding off the ridgeline earlier each evening like a guest who'd overstayed his welcome and knew it.

And she counted the weight in her coat pocket, where the journal had been riding against her hip for six days now, since the Halfway House, since she'd pried it out of the wall with a crowbar and a rising sense that she was holding something that could sink them all.

Tomas Ishikawa's handwriting. She'd know it anywhere; she'd seen it on the Marrow's old cargo manifests, on the chalkboard in the galley where he used to list the day's menu in looping, ridiculous script — *fresh pike, if the pike cooperate* — back when the crew had been five and the river had been a friend. Nine years dead, and his voice was still in her pocket, still warm as a coal, and she had not given it to his brother.

She told herself it was strategy. That Rafael wasn't ready. That the ship was limping and the syndicate was hunting and a man could not steer through calving fields with his hands shaking. All true. All true, and none of it the truth.

The truth was simpler and meaner: she was afraid of what the journal would do to him, and she was afraid of what it would do to *them*, and so she had chosen, day after day, to keep a dead man's voice a secret from the one person alive who needed it most. Love as complicity. She'd written that phrase once in her own notebook, back at the waystation, and had hated herself for the accuracy of it.

* * *

She found him at the wheel after midnight, because of course she did. Rafael Ishikawa slept the way other men kept vigil — lightly, badly, in snatches — and when sleep failed him he came up to the pilothouse and stood at the wheel with the engine idled to a whisper, watching the black water slide by under a sky the color of unpolished pewter. The stars up here were brutal. They came out in such numbers the sky looked bruised, salted, alive.

He didn't turn when she came up the stairs. He knew her step. He'd always known her step.

"Can't sleep either," he said. Not a question.

"Juno finally talked me into letting her look at the shaft bearing." Isela leaned against the chart table, arms folded. "She says if we baby it, we make the source."

"And if we don't baby it?"

"We walk. Two hundred miles of riverbank." She let that sit. "She'll make it. She's got her mother's stubbornness."

That earned him a half-smile, there and gone, the kind he rationed out like the fuel. Isela watched his profile against the wheelhouse glass — the jaw he hadn't shaved in four days, the scar through his eyebrow that he'd never explained and she'd never asked about, the way his eyes kept moving, always moving, sweeping the water the way a man sweeps a room where something once went wrong.

"Rafael." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "I have to show you something."

He turned then. Slowly. He'd heard something in her tone — he always heard everything, that was the curse of him, he heard what people didn't say — and she watched him go still the way deep water goes still, the way the Kestrel went still above a drop.

She took the journal out of her coat.

For a moment he didn't move. Then his eyes went to it, and she saw the recognition land in stages, the way grief always landed in him: first confusion, then the shape of it, then the name, and then the whole nine-year weight of it arriving at once, like a wave that had been traveling under the surface the entire way and only broke when it reached the shallows.

"Where," he said. His voice was gravel.

"Halfway House. Behind the plaster in the east room. There was a gap in the lath." She set it on the chart table between them, not handing it to him — she couldn't hand it to him, her arms wouldn't do it — and she watched his hands hover over the cover without touching, the way a man hovers over a stove he knows is hot.

"That's his hand," Rafael said. "That's—" He stopped. Swallowed. "You've had this since the waystation."

"Yes."

"Six days."

"Yes."

The silence that followed was the worst silence of her life, and Isela had lived through the Lower Stair at flood, through the Narrows with the rudder pins loose, through nine years of watching a man carry a wound he couldn't name. He looked at her and she saw the hurt arrive — real hurt, the kind that doesn't shout, the kind that just stands there in the chest and refuses to leave.

"Why," he said.

"Because you weren't ready." She heard how thin it sounded even as she said it. "Because the ship was limping. Because—" She stopped. Made herself say it straight, the way she'd want it said to her. "Because I was afraid, Rafael. Of what's in it. Of what it would do to you. And I chose protecting you over telling you the truth, and I'd probably do it again, and I hate that about myself, and I'm done now. It's yours. It was always yours."

He looked at the journal a long time. The river made its small sounds against the hull. Somewhere up the bank, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"You kept a ledger," he said finally, and there was no anger in it, which was somehow worse. "Voss's lies. Your own."

"Yes."

"Did you put me in it?"

"Every page."

He nodded, once, slow. Then he picked up the journal, and Isela watched the strongest man she knew hold a dead man's notebook like it was made of ice.

"I'm going to read it," he said.

"I know."

"Whatever's in it."

"I know."

He sat down at the chart table. She should have left. Every instinct of decency said leave a man alone with his dead brother's voice. But she had spent six days being a coward in the name of love, and she was done with that, so she sat down across from him and did not look away, and after a moment he did not ask her to.

He opened the cover.

* * *

The first pages were nothing. That was the cruelty of it — how *nothing* they were. Cargo notes. Water levels. A sketch of a kingfisher Tomas had seen on the delta, rendered with the fussy precision of a man who drew birds the way other men drank. Isela watched Rafael's face as he read, and she saw him smile at the kingfisher, an actual smile, broken and involuntary, and she thought: *there it is, there's the boy he was.*

Then the pages turned, and the smile died.

She watched him read the entries from nine years ago. She had read them herself, twice, by lamplight in the Halfway House, and she knew what was coming the way you know what's coming in a river you've run before — the bend, the drop, the standing wave — and she could do nothing now but watch it take him.

Tomas's voice changed in the journal that summer. The looping script tightened. The kingfishers disappeared. What replaced them was a man writing fast and writing scared, a first mate keeping a private ledger of his own.

*Voss has found something at the source,* the entry read. *Old provision cache from the survey years — sealed, intact, and full of things the ministry says doesn't exist. Records. Supply manifests. Names. Sable thinks it's worth a fortune. I think it's worth a noose, and I told him so. He laughed. He's been laughing a lot lately and it doesn't reach his eyes.*

Rafael's breathing had gone shallow. Isela reached across the table and put her hand flat on the wood, not touching him, just there, an offer. He didn't take it. He turned the page.

*The syndicate knows. Of course they know — half the ministry is theirs already. Voss says we can outrun them to the source and back before calving. I say the ice doesn't care whose flag you fly. Rafa agrees with me, which makes two of us, which makes Voss call us the church choir. God, I love my brother. He's the only honest man on this river and he doesn't even know what he's honest about.*

Isela heard Rafael make a sound. Small. She would not have caught it if the engine weren't idled to nothing. It was the sound a man makes when a wound that has been numb for nine years suddenly remembers it's a wound.

Then the entries from the last week. The ice field. The calving season coming early, the way it was coming early now. And then — the last entries, the ones Isela had read three times and still could not read calmly, the ones she had kept in her pocket for six days like a stone.

*Voss cut the rope.*

Rafael read the words and went absolutely still, the way the Marrow went still when it struck something.

*I have to write this while I can still hold a pen. They'll tell it wrong. They'll tell it however the survivors tell it, and survivors tell it to survive. So: Voss cut the rope. My rope. The one holding me on the ice shelf when it went. And I am alive to hate him for it, or I would be, except—*

*Except here is what nobody saw but me. The shelf was going. All of it. The whole lip of it, and Rafa was anchored to me and I to him, and when it went we were both going into the water under the ice, both of us, and the water under a calving glacier is a door that does not open twice. Voss cut my line because the shelf was taking me down and I was taking Rafa with me. He didn't kill me. He tried to save us both and could only save one, and he chose my brother.*

*And here is the part I will never say out loud, so I'll say it here: it was Rafa's order that put me on the ice at all. He'd taken a knock — a throwing pin off a winch, a bad one, he was seeing double and wouldn't say so, too proud, too much my big brother. And I asked him, I asked him straight, is the channel clear, and he said run it, and I ran it, because his voice has always been the river's voice to me. The concussion. He doesn't know. He thinks he chose. He didn't choose anything. He was a hurt man giving orders on bad ice, and I was a fool who trusted him, and Voss was a coward who did the arithmetic nobody should ever have to do.*

*If I don't come back from the source — and we're going, Voss won't be argued with, and the syndicate is already on the water — I want this page to find my brother. Not to hurt him. God, not to hurt him. But because he'll spend his whole life thinking he killed me, and he didn't, and a man shouldn't carry a weight that was never his. Voss cut the rope. The ice did the rest. And I loved them both, the honest one and the coward, and I'd have followed either of them anywhere, and I did, and here I am.*

*Tell Rafa the pike never cooperated. Tell him it wasn't his fault. Tell him—*

The entry stopped. The next page was blank. The page after that had one line, in a shakier hand, dated the day before the disaster:

*Voss came to me tonight and asked me to leave the crew after this run. He said the syndicate would kill us all for what's in that cache. I told him the Ishikawas don't leave ships mid-river. He looked at me like a man looks at a door he can't close. I wonder, sometimes, what my brother isn't telling me.*

Rafael closed the journal.

He did it very gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child, and then he sat with his hands flat on the cover and his head bowed, and Isela watched nine years come off him and go back on again, heavier, rearranged. His shoulders didn't shake. She had expected them to shake. They didn't. He just breathed — long, deliberate breaths, the breathing of a man hauling something enormous up from depth, hand over hand, refusing to let it drop.

"He cut the rope," Rafael said at last. His voice was wrecked, scraped down to the grain. "To save me. He cut Tomas's rope to save *me*, and I—"

"Rafael—"

"Nine years." He looked up, and his eyes were dry, which frightened her more than tears would have. Dry eyes and that terrible stillness. "Nine years I've hated him. Nine years I've paid a *debt* to a man I thought murdered my brother, and the whole time—" His hand came down on the journal, not hard, but the table jumped. "I gave the order. Isela. *I* gave the order. Concussed, bleeding, seeing double, and Tomas asked me straight and I said *run it*, and he ran it because he trusted my voice more than his own eyes."

"He trusted you because you were worth trusting."

"I killed him."

"No." She was on her feet now, around the table, her hands on his face before she'd decided to move, making him look at her the way she'd make a green deckhand look at the water instead of the wave. "You listen to me, Rafael Ishikawa. A hurt man made a call on bad ice. That's not murder, that's a river. The river does that. The river *did* that. And your brother — your brother who knew you better than anyone alive — spent his last night on this earth writing down, in his own hand, that it wasn't your fault. Are you going to sit there and call Tomas a liar?"

The question landed. She watched it land, watched it break something loose behind his eyes, and finally — finally — the dry stillness cracked, and he bowed forward until his forehead was against her shoulder, and his shoulders shook, and Isela held the back of his head the way she'd hold a line under load, both hands, no slack, and let him.

The owl asked its question again. The river answered in its small voice against the hull. Juno slept in the fo'c'sle, dreaming whatever nineteen-year-olds dream, and the shaft bearing ground its slow complaint, and the stars wheeled overhead, indifferent and enormous, and Isela held the man she loved while he wept for a brother who had spent his final hours trying to save him from exactly this.

It took a long time. She didn't count it.

When he finally straightened, his face was different. Not healed — nothing was healed, nothing would ever be healed — but rearranged, the way a river rearranges a channel after flood: same water, new course. He looked at the journal, and then at her, and something moved through his expression that she couldn't name until he said it.

"Voss knew." His voice was quiet now, level, and there was something in it she'd never heard before — not hatred, not grief. Something colder and more deliberate. "He's known for nine years what the rope was for. He's known what my debt was buying. He let me hate him. He *built* it, Isela — the silence, the payments, all of it — he let me hate him because hating him was easier on me than the truth."

"Or because he couldn't carry it either," Isela said. "Both things can be true."

"Both things are true." Rafael picked up the journal and held it a moment, thumb moving over the cover like a man touching a scar. Then he stood, and Isela saw the decision arrive in him before he spoke it, the way she always did — the stillness first, then the course.

"We don't turn back," he said. "Not for him. Not for the syndicate. We run the last of this river to the source, and I put my brother's journal on that ice where he died, and then Voss and I are going to settle nine years of accounts. Every one of them."

Above the ridgeline, far to the north, the sky flickered — once, twice, a green shimmer too low and too steady to be any aurora Isela knew. And beneath the hull, faint but unmistakable, coming up through the deck plates and into the soles of her feet, the river carried a sound she had only heard once before, nine years ago, and had spent nine years trying to forget.

The glacier, far upstream, had begun to groan.
