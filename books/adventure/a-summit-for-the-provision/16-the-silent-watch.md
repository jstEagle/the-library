## 16. The Silent Watch

The journal lay closed on the chart table between them, and Rafael could not look at it.

He had read it twice — once fast, the way a man snatches his hand from a stove, and once slow, because the second reading was the one that made it true. Tomas's handwriting, slanting right the way it always had, the ink gone brown with nine years of hiding in a wall at Halfway House. *Voss cut the rope.* Not to drop him. To cut him free of the line that had snagged and was dragging him under the shelf ice. And beneath that, in a hand that shook by the final pages: *Rafael gave the order. He doesn't remember. God help him, he doesn't remember, and I'm glad.*

Glad. His brother had been glad that he didn't remember.

Rafael stood at the wheel of the Marrow and said nothing.

The river above Halfway House ran narrow and quick through a corridor of black spruce, the water so dark under the overcast that it seemed less like water than like a seam in the world, stitched shut ahead of them and unpicked behind. The current shouldered the hull. He let it. His hands rested on the spokes without gripping, and the wheel answered him the way it had answered him for twenty years, before words, before names, before memory — the way it would answer him after everything else was gone.

"Captain." Juno, at the rail, her voice careful. "Fuel gauge again. We're sitting at maybe forty percent of what we had at the Narrows."

He looked at her. She waited for an answer. He gave her none.

Her face did something complicated — hurt, then worry, then the quick arithmetic of a young person deciding not to take another person's silence personally. "I'll log it," she said finally, and went aft.

Isela came up beside him. She didn't speak either. That was the difference between them and everyone else on this boat, and maybe the difference between them and everyone else alive: she understood that what he needed was not comfort but company, and she provided it the way the river provided banks — silently, steadily, defining him without confining him. She leaned her hip against the wheel housing and watched the channel with him, and after a while she put her hand flat on the wood near his, not touching, just there, the way you'd set down a lantern in a dark room so the other person knew where the light was.

* * *

The memories came back the way floodwater comes into a low field. Not all at once. In sheets.

He steered, and the river unspooled, and the past rose through him in layers, each one colder than the last.

First the small things, which he had never actually lost and only pretended to: Tomas at seven, standing on the dock at the delta with a fish too big for him, refusing help, refusing even to smile until it was landed. Tomas learning knots from their father with his tongue between his teeth. Tomas, sixteen, saying *I'm coming upstream with you, Rafa, don't be stupid about it* — and Rafael laughing, because who would be stupid about it? Who would leave his brother on the dock?

Then the year itself. Nine years ago, the same run, the same river, the same glacier waiting at its head like a held breath. Voss younger then, sharp as a filleting knife, hungry in a way Rafael had mistaken for ambition and now understood had been desperation — debts of his own, though he'd never said whose. The crew of eleven. Names surfacing one by one like bodies: Okonkwo at the engine, laughing through oil smoke. The Brandt sisters on deck. Little Ferro, who'd signed on to see ice and had seen it.

And then the calving fields. The order.

It came back whole, and it came back in his own voice.

*Take the eastern channel.* He had stood where Isela stood now, or nearly, and he had said it with the flat certainty of a man who trusted his own reading of the ice more than anyone else's, because he was Rafael Ishikawa and his readings were never wrong. The eastern channel was faster. The western channel hugged the shore and cost half a day, and they were racing the season, and Voss had argued — Voss had argued, Rafael remembered that now, remembered the set of his jaw, the way he'd said *it doesn't feel right, Rafa* — and Rafael had overridden him. Had given the order. Had watched Tomas walk out onto the shelf ice to set the marker buoys because Tomas always volunteered, because Tomas trusted his brother's channels the way other men trusted scripture.

The crack had come at dusk. Not loud. That was the thing no one believed — the worst sounds on a glacier were quiet, a sigh, a click, like a lock turning. Then the shelf tilted, and Tomas went down with the buoy line, and the line snagged, and the water took him the way cold water takes everything: completely, without malice, without hurry.

Rafael had gone in after him. He remembered that now too — the cold like a fist closing around his heart, the green murk, the rope in his hands. He remembered Voss diving in behind him. He remembered the impact — ice, or the boat's fender iron, he'd never know — a white detonation behind his eyes. And he remembered nothing after that for six weeks, and when he woke, the world had been rearranged around a hole shaped like his brother, and everyone had been very kind, and no one had told him anything, and eventually he had stopped asking, because the asking hurt worse than the not-knowing, and not-knowing had become a country he learned to live in, and he had built the Marrow inside it, and paid his strange debt, and called the silence peace.

It had never been peace. It had been anesthesia. And someone had been paying for the dose, all along.

* * *

Midnight came down gray, not black; this far north in this season the sky never fully surrendered. The spruce corridor opened into a lake reach, long and windless, the water holding the sky's dull pewter light so perfectly that the Marrow seemed to travel through cloud. Rafael ran the engine down to a whisper. There was no need for speed. There was no longer any reason for anything except forward.

Juno slept in the pilothouse chair, curled under a canvas jacket, her breathing the only human sound aboard besides the engine. Isela had gone below an hour ago and come back with two mugs of tea she set on the chart table without comment. One went cold untouched. She drank hers slowly, watching him watch the water.

His voice felt like something packed in ice, stored in a hold he couldn't open. Twice he tried. Twice his throat worked around nothing. It wasn't grief exactly, or not only grief — it was that every sentence he owned had been built on the foundation of the lie, and now the foundation was gone and the sentences stood in midair, impossible, absurd. *We'll make the source by Thursday.* Said to whom? For whom? The man who said it had ordered his brother onto the ice and couldn't remember doing it, and the remembering had changed the shape of his mouth.

So he said nothing. He steered.

Somewhere past the middle of the lake reach, Isela moved. She didn't touch him — she never touched him first when he was like this, some old instinct of hers, some courtesy of wild things — but she sat down on the low bench beside the wheel, close enough that their shoulders would meet if either of them swayed, and she stayed. An hour. Two. The tea went cold in both mugs. Once, far off across the water, a loon called, that sound like laughter run backward, and neither of them turned to look.

He wanted to tell her. The wanting was enormous, tidal. He wanted to say: I killed him. I gave the order and my brother walked out onto the ice because my word was gospel to him, and the river took him, and I have spent nine years being quietly, comfortably sad about a tragedy instead of honestly, unbearably guilty about a choice. He wanted to say: Voss has been paying me to not know myself. Every installment at the harbor clerk's office — nine years of them — was Voss buying my ignorance, and I sold it cheap, and I lived well on the price.

But if he opened his mouth the ship would come apart, and someone had to keep her off the bars.

So Isela sat, and he steered, and the silence between them did the work that words do between other people. Near dawn she reached over and turned his cold tea into the scuppers and set a full mug in his hand, and he drank it without tasting it, and that was the whole of their conversation, and it was enough. It was more than enough. It was the only thing in the world that was.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of old bone. The lake narrowed back into river, and the river began, almost imperceptibly, to climb. He could feel it in the wheel — the push against them strengthening, the water going from pewter to a hard glacial green, cold rising off the surface like breath off stone. Somewhere ahead, beyond the next bend and the next, the calving fields were groaning awake. He couldn't hear them yet. But he could feel them the way you feel weather in a scar.

Voss appeared at the pilothouse door as the sun cleared the ridge — a red smear behind cloud, giving light without warmth. Rafael saw him in the glass first: standing there with his hat in his hands like a man at a funeral, opening his mouth.

"Don't." The word came out of Rafael cracked and rusted, the first he'd spoken in fourteen hours, and it surprised them both. He kept his eyes on the channel. "Not yet. Whatever it is you want to say to me — the apology, the explanation, the part where you tell me how much you suffered — not yet. I'm not ready to hear it, and you're not ready to say it, and if you try we'll both say things the river will have to carry for another nine years."

Voss stood there a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was stripped down to studs. "Where are we headed, then? If not back."

Rafael turned the wheel two degrees to starboard, lining up the channel markers that weren't there anymore but that he could still see, burned into the water like a brand. The eastern channel. His channel. The one that had cost everything.

"I'm going to the place where my brother died," he said. "Not for you. Not for the cache, not for the syndicate, not for whatever's left of your plan. For him. Someone in this world is going to stand on that ice and know exactly what happened there, all of it, every piece — and it's going to be me."

Behind him, he heard Juno wake in the chair, heard her go still, listening. Heard Isela breathe once, slow, the way she breathed when she was bracing a line against a load.

"And after?" Voss asked quietly.

Ahead, faint as a heartbeat under the engine's drone, the glacier spoke — one long groan rolling down the valley, ice shifting in its sleep, or waking. The window was closing. They all heard it.

"After," Rafael said, "we find out what the river keeps."

He put the Marrow's bow at the head of the eastern channel and drove her upstream into the sound of the breaking ice.
