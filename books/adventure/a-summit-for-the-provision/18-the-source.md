## 18. The Source

They came through the last of the calving fields in the hour before dawn, when the world was the color of a bruise healing — violet fading to gray, gray to a pale green that had no business existing on water.

Rafael stood at the wheel and did not breathe.

The channel he remembered — the channel his body remembered, because his mind had surrendered all claim to it nine years ago — opened ahead of the Marrow like a throat. Ice floes the size of chapel roofs drifted in slow rotation, grinding against one another with a sound like millstones, and between them ran threads of open water no wider than the boat herself. He took them without charts. He took them without speaking. His hands moved on the wheel the way a man's hands move in a dream he has had ten thousand times, and Isela stood at his shoulder with the lead line coiled in her fist, calling depths in a voice gone quiet as prayer.

"Twelve feet."

"Twelve," he echoed, though she hadn't asked him to.

"Nine."

"Nine." The keel kissed something soft — silt, not stone — and slid free. Juno hung off the bow rail with a boathook, fending ice away from the hull plating, her breath smoking in the cold that poured down off the glacier like water running backward.

And above them, filling half the sky, the glacier itself.

Rafael had forgotten its size. Or perhaps he had never known it; perhaps memory is a mercy that shrinks what it cannot let you carry. The ice wall rose from the river's end in tiers and buttresses, blue at its base where the water ate at it, white as bone at its crest, streaked with the ash of some fire no living person had set. It did not look like geography. It looked like architecture — like the facade of a cathedral built by something with too much time and no interest in being worshipped.

From somewhere inside it came a groan, long and low, the sound of a giant turning over in sleep. The water shivered. The floes rearranged themselves by inches.

"Captain." Voss stood at the foot of the wheel stairs, dressed for cold, face raw with wind. She had barely slept — none of them had — but her eyes were lit from behind, the way they'd been lit the first day Rafael ever saw her, nineteen years old and certain the river would give her everything if she only went far enough up it. "Half a mile. Then the lake."

"I know where the lake is."

"You know where it *was*." She said it gently, which was worse. "Ice moves. Channels move. Let me—"

"Stay off my deck, Sable."

She held his gaze a moment longer, then nodded once and withdrew. That was new, too. Nine years ago Sable Voss would have argued until the argument became a fight. Now she simply obeyed, and Rafael understood that obedience was its own kind of confession, and hated how much he wanted to hear the rest of it.

* * *

The river ended the way rivers do at their beginnings: all at once, and quietly.

One moment the Marrow was threading the last narrows between drifting bergs; the next the walls fell away and there was nothing ahead but the lake — a vast round eye of water cupped in the glacier's palm, still as glass, so turquoise it seemed lit from beneath by some drowned sun. The color was wrong. It was the color of tropical shallows, of jewelry, of things that belong in warm places, and here it sat under a sky the color of iron, ringed by ice cliffs three hundred feet tall, and it glowed.

Nobody spoke. Even the glacier seemed to hold its breath.

Juno broke it first, because Juno always broke things first. "It's *warm*," she said, hanging over the rail, one hand trailing. "Captain — the water's warm. How is it—"

"Springs under the ice." Rafael heard his own voice come out flat and far away. "Glacier sits on volcanic ground. The lake never freezes. Not even in calving season." He turned the wheel and brought the Marrow around the rim of the eye, toward the eastern shore where — where —

Where his memory opened like a wound.

There. A shelf of rock below the cliff face, sheltered from the worst of the calving, with a scree ramp running up to a seam in the ice. And set into the seam, half-swallowed by nine years of refrozen melt, an iron door.

He had put his hands on that door. He knew it the way he knew his own heartbeat — suddenly, horribly, completely. He could feel the frost biting his palms. He could hear Tomas laughing behind him, saying something about—

About—

The groan came again, deeper this time, and a crack ran up the cliff face like lightning frozen mid-strike. Somewhere high above, a serac the size of a house tilted, considered, and did not fall. Yet.

"Dock us," Isela said quietly, at his elbow. "Rafael. Dock us, love. Before the window shuts."

He docked them. His hands never shook. He wondered distantly whether that meant he was brave or already dead.

* * *

The cache door took two hours and a crate of blasting compound to open — Voss's ministry permits finally spent on the thing they'd been forged for, Juno setting the charge with hands that shook only a little, the blast cracking the refrozen seal in a cough of blue smoke and ice-shards. And then the seam widened into a passage, and the passage opened into the mountain, and Rafael Ishikawa stood in the doorway of the provision cache and understood, finally, why men had killed for it.

It was not treasure. That was the first thing. He had imagined gold, maybe, or guns — something worth the syndicate's hunger. But the cavern ran back into the ice a hundred yards, timbered and dry, and it was filled with *supplies*. Crates of preserved food stamped with ministry seals. Medical stores. Fuel. Rope, canvas, tools, rifles wrapped in waxed paper. Enough provisioned goods to outfit an expedition or feed a garrison — enough, Voss had said once, drunk on ambition, to buy a country's loyalty in a famine year.

And along the near wall, in oilcloth and steel cabinets, the other thing. The records.

Voss pulled a cabinet open with hands that were not steady. Ledgers. Shipping manifests. Letters bearing signatures Rafael recognized from newspaper front pages — ministers, magistrates, the polite faces of the syndicate that owned half the delta and every toll bridge on the upper Kestrel. Dates. Amounts. Routes. Proof, in ink, of everything: the smuggling, the bribes, the wrecks arranged for insurance, the crews written off as weather.

"Nine years I've known this was here," Voss said. Her voice echoed strangely off the ice. "Nine years I've been paying for the right to stand in this room. Do you understand now? This isn't wealth, Rafael. It's a *verdict*. Every page in these cabinets is a rope with a syndicate neck in it."

"And that's why they're coming." Isela stood in the doorway, silhouetted against the turquoise light, and behind her Rafael heard the thing he had been refusing to hear for two days: engines. Faint. Downriver. Growing.

"They can't let this exist," Isela said. "Not the supplies. The *paper*."

Voss closed the cabinet. "Then we'd best decide quickly what we save and what we burn."

* * *

They worked like people possessed, because they were. Juno and the deckhand Pell hauled crates toward the entrance ramp; Isela sorted records into two piles — evidence worth dying for and paper worth nothing; Voss moved through the cabinets naming names aloud, half to herself, half to Rafael, as though reciting a rosary of the damned.

Rafael carried crates and listened to the glacier.

It had stopped groaning. That was the trouble. For an hour the ice had been silent in the way a drawn bow is silent, and the silence pressed against his eardrums harder than any sound. Twice he caught himself standing motionless amid the work, head cocked, listening to nothing, feeling the floor of the world gather itself beneath his boots.

On the third time, Isela found him like that.

"Tell me," she said. No preamble. Her hands were black with ink and cold, and she took his face in them anyway. "Not about Tomas. Not yet. Just tell me what you're hearing."

"Nothing."

"And that frightens you."

"Everything quiet is either resting or loading," Rafael said. "I've run this river twenty years, Isela. Nothing quiet is ever done."

She rested her forehead against his. For a moment, in the turquoise dark among the crates of other men's crimes, they simply breathed together, and Rafael thought: *this is the thing I chose instead of vengeance, and I would choose it again, and I am going to have to choose it again very soon.*

Engines. Closer now. He could see them in his mind without looking — the syndicate skiff coming up the last channel, riding light, running hot, carrying men whose job it was to make sure verdicts never got read aloud.

"We load the boat," he said. "We take the ledgers and we leave the rest. Sable—" he raised his voice, and it cracked on her name, "—Sable. We're leaving. Now."

Voss looked up from the open cabinet, and across her face moved something Rafael had never seen there before: not greed, not triumph, but terror — the terror of a woman watching the last door of her life begin to close while she stands on the wrong side of it.

"One more cabinet," she said. "Rafael. One more. It has Tomas's name in it."

The words went through him like ice water through a hull.

Above them, faint but unmistakable, the glacier exhaled — a single vast *crack*, echoing down through the timbered cavern, through the iron door, through the turquoise lake where the Marrow lay tied to a rock shelf older than nations.

And downriver, around the last bend, the syndicate skiff came into view, running lights blazing, closing fast.
