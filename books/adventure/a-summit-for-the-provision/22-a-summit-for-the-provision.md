## 22. A Summit for the Provision

The delta in autumn smelled of rot and gold — reeds dying back into the mud, salmon running their last run up the channels, woodsmoke from the stilt houses drifting out over the water like a blessing nobody had asked for. Rafael Ishikawa stood at the wheel of the *Marrow* and let it all come to him on the wind: the sweetness, the decay, the ordinary miracle of a river doing what rivers do.

Six months since the source. Six months since the glacier calved with a sound like the world's spine cracking, since Sable Voss went under the ice with nine years of guilt and a syndicate skiff and a lake full of stolen provision records. Six months since Rafael had stood on a heaving deck and watched the water close over the man who had bought his silence, kept his secret, and died trying to give something back.

He still dreamed about it. But the dreams had changed. Tomas no longer came to him drowning. These days his brother walked the ice fields in the dreams, unhurried, hands in his pockets, looking north toward the white summits as if checking on them. As if they were his now.

"Sandbar off the port bow," Juno said from beside him, her voice steady, her hand hovering near the throttle without being told. "Shoaling water past the third marker."

"I see it." He didn't need to see it. He had known that sandbar since before Juno was born, back when it sat forty meters east of where it sat now, because sandbars move the way grief moves — slowly, and never quite where you left them. "Take us through. Your wheel."

Juno's eyes lit the way they always did when he handed her the *Marrow*. She took the spokes with both hands, feet planted wide, tongue caught between her teeth in concentration. The riverboat answered her gently, sliding through the green shallows with barely a whisper of silt against the hull. Six months ago she'd been a deckhand who froze at the first sight of whitewater. Now she read the water's face the way Isela read people — quickly, skeptically, and usually right.

"Nice line," Rafael said.

"Nice river," she shot back, grinning, and he laughed, and the laugh felt easy in his chest, unguarded, like a door left open on purpose.

Isela came up from below with two cups of chicory coffee, steam curling off them, and pressed one into his free hand. She didn't stand next to him so much as settle against him, shoulder to hip, the way she'd been doing for six months now, the way she would probably do for sixty years if the river allowed them that long. Her hair was tied back with the red cord he'd given her at midsummer. Her ledger — the one where she'd once kept her own private accounting of Voss's lies — was tucked under her arm, though these days it held cargo manifests and fuel tallies instead of suspicions.

"You're smiling," she said. "Somebody check the barometer."

"The barometer's fine. I'm just thinking this is the first season in nine years I haven't been hiding from anything."

She studied him over the rim of her cup. Isela never wasted words, but she wasted looks even less. "And how does it feel? Not hiding?"

"Lighter," he said. Then, honestly: "Stranger. Like I'm carrying less weight but I keep reaching for it anyway."

"That's not strange." She leaned her head briefly against his shoulder. "That's just what healing feels like from the inside. Like a missing tooth. You keep tonguing the gap."

Below them, the hold rode light and honest: salt fish, timber, seed grain, lamp oil. Ordinary cargo for ordinary runs. The ministry investigator had taken everything else — Voss's confession, the surviving pages of Tomas's journal, the coordinates, the names of every syndicate hand from the toll bridge to the capital. Three arrests in the delta towns already. Two more warrants pending upstream. The clerk at the harbor office, the one who had collected Rafael's mysterious installments all those years, had turned state's evidence and wept through most of his deposition. Rafael hadn't gone to watch. He'd found he didn't need to.

The debt was settled. Not paid — some things couldn't be paid — but settled, the way a body settles into rest.

* * *

They made the fishing village by late afternoon, threading the channel that appeared on no chart, marked by nothing but a leaning alder and a submerged rock shaped like a sleeping dog. The village looked the same as it had a year ago: stilt houses on the mudflats, nets hung like grey curtains, smoke rising thin and blue from a dozen chimneys. Children ran down the rickety dock before the *Marrow* had even finished tying off, shouting greetings at Juno, who shouted back and tossed an orange to the boldest of them.

Old Wren was waiting at the top of the dock ladder, wrapped in a coat made of oiled canvas gone soft with years, her face brown and seamed as driftwood. She had been old when Rafael first met her, nine years and a lifetime ago, when he'd come up this channel with a crew of six and a brother of nineteen and a name he used to answer to.

She looked at him now the way she always did — as if seeing straight through the years, straight through the changed face and the grey in the beard, to whatever remained constant underneath.

"Rafael Ishikawa," she said. "You got old."

"You got older."

"Only the lucky ones do." She gripped his forearm, hard, the grip of someone who measured men by what their hands could bear. Then her eyes went past him to Isela, to Juno, to the repaired hull with its new planking pale against the weathered old. "You brought your family."

"Some of it." His throat tightened, the way it always did here. This was the place where the fragments had first surfaced — the fire, the shouting, the cold water. This was where an elder had called him by a name he didn't remember using, and the past had begun refusing to stay sunk. "Wren. I brought you something."

* * *

They sat in her house above the water, on stools worn smooth by generations, while tea steeped in a dented copper pot. Isela and Juno had gone to trade with the other households — flour and lamp oil for smoked fish and news — leaving Rafael alone with the old woman and the thing in his pocket.

He took it out and set it on the table between them: Tomas's compass.

It was a plain instrument, brass-cased, the glass scratched, the needle settling north with a small patient tremble. It had been in the *Marrow*'s wheelbox since the disaster; Rafael had carried it through nine years of amnesia without knowing whose it was, the way he'd carried the hole in his memory without knowing its shape. Only after Isela gave him the journal, only after he'd read his brother's handwriting — *R. says the ice will hold. R. is never wrong. R. R. R.* — had he understood what he'd been keeping.

Now the needle trembled toward true north, and Rafael turned the compass slowly until it pointed along a bearing he had calculated a dozen times and checked a dozen more: northeast by east, across the flats, up the hidden tributary, toward the high country where the glacier stood.

"What's this?" Wren asked.

"A bearing." His voice came out rougher than he intended. He cleared his throat. "It points to the place my brother died. The glacier source. There's a lake there — turquoise, under the ice wall. That bearing will take you to the ridge above it, the safe one, the one where you can see the whole valley without setting foot on the ice."

Wren looked at the compass for a long time. Her hands, folded around her tea, were still.

"Why are you giving me this?"

Rafael had asked himself the same question for weeks, turning it over on night watches while Isela slept and the river talked to itself in the dark. The answer had come slowly, the way all true answers come.

"Because names die when the last person who remembers them dies," he said. "Tomas Ishikawa drowned on that ice nine years ago, and for nine years nobody remembered him right. I couldn't remember him at all. The syndicate buried what happened. Voss buried what happened. Even I buried what happened, without meaning to. And then the glacier took the proof, and the confession, and Voss himself, and there was almost nothing left of him anywhere in the world except—" he touched his own chest, "—in here. And in here isn't enough. In here fades."

He turned the compass so the needle swung, settled, steadied on the bearing.

"So I want the name somewhere it won't fade. You knew him. You fed him smoked fish on this dock and called him *little captain* because he followed me around like a gull follows a boat. You're the last one left who knew him before. Teach the bearing to your children. Tell them a young man named Tomas Ishikawa loved this river and died too far up it, and that his brother finally brought him home. Let them look northeast by east sometimes and know there's a summit up there that belongs to our family. A summit for the provision. For everything he gave and everything that was taken."

The old woman picked up the compass. She held it in both palms like an egg, like a coal, like something alive. The needle trembled between her weathered fingers.

"My eyes aren't what they were," she said. "But my granddaughter reads stars and stones better than I ever did. She'll carry it." She looked up at him, and her eyes were wet and fierce at once. "Every autumn, when the salmon run, we'll speak the name. Tomas Ishikawa. And the children will ask who he was, and we'll tell them, and they'll get tired of hearing it, and that's exactly how it should be. That's how the dead stay warm."

Rafael bowed his head. He had thought he was coming here to give something away. He hadn't understood until this moment that he was also receiving: a promise, a grave marker made of memory and bearing-lines, a place for his brother in the living world that no glacier could calve away and no syndicate could buy.

"Thank you," he whispered.

"Don't thank me." Wren closed her fist around the compass and pressed it to her breastbone. "Live long, little captain's brother. Live loud. That's the thanks the dead want."

* * *

They stayed the night, moored against the village dock with lanterns strung from the rail, and the villagers brought food out to them because that was the custom and because Isela had spent the afternoon making friends the way she made everything — thoroughly and without apology. There was music later, a fiddle and a drum, and Juno danced with three different fishermen's daughters and tripped over all of them equally. Rafael sat on the deck with Isela tucked under his arm and watched the firelight play on the water.

"Do you remember," Isela said quietly, "the night Voss first came to the dock? You told me no amount of money was worth going back up that river."

"I remember."

"And then I argued you into it. I stood in your cabin and told you pride was a luxury and the crew's purse was empty, and I pushed until you said yes." She turned her face up toward him. Firelight moved in her dark eyes. "I've wondered for months whether I owe the crew an apology or thanks."

"Both," Rafael said. "Same as me. If we hadn't gone, I'd still be a man paying installments on a lie, married to a ghost I couldn't name. If we hadn't gone, Tomas would still be nowhere." He tightened his arm around her. "You didn't push me into a mistake, Isela. You pushed me into the truth. It just happened to be wearing a monster's clothes when it arrived."

She was quiet a moment. "Voss wore a lot of clothes," she said at last, and he laughed, surprising himself, and she laughed too, and the sound rose over the fiddle music and out across the black water.

Later, when the fires burned low and the village slept, Rafael took the wheel alone for the middle watch, not because anyone asked but because some nights the wheel was where his thoughts sorted themselves best. The river slid past, silvered by moonlight. Somewhere northeast by east, under a hundred miles of stone and ice, his brother lay beneath a turquoise lake, and an old woman's granddaughter would learn a bearing, and children yet unborn would grow tired of hearing a name.

That was enough. More than enough. It was, he understood at last, the whole provision — not the cache the syndicate had wanted, not the supplies and records locked under the glacier, but this: the slow, stubborn river of memory, carrying what mattered downstream to the people who would keep it.

* * *

Spring came early the following year, and with it the *Marrow*'s first run of the season: grain downstream, salt and tools up. Juno stood at the wheel for the entire passage through the Lower Stair — the same rapids where she'd once frozen — and brought them through clean, and Rafael clapped her shoulder and said nothing, because some praise is too big for words and too obvious for speeches.

At the delta dock, the harbor clerk — a new one, the old one having traded his ledgers for a cell and a lighter conscience — handed Rafael a letter with a ministry seal. Rafael broke it open standing on the gangway, Isela at his elbow.

The charges were finalized. The syndicate's holdings on the Kestrel were forfeit. And enclosed, in a clerk's careful hand, was a single line that made Rafael go still:

*In accordance with the recovered testimony of S. Voss, the tributary and glacial source formerly designated Provision Site Nine has been renamed on all official charts.*

Below it, printed in formal type:

**Ishikawa Reach.**

Rafael read it three times. Then he handed it to Isela without a word, and watched her read it, and watched her hand rise slowly to her mouth.

"They named it after him," she said. "After both of you."

"After him." Rafael shook his head, though his eyes were burning. "I just drove the boat."

He looked upriver, past the docks and the smoke and the gulls wheeling over the fish market, to where the distant peaks stood white against the spring sky — northeast by east, the bearing written now on maps and in an old woman's memory and in the marrow of everyone who had loved either brother. The glacier would calve again this summer. The river would rise and fall and rise. Sandbars would shift, and children would grow, and the *Marrow* would run her routes until her planks gave out, and then another boat would take her name, or a new one, and the work would go on.

Rafael Ishikawa put the letter carefully inside his coat, over his heart, took his first mate's hand, and stepped aboard.

"Cast off," he said. "We're expected downstream."

And the *Marrow*, light and honest in the water, turned her bow to the current and ran — not fleeing, not chasing, not hiding, just going where rivers go, which is home, which is onward, which is always both.
