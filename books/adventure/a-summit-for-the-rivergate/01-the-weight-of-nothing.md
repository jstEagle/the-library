## 1. The Weight of Nothing

The salt flats began where the world's patience ended.

Ivo Eastwick had been walking them for six days, and in that time he had counted every kind of nothing the desert could offer: the nothing of a horizon that refused to resolve into anything at all, the nothing of wind moving across crust so flat it seemed the sky had fallen and frozen there, the nothing inside himself that he carried the way other men carried water. He had learned, over eleven years as a ranger, that nothing was not empty. Nothing had weight. It pressed down on a man mile by mile, and most men folded under it eventually, and the ones who didn't were the ones who had learned to stop asking what the weight was for.

He stopped asking years ago.

The thief's tracks ran ahead of him, faint as breath on glass. She — and he was increasingly sure it was a she, from the narrowness of the stride and the way the prints avoided the salt crust's weak places with an economy no panicked man managed — had crossed the flats at night, when the surface hardened and held a print without swallowing it. She knew the desert. That was the trouble with this particular thief. Sable Corren, the warrant said. The paper was folded in his breast pocket, soft as cloth now from handling, and it named her crimes in a clerk's dry hand: theft of documents from the Meridian Survey Company, value unassessed; flight from custody at Duncarrow; suspected prior takings. No violence. The clerk had written *no violence* the way clerks wrote such things, as though it were a footnote rather than the whole of what Ivo needed to know.

He crouched at the edge of a salt pan and read the ground the way other men read letters. The prints were a day old here, maybe less — the wind had begun to round their edges but hadn't yet erased the crisp line of the heel. She traveled at night and slept in the day, and when she slept she chose her ground with care. Twice in the last three days he had found where she'd rested: a shallow scrape in the lee of a stone, no fire, no litter, nothing left behind but the faint impression of a body that had lain down for four hours and risen and gone on. She was rationing herself. She was good.

Ivo was better. It was the one thing about himself he had never doubted, and so it was the one thing he had built everything on, the way a man builds a house on a single stone when he can't find bedrock.

He stood, worked the stiffness out of his knees, and checked his compass without needing to. Northeast. The needle held steady on northeast, and beyond the flats, somewhere past the shimmer where the salt gave way to the first red shoulders of canyon country, lay the Rivergate — and beyond the Rivergate, the river itself, rising out of country where rangers had no jurisdiction and no business and no right to follow a thief. Everyone knew where the river came from. Everyone agreed not to know. That was the arrangement, older than the stations, older than the survey companies with their brass instruments and their hunger: the river rose where it rose, and no one went to see, and the water came down to the towns and the orchards and the mills, and everyone pretended this was the natural order of things and not a bargain struck in the dark by men long dead.

Ivo had been to the edge of that country once. Only once.

He didn't think about it. He was expert at not thinking about it — had made a discipline of it, the way he'd made a discipline of tracking, of rationing, of walking. He put the thought down the way he'd put down a heavy load at a rest stop, and told himself, as he always told himself, that he'd pick it back up when he needed to.

He never needed to.

* * *

The water was the problem, as it always was. He had cached two skins at the last well, a rusted windmill pump forty miles back that groaned like a dying animal but gave cold, mineral-heavy water. Now he had one skin half full and the flats stretched ahead of him, white and merciless, and the thief had a head start measured in days. He did the arithmetic without wanting to. Six days out. Two days back to the well if he turned around now. Four days, if the tracks held, to the edge of the flats and the first canyon water, which might be a seep or might be a bitter spring too mineral to drink. And past that — the Rivergate, and the end of his authority, and the beginning of something he had no warrant for.

He drank a mouthful, warm and tasting of the skin's canvas, and walked on.

The sun climbed and the salt threw light up at him from below, so that he was burned from above and below at once, lit like a man on a stage with no audience. He thought, not for the first time, about how strange it was that he had become the man who did this. Someone had to track the thieves and the runaways and the lost, someone had to read the ground and follow the nothing across the flats, and Ivo Eastwick had turned out to be the one, and no one had ever asked him whether he wanted it, and he had never thought to ask himself. The commission had come when he was nineteen, signed by a station chief he barely remembered, and he had signed the papers they put in front of him, including the one about the debt, and he had carried the debt ever since without once — not once in eleven years — sitting down to read it closely.

It lived in his pack, wrapped in oilcloth, in a tin that had once held tea. A paper of debt. His father's name on it, and his own beneath, and a sum that had never been explained to him in full, and a family name he knew only as a word on a page: *Corren*. Every month he sent a portion of his pay east to a solicitor in Duncarrow who sent back a receipt, and the receipt said the debt stood, and Ivo filed the receipt and rode out and read the ground and followed the nothing, and this was his life, and it fit him the way a well-worn boot fits — snug, and shaped to him, and impossible to say whether he had made it or it had made him.

The debt was the reason he took this warrant personally. That was what he told himself. A Corren had stolen from the Meridian Survey, and a Corren held his family's paper, and there was a neatness to it that felt almost like fate, and Ivo had learned to distrust neatness in the desert because neatness was usually the sun playing tricks. But he had taken the warrant anyway, before anyone could assign it, and he had ridden hard, and now he was walking, because the horse had gone lame at the flats' edge and he had turned it loose with the saddle at a line camp and gone on alone, because that was what he did. He went on alone. It was the one skill no one had taught him.

* * *

He found the locket on the seventh day, at the place where the salt began to fail.

The crust had thinned there, broken into plates and pans with red earth showing through the seams, and the thief's trail bent toward a low rise of dark stone — the first real rock in a hundred miles, a whale-back of basalt that offered shade in the afternoon and a view in every direction. She had camped there; he could see the scrape of her body-shelter behind the stone, and the small neat ring of stones where she had permitted herself a fire, one night only, small and hot and brief. Beside the fire ring, half sunk in the salt-melt where the pan had softened in some old rain, lay a locket.

It was brass, cheap, the kind sold at river-town fairs to girls with sweethearts. The hinge was broken. Inside, where a portrait would have been, there was instead a scrap of paper gone soft with damp and salt, and on the paper, in a careful, old-fashioned hand, a name and a date.

*Elias Corren. Beloved. Taken by the water.*

Ivo sat back on his heels and the flats were very quiet.

He knew the name. Of course he knew the name — it was on his papers, *Corren*, the family that held his debt, the family his father's failure had been paid to. But it was more than that, and the more-than-that rose in him slowly, like cold water coming up through boot leather. *Elias Corren.* He had heard that name spoken once, long ago, in a voice he had spent eleven years not remembering. A woman's voice, at the edge of the source country, saying a dead man's name over and over into the wind while a boy stood in the rocks and did nothing and then ran.

He had been a boy. He had been a boy and he had run, and afterward there had been men in good coats who spoke to his father, and papers, and a sum of money that became a debt, and the whole machinery of it had closed around the thing he had seen the way a bandage closes around a wound, and he had never — not once — lifted the bandage to look.

Now he was holding a dead man's locket in his hand, in the middle of the salt, and the dead man's daughter was a day ahead of him, and his warrant said *thief*, and the word sat wrong in his mouth for the first time.

He should have felt triumphant. The trail was certain now; the locket was hers, had to be hers, and it meant she had come this way and dropped the one thing she could not afford to lose, which meant she was tired, or hurried, or both. He should have felt the cold clean pleasure of a trail closing. Instead he felt the flats tilt, very slightly, the way the ground tilts in the last moment before a man understands he has been standing on the edge of something all along.

He wrapped the locket in a square of cloth and put it in his breast pocket, next to the warrant. The two papers lay against his chest together, the law and the name, and he could feel them there, small and hard, all the way down.

* * *

That night he made no fire either. He ate cold meal and dried meat in the lee of the basalt, in the shelter the thief had used, and he told himself it was for practical reasons — a light on the flats could be seen for miles, and she might double back, and he was a ranger and rangers did not waste advantages. All true. But there was another truth under it, one he did not look at directly: he did not want to sit across the dark from the shape of her fire and feel what he was feeling, which was not pursuit. It was something older and less nameable, a sense of walking a road someone had walked before him, of following not a thief but a story that had begun long before the warrant, long before the flats, back at the edge of the water where a boy had stood in the rocks with his heart going like a trapped bird.

The stars came out in their terrible multitudes. On the flats there was nothing to interrupt them, no ridge, no tree, no smoke, and they came down so close it seemed a man could drown in them. Ivo lay on his bedroll with his hands behind his head and did the thing he had not done in eleven years.

He took out the debt paper and read it.

By starlight, by the light of a match he let burn down to his fingers, he read it — every clause, every line, the clerk's hand and the solicitor's seal and the signatures at the bottom. His father's signature, shaky. His own, added later, a child's careful letters confirming an obligation he could not possibly have understood. And the sum, and the terms, and the phrase that recurred through it like a refrain: *in consideration of services rendered and silence maintained.*

*Silence maintained.*

He had read those words a hundred times and never seen them. They had been furniture, background, the wallpaper of his life. Now they stood up off the page and looked at him. Services rendered. Silence maintained. A boy had seen something at the edge of the source country. A man named Elias Corren had been taken by the water. And a family had been paid, and a boy had been bound, and the whole arrangement had been dressed in the respectable clothing of a debt so that everyone could go on pretending it was about money.

The match burned his fingers and he dropped it and the dark came back and he lay in it, wide awake, with the flats' silence pressing on him like a hand.

It didn't mean anything, he told himself. It was an old paper, badly drafted; solicitors wrote phrases like that into everything; it was how they were paid. Silence maintained could mean a hundred innocent things. His father had been a quiet man; perhaps he had witnessed some private matter, some boundary dispute, some — 

But the locket was in his breast pocket, and through the cloth he could feel its small weight against his chest, and the name on the scrap of paper inside it was the name on his debt, and a woman's voice eleven years dead was saying *Elias, Elias* into the wind, and Ivo Eastwick, who could read a day-old print in crusted salt and tell you the weight and the mood of the man who made it, could not read his own life, and knew it, and had always known it, and had made a discipline of not knowing.

He slept badly. In his sleep there was water, and rocks, and a boy running, and he woke before dawn with his heart going hard and the flats gray and still around him, and the thief's trail leading away northeast, toward the Rivergate, toward the water, toward the country where his warrant ended and his debt began.

He rose, and shook the salt from his bedroll, and drank the last of his water, and stood for a moment facing northeast with the compass in his hand. The needle held. It always held. That was the joke of it, he thought — that he had carried a compass all these years and never once asked himself where he was going, only which way was north.

Then he shouldered his pack and walked on, following a thief across the weight of nothing, and by noon the salt had given out beneath his boots, and the red rock of the canyon country stood up ahead of him like a wall, and in a fold of that wall, far off, he caught the flash of something moving — a figure, small with distance, climbing steadily toward the Rivergate, not running anymore, not hiding, just going, as if she knew he was there and had decided it no longer mattered.

Ivo stood at the edge of the flats with the locket against his chest and the debt in his pack and his warrant in his pocket, and he understood, with the flat certainty of the ground under his feet, that whatever waited for him in the Rivergate, it was not going to be an arrest.
