## 2. Tracks in Salt

The thief turned south on the third morning.

Ivo Eastwick read it in the salt the way other men read a newspaper — without hurry, without doubt. The boot prints that had run westward along the flat's northern margin for two days now bent toward a line of low mesas on the horizon, and beyond them, though nothing on the ground said so yet, lay the Rivergate.

He crouched over the tracks with his hat brim shading them and let himself feel the small cold satisfaction of competence. That was the thing about the flats: they kept everything. Rain came maybe four times a year, wind came every day, but wind only moved what was loose, and a boot print pressed into sun-hardened alkali was not loose. It was a signature written on stone that would last until the next storm, and no storm was coming. He could see it in the sky's hard blue indifference.

She had stopped here. That was the first interesting thing.

Not slowed — stopped. Both feet planted, weight even, facing south toward the mesas. Then she had stood for a while, because around the prints there were the faint twin dimples of her heels settling as she shifted her weight from one leg to the other, once, twice, three times. A person waiting. A person thinking.

Ivo straightened and looked where she had looked. The mesas sat on the horizon like sleeping animals, red-brown shoulders dusted white at the base where the salt crept up their flanks. Behind them, sixty miles as the crow flew and a hundred and twenty as the river wound, was the Rivergate: the canyon country where the great river rose out of rock no one was permitted to see, and where the rangers' writ ended at a surveyed line drawn on maps by men who had never walked it.

His warrant was paper. Paper stopped at that line.

He took out the compass anyway, out of habit, and checked it against the sun, and put it away again. South. Of course south. Everything in this country went south eventually; the river did, the birds did, the water under the salt did. He wondered if the thief knew she was walking along the grain of the world or whether she'd simply picked the direction that looked most like escape.

Then he shouldered his pack and followed.

* * *

By midday the salt had begun to give way to sand and broken shale, and the tracking changed character. On the flats he could have followed her at a trot. Here he had to work — reading scuffed stone, a displaced pebble, the way a desert holly's branches were bent back where a body had pushed through. She was good. He'd known that since the second day, when he'd realized she was pacing herself to leave the shallowest possible prints, rolling heel to toe like a man trained to walk soft, except she wasn't a man and the stride gave her away every time. Shorter than a man's. Quicker. And always, always economical — she never wandered, never doubled back, never wasted a step on indecision.

Except this morning. This morning she had stood still long enough to sink her heels into the alkali, staring south.

He found the reason for it an hour past noon, in the shadow of the first mesa where the trail cut between two fallen slabs of rock.

A glint. Small, at the edge of the track, half-buried where something had been dropped and then covered by a skitter of sand driven off the slab above. If he hadn't been reading the ground foot by foot he would have walked past it. He knelt, brushed the sand away with one finger, and lifted out a locket.

Silver, worn nearly smooth, dented along one edge as if it had taken a fall sometime in its long life. The chain was broken — snapped clean, not pulled, which meant it had been hanging outside her collar where it could catch on things, which meant she wore it where she could reach it, which meant losing it would be like losing a tooth. He turned it over. No engraving on the back. The front clasp was stiff with age and grit, and he worked it open with his thumbnail, careful, the way you open anything that belongs to grief.

Inside were two portraits, the old kind, painted in miniature on ivory behind glass. On the left, a woman with dark hair and a level gaze, the paint faded to sepia at the edges. On the right, a man — younger than Ivo expected, thirty perhaps, with a lean face and eyes that looked slightly past the painter, toward something over his shoulder.

And beneath the man's portrait, scratched into the ivory with a fine point, in a hand that had gone over each letter twice to make it permanent:

**T. CORREN**

Ivo did not move for a long time.

The wind came down off the mesa and stirred the grit around his knees and made the locket's broken chain tick softly against his knuckle, and he stayed kneeling in the thief's shadow like a man praying to the wrong god.

Corren. He knew the name the way you know a scar — not by looking at it, by wearing it. It lived folded inside his shirt pocket in a packet of papers he had carried for eleven years: a debt instrument, signed before witnesses in a station office that had since burned down, binding Ivo Eastwick, ranger, to pay to the family of Tomas Corren, deceased, a sum specified, on terms specified, in perpetuity if necessary. Eleven years of wages, tithed. Eleven years of a life arranged around an obligation like a tree growing around a fence wire.

He had never questioned it. That was the honest truth of it, and the truth arrived now with the locket open in his hand and hit him harder than any blow he'd taken on duty. He had never questioned it because questioning it would have meant opening the box he'd built around that night at the edge of the source country, and the box was load-bearing. Take it out and who knew what else came down.

But the name was the same. Not similar. The same. T. Corren. Tomas Corren, drowned man, dead father, and here was his face painted in miniature, carried against a thief's skin across two hundred miles of empty country.

Sable Corren. The warrant said Sable Corren, wanted for theft of documents and monies from the Meridian Survey Company, and Ivo had memorized the description the way he memorized all of them — height, hair, distinguishing marks — without ever letting the surname land anywhere. Names were handles. You didn't grip the handle too hard or you felt what was attached to it.

Now he was gripping it, and his hand wouldn't close properly.

* * *

He made camp early, in the lee of the mesa, and told himself it was because the light was going flat and the trail would keep till morning. That was true. It was also true that his hands weren't steady and he didn't want to lose the trail through carelessness, and it was also true — the deepest true, the one underneath — that he didn't want to catch her today.

He boiled coffee over a smokeless fire of dry greasewood and sat with the locket on his knee, turning it in the last light.

Think it through, he told himself, in the voice of every training officer he'd ever had. Coincidence is a lazy investigator's conclusion. So: possibilities. One — the locket belonged to someone else and she stole it too, and the name meant nothing. But she'd carried it against her skin, chain worn bright at the break point, and thieves didn't wear what they stole; they buried it, sold it, mailed it ahead. Two — the name was common enough. Corren. Maybe. He'd met two Correns in his life and both had been related to each other. Three —

Three. She was the daughter. The daughter of the man whose death had bought Ivo's debt, walking south toward the Rivergate with survey company documents in her pack, and Ivo Eastwick following her with a warrant in his pocket and eleven years of unexamined obligation in his chest, and somewhere behind all of it, at the bottom of everything, a night beside black water that he had never once allowed himself to look at directly.

He closed the locket. Opened it again. Looked at the man's face.

He didn't remember Tomas Corren's face. That was the thing that shamed him most, when he finally let the thought surface and float there where he could see it. He remembered the night in fragments — lantern light on moving water, shouting, the terrible specific sound of a body going under a current that did not slow — but the man himself, alive, the man whose family Ivo had been paying for a decade, was a blank. He had signed the debt papers three weeks later in a state he could only describe now, from this distance, as sleepwalking. Someone had put the papers in front of him. Someone had explained the terms. He had signed because signing was the only action available that felt like anything at all, and he had been carrying the papers ever since the way you carry a stone in your boot: painfully, constantly, and after enough years, invisibly.

Who had put the papers in front of him?

He tried to remember and found, with a small cold shock, that he couldn't. There had been men — Corren family men, he'd assumed, grieving relatives with business to settle — but faces wouldn't come. Only a voice, maybe. Or not even a voice. A sense of being handled gently by people who wanted something from him and knew exactly how to take it from a boy half-dead with shock.

The fire cracked. Somewhere out on the flats a coyote began its evening argument with the sky.

You're tired, he told himself. You're building ghosts out of bad light. The debt is the debt. The warrant is the warrant. In the morning you'll pick up her trail, and in three days you'll have her, and you'll bring her back across the line, and the courts will do what courts do, and none of it is yours to carry beyond that.

It sounded correct. It sounded like every rule he had lived by for eleven years. And for the first time since he'd pinned on the ranger's badge, it sounded thin — a wall with wind whistling through it.

Because here was the question he couldn't put down, now that it was out: why was she running *south*? South was the Rivergate. South was the one direction in this whole territory where a warrant died at a surveyed line. Any thief with sense ran for a city, for a rail head, for a crowd to disappear into. She had instead spent two days crossing open salt in plain view of anyone with a spyglass, moving with the deliberation of a woman who knew exactly where she was going and why — and the place she was going was the same place the Meridian Survey had gone silent, the same place her father had died, the same place Ivo had seen whatever it was he'd seen and buried so deep that digging for it now was like probing a numb tooth.

The survey. He turned that over too. The Meridian Survey Company, chartered to map the upper river, well-funded, well-armed, and lately — if the station gossip that drifted downriver with the boat traffic was worth anything — asking questions in the towns about land titles and water rights that made old-timers go quiet. And a clerk-thief runs out of their offices with a satchel of their documents, and nine days later the survey stops answering its radio, and the thief's trail bends south toward all of it like iron filings toward a magnet.

None of that was coincidence either. He could feel the shape of something under the salt, big and patient, the way you can feel a buried river under a dry wash by the way the plants grow.

He banked the fire and lay back on his bedroll with the locket in his closed fist, and slept badly, and dreamed of water where no water was.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of hammered tin. He broke camp fast, drank the coffee cold, and stood over the trail where it left the mesa's shadow and ran on south.

Her prints from yesterday afternoon were crisp, unhurried. She'd made camp somewhere ahead — he'd find the ash circle by noon. She wasn't hurrying because she didn't think she was being followed, or because she didn't care, and after last night he suspected the second. A woman who paced herself like that across open salt, who left a trail a child could read, was either arrogant or leaving a message. He wasn't sure which possibility he liked less.

Two miles on, the country changed. The salt gave out entirely and the land lifted into broken red country of gullies and juniper, and the Rivergate announced itself the way it announced itself to everyone: not by sight but by sound. Faint, below hearing almost, a sustained low note under the morning wind — the river working through rock far down and far ahead, the pulse of the only great water in a thousand miles of thirst.

Ivo stopped on a rise and looked south. From here, on clear mornings, you could see the Gate itself: the canyon's mouth, a black seam in the earth where the red country simply opened and swallowed the river, walls rising sheer for half a mile. Even at this distance it looked less like geography than like architecture. Something built. Something with a purpose.

Rangers don't go past the line. That was drilled into every recruit with the rest of the liturgy. The source country belonged to its own law — old compacts, older families, agreements signed before the territory had a name. The rangers' charter stopped at the surveyed boundary, and every ranger learned to recite the reasons: jurisdiction, treaty, the impossibility of supply, the number of good men lost before the line was drawn. All sensible. All true.

And underneath all of it, unspoken in every briefing he'd ever sat through, a simpler reason, passed along in the tone of voice rather than the words: *the people in there don't want us, and the people above us don't want us to want it.*

He took out the warrant and read it again in the full morning light, the way he should have done on the first day. *Sable Corren. Theft of documents and monies, Meridian Survey Company. Apprehend and return for examination.* Signed by a magistrate in the territorial capital. Funded, if you followed the money two steps back — and he'd never once followed it two steps back — by the Meridian Survey Company itself.

The company whose expedition had gone silent. The company whose documents the woman was carrying south. The company that had, eleven years ago, held land options and water filings across half the upper basin, before the old compacts had frozen them out.

He folded the warrant away. His hands were steady now. Whatever had cracked in him yesterday at the mesa's shadow had not healed overnight, but it had settled into something more useful than doubt: attention. The particular total attention of a tracker who has realized the track he's following might be leading somewhere he's afraid of.

He touched his shirt pocket, where the debt papers rode against his heart like a second badge, and started down off the rise.

An hour later he found her night camp in a shallow draw — cold ashes, carefully scattered, a bedroll's ghost pressed into the sand. And beside the ashes, placed deliberately on a flat stone where no one could miss it, a single item, weighted down against the wind with a smaller stone:

One dried fig. Cut precisely in half.

He stood over it for a long moment. Then he looked up the draw, south, toward the sound of the river, at the empty country where a woman who knew she was followed had left him a piece of fruit and a choice.

The fig was sweet. It tasted like a country he used to know.
