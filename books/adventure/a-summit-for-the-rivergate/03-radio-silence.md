## 3. Radio Silence

The Rivergate station sat where the river left the canyon and the canyon left the desert — a squat stone building with a tin roof gone rust-red, a corral of sun-bleached posts, and a radio mast that leaned east like a man listening hard for something he didn't want to hear.

Ivo had been here twice before in his eleven years as a ranger. Both times it had been to file reports and resupply, both times in winter, when the river ran low and green and honest. Now it was late in the dry season, the water was down to a brown whisper over its gravel bars, and the station looked smaller than he remembered. Smaller, and more like a trap that hadn't finished closing.

He'd followed Sable Corren's trail to within a day of the gate and then lost it — deliberately or not, he couldn't say. The tracks simply ended on a slab of sandstone above a side wash, as if she'd stepped off the edge of the world. He'd spent half a day circling, reading the stone, reading the wind, finding nothing but his own frustration reflected back at him. In the end he'd come on to the station because there was nowhere else to go, because his water was low, and because a ranger who loses his quarry is still a ranger, even if he feels like something less.

The duty man at the corral fence was young, freckled, and visibly relieved to see anyone at all.

"Eastwick? They said you might come through. Chief's been asking after you since yesterday." He took Ivo's horse without being asked, which told Ivo how badly they wanted him inside. "You'll want to hear this before you eat. Trust me."

"Hear what?"

The boy just shook his head and led the horse away.

* * *

Chief Aldous Renner was not a large man, but he had the gift of filling whatever room he stood in, the way water fills a basin regardless of its shape. He was behind his desk when Ivo came in, and on the desk, among the maps and the coffee cup and the brass weights, sat a radio log book, open, its last pages crowded with entries in three different hands.

Renner didn't offer a chair. That, too, was a message.

"Eastwick. Good. Sit down anyway." He waved at the stool across from him, and Ivo sat, and Renner studied him for a moment with pale eyes that had spent forty years squinting into canyon light. "You were tracking someone. Corren. The thief out of Meridian County. Where'd you lose her?"

"Sandstone bench above Redman Wash. Tracks stopped clean."

"Stopped, or ended?"

Ivo held the chief's gaze. "There's a difference?"

"There usually is, with that family." Renner said it flatly, without malice, the way a man reads a weather report, and Ivo felt something shift under his ribs — small, cold, quick. *That family.* As if the name meant something here that it hadn't meant on the flats. He filed it away and said nothing.

"You know her, then," Ivo said.

"I know the Correns. Everyone upriver knows the Correns." Renner turned the log book around and pushed it across the desk. "But that's not why you're here. Read the last entry. Then read the nine before it."

The entries were short. Radio checks, logged by time and operator initials. The first eight were routine: *Meridian Survey, Station calling, no response. Meridian Survey, Station calling, no response.* Nine days of them, each initialed by a different hand, each one a little shorter than the last, until the final entry, made that morning, which said only:

*Day 9. No contact. Requesting guidance from District.*

"They went silent nine days ago?" Ivo asked.

"They went quiet nine days ago. There's a difference there too." Renner leaned back. "Meridian Survey. Twelve souls, maybe fourteen with porters. Hydrographic expedition, chartered out of the capital, licensed to run the upper river — soundings, flow rates, mapping the gorge country above the Narrows. Well-funded. New equipment. A radio set worth more than this whole station." He tapped the log. "They checked in every evening at sundown like church bells. Then, nine days ago, nothing. Not static, Eastwick. Nothing. The frequency's clear. Either their set is dead, or they're not turning it on, or—"

"Or there's no one left to turn it on."

"I was going to say *or they don't want to be found,*" Renner said, "but yes. That too."

Ivo looked at the log again. Nine days. In canyon country in the dry season, nine days was a long time. Long enough for a rope to fray, a cistern to crack, a disagreement to turn fatal. Long enough for a flash flood to come down a side drainage and take a camp off the face of the earth, leaving nothing behind but silt and silence.

"What's District say?"

"District says send someone." Renner's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "District always says send someone. The problem is who. My rangers are valley men. Brannigan's never been above the second fork. Delgado broke his ankle in June and walks like a crab. You—" and here Renner pointed one blunt finger at Ivo, and Ivo felt the trap click shut around his ankle, gentle as a sprung jaw, "—you ran supply routes through the upper canyons for four years. You've been above the Narrows. You're the only man on my roster who knows that country well enough to move fast in it."

"I'm not on your roster," Ivo said. "I'm Meridian County. This isn't my jurisdiction. The Rivergate's outside every jurisdiction — that's the whole point of it."

"The whole point of it used to be that nobody needed jurisdiction up there, because nobody went." Renner pulled a paper from under the maps and slid it across. It was typed, official, bearing a district seal Ivo recognized and a signature he didn't. "Things change. There's money upriver now. Money means paperwork, paperwork means authority, and authority means somebody has to go find out why fourteen people stopped talking. You're holding a warrant for a fugitive whose trail runs straight up that same river. So as far as I'm concerned, Eastwick, your jurisdiction starts wherever her tracks do — and hers, if you ask me, are tangled up in this somewhere."

Ivo stared at the paper without reading it. His mind was doing something it did rarely and badly: arguing with itself.

Because the chief was right, and that was the worst part. Sable Corren's trail had run south along the river corridor for two hundred miles, and now it had vanished on stone above a wash that drained toward the upper canyons. And here was an expedition gone silent in exactly that country, at exactly that time. Coincidence was a word rangers learned early to stop believing in. Country didn't deal in coincidence. Country dealt in convergence — water finding water, tracks finding tracks, trouble finding trouble.

And underneath all of it, quieter than any of it, was the other thing. The thing he carried folded in oilcloth at the bottom of his saddlebag, next to his mother's compass: a paper of debt, signed when he was nine years old, binding him to the family of a drowned man. A Corren debt. He had never once, in twenty years, let himself think about what it would mean to walk back into the country where that paper had been signed.

Now the country was asking him in.

"Who else is going?" he asked, which was as good as saying yes, and both of them knew it.

"A survey recovery party's already assembling at the last boat landing. Captain Anselm Voss, second-in-command of the Meridian operation — he was downriver resupplying when the silence started. He's got boats, gear, and a district mandate. What he doesn't have is anyone who knows the water above the Narrows, and the Narrows is where any search has to start." Renner stood, which ended the meeting the way standing always does. "You leave tomorrow at first light. Voss will brief you at the landing. Your warrant stays valid — you find your thief, you take her. You find the survey, you report. Both jobs, one river. Efficient."

"Efficient," Ivo repeated. The word tasted like the salt flats: flat, white, empty.

Renner paused at the window, looking out at the leaning mast. For a moment the chief looked older than his room could hold.

"For what it's worth," he said, not turning around, "I've read the Corren file. All of it. Her father drowned upriver when she was a child — some kind of accident at the source boundary, before your time. She grew up in those terraces. If she's gone to ground anywhere, it's up there, in country she knows better than any map we own." He turned then, and his pale eyes were unreadable. "So maybe it's fitting that the man chasing her is the only one who can follow."

* * *

That evening Ivo sat on the station's front step with a plate of beans going cold in his hands and watched the light die on the western rim.

The canyon walls across the river had gone the color of old blood, then ash, then nothing. Somewhere up there, past the darkening stone, was the source country — the springs where the great river rose from rock no one was permitted to see. Every child in the basin grew up on the story: the river was born in the deep canyon and no man should look upon its birth, and the law said so, and the law had said so so long that nobody remembered whether the prohibition was sacred or merely bureaucratic. Rangers patrolled the boundary. Maps showed blank space above the last surveyed bend, marked with a single printed line: *SOURCE COUNTRY — ENTRY PROHIBITED.*

He had been to the edge of it once. Once, as a boy. And everything that had happened since — the debt, the badge, the long gray years of competent, hollow service — had flowed downstream from that one day like water from a wound.

He ate the beans without tasting them. Then he went to the corral, where his horse stood drowsing, and dug into the saddlebag, and took out the oilcloth packet, and unwrapped it by feel in the dark.

The compass first. Brass, dented, older than he was. It had belonged to his mother, and it pointed north the way she had pointed him: steadily, silently, without explanation.

Then the paper. He didn't need light to read it; he had read it so many times the words lived behind his eyes.

*Ivo Eastwick, aged nine years, being indebted to the family of Tomas Corren, deceased, for services rendered and losses borne, agrees to payment in labor and loyalty until such debt is discharged...*

Signed by a magistrate whose name meant nothing. Witnessed by men he couldn't remember. He had been nine. He had signed where they told him to sign, because adults had been crying, and because the alternative — understanding what had actually happened at the water's edge that day — was more than a nine-year-old could carry.

Twenty years later he still carried it. He had built a life on top of it the way the desert builds soil on rock: thinly, patiently, without ever pretending the rock wasn't there.

*Tomas Corren.* And now: *Sable Corren.* The same name on his debt papers and his warrant, riding the same river, pulling him in the same direction. He had told himself for two hundred miles that it was chance. Sitting in the dark at the Rivergate station, with nine days of radio silence stacked on the desk behind him and a conscription order in his pocket, he found the story harder to hold.

Country didn't deal in coincidence.

* * *

He was up before dawn, and the boat landing was already awake.

It lay two miles below the station, where a gravel shelf made the last reasonable place to launch before the current stiffened. Two flat-bottomed survey boats sat in the shallows, loaded to the gunwales — crates, coiled line, canvas bundles, a second radio set lashed under tarpaulin. Men moved among them in the gray light, breathing steam, speaking in the low clipped voices of people who have already argued about everything and agreed about nothing.

A tall man stood at the water's edge consulting a sheaf of papers by lantern light. He wore a survey officer's coat with the insignia picked off, and he had the look of a man assembled entirely from straight lines — straight back, straight shoulders, a straight part in iron-gray hair. When Ivo came down the bank, the man looked up without smiling.

"Eastwick. The ranger." Not a question. "Anselm Voss. You're late."

"It's not light yet."

"Light is a courtesy the sun extends to farmers." Voss folded his papers away. "We launch in twenty minutes. Can you handle a boat?"

"In this water, yes."

"Good. Because the water won't stay like this. Dry season's peaking. Every day we wait, the river drops another inch, and there's a stretch above the Narrows that goes unnavigable by cartography's estimate in—" he consulted a page he no longer needed, having clearly memorized it, "—nineteen days. Possibly fewer. After that, anything above the shallows is there until the rains. Which means whatever we're going to do upriver, we do it quickly, or we do it on foot, and I did not charter those boats to carry them home empty."

Ivo looked at the boats, the crates, the cold-faced crew. Fourteen people missing, and this man talked like a schedule.

"How many are you?"

"Six, counting you. Myself, two boatmen, a signaler, a porter, and your expertise." Voss's eyes went over Ivo's worn gear, his dust-caked coat, and found it adequate in the way a ledger finds a column adequate. "The rest of the party will follow if we establish a base. Tell me about the Narrows. The men who've run it say it can't be run loaded."

"The men who say that haven't run it with me." Ivo crouched at the waterline, wet a finger, and held it up. The air was moving faintly from the south — from upriver, from the canyon. "Wind's wrong today. We'll want to reach the mouth by afternoon or we'll be rowing against it."

Voss raised an eyebrow a fraction of a degree, which Ivo would learn was the man's entire vocabulary of approval. "Then we launch now."

They pushed off into the brown current as the sun cleared the eastern rim and set the canyon walls burning. The station shrank behind them — the tin roof, the leaning mast, the young duty man raising one hand in a wave that might have been farewell or sympathy. Ahead, the river bent north into country Ivo had sworn, in the way men swear things they know they'll break, never to see again.

The current took the boats. It always had, he thought. It had taken him when he was nine years old, and it had never given him back — it had only carried him, year after year, to whatever place the water decided.

He put his hand on the tiller and steered them upstream anyway.

Behind them, far to the south, a hawk circled once over the landing and flew on. And somewhere ahead — above the Narrows, above the blank space on the maps, in the silence where fourteen voices had stopped — the river ran on toward its secret birth, carrying whatever had happened there the way it carried everything:

patiently, and toward the sea.
