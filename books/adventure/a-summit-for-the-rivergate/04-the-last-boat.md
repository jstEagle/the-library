## 4. The Last Boat

The Rivergate station smelled of lamp oil and wet rope, and by the time the sun cleared the eastern rim Ivo had already counted the boats twice.

Three of them, lashed to the dock pilings below the station's back stairs: flat-bottomed survey skiffs with shallow drafts and patched hulls, the kind built for water that changed its mind. They rode low against the current, which came down out of the canyon country in long green ropes, cold even in this heat, moving with the patience of something that had been going downhill since before there were names for it.

Nine days of silence from upriver. That was what the station chief had told him last night over a tin cup of coffee that tasted like boiled bark. Nine days, and no word from the Meridian Survey.

"You're Eastwick." The voice came from behind him, clipped and dry as a file drawn across iron.

Ivo turned. The man on the stairs was fifty or so, lean in the way of someone who'd spent decades rationing himself — rationing food, sleep, sentiment. He wore a surveyor's coat with the brass buttons polished and a canvas satchel slung across his chest like a bandolier. His eyes were pale gray and went straight past Ivo's face to the boats, counting them too, apparently finding the number correct.

"Captain Anselm Voss," the man said, though he offered no hand. "Meridian Survey. You're the ranger who knows the upper canyons."

"I know some of them."

"The chief says all of them." Voss's gaze finally settled on Ivo and held there, weighing. "He also says you were conscripted under protest. I don't care about your protest. I care whether you can read water."

"I can read water."

"Then we leave within the hour."

Voss turned back toward the station without waiting for an answer, which told Ivo most of what he needed to know about the man. He'd met his kind before — men who loved the country the way a moneylender loved collateral. Voss would look at the canyons and see blank spaces on a map, and every blank space would itch at him like an unpaid account. People, Ivo suspected, were figures Voss entered into columns and occasionally lost.

He looked at the boats again. Three skiffs. Against that current, loaded, in dry season, when the river ran thin but fast through the Narrows where the walls came together so close a thrown stone could ring off both sides.

*You could still walk away,* said a small voice at the back of his skull. *Rangers have no jurisdiction above the gate. Let the Survey drown in its own silence.*

But the chief had made it official last night, stamping the paper with the particular satisfaction of a man transferring a problem downstream — or in this case, upstream. And besides, Ivo thought, turning the compass in his pocket, there was the other thing. The thing he hadn't said aloud to anyone.

Sable Corren's trail had gone into the Rivergate. And now the Survey had gone silent in the same direction. He didn't believe in coincidence that neat. He believed in threads, and two of them had just run into the same dark hole in the earth.

* * *

They loaded the boats in the gray-gold light of early morning, and Ivo used the work to take the measure of the party.

There was Wren Halloway, the cartographer — a compact woman of thirty-odd with ink-stained fingers and a way of looking at terrain as though she were already drawing it. She carried her instruments wrapped in oilcloth like a mother carries an infant, and when one of the porters set a crate down hard she flinched as if the sound had struck her personally.

There were four porters, rivermen from the lower towns, hired for the run: big, quiet men named Odo, Pell, Marsh, and a younger one called Finch who laughed too easily and would probably stop laughing somewhere around the Narrows. Ivo had seen that pattern before. Everyone laughed at the start of a river trip. The river decided who was still laughing at the end.

And there was Captain Anselm Voss, directing everything, touching nothing, standing on the dock with his charts spread on a folding table weighted with stones.

"Eastwick." Voss didn't look up. "Come here. Tell me if this matches your knowledge."

Ivo leaned over the table. The chart was beautiful — fine linework, careful soundings, elevation hatching done by a master hand. It was also wrong. Not badly wrong, not obviously wrong, but wrong in the way that mattered: the upper reaches beyond the second confluence thinned out into guesswork dressed up as certainty. Contour lines marched confidently across country where no surveyor had ever stood. A side canyon Ivo knew well — a place called the Throat, where Sable Corren's people had once kept their summer camps — appeared on Voss's map as a gentle tributary valley.

"Where did you get the upper section?" Ivo asked.

"Meridian archives. Twenty-year-old reconnaissance." Voss tapped the paper. "It's sufficient."

"It's fiction." Ivo straightened. "Whoever drew that never went past the second confluence. Past there the river drops through the Narrows and the walls are sheer for eleven miles. There's no bank to walk, no bench to portage. If your missing expedition went above the Narrows, they either floated through or they climbed, and neither is something you plan for off a twenty-year-old sketch."

Voss's jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. "Then it's fortunate," he said, "that the Survey retained a guide who claims to know better." He rolled up the chart with crisp economy. "You'll correct my map as we go. In pencil. Nothing goes in ink until it's verified."

"That's the first sensible thing anyone's said at this station."

Something flickered across Voss's face — not quite amusement, but its distant cousin. "Halloway," he called. "You'll share a boat with Eastwick. Keep his corrections honest."

Wren Halloway looked up from her instrument case and studied Ivo with frank, unembarrassed curiosity, the way she might study an unusual rock formation. "You've been above the second confluence," she said. It wasn't a question.

"When I was younger."

"And?"

"And it doesn't want visitors." He picked up a crate of hardtack and carried it down to the boats, ending the conversation the way he preferred to end most conversations.

But she followed him onto the gravel anyway, falling into step. "Nine days of silence," she said quietly. "Radio works fine down here. We've tested it twice this morning. Either their set broke, or they turned it off, or—" She stopped herself.

"Or there's no one left to speak into it."

"I was going to say *or they don't want to be found.*" Her mouth twisted. "But yes. That too."

Ivo glanced at her. Most people filled silences with noise. This one seemed to spend them doing arithmetic. "Which do you think it is?" he asked.

She was quiet for three boat-lengths of walking. "I think," she said finally, "that the Meridian Survey didn't come up here to measure a river. Rivers get measured every year. Somebody paid for a full expedition — twelve people, four months' provisioning, a radio set worth more than this station — to go into country the law says nobody's supposed to enter." She shook her head slowly. "Somebody wanted something up there badly enough to pay for it. And nine days ago, whatever they wanted either killed them or finished with them."

"You've got a suspicious mind, Halloway."

"I've got a suspicious employer." She said it lightly, but her eyes didn't. Then she turned away to check her instruments, and the conversation was over whether Ivo liked it or not.

He stood a moment on the gravel, watching the current slide past the dock, green and cold and utterly indifferent. *Somebody wanted something up there.* He touched the folded papers in his breast pocket — the debt, the numbers, the name. *Corren.* It sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. He had carried it so long he'd stopped feeling its weight, the way a man stops feeling a boot that fits. But lately the boot had been pinching.

* * *

They launched at midmorning, three skiffs in line, poles and oars against a current that did not care.

Ivo took the bow of the lead boat because that was where a guide belonged, reading the water ahead and calling the lines. Voss took the stern of the same boat, ostensibly steering, actually charting — his pencil moving constantly, recording distances, times, bearings, as though the expedition were already a report being written. Wren sat amidships between them with her instruments boxed at her feet, and the porter Odo handled the middle pole with the unhurried competence of a man who had grown up on this water and expected to die on it, many years from now, of old age, preferably in a chair.

The first miles were easy. The river ran wide here below the gate, braiding around gravel bars, and the boats made good time with the current arguing but not fighting. The canyon walls rose gradually on either side, red sandstone streaked with black desert varnish, and the morning sun came down the corridor in shafts that turned the dust of their passage to gold.

Ivo called the lines — *left of the snag, hard right past the bar, watch the eddy line* — and the boats obeyed, and for a while there was nothing in the world but water and rock and the rhythm of the poles. It was the closest thing to peace his trade allowed, and he took it greedily, knowing how little of it lay ahead.

Voss, naturally, could not let it be peace.

"Eastwick. Distance to the Narrows?"

"Two days, if the water holds. Three if we lose a boat."

"We won't lose a boat."

"Everyone says that on the first day."

Voss's pencil paused. "You have a talent for encouragement."

"I have a talent for accuracy. Encouragement is somebody else's department." Ivo planted his pole and swung the bow around a submerged rock that showed itself only as a long smooth V in the current. "How many in the missing party?"

"Twelve. Chief surveyor Halvard Brann and eleven others, including two assistants, a cook, six field hands, and a doctor."

"A doctor. For a mapping expedition."

"For an *expedition*," Voss corrected, and Ivo heard the wall come down behind the words. "The Meridian's provisioning is not your concern."

"Everything about this expedition is my concern. I'm the one who has to find them."

"Then find them efficiently." Voss returned to his chart. But Ivo noticed he'd stopped writing, and that his pale eyes had gone upriver, fixed on the darkening gorge ahead, and stayed there longer than any map required.

* * *

They camped that night on a sand shelf beneath a wall so tall the stars didn't reach the bottom of the sky until well after dark. The river talked to itself all night, low and continuous, a sound like a crowd murmuring in another room. Ivo took first watch because he always took first watch, sitting on a rock above the boats with the compass open in his palm, its needle steady, pointing north into country that grew less mapped by the mile.

Footsteps on gravel. Wren Halloway lowered herself onto the next rock, uninvited, a tin cup steaming in both hands. She poured a second cup from the pot at her feet and held it out. After a moment, Ivo took it. It was terrible coffee. It was also hot.

"You're awake," he said.

"I'm always awake. It's a professional condition." She blew on her cup. "Can I ask you something, ranger? And you can refuse to answer. Everyone does."

"Ask."

"The thief you were tracking. Before the chief conscripted you. The one whose trail runs up this same river." She watched the firelight across the camp, not watching him. "Is it a coincidence?"

Ivo drank the terrible coffee and considered lying. Lying was easy; he'd done it professionally for years, in reports and testimony and the quiet omissions a badge permitted. But there was something about a woman who did arithmetic in silences that made lies feel like wasted material.

"No," he said. "I don't think it is."

"Thought not." She nodded slowly, as if confirming a calculation. "The Survey's financier sent a man ahead of the expedition, months back. A fixer. He came through the station asking questions about the upper canyons — who traveled there, who lived there, who'd object to traffic." She paused. "There was a family living up there once. Terraces, above the third confluence. They kept goats and orchards and they'd been there longer than the towns downstream. The fixer asked about them specifically."

Ivo's hands went very still around the tin cup. "What family?"

"Had a name." Wren frowned, reaching for it. "Corren. Something like that. Old river family. They're gone now — years gone. Nobody at the station would say much about why."

The river murmured. Somewhere upstream, a night bird called once and got no answer. Ivo looked at the compass needle trembling faintly in his palm, pointing north, pointing up, pointing at everything he had spent ten years not thinking about.

"Why are you telling me this?" he asked.

Wren shrugged, a small tight motion. "Because you read water, and you read maps, and you read people, and you pretend you don't read anything. Because Captain Voss wants the blanks on his chart filled in and doesn't care what fills them. And because I've started to suspect that when this expedition finds what it was sent to find, it isn't going to be good news for anybody downstream." She stood, brushing sand from her trousers. "So I'm collecting facts while facts are cheap. That's all."

She walked back toward her bedroll. Ivo sat alone with the compass and the cold growing out of the canyon walls, and turned the name over in his mind like a coin he suspected was counterfeit.

*Corren.*

A dead father. A debt signed in a boy's shaking hand. A daughter who became a thief. And now a financier's fixer asking questions about the family by name, months before anyone went missing.

Threads. All of them running into the same dark hole in the earth.

* * *

They launched again before dawn, into mist.

The river narrowed as the second day wore on. The walls drew closer, taller, older; the light came down in narrower blades; the current quickened from a walk to a march. By afternoon the gravel bars were gone and the water ran clean and green over drowned boulders, and Ivo stood in the bow calling lines in a voice pitched to carry, and the boats threaded the gaps with inches to spare.

Toward evening the canyon closed its fist. Ahead, the walls converged until they seemed to touch, and the river poured between them in a single smooth rushing lane, and the air coming out of that gap was cold as well water.

"The Narrows," Ivo said.

Behind him, he heard Voss's pencil stop.

Eleven miles of sheer stone, no banks, no benches, no way out except through. Eleven miles of water that would not forgive. Ivo looked at the gap and felt the old fear rise in him, familiar as a scar, and beneath the fear something else — the sense, sudden and total, that everything that mattered waited on the far side of those walls. The missing surveyors. The thief. The truth about a paper he'd carried half his life.

"Make fast tonight," he called back to the porters. "We run it at first light, all three boats, full crew, no cargo loose. Anyone who wants to pray, pray tonight."

Finch laughed. Nobody else did.

That night Ivo sat his watch again, and near midnight he heard footsteps and braced for Wren and her arithmetic — but it was Voss who came and stood beside him at the water's edge, charts forgotten, hands clasped behind his back like a man at a funeral.

"Brann is down there," Voss said quietly. It was the first time he'd used the chief surveyor's name. "Halvard Brann taught me the trade. He's stubborn, arrogant, and the finest field man I've ever known. If anyone could keep twelve people alive for nine days in that country, it's him." A pause. The river ran past, black and tireless. "If we find only graves, Eastwick, I want to know it precisely. Where, how many, and how. Can you do that for me?"

Ivo studied the captain's profile against the starlight — the dry, exact, unbending man — and understood suddenly that the maps had never been the point. Or rather, that for Voss the maps and the people had always been the same thing: a record that someone existed, that someone had stood in a place and measured it and refused to be erased.

"Yeah," Ivo said. "I can do that."

Voss nodded once and walked back to his bedroll.

Ivo remained at the water's edge until the sky went gray, watching the current pour into the Narrows, and at first light he stepped into the bow, planted his pole, and gave the order to cast off — upstream, against the current, into the throat of the world.

The walls closed over them like a book shutting.
