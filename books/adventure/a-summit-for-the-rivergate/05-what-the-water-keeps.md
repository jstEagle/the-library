## 5. What the Water Keeps

The river taught them its first lesson before noon.

Ivo had poled flatwater all his life — the slow brown channels of the lower basin, the irrigation cuts near the station, the shallow braids where cattle drank. This was none of that. The current here had a will, and it was not their will. Every stroke forward bought them a foot; every pause gave back two. The boat wallowed, spun, and had to be hauled straight again by men who were learning, stroke by stroke, that the Rivergate did not negotiate.

"Angle into it," Ivo called from the bow. "Don't fight it head-on. Let the eddies carry you along the walls."

Voss stood amidships with his notebook open against his chest, writing even as the deck pitched under him. He wrote the way other men breathed — constantly, without thought, as though a moment unrecorded was a moment lost forever. "You know these channels," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I guided supply boats up this far twice, years ago." That much was true. He left out the rest: that both trips had been in high water, when the current was strong but honest, and that this dry-season river ran low and strange, baring rocks that no chart accounted for.

They made eleven miles by dusk, which Voss pronounced acceptable and Ivo knew to be poor. The survey party, traveling light and downstream, would have covered that distance in half a day. Whatever had happened to them had happened fast, or they hadn't been trying to move at all.

The lower camp was supposed to be at mile fourteen, on the right bank where the canyon opened into a gravel bench. They found it an hour after dawn the next day, and Ivo knew something was wrong before anyone spoke, because there were no birds.

A camp abandoned by living people keeps a kind of noise. Canvas flaps lift in the wind. Pots knock together. Something always moves. This camp made no sound at all. The tents stood — three of them, still pegged — but their flaps hung dead-still in air so calm Ivo could hear the river chewing stones fifty yards away. The fire pit held ash gone gray and cold, cold for days, maybe a week. A tin plate sat upright on a crate with nothing on it, as if someone had set it down to eat and simply never come back.

"Hold here," Ivo said, and stepped off the boat alone.

He heard Voss behind him saying something about protocol, about approaching unknown sites systematically, and let it wash past him. Protocol was for places that might still make sense. Ivo had been reading ground since he was nine years old, and what this ground was telling him made his hands go quiet and careful, the way they did around snakes.

The instruments told the first story. A theodolite lay on its side in the gravel, tripod legs folded, lens cap off. A case of drafting tools stood open beside a tent pole, dividers and pens laid out neat as surgery. A stack of survey maps sat weighted under a stone — weighted deliberately, so the wind wouldn't take them. Nobody fleeing danger stops to weight down their maps. Nobody leaving in a hurry opens a tool case and arranges it.

"They didn't run," Wren Halloway said quietly. She had come up beside him despite his order to hold, and he found he didn't mind. She crouched over the drafting case without touching it. "This is organized. Someone packed the essentials and left the rest."

"Left the rest," Ivo repeated. "Or couldn't carry it."

"Or didn't need it anymore." She said it lightly, but her eyes when she looked up weren't light at all.

The fire pit told the second story. Ivo knelt and sifted the ash with two fingers. Beneath the gray top layer the coals were fused hard, burned long and hot and then smothered — doused, by the look of it, not starved. Someone had put this fire out on purpose rather than letting it die. And scattered through the ash, catching the morning light like fish scales, were slivers of paper. Burned fragments, dozens of them, worked deep into the ash as though someone had fed a whole notebook to the flames a page at a time and stirred the remains.

Voss arrived at his shoulder, saw the paper scraps, and went very still. "That's a field journal's worth," he said. His voice had changed — flatter, more careful. "Standard issue. Every surveyor keeps one. Daily entries, observations, coordinates."

"You'd know," Ivo said.

"I issued them." Voss crouched, picked a fragment from the ash with a delicacy that surprised Ivo, and held it to the light. A few letters survived on the scrap — *…elev. 4,120…* and below that, half a word that might have been *flow*. He turned it over. Blank. He set it down on the crate next to the tin plate, aligned its edge parallel with the plate's edge, and stepped back.

It was such a small thing, that alignment. But Ivo watched him do it and felt the first cold thread of understanding: Anselm Voss was a man who could not tolerate disorder, and everything about this camp was arranged to look orderly while being wrong in every particular that mattered.

The third story was written in the dirt, and that one was Ivo's alone to read.

The bench was graveled, but the margins — along the tent lines, around the fire, at the path down to the water — held patches of fine silt where boots printed clean. He circled the camp slowly, hands clasped behind his back, cataloguing. Six distinct sets, maybe seven. Heavy survey boots, hobnailed, the kind issued from Meridian stores; he could see the nail pattern ghosted in the silt. Two sets lighter, probably porters. One set small and narrow — Wren Halloway's, he guessed, or another clerk's. All of it ordinary. All of it consistent with a working camp going about its business.

Then, at the upstream margin, where the bench narrowed against the canyon wall, he found the eighth set.

He crouched over it for a long time.

The boots were wrong for a survey man. Too worn at the outer heel, too supple — desert boots, soft-soled, patched at the toe with a stitch pattern Ivo recognized because he'd spent four nights in salt flats following exactly that stitch pattern, measuring exactly that stride. Long stride. A walker who covered ground without seeming to hurry, left foot landing a half-inch ahead of the right, weight rolling off the big toe. In the flats that stride had read to him as arrogance. Here, at the edge of a vanished expedition's camp, it read as something else entirely.

Sable Corren had stood on this bench. Had walked its length, unhurried, after the surveyors left or while they were leaving. Her prints crossed the surveyors' prints in six places, and every time, hers lay on top.

She came here after. Or during. Either way, she had walked through this camp like a woman walking through her own kitchen.

"Ivo." Voss's voice, tight. "What have you got?"

He should have said *nothing*, or *uncertain tracks, weathered*, and bought himself time to think. Instead he heard himself say, "One set of prints that doesn't belong. Coming from upstream, crossing the camp, going out again the same way." He paused. "Recent. Day-old, maybe less."

"Describe the boots."

"Desert pattern. Soft sole. Patched left toe." He kept his voice level. "I've been following those boots for three weeks across the flats. They belong to Sable Corren. The thief I was tracking when your expedition stopped answering its radio."

The silence that followed had texture. Wren looked between them. The two porters, hauling crates up from the boat, had stopped to listen without pretending otherwise.

"Your thief," Voss said slowly, "was headed toward the Rivergate when you lost her."

"She wasn't headed toward it. She was already inside it. She's been inside it longer than we have." Ivo stood. "And she came to this camp after your people left it. Or—" he stopped himself, but the thought finished itself anyway, out loud, because some part of him needed it said— "or while they were still here."

"That's speculation."

"It's boot leather. Boot leather doesn't speculate." He pointed at the crossings. "Her prints over theirs. Never under. She moved through here freely, Voss. Nobody challenged her, or nobody was in any condition to challenge her."

Wren had drifted to the tents. Now she made a sound — small, bitten-off — and when Ivo turned, she was standing very straight, one hand pressed flat against the nearest canvas flap. "There's a journal page torn out," she said. "Not burned. Torn."

Inside the middle tent, a folding table held the expedition's log book, open, its spine cracked from heavy use. Pages of dense entries in several hands — Voss identified them at a glance, naming each surveyor like a father naming children — marched up to a date nine days past. Then, cleanly, at the gutter, a single leaf had been torn away. The stub remained, and on the stub, faintly, the mirror-image pressure of ink from the missing page: a few ghost-words, illegible except for one figure repeated twice near the bottom.

*Ivo leaned close. Wren leaned closer.*

"Discharge," she said. "Cubic feet per second. Someone measured the flow and wrote it down twice — once in the body of the entry, once as a summary. Surveyors do that when a number matters." Her finger hovered over the ghost-figure without touching it. "And then someone tore the page out."

"Who?"

"The hand that tore it was large." She studied the ragged edge. "Torn fast, from the bottom up. Not careful. Not archival." She straightened. "But the burns in the fire pit were careful. Pages fed in one at a time. That's two different people, or one person doing two different things for two different reasons."

Ivo looked at the open log, at the neat columns of a dead expedition's last days, and felt the dread in his chest change shape. It had been a dull weight since the station — worry for strangers, duty wearing him like a coat two sizes small. Now it sharpened to a point. Because Sable Corren's prints led away from this camp upstream, toward the Narrows, toward whatever the survey had measured twice and someone had wanted erased. And because the thief he had sworn to drag back in irons was now the only thread connecting every wrong thing on this bench.

She had come here for a reason. She had taken something, or left something, or seen something. And she was moving deeper into country where rangers had no jurisdiction and rescue parties had no business, and every hour he stood here was an hour she gained.

"We should document everything," Voss was saying. "Photograph the site, inventory the instruments, note positions relative to—"

"How many days has your radio been silent?" Ivo asked.

"Nine."

"And how far does the next camp sit upstream?"

"Twenty-two miles, at the mouth of the second tributary. The middle camp."

"At our rate, four days. At their rate, two." He turned and looked upriver, where the canyon bent and the water slid dark and quick around the bend like something swallowed. "Whatever happened here happened nine days ago. If your people are alive, they're either upstream or they're not anywhere. If Sable Corren knows what happened — and her boots say she does — then she's the closest thing we have to a witness." He met Voss's eyes. "We're not stopping to photograph ash."

Voss's jaw worked. For a moment Ivo thought the captain would pull rank, invoke the survey's authority, bury the decision in procedure. Then his gaze dropped to the torn journal stub, to the ghost of the discharge figure, and something in his face closed like a fist.

"Two hours," he said. "We inventory fast, we take the log book, and we mark the site for the recovery team that comes later." A beat. "There will be a recovery team, Eastwick. Whatever else happens."

"There will be," Ivo agreed, and did not say what he was thinking: that recovery teams recover bodies, and that the word had been sitting in all their mouths since the birds went quiet.

* * *

They worked through the morning. Wren inventoried with a speed that impressed him — she touched everything once, recorded it, and left it exactly as found, her pencil moving in shorthand he couldn't read. The porters lashed the log book and the weighted maps into the boat. Voss stood at the fire pit for a long time, sifting fragments, assembling a handful of burned scraps on the tin plate like a man laying out a hand of cards he already knew he'd lose.

Ivo walked the perimeter one last time.

At the path down to the water, he found what he'd been half-looking for and wholly dreading. Sable Corren's prints left the camp and went to the river's edge — and stopped there. Stood there. A cluster of impressions, deeper than the walking prints, weight settled onto both feet, facing upstream. She had stood at the waterline looking up the canyon for a long time. Long enough for her heels to sink a quarter inch into wet silt.

Beside the deepest print, pressed into the mud at the exact spot where her right foot had planted, was something else. Small. Deliberate.

He pried it free with his knife tip and wiped it clean on his sleeve.

A locket. Brass, cheap, the kind sold in river towns to people who couldn't afford silver. Its clasp was broken — broken old, the hinge green with age, not snapped recently. He'd found its twin three weeks ago on the salt flats, dropped in her trail like a signature, bearing a name that had cracked his certainty down the middle. He'd told himself then it was coincidence. Lockets were common. Names were common.

He opened this one.

Inside, behind clouded glass, a curl of hair gone gray, and on the backing, scratched with a pin in a child's uneven hand: *Papa — come home from the water.*

Ivo stood at the river's edge with the little brass thing burning in his palm, and the dread in him stopped being dread and became something with a direction. She hadn't dropped this. Nothing about its placement was accidental — pressed into her own footprint, at the waterline, facing upstream. It was a message, and the message was for whoever followed well enough to find it. *I was here. I am going where my father went. Come and see.*

She knew he'd follow. She was counting on it.

"Eastwick!" Voss, at the boat, impatient. "We're casting off!"

Ivo closed his fist around the locket and stood one moment longer, watching the current slide past, dark and patient, keeping everything it kept. Nine days of silence upstream. A torn page. A burned journal. A thief who left signs instead of trails.

He pocketed the locket, turned up the canyon, and went to tell them they were sailing into the throat of whatever this was — and that the only person who understood it was the woman he'd sworn to arrest.
