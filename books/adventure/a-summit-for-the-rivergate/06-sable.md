## 6. Sable

The year Ivo Eastwick turned fourteen, the river was a rumor and his father's mules were real, and both of them were headed upstream.

He remembered it that way ever after — as two weights in the same balance. The rumor lived in the way the old men at the Rivergate station talked when they thought no boy was listening: *the water comes up out of the rock whole, they say, no glacier feeding it, no snowmelt, just the earth opening its hand.* The mules were Bess and Juniper, gray and dun, patient under loads of survey chain and salt pork, and his father drove them because the Meridian Company paid better than any ranch in the basin and asked fewer questions than most.

They were not going to the source. His father had been clear about that, the way he was clear about everything — with a nod toward the canyon wall and a sentence short enough to carry. *We stop at the last ford. Company men go on past there, and company men can mind their own water.* But the last ford was close enough to the edge of the forbidden country that a boy could feel it, the way you feel a storm building behind a ridge: pressure in the ears, a hush over everything, the sense of standing at the hem of something enormous.

Ivo had grown up downstream of every mystery the desert owned. He knew where the dead springs were, where the petroglyphs watched the washes, which wells went bitter in late summer. He had never once been upstream of the beginning of things.

That spring, he was.

* * *

The party was small — his father, two company men whose names Ivo never learned properly because they introduced themselves by function (*chainman*, *leveler*, words like tools rather than people), and a guide hired out of the terraces above the gate. The guide was a Corren. That name meant nothing to Ivo then except that he said it wrong the first time and the man corrected him without heat, the way you'd correct someone mispronouncing your own mother.

Corren moved through the canyon country like water finding its level. He was not a large man, but he carried himself with a stillness that made the mules calm and made the company men irritable, because stillness is a kind of judgment and they felt judged. He wore his hair tied back with a strip of red cloth, and he talked to the land under his breath — not prayers exactly, or not only prayers. Names. He named the seeps as they passed them. He named the narrows, the terraces, the stone arches worn thin as wishbones. Once, passing a slab of rock scored with old markings, he laid his palm flat against it for a moment, the way you'd touch a doorframe you grew up under.

"Whose marks are those?" Ivo asked, because fourteen-year-olds ask.

"Mine," Corren said. "And my father's. And his." He looked at Ivo then, and his eyes were the color of the river in shadow, green going to black. "You mark what keeps you alive, boy. So the next one knows somebody passed here and lived."

Ivo's father called from the lead mule, and the moment broke, and Ivo did not think about it again for years — not consciously. But it stayed in him the way a splinter stays, working deeper whenever the weather changed.

They took four days to reach the last ford. The canyon narrowed around them by degrees, walls rising until the sky was a bright ribbon overhead and the river ran quick and glass-green below, so clear Ivo could count the stones on the bottom and watch fish hold themselves in the current like held breaths. The air smelled different up here. Colder. Sweeter. It smelled the way snow smells before it falls, though there was no snow within a hundred miles.

At the ford, Corren stopped the mules and stood listening.

"What is it?" the leveler asked.

"Water's high for the season," Corren said. "Someone's holding it back up-canyon."

"Holding it back," the chainman repeated, and laughed. "It's a river, man. Who holds back a river?"

Corren didn't answer. He looked upstream into the blue distance where the canyon bent away, and his face did something Ivo had no word for yet — later, much later, he would have the word, and the word would be *grief arriving early.*

* * *

That night the camp split itself without anyone deciding to. The company men pitched their tent apart and drank from a bottle they'd packed in false-bottomed instrument crates, and Ivo's father sat by the fire cleaning bits off the mules' hooves, and Corren sat alone on a flat rock above the waterline, looking upstream, and Ivo — because he was fourteen and drawn to whatever the adults were keeping from him — went and sat near Corren, not too close, the way you sit near a fire you're not sure you're welcome at.

"You want to see something," Corren said. It wasn't a question.

Ivo's heart banged. "My dad says—"

"Your dad says we stop at the ford. We've stopped at the ford." Corren stood. "There's a side channel a quarter mile up. Where the water goes in and doesn't come out. You can see it and be back before the fire burns down. Or you can sit here and wonder about it the rest of your life." He glanced down at Ivo, and there was something almost kind in it. "Wondering's heavier than walking. That's the first rule of this country."

So they walked.

The moon was three days off full and gave them enough light to pick their way along the shelf above the main channel. The sound of the river changed as they went — deepened, gathered itself, began to make a noise Ivo had never heard water make before, a low continuous thrumming like a struck string that never finished its note. Then the canyon opened into an amphitheater of stone, and Corren put a hand on Ivo's shoulder and pointed.

The side channel came down out of a crack in the north wall and poured into a slot in the bedrock — poured *in* and vanished. Not drained. Not sunk into gravel. It went into a black throat in the living rock with a smoothness that looked deliberate, engineered, as if the earth had built a doorway and posted no sign. The water went in silent at the edges and roaring at the center, and the thrumming Ivo had heard was the sound of the mountain drinking.

"It goes under," Corren said quietly. "Under the whole canyon. Comes up somewhere nobody's found, or everybody's pretending hasn't been found." He crouched and touched the wet stone at the lip of the slot, and Ivo saw that the rock around it was grooved — channels cut long ago, precise as carpentry, running off toward the main river. Old work. Older than the Company. Older, maybe, than anything with a name.

"My people kept these," Corren said. "Diversion channels. In dry years you could open them and give the low country a little more. In flood years you closed them and saved the terraces." He traced one groove with a finger. "Somebody taught me this who learned it from somebody who was taught. That's how water knowledge works. Hand to hand. You can't write it down without losing half of it."

"Who's teaching you now?" Ivo asked.

"No one left to teach me," Corren said, and stood, and that was all he said, and Ivo understood even at fourteen that some doors open onto rooms you're not tall enough to enter yet.

They started back along the shelf. And that was when the lanterns appeared upstream.

* * *

There were five of them, maybe six — figures moving along the far bank with covered lights, and the clink and scrape of gear being handled carelessly, gear that clinked like metal pipe and chain. Corren pulled Ivo into the shadow of a boulder and went very still, and Ivo learned in that minute what stillness was actually for.

"That's past the ford," Corren breathed. "Company crew. Working at night."

"So?"

"So nobody surveys at night unless they don't want witnesses." Corren's voice had gone flat and hard. "Stay here. Don't move. Don't make a sound."

"I'm coming with—"

"*Don't move.*"

He went. Ivo watched him cross the open stretch of shelf low and fast, a dark shape flowing along the rock, and then the boulders swallowed him and Ivo was alone with the thrumming water and the distant lanterns and his own heartbeat loud in his ears.

What happened next took less time than it takes to tell, and Ivo spent the rest of his life telling it to himself anyway, trying to make it longer, trying to find the version where it went differently.

He heard voices raised — Corren's, level and carrying; another man's, angry, slurred at the edges. He heard the word *permit* and the word *no permit issued for night work* and a laugh that had no friendliness in it. He heard metal dragged on stone. And then he heard the sound that would follow him down every year of his life afterward: a shout cut short, a splash, and the roar of the slot swallowing something that was not water.

Ivo moved. He would spend years ashamed of how long he stood frozen first — five seconds, ten, an eternity measured in the space between heartbeats — but he did move, eventually, scrambling along the shelf toward the lantern light with his legs shaking and his throat closed.

He came around the boulder field in time to see the far bank: lanterns swinging wild, men shouting at each other, and out in the channel, at the lip of the black throat, a head breaking the surface once — twice — an arm coming up, reaching, not waving, just reaching, the way a man reaches when he has already decided the reaching is hopeless but does it anyway because the body refuses to agree with the mind.

Then the arm went down, and the river smoothed itself over the place where it had been, and the thrumming went on, unbroken, indifferent, the same note it had been playing since before there were people to drown.

Corren reached the water's edge and went in. Ivo saw him dive, saw him come up gasping, dive again. One of the company men grabbed Corren's collar from behind and hauled him back and took a fist to the face for it, and then there was a tangle of bodies and lantern light and shouting, and Ivo stood on his side of the channel doing the only thing his body would let him do, which was scream — a boy's cracked, useless screaming across the water, *he's down, he's down, get him, get him* — as if they couldn't see, as if sound could pull a drowned man back up through forty feet of mountain.

They never got him. The river kept him. By full dark the lanterns were gone, the company men had scattered upstream like cockroaches from a lifted stone, and Corren knelt at the lip of the slot with the red cloth loose in his hair and the water sliding past his knees, and he did not make a sound at all.

Ivo crossed the channel on the stepping stones with his heart in pieces and stood behind him, not knowing what to do with his hands, with his voice, with any part of himself.

"He was stopping them," Corren said finally, without turning. His voice was scraped hollow. "They were rigging a dam in the upper channel. Diverting the flow into a holding basin — stealing the water out from under the whole terrace country, and calling it a survey." He turned then, and his eyes in the moonlight were dry, which was worse than weeping. "My brother. He was my brother. He saw the pipes and he waded out to break them and they shoved him and the current—" His voice failed. He looked back at the water. "The current did the rest."

"I saw," Ivo said. His own voice sounded foreign to him. "I saw all of it. I'll tell everyone. I'll testify, I'll—"

"Tell who?" Corren said. "The Company owns the magistrate at the gate. Owns the station chief. By morning those men will be a survey crew that lost some equipment in high water, and my brother will be a drunk who fell in, and there will be papers saying so." He looked at Ivo — really looked, the full weight of a grieving man's attention settling on a fourteen-year-old boy like a hand on the shoulder. "You saw. That matters. But seeing isn't the same as being believed, boy. And being believed isn't the same as being safe."

* * *

They walked back to the ford camp together, and Ivo's father met them at the fire with a face already gone gray, because grief travels faster than walkers and something in the night air had told him. What was said between Corren and his father, Ivo never fully knew. He caught pieces: *the Company,* and *papers,* and *the boy saw,* and his father saying, low and steady, "He's fourteen. Whatever you need from him, you take it through me."

But it wasn't Corren who needed anything. It was the Company, and what the Company needed was silence, and silence, it turned out, had a price schedule.

Two days later, at the Rivergate station, in a room that smelled of lamp oil and ink, a man with soft hands and a soft voice laid papers on the table in front of Ivo's father. Ivo sat beside his father on the bench, still bruised inside from the night at the slot, still hearing the arm come up and go down.

"In consideration of services rendered and discretion maintained," the soft-voiced man read aloud, "the undersigned acknowledges receipt of payment in the amount of—" a figure that made Ivo's father flinch, a figure that was three years of ranch wages, "—payable to the estate of the deceased, and further agrees to hold no claim, press no testimony, and speak no account of events witnessed on the night in question."

"It's hush money," Ivo's father said. His jaw worked. "You're buying the boy's mouth."

"We're compensating a grieving family," the man said pleasantly, "and documenting a regrettable accident. The boy saw a man drown. Men drown. The paper simply ensures his memory of the evening remains... uncomplicated." He slid the paper across, and a pen after it. "Sign, and the Corren family is provided for. Refuse, and I'm afraid the alternative accounts of the evening begin circulating — and the Correns receive nothing at all."

Ivo looked at his father. His father looked at the paper, and then at Ivo, and something passed between them that Ivo would spend decades decoding: shame, and love, and the arithmetic of a man with no power choosing the least-bad thing.

His father signed his own name as witness. Then he put the pen in Ivo's hand.

"You don't have to," his father said quietly.

But the picture the soft-voiced man had painted was already nailed up inside Ivo's head — the widow, the children, the terraces going dry while the money men drank from a stolen river — and a fourteen-year-old boy who had watched a man die and could save no one wanted, more than anything in the world, to save *something.* Even if the something was only a number on a page.

He signed. His hand shook. The letters of his name came out crooked, and the soft-voiced man blotted them carefully, as if preserving a flower.

"There," the man said. "Now it never happened."

* * *

Except it had happened, and it went on happening, every day, in the private country behind Ivo's eyes.

He grew up the way boys grow up in the basin — hard and fast and outwardly fine. He took a ranger's commission at nineteen because rangers answered to the territory, not the Company, and he told himself that was why, told himself he'd chosen the badge for the open country and the honest work. He became good at tracking, good at surviving alone, good at the particular competence of a man who never lets anyone close enough to notice he's carrying something.

Because he was carrying something. Two things, really, though he counted them as one: the image of the arm coming up out of the green water, reaching; and the folded paper of debt that rode in his breast pocket through every year and every assignment, softened at the creases by handling, its terms recited so often in his head that they'd worn smooth as river stones. *In consideration of services rendered and discretion maintained.* He paid it faithfully, every season, a portion of his wage sent upstream to an address in the terrace country. He never questioned it. Questioning it would have meant opening the box he'd built around that night, and the box was load-bearing. Take it out and he did not know what else might fall.

He never met the family. He told himself that was respect — that the Correns wanted nothing from the boy who'd watched and signed and run. He told himself the payments were enough. He told himself a great many things, and the desert let him, because the desert is patient and does not argue; it only waits, the way water waits, for the crack in the stone.

The crack came years later, in the shape of a thief on the salt flats, and a locket in the sand with a name inside it that stopped Ivo's heart like a hand.

Corren.

* * *

Present tense returned to him like cold water. Ivo stood at the window of the Rivergate station, dawn graying the canyon rims, the locket's chain wound tight around his fingers, biting crescents into his skin. Down the hall, the station chief was shouting into the radio again — *Meridian Survey, Meridian Survey, do you copy, do you copy* — and getting back the same answer as the last nine days, which was none.

Nine days of silence from an expedition upstream. Nine days, and now a thief with a dead man's name around her neck, running toward the country where the river began.

Ivo opened his hand and looked at the locket lying in his palm, small and warm as a coal. Somewhere upriver, past the last ford, past the black throat in the living rock, something was waiting for him that he had spent fifteen years refusing to walk toward.

The station chief's boots came pounding down the hall.

"Eastwick," he said, breathless, filling the doorway. "I need a word. Now."
