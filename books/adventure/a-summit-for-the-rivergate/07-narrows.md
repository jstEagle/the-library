## 7. Narrows

The river changed its mind about being a river on the third morning above the last boat landing.

Ivo heard it before he saw it — a sound like a crowd muttering behind stone — and then the canyon walls closed in and there it was: green water moving fast between rock so sheer that a man could press both palms flat against it and find no crack wider than a knife blade. The Narrows. Every ranger who worked the lower canyons knew the name the way sailors know the names of reefs. You did not enter it lightly, and you did not enter it at all when the sky upstream looked like this one did — bruised and heavy, stacked with cloud that had come over the rim country in the night and now sat on the high plateau like something brooding.

Voss stood at the bow of the lead skiff with his field glasses up, and Ivo watched the surveyor's shoulders go rigid before he said anything.

"Half a mile of that," Voss said finally, lowering the glasses. "Maybe more. No banks worth the name. We run it or we climb over it, and we can't climb over it."

"We don't run it," Ivo said. "Not today."

Voss turned. He was a long man, dry as a fence rail, with the particular patience of someone who believed the world was a problem that would eventually yield to measurement. "The expedition has been silent for eleven days now, Eastwick. Every day we spend being careful is a day they might be bleeding."

"Every day you spend being careless," Ivo said, "is a day we might be joining them." He nodded upward, at the sky. "That cloud's been building over the rim since midnight. If it lets go anywhere in forty miles of drainage above us, all of it comes down this slot at once. You've seen a flash flood in a canyon like this?"

"I've read accounts."

"Reading isn't seeing." Ivo crouched at the waterline and put his hand in the current, an old habit, reading temperature the way other men read weather. Cold. Colder than yesterday. Snowmelt and storm somewhere far off, feeding down through country he couldn't see. "The water's already telling us. It knows something we don't."

Behind them, the three porters — Hallam, the brothers Teague and Rost, and old Maretto, who had hauled freight through half the canyon country and claimed to be afraid of nothing but paper — were securing the loads under oilcloth. Wren Halloway sat on a boulder with her map case open across her knees, drawing the canyon mouth freehand, because of course she was; the woman drew everything, as if ink were a way of holding on to places that would otherwise slip away.

She caught Ivo looking at her and lifted her chin toward the walls. "How sheer is sheer?"

"Sheer enough that the sheep don't cross here," Ivo said. "And sheep will cross anything with a ledge the width of my boot."

"That's not a measurement."

"It's the only honest one I've got." He stood, wiped his hand on his thigh. "There's a route. Partway. A bench on the left wall about sixty feet up — my father used it, and his father before him. It won't take the boats. It'll barely take us. We line the skiffs through empty, rope them from the bench where the water slows, and we keep our eyes on the sky the whole time."

Voss was quiet a moment. Ivo had learned in four days of traveling with him that the surveyor's silences were not agreement; they were arithmetic. He was counting something — hours, risk, the weight of his own authority against the weight of the water.

"Sixty feet," Voss said. "And if the flood comes while we're on the bench?"

"Then we're sixty feet above a river that wants to be two hundred feet deep," Ivo said. "Which is a better bargain than being at zero."

* * *

They entered the Narrows an hour later, and Ivo understood within the first hundred yards why the old routes avoided it entirely.

The walls rose straight off the water, polished in bands, striped red and black and a strange pale gold where some ancient flood had left its silt like paint. There was no walking. The river filled the canyon floor edge to edge, fast and glassy-smooth in the center, boiling white where it struck the bends. They ran the skiffs in relays — two men aboard, poles and oars, shooting each stretch while the rest scrambled along whatever footholds the rock offered, which were few and mean. Twice the current took a skiff's stern and swung it broadside, and twice the men aboard recovered by inches, faces gray, saying nothing, because there was nothing to say that the river hadn't already heard.

Ivo took the first relay himself. Not because he was the best boatman — Maretto was, and said so — but because he could not stand to watch another man do it first. That was the ranger's arithmetic, the one he'd carried so long it felt like bone: your own fear was yours to spend, never anyone else's.

By midday they reached the bench route. It began as a crack in the left wall, a chimney of sorts, then traversed out along a ledge no wider than the claim Ivo had made for it. Below, the river ran white and continuous, a single unbroken rapid for as far as the canyon bent out of sight. The roar came up through the rock itself. Ivo felt it in his boot soles, in his back teeth, in the cage of his ribs — the whole gorge humming like a struck string.

They ferried the loads up the chimney on ropes. The skiffs they emptied, dragged to the base of the wall, and tied off to pitons Ivo drove himself, testing each one with his full weight. Then the work of lining them through began — hauling against the current inch by inch from above, ropes singing, men leaning out over the void with their heels hooked under the ledge lip.

It was slow, brutal, exacting work, and it was going well, right up until the light changed.

Ivo noticed it the way animals notice things — not as a thought but as a flinch. The air cooled. Not the gradual cool of afternoon; a sudden drop, five degrees in as many minutes, and with it a smell he knew from childhood, from the year the wash behind the station took the corrals: wet stone. Wet stone and ozone and something underneath, faint and wrong, like blood in the water.

He stood up on the bench and looked upstream, and the sky above the canyon rim had gone the color of a bruise gone bad.

"Off the ropes!" His voice cracked out of him louder than he'd meant, loud enough that the echo went down the gorge and came back doubled. "Everyone off the lines — get up, get high, NOW—"

"What—" Voss started.

"Flood. Move!"

To his lasting surprise, they moved. Maybe it was the tone. Maybe it was that Ivo Eastwick, who had spent four days being the quiet, competent, unimpressive ranger whom Captain Anselm Voss had conscripted the way a man requisitions a mule, suddenly sounded like something the men had never heard before — and believed instantly. Maretto dropped his rope and grabbed the youngest Teague brother by the collar. Hallam, big and deliberate, hauled the load bags off the ledge lip and slung them up-slope. Wren was already climbing, map case strapped tight, moving with a sureness that told Ivo she'd done this before, somewhere, in some other canyon that had tried to kill her.

Only Rost Teague froze. He was halfway down the chimney with a coil of line over his shoulder, and the word *flood* seemed to have locked every joint in him. Ivo saw it happen — saw the man's eyes fix on the white water below and stop seeing anything else.

Ivo went down the chimney after him.

It was a stupid thing to do and he did it without deciding to, hands and boots finding holds by themselves, the cold coming down the gorge now like breath from an open cellar door. He reached Rost, took a fistful of the man's shirt, and put his face close.

"The water comes at the speed of a running horse," Ivo said. "You can outrun it. Your fear can't. Climb."

Rost climbed. Ivo stayed below him, one arm hooked through the rope anchor, pushing the man's boots to the next hold, the next, hearing behind and below them the sound arrive.

It did not sound like water. That was the thing no account ever got right. It sounded like the mountain clearing its throat — a deep, grinding boom, then a hiss that rose and rose, and then the whole canyon roared, and the air itself shoved past them, warm and foul with torn-up silt, ahead of the water like a shockwave made of weather.

Ivo got Rost onto the bench and threw himself flat beside him as the flood came through.

He had seen flash floods before. He had seen one take a horse. Nothing had prepared him for the sight of the Narrows simply ceasing to exist — the white rapids swallowed whole, the skiffs at the wall's base plucked up like leaves, one of them vanishing under a rolling brown head of water thick as wet concrete, whole trees turning end over end in it, boulders the size of wagons grinding along the bottom with a sound he felt in his spine. The river rose thirty feet in less time than it takes to tell about it. Spray climbed the walls and fell back as rain. The roar was total. It was not a sound you heard; it was a place you were inside of.

Beside him, Rost Teague was shaking. Ivo put a hand on the back of the man's neck and held it there, the way you'd steady a spooked animal, and shouted nothing, because nothing could be heard, and after a while the shaking slowed.

Above them, wedged against the wall, the others watched with the expressions of people revising their understanding of the world. Voss's face was unreadable — except that it wasn't; Ivo had gotten good enough at reading the surveyor to see the calculation running behind his eyes, the furious recalculation of a man whose numbers had just failed him. Wren Halloway was not watching the water at all. She was watching the walls — the height of the flood debris lodged in cracks forty feet above even this crest, old driftwood and silt lines from floods older than any of them — and Ivo understood she was mapping the river's history in her head, learning how often it did this, and therefore how likely it was to do it again.

The flood peaked, hung there a long minute like a held breath, and began, slowly, grudgingly, to fall.

* * *

By late afternoon the water had dropped enough to show the tops of the boulders again, brown and scoured, and the canyon floor was a wreck of new sand, splintered wood, and silt laid down smooth as poured plaster. One skiff was gone. The other hung in a tangle of driftwood twenty yards downstream, hull stove, but repairable — Maretto pronounced it so with the grim satisfaction of a man who liked being right about wreckage.

They made camp on a shelf of clean sand above the new high-water mark, and nobody suggested a fire. The sky upstream still looked wrong.

It was around the cold camp, in the blue hour, that Rost Teague quit.

He didn't make a speech. He sat apart from the others, wrapped in his blanket, and when Ivo came near he looked up with an expression stripped of shame, which somehow made it worse.

"I'm going back down," Rost said. "On foot, along the benches, however far it takes. I'll take half rations. I'll sign whatever paper says I owe the difference."

"You nearly died today," Ivo said. "Nobody thinks less of you for—"

"You think less of me." Rost said it without heat, almost kindly. "You should. I froze. And here's the thing, Ranger — I knew I would. I've known it since the boat landing. This country—" he gestured at the black walls rising into the dark, at the sound of the river still running heavy below, "—it doesn't scare me the way it scares other men. It scares me the way I scare me. I looked at that water and I saw exactly what I am, and I don't want to look again tomorrow. Or the day after."

Ivo crouched down. For a moment he almost said the true thing — *I ran once too, from something worse than water, and I've been paying for it ever since* — but the words jammed in his throat the way they always did, behind the paper in his breast pocket, behind the debt that had organized his whole adult life.

"Walk out safe, then," was what he said instead.

Rost nodded. In the morning he was gone, taking the least of the rations and leaving the most of the apology, in the form of a note pinned under a stone that Wren read aloud without comment and then folded into her map case, because she kept everything, even other men's failures.

Voss took the loss harder than he showed it. Three porters now, plus Wren, plus himself, plus Ivo. Five, and half their boats, and a river that had just demonstrated, with great clarity, what it thought of their plans.

"We go on," Voss said that night, standing at the edge of the firelight with his arms crossed. "Slower. Higher ground whenever there's higher ground to take. But we go on."

Ivo looked upstream, where the canyon bent away into darkness and the river muttered to itself over its rearranged bed. Somewhere up there — two camps, a gorge, a week of hard travel, if the maps were honest, and Wren had already begun quietly suggesting they weren't — the Meridian Survey had stopped answering its radio. Somewhere up there was a thief named Sable Corren, whose trail had led him here, whose locket rode in his pocket with a name on it that matched the name on his debt papers, and whose stride matched the prints at the abandoned lower camp.

Somewhere up there was the reason he'd come, wearing a shape he didn't yet understand.

He rolled into his blanket and listened to the water, and the water sounded, all night long, like footsteps.
