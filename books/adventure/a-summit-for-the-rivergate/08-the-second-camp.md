## 8. The Second Camp

They found the middle camp on the fourth morning, and it was the emptiness that frightened Ivo most.

Not the emptiness of abandonment — he knew that kind well enough from a decade of ranger work, the way a place goes feral when people leave it, packrats moving into the flour tins, wind taking the light things first. This was different. This was an emptiness that had been *arranged*. The tents stood pitched and staked, guy-lines taut as harp strings. The cook fire had been banked rather than scattered, its ash ringed with stones set in a careful crescent. A canvas tarp lay folded over the instrument crates, corners squared, weighted at each edge with river stones chosen for their size and placed with something close to tenderness.

"Nobody leaves a camp like this," Wren Halloway said. She had walked the perimeter twice already, her notebook out, sketching the layout in her quick architectural shorthand. "Nobody who's running, nobody who's been taken. You leave a camp like this when you want whoever comes next to understand that you left on purpose."

Voss stood in the center of it all with his hands behind his back, turning slowly, reading the ground the way he read contour lines — for information, not for feeling. "Or," he said, "when you're being tidy under orders. Discipline. Meridian ran a disciplined outfit."

"Meridian ran a *frightened* outfit," Wren said, not looking up from her page. "There's a difference."

Ivo said nothing. He was crouched at the fire ring, and what he was looking at made the skin along his forearms prickle despite the morning heat. The ash inside the stone crescent had been disturbed — not by wind, not by animals. Someone had drawn in it. A line of characters raked through with a finger or a stick, deliberate, and then smoothed over again by the same hand, badly, so that the shapes still ghosted up through the gray like bones under sand.

He brushed the ash aside with two fingers.

Beneath it, scratched into the flat face of the largest hearthstone, were four words:

**GOING TO THE SOURCE**

The letters were deep, cut with something harder than a finger — a knife point, a tent stake, the broken shank of a survey pin. Whoever wrote them had wanted them to last. And then whoever wrote them had covered them with ash, which meant they hadn't wanted just anyone to read them. Only someone who looked. Only someone who knew how to look.

Ivo sat back on his heels and let the words sit in him for a long moment.

The source. The word every child in the canyon country learned before they learned to swim, because you learned it alongside the rule: *the river rises where it rises, and no one is permitted to see.* Not forbidden by any law Ivo had ever been able to find in the ranger codes. Forbidden the way gravity is forbidden to a falling man — by something older than law, enforced by distance and sheer wall and the simple fact that in three hundred years of settlement, no expedition sent upriver had ever come back with proof of anything except that the river kept rising past the maps' last confident lines.

And now eleven surveyors with government instruments and a financier's money had walked off into that country, leaving their boots oiled and their tents standing, and scratched four words into a hearthstone like a message passed under a door.

"Ivo." Voss's voice, clipped. "Something?"

Ivo considered lying. It would have been easy. He could have said *nothing*, could have swept the ash back with his boot, and Voss would have ordered the camp searched, found nothing conclusive, and turned the party around by nightfall — which was, Ivo suspected, exactly what Voss wanted to do anyway, and exactly what some careful part of Ivo's own mind whispered he ought to allow.

But Sable Corren's stride was printed in the salt flats forty miles south, and her locket was in his breast pocket, and her name was stitched through every mile of this country like thread through cloth. If she had gone to the source — if she had gone there ahead of eleven trained men — then turning back now meant never understanding why. And Ivo had discovered, somewhere in the last hundred miles, that not understanding had become unbearable.

So he stepped aside and let Voss see.

* * *

Voss read the inscription twice. Then he did something Ivo had not seen him do before: he took off his hat.

It was a small gesture, almost unconscious, but it changed his face. Without the brim shadowing it, Captain Anselm Voss looked suddenly older and less certain, a man in his middle fifties with sun-creased skin and eyes that had spent too many years measuring things too large for them.

"The source," he said quietly. "They went to the source."

"It appears so."

"That's not possible." Voss replaced the hat, and the officer came back into his posture like a man pulling on a coat. "The approaches don't exist. The upper gorge is a dead end — I've surveyed the tributaries myself, twenty years ago. There is no route. Whatever they've written on that stone is either a joke, a delusion, or a marker for something else entirely."

"Or there's a route nobody's mapped."

"There is *always* a route nobody's mapped," Voss snapped. "That is not the same as a route that can be traveled. Eastwick, think about what you're proposing. Eleven experienced surveyors abandoned their equipment — their *instruments*, which cost more than this entire rescue operation — and walked into country with no recorded water, no recorded feed, no exit. That is not an expedition. That is a mass delusion or a mass suicide, and either way, our task is recovery of remains and documentation, not—" he gestured upstream, at the river sliding green and patient between its walls, "—*following*."

"Your task," Ivo said.

Voss's jaw tightened. "Our task."

"Our task," Ivo agreed, "was to find the Meridian Survey and bring back answers about why they went silent. We've found half of it. They didn't go silent because they died. They went silent because they *left*." He stood, brushing ash from his knees. "You want to turn around and report that? 'Found the camp, found the note, everyone's fine, they've just gone to see the thing we're all forbidden to see, no further action recommended'? How does that read in your report, Captain?"

"It reads as *prudent*."

"It reads as a man who got scared at the second camp and quit." Ivo heard his own voice come out flat and hard, and part of him — the ranger part, the part that had spent ten years keeping his head down and his debts paid and his opinions behind his teeth — flinched at it. But another part, a part that had been waking up mile by mile since the salt flats, leaned into the words like a man leaning into a current. "Nine days of radio silence, Captain. Your financiers sent us up here because they wanted answers. Those answers are upstream. All of them."

Voss studied him for a long moment. Around them, the porters had gone still, pretending to sort gear, listening the way men listen when the argument decides whether they walk toward danger or away from it.

"You know these canyons," Voss said at last. It wasn't a question. "Better than anyone on my roster, according to the station chief. So tell me honestly, Eastwick, man to man: what's above the next bend?"

Ivo thought about it. Thought about the Narrows they'd come through two days ago, the walls closing until the sky was a blue ribbon overhead, the water running fast and cold over stones the color of old blood. Thought about the country beyond, which he had only ever seen from the rims, on long patrols, looking down into a labyrinth of red and gold stone that no trail crossed and no map trusted.

"Honestly?" he said. "I don't know. Nobody knows. That's the whole point of the rule."

"And that doesn't frighten you."

It did. It frightened him down to the marrow, and had since he was nine years old, since a day beside the water he had never spoken of aloud, a day whose memory lived in him like a splinter worked too deep to dig out. But he looked at Voss — at the map-case hanging from the captain's shoulder, at the neat instrument crates under their weighted tarp, at the whole apparatus of measured, documented, *purchased* knowledge — and what came out of him surprised even himself.

"No," Ivo said. "What frightens me is going back and not knowing."

* * *

They argued through the midday heat, in the shade of the largest tent, while the porters mended straps and pretended deafness. Voss laid it out with a surveyor's precision: supplies sufficient for six more days at full ration, eight at short; the dry season dropping the river a hand's width per day; the return journey against nothing but time, whereas the return from further upstream would be against both time and current. Every mile they advanced cost them a mile and a half of retreat. The arithmetic was merciless and he recited it like scripture.

Wren, to Ivo's surprise, backed him.

"The canteen," she said, setting it on the crate between them. She'd found it tucked beneath the folded tarp, wedged deliberately, placed to be found by the right person and missed by the wrong one. It was standard Meridian issue, dented, its owner's initials — *R.M.* — scratched inside the cap. Below the initials, in the same deep-cut hand as the hearthstone, ran a second line Ivo hadn't noticed before, hidden where only someone turning the canteen over in their hands would catch it:

**TELL THE RANGER**

Voss stared at it. "That's nonsense. What ranger? Meridian didn't request a ranger escort. They refused one."

"They refused one officially," Wren said. "But somebody in that expedition knew a ranger might come anyway. Knew one might be *sent*." Her eyes flicked to Ivo, quick and unreadable. "Somebody left this where it would be found by a tracker and not by a casual passerby. That's not a delusion, Captain. That's a message with an intended recipient. And the recipient is sitting in this tent."

The silence after that had weight. Ivo picked up the canteen and turned it in his hands. The metal was warm from Wren's grip. *Tell the ranger.* Tell him what? That they'd gone willingly? That they'd gone unwillingly? That whatever they'd found upstream was something the world outside needed to hear about from someone who wasn't on Meridian's payroll?

His thumb found the edge of the lettering, the sharp bite of the cuts. Whoever had scratched it had pressed hard enough to hurt.

"We go on," Ivo said.

"One voice," Voss said. "Out of five."

"Then put it to a vote and lose." Ivo met his eyes. "Or keep your authority and spend it turning back, and explain to your financier why his expedition's own people left him a message for me and not for him."

He hadn't known, until the words were out, how much he meant the last part. But watching Voss's face, he saw it land — saw the captain register that the message had been addressed to a ranger, that the surveyors had trusted the law's man over the money's man, and that this fact sat in Voss's chest like a stone he couldn't cough up.

Voss called the vote anyway. Three to two. The porters followed Ivo; Wren voted with him without hesitation; Voss and the senior porter, a silent gray-bearded man named Teodor, voted to turn back. Teodor's vote surprised no one — the old man had been muttering against the upper river since the Narrows, crossing himself at the water like a farmer crossing himself at a snake.

"We advance," Voss said formally, "under protest, and with conditions. Short rations from tomorrow. We mark every camp. And at the first sign of genuine hazard — flood season water, injury, loss of supply — I turn this party around regardless of votes. Is that understood?"

"Understood," Ivo said, and shook the offered hand, and felt the captain's grip like a man gripping a rail at the edge of a drop.

* * *

They broke camp late, in the long amber light of late afternoon, and Ivo walked the site one final time before they shouldered the loads.

He stood where the surveyors had stood, or where they had knelt, and tried to read the story written in their leavings. Boots by the tent doors, laced and ready — they'd expected to move fast. Food stores left intact but inventoried, sacks re-tied with precise knots — they'd taken only what they could carry and had wanted whoever followed to know the rest was there, unspoiled, a lifeline for anyone coming behind. The instruments under the tarp, wrapped in oilcloth — they hadn't needed them anymore, or couldn't carry them, or—

Or the instruments were the problem. Ivo turned that thought over. Eleven men abandoning thirty thousand crowns of theodolites and levels and glass, in country where measurement was the whole religion of their trade. Unless the measurements themselves had become dangerous. Unless knowing precisely where things were had stopped being safe.

He crouched at the hearthstone one last time and put his palm flat over the four cut words. The stone still held a little of the day's heat, like a body recently departed.

*Going to the source.*

And beneath his hand, fainter than the words, he noticed what he'd missed before: a fifth mark, small, low in the corner of the stone, half worn away. Not letters. A symbol — a circle bisected by a wavy line, cut shallow and quick, the way a man signs something he's signed a thousand times.

Ivo knew the mark. He had grown up seeing it chiseled into spring houses and carved above wellheads all through the canyon country: the old sign of the water-keepers, the families who had tended the seeps and guarded the approaches back before the towns had names, back when the rule about the source was not a regulation but a covenant. His grandmother had made the sign over every cup of water she ever handed him.

Sable Corren's family were water-keepers. He'd known that from the debt papers — the family name appeared in the old rolls, the way such names always had.

Someone in the Meridian Survey had carried that mark into the wilderness and pressed it into a hearthstone like a signature on a letter. Like a plea. Like a hand reaching back downriver, grasping for the oldest authority in the canyon country, the one authority no financier's money and no captain's commission could outrank.

Ivo rose. Downstream, the river murmured over its stones, patient as ever, carrying everything toward the sea and telling nothing. Upstream, the canyon bent away northward into country that grew darker and taller with every mile, walls rising like the shoulders of something vast beginning to wake.

"Eastwick!" Voss, already loaded, impatient. "Daylight's spending itself."

Ivo took one last look at the camp — the tidy tents, the weighted tarp, the ash-smoothed secret — and then turned his back on it and started walking.

Behind him, unnoticed by anyone, the wind moved through the empty camp and lifted a fine veil of ash from the hearthstone, so that by the time the last of the daylight failed, the four words stood open to the stars, plain as a shout: *going to the source, going to the source, going to the source* — and below them, in the corner, the old sign of the water-keepers caught the starlight like a scar.
