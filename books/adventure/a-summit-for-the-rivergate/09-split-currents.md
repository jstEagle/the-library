## 9. Split Currents

Wren Halloway had learned to read a man's hands before she trusted his words. Hands told you what a person did with their days, and days told you what they were. Captain Anselm Voss had the hands of a man who had never once touched anything he was afraid to lose — long, dry, precise fingers that handled instruments like they were already catalogued, already filed, already dead.

She watched those hands now as Voss stood on the gravel bar and divided the rescue party like a man dividing a shipment of goods.

"The river's dropping," he said. He didn't raise his voice; he never had to. The canyon did it for him, carrying his words off the water with a flat, official clarity. "Nine days of silence, and now the gauge at the lower station shows a fall of nearly a foot. That's not seasonal. That's structural. Something upstream is holding water back, or the survey breached something and let it go. Either way, every day we wait, the current gets harder to fight and the shallows get closer."

He turned, and his eyes went down the line of them — the two porters, Broch and little Tammas; Ivo Eastwick standing slightly apart with his arms crossed and his hat pulled low; the half-wild thief they'd pulled out of the side canyon two days ago, wrists still rope-marked; and Wren herself, with her satchel of maps and her aching shoulder.

"Four go on with Eastwick. Three come back with me." He said it the way he said everything, as though it had already been decided in some ledger Wren hadn't been shown.

"And who decides the split?" Ivo asked. His voice was quiet. Wren had noticed that about him — the ranger never spent a word he didn't mean to spend.

"I do. I'm the ranking officer of the Meridian Survey and this is a Survey rescue operation."

"It's not," Ivo said. "The Survey's upriver, silent. This is a ranger-guided party that you attached yourself to. And the thief comes with us or doesn't come at all — she's my prisoner, and she's the only one who knows the terraces."

Sable Corren said nothing. She sat on a flat stone at the water's edge, sharpening a knife on a river pebble with slow, unhurried strokes, as if the entire conversation were weather happening to other people. Wren had been watching her for two days and had not once caught her looking at the river the way everyone else did — with longing, or fear, or the simple animal respect that moving water demanded. Sable looked at the river the way Wren looked at a map that had been altered. Like she was checking it for lies.

"Then it's settled," Voss said. "Eastwick, the Corren woman, and two others continue. The rest turn back with me. I'll take the porters."

"You'll take Tammas," Ivo said. "Broch stays."

Broch, the big porter with the burned forearm, straightened up like a man called by name in church. He looked at Voss. He looked at Ivo. Wren saw the arithmetic happen behind his eyes — wages owed against wages promised, fear against pride — and she saw the moment it finished.

"I'll go on," Broch said.

Voss's jaw tightened by a fraction. "You'll do as you're instructed."

"I signed on to find people," Broch said. "Not to walk away from them."

There was a silence then, filled only by the river. Wren felt her own heart doing something complicated and unwelcome. She raised her hand.

"I'm going on as well."

Voss turned to her, and for the first time since the launch, she saw something flicker behind the official calm. "Halloway. Your maps are needed at the station. The relief survey —"

"Are needed where the survey went," Wren said. "That's rather the point of maps, Captain. They're no good describing places nobody's standing."

It was a small defiance, dressed in professional language, and she knew he heard it for what it was. Voss had reviewed her work. Voss had *praised* her work, in that bloodless way of his, and she had spent three years telling herself that the praise meant nothing and the alterations she'd made to the official sheets meant less. A line smoothed here. A canyon approach left blank there. Nothing, she'd told herself. Housekeeping. The Survey's right to control its own cartography.

But she had been lying to herself with the same precision she brought to everything else, and lying to oneself, Wren had found, required a great deal of maintenance.

"You'll be responsible for your own rations," Voss said at last. "And your own report. Whatever you find up there, it will be entered into the record through me."

"Then it won't be entered into the record," Wren said, and heard Sable Corren make a sound that was almost, almost a laugh.

* * *

They launched the two remaining boats an hour later, and Wren used the hour the way she used every hour — drawing.

She sat on a boulder with her board on her knees and worked in quick, sure strokes: the gravel bar, the split party, Voss's face as he shoved off downriver with Tammas and the third boatman, a silent man named Pell who had not spoken a full sentence in six days. She drew the way other people prayed. It was the only form of honesty she had never been ordered to alter.

"You draw fast," Sable said, appearing at her shoulder without sound. The woman moved like that. Like a rumor.

"Fast and accurate," Wren said. "The second part's the hard one."

"Is it." Sable looked at the sketch — at Voss's boat, already small, already swallowed by the downstream bend. "You don't like him."

"I don't know him. I've worked under his commission for three years." Wren capped her pen. "Those are different things."

"And which one do you trust?"

Wren looked at her. Up close, the thief was all sharp edges and hollows — cheekbones like cut stone, hands that were far too capable for a person who claimed to be merely a criminal. Her wrists, where the rope had been, were ringed with healing welts she never once touched or complained of.

"I trust what I can verify," Wren said. "Which is why I'm going upstream. Everything about this expedition has failed to verify."

Sable's eyes narrowed — not in hostility, Wren thought, but in the manner of someone recalculating. "Say more."

"Nine days of radio silence. The Survey's transmitters don't fail. I helped install two of them; they're built to survive everything short of a flood, and the last report before the silence was routine. Then Voss arrives at the station with a rescue commission signed before the silence was even *confirmed* — did you know that? The station chief showed me the paperwork. The commission is dated four days before anyone officially knew the expedition was missing." Wren kept her voice low, pitched under the river. "Somebody knew they were in trouble before the trouble was announced. And somebody wanted a rescue party in the field fast, with the right paperwork, and didn't much care who came back."

Sable was very still. "You've been carrying that since the station."

"I've been carrying worse since the station," Wren said, and immediately wished she hadn't, because the thief's gaze sharpened like a blade finding a whetstone.

"Such as?"

"Such as nothing you'd be interested in."

"I'm interested in everything," Sable said. "It's my worst quality."

* * *

They made nine miles that afternoon, and Wren spent all of them watching.

Not the river — the river was magnificent and terrible in the way it had been since the Narrows, running fast and green between walls the color of old bone. She'd drawn it a hundred times from survey data, and the data had been wrong in a hundred small ways, and she was only now understanding how deliberate those small ways might have been. No, she watched the people. A party that splits is a party that has already broken, and broken things show their seams.

Ivo Eastwick rowed the lead boat and said almost nothing, but Wren noticed that he'd stopped watching Sable the way a jailer watches a prisoner. Somewhere between the side canyon and this morning's split, the watching had changed quality. Now he watched her the way a man watches a fire he's decided not to put out.

Broch rowed second and nursed his burned arm and told Tammas-sized jokes to nobody, because Tammas was gone, and Wren suspected the jokes were how he kept his fear from settling into the boat.

And Sable — Sable sat in the bow and read the water like a page she'd memorized in childhood. Wren saw it happen three times before she was sure. At each bend, before Ivo called the line, Sable's weight would shift, her paddle would hover, and she would already know what the river did next. Once, at a submerged rock shelf that showed as nothing but a faint seam in the current, she said, "Left of the seam," and Ivo took them left of the seam without asking how she knew, and Broch followed, and they slid through water that would have opened any other boat like a can.

"You've run this river," Wren said that evening, at the cold camp on the gravel shelf where the canyon widened just enough to hold them.

"Everyone's run this river," Sable said.

"No. Everyone's run the *lower* river. The mapped part." Wren spread her hands at the darkening water upstream, at the walls closing in ahead. "This reach doesn't exist on the official sheets. I drew the official sheets. There's a blank here — a deliberate blank, a survey gap that was never filled across four expeditions and thirty years. And you paddle it like a road home."

Sable fed a stick into the tiny fire — Ivo allowed a fire no bigger than two fists — and the light moved on her face. "Maybe I have a good memory for other people's maps."

"Nobody has that good a memory."

"Then maybe," Sable said, and her voice had gone quiet in a new way, "somebody drew a blank where my family has lived for a hundred years, and I'd like to know why."

Wren felt the question land in her own chest like a dropped instrument. *Why.* She had a dozen answers filed and catalogued — administrative convenience, security classification, the usual bureaucratic sediment that accumulates around any powerful thing. She had told herself all of them for three years.

But sitting here, in a canyon that did not officially exist, guiding herself by a thief's memory because her own maps were hollow, the bureaucratic sediment washed away and left something simpler and much worse.

Because the blank wasn't an accident. Blanks were drawn by people who wanted the space empty. And spaces were only worth emptying if there was something in them worth hiding.

* * *

She woke in the grey hour before dawn to voices, low and urgent, and lay still with her eyes closed and listened.

"— the cairns," Broch was saying. "I'm telling you, that's Survey work. Three-stone cairn, flat cap, that's how the chief builds them. That's not a decade old, Eastwick. That's a season old. Maybe less."

A long pause. Wren cracked her eyes to a slit. The fire was down to coals. Ivo crouched at the edge of the gravel shelf, and in his hand was a stone — a flat, grey capstone, the kind surveyors stacked to mark a line. Sable stood beside him, and she was holding one too.

"Count them," Ivo said.

"I counted eleven since the last bend."

"And they're heading upriver."

"They're heading *up*," Sable said. "Not along. Up. Look at the line — it doesn't follow the water. It climbs." She set the capstone down with a strange gentleness, the way you'd set down something that used to belong to you. "Whoever's building these isn't mapping the river. They're mapping a way to the source."

Wren lay in her bedroll and felt the words move through her like cold water through a crack.

The source. The word every Survey document touched and flinched from. The word that had been redacted out of her briefing papers with a blackness so thorough it had torn the page. She had spent her whole career drawing a river and had never once been permitted to ask where it began, and she had told herself that was normal, that every institution had its closed rooms.

But the surveyors hadn't gone silent because they drowned. Drowned men didn't build cairns. The surveyors were alive, and they were moving — deliberately, methodically, in the direction of the one thing the Survey had spent thirty years keeping off its maps.

And Captain Voss had come upstream after them with a commission dated before the silence was known, and had turned back at the first sign of real distance, taking with him the radio, the spare rations, and the only other boat.

Wren closed her eyes and did the thing she did instead of praying. She drew the situation in her head: the cairns climbing, the river falling, Voss's boat going down, and herself — small, exact, complicit — standing in the middle of a map she had helped make empty.

*They found something they shouldn't have,* she thought. *And whatever it was, it scared the right people enough to send the wrong people after it.*

She opened her eyes. Ivo had turned and was looking at her across the dead fire, and she realized with a small, cold shock that he had known she was awake the whole time.

"Cartographer," he said quietly. "Those cairns. Are they on any map you've ever seen?"

Wren sat up. She pulled her satchel over, took out the field sheets, and held them to the coal-light — the official survey of the upper Rivergate, the one bearing her own careful linework, the one with the gap.

"No," she said. "And that's the problem. I drew this river's blanks myself." She looked upstream, where the first light was finding the canyon walls and turning them the color of a held breath. "I think it's time I found out what I was drawing around."
