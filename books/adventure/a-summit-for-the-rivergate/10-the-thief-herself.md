## 10. The Thief Herself

Sable Corren had been watching the party for two days before they ever saw her.

She had learned watching from her father, and her father had learned it from the terraces themselves — how to lie flat in the shadow of a wind-carved ledge until your body forgot its own weight, how to read a camp by its smoke, how to know men by the way they broke trail. So she lay on the high shelf above the side canyon with her cheek against warm stone and counted them as they came up the river path: four now, not seven. Voss had taken his three downriver days ago, and she'd watched that parting too, watched the cartographer woman watch *her* — or watch the space where Sable might be — with eyes that missed nothing and said less.

Four. The ranger, Eastwick, walking point with that stiff desert stride she remembered from years ago, from a boy standing at the edge of a drowning he never spoke of. The cartographer, Halloway, favoring her left knee but keeping pace. Two porters who still flinched at echoes.

And below her, half a mile upstream where the side canyon split from the main gorge like a green vein, water pooled in a stone tank her grandfather had cut before the survey men were born. That was why she'd come back this way at all. Three days without food. The documents heavy against her ribs in their oilskin wrap, heavier every mile, because paper was no good to anyone if she died of hunger holding it.

She waited for the party to pass the canyon mouth. She waited for them to round the bend beyond it. Then she went down for water.

It should have been simple. It had been simple a hundred times: down the goat track her family had worn smooth over four generations, drink fast, fill the skin, climb again before any echo could carry. But starvation makes a thief of even careful people, and she drank too long, and the skin slipped from her hands and struck the stone lip of the tank with a sound like a struck bell.

The echo went up the canyon. And Ivo Eastwick came back around the bend at a dead run.

* * *

She heard him before she saw him — boots on rock, no attempt at quiet, which told her he wanted her to hear, wanted her to run so the chase would justify itself. She almost laughed. Instead she stood up out of the tank's shadow, water dripping off her chin, and let him see her.

He stopped ten feet away, hand on his sidearm, and stared at her as if she were a ghost he'd been paid to believe in.

"Sable Corren," he said.

"You say that like you've been practicing."

"I have." His voice was rougher than she remembered, or maybe she'd only ever heard it young. "Nine days of tracks. You walk on the outside of your feet when you're tired."

"You read my boot prints." She wiped her mouth with the back of her hand. "That's a strange thing to do to a stranger."

"I'm not tracking a stranger. I'm tracking a thief."

"Then you're wasting your time, because the things I stole weren't yours." She saw his eyes go to the oilskin bundle under her jacket, and she shifted her weight so it sat against her spine, out of easy reach. "Go ahead," she said. "Ask me what's in it. Ask me what I traded nine days of hunger to keep out of certain hands."

"I'm not asking you anything. I'm arresting you."

"For what?"

"Theft."

"Of what? Name one thing of mine that's missing." She took a step toward him, deliberately, and watched him not draw. "You can't. Because I didn't steal from people, Ranger. I stole from paper. From a map. From an idea some rich man downstream had about owning a spring he's never seen."

Something moved behind his eyes — not belief, not yet, but the place where belief would go if she could hold him here long enough. Behind him, at the canyon mouth, the others were coming: Halloway limping fast, the porters hanging back with the wariness of men who'd already survived one disaster on this river.

"Halloway," Sable called past him. "Your survey's lower camp. Did you find the journal?"

The cartographer stopped dead. "How did you—"

"Torn page. Right?" Sable kept her eyes on Eastwick. "I took it. Page forty-one. Your chief wrote it the night before they broke camp and went upstream in the dark, off the record, off the maps. Ask her what page forty-one says. Ask her why a hydrographic survey needs a clause about *transfer of rights*."

Halloway's face went through several weathers. "That's not possible. The expedition scope was measurement only—"

"Then ask yourself why I'm starving in a canyon instead of selling what I know." Sable turned back to Eastwick. He hadn't moved. His hand had come off the sidearm, though, and she marked that the way her father would have marked it: the body honest before the mind.

"You were at the source country once," she said quietly. "As a boy."

His jaw tightened. "Who told you that?"

"My father. Before he drowned there." She let that sit in the air between them, ugly and true. "You signed papers after. Debt papers. I've spent six years wondering what a man pays a debt *for*, Ranger, when the man he owed is dead."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"Yes, you do." She stepped closer, close enough to see the salt of dried sweat in the creases of his neck, close enough to see that he was afraid — not of her, but of the sentence she was building. "But you didn't come all this way for me. Voss conscripted you. Nine days of radio silence, and suddenly the only ranger who knows the upper canyons gets drafted into a rescue. Doesn't that arithmetic bother you? A survey goes missing, and the first thing its financier's people do is send a man whose silence they already own?"

"That's—" He stopped. Started again. "You're saying the rescue is a cover."

"I'm saying nothing about the rescue. I'm saying everything about the *rescuer*." She pulled the oilskin bundle out at last, slowly, and held it where they could all see it. "Page forty-one. Survey correspondence. A letter of intent from a man named Aldous Crane — look at the signature block, Halloway, you'll know the name from your own funding lines — offering to purchase 'all water rights appurtenant to the upper Rivergate source, including any rights not presently of record.' Not presently of record. Meaning rights nobody knows exist. Meaning the source itself, bought in the dark before anyone downstream learns it's for sale."

The canyon was very quiet. Somewhere above them a raven made its one dry comment and was gone.

"That letter proves intent," Halloway said at last, carefully, "not action."

"It proves intent, and then your expedition went silent, and then armed men started climbing the river behind us." Sable wrapped the documents again, methodically, making herself slow down. "I was at your middle camp before your party reached it. I watched your chief argue with his own second about turning back. I heard the word *dam*. I heard the word *price per acre-foot*. And when they moved upstream in the night, I took the papers that proved what they were, and I ran, and I've been running since, eating lizards and cactus fruit, because there is no one in the territory I can give these to who isn't already bought."

"And so you gave them to no one," Eastwick said. "You just carried them."

"I carried them *here*." She looked up at the terraces rising tier on tier above the canyon, the wind-carved shelves where her family had hidden water and seed and children through a century of droughts and confiscations. "This is my country. Every seep, every tank, every mark on stone. I was bringing them home to decide what to do with them."

"Home," Eastwick repeated. Something in his voice cracked along an old seam. "You think you have a home. You think these rocks are going to rule on anything."

"They ruled on my family for four generations."

"And they watched your father drown!" It came out of him raw, louder than he meant, and she saw the instant he regretted it — saw the boy underneath the ranger, the one who had been at the water's edge, the one who had run. The porters exchanged a glance. Halloway had gone very still.

Sable felt something turn over in her chest, slow and enormous, like a boulder shifting in a flood. Because she had rehearsed this meeting a hundred times on the trail — the accusation, the contempt, the cold exchange of prisoner for papers — and in none of those rehearsals had he looked at her like *this*: like a man seeing his own guilt wearing another person's face.

"You were there," she said. Not a question.

"I don't remember."

"Liar."

"I don't *talk* about it." His voice had gone flat, controlled, the voice of a man holding a door shut with his whole body. "There's a difference."

"There is," she agreed softly. "My father talked about it constantly and did nothing. Maybe running and talking are the same failure wearing different clothes." She held his gaze. "Or maybe staying silent and staying are. I haven't decided. Have you?"

He didn't answer. Behind him, Halloway cleared her throat — a small, deliberate sound, a woman reinserting herself into a conversation that had become dangerous.

"The letter," Halloway said. "Crane's letter. May I see it?"

Sable weighed her, this cartographer with the careful linework and the gap in her own maps, and decided — on instinct, on exhaustion, on the simple animal logic that a fellow keeper of records would understand what records cost — and handed it across.

Halloway read it twice. When she looked up, her face had the particular grayness of a professional watching her profession get used as a weapon. "The funding line is real. This is our money. Our expedition." She folded the letter along its existing creases, precisely, as if order could contain what she'd just read. "We weren't measuring the source. We were appraising it."

"Now you understand the radio silence," Sable said. "They found it. They confirmed it. And someone decided the confirmation was worth more than the crew."

Eastwick had been silent a long time. Now he spoke, and his voice had changed — lower, stripped of the warrant's authority, just a tired man in a canyon talking to another tired person.

"If I take you in," he said, "the papers go into an evidence locker in a station house that takes its orders from the same money that wrote that letter. If I leave you, I'm abandoning my commission in front of witnesses." He looked at the oilskin bundle, then at her face, then away upstream, toward the source country he had been fleeing and circling for twenty years. "So tell me why I shouldn't do either. Tell me what you actually want."

It was the first honest question anyone had asked her in nine days, and it deserved better than the answer she'd prepared.

"I want my father's name cleaned," she said. "The official record says he was poaching at the headwaters. That he died stealing water. I want the record to say what actually happened — that he died stopping men exactly like the ones your financier sends. And I want these papers somewhere they can't be buried, burned, or bought." She paused. "And I want to know what you saw. Not for me. For him. Someone has to be the witness, Ranger, and it seems the river has been saving you for the job."

"The river," Eastwick said, "has terrible timing."

"So does justice. My father used to say they keep the same office hours."

Despite himself — she saw it happen, saw the corner of his mouth lose its fight — he almost smiled. And in that almost-smile she recognized the shape of the thing she'd been circling for six years: not an enemy. A mirror. A man carrying a debt he'd never examined, the way she'd carried her father's name, both of them bound to the same drowning by different ends of the same rope.

"We rest here tonight," Eastwick said finally, to the group, in his ranger's voice. "Water's good, walls are high, and nobody climbs anything in the dark." Then, to her, quieter: "You're not a prisoner. But you're not free either. You walk with us, you eat with us, and tomorrow you show us what page forty-one points at. Deal?"

"Deal," Sable said. "One condition."

"Name it."

"When we make camp — you sit by my fire. Just you. There's something about my father I need to tell you, and you're going to listen to all of it before you decide what you are to me. Hunter, witness, or something else."

He held her gaze for a long moment. Above them the terraces caught the last of the light and turned the color of old gold; below, the river went on speaking its one word to the stones, over and over, patient as testimony.

"All right," Ivo Eastwick said. "Tonight. By your fire."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the tank, and Sable built the fire small and clean, the way her father had taught her — fuel arranged like an argument, each piece placed to feed the next. She watched Eastwick cross the camp toward her when the others settled, moving like a man reporting to something, and thought: *he doesn't even know he's obeying a summons twenty years old.*

She fed the fire a branch and began at the beginning — the terraces, the seeps, the marks on stone, a family that had kept water alive in dead country while empires drew lines around it. She watched his face as she talked, and she watched the moment the land stopped being terrain to him and became a biography.

And when she reached the part about the night her father left — the argument at the middle camp, the word *dam*, the dark river, the shot she'd heard from a ridge and never found the truth of — she stopped, and looked at him across the flames, and asked the question she'd carried nine days and six years:

"What did you see, Ivo Eastwick? At the water's edge. Tell me, and don't tell me you don't remember — because I've watched you track me by the outside of my own feet, and a man that observant remembers *everything*."

The fire popped. Upstream, far off, thunder muttered in a sky that still held no clouds — the sound of the high country deciding something.

Eastwick opened his mouth. Closed it. And Sable saw, in the play of light on his face, that whatever came next would change the shape of both their lives — that the answer had been waiting in him like water behind a dam, and she had just put her shoulder to the wall.
