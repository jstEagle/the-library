## 11. Terms

She sat with her back against the canyon wall and her wrists loose in the rope, and she looked at him the way a person looks at weather — measuring how long it would last.

Ivo had built a small fire because the side canyon's shadow came early and came cold, and because a man who wanted answers could not get them from someone shivering so hard her teeth knocked like dice in a cup. He'd given her half his jerky and all of his water flask, and now he crouched across the coals from her with the survey documents spread between them on a flat of sandstone, weighted at the corners with stones.

They were good documents. That was the first thing he noticed, and it bothered him more than anything else she'd said since he'd found her. He knew paper. He'd spent eleven years reading warrants, land claims, water rights filings, the small bureaucratic scripture by which the desert was divided up and sold. These had the right weight, the right watermark, the Meridian Survey's seal pressed deep enough to feel with a thumbnail. The ink wasn't faded. They hadn't been carried long.

"You stole these," he said. "From their own camp."

"From Voss's strongbox." She tore a strip of jerky with her teeth. Her wrists turned in the rope without any apparent effort, as if she were simply resting them there. "Third night after they passed the second camp. He kept it under his cot. A man who locks his maps in an iron box in the middle of nowhere is telling you something about the maps."

"And then you ran."

"Then I ran." She shrugged. "You'd have run too."

"I've never stolen anything in my life."

"No," Sable Corren said, and there was something in her voice that made him look up from the papers. "But you've been paid for something. Haven't you, Ranger Eastwick?"

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the junipers on the rim, a sound like slow breathing.

He didn't answer, and she didn't need him to. She leaned forward as far as the rope allowed and tapped one finger against the topmost document — a letter of authorization, signed by a name Ivo didn't know: *Aldous Renner, for the Renner Consolidated Water & Land Company.*

"Do you know who that is?" she asked.

"Should I?"

"He's the man who paid for this expedition. Not the government. Not the Survey itself. Renner. Every ration they eat, every boat they float, every page of that map they're making — his money. And do you know what a financier gets when an expedition finds something?" She smiled, and it wasn't a friendly smile; it was the smile of someone laying out cards. "Whatever he wants."

Ivo turned the letter over. Blank on the back. He set it down carefully, the way he'd set down a snake he wasn't sure was dead.

"You're trying to make me care about the politics of rich men," he said. "I'm a ranger. My jurisdiction ends where that river begins, and my warrant covers exactly one crime, committed by exactly one person, and she's sitting right there."

"Is it theft?" she asked. "What am I accused of? Say it."

"Theft of company property. Flight from—"

"Flight from what? From whom? Who chased me across the salt flats, Ivo? Was it the Survey? Did Captain Voss radio down and say *we've been robbed, send a ranger*?" She shook her head slowly. "No. You were already tracking me before the expedition went quiet. Nine days of silence, your station chief said. But you'd been on my trail for weeks. So ask yourself who wanted me caught badly enough to keep a ranger moving through empty country on a thief's trail, alone, for a month."

The cold came up out of the stone then, or he imagined it did. He fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch.

"Renner," she said softly. "Say the name again."

"Why does it matter to me?"

"Because," Sable said, "it's the name on your debt papers."

* * *

He should have laughed. He almost did — a short, ugly sound that came out of him before he could stop it. "You've been in the country two days ahead of me the whole way. You went through my saddlebags?"

"I don't need to go through your saddlebags." Her eyes were steady and dark and absolutely unblinking. "Everyone in the terraces knows about the ranger with the paper. Eleven years, Ivo. Eleven years you've carried a debt to the family of Tomas Corren, paid quarterly through the Rivergate station, never missed, never questioned. You think that's a secret? The porters talk. The station men talk. A ranger who pays a dead man's family like clockwork — that's a story people tell around fires." She paused. "My fire, especially."

"Tomas Corren died before I ever—" He stopped. The sentence wouldn't finish, because he didn't know how it finished. *Died before I ever met him. Died before I ever saw him. Died before—*

Before what?

There was a hole in his memory where that sentence should have gone, and he'd lived with the hole so long he'd stopped noticing its shape. He'd been a boy. There had been water. There had been a name on a paper that a station clerk had slid across a desk to a fourteen-year-old orphan with no other family and no other prospects, and the clerk had said, *sign here, son, it's an honor, it's restitution,* and he had signed, because fourteen-year-olds sign things, because the alternative was nothing at all.

"What do you know about my father's death?" Sable asked. Not cruelly. Almost gently, which was worse.

"That he drowned," Ivo said. "Upstream. Near the source country. That a boy was involved — some boy who was never named — and that the family was owed compensation, and that I was the compensation." He heard how it sounded, laid out flat like that, and hated it. "That's what I was told. That's what I've believed for twenty years."

"By whom?"

"The station. The clerks. The—"

"By whom, Ivo? Give me a name. One name of a person who told you what happened that day."

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Went back through twenty years of memory, quarter-payments and transfers and the worn paper in his breast pocket, and found — nothing. No witness. No account. No coroner's report he'd ever read. Only the paper itself, and the payments, and the shape of a story everyone seemed to know and no one had ever actually told him.

"One name," Sable repeated. "Take your time."

The fire burned down a notch. In the silence he became aware of how far they were from anything — four days below the second camp, three days above Voss's turnback point, in a slot of red stone that didn't appear on any map Wren Halloway carried. Just the two of them and the coals and the documents, and the river murmuring somewhere below the canyon floor, patient as everything else out here.

"Renner Consolidated," he said finally. "You're saying Renner forged the debt."

"I'm saying the debt exists," she said. "I'm saying my mother received it, every quarter, for nine years until she died, and she never once asked where it came from, because we needed it, and because grief makes you grateful for anything that remembers your dead." Her voice roughened on the last word and smoothed itself immediately, professionally, and he understood that she'd had years of practice smoothing it. "And I'm saying that when I got old enough to ask questions — who sent the money, why, what happened at the water that day — the money stopped coming. Within a season. Like a tap being closed."

"So you stole from them."

"So I stole from them," she agreed. "I spent two years getting hired onto survey support crews. Cooking, mending, carrying. Getting close enough to learn which expeditions Renner funded and what they were really mapping. And when this one started asking questions about the source — actual questions, instrument readings, flow measurements — I knew it was time to see what Voss kept locked up." She nodded at the papers. "Read them. All of them. Not the maps — the letters. Read the third one."

He picked up the third document. It was a memorandum, Renner's letterhead, addressed to Voss personally, and most of it was the usual language of money — contingencies, disbursements, schedules. But near the bottom, in a paragraph that read like it had been added late and initialed twice:

*Re: personnel. Note that the ranger Eastwick remains reliable per our arrangement of long standing. His obligations are well understood and his discretion may be presumed. Should the expedition require a guide familiar with the upper canyons, he is available and motivated.*

Ivo read it twice. Then a third time, though the words didn't change.

"*Our arrangement of long standing*," he said aloud. His voice sounded strange to him, flattened, like sound over the salt flats.

"There it is," Sable said quietly.

"This proves nothing." He dropped the memo onto the stone harder than he meant to. "It's a paragraph. Anyone could have written it. You could have written it — you've had these papers for weeks, you could have—"

"With what? A printing press?" She almost laughed. "Look at the seal again. Look at the watermark. Feel the paper. You're the expert on documents, Ranger — you've been reading them your whole life. Tell me it's forged."

And that was the trap inside the trap, because he couldn't tell him it was forged. The seal was right. The watermark was right. The initials matched the ones on the first page. Everything was right, and that was exactly what a real document looked like, and also exactly what a very good forgery looked like, and he had no way — sitting in a dark canyon with a woman in ropes — to know which.

"I don't believe you," he said.

"I know."

"I think you found those names, put them together, and built a story that turns your theft into a crusade and turns me into your ally. It's a good story. It's aimed at me like an arrow. Of course it lands."

"Yes," Sable said. "It probably is. And it might even be true anyway. Both things can be true, Ivo. That's the part nobody tells you." She shifted against the wall, winced — her ankle, he remembered, the one she'd turned in the scree during the chase — and settled again. "Here's my offer."

"I haven't agreed to an offer."

"Nobody ever agrees to an offer. They just stop disagreeing." She held up her bound hands, counting off fingers. "One: you don't take me downriver. Not yet. Two: you come upstream with me, not to arrest anyone, not to save anyone — just to see. The second camp. Whatever the survey found. Three: if I'm lying, if any single thing I've told you checks false, you can bind me hand and foot and drag me to the station yourself and I won't fight you. I'll give you my word."

"Your word." He let it hang there. "The word of a confessed thief."

"The word of the only person in this canyon who's told you the truth all day." She tilted her head. "Which is more than your employers ever gave you."

He stood up. Walked to the canyon wall. Put his palm flat against the rock because he needed to touch something that wasn't her voice. The stone was cold and enormous and utterly indifferent, and he loved it for that.

The problem — the real problem, beneath all the others — was that the memo explained things he had spent twenty years not explaining. Why a boy with no connection to a drowned stranger had been handed a lifetime obligation instead of a fine or a sentence. Why the payments came through the station rather than the courts. Why no one, in all his asking, had ever given him a straight account of that day at the water. He had built his whole life on top of that gap like a house built over a sinkhole, furnished it, swept its floors, called it duty. And now a thief with rope-burned wrists had walked into his canyon and pointed at the floor and said: *there's nothing underneath.*

If she was lying, she was the finest liar he'd ever met, and he'd met liars who could sell sand to a sandstone quarry.

If she was telling the truth, then everything he was — every mile he'd walked, every payment he'd made, every year he'd given to the badge — had been purchased. Bought and paid for, quarterly, by men who needed a boy's silence more than they needed a boy's soul, and who had gotten both at the same price.

He didn't believe her. He held that thought like a rail in a flood.

But he didn't untie her either, and he noticed, with a kind of distant professional horror, that those two facts had stopped contradicting each other somewhere in the last hour.

* * *

Near midnight he woke to find her watching him across the dead fire, her head tipped back against the stone, her eyes open and reflecting the last of the coals' red.

"You're not sleeping," he said.

"Neither are you."

"I asked you a question earlier you never answered," he said. "In the chase. When I had you at the scree. You said — *you don't even know what you're collecting me for.* What did you mean?"

Sable was quiet for a long moment. Above the canyon rim, a star crossed behind a ridge of cloud and came out the other side.

"I meant," she said, "that rangers don't cross the salt flats alone for a month over a stolen box of papers. Somebody told you to. Somebody with enough pull to conscript you the moment the expedition went quiet — before anyone even knew I was involved. Ask yourself who benefits from bringing me in alive and quiet, Ivo. Dead men talk in rumors. Live prisoners testify." She closed her eyes. "Or don't ask. Sleep. We've got hard country tomorrow, whichever way you decide."

"Whichever way I decide," he echoed.

"You're the one holding the rope," she said. "That's the whole point of the terms. It has to be your choice. Otherwise it's just another arrangement of long standing."

He lay back down and stared at the black seam of sky between the canyon walls, where a few stars showed like holes punched in tin. The memo's words sat in him like swallowed stone. *His obligations are well understood.* Twenty years of obligations, well understood by everyone except him.

Toward dawn he slept a little, and dreamed of water where water shouldn't be, and woke with the name *Renner* in his mouth like a bad taste.

When he rose, Sable was already awake, watching him with that weather-reading patience, waiting to see which way the wind had turned in the night.

"We go upstream," Ivo said. "To the second camp. That's all I'm agreeing to. And the rope stays on."

"Fair terms," she said, and something flickered across her face too fast for him to name — relief, or triumph, or grief — before it was gone.

He kicked dirt over the coals and began breaking camp, and it was only when he reached for his pack that he saw Wren Halloway standing at the mouth of the side canyon, one hand on the rock, her face pale in the early light.

"How long have you been there?" Ivo said.

"Long enough," Wren said. "We have a problem. Come look at the river."
