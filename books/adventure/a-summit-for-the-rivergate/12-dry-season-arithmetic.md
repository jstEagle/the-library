## 12. Dry Season Arithmetic

Wren Halloway had never trusted rivers that shrank. Growing up in the canal towns below the Meridian delta, she had learned that a falling river was a liar — it showed you gravel bars where the charts promised depth, it whispered around bends it had no business whispering around, and it made men believe they had more time than they did. A rising river killed you honestly. A falling one killed you with arithmetic.

She stood at the edge of the water below the second camp's terrace and did the arithmetic now, and she did not like the sum.

"Here," she said, when Ivo and the thief came down the talus slope. She didn't wait for them to reach her. She crouched and set her brass surveying rule against a boulder at the waterline, where a band of wet stone — dark, clean-edged, unmistakable — ran along the rock like a tide mark. "Look at this. That line is yesterday's level. Maybe the day before. The river's dropped four inches since we passed the ford, and we've been moving upstream the whole time."

Sable Corren crouched beside her. The rope still ran from her wrists to Ivo's belt, slack between them, and she had learned to move with it the way a hawk learns a jess — without fighting it, without forgetting it. She touched the wet band with two fingers, then the stone above it, dry and pale.

"Four inches in two days," Sable said.

"Call it two inches a day and I'm being generous." Wren pulled her field book from her jacket, thumbed past the pages of the official Meridian charts — the ones she had drawn, the ones she had reasons now to distrust — and opened to a fresh page. Her pencil was already moving. "The question isn't whether the river's falling. The question is where it stops being a river and becomes a chain of pools."

Ivo stood over them, arms crossed, the morning light catching the gray in his stubble. "How bad?"

"That depends on how honest your maps are." Wren said it flatly, and watched him flinch. Good. He should flinch. She flinched at her own charts every time she opened them now. "The official survey puts the last navigable rapids — the Staircase, the porters called it — thirty-one miles downstream of the second camp. Below that, the canyon widens and the water spreads. Above it, the channel narrows and the gradient steepens. In high water you can pole a boat up to the Staircase and line it through. In low water—"

"In low water it becomes a boneyard," Sable said. "My father called it that. The rocks come up out of the channel like ribs."

Wren looked at her. The thief's face was unreadable, but her voice had gone somewhere else when she said *my father* — down into some older stratum of herself. Wren marked it and moved on. She was a cartographer; she collected features.

"Then let's assume the charts are honest for once," Wren said. "Thirty-one miles of channel above the Staircase. We came up nine miles from the lower camp in a day and a half, and the water was already dropping under us." She turned to a blank page and began to sketch the profile from memory: the long pool below the second camp, the riffle at the bend, the Narrows they'd survived two days ago, the wide gravel shallows where the porter Doss had quit and turned back. "The boats draw eight inches loaded. Call it six, stripped, with everything carried on our backs and the hulls dragged. The river's falling two inches a day and accelerating — dry season doesn't fall linear, it falls like a debt coming due. Slow, slow, then all at once."

She worked the numbers as she talked, because saying them aloud was the only way she trusted them. "Twenty days. Maybe twenty-two if we get a storm on the headwaters and it sends a pulse down. Maybe eighteen if we don't." She underlined the figure twice. "Twenty days before the Staircase becomes a staircase we can't climb with boats, and we're stranded above it with a wounded party, no radio, and winter coming down off the plateau behind us."

Silence, except the river. It sounded different than it had two days ago — thinner, somehow, its voice higher. A river clearing its throat to tell them no.

Ivo crouched and studied the wet band on the stone for a long moment. "Twenty days to do what? Get out?"

"Get out, or stop going up." Wren closed the book. "That's the arithmetic, ranger. Every mile upstream costs us time we cannot buy back. The river only runs one direction, and so does the calendar. Right now we are nine miles above the lower camp and thirty-one above the Staircase. If we turn around today and run the current, we're at the ford in two days, the lower camp in four, the Staircase in six, and we're fine. Every day we go up adds two days to the way down — one to get there, one because the water will be lower when we come back through water we already barely fit through."

"Two days for one," Ivo said.

"Two days for one, compounding." She met his eyes. "I've run this kind of calculation on paper for the Meridian office for eleven years. Routes, supply lines, seasonal windows. It was always abstract. Somebody else's boats, somebody else's season." She tapped the wet stone. "This one is ours."

* * *

They held council on the terrace where the second camp stood emptied, its fire ring cold, its message scratched into the canteen still lying where they'd found it: GOING TO THE SOURCE. Wren had photographed nothing — they had no camera — but she had copied the letters into her field book, tracing them twice, because the handwriting bothered her. She knew the surveyors' hands. She'd spent three years proofing their field notes. The letters were cut deep, gouged with a knife point, by someone writing in anger or in haste or both.

She sat on a flat rock with her book open on her knees and let the others argue while she drew.

Ivo wanted to go on. She could see it in him the way she could see a fault line in a cliff face — not on the surface, but in the way the surface was organized around it. He talked about the missing surveyors, about duty, about the message, but underneath it all was the thief. Sable Corren sat with her roped wrists in her lap and her back against the canteen-scratched stone, and every argument Ivo made, Wren watched him glance at her afterward like a man checking whether a bridge held.

The two porters who remained — Ferro and the boy, Ansel — wanted to go down. Ferro said so plainly, the way he said everything. "The river's telling us the time, and it's telling us to leave." Ansel said nothing, but he looked at the water a lot.

And Wren herself — Wren, who had done the arithmetic, who had the best claim to say *turn back* — sat with her pencil moving and found she could not say it.

Because of the canteen. Because of what the survey had been doing up here. Because of the torn page in the journal at the lower camp, and the instruments packed with more care than a fleeing party usually manages, and the fact that the Meridian Survey — her survey, her maps, her eleven years of careful, honest-seeming lines — had come up this river asking questions the official charts had been drawn specifically never to answer.

She had erased the source approaches herself. Under orders, years ago, a red pen and a supervisor's smile, *cartographic simplification, Halloway, nothing more.* She had told herself it was nothing. She had built a career on the nothing.

And now the nothing had gone silent upriver for nine days, and a thief was wearing its stolen documents against her ribs, and the river was falling like a curtain between them and home.

"Twenty days," Ivo said again, when the argument had gone around twice. "If we push hard. What does hard mean, Wren?"

She looked up. "Fifteen miles a day on the good stretches. Five in the Narrows country. We strip the boats to the frame and drag them. We eat half rations starting now." She turned to a supply page. "We have food for nine days at full ration for six people. At half rations, eighteen. That's not a coincidence — that's the same number twice, and both of them are twenty minus a margin we can't afford." She hated how her voice tightened. She kept it tight anyway. "If anything goes wrong — an injury, a flooded gorge, another ambush, weather — the margin is gone. I want that understood by everyone. We are not a rescue party anymore. We are a bet."

"A bet on what?" Ferro asked.

Wren looked at the canteen, at the gouged letters. "On finding out why the survey stopped answering its radio before the river decides we don't get to ask."

* * *

They spent the afternoon stripping the boats, and Wren worked with her hands while her mind worked the numbers, because the numbers never stopped. That was the truth about her kind of mind that no one warned you about: it was a room you couldn't leave. Eleven years she had lived in it, converting the world into figures, and the figures had always been kind to her — until the day she'd picked up the red pen, and after that the figures had started keeping her instead.

Two inches a day. Thirty-one miles. Eighteen days of food at half rations. Twenty days of water, if the water could be called a promise.

She measured the river again at midday and again at dusk. Two and a quarter inches. Two and a half. The acceleration had begun, exactly as the dry season's curve demanded. She plotted the points in her book and drew the line forward, and the line crossed the six-inch draft threshold at the Staircase on the nineteenth day, and she sat looking at the crossing point for a long time after dark, her lamp turned low, listening to the river get thinner.

Ivo found her there. He came quietly — the man moved quietly for someone built like a door — and stood at the edge of her lamplight with his hands in his pockets.

"You're still up."

"Someone has to know what day it is." She didn't look up. "Out here the days all look alike. The river's the only clock, and it's running down."

He crouched across the fire coals from her, careful to keep the rope's slack between them and the sleeping thief visible. Even now. Even after the side canyon. Wren watched him do it and thought: *he doesn't trust her, and he can't leave her, and he doesn't know which of those is the rope.*

"Ask me," Wren said.

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever you walked over here to ask. You've been standing there for a minute deciding."

He was silent a moment. "You've been with the Meridian a long time," he said finally. "You drew these charts. You know this survey. If they found something up here — something worth going silent over — what would it be worth? To the wrong people?"

Wren turned her pencil over in her fingers. The question was a knife with her own name on the handle.

"A river that rises from a source no one's permitted to see," she said slowly, "is a river that can be sold twice. Once as water. Once as the *idea* of water — the mystery, the permit, the gate. And it can be sold a third time as a grave, if someone decides a dead source is cheaper than a contested one." She looked at him. "I've spent eleven years drawing maps that keep people from asking where it comes from. I told myself it was tidiness. I'm no longer sure it was tidiness."

"You think they found it."

"I think the survey's chief has wanted to find it for as long as I've known him, and I think the men who pay the survey have wanted something else entirely, and I think those two wants were always going to collide up here where no one could hear the radio." She closed the book. "And I think the river is falling two and a half inches a day, and we have nineteen days, and I have not told anyone the worst part yet."

"Which is?"

She turned back a page and showed him: the profile sketch, the line of the channel, the mark she'd made at the Narrows. "The Narrows. We came through it with the water two inches higher. On the way down, if we're a day late, the pools in the Narrows won't connect. We'd have to portage the boats through slot country for six miles. That's three days with a full crew. Four with an injured one." She met his eyes across the coals. "The river doesn't just close behind us, ranger. It closes *in* the middle of us."

Ivo looked at the sketch a long time. The fire had gone to embers, and his face was half light and half dark, and Wren — who had spent her life reducing things to lines and figures — found she could not reduce him at all.

"Then we don't be a day late," he said.

"That's not a plan."

"It's the only plan anyone's ever had." He stood. "Get some sleep, Halloway. You're the only one who knows what day it is. I need you counting."

He went back to his bedroll beside the thief's, and lay down with the rope between them, and Wren sat alone with her lamp and her book and the sound of the water getting thinner, and she did the arithmetic one more time, because the numbers were the only prayer she knew.

Nineteen days. Two days for one, compounding. A bet.

She was still awake when she heard it — faint, from the dark terrace above the camp, the sound of a boot on stone where no one should be walking. She lifted the lamp. The light found nothing but rock and wind.

But the river, falling in the dark below her, seemed suddenly very loud, like a clock that had learned to shout.
