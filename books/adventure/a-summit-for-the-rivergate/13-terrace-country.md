## 13. Terrace Country

The canyon let them go at dawn.

One moment the walls were pressing close enough that Sable could have touched both sides with her outstretched hands, and the next they opened like a fist unclenching, and the light came pouring down onto a country of steps — great flat shelves of red and gold stone, stacked one above another, each terrace a hundred feet wide or more, carved by wind that had been working longer than any nation had existed. The river ran somewhere below, hidden in its own cut, but they could hear it, a low continuous murmur under everything, like a voice speaking just beneath the floor of the world.

Sable stopped at the rim and let the others come up past her.

She had not meant to feel anything. She had told herself, all through the Narrows and the flooded gorge and the long cold climb out of it, that this was only country, only stone, that she could cross it the way she crossed any country — reading it, using it, leaving nothing of herself behind. But standing here at the top of the first terrace with the morning wind coming off the high plateau and carrying the smell of sage and sun-warmed rock, she felt something in her chest unlock that she had kept locked for eleven years.

"This is where you're from," Wren Halloway said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Where my mother's people wintered." Sable heard her own voice come out flatter than she intended. "My father married into it. He learned it after her. Learned it well enough to teach me."

Ivo Eastwick stood a few feet away, looking out over the terraces with his ranger's eyes — measuring distances, noting shade lines, doing whatever arithmetic rangers did when they looked at open ground. She watched him do it and felt an old irritation rise, and then watched it falter as he went still instead. Something in the scale of the place had gotten past his training. His mouth was slightly open. The wind moved his hair.

"It doesn't look real," he said finally. "Like somebody built it."

"The wind built it. Give it ten thousand years and no one to stop it, and it builds whatever it wants."

"How far across?"

"Two days' walking, if we take the routes. Four if we don't." She shouldered her pack. "Stay on my heels. And nobody wanders. This country looks gentle from up here. It isn't."

* * *

The first thing she showed them was a seep that no map would ever hold.

It came halfway through the first morning, in the shadow of an overhang where the terrace rock folded back on itself like a page turned down at the corner. There was nothing to see — that was the point. A smear of darker stone, a fringe of maidenhair fern no bigger than a hand, a smell of wet that the wind almost erased. Sable crouched, pushed back the fern, and pressed her palm to the rock. Cold water beaded against her skin and ran down her wrist.

"Drink," she said. "Fill everything. We won't find water again before nightfall."

Wren knelt beside her with the careful reverence of a woman handling evidence. "How do you find it? There's no drainage. No vegetation line. Nothing."

"You listen." Sable held up a hand. They waited. Under the wind, under the distant river, there was a third sound — faint, arrhythmic, a dripping somewhere inside the stone. "The water comes through cracks from the plateau above. It takes days to get here. My mother called these the slow springs. She said the mountain thinks about giving you a drink for a long time before it does."

"And your father marked them?" Ivo asked.

Sable didn't answer right away. She got up, walked three paces along the base of the overhang, and pointed. "There."

At first none of them saw it. Then Wren made a sound low in her throat and stepped closer, and the mark resolved out of the stone: a spiral, no bigger than a dinner plate, pecked into the rock with a tool finer than a hammer — hundreds of small strikes, patient work, the kind of work a person does when they expect someone else to need it someday. From the spiral's center, a single groove ran downward and left, pointing along the terrace face.

"Spiral means water," Sable said. "The tail shows which way to walk. If the tail curls inward, the water's close — within a mile. Outward, farther. Two tails means running water year-round. One tail means seasonal." She traced the groove without touching it. "He cut these over thirty years. Every seep between here and the source country. Hundreds of them. He used to say a map can be taken from you, burned, sold. Marks in stone stay where you put them."

"He was preparing a route," Voss said. The survey captain had hung back, as he always did, but his eyes had gone bright and hungry in a way Sable distrusted on principle. "A complete water network, undocumented. Do you understand what this is? Hydrologically, this is—"

"My father's courtesy," Sable said, "to anyone dying of thirst who knew how to read it. That's what it is. That's all it is."

Voss closed his mouth. But she saw him look at the spiral again, and she saw him thinking in numbers, and she filed that away with the other things about Anselm Voss she intended not to forget.

They filled their canteens and moved on.

* * *

Near midday they crossed a terrace where the wind had done stranger work than carving shelves. Here the soft layer beneath the hard caprock had eroded into arches and windows — holes in the stone the size of doorways, some big enough to ride a horse through, framing slices of sky and neighboring cliffs so precisely placed they looked deliberate. The light came through them in long amber blades and lay across the ground in shapes that shifted as the sun climbed.

Sable led them through without slowing, but she counted the arches as she passed. Seven. There had been seven when she was nine years old, when her mother had stood under the fourth one and taught her the whistle that carried through them — a rising-falling note that the stone caught and repeated and bent, so that a call made at the first arch came out the seventh sounding like it came from underground, from below the world, from the direction of the river's hidden heart.

On impulse, she stopped under the fourth arch, filled her lungs, and whistled.

The note went in thin and came out the far arches one after another, each repetition lower and stranger, until the last of it seemed to rise out of the rock itself rather than pass through it. The party had gone quiet. Even the porters, who had barely spoken in two days, had stopped to listen.

"That's how they talked across the terraces," Sable said. Her voice sounded odd to her, loosened. "No fires at night, no signals a stranger would see. Just the whistle. You could tell who was calling by how they bent the note. My mother could name every family within twenty miles by one phrase."

"Could you?" Ivo asked.

"Some of them." She looked at him. "Not mine anymore. Voices change. Mine changed."

He didn't say anything to that, which was, she was learning, one of the tolerable things about him. He just nodded once and looked out through the nearest window-arch at the terraces falling away below, golden in the noon light, and she caught him doing it again — seeing the country not as a ranger sees it, not as ground to be searched or crossed, but as something inhabited. As if her memory had gotten into him somehow, through the whistle or the seep or the spiral marks, and was showing him things his own eyes couldn't.

She didn't know what to do with that. She turned and walked on, faster than she needed to.

* * *

In the afternoon she took them down a route that wasn't a route.

It began at a break in the terrace rim that looked like nothing — a crack too narrow for a man with a pack — and became, forty feet down, a staircase of natural ledges, each one exactly the height of a tired person's comfortable step. Not carved. Chosen. Someone had found these stones, one at a time, over generations, and used them so often that the surfaces were worn smooth and slightly hollowed, polished by centuries of boots and moccasins and bare feet.

"Your father's work too?" Wren asked, half sliding, half climbing.

"His grandmother's. Maybe older. He improved it." Sable dropped the last six feet onto the lower terrace and turned to catch Wren's arm as the cartographer landed. "There are eleven ways down this rim between here and the gorge. Your maps show two. Both wrong, or both watched, depending on who drew them."

Wren went very still. "Who else draws maps out here?"

"Anyone paid to." Sable let that sit. She watched Wren's face do something complicated — guilt and calculation and fear braided together — and decided not to push it. Not yet. There would be time, later, alone, to ask the cartographer what she'd erased and for whom. Some questions you asked in daylight with witnesses. Others you saved.

Below them, the new terrace opened wide and green-edged, and Sable's breath caught despite herself, because she knew what grew here and she hadn't let herself think about it in years.

Wild plums. A whole stand of them, stunted and wind-bent, hugging the lee side of a rock fin where snowmelt lingered latest. Her father had brought her here every autumn of her childhood, had lifted her up into the branches while her mother spread a blanket below, and they had eaten until their mouths were raw and their fingers purple, and her father had told her — every year, the same joke — that a person could navigate the whole Rivergate by plum trees, because plums only grew where water had been generous sometime in the last hundred years, and water was never generous twice in the same place unless it was faithful.

"Go on," she said gruffly, waving at the trees. "Eat. We have miles yet, and sugar keeps legs working better than jerky does."

They ate. Even Voss ate, standing apart, methodical, but he ate. Ivo sat down against the fin with a handful of fruit and looked out at the view the terrace offered — the whole stair-step country descending westward into haze, the river's canyon a dark seam stitched through it — and Sable found herself sitting near him without having decided to.

"You knew this place," he said. Not a question either.

"I grew up on it."

"No. I mean—" He turned a plum over in his fingers. "You knew it the way I know the flats. Where the salt crust holds a horse and where it doesn't. Which dunes move and which ones pretend to. I've been watching you all day. You don't look at the ground to find the trail. You already know where it is before you look."

"Eleven years is a long time. Things change."

"Not this kind of thing." He ate the plum. "This kind of thing stays in the body."

She wanted to argue. Instead she heard herself say, "Under the fifth arch, there's a cache. My family's. Dried meat, seed grain, a medicine bundle. Sealed in clay jars. It'll be eleven years spoiled now, probably, but the seal might have held." She stared out at the haze. "I'm telling you because we may need it coming back. And because—" She stopped. Started again, angrier at herself. "Because you should know I'm not leading you anywhere blind. Whatever you think I am. Thief, guide, liar. On this country, I'm none of those things. On this country I'm the Correns' daughter, and the Correns' daughter doesn't lose people."

Ivo was quiet a moment. "I believe you," he said.

"Don't. Believing people is how rangers die."

"Probably." He stood, brushed off his trousers, and offered her the last plum from his handful. "But I've been believing the wrong things written on paper for fifteen years. Figured I'd try believing something I've seen with my own eyes for a change."

She took the plum. She didn't thank him. But she ate it walking beside him rather than ahead of him, and if he noticed the difference, he had the grace not to say so.

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow beneath a terrace wall that held the day's heat like a banked oven. Sable built the fire small and shielded, out of habit, though there was no one for fifty miles to see it — or so she believed. While the others slept, she walked the rim alone, as she had every night since the Narrows, checking their backtrail.

The stars were thickening overhead when she saw it.

Not on the backtrail. Ahead. Far out on the highest terrace, where the last light still touched the caprock, something moved — a shape, upright, brief, gone. A man's silhouette against the sky, there and then not there, the way a heron vanishes when it turns sideways to you.

She dropped flat behind a rock and waited, counting her own heartbeats. One hundred. Two hundred. Nothing moved. The wind came up the terraces in long slow breaths, and the plum trees rustled below, and the river murmured in its hidden cut, saying whatever it always said.

But she had grown up on this country, and the country had taught her its arithmetic, and the arithmetic said: no heron stands on dry stone at dusk. No shadow falls where nothing casts it. Someone was out there, ahead of them, on the high terraces — someone who moved when they moved and stopped when they stopped, and who had been doing it long enough to be good at it.

She lay in the dark a long time, watching the rim, and thought about her father's spirals, and who else besides her family had ever learned to read them.

Then she went down and woke Ivo Eastwick, quietly, with a hand on his shoulder, and told him what she'd seen.
