## 14. The Watcher

The first time Ivo saw the rider, he almost didn't see him at all.

It was midmorning on the fourth day above the split, and the party had come up out of a side drainage onto a long shelf of red sandstone that ran like a road along the river's shoulder. The country had opened again after the Narrows — terraced benches stacked like fallen masonry, juniper scattered thin and dark against the pale rock, the river below them running green and quiet in its gorge. Good walking. Too good. Ivo had learned years ago that easy ground made people careless, and he was checking their backtrail out of habit when the movement caught his eye.

A quarter mile behind and three hundred feet above, on the rim of the bench they'd just crossed: a horse. A man on it. Both motionless.

By the time Ivo's hand found Wren Halloway's sleeve, the rim was empty.

"You saw something," she said. Not a question. She was a small woman with ink-stained fingers and eyes that missed nothing she chose to look at, and she was looking at him now with the particular patience of someone who had spent her life being told things twice.

"A rider. Up top." He shaded his eyes and studied the skyline where the figure had been. Nothing moved but heat shimmer off the stone. "Dark horse. Standing still."

"Standing still is worse than moving," Sable said.

She had come up beside them without sound, as she always did, and she stood with her weight on her heels and her face turned toward the rim the way other people turned toward weather. Four days since the side canyon where Ivo had taken her alive, four days of her walking between them in borrowed boots with her wrists free because there had been no rope to spare and no point in pretending she'd run — and in those four days she had not once looked at the country behind them until now.

"Why worse?" Wren asked.

"Because a man who's following you moves when you move and stops when you stop. He wants to keep pace. But a man who stands still—" She paused. "He's already where he's going. He's waiting to see if you'll come to him."

The words settled over the party like a change in temperature. Behind them, the two porters — Teodor and the boy called Finch — had stopped loading the packs and were watching the exchange with the flat expressions of men who understood that whatever was being discussed would be carried on their backs.

"Could be Voss," Finch offered. "Downriver party might've climbed out, circled—"

"Voss has no horses," Ivo said. "They took the boats down through the lower rapids. There's no way to get stock up this side of the gorge." He heard how certain he sounded and hated it, because certainty was the one thing he couldn't afford anymore. Every certainty he'd started this journey with had been cracked or stolen: the thief was a stranger; the debt was honest; the trail led somewhere an arrest could end it. Now he tracked a woman he no longer believed was a thief, toward a source he no longer believed was forbidden for any reason but shame, carrying papers he no longer believed were true.

"Then who?" Wren asked.

Nobody answered. The river answered instead, sliding past below with its low continuous voice, saying nothing.

* * *

They made nine miles that day, and Ivo counted every one of them against the skyline.

He developed a system without deciding to. Every hour, at some unremarkable pause — a water break, a strap adjusted, a boot examined for nothing — he would put his back to the party and sweep the rims. North rim, south rim, the notches and shoulders where the plateau broke apart into towers and fins. He never told the others he was doing it. He told himself it was habit, ranger's discipline, the same way he'd once told himself the debt paper in his breast pocket was just paper.

Twice more that day, he saw the horse.

The second time was early afternoon, far ahead of them rather than behind — a dark speck crossing a distant saddle, moving with the unhurried walk of an animal that knew exactly where it was going. When Ivo raised his field glasses the saddle was empty. He checked the wind. It came out of the west, steady, carrying their scent away from anyone east of them, which meant the rider could be smelling them all day if he held the high ground.

The third time was near dusk, and it was the worst one, because this time the horse wasn't alone in his attention. This time there was a glint — a single flash of reflected light from the rim above their campsite-to-be, there and gone, like a coin dropped into deep water. Glass. Someone had glass, and someone had been looking at the exact shelf of stone where Sable had marked a seep for their evening water.

"We're not camping there," Ivo said.

Sable looked at him. "There's no other water inside six miles."

"Then we take water and move on. Camp dry."

"It's eight miles to the next seep. In the dark. Over broken ground." Her voice was level, but he could hear the edge under it, the same edge that had been in her voice in the side canyon when she'd told him the survey was hunting the source to sell it. "You're chasing a shine off a rock. That rim catches sun until the last minute. Mica does that. Quartz does that. Birds do that, if you want to be frightened by birds."

"I know what mica looks like."

"Do you? Or do you know what fear looks like and you're calling it mica?"

The porters had gone very still. Wren was watching them both with her notebook half-raised, the way she watched everything now, recording even the arguments — especially the arguments, Ivo suspected, because Wren Halloway had stopped believing in coincidences somewhere around the second emptied camp, and she was building a case out of observations the way other people built fires.

"We move," Ivo said. "All of us. Now."

Sable held his eyes for a long moment. Then she shrugged, shouldered her pack, and walked, and it occurred to Ivo, watching her go, that she had obeyed him faster than she needed to — and that obedience from Sable Corren was itself a kind of information. She wanted to move too. She knew something about who might be on that rim, and she wasn't sharing it, and he filed that away in the place where he kept everything else he didn't yet understand about her.

They walked the last eight miles by starlight, feeling for the trail with their feet, and camped dry in a hollow of black rock with no fire and cold food. Nobody complained. That was the thing Ivo noticed most — nobody complained, nobody asked why, and that silence was louder than any argument. They were all counting the same arithmetic now. Something was on the rims around them, patient as stone, and whatever it wanted, it wanted badly enough to follow four days without closing.

Ivo took first watch. He sat with his back against the rock and the rifle across his knees and watched the stars wheel, and he thought about the last time he had felt watched like this, and the memory rose in him the way floodwater rises in a slot canyon — fast, silent, from below.

He had been eleven years old, and he had been hiding in the rocks above a place he was not supposed to be, and men had been working the water with tools and lanterns, and one of them had looked up — straight up, straight at him, or so it had seemed in that terrible instant — before the shouting started downstream and the lanterns went tumbling and a man's voice cried out and cut off. Ivo had run. He had run for miles in the dark with his heart slamming, certain the whole way that he was being followed, and no one had ever been there, and he had carried that certainty home like a stone in his chest and set it down and never picked it up again.

Until now.

* * *

The radio woke them all an hour before dawn.

It was Wren's set — a Meridian Survey unit, salvaged from the middle camp, heavy as sin and nearly dead. They'd managed one transmission a night on the last of its charge, cranking the generator while the rest of the party stood in the dark listening to static breathe. Each night Ivo had called the station at Rivergate and each night gotten nothing but the long hiss of empty frequencies, and each night the silence had felt less like malfunction and more like a door closing.

Tonight, the static spoke.

"—do not— repeat, do not—" A man's voice, shredded by distance and interference, vowels stretched into ghosts. Wren was already cranking, leaning into the generator with her whole slight body, and the voice surged and faded like something drowning. "—Voss. We are— boats gone, we are on foot at— can't hold this frequency—"

"Voss!" Ivo grabbed the handset. "Captain Voss, this is Eastwick. Say your position. Say your position."

Static. Then, impossibly clear for one second, close enough that Ivo could hear the man breathing behind the words:

"—they're not surveyors—"

And then the hiss came back and swallowed everything, and Wren cranked until her arms shook, and the hiss stayed hiss, and after ten minutes the radio gave a small internal click and died altogether, and the dark came back in around them like water filling a footprint.

Nobody spoke for a while. The stars were hard and white overhead. Somewhere down the canyon, the river kept its one long sentence going.

"They're not surveyors," Finch repeated finally. His voice was very young in the dark. "Who isn't?"

"The men who found Voss's party," Sable said quietly. "Or the men Voss's party found. Either way — someone reached them. Someone reached them days ago, and tonight they got one breath out on a dying battery before that someone stopped them."

"You don't know that," Teodor said. "Radios fail. Batteries die. Mountains eat signals. My uncle lost a whole winter to a mountain that ate signals."

"Yes," Sable said. "That's possible." She said it the way people say *yes, the river might spare you*, and nobody found it comforting.

Ivo crouched by the dead radio and turned it over in his hands as if the casing might explain itself. His mind was laying tracks the way water lays tracks — finding the lowest line, the inevitable one. Voss had taken three men downriver nine days ago to raise the alarm and bring help. Instead: boats gone, on foot, one broken transmission, silence. And above them, on the rims, a rider who never approached and never left, pacing them day after day like a shepherd pacing a flock.

Not scouting them. Counting them.

"He's not following us," Ivo said aloud. The others turned. "He's herding. Keeping us on this route. Every time we've had a choice — the fork at the red benches, the side canyon yesterday — he's shown himself on one branch or the other. Showing himself is steering. Somebody wants us going up this canyon, toward the source, and they want to know exactly where we are while we do it."

"Or they want us tired," Wren said. "Dry camps, forced marches, no fires. Two more nights of this and we're stumbling."

"That too." Ivo looked at Sable. In the starlight her face was all planes and shadow, unreadable, and he realized he was about to ask her a question in front of everyone that he should have asked alone — and asked it anyway, because the hour was too late for tact. "Whoever's on that rim. Do you know them?"

A pause. Long enough to mean something.

"I know who hires riders in this country," she said. "Same kind of men who hire surveyors. Same money. You've seen the marks they leave — the diverted channels, the torn journal pages, the camps stripped clean. This is just the same work done slower."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

Finch laughed — one short, nervous bark that died as soon as it left him. Teodor muttered something in the old river dialect, a word for bad water. Wren wrote in her notebook by feel, the pencil scratching in the dark like an insect.

Ivo stood up and faced the canyon walls, north and south, invisible now except as deeper blackness against the black sky. Somewhere up on one of them a man sat a dark horse and waited, and somewhere down behind them three men from Voss's party had either failed to reach help or been prevented from reaching it, and somewhere ahead — two days, maybe three, if the terraces went easy — lay the springs, and whatever truth the whole country had been arranged to hide.

His breast pocket felt heavier than the rifle. He didn't touch the papers in it. He hadn't touched them in days, but he felt them the way you feel a splinter gone too deep — not pain exactly, just a knowledge of something wrong lodged under the skin, working its way toward the surface whether he wanted it out or not.

Sable had told him the debt was forged. Had told him the money that raised him out of his father's ruin had been hush money, bought to seal a child's mouth about what he'd seen at the water. He had called it a lie. He had kept calling it a lie through the Narrows and the empty camps and the long climb, because the alternative was to admit that his whole life — every dry season, every warrant, every mile of competent lonely tracking — had been built on a foundation somebody else had poured, for purposes of their own.

But the rider on the rim changed the arithmetic. Men don't send riders after a thief. Thieves get warrants. What rode the rims above them, patient and dark, was the shape of something much older and much larger — the shape of the thing that had been working the water with lanterns when he was eleven years old, still working it, still erasing whoever came too close.

"First light," Ivo said, "we move fast and we stop being predictable. No more marked seeps. No more open benches. Sable — you grew up in this country. Take us the way your father would have gone."

Something moved through her expression, quick as the glint on the rim — surprise, or grief, or both wearing the same face.

"And Eastwick," she said. "When we stop tonight. There's something you need to tell them. All of it. About what you saw."

"I don't know what you—"

"You do." Her voice was quiet and absolute. "You've known since the side canyon. And whatever's riding these rims, whatever killed the radio, whatever's coming up this river behind us — you can't outrun it carrying that stone. None of us can. Tonight, Ivo. Tell them what you saw at the water."

Above them, faint as breath, a pebble clicked loose from the southern rim and fell a long way down.

Nobody moved. Nobody breathed. And in the silence that followed, Ivo Eastwick understood that the watcher had heard every word.
