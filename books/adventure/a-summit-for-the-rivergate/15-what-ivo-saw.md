## 15. What Ivo Saw

They made camp in a hollow of red stone where the wind couldn't reach them, a cup of rock the size of a chapel with a floor of sand and fallen slabs. Sable chose it without hesitation — she had been choosing their camps for three days now, reading the terraces the way Ivo read salt flats, and no one argued. Wren Halloway sat against a boulder with her maps wrapped in oilcloth on her knees, guarding them like scripture. The two porters, Hallam and the boy called Finch, built a fire small enough to hide behind a screen of stone, because Sable said smoke would show on the rims at dusk and she was right about everything lately.

Ivo didn't eat.

He sat apart, as he always did, his back to the warm rock, turning his compass over in his hands. The needle swung and settled, swung and settled, pointing faithfully toward a source he was walking toward with a lie strapped to his back like a bedroll. The debt paper was folded in his breast pocket, soft as cloth now from years of carrying, its creases gone gray. He could feel it there the way you feel a splinter — not painful, exactly, just present, just wrong.

Sable Corren sat across the fire and watched him not eating.

She didn't say anything. She'd said her piece on the trail that morning, in front of everyone, and she'd let it lie all day like a snare set across the path. That was her way. She never rushed a thing. She let the country do the work.

The others felt it too. Hallam kept glancing between them over his tin cup. Finch asked twice if anyone wanted more tea and got no answer either time. Even Wren stopped writing in her field book, pencil hovering, listening to the fire tick.

"You knew my father," Sable said at last. Not a question. Not an accusation. Just a fact laid on the sand between them, plain as a stone.

"I knew of him," Ivo said.

"That's not what I asked."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, on the southern rim, the wind moved through a crack in the rock and made a low moaning note, like breath drawn through teeth.

"His name was Tomas Corren," Sable said. "He worked the upper water. Seeps, springs, the little channels that feed the river before anyone thought to call it a river yet. He read stone the way your surveyors read instruments." Her voice stayed level, but Ivo heard what was under it, the way you hear water under gravel. "He went out one autumn when I was nine years old and didn't come back. They told us he fell. A slip at a gorge rim. It happens out here. Men fall."

"Yes," Ivo said. "It happens."

"But you were there." She leaned forward. The firelight moved on her face and made her eyes hard to read, bright and dark at once. "You were a boy, and you were there, and whatever happened that autumn is the reason a man who's never met me has carried my family's name on a piece of paper for twenty years. So don't tell me what you knew *of* him. Tell me what you saw."

Hallam set down his cup. Finch went very still. Wren closed her field book without marking her place.

Ivo looked at the fire. He had rehearsed this confession a hundred times over twenty years, alone on night watches, alone on the flats, alone in the station bunkhouse while other men slept — and every rehearsal had ended differently, with different words, with him braver or more eloquent or less ashamed. None of the rehearsals survived contact with the actual moment. There were no good words. There was only the thing itself, and the thing itself was this:

"I ran," he said.

Nobody spoke. The word hung in the hollow of the rock and echoed off it, small and enormous.

"I was eleven years old," Ivo said. "My father ran freight mules through the lower canyons, and that season he took me along because my mother had died in the spring and he didn't know what else to do with me. We were camped below the last narrows — up past where any map stops pretending to be accurate. There was a side canyon there. Dry most of the year. My father told me never to go up it, which of course meant I went up it."

He turned the compass over again. The needle trembled.

"There's a place up that canyon where the rock opens out. You wouldn't call it a valley. More like a throat that swallowed a valley. And in the middle of it there's water coming up out of the ground — not a spring, not seeps. Water. Coming up like it was being pushed from underneath by something with lungs. Pools, channels, green in a country that has no business being green. I'd never seen anything like it. I didn't know it existed. Nobody's supposed to know it exists. That's the whole law of the Rivergate — the river rises where it rises, and no man goes to see, and no man asks why."

"And someone was there," Wren said quietly. Not pushing. Naming the shape of it so he didn't have to carry the whole weight alone.

"Men," Ivo said. "Six of them, maybe seven. With mules and timber and tools I didn't have names for then. Dams. Small ones, low ones, built across the outflow channels. They weren't taking the water. They were *backing it up*. Diverting it. Measuring how much came out and where, and then changing where. I stood behind a slab of rock and watched them work for the better part of an hour, and I understood even at eleven that I was looking at something that was going to get people hurt."

Sable hadn't moved. Her hands were wrapped around her knees, knuckles white, and her eyes never left him.

"One man wasn't with them," Ivo said. "One man came up the canyon from the other side, quiet as weather. Older. Gray in his beard. He had a rifle he never raised and a voice he used instead, and he walked right down among those men and told them to break their dams and go home. And they laughed at him. Two of them did. The others looked nervous. And then—" His throat closed. He made himself go on, because he owed it, because running away from this sentence was the whole shape of his life and he was done with that shape. "Then one of them shoved him. Near the main pool. Where the rock shelves down under the water and you can't see the drop until you're over it. My father told me about that shelf once, years later, without knowing why I asked. The water over it looks like it's six inches deep. It's ten feet."

"He didn't come up," Sable said. Her voice had gone very flat, the way voices go when feeling has gotten too large to show expression through.

"No. He didn't come up. They panicked — some of them. One tried to go in after him and the others pulled him back, shouting about the current, about the undertow where the spring pushes up. And then they started arguing about what to do, and the argument got loud, and I—" Ivo's hands had stopped moving. The compass lay still in his palm, needle swinging in tiny useless arcs. "I ran. Down the canyon, past our camp, I didn't stop, I ran until dark. I told my father men were fighting up the side canyon and a man might be drowned and I didn't say I'd seen it, I said I'd *heard* it, because I was afraid of being caught where I shouldn't have been. And my father went up with two other freight men with ropes, and they found nothing, because by then the others had broken camp and gone, and the pools had settled, and the water doesn't keep bodies, Ivo's father said later, the water gives everything back to the rock."

Silence. The fire burned low. Finch fed it a stick with a hand that shook slightly.

"The debt," Sable said.

"Three weeks later," Ivo said. "A man came to our house in Terrapin Wells. Well-dressed. Soft hands. He had papers, and my father signed them because the alternative was trouble — the kind of trouble that comes to freight men whose contracts can be taken away. The paper said the family of Tomas Corren had claim against me for trespass and disturbance, and that the claim would be satisfied by my service — service to be directed as the family's trustees saw fit, for a term of years, in exchange for silence." He laughed, and it came out wrong, dry and awful. "Silence. An eleven-year-old's silence. They wrote it up like a contract because that's what soft-handed men do. They make shame look like arithmetic."

"Who sent him?" Wren asked. "Did the paper bear a name?"

"A firm name. Meridian Holdings." He watched Wren's face change. "You know it."

"Everyone in the survey world knows Meridian Holdings," Wren said slowly. "They fund half the expeditions in the territory. Voss's expedition is Meridian money. Half the maps I've ever inked were paid for by—"

"By the same soft hands," Ivo finished. "Yes."

Sable unfolded herself and stood. For a moment Ivo thought she was going to strike him, and he found, to his own surprise, that he would have accepted it. But she didn't. She walked to the edge of the firelight and stood there with her back half-turned, looking up at the strip of stars visible between the canyon walls, and when she spoke again her voice had a crack in it that she didn't try to hide.

"They told us he fell."

"I know."

"My mother spent four years writing letters. To the rangers, to the territorial office, to anyone with a stamp. Asking them to search the upper country for him. Every letter came back the same way — *the source approaches are closed, madam, for your safety.* Closed. By order of whom? By order of the men who killed him, that's who. They closed the country so no one would ever find what was left of him, and they bought a child's silence to seal it shut, and they let a widow write letters into a drawer for four years." She turned around. In the firelight her face was wet and furious and, worse than either, young. "And you *signed*, Ivo. You were a child, they made you sign, I understand that. But you grew up. You became a ranger. You had a badge and a horse and twenty years, and you never once went back up that canyon. You never once asked what happened to the body. You carried the debt like it was holy."

"Because the alternative was that it was filth," Ivo said. "It was easier to owe than to know. If the debt was real, then I was paying something real. If it was real, then my silence had a price and the price was being paid and the ledger balanced." He looked down at the compass in his hand. "But it never balanced. Twenty years, and it never once balanced. Because a debt you pay isn't supposed to feel like this. It's supposed to lighten. This only got heavier."

Wren opened her field book again and began, very quietly, to write. Hallam stared into the fire. Finch had pulled his knees up to his chest like a much smaller boy.

"The watcher," Hallam said at last, gruffly, because somebody had to say it. "On the rim. Whatever's been following us since the Narrows. You think it's connected?"

"It's connected to everything," Sable said. "Meridian Holdings funds the survey. The survey goes silent after reaching the upper water. Meridian's hired men start riding our rims the same week. And twenty years ago, Meridian's agent bought the silence of the only witness to what they did at the source." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, roughly, like closing a gate. "This isn't a rescue anymore. It never was. We're walking up the spine of something that's been buried a long time, and the men who buried it are making sure it stays buried."

"Then we should know everything," Wren said, "before it buries us too." She looked at Ivo, pencil poised. "The men at the pools. Could you identify them? Any of them?"

Ivo was silent a long moment. The fire had burned down to coals, and the coals put out a deep red light that made the hollow feel older than it was, like standing inside the earth's own memory.

"One of them," he said finally. "The one who shoved him. He was younger than the rest, and he wore a ring — a heavy signet, gold, with a mark on it I didn't recognize as a boy. A triangle with a line through it." He looked up. "Twenty years later, I saw that same mark stamped on the crates we loaded at the Rivergate station for Voss's expedition. Survey instruments. Meridian property."

Wren's pencil stopped.

"So the man who pushed my father," Sable said slowly, "is with this expedition. Or was. Or sent it."

"Or is waiting for it," Ivo said, "upstream."

No one answered that. Above them the wind changed direction, and the moaning note in the rock died away, and in the new silence they all heard it at once: a sound from the south rim, faint and far, that none of them could quite name and none of them could dismiss. Not a pebble this time. Something heavier. A boot sole on stone, perhaps, or the creak of a saddle shifting. It came once and did not come again.

Finch whispered, "Do you think it heard?"

"It's heard everything since the Narrows," Sable said. She crouched and began scattering the coals with a stick, killing the fire methodically, her face unreadable again. "So we stop whispering around it. Tomorrow we move fast and we stop being followed. And tonight—" she glanced at Ivo, and something passed between them that had no anger left in it, only exhaustion and a strange, unwilling closeness, "—tonight nobody sleeps alone. Two-hour watches, pairs. I'll take first with Eastwick."

The camp settled into blankets and silence. Ivo sat with his back to the warm rock, the debt paper a gray ghost of weight against his chest, and watched the stars wheel in the slot of sky above the hollow. Beside him Sable Corren kept watch with her father's rifle across her knees, and neither of them spoke, because everything had already been said, and because what remained unsaid between them was larger than anything they'd managed to put into words.

An hour into the watch, her hand found his in the dark. She didn't look at him. He didn't look at her. Her grip was fierce and brief and gone, and it undid him more thoroughly than any accusation had.

"For the record," she murmured, eyes on the rim, "my father would have liked you. He collected strays who ran."

And from the darkness of the southern rim, clear across the mile of empty air, came the thin, unmistakable sound of a man laughing.
