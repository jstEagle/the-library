## 16. Cold Camp

The cold came down off the rim like something poured.

Ivo had chosen the camp badly and knew it — a hollow beneath a shelf of sandstone that would hide their fire from the rider on the ridge, except there could be no fire, because a fire was a signature written in light for anyone watching. So they sat in the dark instead, four of them now, wrapped in every blanket they owned, eating cold beans out of tins while the temperature fell through the floor of the world.

Wren Halloway had gone to sleep mid-sentence, her head tipped against the pack she used for a pillow, one hand still curled around her pencil as if even in dreams she might be called upon to fix an elevation. The two porters, Tomas and the boy Rill, had burrowed into a shared bedroll near the wall, breathing in the slow unison of the exhausted.

Which left the watch. And left Sable Corren sitting three feet from him in the black, close enough that he could hear her breathe.

"First watch is mine," he said.

"It's mine." Her voice came low and even out of the darkness. "You slept on the boat. I didn't."

"I slept four hours on the boat, and you slept through none of it either, so don't pretend—"

"You counted my breathing too?" A pause, and something in it that wasn't quite a laugh. "That's a strange thing to admit to a thief."

He didn't answer. The wind moved through the hollow above them, sifting sand against stone with a sound like slow applause. Somewhere far off, a coyote said one thing to another coyote and got no reply.

"You should sleep," she said again, softer. "You're no good to anyone frozen and stupid."

"And you are?"

"Frozen, yes." He heard cloth shift; she was drawing her knees up, wrapping her arms around them. "Stupid, never."

He almost smiled. It startled him, the almost of it — how easily the shape of a smile still fit his face after all these years of not using it. He settled back against the rock and pulled his coat tighter and told himself the warmth gathering in his chest was only the shelter of the hollow doing its work.

Above them, the stars came out all at once, the way they did in high desert country — not gradually but like a lamp turned up, the whole sky suddenly crowded and burning. The Milky Way laid itself across the canyon gap like a river of its own, and Ivo thought, not for the first time, that the sky was laughing at him: everywhere water, and none of it where anyone could reach it.

"Sable," he said, before he could stop himself.

A long silence. Then: "You say my name like it costs you money."

* * *

She watched him in the starlight — or rather she watched the shape of him, the set of his shoulders, the way he sat like a man braced against a blow that had already landed years ago. She had been studying Ivo Eastwick for eleven days now, since the side canyon, since the moment he'd stepped out from behind the boulder with his rifle half-raised and his eyes full of something worse than anger. She had catalogued him the way she catalogued everything: water sources, escape routes, weaknesses. And what she had found, to her considerable irritation, was that his weakness was the same as hers.

Fathers.

"My father used to say the desert doesn't forgive," she said, when the silence had stretched long enough to hurt. "He was wrong. The desert forgets. That's crueler. You can spend your whole life waiting for forgiveness from a place that never wrote your name down in the first place."

Ivo turned his head. In the starlight she could see the line of his jaw tighten.

"He talked like that? Your father?"

"All the time. Drove my mother mad. He'd sit on the terrace edge after a storm and watch the flash flood come down the wash and say things like *the river remembers for us*." She heard her own voice go strange and stopped. "You knew him. Didn't you. That day at the source country's edge. You were there."

The silence that followed had weight, texture, temperature. She felt it settle over both of them like snowfall.

"I don't want to talk about that," Ivo said.

"No," she agreed. "But we're going to anyway, because it's freezing, because nobody can sleep, and because if I have to sit here one more night pretending this is about a warrant, I'm going to scream loud enough to bring the rider down off that ridge myself."

Another silence. Longer. When he finally spoke, his voice had dropped into something raw and careful, a man walking on ice he didn't trust.

"What do you want me to say?"

"The truth. Whatever version of it you've been carrying. Because here's mine, Eastwick, free of charge: I have spent nine years believing my father died a hero, and then I read those survey documents, and I found the incident report, and it says a trespasser drowned interfering with lawful operations, and it lists no name, and it files him under *losses, incidental*. Nine years of heroism, filed under incidental." Her breath came out white and hung between them. "So whatever you saw that day — however bad it is, however much it makes me hate you — it's better than incidental. Say it."

He was quiet for so long she thought he wouldn't. The stars wheeled. Rill murmured in his sleep and rolled against the wall.

Then Ivo Eastwick began to talk.

* * *

He told it plainly, because there was no other way to tell it and no strength left to dress it up.

He was twelve. His uncle had taken him upriver on a freight run, hauling pipe and pump parts for a company whose name Ivo hadn't understood then and had spent twenty years not trying to understand. They'd camped below the last permitted bend, and in the morning his uncle had drunk himself stupid and Ivo had wandered, the way twelve-year-olds wander, up a side drainage he had no business in, following the sound of water that shouldn't have been there.

He'd found men working in the dry wash above the springs' outflow. Not many — five, six. Diverting it. Building a low weir of stone and canvas to turn the young river aside into a channel that led nowhere he could see, bleeding the source before it ever became a river. And a man had come up the wash behind them — a lean man with grey in his black hair, moving fast and furious, shouting about water rights and children downstream and words Ivo hadn't known yet like *felony* and *witness*. The man had started pulling the weir apart with his bare hands.

And one of the crew had shoved him. Or hit him. Ivo had never been sure, even in memory, even a thousand times through. The man had gone down among the stones of the weir, and the diverted water had come back all at once when the canvas tore, and the wash had taken him the way flash floods take everything, instantly and without malice.

The crew had scattered. One of them had seen Ivo — a big man with a red scarf, who had looked at him for a long, terrible moment and then simply pointed, as if marking him, filing him under losses, incidental.

Ivo had run. Twelve years old, and he had run all the way down to the boat, and he had never told his uncle, and he had never told anyone, and the man in the wash had been Aldous Corren, and somewhere in the weeks that followed a paper had arrived at the ranger station bearing Ivo's name — a debt, owed to the family of the deceased, to be discharged by service, by silence, by obedience. He had signed it because he was twelve and grieving and guilty, and because the ranger who brought it told him signing was how a boy made a terrible thing mean something.

"That's the truth," he finished. His voice sounded scraped. "I ran. I let them file him. I've been paying a debt to a dead man for twenty years and I never once asked who drew it up, because asking would have meant looking at it, and looking at it—" He stopped. Started again. "Your father died fighting them. I want you to know that. Whatever else is true, that's true."

Sable said nothing for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice was thick in a way he'd never heard from her.

"The red scarf. Big man. Was there a scar through his eyebrow?"

"Yes."

"Then he's the foreman. The one whose name is on half these documents. Harl Vanty." A breath that shook slightly on the way out. "We're walking toward him, Ivo. We've been walking toward him the whole time."

* * *

They sat with that. The cold deepened. Ivo found that his hands had gone numb inside his gloves and that he didn't care, because some part of him had come loose and was floating, twenty years of ballast cut away all at once.

"I signed papers I never read," he said at last. "Twenty years. I tracked men across salt flats for debts I never questioned, because questioning felt like running again."

"And now?"

"Now I don't know what I am without it. That's the honest answer. The debt was rotten, probably forged, exactly like you said — but it was also the only structure I had. Guilt makes a fine load-bearing wall until you notice it's guilt."

Sable shifted beside him, and when she spoke her voice had changed — quieter, more careful, the voice she might have used on a spooked animal.

"There's a difference," she said. "Between guilt and responsibility. My father taught me that too, though he'd have hated hearing it quoted back. Guilt is about the past. Responsibility is about what you do next. You were a child, Ivo. You ran because children run. But you're here now. You walked upriver past every line you were supposed to respect, carrying a woman who hates you and a mapmaker who trusts you, because someone had to. That's not guilt. That's the other thing."

He looked at her. In the starlight her face was pale and sharp and open in a way that undid him more thoroughly than any accusation ever had.

"Why are you being kind to me?" he asked. "I'm the reason your father's death got filed away. I'm the witness who never testified."

"You're the witness who just did." She hugged her knees tighter. "Nine years late. But the river's patient. It's the one thing my father and I agreed on."

Something passed between them then that neither of them named. It wasn't forgiveness — it was too early, too unfinished, too tangled in warrants and stolen documents and a watcher on the rim. But it was adjacent to forgiveness the way a seep is adjacent to a spring: the same water, arriving slowly, through different stone.

Ivo became aware, with a clarity that frightened him, that he had stopped thinking of her as the thief. Somewhere in the last hundred miles the categories had failed. She was Sable. She was the daughter of the man in the wash. She was the person keeping watch beside him in the cold, and when the cold pressed hardest, some animal part of him leaned a degree closer to her warmth, and she did not move away, and that small fact seemed larger than the whole canyon country around them.

"Sleep," she said finally. "I'll take first watch. All of it. You look like a man who needs to dream about something better than a wash."

"There isn't anything better," he said, and lay back, and was asleep within minutes — and dreamed, for the first time in twenty years, not of drowning water but of standing beside it, dry, in daylight, next to someone whose name he knew.

* * *

Sable kept the watch alone and let him sleep.

She sat with her father's compass open in her palm, the needle steady on north, and thought about debts. Her mother had kept a ledger once — household accounts, egg money, feed — and in the back pages, in her father's hand, a single entry Sable had found as a girl: *Owed to the river: everything. Repaid: nothing.* She had thought it poetry then. Sitting in the freezing dark beside a sleeping man who had carried her father's death like a stone in his chest for twenty years, she understood it was arithmetic.

Vanty had forged the debt. She was nearly certain now — the dates were wrong, the signatures inconsistent, and the financing trail led straight back to the Meridian Survey's patron. Which meant Ivo Eastwick had been bought as a child and aimed like a tool, and had only just learned it, and was sitting with that knowledge the way a man sits with a wound he hasn't yet looked at.

And which meant, she admitted to the compass needle, that she had lied to herself too. She had told herself she stole the documents for justice. Partly true. But she had also stolen them because her father's ledger had one entry she could never balance, and she had wanted, desperately, to hand the debt to someone else — to the survey, to Vanty, to the world. Instead she had handed it to the one man who'd been carrying a piece of it all along.

Against her will, entirely against her will, she found she trusted him. Found she had trusted him since the ford, maybe since the side canyon, maybe — she examined this thought the way Wren examined a suspect contour line — since the locket. Since the moment he'd held her father's name in his hand and looked like a man who'd been struck.

The eastern sky had not yet begun to change when she heard it: a sound from the rim, faint and wrong. Not the coyote. Not the wind. A metallic click, deliberate, like a boot heel on stone — or like the action of a rifle being checked.

She rose without noise and put her hand on Ivo's shoulder.

His eyes opened instantly, fully awake, and she understood that he had heard it too.

"Rider," she breathed.

"No," Ivo whispered, staring up at the black line of the rim, where a shape had detached itself from the rocks and stood motionless against the stars. "Riders don't stand."
