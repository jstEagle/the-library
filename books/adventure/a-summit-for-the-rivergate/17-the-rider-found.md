## 17. The Rider Found

The rider appeared at dawn on the fourth day after the garbled radio call, and this time Ivo was ready for him.

He had stopped sleeping properly since the Narrows. Some animal part of his brain had taken over the watch, waking him at odd hours to check the rim, and it was that animal part — not the trained ranger, not the man with the compass and the debt — that caught the flicker of movement against the paling sky. A shape on the eastern rim, horse and rider fused into one dark silhouette, motionless as a hoodoo.

Then gone.

"Rim's clean," Pell said when Ivo woke him, rubbing his eyes. The porter had come back to the party after the flood with the hollow look of a man who had already died once and found it boring. "You keep seeing things, Eastwick."

"I keep seeing one thing. Same place. Same hour." Ivo crouched and studied the ground where their camp had been. "He's not following us. He's pacing us. There's a difference."

Wren Halloway looked up from her maps, her face gray with two days of calculation. "A difference that matters?"

"Followers want to catch you. Pacers want to know where you're going." Ivo straightened. "He's not reporting our position. He's confirming our heading."

Sable Corren stood at the edge of camp with her arms crossed, looking at the eastern rim the way she looked at most things — as if she were reading a letter someone else had written about her family. "There's a stock trail up that side," she said. "My father used it to run salt. It cuts the switchbacks. If your rider knows this country, that's how he stays ahead of us without breaking a sweat."

"Then we stop letting him stay ahead," Ivo said.

* * *

The plan was Sable's, though she delivered it like a woman handing over something she'd rather have kept.

The trail they followed bent north through a shallow valley called the Loom — a mile of braided sandbars and tamarisk where the river spread wide and slow and the canyon walls stepped back to admit the sky. Anyone pacing them from the rim would have to descend eventually, because the rim country ahead broke into a maze of box canyons that even a native would not cross at night. The only practical route down was a scree chute two miles ahead, where an ancient rockslide had built a ramp of shattered stone from the rim almost to the water.

"So we don't go two miles ahead," Sable said, drawing in the sand with a stick. "We camp in the open, big and visible, like fools. Then after dark, three of us go back up the way we came and sit on the chute. If he comes down tonight, he comes down to us."

"And if he doesn't come down?" Pell asked.

"Then he's smarter than we are, and we've learned that too."

Ivo chose himself and Sable and Wren for the ambush. Wren because her eyes were the best in the party and she had stopped pretending to be a civilian; the flood and the radio silence had done something to her, burned off the surveyor's detachment and left a harder woman underneath. Pell and the two remaining porters would keep the fire burning and make noise enough for eight men.

They climbed back through the dark, Sable leading without a lantern, her feet finding the trail by some sense older than sight. They reached the base of the scree chute an hour before moonrise and settled into the shadow of a fallen slab, and there they waited.

The desert at night is not quiet. It ticks and settles. Wind moves sand grain by grain across the flats with a sound like paper being turned. Ivo lay with his cheek against cold stone and listened to the canyon breathe, and beside him Sable was so still she might have been part of the rock, and above them the chute rose pale and steep as a stairway to nowhere.

The moon came up a little after midnight and turned the scree to silver.

It was Wren who heard it first — not a sound but a wrongness in the sound, a rhythm missing from the tick and settle. Then, faintly, the scrape of a boot on stone.

The rider came down the chute on foot, leading his horse, and he was not careful about it. That was the first thing Ivo noticed: the man descended like someone who had forgotten how to be careful, or no longer saw the point. He stumbled twice. The horse's shoes clicked on stone. He was talking — Ivo could hear the murmur of it, low and constant, the way a man talks when there is no one left to listen.

Sable moved. It was not a dramatic movement; she simply rose out of the shadow of the slab and stood in the moonlight at the base of the chute with a lantern in her hand, and struck the match, and lit it.

The light hit the rider full in the face.

He was younger than Ivo expected, and older. That was the only way to put it. Thirty perhaps, with hair gone white at the temples and eyes that flinched from the lantern like a man stepping out of a cellar into noon. He wore the remains of a Meridian Survey uniform, sun-bleached to the color of bone, and he was thin in the way that men get thin when they have been eating things they don't want to think about. The horse stood behind him with its head down, as tired of him, Ivo thought, as anything could be.

The man looked at the lantern. He looked at Sable. He looked at Ivo and Wren rising from the dark on either side.

"Finally," he said, and his voice cracked on the word. "Finally, finally. I've been walking beside you for nine days and nobody would look up."

* * *

His name was Teodor Brask, and he had been the Meridian Survey's instrument man — the one who carried the levels and the theodolite and checked the readings twice, because that was the kind of man he was, he said, twice, three times, always twice. He had been at the middle camp when the survey broke apart. He had walked out alone, and then he had not walked out at all; he had turned around, because there was nowhere to walk to, and for weeks he had moved through the canyon country like a ghost haunting its own funeral.

They got a fire going in the lee of the fallen slab, out of the wind, and gave him salt pork and hard bread and water, and he ate the way starving men eat, mechanically, without pleasure, watching the fire as if it might tell him something.

Ivo let him eat. He had learned that much from interrogation training and more from years of reading people on the trail: a starving man's story is worth nothing until his stomach is quiet.

Sable sat across the fire and watched Brask with an expression Ivo was beginning to know well — the look of a woman taking inventory. Wren sat beside him with her notebook open and did not pretend otherwise.

"Nine days," Ivo said at last. "You've been pacing us for nine days. Why not come down sooner?"

Brask's hands stopped. "Because I didn't know what you were." He looked up. His eyes were terrible up close — bright and blown, the eyes of a man who had seen the thing he was built to see and could not unsee it. "Rangers, I thought. Or the other kind. The ones in the gray coats. I couldn't tell anymore. I followed you because you were going the right way and I needed to know if anyone was going the right way, and I couldn't—" He stopped. He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I haven't talked to anyone in a long time."

"The gray coats," Ivo said. "Tell me about them."

Brask lowered his hands. And the story came out of him the way water comes out of a cracked cistern — at first a trickle, then all at once, everything, in no order at all.

* * *

The Meridian Survey had never been about mapping. That was the first thing. Brask had known it in his bones for months before he knew it in his head. The financing was wrong — too much money, too few questions, and a standing order from the expedition's patron that the upper gorge be surveyed for "structural suitability." Brask had asked, once, in a letter, what structure. The reply had come not from the patron but from a man Brask had never met, a lawyer's letter with a lawyer's careful language, and after that his letters home were read before they were sent. He knew because the seals were wrong. He was an instrument man. He checked things twice.

"They found it," Brask said. "The source. Or close enough. Chief Aldous kept the final approach off the official log — wrote it in his private book — but the preliminary soundings came back and we all knew. A cavern. A single cavern, the whole river out of one throat of rock. Do you understand what that means?" He looked around the fire, and none of them answered, and he nodded as if they had. "It means the river can be owned. One canyon mouth. One wall. You dam it, you hold every drop between here and the delta, and every farm, every town, every rail line that drinks from the Rivergate holds its life in your hand. The patron's engineers ran the numbers before we ever found it. I saw the numbers. They'd been waiting for the numbers."

"Who is the patron?" Wren asked, pen moving.

Brask laughed — a short, awful sound with nothing in it. "Corren Meridian Holdings. You've heard the name. Everyone's heard the name. Old man Corren built the shipping lines on the lower river, and his money is in everything now — rails, banks, land offices. The survey is his. The expedition is his. We were never government. The commission papers were real enough to fool the porters, but the seal at the bottom was bought, not granted." He was talking faster now, the words tumbling. "And then the survey confirmed it, and the orders changed."

Ivo felt the cold start in his chest. "What orders?"

"Voss." Brask said the name like spitting. "Captain Anselm Voss, who loves his maps more than his men — he got new instructions at the middle camp. Private instructions. I wasn't meant to see them but I carried the mail pouch and the seal was broken and I am a man who checks things twice." His hands were shaking now. "The instructions said the survey was concluded. That the findings would be transmitted by Voss alone. That the remaining personnel were to be — " he swallowed — "reduced."

"Reduced," Wren repeated flatly.

"That was the word. Not killed. Reduced. As if we were a line item." Brask stared into the fire. "The gray coats came up the river three days later. Six men, professional, well mounted, well armed. Not bandits — bandits don't carry survey-grade field glasses and burn their own camp latrines. They came to tidy up. Chief Aldous found out — I don't know how, maybe Voss told him, maybe Aldous read the same mail I did — and Aldous did the only thing left. He took the half of the expedition that would follow him and he ran upstream, toward the source, to get there first. To be inside it when they came. Because you can't reduce a man who's standing in the thing they want to own. You have to come and do it in front of witnesses. The river itself as witness."

"And the others?" Sable asked. Her voice was very quiet. "The ones who wouldn't follow him?"

Brask didn't answer for a long time. The fire cracked. Somewhere down the valley, the river moved over its bars with a sound like slow applause.

"Three of them tried to come downriver," he said finally. "Past the gray coats' picket at the ford. I heard the shots from the rim. I didn't go down." His voice broke and he let it. "I didn't go down, and I've been walking beside you for nine days because I thought maybe you were them, coming back, and if I could just — if someone would just look up—"

Ivo looked at Sable. She had gone very still, and her face in the firelight had the terrible composure of a woman holding something shut that wanted very badly to open. Her father had gone upstream too, years ago. Her father had died at the edge of the source country, and the story Ivo had been carrying in his pocket for twenty years — the story of a thief, a debt, a drowning — had never once included gray coats or bought seals or men whose job was to reduce other men.

"Voss," Ivo said. "The Voss who led our rescue party. The Voss who split off downriver with three men and a radio that's been dead for four days."

"He was never running a rescue," Brask said. "He was running a sweep. You were the mop behind the broom, ranger. They needed someone who knew the upper canyons to find where Aldous had gone, and they needed him to be clean — a real ranger, no connection to the patron. So they pulled you out of the Rivergate station with a story about missing surveyors." He looked at Ivo with those blown bright eyes, and something in them sharpened, focused, almost sane. "You didn't know. I watched you for nine days. You didn't know. That's the worst part. They used you clean."

* * *

They talked until the fire burned to coals. Wren filled six pages of her notebook. Brask gave them the gray coats' picket location — the ford below the Loom, the one their own route crossed in two days — and their number, six minus one, because a week ago one of them had gone into the river at the Narrows and not come out, and Brask had watched that too, from the rim, and done nothing, and that nothing was the stone his whole madness had been built on.

"They're waiting for you at the ford," he said. "It's the last easy crossing. After that the canyon walls close and there's no road for horses and they'd have to come after you on foot. They won't do that. They'll hold the ford and let you come to them, because everyone comes to the ford. Water runs downhill and men run to water." He hugged his knees, suddenly small. "You could turn back. You could still turn back."

Ivo looked down the valley, toward the dark line where the river and the night became the same thing. Twenty days of dry season, Wren had calculated. Every mile upstream cost time they could not buy back. And somewhere above, past the ford, past the flooded gorge, past the terraces where Sable had grown up, a gaunt chief and his faithful half of a murdered survey were walking toward the source with the truth in a private book, and six men in gray coats were sitting at the crossing with rifles, waiting to close the door behind them all.

"We're not turning back," Ivo said.

Brask nodded, unsurprised, as if the answer had been written into him long ago. "No," he said. "I didn't think so. That's why I finally came down."

Sable stood. She had been silent through all of it, and now she spoke, and her voice was steady in a way that made Ivo's skin prickle.

"The ford," she said. "Describe it. Every rock, every channel, the picket positions if you saw them." She looked at Ivo across the coals, and the firelight caught the locket at her throat — the one he had carried out of the salt flats, the one with her father's name inside it. "Because my father crossed that ford a hundred times, and he never once crossed it the way the map says."

Ivo looked at the dark downstream, where six armed men sat between them and the source, and understood that whatever came at the ford, it would not be a crossing. It would be a fight — and for the first time since the Rivergate station, he knew exactly whose side he was on.
