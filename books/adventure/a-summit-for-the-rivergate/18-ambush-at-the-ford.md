## 18. Ambush at the Ford

Wren Halloway had drawn a thousand crossings in her life, and she had never once believed she would die at one.

She crouched behind a fallen slab of sandstone forty yards from the water, the survey's last radio cradled against her chest like an infant, and did what she always did when fear came: she measured. The ford ran shallow and braided over three channels, split by two gravel bars furred with tamarisk. The near bar was maybe sixty paces long. The far bank rose in a low bluff, broken by a game trail — Sable's father's trail, the one that didn't appear on any map Wren had ever signed her name to. Above it, on the rimrock, the morning sun had not yet reached, and that shadow was where the pickets would be. She had counted muzzle-flash glints there twice since dawn. Two men. Possibly three.

Behind her, the party waited in the wash: Ivo with his rifle across his knees, Sable with her father's compass open in her palm as if it might tell her something the sky could not, and Tam and Odele, the two porters who had stayed when Voss took his three downriver — porters who now looked at the ford the way men look at weather they cannot outrun.

"Radio check," Ivo murmured.

Wren keyed the set. Static, thin and hissing, like wind through a crack in a door. She tried the downriver frequency anyway, the one Voss's party should have been holding. Nothing. Not even the garble from two nights ago. Just the long flat tone of a channel no one was listening to.

"That's it, then," she said, and heard how steady her voice came out, and hated how much practice that steadiness had taken.

Ivo looked back at her. In the gray light his face was all planes and patience, the face of a man who had spent twenty years being paid to notice things. "Say it plain."

"The set's dying. The battery's down to a whisper, and the antenna cracked in the climb yesterday. Even if I held it together, we've got maybe one transmission left in it. Maybe." She turned the radio over in her hands. It weighed nothing and everything. "And nobody's answering."

"So we're alone," Tam said. He said it without drama, the way you'd note that the river had dropped another inch overnight. Dry season arithmetic. Wren had taught them all to count that way.

"We were always alone," Sable said. "The radio just let us pretend."

* * *

They crossed at midmorning, but not at the ford.

That was Sable's doing. While the hired men watched the obvious crossing — and Wren knew they watched it, because twice she'd seen a glint of glass on the bluff, a lens catching sun, someone sweeping the water with field glasses the way she herself swept terrain — Sable led them half a mile upstream through a maze of fallen boulders to a place where the river spread wide and ankle-deep over a shelf of bedrock.

"My father called this the Table," she said. "Water runs so thin here you can read coins through it. The company maps show a canyon wall." She smiled without warmth. "Because I drew the company maps' corrections myself, and I never gave them this."

Wren felt that sentence go into her like a splinter. *Corrections.* She thought of all the blank spaces she had inked onto official sheets under orders, all the approaches she'd smoothed away, all the white silence she'd helped manufacture around the source country. Sable had fought the maps with omissions. Wren had built them. They had been fighting the same war from opposite trenches for years and neither of them had known.

They waded the Table in single file, packs held high, the cold pulling at their legs, and the river — the river that had shrunk all season until it whispered instead of roared — slid past their shins with a sound like paper turning. On the far side, Wren looked back once at the ford, at the empty gravel bars, at the bluff where the glint had been.

"They'll figure it out," she said. "When they see our tracks don't come out at the ford, they'll come upstream."

"Then we don't stop," Ivo said. "We make miles while they're reading sign."

But the river had other ideas about miles. Two hours on, the canyon pinched and the water dropped into a gorge they couldn't follow, forcing them up and over a shoulder of slickrock that cost them the whole afternoon. By the time they descended again to the river, the light was going copper and long, and Wren's legs were shaking, and the radio — she'd carried it herself rather than trust it to a pack strap — knocked against her hip with every step like a small dead heart.

It was Sable who found the second ford. Or rather, it was Sable who stopped walking, went very still, and said, "This is wrong."

"What?" Ivo's rifle came up.

"There's no birds." She turned a slow circle. "All afternoon there have been doves coming down to drink at dusk. Hundreds. You could set your watch by them." Her eyes went along the far bank, to a stand of cottonwoods whose shadows were already pooling into night. "Something is standing where the birds want to be."

Wren looked where Sable looked and saw only trees. But she had learned, these last weeks, to distrust her own trained eye in favor of Sable's inherited one, and inherited eyes had kept them alive more than once.

"Back up," Ivo breathed. "Slow. Into the rocks."

They had gotten perhaps thirty yards back toward the slickrock shoulder when the first shot came.

* * *

Later — much later, lying on her back with her shoulder on fire and the stars wheeling overhead — Wren would try to reconstruct those four minutes and find that memory had broken them into shards, the way glass breaks: all the pieces present, none of them in order.

A shot from the cottonwoods, cracking off stone somewhere above her head. Ivo shouting *down, down, down*, his voice stripped of everything calm. The crackle of boots in gravel — not two men, not three, six of them, maybe seven, spread along the far bank in a line that some professional hand had laid out, because the shots came in sequence, left to right, herding, not aiming to kill but to move them, push them like sheep toward —

Toward the ford.

Of course. Toward the ford. The one crossing the hired men actually controlled, where the rest of their crew waited on the near side, and where the ground funneled anyone driven from the rocks down onto open gravel with no cover at all. They weren't trying to kill them in the cottonwoods. They were trying to *deliver* them.

"They're driving us!" Wren screamed, and the mathematics of it hit her all at once, beautiful and terrible, the geometry of a trap drawn by someone who understood terrain the way she understood terrain. "Ivo — they want us at the ford — the whole line bends downstream—"

"I see it!"

He did something then that Wren would think about for years. Instead of running from the fire, he ran *through* it — straight downslope toward the river at the one point the shooters' line hadn't closed yet, firing as he went, not at the men in the trees but at the gravel bar itself, kicking up spouts of sand, and he was screaming for the others to follow the sound of his rifle. And they followed, because in that moment his certainty was the only fixed point in a world gone to noise.

Sable came behind him with Tam's arm over her shoulder. Then Odele. Then Wren, running with the radio hugged to her chest, her lungs full of knives—

The bullet took her high in the back of the shoulder, spinning her sideways, and the world became a smear of copper water and sky.

She didn't remember falling. She remembered the river's cold closing over her pack, remembered thinking with absurd professional clarity, *the set is not waterproof, the set has never been waterproof,* and then Ivo's hand fisting in her collar, hauling her up out of the shallows like she weighed nothing, dragging her behind a tumbled slab while gravel spat around them from returning shots.

"Talk to me," he said. "Wren. Talk."

"Left shoulder," she managed. "Through and through, I think. I can still—" She flexed her fingers. They worked. That was the important thing; hands were the whole of her trade. "I can still draw."

"Good. Keep that promise."

Then the shooting stopped, all at once, the way rain stops, and the silence that followed was worse than the noise.

From the far bank, a man's voice carried across the evening water, unhurried, almost courteous. A voice used to being obeyed in boardrooms if not canyons.

"You've got a wounded woman and no way back downriver that we don't control. Mr. Eastwick. We know who you are. Your employers are reasonable people. Walk away from the thief and the mapmaker, and you'll be paid, and forgiven, and home inside a month."

Wren, pressed against the stone with her blood soaking warm between her shoulder blades, heard Ivo's answer before he made it — heard it in the set of his shoulders, in the way he checked his rifle with slow, deliberate clicks.

"Tell your employers," Ivo called back, "they bought my silence once already. It didn't hold."

A pause. Then, almost sadly: "So be it."

Boots moved away through the cottonwoods. The birds, Wren noticed distantly, still hadn't come down to drink.

* * *

They climbed away from the river in the dark, because staying near water meant staying near the men who owned it, and Sable led them up a goat track she seemed to know by instinct into a fold of rock where a seep wet the stone and a single twisted juniper gave fuel for a fire the size of two cupped hands.

Tam cut Wren's shirt away from the wound by lamplight. The bullet had gone through the meat above her shoulder blade and out beneath her collarbone — ugly, bleeding freely, but missing everything that mattered, or so Sable judged after a long examination with fingers that did not shake. They washed it with boiled water, packed it with the last of the expedition's medical kit, bound it tight.

"You're lucky," Sable said.

"I'm a cartographer," Wren said through her teeth. "Luck is just terrain you haven't surveyed yet."

But when she took inventory by the tiny fire, the joke died in her throat. The ambush had cost them more than blood. In the scramble down to the river, Odele had dropped his pack — and Odele's pack had carried most of the food. What remained across five people: nine days of rations, if they ate thin. Half the rope. No spare ammunition beyond what hung on their belts. And the radio lay in Wren's lap, its casing starred open where a bullet fragment — or the river, or the fall, she'd never know which — had found it. She lifted the lid. Water had gotten inside. The boards within were dark and swollen.

She pressed the key anyway. Once. Twice.

Static. Then nothing. Not even static.

"No way back," Tam said quietly. He hadn't meant it about the radio, exactly. He'd meant it about all of it — the supplies, the season, the miles behind them crawling with hired guns, the miles ahead climbing toward a source that might already be wired to blow.

Wren looked at the ruined set in her lap and felt something strange loosen in her chest, some last thread of the woman she had been at the Rivergate station, the one who believed every problem yielded to measurement and every journey had a return leg plotted on the sheet. That woman had assumed the radio would always connect them to the world of warrants and reports and people who could be appealed to.

The radio was dead. The appeal was dead. Whatever happened in the high country now would happen to them, by them, and be witnessed by no one unless they themselves walked back out to tell it.

"No way back," she agreed. And then, because her hands needed work the way other people's hearts needed hope, she pulled her field book from its oilskin sleeve, opened it to a fresh page, and began to sketch the day's ground by firelight — the trap's geometry, the drive line, the ford — annotating it in her small precise hand: *They fight like engineers. Remember that. Engineers can be mapped.*

Ivo sat down beside her, close enough that she could feel the warmth off his shoulder.

"How bad?" he asked.

"I've been shot worse-adjacent before." She glanced at him. "You could have walked away just now. He offered."

"I know."

"He'll offer again. Men like that always do. And next time you might be colder, or hungrier, or standing over my body instead of beside it." She said it flatly, testing him, the way she tested a bridge before trusting it with weight.

Ivo looked into the fire for a long moment. "Twenty years ago somebody drew a line for me and told me which side of it to stand on, and I stood there because I never asked who held the pencil." He touched the edge of her field book, the sketch of the ambush taking shape under her pencil. "You're holding the pencil now. All of us are. That's the difference."

Above them, the stars burned hard and cold over country no honest map had ever recorded. Below, far off, a lantern moved along the far bank of the river — patient, methodical, following their tracks upstream the way water follows gravity.

Wren watched it for a moment, then bent back over her page.

Nine days of food, she wrote in the margin. Twenty days, maybe, before the shallows closed the river behind them. One working rifle among five. And somewhere ahead, a chief with demolition charges and a financier with more men than they had bullets.

She blew on the ink, closed the book, and allowed herself, just once, to press her good hand against the bandage over her heart.

*We go up,* she wrote, though she said nothing aloud. *There is no longer any direction but up.*
