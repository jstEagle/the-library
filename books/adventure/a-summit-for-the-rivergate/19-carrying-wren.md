## 19. Carrying Wren

The slot canyon took them in the gray hour before dawn, and Sable felt it close around them like a hand.

She had walked this stone since before she could name it. Her father had carried her through these narrows on his shoulders, pointing out the flood lines scored into the walls — dark bands of silt laid down year after year, a river's diary written in mud. *Read the walls,* he used to say. *The stone remembers everything. People are the only liars in a canyon.* She had not thought of that in years, and now she thought of it constantly, because Wren Halloway lay on a litter made of two tent poles and a folded survey tarp, her left leg splinted with a tent stake and strips of shirt, her fever climbing by degrees the way the canyon walls climbed toward their knife of sky.

"Talk to me," Wren said. Her voice had gone thin, papery, a sound like wind through a keyhole. "If I stop hearing voices, I'll start counting my pulse, and then I'll count it wrong, and then I'll think I'm dying, and then I'll be insufferable."

"You're insufferable already," Sable said, and adjusted her grip on the front poles. The litter swayed. Wren made a small sound and bit it off.

"Fair," Wren whispered.

Ahead, Ivo Eastwick moved through the slot like water finding its level — silent, economical, his eyes going up the walls every thirty seconds the way she'd taught him two days ago. He was faster at it now. He still didn't understand what he was looking at, not really, but he'd learned the rhythm of it: check the high ground, check the sun, read the flood debris wedged in the cracks overhead. Fresh cottonwood branches meant recent water. Old, gray, sun-bleached wood meant the flood was years past. Between the two lay everything a person needed to know about whether the canyon would let them live through the day.

"Seep at the next bend," Sable called forward. "Ten minutes. We drink there and nowhere else."

Ivo raised a hand without turning. Acknowledgment.

Behind her, the surveyor Pell grunted under the rear poles, and the boy Tam — not a boy really, twenty at most, one of the four who'd chosen to follow Ivo upriver when Voss took his three back down — shifted his pack and said nothing, which by now Sable understood to be his whole vocabulary. Four of them plus a wounded woman, hauling through stone so narrow that in places the litter had to be passed overhead, hand to hand, Wren clenched and silent and white as the rock above her.

Her father's routes. That was the thing Sable kept coming back to, the thing that sat in her chest like a coal that wouldn't cool. These slots, these hidden stairs, these water sources marked by nothing — cairns of three stones, a notch cut in a juniper root, a spiral scratched into varnish — they were his. He'd walked them for twenty years, guiding, mapping in his head, learning the country the way the surveyors never had, because they learned it to own it and he'd learned it to live in it. And now she was walking them with the daughter of the woman who'd erased them from the official maps, carrying a thief's stolen papers and a ranger's broken oath, and the country held them all without complaint, the way it had held her father's body for three days before they found him.

She didn't say any of that. She said: "Mind the third step. It's loose."

* * *

The seep was a thread of water no wider than a finger, weeping from a crack in the shade side of the canyon, pooling in a basin her father had deepened with a chisel when she was nine. She remembered the sound of the chisel. She remembered sitting on the warm rock above, eating dried apricots, watching him work and understanding, without words, that he was leaving something behind for whoever came next.

Ivo crouched at the basin and filled the skins without being asked. He'd learned that too — that water came first, talk later, always. He handed her a skin and their fingers touched and neither of them looked away fast enough, and then both of them did.

"Her fever," Ivo said quietly, nodding at Wren, who'd been propped against the wall with her eyes closed. "It's worse."

"It's infection, and infection needs cleaning, and cleaning needs time, and time is the one thing—" Sable stopped. Recalculated. "The dry season arithmetic. Wren's own numbers. We have maybe fifteen days now before the shallows strand us above the rapids. Less, if the heat holds."

"Then we don't stop."

"We stop when she needs us to stop." Sable heard her own voice go flat and hard, her father's voice, the one he used on men who wanted to push through a flood line. "She's the one who figured the river's fall. She's the one who knew the survey was lying before any of us did. You leave her in this canyon because she slowed us down, and you'll carry that out of here instead of her, and it'll weigh more."

Ivo looked at her for a long moment. The light in the slot was blue-gray and shadowless, and it made his face hard to read, but she was getting better at reading it anyway. There was something in it that wasn't the ranger. Something that had cracked open at the cold camp and hadn't sealed back over.

"All right," he said. "We stop when she needs it."

"And you carry the litter longer."

"And I carry the litter longer."

Pell, filling his own skin downstream, muttered something that might have been a prayer or a complaint. Tam drank in silence. Wren opened one eye.

"I can hear all of you," she said. "I'm not dead. I'm cartography."

* * *

They moved again through the middle of the day, the hottest hours, when the slot offered shade as a mercy. Sable led, and as she led she taught — not in lectures, because Ivo didn't learn that way, she'd figured that out, but in the language of the ground itself.

"See the polish on that wall, hip height? Flash flood. That's the high-water mark of the last big one. Everything below it got scoured." She touched the stone as she passed, an old habit, greeting the rock. "When the sky goes dark upstream and you hear a sound like a train, you don't run downcanyon. You climb. Any crack, any ledge, anything. Thirty seconds is enough if you start fast enough."

"And if there's nothing to climb?"

"Then you were dead before you heard it." She said it without cruelty. It was just true, and the canyon was a place where truth was the only currency that held its value.

Ivo absorbed it. She could see him absorbing it, filing it, the way he'd once filed the tracks of the thief he was hunting — except now the thief was walking beside him, and the tracks they were both reading were her father's, cut into stone twenty years deep, and the warrant had burned in a cook fire three nights ago and neither of them had mentioned it since.

At a fork where the slot split around a fin of stone, she stopped.

"Left goes down to the river. Two days, good water, but the walls are sheer and if it rains anywhere in the headwaters you're in a pipe with a flood coming. Right climbs. It's harder — there's a route, my father cut the first steps — but it comes out on the terraces, and from the terraces you can see weather coming from fifty miles." She looked at him. "Which do you take?"

Ivo studied the fork the way he studied everything now, with a patience he hadn't had on the salt flats. "Right," he said. "We're not trying to be fast. We're trying to arrive."

Something loosened in her chest. She turned away before he could see it.

The climb took the rest of the day. Her father's steps were still there — chisel cuts in the sandstone, a handhold worn smooth by decades of palms, hers and his and whoever had come before him. She went up them with Wren's litter passed overhead in pieces, the woman herself hauled up by sling on Tam's back, swearing in a small precise voice at every jolt, cataloging her own injuries aloud because that was how Wren handled fear: she measured it.

"Fracture, mid-shaft, tibia, closed. Fever, subjective, high. Morale—" a pause, as Ivo's hand found her wrist and steadied her over a bad step, "—morale unexpectedly adequate."

"High praise," Ivo said.

"Don't let it go to your head, ranger."

They came out on the terraces at sunset, and the whole country opened like a book.

* * *

Sable had meant to keep watch alone. That was her habit, her father's habit — one person awake, the fire cold, the stars doing the work of lamplight. But Ivo sat down across the dead ring from her anyway, and Tam and Pell were already asleep, and Wren was asleep too, truly asleep for the first time in two days, her fever broken at last by willow bark and cold cloths and the stubbornness of her own body, and so Sable let it happen. Two people awake. It wasn't the worst idea. The country had changed her mind about a lot of things.

Below them the terraces fell away in long pale steps toward the black line of the slot canyons. Above, the stars were thick and indifferent, and somewhere out there, in the direction of the source, the missing surveyors were walking toward whatever they intended to do, and behind them, far downriver, the financier's hired men were climbing, and none of it could be seen, and all of it was coming.

"Tell me about him," Ivo said. "Your father."

She almost didn't. The old reflex — that part of her life was hers, sealed, paid for in blood. But the fire ring was cold and the stars were out and this man had frozen halfway up a cliff face that morning and let her talk him through it hand over hand, which was a kind of honesty that deserved a little back.

"He guided," she said. "Before the surveys came through and made maps to sell, he knew this country the old way. Seeps. Routes. Where the water goes in a dry year." She turned a stone over in her fingers. "He hated the idea of the source being found. Not because he wanted it secret for himself. Because he knew what happens to a secret once someone figures out it's worth money."

"And what he was doing, the day he died."

"Stopping them." The words came easier than she expected. "Diverting the outflow. There was a crew — private, no permits, no marks on their gear. He went down into the channel to break their dam and the water took him. And a boy watched it happen and ran." She looked at Ivo. "I used to hate that boy. For years. The boy who saw and ran."

Ivo didn't flinch. That was new too; the man she'd met on the salt flats would have flinched, would have armored up, would have reached for the law like a shield. This one just sat with it.

"And now?"

"Now I think running was the only thing he could do. He was twelve. And I think—" she turned the stone over again, "—I think what matters isn't that he ran. It's that he came back. Twenty years later, with a warrant he didn't know was fake, chasing a woman he thought was a thief, all the way to the water." She set the stone down. "He came back. That's the part I'd have hated him for if he hadn't."

The silence after that was long and not uncomfortable. Somewhere downcanyon a coyote spoke once and thought better of it.

"Pell asked me today," Ivo said, "why we're still going. Voss turned back. The mission's dead. There's no pay in it, no jurisdiction, no orders. He wanted to know what we are now."

"And what did you tell him?"

"I told him I didn't know yet." A pause. "I think I know now. We're not a rescue party. We haven't been since the ford. We're—" he searched, and she let him search, because she wanted to hear what he'd find, "—we're a crew. We're going where we're going because it's right, and we're carrying each other because that's how you get there."

Sable looked at him across the cold fire ring, this man who had been an instrument for so long he'd mistaken the debt in his pocket for a spine, and she felt the fragile, frightening thing that had been building between them since the night of the falling star shift its weight again — not love yet, or not a word she was willing to use yet, but its foundation: trust, laid stone by stone, the way her father had laid his chisel cuts in the rock, one at a time, for whoever came next.

"Sleep," she said. "I'll take first watch."

"We'll take first watch," he said, and settled back against the stone, and she didn't argue.

* * *

They made eleven miles the next day, which was eleven miles more than Wren's leg had any right to permit, and twelve the day after, following the terraces north and west through country that grew stranger and more beautiful with every hour — wind-carved alcoves like cathedral naves, gardens of desert paintbrush blooming out of bare cracks, a natural bridge so wide that Pell walked under it twice just to feel it. Sable taught and Ivo learned and the learning changed him visibly, the way water changes stone: slowly, and then all at once. By the third day he was reading flood lines on his own, calling seeps before she pointed them, and once — she caught him doing it — he paused at a fork and touched the notch her father had cut in a juniper root, lightly, the way you'd touch a stranger's shoulder to pass in a narrow place. A greeting. She said nothing. She kept the saying-nothing like a coin in her pocket.

Wren, fever down to a warm flicker, insisted on working. She lay on the litter with a scrap of paper on her knee and a pencil stub, sketching the terraces from memory, correcting the official maps in the margins of her own mind. *The source approaches do not exist,* the old maps said. *Here they do,* her new notes said, in a hand that grew shakier and more certain at the same time. Sable watched her do it and understood that this was Wren's version of her father's chisel cuts — leaving something true behind for whoever came next, in a country that had been lied about on paper for a generation.

On the fourth day, at the top of a rise where the terraces gave onto a long view of the upper gorge, Tam stopped walking and pointed.

Smoke. Not their smoke. A thread of it, thin and gray, rising from a side canyon three miles ahead — from the direction of the last defensible pass, the one Sable had been quietly dreading for two days.

And below it, on the rim, catching the sun for half a heartbeat before it dropped out of sight: the glint of glass. A spyglass, or a rifle scope. Watching them the way they had been watched for a week.

Sable felt the fragile unity of the last four days tighten around her like a rope coming taut. She looked at Ivo, and Ivo looked at the smoke, and Wren, from the litter, said quietly, "That's not a camp. That's a picket. They're waiting for us."

"Then we don't give them what they're waiting for," Sable said, and turned her father's map in her head, and began, already, to plan.
