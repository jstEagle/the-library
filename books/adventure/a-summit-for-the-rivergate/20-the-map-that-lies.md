## 20. The Map That Lies

The fever came at dusk, the way storms came to the terraces — first a stillness, then a pressure, then the whole sky breaking.

Wren Halloway felt it arrive in her body before her mind agreed to host it. The wound in her side, a shallow gouge from the ambush at the ford, had seemed a small thing for two days. A scratch, she'd called it. A tax paid on the road. She had let Sable Corren bind it with boiled cloth and a paste of some bitter root, and she had kept walking, because walking was the only thing she knew how to do when the world went wrong.

Now the world had gone soft at the edges. The canyon walls breathed. The river, somewhere below them in its slot of stone, spoke in a language she almost understood, and the almost was the worst part.

"We camp here." Ivo Eastwick's voice, low and level, the voice of a man who had learned to make decisions the way other men made breakfast. "There's an overhang. Wind won't reach us."

She felt hands under her arms, and then the ground became a bedroll, and the sky became stone, and she was lying in the blue hour under a lip of rock with the fire — small, screened, the size of a cupped palm — throwing its miserly light across four faces.

Four faces. It had been more, once. It had been a survey party of eleven, and then a rescue party of seven, and then the ford had taken its toll, and now they were this: a ranger, a thief, a porter named Odell who had not quit when the other one did, Sable Corren, and herself, burning quietly from the inside.

"Water," she said.

Sable brought it. The thief — she supposed she should stop thinking of her that way, but habits of classification died hard in a cartographer, who lived and died by what things were called — Sable knelt and held the tin cup to her lips with a steadiness that Wren found vaguely insulting. As if fever were a river crossing and Sable had forded a hundred of them.

"You're hot as a stone at noon," Sable said.

"I'm a cartographer," Wren said. "We run warm. All that ink."

Nobody laughed. That was how she knew it was bad.

* * *

The night went long. Fevers do that; they take the hours and stretch them like taffy, like the longitudes of a map drawn by someone who has never seen the places he's distorting. She drifted. She surfaced. Each time she surfaced, someone was watching — Ivo on first watch, motionless as the rock behind him; Sable on second, sharpening nothing, just moving the whetstone along the knife for the sound of it; Odell snoring like a landslide in miniature.

And in the drifting, the thing she had been carrying rose in her like silt in a flooded river, stirred up, unavoidable, turning the water brown.

She had altered the maps.

There. It could be thought, at least, inside the privacy of her own cooking skull. She had altered the maps, and she had told no one, and she had watched Ivo Eastwick navigate by her work for three hundred miles of canyon country, and she had said nothing.

Not nothing, exactly. She had said the thing cartographers say: *the surveys in this reach are old. The source approaches were never well mapped.* True, in the way that a locked door is merely closed. She had let him believe the blank spaces on her charts were ignorance, when they were amputation. Deliberate. Ordered. Paid for.

"Drink," Sable said, out of the dark, and Wren realized she had been moaning. Or talking. She couldn't tell which.

* * *

Near morning the fever peaked, and with the peak came a clarity that felt like falling.

She was nineteen again, in the drafting room of the Meridian Survey's headquarters, a long room smelling of lamp oil and linseed, with forty drawers of copperplate and a window that looked out on nothing, because windows in that building had been bricked over during some earlier administration's paranoia. Her supervisor — a soft man named Grieves who wore his authority like an ill-fitting coat — stood over her table with a sheaf of her finished sheets and a red pencil.

"These approaches," Grieves said. "The upper canyons. The springs country."

"What about them?"

"They're to come out."

Wren had laughed. She remembered laughing — a short, incredulous sound, the laugh of a girl who had walked six hundred miles with a theodolite to survey exactly that country. "They're the whole point of the expedition. The source approaches are the—"

"The sheets are to come out, Halloway." He set the red pencil down with terrible gentleness. "The Survey's patron has views on who should know how to reach the water. You are nineteen years old, you are in debt for your instruments, and you have a talent I would hate to see wasted on a farm. Redraw the sheets. Leave the approaches blank. Note the country as unmapped, impracticable, waterless. No one will ever check. No one ever checks the blank places. That's why they're blank."

And she had done it. That was the thing, the thing the fever would not let her set down: she had done it. Not under threat of violence, not with a gun at her back. For her instruments and her position and a soft man's approval, she had taken the red pencil and erased the approaches to the source of the great river, and she had hatched the margins with the cartographer's lie — *unsurveyed* — and she had signed the sheets with her own proud name.

She had been telling herself for eleven years that it was a small thing. A blank space. What harm in a blank space? The world was full of them.

Then the survey had come upriver, her old employers, following her erased lines, hunting the water she had hidden, and men had died or vanished, and a thief had stolen the true documents, and a ranger with a forged debt had been dragged into the current of it all — and every mile of it, every mile, had been made possible by a nineteen-year-old girl with a red pencil.

* * *

She woke — or surfaced — to gray light and Ivo crouched at the fire, coaxing it up for breakfast. Her mouth tasted of copper and lies.

"Ivo."

He turned. Up close, in the flat morning light, his face had the quality of the country they were crossing: weathered, guarded, carved by things that had happened to it a long time ago.

"I need to tell you something," she said, "before the fever takes it back."

He came and knelt. Behind him, Sable paused in the work of breaking camp, and Odell went still with the load half-hoisted. Wren supposed she should have asked for privacy. She found she didn't want it. Confession, she was learning, was like surveying: the readings had to be taken in the open, with witnesses, or they didn't count.

"The maps," she said. Her voice came out scraped. "The ones you've been navigating by. The ones the whole expedition used. I drew them."

"I know you drew them. You're the cartographer."

"No." She pushed herself up on one elbow, and the canyon tilted politely and righted itself. "I mean I drew them *twice*. The first time, I drew them true. Every approach to the springs country. Every seep, every route, every canyon mouth that opens on the source. I surveyed them myself when I was nineteen, with the Meridian Survey, before your time, before any of this. And then my supervisor took the sheets out of my hands and told me the approaches were to be erased. On orders. The Survey's patron — the man who financed it, the man whose hired guns shot us at the ford — he didn't want the water findable. He wanted it *his*. Findable only by the people he paid."

Ivo said nothing. His stillness had a different texture now. She had spent weeks reading this man's silences, and this one was the silence of a man watching a door he'd suspected was there finally swing open.

"I redrew them," Wren said. "I left the approaches blank. I wrote *unsurveyed, impracticable, waterless* across country I had personally walked. I signed it. Eleven years ago." She heard her own breath go ragged. "You've been following my lie up this river, Ivo. Every camp, every crossing, every route I've given you — I gave you the version of this country that a rich man paid to have created. I'm not just wounded cargo you've been hauling. I'm the reason the thief had to steal the true maps in the first place. I'm the reason the survey could come up here and get lost in its own erasures. I'm the reason—"

"Stop." Ivo's voice was quiet. "Breathe."

"I will not stop." The fever had her now and she was glad of it; it burned away the caution she'd wrapped around this truth for a decade. "Do you know what a blank space does, Ivo? On a map? It doesn't say *nothing is here*. It says *nothing need be here*. It gives every government, every company, every man with money permission to treat the place as vacant. I erased the approaches and I told myself it was a small thing, a career, a girl doing what she was told — and the blank I made is the exact size of everything that's happened to us. The expedition. The guns. The ford. This hole in my side. I drew all of it. I drew it in red pencil when I was nineteen years old."

The fire cracked. Somewhere down in the slot of the canyon, the river ran on, indifferent, keeping its own counsel.

Sable had come closer without Wren noticing. She crouched at the edge of the overhang, arms on her knees, and her face — Wren had expected contempt, or the cold satisfaction of a grievance confirmed — her face held something worse. Recognition.

"You were nineteen," Sable said.

"Nineteen."

"They didn't put a gun to your head."

"No."

"They didn't have to." Sable looked out at the canyon light. "My father used to say the water never lies, but the maps do, because maps are drawn by people who've been paid to look away. I grew up hating the men who made those blank spaces. I never once thought about the hand that held the pencil." She turned back, and her eyes were dry and very clear. "I stole the survey documents to prove my father died protecting the water. You just told me the water was stolen on paper first, by a girl younger than I am now. So." A breath. "I don't get to hate you any more than I hate myself for not stopping any of it sooner."

"That's not—" Wren began.

"It is," Sable said. "It's exactly that."

* * *

Ivo had not moved through any of it. Now he stirred the fire, and when he spoke, it was in the low even voice he used on the trail, but she heard what was under it, the way you hear the deep current under a river's skin.

"Eleven years ago," he said. "I was eleven years old, eleven years ago."

Wren went still.

"I saw something at the edge of the springs country when I was a boy," Ivo said. He wasn't looking at her; he was looking into the fire as if it were a survey instrument. "I've told part of it. I haven't told all of it. I signed a debt to a dead man's family that I've carried for most of my life, and I've just spent this whole journey learning it was forged — bought silence, dressed up as obligation. And here's the thing I keep arriving at, Wren. The men who forged it, the men who paid you to hold the red pencil, the men who sent guns up this river — they're all the same animal. They don't do their own erasing. They find someone young, someone in debt, someone with a talent or a conscience or a family, and they hand them the pencil. Or the paper. Or the gun." He looked up. "You held a pencil. I signed a paper. Somebody else pulled a trigger at the ford. We've all been the instrument. The question the fever's asking you — the question it's been asking me since the Narrows — is whether we're going to be the instrument or the hand."

"The hand gets cut off," Odell said unexpectedly, from behind the load. "In my experience."

"Sometimes," Ivo said. "Sometimes it draws a better map."

Wren laughed — a real laugh, cracked and feverish and surprised out of her — and then the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing pulled at the wound, and the morning went white at the edges again. But through the white she heard Sable say, in her flat practical voice:

"She can't walk today. Maybe not tomorrow. The fever needs to break."

"And if it doesn't?" Odell asked.

"Then it doesn't," Sable said. "But it will. She's too stubborn to die with a confession half-finished. I've seen her kind before. My father was her kind."

* * *

The fever broke at sunset, the way it had come — all at once, the pressure lifting, the sky clearing. Wren woke drenched in sweat inside her bedroll, weak as wet paper, but *present*, horribly, completely present, in a body that ached and a canyon that was real and a truth that was now out in the air among four other people, cooling, taking its place in the world.

Sable brought her broth. Ivo sat down across the small fire, and in the light of it he laid out her charts — the ones that had survived the ford, water-stained, corner-burned, the working sheets she'd guarded like a heartbeat.

"I want you to do something," he said. "When you're strong enough to hold a pencil."

Wren looked at the maps. Eleven years of blank space looked back at her, all those hatched margins, all those careful lies in her own hand.

"You want me to redraw them," she said. "The approaches. From memory."

"From memory, from Sable's father's routes, from whatever we find. The true sheets. Every seep, every route, every canyon mouth." He said it the way he said everything, level and unhurried, but his eyes were not unhurried at all. "Not for the Survey. Not for the patron. Not for the record or the reward. For the blank places to stop being blank. That's the only repair I know of that's worth anything. You can't un-erase. But you can redraw."

Wren looked down at her hands. They were a surveyor's hands, ink-stained at the knuckles no matter how she scrubbed, steady enough, even now, even after fever, even after everything.

"I'll need paper," she said. "And I'll need to live long enough to finish."

"You'll live," Sable said. "I told you. Stubborn."

* * *

They broke camp at first light the next morning, and the canyon took them back.

But a mile on, the walls pinched, the river roared somewhere below out of sight, and the route they'd been following — Sable's father's route, the old family way — came up against a wall of stone where the gorge ahead had flooded, black water churning through a slot too sheer to climb and too deep to ford. Sable stood before it with her hands on her hips, and Wren, leaning on Odell's shoulder, watched her read the rock the way other people read print.

"There's a way up," Sable said at last. "My father cut it. A climbing route, up and over the gorge. He showed me the lower pitches when I was a girl." She paused, and Wren saw something cross her face that she had never seen there before — doubt. "I never went all the way. He never took me past the third pitch. He said the rest you learn once, or you don't."

Wren looked up. The wall went up and up, cracked and ribbed, disappearing into glare. Somewhere above it, she knew, lay the springs country — the country she had erased, the country she was now under contract, of a kind, to restore.

She felt for the stub of pencil in her breast pocket. It was still there. It had survived the ford, the fever, the confession.

"Then we learn it once," Wren said.
