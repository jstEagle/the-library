## 21. First Ascent

The gorge announced itself before they saw it — a low, grinding roar that rose through the stone under their boots like something breathing in its sleep.

Sable stopped at the lip of the terrace and held up a closed fist, and the whole line of them halted: Wren leaning on Ivo's shoulder with her bandaged leg held stiff, the porter Tam with the salvaged packs, and old Marette, who had not spoken a full sentence since the ambush at the ford but whose eyes missed nothing.

Below them, where the map promised a dry wash and an easy mile of riverbed walking, water stood forty feet deep and moving fast. The flash flood had come down from some storm they'd never seen, filling the gorge wall to wall, brown and muscular, carrying whole junipers on its back like kindling.

"There's no crossing that," Tam said flatly. "Not today. Not this season."

"We're not crossing it," Sable said. "We're going over it."

She turned and pointed up, to where the gorge's south wall sheared out of sight into haze. Somewhere near the top, invisible from here, a seam of lighter stone ran diagonally across the cliff face like a scar.

"My father cut that route," she said. "Before I was born. It was how he moved between the terraces when the river was high. He called it the Ladder."

Ivo looked at the wall for a long time. He had climbed things before — scree slopes, broken rimrock, the occasional chimney in the canyon country where a man could wedge himself and pray. But this was different. This was a face of stone that went up and up without apology, and somewhere above it the air would be thin and the wind would have opinions, and below it was forty feet of floodwater that would not give a body back.

"How long since anyone used it?" he asked.

"Twenty years." She said it evenly, but her jaw tightened. "Since he died."

* * *

They spent the afternoon at the base of the wall while Sable read the rock the way Ivo would have read tracks. She walked the first two hundred feet of the route three times, touching holds, testing edges, occasionally knocking loose a flake that dropped away into the flood and vanished without a sound. Once she stopped and pressed both palms flat against the stone and stood there so long that Ivo thought she might be praying. Maybe she was. He didn't ask.

"He drilled these," she said finally, coming back down. She showed him a row of small holes, hand-cut, precise, spaced along a diagonal crack. Iron pins had once sat in them; a few remained, rusted to orange stubs. "He carried a star drill and a hammer up here every summer for six years. My mother said he loved the wall more than he loved us." A ghost of a smile. "She didn't mean it as a complaint."

"And you've done this climb?"

"I was nine." She met his eyes. "He led me up it on a rope he'd braided himself. I cried the whole way. He told me fear was just the body paying attention." She looked back up at the face. "I've been paying attention ever since."

The plan, such as it was, took shape around the one thing they had left: rope. Two lengths of it, sixty feet each, plus the shorter coil from the wrecked supply cache. Not enough to fix lines the whole way. Enough to make the worst sections survivable, if the worst sections were survivable at all.

Wren insisted on going up. They argued with her until the light went gold and then gave up arguing, because there was nowhere to leave her and no one to leave her with, and because — though none of them said it aloud — the alternative to climbing was turning back, and turning back meant the financier's men, who were somewhere behind them and closing.

So: Wren on Ivo's back in a improvised sling of pack straps, her good arm locked around his neck, her instruments case strapped to his chest like armor. Tam and Marette with the packs. Sable leading, always leading, finding holds twenty years after her father's hands had made them.

They started at dawn.

* * *

The first third was almost kind. A ramp of tilted slabs, then a ledge wide enough to walk, then a series of steps Sable's father had cut into a corner where the rock folded inward like a cupped hand. Ivo climbed with Wren's weight shifting against his spine, her breath quick in his ear, and found that the work of it — the arithmetic of foot and hand and balance — pushed everything else out of his head. There was no debt on this wall. No warrant. No dead men downstream or living ones behind. Just the next hold, and the next.

Then the ledge ended.

Beyond it the route turned into what Sable had called the Traverse: a diagonal seam no wider than a boot sole, running forty feet across a face that leaned out over the void. Below, the flood churned. The drop was not fatal in itself, she'd explained — a fall would land you on a lower shelf, probably. Probably. Unless you bounced outward, in which case the river would take you, and the river kept what it took.

Sable went first, fixing a rope through the old pins where any remained. Tam followed, white-faced, moving like a man crossing ice. Marette surprised everyone by flowing across the seam like water finding its level, muttering something in her hill dialect that might have been a prayer or an insult.

Then it was Ivo's turn, with Wren on his back.

He got one hand into the seam. Then the other. He stepped out onto the ledge's broken edge and felt the world tilt — not the rock, the rock was solid, it was everything else that tilted, the sky leaning, the river rising up toward him like a mouth.

"Wren," he heard himself say, very calmly, "I can't."

"You can." Her voice came steady against his ear. "But not yet. Breathe first."

"I can't—"

"Breathe. In for four. I'll count."

He breathed. The river roared below. His hands were welded to the rock and shaking, and the shaking traveled down through his arms into his shoulders and he thought, absurdly, of the salt flats, of all those years of walking alone across empty ground where the only thing required of him was endurance, and how he had mistaken that loneliness for strength. Endurance was easy. Endurance was just refusing to stop. This was different. This was asking his body to trust a seam of stone thinner than his own resolve.

"Ivo." Sable's voice, from above and behind — she'd come back out onto the traverse, anchored at the far pin, her body wedged against the face. "Look at me. Don't look down. Look at me."

He turned his head, careful, careful, and found her eyes eight feet away, dark and level.

"My father froze right there," she said. "First time he free-soloed this stretch. Halfway across, with nothing under him but air, and he told me later he forgot how to move. Just stood there with his fingers bleeding, certain he was going to die." Her voice stayed even, but something moved underneath it. "You know what he did?"

"What."

"He talked to the wall. Out loud. Like it was a person. Said, *you don't get to decide, I do.*" A half-smile. "Crazy old man. But he crossed. And then he drilled those pins so nobody else would ever have to cross it alone."

"I'm not alone," Ivo said, and heard the truth of it leave his own mouth like something set down.

"No," Sable said. "You're not. Now — your left hand. There's a pinch hold at the edge of that flake, about six inches up. Feel for it. Don't look for it. Feel."

He felt. Found it. The flake was sound, warm from the sun.

"Good. Now your right foot — there's a pocket below the seam, knee height. Trust it."

He trusted it. The pocket held.

Hand over hand, word by word, she walked him across the wall. *Left hand, edge. Right foot, pocket. Shift your weight before you move, not after.* Wren's weight on his back became not a burden but a ballast, something to lean into, and slowly — hold by hold, breath by breath — the shaking drained out of his arms and the traverse became what it actually was: a sequence of problems, each one solvable, each one already solved by a dead man who had wanted strangers to be able to follow him home.

Halfway across, his boot scraped and a pebble the size of a fist broke loose and dropped. They all watched it fall — a full second, two — before it hit the lower shelf and cracked apart.

Nobody spoke. Then Marette, from the far anchor, said something dry in her hill tongue, and Tam laughed, a single startled bark, and the spell of the drop broke.

"See?" Sable said softly. "The wall doesn't get to decide."

* * *

The last pitch was the cruelest — a chimney above the traverse where the rock pinched together and then opened again into a slot you had to bridge with your back and knees, pushing upward on friction and faith. Sable went up it like smoke. Tam needed coaching at every move. Marette went up talking to herself the whole way, and whatever she said, the rock seemed to listen.

Wren last, on Ivo's back again, and by now something had changed in him. He still feared the wall — his palms were slick, his forearms burning — but the fear had become a companion rather than a master, something riding alongside instead of driving. He bridged the chimney thinking of Sable's voice. *Shift your weight before you move.* Thinking of her father, whoever he had been — a man who drilled holes in cliffs for people he'd never meet, who taught his daughter that fear was attention, who died — Ivo still didn't know exactly how, only that it had something to do with water and with men like the ones chasing them now.

He came over the top into sudden open light and lay on the broad summit slab, gasping, Wren sliding off his back beside him, and above them nothing but sky, and below them the whole gorge laid out like a wound in the earth, the flood running silver-brown in the late sun.

Sable crouched next to him. She didn't say *well done* or anything of the sort. She just put her hand briefly on the back of his neck, the way you'd steady a horse, or a friend, and left it there for three seconds longer than necessary.

"Your father," Ivo said, when he could talk. "On the traverse. Did that really happen? Him freezing?"

"Every word." She looked out at the river. "He told me the story the night before he died. He said he told it so I'd know that brave people aren't people without fear. They're just people who let someone talk them across." She paused. "I never understood what he meant until today."

* * *

They camped that night on the summit shelf, the first ground in days that felt safe — a natural fortress of stone with one approach, which Tam watched. They dared a small fire, screened by rocks, and ate the last of the decent food: beans, a strip of dried goat, the dregs of the tea. Wren, propped against her pack with her leg elevated, unrolled a scrap of oilcloth and began, by firelight, to sketch the route they'd climbed — not the official survey style, all grids and elevations, but something looser, annotated in her small fierce handwriting. *Ladder route, S. Corren's father, circa 20 yrs past. Sound rock. Pins rusted — replace.*

"You're mapping it," Ivo said. "Even now."

"Especially now." She didn't look up. "A route nobody records is a route that dies with the people who knew it. And then somebody has to freeze on that traverse all over again." She glanced at him, firelight catching the fever-bright edge of her exhaustion. "Consider it a lesson in why maps matter. And why mine haven't always deserved to."

Before he could answer, Sable, who had taken the first watch at the shelf's edge, said quietly: "Lights."

They killed the fire instantly. Down in the gorge, far below and upstream — on the far side of the flood they'd spent the day defeating — a string of lanterns had appeared along the opposite rim, moving with the unhurried discipline of men who knew exactly where they were going. Six lights. Seven. Then a eighth, separate from the rest, higher up, pausing at the top of the Ladder's descent route on the north side.

Pausing. As if reading the wall. As if considering.

"They can't cross the flood," Tam whispered.

"No," Sable said. Her voice in the dark was very calm, and very cold. "But floods go down. And we just showed them the way up."

The lone eighth light lingered a moment longer at the base of their route — a long, deliberate pause that felt, impossibly, like a salute — then dropped out of sight behind the rim.

In the silence that followed, Wren said, almost to herself: "That's not a search party. Search parties spread out. That's a column." She turned her sketch pad over, quickly, as if the drawing of the Ladder were suddenly dangerous. "They know this route exists. Which means someone told them. Someone who's climbed it before."

No one answered. Above them the stars came out hard and cold, and below them the river ran on through the dark, carrying everything it carried — and Ivo lay awake a long time, listening to the water, wondering which of the dead had sold them, and which of the living.
