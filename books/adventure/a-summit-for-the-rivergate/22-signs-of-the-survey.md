## 22. Signs of the Survey

Sable found the first cairn at dawn, and she knew at once it was wrong.

Not wrong the way a broken thing is wrong — wrong the way a stranger's handwriting is wrong on a letter that claims to come from family. It stood on a shelf of gray limestone above the seep where they'd watered the night before, three stones stacked with a precision no wind or water could manage. Each rock seated flush against the next. The top stone was flat as a plate.

Her father had taught her to read cairns before he'd taught her letters. *A trail cairn says come this way,* he'd told her, crouched beside her in the dust of Terrace Country, his hands shaping stones while she watched. *But a survey cairn says I was here. Remember the difference, little bird. One is an invitation. The other is a claim.*

This was a claim.

She circled it twice without touching it. The stones were local — she recognized the lichen-crusted limestone from the shelf itself, which meant whoever built it hadn't carried material in, hadn't hurried. They'd had time. They'd had calm. And they'd built it high enough to be seen from the canyon floor below, which meant they wanted to be found.

Or wanted someone to think they could be.

"Sable." Wren Halloway's voice came up from below, hoarse with the dry air. "You see something?"

"Come up," Sable said. Then, because the word felt too much like an order and she was not these people's captain: "Careful. The last ten feet are loose."

Wren climbed badly — she climbed everything badly now, her wounded thigh making each movement a negotiation — but she climbed, and Ivo came behind her with his hands ready at her back the whole way, not touching, just there. Sable had stopped pretending not to notice that. She'd also stopped pretending it didn't do something strange to the inside of her chest, watching a man who had spent nine chapters of his life chasing her turn all that attention into something gentler.

Ivo reached the shelf and stopped when he saw the cairn. She watched him read it the way she had — the deliberate stacking, the sightline down to the canyon, the absence of any mark that belonged to the country itself.

"That wasn't here six days ago," he said. "I scouted this shelf when we crossed below."

"You're sure?"

"I'm sure." He crouched, not touching either, studying the base stones. "There's no dust in the seams. This went up inside two days. Maybe one."

Sable looked north, up the long throat of the canyon toward the country none of them could see yet — the flooded gorge bypassed, the timberline still days ahead, and beyond it whatever waited at the head of the river. Somewhere up there were eleven missing surveyors who had abandoned their own camps, scratched GOING TO THE SOURCE into a canteen, and walked into the most forbidden country on the map.

She had assumed they were dead. She had assumed it almost comfortably, the way you assume anything about people you've never met — it made the story simpler. Dead men couldn't sell the source. Dead men couldn't dam it. Dead men were just a tragedy, and tragedies didn't require decisions.

The cairn said otherwise. The cairn said: *we are alive, we are moving, we are leaving signposts.*

"They want to be followed," Wren said. Her fingers hovered over the top stone without landing. "That's what I can't get past. Surveyors don't mark their route for rescue parties unless—"

"Unless they're not lost," Sable finished.

* * *

They found the second cairn by midmorning, and the third an hour after that, and by the time the sun stood straight overhead Sable had stopped counting them as surprises and started reading them as sentences.

Each one sat at a junction where the canyon offered a choice — a side draw, a fork, a climb versus a contour. And at every junction, the cairn pointed the same direction: upstream. Toward the source. Not once did a marker indicate water, or shelter, or a descent to easier ground. Whoever built them wasn't asking for help. They were leaving instructions.

"It's a route," Ivo said quietly, standing at the third junction with his hat brim shading his eyes. "They're publishing their route."

"To whom?" Wren asked. "There's nobody out here but us."

"There's somebody," Sable said.

She didn't say the rest of it. She didn't have to. They all remembered the rider on the rim, gone every time they looked; they remembered Voss's garbled transmission and the static after; they remembered the ambush at the ford and the hired men who had attacked them with a thoroughness that spoke of pay rather than malice. If the financier's force was climbing the river — and everything about the ambush suggested it was — then a line of cairns pointing upstream was either a taunt or a beacon.

Or both.

But there was a third possibility, and it had been sitting in Sable's chest since the first cairn like a swallowed stone, and around midday, at the fourth marker, she finally said it aloud.

"These aren't rescue signals," she said. "Look at the placement again. Every junction, same bearing, always upstream. My father marked routes like this when he was guiding parties through country he didn't trust them to remember. It's not *help us.* It's *this is the way.*" She turned to face the other two. "They're not lost. They're not hiding. They're marching."

Ivo's jaw worked. "Marching where? We're past the last of their camps. There's nothing up there but—"

"The source," Sable said. "They're going home to the source. On purpose. All of them."

Wren sat down slowly on a boulder, the way people sit when their legs make the decision for them. "Eleven people," she said. "Eleven trained surveyors, with instruments, with supplies, abandoning a funded expedition to walk into closed country. That's not desertion. Desertion runs downhill, toward towns, toward pay. This—" she gestured at the cairn, at the canyon, at the whole patient geography of stone "—this is conviction."

"Conviction of what kind?" Ivo asked.

And there it was, the question Sable had been circling since dawn. Because she had grown up on her father's stories of the source country, and she knew how many kinds of conviction existed in the world. There was the conviction of men who wanted to own a thing. There was the conviction of men who wanted to protect a thing. And there was a third kind, rarer and far more frightening, that lived in people who decided a thing was too important to be owned *or* protected — people who concluded that if they couldn't keep the world's hands off it, the world shouldn't have it at all.

Her father had spoken of such men only once, in a voice she'd never heard him use otherwise. He'd been talking about an old prospector who had tried to collapse a silver seam rather than let a company take it, taking himself along with it. *Some men,* her father had said, *when they find out they can't guard the door, decide to burn the house.* He'd shaken his head. *I never decided whether they were wrong.*

She looked at the cairn. Flat-topped, precise, patient.

"Burn the house," she murmured.

"What?" Ivo said.

"Nothing." But it wasn't nothing, and she saw him register that, and she saw him decide — as he had been deciding more and more often lately — to let her keep it until she was ready to give it up. That was another thing doing strange work in her chest: being allowed to hold a silence instead of having it taken.

* * *

They made camp early that evening in a hollow beneath an overhang, a place Sable knew from childhood — her father had called it the Kettle, a pocket of shade where cold air pooled even in high summer, and where a small fire could be hidden from any eye above the rim. She showed them how to build it low, using dead tamarisk that burned nearly smokeless, and she caught Ivo watching her hands as she arranged the stones and thought: *he's learning my father's country through my father's daughter,* and didn't know what to feel about that except that it was happening.

Wren spread her surviving maps on a flat rock and worked by firelight, redrawing by hand what the ambush had cost them in paper. Her fever had broken two days ago, but it had left her thinner, and there was a new stillness in her that Sable recognized from grief — the stillness of someone who had burned part of herself for fuel and was learning to run on less.

"The arithmetic keeps getting worse," Wren said, not looking up. "Every day we spend climbing is a day the river drops. If we're not below the last rapids within—"

"We know," Ivo said gently.

"No, you don't, actually, because I recalculated." Wren set down her pencil. "With the supplies we lost at the ford, our margin isn't twenty days anymore. It's fifteen. Maybe fourteen. Which means every cairn we stop to study, every hour we spend wondering about these surveyors, is coming directly out of our lives." She looked up, and the firelight made her face older than it was. "So I need to ask the question nobody wants to ask. Do we follow them? Or do we cut east through the terraces, drop to the lower river, and get out alive?"

Silence. The fire ticked. Somewhere above, wind moved along the rim with a sound like slow breathing.

"They're alive," Ivo said finally. "Eleven people are alive up there, and the men who tried to kill us are climbing toward them, and you're asking if we should walk away."

"I'm asking if we should die for people who may not want saving," Wren said. "That's a different question, and pretending it isn't will get us killed faster."

Sable had been turning a smooth stone over in her fingers, and now she set it down. "They don't want saving," she said. "That's exactly what the cairns are telling us. Rescue parties leave distress marks — three stacked, one crossed, signs any ranger reads. These are route markers. They're saying *come this way* to anyone who comes at all." She met Ivo's eyes across the fire. "Which means they know someone's behind them. And they want that someone to catch up."

"Why?" Ivo asked. "Witnesses? Recruits?"

"Witnesses," Sable said. "Maybe. Or maybe they want to show us something before they do it." The stone-cold certainty from the morning rose in her again, and this time she let it out fully. "My father used to say there were three things a man could want from sacred water. To drink it. To sell it. Or to make sure no one else ever could. The financier's men want to sell it — we've known that since the ford. But these surveyors walked away from the man who paid them. Walked *uphill*, into worse country, with no radio and no supply line." She paused. "What if they figured out what the source is worth, and decided worth was the problem?"

Wren had gone very still. "You think they mean to destroy it."

"I think," Sable said carefully, "that some of them might. And I think their chief has been walking them toward it one cairn at a time, and every marker points the same direction, and not one of them asks for help."

Ivo stared into the fire. When he spoke, his voice was low. "At the second camp, the message on the canteen. GOING TO THE SOURCE. I read it as desperation. Lost people striking out for the one landmark they trusted." He shook his head slowly. "It wasn't desperation. It was a declaration."

"And now they're signing their work," Wren said bitterly. "Cairns instead of signatures."

* * *

Sable took the middle watch, as she had taken it most nights, and Ivo took it with her, as he had begun to without either of them discussing it. They sat side by side at the mouth of the hollow, looking out at the canyon under a sky salted with stars, and for a long time neither spoke.

It was Ivo who broke the quiet, in the voice he used for hard things — flat, careful, each word placed like one of those cairn stones.

"When I was a boy," he said, "I saw men diverting water out of the upper springs. Channels dug in secret, flow stolen before it ever reached the river. Your father died stopping them. And I ran." A breath. "I've been running the arithmetic on that my whole life. What one boy could have done. What one shout would have cost."

Sable said nothing. There was nothing to say that he hadn't already said to himself ten thousand ways.

"Now there are eleven grown surveyors," he went on, "armed, supplied, organized, walking toward that same water with a purpose I can't read. And I keep thinking — this time I'm here. This time I'm not a child on a ridge. Whatever they intend, I could stand between it and them." He laughed softly, without humor. "Except I don't know which side to stand on. That's the thing I can't figure. If they mean to sell it, I fight them. If they mean to save it, I help them. But if they mean to kill it—" His hands opened, empty. "Sable, what do you do when the people guarding the door decide to burn the house?"

She had been asking herself the same question since dawn, and she still didn't have an answer, and somehow it helped enormously that he didn't either.

"You find out which it is," she said. "Before you pick a side. That's all. You climb fast and you watch close and you don't decide until the deciding is honest."

He nodded. In the starlight his profile was all edges — the jaw, the brow, the scar over his ear she'd noticed weeks ago and never asked about. She found, not for the first time, that she trusted the edges. Trustworthy things usually had them.

Then he stiffened.

She saw it before she heard anything — the particular stillness that came over him when his body caught what his mind hadn't yet. She followed his gaze up the canyon, north, into the dark.

At first there was nothing. Then: light. Faint, high, far up the canyon's throat — a flicker of orange that appeared, held, and vanished. Appeared again. Rhythmic. Deliberate.

"A fire?" she whispered.

"No." His voice had dropped to almost nothing. "Too regular. Too short. That's a signal lamp. Shuttered lantern — flash, cover, flash." He counted under his breath. "Three long. Two short. Pause. Three long, two short."

"Do you know it?"

"Ranger code." His jaw tightened. "It's not a distress call. Distress is two long, one short, repeated. This is—" He stopped. Started again, slower. "This is an acknowledgment. Three long, two short means *message received. Continue as planned.*"

The words settled over the hollow like cold air pooling.

"Somebody's answering them," Sable breathed. "From downriver."

"From behind us." Ivo was already moving to the lip of the hollow, flattening himself against stone, though the light had gone and the canyon was black again. "The financier's force. They're climbing. They've been climbing this whole time, and the surveyors know it, and they're confirming the schedule." He turned back to her, and the starlight showed her something she hadn't seen on his face before — not fear exactly, but the specific grim clarity of a man whose last comfortable assumption has just died.

"They're not fleeing the hired men," he said. "They're coordinating with them. The expedition was never lost. It was *delivered* — and whatever happens at that source, both sides mean to be there for it."

Above the rim, the wind changed, carrying down from the north a smell that didn't belong to the high desert at all: green water, wet stone, the cold breath of living rock. The smell of the source, reaching for them across miles of dark.

Sable put her hand on Ivo's arm. "Then we climb tonight," she said. "While they're still signaling. Whatever they're planning — we get there first."
