## 23. The Financier's Men

Ivo saw the smoke before he saw the camp.

It rose from the notch of the pass ahead — a thin gray thread against the morning, too deliberate to be an accident, too small to be carelessness. Someone wanted a fire and didn't much care who knew it. He lay flat on the rimrock with his binoculars pressed to his eyes and counted, because counting was what he was good at, counting and waiting and knowing exactly how bad a thing was before anyone else did.

Eight men. Maybe nine — one shape kept moving behind a rock wall and he couldn't be sure it wasn't two. Four tents, a mule line, a cook fire built in a stone ring that had been there for years, which meant they'd chosen the site from knowledge, not luck. Rifles stacked on a tarp within reach of the fire. A man on the high ledge above the pass with a scoped rifle slung across his back, scanning the terraces below with the patient boredom of someone being paid by the day.

"They're sitting on the only way through," Wren whispered beside him. She'd come up the slope on her hands and one knee, favoring the wound in her side, and she'd refused every offer to stay behind with such flat fury that Ivo had stopped offering. Her face was gray with the effort and her voice came out thin. "That's the last defensible ground before the high country. After that it's open plateau for two days — nowhere to hold, nowhere to hide. If we don't take this pass, we don't take any pass."

Ivo lowered the binoculars. Below them, the party waited in the shadow of an overhang — Sable, the two porters, the half-mad survivor Halvard, who sat with his back against the stone and his hands over his ears, muttering the names of the men who'd tried to erase him. Nine days out from the ford where the ambush had cost them their supplies, their radio, and nearly Wren's life. Nine days of short rations and longer silences.

"Eight rifles," Ivo said. "We have four, and one of them belongs to a man who flinches at his own shadow. They have water cached, high ground, and all the time in the world. Their job is to wait. Ours is to move. That's the whole arithmetic of it."

"Then we go around," Wren said.

"There is no around. That's what makes it the pass."

Sable had been quiet the whole time, lying on her belly with her chin on her folded hands, watching the camp the way she watched everything — as if it were a river and she were reading its current. Now she spoke without lifting her eyes.

"There's a way."

Ivo turned his head. She was looking at the terraces to the west of the pass, a chaos of wind-carved shelves stepping up toward the plateau, banded red and white like the ribs of some enormous buried animal.

"That's not a way," he said. "That's a cliff with ambitions."

"It's a way if you know it." She rolled onto her side, propping herself on one elbow, and her eyes when they met his had the flat bright certainty he'd learned to both trust and dread. "My father ran water out of the high country through those terraces. There are channels cut into the shelves — old ones, older than the survey, older than the stations. He showed me when I was nine. We can come up behind their camp from the west rim, above the sentry, and be looking down the back of their necks by tomorrow's dark."

"And if your memory of a route you walked as a child is off by one terrace?" Ivo kept his voice level, though he felt the old heat rising in his chest. "There's no water on that route. No shade. If we're seen on those open shelves we're targets on a wall, and they have a scoped rifle and nothing better to do than shoot."

"If we go straight at them, we're targets in a doorway, and they have eight rifles and the high ground." She sat up. "You want to charge a fortified pass with four men and a limping cartographer because charging is what you know how to do. That's not tactics, Ivo. That's pride wearing tactics' clothes."

The words landed harder than she probably meant them to, or maybe exactly as hard as she meant them to. He looked away, down at the smoke thread, and made himself think like a ranger instead of like a man who'd spent three weeks learning that his whole life had been bought and paid for by the people now waiting to kill them.

"I'm not saying we don't use your terraces," he said at last. "I'm saying we don't bet everything on them. We split it. You take Wren and Halvard up the west shelves — slow, quiet, no fires. I take the porters and stay here, make noise, make them think we're massing for a frontal push. While they're watching the front, you come down on their rear and we take the pass from both ends."

"No." It came out flat and immediate. "You're not staying in front of eight rifles to be a distraction."

"Somebody has to hold their eyes."

"Somebody who isn't you." She was on her knees now, close enough that he could see the wind-chap on her cheeks and the exhaustion underneath the anger. "You've been trying to get yourself killed since the ford. Every argument we've had, you've argued for the option with the most blood in it. At the narrows you wanted to run the rapids instead of portage. At the dry camps you wanted to push through the night instead of resting. You keep dressing it up as duty, but I've watched my father's people do this — the ones who carry a debt too long start thinking the only clean thing left is to die paying it. Except yours was a lie, Ivo. You don't owe anybody your life. So stop spending it like you do."

The porters had gone very still by the mules. Wren was studying the terraces with sudden intense interest. Even Halvard had lifted his hands from his ears.

Ivo felt the argument in his chest like a splinter working toward the surface — because she was right, and he knew she was right, and knowing it made it worse. Pride was a thing he'd never had much use for. He'd told himself that for twenty years. He was a careful man, a patient man, a man of small competent motions. But she'd put her finger on the truth of it: every time the choice came, he chose the hard way, the direct way, the way that put his own body between the danger and everyone else. It had looked like duty. It had looked like the debt. And now that the debt was ash, it just looked like a man who didn't know how to be worth anything except as a shield.

"The terraces might not hold," he said, quieter.

"They'll hold. My father's routes hold. That's the whole point of my father's routes." She put her hand on his forearm — brief, hard, gone again. "Trust the land I'm from. You've been trusting your own for twenty years and look where it got you."

* * *

They argued the shape of it for another hour, and what finally emerged was neither of their plans entire, but a thing built in the space between them, which Ivo suspected was the only place anything good had been built in a long time.

Sable would take the west terraces at first light with Wren and Halvard. Wren knew the value of it — the survey's own maps showed nothing on those shelves, blank space where blank space had been drawn by order, and there was a bitter rightness in using the erased country to undo the erasers. Halvard, in his lucid hours, was a crack shot, and his madness had a direction now; he wanted to face the men who'd signed the order to erase him, and Sable promised him he would.

Ivo would hold the front with the two porters. Not to charge — he'd conceded that much, and the conceding had cost him something he didn't have a name for — but to be *visible*. To build fires in the draw below the pass, to move mules in plain sight, to let the sentry count the same four men a dozen times until the number became the whole truth in the hired men's heads. A garrison that's sure of what's in front of it forgets to check behind.

"And when we're in position above their camp," Sable said, tracing it in the dirt with a stick, "you make your move at the front. Not a charge. Noise. Shots into the rocks, mules driven at the draw, everything that says *we're coming through the pass now*. They'll turn every rifle to the front. That's when we come down on the mule line. We don't kill anyone we don't have to. We scatter their stock, take their water, and put them on foot in country they don't know. Then the pass is ours and their job is survival, not ambush."

"And if they don't turn?" Ivo asked. "If they keep eyes on the rear?"

"Then you don't make your move, and we pull back, and we try it again the next night. We're not in a race." She paused. "We're in a race. But not that kind. Twenty days, Wren said. We spend one of them getting this right instead of getting it dead."

He looked at the plan in the dirt, at the small X she'd drawn above the camp on the west rim, at the arrows converging. It was a good plan. It was better than his. He'd known it since the first hour and had kept arguing anyway, and he understood finally that the arguing wasn't about the plan at all. It was about the fact that following her route meant following her father's country, her father's knowledge, her father's ghost — walking into the source country on a dead man's paths instead of arriving as the law, guns first, warrant in his pocket. He'd been the law for twenty years. It had been a lie the whole time. And a man whose law was a lie will reach for the next hardest true thing, which is his own two hands and a straight fight, because at least a straight fight belongs to him.

"All right," he said. "Your terraces. My noise."

"Your noise," she agreed, and almost smiled. "You're good at noise. You argued with me for three hours about a plan you agreed with after ten minutes."

* * *

They spent the rest of the day preparing, and the preparation had the quality of a held breath.

The porters — old Tamas and his silent nephew Brek — cached the mules in a box canyon two miles back and carried forward only what the plan needed: the two rifles, the last of the coffee, a coil of rope, and a signal arrangement of mirrors and cloth that Wren devised for the terraces, three flashes for *in position*, two for *pull back*, one long steady glare for the thing nobody wanted a word for.

Sable and Ivo walked the first mile of the west route together before dark, because he'd demanded it and because she'd seen he needed to. The terraces were worse and better than they'd looked from below — worse because the shelves tilted and shed gravel in long whispering slides; better because she moved through them like water finding its level, never hesitating, her hand going to holds he couldn't see until she showed him. Here a chisel mark in the rock, decades old, lichen-filled: her father's. Here a stacked cairn no taller than a fist. Here a seep, a dark stain on the underside of an overhang where a tin cup could fill in an hour, and she touched the stone when they passed it the way another person would touch a grave.

"He cut these to move water down to the dry terraces," she said. "Farming. Before the stations came, before the surveys, people lived up here. The maps say nobody ever did." Her jaw tightened. "Wren's maps say nobody ever did. She's not the only one who erased things."

"She was ordered."

"So were the men in that camp, probably." She looked west, where the sun was going down in a furnace of red. "That's the thing you still don't understand, I think. You keep looking for the evil ones. There aren't any. There's just men with orders and men without them, and the whole difference in the world is which you decide to be."

They came down in the dark, and he lay that night under the overhang listening to the wind move through the pass, and did not sleep, and did not pretend to himself that it was only the plan keeping him awake.

* * *

First light came up gray and cold.

He watched them go. Sable first, with Wren's pack redistributed onto her own shoulders without discussion, then Wren, moving slow and set-jawed, then Halvard with the rifle slung and his lips moving through his litany of names like a man counting rosary beads. They went up the first shelf and the second, and then the morning haze took them, and Ivo stood in the draw below the pass with two porters, two rifles, and the specific loneliness of a man whose job now was to be seen.

He built the first fire big. Green greasewood on the coals so it smoked white and tall, a flag of smoke standing over the draw like a declaration. He moved the mules — the two spare animals they'd recovered at the ford — back and forth along the visible stretch of the draw, letting the sentry's glass find them, lose them, find them again. Tamas built a second fire. Brek stacked rocks in a rough breastwork where anyone glassing the draw would read it as preparation for a fight.

By midmorning the sentry on the high ledge had shifted his weight twice. By noon a second man had joined him. Ivo lay in the shade of the rocks with the binoculars and watched the camp's attention swing to the front like a door on its hinge, and he felt the old pride stir and made himself name it, finally, in his own head: not courage, not duty. Fear. The direct fight had always been fear wearing a uniform — because a charge was over in minutes, and a plan required him to sit still and trust, and trust was the one thing twenty years of lies had taught him not to do.

He was still learning it. He'd probably be learning it at the source, if they reached the source.

The mirror flashed at mid-afternoon. Three flashes, off the western rim, so brief that Tamas missed it and Ivo had to swear he'd seen it. Three. In position.

The sun went down. The camp fires of the hired men glowed in the notch, and their voices carried faintly on the evening wind — laughter, the clink of a pot, the easy sounds of men who believed tomorrow would be like today. Ivo ate a strip of dried meat he didn't taste and checked the rifles and waited for full dark, and when it came, thick and starless behind the high clouds, he gave the word.

Tamas drove the mules up the draw with a shout and a thrown stone, and Brek fired three rounds into the rock face above the pass, and Ivo stood up in the open and fired four more, spaced and deliberate, the noise rolling down the canyon walls like a rockfall. The camp erupted. Lanterns. Shouts. The scoped rifle on the ledge cracked twice into the dark at nothing. Every light in the pass swung to the front, every voice, every rifle — and Ivo stood in the open with his heart slamming and did the hardest thing he had done in twenty years of hard things, which was nothing. He stood and made noise and trusted.

For a long minute, nothing happened on the west rim.

Then the night above the hired men's camp lit up — a single mirror flash, long and steady, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the sound of mules screaming and a man's voice rising in panic, and the lantern light in the camp began to swing wildly, front to rear, front to rear, like a man turning to see which direction the world was ending from.

Ivo was already moving up the draw, and the pass — the last defensible pass — was coming apart in front of him.
