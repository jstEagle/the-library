## 24. The Long Detour

Wren Halloway had spent eleven years learning to read stone, and the stone of the detour country said one thing, over and over, in every dialect it possessed: *nothing lives here.*

They stood at the mouth of a side canyon at first light, the four of them and their wounded cartographer, looking out across a plateau of pale sandstone that ran to the horizon in long wind-carved waves. No seeps marked on Sable's memory-map. No shade taller than a man. The sun would come up over that plain like a hammer coming down on an anvil, and they would be the metal.

"Two days," Sable said. "Maybe two and a half if the wind turns. There's a slot system on the far side — my father called it the Throat. It lets us come up behind the pass instead of through it."

"And there's no way around this?" Ivo asked, though Wren could see he already knew the answer. He'd been studying the rim all morning with his binoculars, watching the hired men's camp smoke at the last defensible notch ahead. Watching them wait.

"Not for us," Sable said. "Not carrying her." She nodded at Wren without apology. Wren had stopped minding being spoken about as cargo around day three of carrying her. Mostly.

Her leg was bound tight from thigh to ankle, splinted with a tent pole and strips of shirt, and she rode in a litter made of pack frames and the last good rope whenever the ground allowed it, which on this plateau it mostly wouldn't. She could hop short distances on the good leg with Ivo's shoulder under her arm. The rest of the time she was weight, and weight was water, and water was the arithmetic that governed everything now.

"Let me see your figures again," she said.

Sable crouched beside her and scratched in the dirt with a stick: four people, one half-crippled. Eighteen liters in the good containers, six in the cracked ones that sweated. Two days' march at a wounded party's pace — call it thirty miles over broken ground. Four liters a person per day was survival rations, not comfort. The math came out to nothing left over. No margin. No error.

"It's honest math," Wren said quietly. "I just hate it."

"Then hate it walking," Ivo said, and shouldered the front of her litter.

* * *

The first six hours were the worst thing Wren had ever done sober.

The plateau wasn't flat — that was the cruelty of it. It rolled, swell after swell of slickrock polished by ten thousand years of wind, each swell higher than the last, so that cresting one only revealed another. The litter had to be carried high, above the head sometimes, when the stone dropped away in shelves. Twice they lowered her down faces by rope, her splinted leg swinging like something that belonged to someone else. The heat came off the rock in visible shimmer, and by noon the air itself seemed to have weight.

She kept herself occupied the only way she knew how: surveying.

Even crippled, even half-delirious with thirst, her eyes did what her eyes had always done. She measured the strata as they passed — the great tilted beds of the plateau dipping eastward, younger over older, a textbook unconformity exposed along a wash where flash floods had stripped the surface skin away. She noted the wind direction from the orientation of the sand ripples. She estimated distances by pacing her own gaze across features whose size she could guess. Somewhere under the pain and the fear, a small dry voice in her kept saying: *this would make a beautiful sheet. Plate fourteen. Structure sections, cross-bedding azimuths—*

"You're smiling," Ivo said. He was walking beside the litter, one hand on its frame, watching her face with that ranger's attention that missed nothing.

"I'm mapping," she said. "In my head. It's how I pray."

He almost smiled back. Then the wind turned, and neither of them prayed or mapped anything for a while, because the wind off the open stone was like standing inside an oven's breath, and they put their backs to it and walked with their collars up and their lips split, and the plateau went on being exactly what it was.

They rested at midday in the shadow of a fallen slab no deeper than a doorway. All four of them couldn't fit; two sat in the shade and two sat against the hot rock outside it and traded places every twenty minutes. Sable rationed water in mouthfuls measured out of a tin cup, and nobody complained, because complaining cost moisture.

It was during the second rest, in the late afternoon, that Sable stopped, looked back the way they'd come, and said, "We have to talk about the load."

* * *

They laid everything out on a flat shelf as the light went long and amber: the packs, the medical kit, the food — what remained of it, a sad scatter of flour, dried meat gone leathery, a tin of rendered fat — the rope, the tools, the water. And then, at the edge of the arrangement, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with survey cord, Wren's instruments.

Her theodolite, its brass body dented from the ambush but true. Her levels. Her chain of scales. Her drawing kit — pens, ink, the waterproof case. And beneath all of it, wrapped separately because she always wrapped them separately, the maps.

Not the official maps. Those were gone, burned or scattered at the ford, and good riddance. These were hers. Eleven years of field sheets. The Rivergate in pencil and ink at scales from 1:250,000 down to the intimate 1:5,000 sheets of individual canyon mouths. Notes in three colors of ink. Elevations she had carried up cliffs on her own back. The corrections she had begun making since joining this party — the erased approaches restored, the false bends struck through, the honest lines drawn over the dishonest ones. Her repair work. Her penance, in cartography.

"We can't carry it," Sable said. Not unkindly. Just factually, the way she said most things. "The instruments alone are thirty pounds. The maps are five more. That's two days of water for one person."

"I'll carry them myself," Wren said. "On the litter they weigh nothing—"

"They weigh what they weigh," Ivo said gently. "And every pound is a pound of water somebody has to haul up a swell in this heat. You know the numbers better than any of us."

That was the obscenity of it. She did know the numbers. She had taught the numbers. She had stood in front of green surveyors in a classroom in Meridian City and told them: *the field is a budget, and sentiment is a line item, and the day you can't strike a line item is the day you die in a canyon.* She had never once believed she would be the line item.

"There has to be a compromise," she said, and heard her own voice go thin.

They compromised the way compromises actually happen, which is to say badly and by degrees. The theodolite stayed — too heavy, too precise, too dead a weight without a tripod to stand it on. The levels went into the hole. The drawing kit she fought for and won, barely: one pen, one pencil stub, a folding rule, a dozen sheets of paper. Enough to record. Not enough to draw.

The maps took until full dark.

She knelt over them on the shelf while the others stood back and gave her the courtesy of distance, and she untied the oilcloth with fingers that shook, and she went through them one at a time, because she could not help herself, because each one was a season of her life.

Here was the Narrows at 1:10,000, drawn the summer she was twenty-four, before she'd learned what the office did with her careful lines. Here was Terrace Country, annotated in Sable's father's hand — she'd copied those annotations faithfully into the official sheets and watched the office strike them out as *unverified local tradition.* Here was the first map she'd ever altered under orders, the source approaches smudged into blankness with a blending stump, and here, folded inside it, the honest version she'd redrawn from memory two years later and hidden in her boot lining ever since, waiting for a courage she hadn't known she was saving up.

"Wren." Ivo crouched beside her. He didn't touch the maps. "We can take three. Choose."

Three. Out of forty-one sheets. She looked at them all lying there in the last light, and understood that choosing was worse than losing. Losing was done to you. Choosing you did to yourself, and had to live with knowing you'd done it.

She chose the honest source approach, because it was the reason for everything. She chose the terrace sheets with Corren's annotations, because some things deserved to survive their author. And after a long time she chose the very first field sheet she'd ever drawn, aged nineteen, of a canyon so minor it didn't have a name, because it was the only one in the stack that was pure — drawn before ambition, before orders, before complicity, just a girl and a canyon and a love of the shapes of the world.

"The rest," she said, and her voice held, which surprised her, "go in the ground."

They buried them properly, because Sable insisted that anything worth grieving was worth burying well. They dug into a soft seam between slabs with the shovel end of the hatchet, and Wren wrapped the maps in the oilcloth and the instruments in the tent fly, and they weighted the whole with stones against the wind and the rain that might someday, improbably, come.

She didn't cry while she worked. She cried after, when it was done, sitting on the shelf with her splinted leg straight out in front of her and the stars coming up over the plateau, and the crying was ugly and silent and lasted longer than she would have guessed grief for paper could last. Because it wasn't paper. It was the eleven years. It was the self who had made them — the young woman with clean hands and a clean eye who had believed a map was a promise you made to strangers: *here is the world, truly, I checked.*

Ivo sat beside her without speaking. After a while he said, "When this is over, we come back for them. I'll carry them out myself."

"You can't promise that."

"I can promise to try. It's not the same thing, but it's what I've got."

She wiped her face with the back of her hand and found that she was laughing a little, wetly, at the absurdity. "A ranger promising to recover contraband cartography from a hole in the desert. Your commission would weep."

"My commission," Ivo said, "can stand in line behind everything else that's weeping lately."

* * *

The second day was harder than the first, which Wren would not have believed possible, and was made bearable only by the fact that her mind, deprived of instruments, began doing its work anyway, raw and unaided.

Without a theodolite she navigated by dead reckoning and the sun, calling bearings to Sable, who moved through her father's country with the unselfconscious certainty of a woman walking her own hallway. Without her levels she estimated the grade of each interminable swell by the effort of the men hauling her and corrected for the slope in her head. She kept the log in her new, reduced kit — one pen, rationed paper — writing small and dense, recording times, headings, water consumed, the character of the ground, and once, in the gray hour before dawn when they'd made camp in a hollow and she couldn't sleep for the cold coming off the stone:

*Burying the instruments felt like burying the person who thought she'd spend her life measuring the world honestly. But a map isn't the territory and an instrument isn't the eye. The eye still works. Note to self, if there is a self left at the end of this: the eye still works.*

They crossed the deep middle of the plateau through the heat of the second afternoon, and this was the hour that nearly finished them. The wind died completely, and the silence that replaced it was total — no birds, no insects, no water sound, nothing but their own breathing and the scrape of boots and litter runners on stone. Wren, riding, watched the horizon swim and realized she was past thirsty into something else, a ringing clarity, and that Ivo had stopped sweating, which she knew from her medical reading was a bad sign, and she made him drink from her own ration by the simple expedient of refusing to drink hers until he did.

"Cartographers," he rasped, wiping his mouth, "are bossy."

"Cartographers are *correct,*" she said. "There's a difference. Look it up. Oh — wait."

Even Sable laughed at that, a short bark, and the laugh carried them another mile.

Near sunset of the second day the plateau finally broke. The land tipped and fractured into the head of the slot system Sable had promised — the Throat — a sinuous crack in the earth running north toward the pass, its walls close enough to touch in places, its floor shaded for the first time in two days. Real shade. Wren nearly wept again, and this time didn't fight it, because the shade was cool on her face and some things deserved tears.

They made camp fifty meters inside the Throat, out of the wind, and Sable climbed the slot wall to look back the way they'd come. Wren watched her go still up there, silhouetted against the last red light, staring south across the ocean of stone they'd crossed.

"What is it?" Ivo called softly.

Sable came down fast, dropping the last eight feet, landing on her good foot and her palm. "Smoke," she said. "Back along our trail. A long way off, but fresh — it wasn't there yesterday." She looked at each of them in turn. "Somebody's following our line. Reading our burial site, maybe. Or just tracking us through waterless country, which means they're either very good or very desperate."

"The financier's men are ahead of us, at the pass," Ivo said slowly. "Voss is downriver. So who—" He stopped. Wren saw him arrive at it the same moment she did.

"The rider," Wren said. "The watcher from the rim. He's been paralleling us for days. Now he's behind us." She looked down at her one pen, her twelve sheets of paper, her empty hands where the instruments used to be. "Which means whatever he wants, he wants it badly enough to cross the worst country I've ever seen. And the only things we're carrying that anyone could want—" she touched the oilcloth bundle holding three maps and a pencil stub, "—are these."

Above them, far up the black throat of the slot, the first stars were coming out. And among them, low in the northeast, Wren noticed — because she noticed everything, it was the one instrument no one could bury — a faint streak of light that fell, and faded, and was followed by another.

"Sable," she said quietly. "Is it going to rain?"

"No," Sable said, following her gaze upward, her face unreadable. "But the sky's about to put on a show. My father called nights like this star-river nights. He said—" she paused, and something moved across her face that Wren had never seen there, something soft and guarded at once, "—he said they were the river remembering where it came from."

Another streak fell. Then another. And somewhere behind them in the dark, faint on the wind, came a sound that none of them could mistake for wind or stone: the far, thin note of metal striking metal, like a hammer on a cairn, like someone building a marker on their trail.

Or like someone signaling.
