## 25. Night of the Falling Star

The plateau opened above them like a held breath.

All day they had climbed out of the slot country — that drowned, echoing world of stone and shadow where every word came back to you changed — and now, near dusk, the land simply stopped trying to be anything but what it was: a high table of wind-scoured rock, flat as a table laid for gods, running to the horizon in every direction. No walls. No cover. Just sky, enormous and unguarded, going violet at its edges as the sun went down behind the terraces they'd left below.

Sable felt it in her chest before she could name it. Open country. Her father's kind of country. The kind where a person could see trouble coming from a day away, or see nothing coming at all, and both of those were honest answers.

"We camp here," Ivo said, and there was no argument in it. Even Voss's porters, the two who remained with them after the ford, had stopped grumbling somewhere around noon. Wren Halloway sat down on her bedroll without being asked twice, her wounded side stiff, her face gray with the particular exhaustion of someone who has been carrying more than her pack.

They made no fire. They'd made no fire since the ambush, since the radio died, since the flares bloomed far below them on the river like flowers of some poisonous season. Fire was a signature. Fire was an invitation. So they ate cold meal cakes and dried meat and drank from the seep Sable had found by reading the stone the way her father had taught her — the dark stain of lichen in a fold of rock, the faint cool breath rising off it at dusk — and then they lay out under the first stars without tents, because tents were weight and weight was time, and time was the one thing the dry season would not sell them cheap.

Sable lay on her back with her hands folded on her stomach and watched the sky do something she had seen only three times in her life.

It began quietly. A single streak, low in the east, so bright it left an afterimage burned into her eye like a brand. Then another, higher. Then a third, and a fourth, and then the sky simply gave up pretending to be orderly and began to rain light.

"Meteors," Wren said softly, from her bedroll. "A storm of them. I've read about these. Dust from a broken comet — the whole earth passes through it, once in a lifetime, sometimes twice if you're lucky." She was propped on one elbow despite her wound, her cartographer's face tilted upward, and in the falling light Sable saw tears on it that Wren made no move to hide or explain.

The streaks came thicker. Some were small and quick, gone between one heartbeat and the next. Some crawled across half the heavens, green-white and hissing — though Sable knew the hiss was imagination, that sound couldn't travel down from that height, that the silence was part of the wonder — trailing sparks that faded like the last embers of a fire nobody had lit.

Nobody spoke for a long while. There was nothing to say that the sky wasn't already saying better.

* * *

Sable did not sleep. She had not slept well since the ford, since Wren's blood soaked into the sand and their supplies burned and the radio died with a crackle that sounded almost human, almost like a voice cut off mid-word. She kept watch by habit, her father's habit, lying still and letting her ears do the work her eyes couldn't in the dark.

But tonight the dark refused to stay dark. The meteors came and came, and each one wrote a brief silver sentence across the inside of her skull, and every sentence said the same thing: *you are small, you are small, you are small, and yet you are here.*

She heard Ivo shift. He was keeping the middle watch, sitting up now against a low shelf of rock, his rifle across his knees, his face turned up. In the strobing light he looked younger than he was. Or maybe just less armored. Out here, under this much sky, all the careful ranger posture fell away from him like a coat hung on a nail.

"You've seen this before," he said quietly. Not a question. He'd learned to read her silences over these weeks the way she'd taught him to read stone stains, and it unsettled her how good he'd gotten at it.

"Twice," she said. "Once when I was six. My father woke us all in the night and carried me outside wrapped in a blanket, and we lay in the yard of our place above the terraces and watched until dawn. He didn't say much. He never said much about beautiful things. He just said, 'Remember this. It won't come again in your life.'" She paused. "He was wrong. It came again when I was nineteen. But I understood, even then, what he meant. He meant: the world is bigger than your troubles. He meant: look up."

Ivo was quiet a moment. "My mother used to say things like that."

"Used to?"

"She's dead. Long time now." He said it the way he said most true things — flatly, quickly, like setting down something heavy before it could make him stagger. "That's partly why the debt papers mattered so much to me. When you have no family, paper is family. Paper tells you who you owe and who owes you and where you stand. I built my whole life on a piece of paper." His laugh was short and had no humor in it. "And it was forged."

The meteors fell. One large one broke apart directly overhead, splitting into three burning fingers that reached down toward the rim of the world and vanished.

"I stole the documents because of my father," Sable said.

She hadn't planned to say it. It came out the way water comes out of a cracked seep — slowly at first, then all at once. Ivo turned his head. She kept her eyes on the sky. It was easier to tell the truth to the sky than to a man's face.

"The Meridian Survey came through the terraces two years ago. Officially they were mapping flood stages. I let them pass through our country because my father always believed the maps were a kind of respect — that being on a map meant mattering. That was his mistake and mine." Her jaw tightened. "But I got curious. And I got careless, or lucky, depending on how you say it. I found the financier's letters in Captain Voss's strongbox. Correspondence between the survey's patron and men in the capital. Buying options. Water rights. Language about 'development potential' and 'exclusive extraction.' They weren't mapping the river, Ivo. They were appraising it. And the source — the thing no one is permitted to see, the thing my father died at the edge of — they intended to find it, buy it, dam it, and own it."

"And the theft," Ivo said. "The reason I was sent after you."

"The reason you were sent after me." She turned her head at last and looked at him. In the meteor light his eyes were very steady. "I took the correspondence. All of it. Because papers are the only weapon that works against men who live by papers. You can't shoot a contract. You can't outrun a deed. But you can take it, and you can carry it to people who will read it aloud, and then the men who wrote it have to live in the same world everyone else does." She looked back up. "I was going to take it to the territorial assessor in Corriander. I never got the chance. Your warrant caught up with me at the salt flats, and everything I'd done got renamed. Thief. Fugitive. As if the only crime in the desert was taking what powerful men had already decided belonged to them."

"And your father," Ivo said, and his voice had changed — lower, rougher, the voice of a man walking carefully across thin ice. "You want to prove—"

"I want the record corrected." The words came hard, and she let them come hard, because softness would have broken her. "When he drowned, the story the towns told was that Tam Corren was a poacher. A man who slipped past the boundary markers at the edge of the source country to steal water for sale, and paid for it. That's what's written. That's what my mother lived under until it killed her, and what I grew up under, and every door in every town closed a little when our name was spoken." A meteor fell, huge and slow, and its light filled the whole plateau white as bone. "But I was there. I was eleven years old, hiding where I shouldn't have been, and I saw what he saw. Men with pumps and pipe, diverting the outflow into cisterns — stealing the river at its throat, before it ever reached a single farm or town downstream. My father confronted them. He was going to report them. And they—" Her breath caught. She made herself finish. "There was a scuffle at the water's edge. The bank gave way. Whether they pushed him or he fell, I don't know; I was too far, and I ran when they started looking around, and I have spent twenty years not knowing, and that not-knowing has done more damage to me than any knowing could."

Ivo said nothing. But she heard him set the rifle down beside him, deliberately, the way a man lays down a tool he no longer trusts himself to hold.

"So yes," she said. "I stole the documents to prove my father died protecting the water. Not stealing it. To prove that the men who called him a thief were the thieves themselves, and that the law they hid behind was bought and paid for before it ever rode out after me." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "And instead the law sent me you. A man carrying a debt to my own family. Do you understand what a joke that is? What a cruel, perfect joke? The universe has a sense of humor, Ivo Eastwick. It's just that none of it is funny."

* * *

The meteor storm thickened toward midnight. It seemed impossible that it could, and then it did — the whole vault of heaven streaked and sown with fire, so many trails crossing that the sky looked like the work of a mad cartographer, inking routes to places no map would ever hold.

Wren slept at last, curled on her side, one hand resting protectively even in sleep on the oilskin roll that held her salvaged field notes. The porters slept. Somewhere below them, days down the terraces, the hired men waited at the last defensible pass, and beyond them the financier's main force climbed the river like a slow tide, and beyond them the missing surveyors moved toward the source with demolition charges and certainty in equal measure. All of it was coming. All of it was real.

But up here, under the falling stars, it felt briefly as though none of it could touch them.

Sable got up and walked to where Ivo sat. He didn't startle — she'd made noise enough on purpose; startling an armed man was a lesson you only needed once — but he watched her come, and made room on the rock shelf without being asked.

She sat. Their shoulders didn't quite touch. The space between was the width of everything unsaid, and tonight it felt narrower than it had yesterday, and wider than it would tomorrow, and she noticed herself noticing, and hated how much she noticed.

"When I was a girl," she said, "my father told me the stars were holes. That the sky was the roof of a great spring underneath everything, and the stars were where the light leaked through." She smiled at the old foolishness of it. "He knew it wasn't true. He was a practical man; he could read water in stone better than any surveyor alive. But he liked telling it anyway, because he said a person should have at least one beautiful lie to balance all the ugly truths."

"My debt papers were an ugly truth," Ivo said slowly, "that turned out to be a lie."

"And the stars are a beautiful lie that turns out to be true — just not the way he told it." She shook her head. "Everything's like that out here. Nothing is what it looks like from a distance. Water isn't where the green is; sometimes the green is a lie and the water runs dry underneath. Maps aren't the land. Warrants aren't justice." She looked at him sideways. "And a thief isn't always a thief."

"No," Ivo said. "And a ranger isn't always on the side of the law." He was quiet a moment, and then he said, in a voice she had to lean slightly toward to hear over the wind: "If we get through what's coming — the pass, the springs, whatever waits in that cavern — I don't know what I am afterward. I resigned nothing yet. I burned nothing yet. But I know I'm not going back to being what they made me. Whatever comes next, I'd like it to be something I chose."

The words hung there between them, offered plainly, asking nothing, which was exactly why they landed so deep.

Sable found that her throat had gone tight. She covered it with dryness, because that was her inheritance too. "Careful, ranger. Talk like that and I'll start thinking you're on my crew."

"Aren't I?" he asked. Simply. Terribly simply.

She didn't answer. Instead she leaned, very slightly, until her shoulder touched his — the smallest possible weight, the kind of touch that could be denied, could be called accidental, could be nothing at all. He didn't move away. The meteors fell and fell, writing their bright vanishing sentences overhead, and the two of them sat shoulder to shoulder on a shelf of stone at the top of the world, guarding a sleeping cartographer and two sleeping porters and a handful of supplies and a truth that was still, against all odds, traveling upstream with them.

After a long time she heard him breathing slower, deeper — sleep taking him at last, sitting up, trusting her to keep the watch. The realization moved through her like warm water: *he trusts me.* After everything. After the warrant and the chase and the salt flats and the lies on both sides. He had laid his sleep in her hands the way he'd laid down his rifle, deliberately, because he no longer trusted himself to hold it alone.

She kept the watch. The storm peaked in the deepest hour of night, a final great fall of fire, hundreds of trails at once, the whole sky streaming silver like the river itself poured out across heaven — and then, gradually, it thinned, and the ordinary stars came back through, patient and few, and the east went the color of ash before flame.

That was when Sable saw it.

Far to the south, down along the black line of the last ridge before the pass — a spark. Small. Steady. Wrong.

Not a meteor. Meteors fell; this held its place. Not a star; stars didn't sit on ridgelines. It flickered once, twice, and she counted the interval without meaning to, the way her father had taught her to count lightning.

Signal fire. Someone down in the pass, talking to someone else in the dark. And the direction of the answering flicker, when it finally came, faint and brief on a second ridge — was north. Behind them. Above them.

The hired men below were not waiting alone.

Sable rose without waking Ivo, stood a moment looking south with the cold wind pulling at her hair, and then knelt and put her hand on his shoulder.

"Wake up," she said quietly. "We have a problem, and we have until dawn to solve it."
