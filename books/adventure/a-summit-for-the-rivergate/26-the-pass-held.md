## 26. The Pass Held

The problem was this: two fires where there should have been one.

Sable laid it out on the flat of her hand in the blue light before dawn, using pebbles and a twist of dry grass. The hired men held the pass itself — a saddle between two shoulders of rock, the only reasonable way north, the only way they could go without adding four days to a journey that had no four days to give. Eight men, by the count of the signal fires and the voices that had carried on the wind. Maybe nine. Camped fat and comfortable at the narrows of the saddle, waiting for morning, because hired men who are paid to wait are paid to be patient.

And behind them — north, above, on the second ridge — another fire. Smaller. Colder discipline. Men who did not talk.

"The ones below are the hammer," Sable said. "The ones above are the anvil. We come through the pass tomorrow, they close it from both ends, and everything we've bled to carry this far goes into their pockets or a shallow grave."

"Then we don't go through," said Ferro, one of the two porters still with them, a Rivergate man with river mud in his voice. "We go around."

"Around is six days through waterless stone." Sable didn't look at him. She was looking at Ivo. "Six days with a wounded woman and no radio and the dry season eating the river under us. Around is dying slowly instead of quickly."

"So we fight," Ivo said. He'd said it the night before, down in the terraces when she'd first sketched the ground. He said it again now, and heard how it sounded — flat, certain, the voice of a man reaching for the simplest tool in a full kit. "Eight men. We know the country better than they do. We hit them at first light while they're cold and stupid—"

"With what?" Sable's voice stayed low, but something under it was sharp as flint. "Two rifles, a scattergun, and a knife between the four of us. You want to trade shots across open ground with men who were sent here to kill surveyors. You want Wren lying on stone with bullets cracking over her while you play soldier."

"I want this finished."

"You want it *simple*." She closed her fist around the pebbles. "Simple is what they're counting on. Simple is what they're *paid* for."

The wind moved through the junipers above their hollow, and below them, faintly, the hired men's fire popped and settled. Ivo looked at the dark shape of the pass against the stars and felt the old thing rise in him — the ranger's arithmetic, the clean ledger of force against force. It was the arithmetic he'd been raised on, the arithmetic his commission had written into his bones: identify the threat, apply overwhelming response, file the report. It had never once occurred to him, in eleven years of service, that the report might be the point. That the whole machinery he'd served might exist to make sure every problem came out looking simple.

He thought about the debt papers in his pack. About how simple they had made his life, all these years. One name, one number, one duty. How easy it was to serve a lie when the lie was tidy.

"All right," he said. "Not simple. What then?"

Sable opened her fist. She rearranged the pebbles slowly, and when she spoke, she spoke like a woman laying out a route through country she'd walked since childhood — which, Ivo realized, was exactly what she was doing. The pass was her father's pass. Every seep, every shelf, every goat-track on those walls had a Corren name on it somewhere, scratched into stone or carried in blood.

"They're camped at the narrows because the narrows is the only place the trail comes up to the saddle gentle enough for mules," she said. "On either side the walls go near-vertical for forty feet, then break into terraces. My father kept a line up the west wall — handholds, chockstones, a ledge system. The hired men will have posted one watcher up there if they're smart. Two if they're very smart. They won't have found the rest of it, because the rest of it doesn't exist unless you know where to put your hands."

"And?"

"And there's a slide chute above the narrows." Her voice went quieter. "Loose scree, packed behind an old retaining wall my grandfather built to keep the trail clear. The wall's rotten. It's been rotten since I was a girl. If someone pulled the right stones loose from above — quietly, in the dark — the whole face would let go at once. Half the mountain coming down into their camp."

Ferro stared at her. "You'd bury them."

"No," Sable said. "I'd *wake* them. There's a gap in the wall at the south end — a channel, deliberate, my father cut it so the chute could be flushed in pieces. If the stones come down right, the slide splits around the channel and hits the camp as two tongues with a lane of clean ground between them. Enough warning to run. Enough noise to think the world is ending." She looked up at Ivo, and in the starlight her eyes were hard and bright and asking him something. "Nobody has to die. But nobody down there sleeps well again either. And the pass stays closed to them for a day while they dig out — a day we use to get above the timberline where their horses can't follow."

Ivo looked at the plan the way he'd been trained to look at terrain, and the training told him it was good, and something older than the training told him why it was good. It wasn't just that it worked. It was that it was *hers* — built out of her family's stone, her family's knowledge, aimed not at destroying men but at unmaking their certainty. A Corren answer. Not a ranger's answer.

"It needs someone above the wall," he said. "Someone to pull the stones and set the direction. That's the whole trick of it — the split has to start clean or it becomes a burial."

"Yes."

"That someone can't be you. If it goes wrong up there, you're the one under it, and you're the only one who knows the routes past the timberline. Wren can't climb. Ferro and Doss don't know the wall." He heard his own voice go quiet and steady, the way it did when he'd stopped debating himself. "It's me or nobody."

Sable was silent for a long moment. Below them the enemy fire burned on, small and orange and oblivious.

"You froze on the ascent at Kestrel Gorge," she said finally. Not cruel. Just true.

"I did."

"This is worse than Kestrel Gorge. Darker. Higher. And if your hands shake at the wrong stone—"

"Then you'll be standing down here watching me die, and you can tell my ghost he should have gone around." He held her gaze. "I spent eleven years being other men's instrument, Sable. Their warrant, their debt, their silence. Tonight I'd like to be something I chose. Let me choose this."

She studied him the way she read weather — for what was coming, not what was showing. Then she reached into her shirt and drew out a stub of chalk-soft white stone, her father's marking stone, worn smooth by thirty years of Corren hands, and pressed it into his palm.

"Then you'll need to know where to put your hands," she said. "Sit down. We have until the moon sets, and I'm going to draw you that wall until you can climb it blind."

* * *

They climbed to the base of the west wall in the last hour of true dark, Ivo and Sable alone, leaving Wren propped against her pack with the scattergun across her knees and instructions that amounted to *if you hear the mountain fall, walk north and don't look back*. Wren had gripped his wrist before he left — her grip still stronger than her legs, though the fever was weeks gone now and only the stiffness remained — and said, in her cartographer's flat way: "For the record, this is the worst-planned expedition I have ever been on, and I once mapped a swamp for a man who believed swamps were a government hoax." Then she'd squeezed his wrist once, hard, and let go.

The wall rose black against the less-black sky. Sable stopped him at the foot of it and touched three stones in sequence, quick as a signature. "Father's marks. Where you feel these, the line starts. Above the third one, reach left until you find the crack that takes your whole forearm. After that it's the route I showed you — chockstone, ledge, chimney, terrace. Say it back."

"Chockstone, ledge, chimney, terrace."

"And at the top, the retaining wall runs along the rim like a spine. You follow it south, counting the drain channels — there are five. The fifth one is the big one, the split. Behind the wall, the scree is packed like bread dough. You're looking for the keystone row — stones the size of your head, set in a herringbone. Pull the third and fourth from the south end, working *upward*, never sideways. Upward lets the face slump into the channel. Sideways brings the whole spine down on top of you." She caught his shoulder. "Ivo. Upward. Say it."

"Upward." He turned the marking stone over in his fingers. It was warm from her skin. "And if I pull wrong?"

"Then I'll hear it," she said, "and I'll come up after you, and we'll probably both be flattened, and it will have been a stupid beautiful idea, and I won't regret having it." Her mouth twitched, not quite a smile. "That's the family way. Ask my father. Oh — wait."

The joke landed like a hand on a wound, and she knew it, and he knew she knew it, and neither of them took it back. That was the strange new country the two of them had been walking through for days now: a place where the grief was spoken aloud and therefore, somehow, lighter to carry.

"Go," she said. "Moonset's in twenty minutes. Climb like a Corren."

"I don't know how to climb like a Corren."

"Liar. I've watched you for three hundred miles." She pushed him gently toward the wall. "You climb like a man reading a page he doesn't trust yet. Read faster."

* * *

The wall was colder than the night, and it talked to him.

Every hold had a voice — the click of solid stone, the scrape of a flake that might or might not hold, the deep dumb silence of the chockstones wedged by some ancient flood. He climbed the way she'd said, reading, testing, trusting each word of her instruction a half-second before his body believed it. At the third mark his fingers found the forearm-crack exactly where she'd promised, and something in his chest unclenched a single degree. Above him the stars wheeled. Below him the enemy fire was a dull ember-smudge, and once, drifting up, a snatch of laughter — hired men playing cards by coals, men being paid to be patient, men who believed the mountain was empty.

Halfway up, his boot skated.

It was nothing — a grain of sand, a smear of lichen, the kind of slip that happens a hundred times on any climb. His hands caught, his weight settled, his heart slammed twice and steadied. But in the half-second of the slip, the old cold poured up through him: Kestrel Gorge, the flooded gorge, the moment his arms had simply refused, the shame of hanging there while Sable's voice came up through the roar telling him *hand over hand, I've got you, look at me, not down.* He hung on the wall now in the dark and felt that same refusal gathering in his forearms like water behind a dam.

*Look at me, not down.*

There was no one to look at. That was the difference. That was the whole price of doing this alone — and also, he understood suddenly, clinging to warm stone sixty feet above sleeping enemies, the whole *point*. Some things a man had to carry up by himself or they were never truly his.

He breathed. Once. Twice. Found the mark-stone in his mind — her voice drawing the wall in the dirt, patient, exact, a map made of love — and climbed.

The rim took him like a threshold. He lay on it for a moment, cheek against stone, lungs working, and let the shaking finish. Then he rolled onto his stomach and began to move south along the retaining wall, low, slow, counting drain channels in the starlight. One. Two. Three — this one choked with old debris, exactly as she'd said. Four. Five.

The fifth channel opened at his elbow like a mouth.

Below it, forty feet down, the camp. Two tents sagging under bedrolls. A picket line with four horses, heads down. A cook fire burned to coals. And men — he counted shapes, counted again — eight of them, wrapped in blankets along the natural windbreak of the narrows, rifles stacked under a canvas lean-to like tools. One sentry sat against a boulder at the south end, awake, chin on chest, fighting sleep and losing by degrees.

Ivo lowered himself behind the wall and found the keystone row.

The herringbone was there under his fingers, exactly as drawn: head-sized stones set in overlapping courses, packed behind with scree that had been compacting for sixty years into something like masonry. He worked the third stone from the south end. It resisted, then gave with a grinding whisper that stopped his heart — but the night swallowed it, and the sentry's chin dipped lower, and Ivo knelt in the dark with a piece of a mountain loose in his hands and thought, absurdly, of the debt papers. Of how many years he had pulled, obediently, on stones other men had set. Of how no one had ever asked him whether the wall they formed was holding anything up worth keeping.

He set the marking stone on the rim where Sable would find it. Then he took hold of the fourth keystone, upward, as instructed, and pulled.

The mountain cleared its throat.

It began as a shudder under his knees, a deep subterranean grumble like a wagon crossing a bridge, and then the face of the chute let go all at once and the sound arrived — enormous, rolling, a landslide's long thunder tearing down the narrows. Ivo threw himself flat and hugged the wall as the scree roared past below him, splitting at the channel exactly as promised, two gray tongues of stone and dust fanning out across the camp, missing the tents by yards, missing the horses by feet, burying the cook fire in a hiss of steam and sparks. Dust boiled up and blotted the stars. Through it he heard screaming — human, terrified, alive — and the shriek of horses, and the crack of the canvas lean-to going over, and someone bellowing orders in a voice stripped of all its pay-grade authority: *the wall, the wall's come down, get clear, GET CLEAR—*

Then, cutting through it, a second sound. From the north ridge. A whistle — sharp, three notes, urgent — and an answering shout, and lantern-light blooming up there, men on the high ground trying to see down into the dust-choked camp, trying to understand what had just happened to their anvil-and-hammer plan.

Let them wonder, Ivo thought, flat on the rim with grit in his teeth and his heart going like a drum. Let them spend the whole night wondering.

He backed away from the edge, stood, and ran — south along the rim, then down the descent route Sable had drilled into him, chimney, ledge, chockstone, crack, the wall reading backward like a page learned by heart. The dust cloud was still rolling when he dropped the last ten feet onto the trail and Sable materialized out of the dark and caught his arm, steadying him, checking him with two swift hands the way you'd check a horse coming off bad ground.

"Well?" she said.

"Split clean. Both tongues. Nobody under it." He coughed dust. "Their north friends are awake and confused. Your father's wall just bought us a day."

For a moment she didn't say anything. In the gray beginnings of dawn, with the thunder still echoing off the far walls and the smell of stone-dust thick as fog, she looked at him — really looked, the way she'd looked at the horizon the night she woke him — and whatever passed across her face she didn't try to hide.

"My father used to say the land gives you one perfect throw per lifetime," she said. "You just spent yours."

"No." Ivo shook his head. Behind them, faint and fading, the hired men were shouting at each other in the settling dust, and above, on the north ridge, lanterns swung in useless arcs. Ahead, north, the trail climbed toward country no map recorded. "I spent yours. I just pulled the stones."

They walked back to where Wren waited with the scattergun and the packs, and the party gathered itself in the first true light and started up out of the pass at a pace that had no room for pride or exhaustion — only distance. By midmorning the pass was behind them, and the hired men's fury with it, sealed on the wrong side of a day's digging.

By noon they had crossed the last of the mapped terraces, and the trees began.
