## 27. Above the Trees

The trees began as a rumor.

For six days they had climbed through country that offered nothing to burn — wind-scoured stone, terraces the color of old bone, seeps so small Sable had to show them twice before anyone believed water lived there. Then, on the morning of the seventh day, Ivo crested a rise and smelled it before he saw it: green. Wet rot and pine pitch and cold earth, a smell so foreign after weeks of dust that it stopped him like a hand on the chest.

"Juniper first," Sable said beside him, reading his face. "Then pine. My father called this the green stair. He said you could climb out of the desert and into another world in half a day, if you knew where the stairs were."

Below them, threaded down the slope in a ragged line, came the rest of them: Wren Halloway walking with a stick she refused to call a crutch, her wounded thigh wrapped in strips of shirt; Marta and Odo, the two porters who had stayed when Voss went downriver and who had stopped asking where they were going somewhere around the second camp; and Sable, who moved through this country the way water moved through it — without argument, without hesitation, without any sign at all that she noticed difficulty.

Ivo had been a ranger for eleven years. He had crossed salt flats in August, tracked men through canyon mazes where the walls turned compasses into liars, survived a flash flood in the Narrows by clinging to a juniper root while the river tried to peel him off the earth like bark. He was not easily impressed by terrain.

But he had never come up out of the desert into trees.

They rose through the juniper first — squat, twisted things growing straight out of cracks, older than any town downstream. Then the pines started, sparse at first, then thickening, until the party walked in shade for the first time since the Rivergate. The ground softened underfoot. Needles. Duff. The dark, sweet smell of soil that had never once been dry.

Odo laughed. It was a strange sound, sudden and helpless, and everyone turned to look at him. He shrugged, embarrassed. "Grass," he said, pointing. "I forgot grass made a noise."

It did. A low whisper when the wind came through, moving down from the heights in long slow waves, bending the pines all together like something breathing.

"We should stop," Wren said quietly.

Ivo looked back at her. She was pale — she had been pale for days — but her eyes were bright, fixed upslope, and there was something in her expression he hadn't seen since the ambush at the ford. Want. Pure, uncomplicated want, the way a starving man looks at bread.

"There's no map of this," she said. "You understand? There is no map. Not mine, not the Survey's, not anything. Every map I've ever drawn ends at the timberline with a hachured edge and the word *unknown*. And here's the unknown, and it has *trees* in it."

"Then you'll be the first to draw it," Sable said.

Wren's mouth worked. She looked away, down at her stick, and Ivo saw her jaw tighten against something — grief or gratitude or both. Since the ford, since the fever, since the night she confessed what she'd done to the official maps, Wren had been carrying herself like a woman repaying a loan. Every mile she walked on that leg was a payment. Maybe this was the first installment she actually wanted to make.

They camped early, in a clearing beside a stream that fell off the rocks above in a thin bright ribbon. Real water, cold enough to hurt, running over stones the size of fists. Marta filled every container they had left — which, after the ambush, was not many — and Odo got a fire going with actual wood, actual kindling, and stood watching it burn with the satisfaction of a man restored to his profession.

They ate. Beans, mostly, and a brace of birds Sable had taken with a sling on the climb, because ammunition was too precious to spend on dinner. The light went gold, then gray, then blue among the trunks. Somewhere above them an owl called, once, twice, and got no answer.

That was when Ivo took out the papers.

He had carried them for eleven years. Folded in oilcloth, then wrapped in waxed canvas after the oilcloth wore through, kept in the inner pocket of his coat through heat and flood and one bad winter when he'd had to sleep in a snow cave and had woken with his beard frozen to the collar. He had carried them the way other men carried saints' medals — not because he believed in them, exactly, but because he could not imagine the shape of himself without them.

*Agreement of Debt.* His own signature at the bottom, age fourteen, in ink gone brown. Above it, in a clerk's careful hand: the sum owed to the family of Tomas Corren, deceased; the terms of service; the name of the guarantor — a name he had only lately learned belonged to the Survey's financier, the man whose hired guns were even now climbing the river behind them.

Sable watched him take it out. She didn't say anything. She had stopped saying anything about the papers three nights ago, when he'd told her the truth of what he'd seen at the source country's edge — the diverted channels, the men with shovels, her father wading into the current to break their dam, and the water taking him the way water takes everything, without malice and without mercy. Ivo had run. That was the part he'd never told anyone until that night. Fourteen years old, and he had run, and later men he never met had arrived with papers that turned his running into a debt, and his debt into a leash, and his leash into a career, and he had worn the whole arrangement like a coat because the alternative was standing naked in front of what he'd done.

"You don't have to," Sable said now, quietly.

"I know."

"That's why you should."

The fire cracked. Across the clearing, Wren was pretending to study her field notes, and failing. Odo and Marta had found somewhere else to look entirely. The owl called again, closer.

Ivo unwrapped the canvas. Unfolded the paper. Eleven years of sweat and river damp had softened it; the clerk's handwriting had faded to the color of tea. He read it once more, top to bottom, though he could have recited it. *In consideration of sums paid... the undersigned binds himself...*

Sums paid. By whom? For what? To buy the silence of a boy who had seen men stealing a river's beginning and done nothing but run. It had never been justice. It had been a purchase, and he had been the goods.

He held it over the fire.

His hand shook. He hated that it shook. It wasn't grief, exactly, and it wasn't fear. It was the feeling of stepping off a ledge in the dark when you've been told your whole life there's no floor below — the body refusing what the mind has already decided.

"Burn it or keep it," Sable said. "But choose it. That's the whole thing. Whatever you do, do it on purpose."

So he let go.

The paper caught at the corner, curled, flared. He watched the clerk's careful letters blacken and lift — the sum, the terms, the guarantor's name, his own fourteen-year-old signature — watched them curl into scrolls of orange and go up as sparks into the dark between the pines. It took less than a minute. Eleven years, and less than a minute.

He expected to feel free. That was the thing nobody warned you about: you did the brave thing and then you sat there waiting for the feeling, and the feeling didn't come. What came instead was a strange, weightless vertigo, like the moment after setting down a load so heavy you'd forgotten you were carrying it — your body still braced for a burden that wasn't there anymore.

"I don't feel different," he said. It came out almost accusatory.

Sable smiled, and there was no mockery in it. "You won't. Not tonight. Maybe not for a year." She fed a twig to the fire, watched it catch. "My father used to say a man isn't what he carries. He's what he does after he sets it down. You haven't done anything yet. That's tomorrow's work."

"And today's?"

"Today you burned a piece of paper." She looked at him across the flames, firelight moving in her eyes. "Eleven years late. But on purpose."

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, because Sable said the birds would tell them where the water went and the birds woke early.

She was right. As the gray light came up through the trunks, the forest filled with sound — thrushes somewhere, and smaller busy voices in the brush, and far overhead the thin crying of raptors riding the thermals off the high stone. Sable stood with her head tipped back, listening, one hand raised like a conductor.

"There." She pointed northeast, up a shoulder of granite where the pines grew stunted and bent. "Songbirds follow water. They won't nest far from it. And those—" she nodded at the raptors— "are working the updraft off the gorge. Which means there's a gorge. Which means the river's cutting deep again. We're close."

"How close?" Marta asked.

"To the source?" Sable lowered her arm. Her face did something complicated — hope and dread sharing the same small space. "Two days. Three if the climbing's bad. After the green stair my father always said you'd smell it before you heard it, and hear it before you saw it, and by then it would be too late to turn around anyway."

"Too late how?" Odo asked.

She glanced at Ivo. "Too late because you'll already have decided," she said. "Everyone decides at the source. That's what it's for."

They climbed.

It was hard country, harder than anything below the timberline, but it was honest hard — switchbacks through pine, then scree fields where each step rang hollow, then bare granite slabs where they moved hand over hand along ledges Sable's father had marked decades ago with small drilled holes and lead plugs, worn smooth as buttons now. Ivo found himself reading the old man's route the way he read tracks: here he'd hesitated, here he'd tested the rock and trusted it, here he'd chosen the long safe line over the short dangerous one because he'd been carrying a child on his shoulders. Because he'd been carrying Sable.

She didn't talk about him much. She didn't have to. The mountain was full of him.

At midday they crossed a saddle and dropped into a hanging valley, and there — impossibly, at this height — was a meadow. Grass to the knee, starred with some small white flower gone to seed, and a stream meandering through it without hurry, as if it had all the time in the world. Which, Ivo supposed, it did. This water had been falling for miles yet. It didn't know it was a river. Up here it was still just weather, just melt, just the mountain letting go of its snow a little at a time.

Wren sat down in the grass and wept.

Nobody made anything of it. Marta put a hand on her shoulder for a moment and then went to help Odo with the packs, and Ivo stood looking at the stream and understood completely. He had burned his papers last night and felt nothing, and this woman was sobbing into a meadow because of a bend in a creek, and it was the same thing. Some releases you couldn't feel until the land itself gave you permission.

They rested an hour. Sable walked the meadow's edge alone, and Ivo watched her stop at a granite boulder near the stream and lay her palm flat against it. When he came closer he saw why: marks cut into the stone, old and lichen-filled. A circle. A zigzag like water. A bird, maybe, or just three strokes.

"Family mark," she said without turning. "Every Corren for four generations signed the places they could reach. My father brought me here when I was six. He said, *this is as far as our name goes. Past here, nobody owns anything.*" She traced the circle with one finger. "I used to think that was sad. Running out of name."

"And now?"

"Now I think it might be the truest map there is." She turned and looked at him, and the afternoon light was behind her and her face was hard to read. "Past a certain point, Ivo, you can't carry a name up the mountain. You just climb."

* * *

The third day, the smell started.

It came faintly at first, mixed with pine — a mineral coldness underneath everything, the smell of water that has been underground, in the dark, in the rock, for a very long time. Scentless, almost, except that it wasn't; it was the absence of every other smell, a clean negative space the nose kept stumbling into. Ivo caught himself breathing deeper without deciding to.

And the sound. All that day, beneath the birdsong and the wind, ran a low sustained note, felt more than heard, coming up through boot soles and hand-palms whenever they touched stone. Like a vast organ pipe sounding somewhere under the mountain, one note, held forever.

"The river," Wren said, stopping on a ledge, her head cocked. "We're hearing the river inside the rock. Before it's a river at all." She pulled out her notebook, then stopped, pencil hovering. "There's nothing to write. How do you map a sound?"

"Start with where you're standing," Ivo said. "That's what maps are. Somebody standing somewhere, telling the truth about it."

She looked at him — a long look, the look of a woman recalibrating a man — and wrote it down.

Near dusk they topped a final ridge of broken granite and the land opened onto a high plateau of tundra and lingering snow, and there, across two miles of open ground, they saw the cairns.

Fresh ones. Dozens of them, marching in a deliberate line toward a black notch in the far wall of the plateau where the mountain gathered itself into a final wall. Each cairn was waist-high, stacked with care, and each was marked — Ivo could see it even at distance — with paint. White paint, in symbols he didn't recognize.

Sable went still beside him. The wind off the snow moved her hair and she didn't touch it.

"Those weren't here a month ago," she said. "My father's routes are south of this line. Nobody comes this way. Nobody *knew* this way."

"The survey team," Marta said.

"Some of them," Sable said slowly. "Moving fast, and organized, and marking their trail like they wanted to be followed." She frowned. "Or like they wanted whoever came next to know they'd passed. There's a difference."

Ivo crouched at the nearest cairn. The stones were fitted with a mason's care. The white symbol painted on the flattest face was precise, unhurried — a triangle inside a circle, and beneath it, smaller, a single letter.

Not a letter. An initial.

*C.*

"Cassian," Wren breathed behind him. "Emrys Cassian. The chief." Her voice had gone strange. "Ivo — Cassian doesn't leave signs. In nine years I never saw him mark a route. He believed maps invited people. He believed some places should stay unmarked." She was staring at the symbol as if it had spoken. "If he's painting cairns, either he's changed..."

"Or he wants company," Odo said.

"Or," Sable said quietly, "he wants witnesses."

They made camp in the lee of the last ridge, cold camp again — no fire this high, nothing to burn but scrub willow and Sable forbade the smoke. They ate cold beans and hard bread and watched the light die off the snowfields, turning them rose, then violet, then the same gray as the stone. The organ-note of the hidden water sounded beneath everything, patient, enormous.

Ivo took the first watch. He sat with his back against a boulder, rifle across his knees, and looked at the line of white-marked cairns fading into the dark, and at the black notch beyond them where the mountain opened like a door.

Tomorrow they would walk through it. Tomorrow they would find whatever remained of the Meridian Survey — and whatever Emrys Cassian had become, alone up here with his certainty and his paint. And behind them, three days down and closing, the financier's hired force climbed the river with orders that Wren's stolen documents had made plain: erase the expedition, seal the secret, sell the water.

He touched his coat pocket, out of habit. Empty. Just cloth, and the shape of nothing.

The owl called again — or something like an owl, too deep, too near the ground — and out on the plateau, at the very edge of what starlight could reach, something moved. One figure, upright, at the fourth cairn. Standing beside it the way a man stands beside a grave.

Ivo came up off the rock, rifle rising, and by the time his eye found the sight-line the figure was gone, and the plateau held nothing but snow and stones and the long held note of the water under the world.

"Sable," he said softly. "Wake up. Someone's leaving us messages."
