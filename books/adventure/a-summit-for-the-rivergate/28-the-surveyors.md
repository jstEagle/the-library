## 28. The Surveyors

The cairns began on the third morning above the timberline, and they were wrong.

Sable knew stone-stacking the way she knew her own heartbeat — her father had taught her before she could write, on the terraces below, how a cairn was a sentence in a language older than letters. *I passed here. The way holds. Walk on.* But these were not trail markers. They were too regular, too deliberate, each one built of flat stones fitted with an almost architectural care, and each one bore a single mark chiseled into its capstone: a circle with a line struck through it.

The null sign. The surveyor's mark for *nothing here. Nothing worth finding.*

"They're lying," Wren said, crouched beside the fourth cairn with her bandaged arm cradled against her chest. She touched the chiseled circle with two fingers, the way a doctor might touch a wound. "That's the standard notation for a dead end. We used it on the official maps all the time." Her voice had a feverish steadiness to it, the voice of a woman holding herself together with arithmetic. "But you don't build cairns to mark a dead end. You don't carry stones. You only build where you want someone to come back."

"They want to be found," Ivo said.

"No," Sable said. She had been reading the ground while the others read the stones, and the ground told a different story. Boot scuffs in the frost-softened earth, dozens of them, going both directions. A latrine trench dug and filled properly, military-fashion. The ash of a cook fire raked flat and scattered — but not scattered well enough to hide that it had been a big fire, a fire for ten or twelve people. "They want to be found by *someone*. And they want everyone else turned back."

She looked up the slope, where the canyon walls fell away into a hanging valley of grey stone and thin grass, and above it, half-lost in the haze of distance, the black seam in the cliff face that she had known about since she was six years old and had never once spoken of aloud.

Her father's mark was on the rock beside the seam. She had checked yesterday, when they camped, when the others were sleeping. A small carved spiral, worn shallow by forty years of weather. *The way holds. Walk on.*

He had walked on. And he had drowned for it.

"Two days," Ivo said beside her. He said it quietly, the way he'd been saying everything quietly since the cold camp, since the night the space between them had changed its shape and neither of them had named it. "Maybe less. If they're moving toward the source, and we're moving toward the source, we converge."

"Then let's converge," Sable said.

* * *

They found the first surveyor at midday, and he was dead.

Not murdered — Sable could read death well enough by now, after the ambush at the ford, after carrying Wren through the slots with her blood on their hands. This was simpler and sadder. A man in his fifties, lying in the lee of a boulder with his bedroll half-unrolled beside him, his boots off, his feet black with frostbite. He had simply lain down. His pack was still buckled. His canteen, when Ivo turned it over, was full.

He hadn't even drunk his water. He'd just decided to stop.

Wren crouched over him and went through his pockets with a gentleness that Sable hadn't expected from her, turning out a tin of tobacco, a folded photograph of a woman and two children in front of a frame house, a survey notebook with a cracked spine. She opened the notebook, read a page, and went very still.

"Read it," Ivo said.

Wren read it aloud, and her voice cracked in the middle, and she read it anyway.

*"Day 31. The chief has stopped eating with us. Halvard's faction sings at night — hymns, of all things, hymns in the dark like we're at a funeral. Merrick's faction counts money. I am not sure which frightens me more. The chief walks the perimeter alone and I saw him today standing at the mouth of the upper canyon for two hours without moving. He has the detonators. He has had them since the second week. Nobody talks about it. Nobody talks about anything anymore."*

Sable looked at the dead man's face. It was peaceful in a way that living faces almost never were, and she found she could not hate him, and could not pity him either. He was just the first one the mountain had finished with.

"Which one was he?" Ivo asked. "Halvard's or Merrick's?"

Wren closed the notebook. "Does it matter?"

It mattered, and they all knew it mattered, and none of them said why.

They buried him under stones — a proper cairn, Sable built it herself, with no mark at all, because he had been a man and not a message — and they took his spare rope and his rations because the dead do not need them and the living were down to hardtack and tea, and they walked on.

* * *

They found the rest of them at dusk.

The hanging valley narrowed to its head, and the black seam in the cliff widened into a canyon mouth — a gate of stone thirty feet across, breathing cold air outward, the air of deep places, the air Sable had been breathing in her dreams since the night her father went into the water. Below the gate, on a shelf of flat rock beside a spring that ran bright and thin over red stone, the surveyors had made their camp.

There were nine of them left. Sable counted twice. Tents pitched in two neat rows with a gap down the middle, like a border drawn through the camp, and on one side the men sat close around a fire talking low and fast, and on the other side they sat apart, each one alone with his thoughts, and between the two fires — because there were two fires, of course there were two fires — a tenth figure stood watching the trail.

He saw them before they saw him. Of course he did. He'd been watching for them, or for someone, for days.

"Ranger," he called, and his voice carried down the valley with strange clarity, the way sound carries in thin air at altitude. "Ranger Eastwick. You're late."

Sable felt Ivo go rigid beside her. Not the rigidity of fear — she had seen him afraid, on the climb, at the ford, and fear made him move *more*, made him quick and loud and alive. This was different. This was the rigidity of a man hearing his own name spoken by a voice he had hoped never to hear again.

"You know him," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Ivo Eastwick," the man on the shelf said again, and now he was walking down toward them, and Sable could see him properly: tall, gaunt in the way that men go gaunt when conviction eats more thoroughly than hunger, grey-bearded, with the sunken cheeks and burning eyes of someone who has stopped sleeping and started *deciding*. He wore a survey chief's badge on his collar, tarnished almost black. "Eleven years old, summer of the low water. The upper outflow channel. Yes. I know exactly who you are."

"Chief Aldous," Wren said, and her voice came out strange — half relief, half horror. "You're alive. All of you — the wire went silent, we thought —"

"You thought correctly that we stopped answering." Aldous had reached them now, and he looked at each of them in turn the way a man looks at inventory: Ivo, Wren, the two remaining porters, and last of all Sable, on whom his gaze lingered a moment too long and with an expression she couldn't read — recognition without warmth, like a man identifying a photograph. "And you thought correctly that we didn't want to be found. So the question, Ranger, is who sent you. Because there are only three kinds of people who come up this river, and I have prepared a different reception for each."

"He was conscripted," Wren said quickly. "Station chief's orders. Nine days of silence, Captain Voss organized —"

"Voss." Aldous smiled, and it was the worst thing Sable had seen all day, worse than the dead man with the full canteen. "And how is Anselm? Still drawing his beautiful maps? Still able to look at a living valley and see only acreage?"

"Voss turned back," Ivo said. His voice was level. Sable gave him credit for that. "At the second camp. Half the party went down with him. The rest of us came up because people were missing."

"People are not missing." Aldous turned and gestured up at the black gate of the canyon, and the last light of the sun caught his face and made it look carved, older than the stone behind it. "People are *assembled*. Every man who chose to keep walking is here. The ones who wanted to go home — Merrick's men, the ones who found a buyer — they left ten days ago, downriver, with the documents and the samples. They'll reach a telegraph office within the week. And the ones who stayed—" his voice dropped, and Sable heard in it something that frightened her more than any threat had, something like tenderness, "—the ones who stayed understand what's at stake."

"And what's at stake," Sable said, "is that you're going to blow it up."

Aldous looked at her again, and this time she read the expression. It was respect. Cold, grudging, and complete.

"The Corren girl," he said. "Of course. You'd know the approaches. Your father drew half the routes on these terraces." He said it without accusation, which was somehow worse. "Yes. We're going to seal it. The charge at the entrance, the charge at the upper channel, the old diversion works collapsed behind us. The river will find its way through the deep rock — it always has, it always will — but no one will ever stand where we're standing. No map. No road. No buyer. No *owner*." He said the last word the way other men said a curse.

"And the men downstream," Ivo said quietly, "the ones your patron sent to follow you. What happens to them?"

"Nothing I can control. That's the point, Ranger. That's the whole point. I can't control them. I can't control Merrick. I can't control the financier whose money bought this expedition and whose patience is, I assure you, at its end. I cannot control a single thing in this world except—" he spread his hands at the valley, the gate, the dark seam breathing cold air, "—except whether this place outlives its discoverers."

Behind him, on the far side of the camp, one of the men at the solitary fire stood up. He was young, hollow-cheeked, and he was looking at Sable's father's compass in her hand — she'd taken it out without thinking, the way she always did at altitude — and his face when he saw it was the face of a man seeing a ghost.

"That's Halvard's compass," he said, loud enough for the whole camp to hear. "That's the compass the old man carried. The one who went into the water."

The camp went silent. Both fires. All nine men. The border down the middle of the camp dissolved in a single moment, because grief, Sable thought, was the one thing they still shared.

Aldous watched her across the silence, and his gaunt face did something complicated — something that was almost apology and almost warning.

"You were a child," he said to Ivo. "You saw what happened at the outflow channel, and you were a child, and they gave your family a paper to make it go away. I know, because I drafted the terms of that paper. I sat at a table in Meridian City eleven years ago with a lawyer and a checkbook and a grieving widow, and I helped buy a boy's silence, and I have regretted it every day since, which is why I am standing here." He took a breath, and the breath shook, and Sable understood suddenly that this man was not a monster. He was something more dangerous: a good man who had run out of good options and knew it. "So ask me, Ranger. Ask me what you saw. Ask me who those men were and who sent them. I'll tell you everything. And then you can decide whether you're the instrument they built or the man you might have been."

The cold air breathed out of the canyon gate behind him, carrying the smell of deep stone and living water, and above the valley the first stars were coming out, and Sable stood with her father's compass in her hand and the needle swinging, swinging, swinging, trying to find a north that existed.

Ivo looked at Aldous. Then at her. Then at the gate.

"I already decided," he said. "Eleven years ago. I just haven't told anyone yet."
