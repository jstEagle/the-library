## 29. Arguments at the Threshold

The final canyon mouth opened before them like a door left ajar in a house of stone.

Ivo had seen a great many mouths in his years of ranging—the Rivergate itself, the Narrows, a hundred slot canyons that swallowed rivers whole—but nothing like this. The walls here rose sheer and pale, a thousand feet of limestone veined with mineral color, and between them the river emerged from a darkness so complete it seemed the stone had swallowed the sky. The water came out cold and green and utterly silent, as though it were being born already old.

The surveyors' high camp sat on a shelf of rock above the waterline: six tents gone gray with weather, a ring of stones, a signal mirror propped against a crate. Eleven people. Ivo counted them twice. Gaunt, bearded, sunburned to the color of the canyon walls, but alive—all of them alive, and that alone should have felt like victory. It did not. Their chief, Aldous Merrow, stood at the edge of the shelf with his hands clasped behind his back, watching Ivo the way a man watches weather he has already decided to walk into.

"You're Eastwick," Merrow said. Not a question.

"You know my name."

"I've known it a long time." Merrow's eyes were the pale gray of the limestone, and they did not blink enough. "You were a boy at Corren's Crossing the summer your father ran freight for the water board. You saw something at the edge of the source country. You signed a paper about it."

The words went through Ivo like cold water through a crack in stone. Behind him he heard Sable go still.

"We can talk about that later," Ivo said.

"No," Merrow said mildly. "I don't think we can. I think that paper is the whole of why you're here, though you may not know it yet. But yes. Later. First we should decide what to do about the water."

* * *

They gathered at dusk around a fire built small, the way men build fires when they do not want to be seen. The surveyors sat in a rough crescent, and Ivo marked how they had divided themselves—four on the left, five on the right, a gap between them wide enough to drive a boat through. Wren Halloway sat closest to the fire, her wounded leg propped on a crate, her instruments wrapped in oilcloth at her feet like a sleeping animal. Sable sat beside Ivo with her father's compass open in her palm, not looking at it, just holding it, the way a man holds a stone he means to throw.

Merrow stood to speak, because Merrow always stood to speak. Ivo had learned that much about him in the three hours since they'd climbed into camp.

"Three choices," Merrow said. "There have only ever been three, and pretending otherwise is how men waste the time they're given. We destroy it. We sell it. Or we reveal it." He held up fingers as he went, a surveyor's habit, counting the world into manageable pieces. "I'll state the cases plainly, and then we'll hear from everyone, because whatever we decide, we'll need every hand committed to it."

He turned to the fire.

"Destroy." The word dropped into the silence like a rock into a well. "The source is the heart of the river. Everything downstream—every farm, every town, every well from here to the delta—depends on the flow that comes out of that cavern. If the cavern is collapsed, sealed, made inaccessible, then no one can own it. No one can dam it, divert it, meter it, sell it. The river keeps its own counsel forever." He paused. "It would also mean the end of any chance to study it. And it would mean the flow itself might change. Collapsing a cathedral of living rock is not a precise science. We might kill the river. We might kill it slowly, over a generation, so that no one could ever prove it was us."

"And you'd take that risk," Wren said. It was not quite a question.

"I'd take any risk that keeps the water out of the hands of men like Hollis Vane." Merrow said the name the way other men spat. The financier. The man whose forged paper had bound Ivo for twenty years, whose hired crew was even now somewhere behind them on the river, climbing. "Vane's survey was never science. It was appraisal. He wanted to know what the source was worth, and now we know, and the knowing is a loaded gun. Destroy is the only choice that unloads it."

"And sell?" Ivo asked.

Merrow's mouth twisted. "Ask Dorn."

One of the surveyors on the right—a heavyset man with a beard gone prematurely white—lifted his chin. "I'll say it plain, since the chief won't. The source exists. That's a fact now, not a rumor. If we don't map it and register it, someone else will, and they won't be men with our scruples. Sell doesn't mean give it to Vane. Sell means put it in the public record under lawful claim, with the water board, with the territorial government, with whatever authority can hold it against the likes of him. Ownership as a shield. A deed is a wall, gentlemen. I've watched what happens to unclaimed water in this territory. It gets taken."

"By men with deeds," Sable said.

Dorn looked at her. "By men with deeds," he agreed. "Which is why we should hold one first."

"And the third choice," Ivo said.

"Reveal." Merrow said it flatly, without inflection, and Ivo understood that the chief had already dismissed it, had probably dismissed it weeks ago, alone, in the dark, with the same cold arithmetic he applied to everything. "Reveal means we publish. Maps, coordinates, testimony, everything. We tell the world where its river is born and we trust the world to do right by it." He turned and looked at Ivo directly, and there was something in his face that was not quite contempt—something wearier and sadder. "That's your position, I believe, Eastwick. So tell us. Convince us. You've walked farther than any of us to be here, and you've got the least to gain. Speak."

* * *

Ivo stood. His legs ached from the climb, and the fire was hot on one side of his face and the canyon cold on the other, and eleven strangers and two friends watched him, and for a moment he could not remember a single word.

He thought of the salt flats, in the beginning. The way the emptiness had felt like a kind of honesty. He started there.

"I've spent twenty years working for a paper," he said. "A debt. I carried it in my chest pocket next to my compass, and I never once questioned it, because questioning it would have meant admitting I didn't know who I was without it. A man came to me when I was nine years old and put that paper in my hand and told me it was the price of something I'd seen. And I paid it. Every month, every year, out of a ranger's wage, into an account I never saw, for a family I never met." He glanced at Sable. She was watching him with an expression he couldn't read, and he made himself go on. "That paper was forged. I learned that a few weeks ago, in a side canyon, from the daughter of the man I thought I owed. The debt was never real. But the paying of it made me. Twenty years of my life were shaped by a lie, and the shape it made was a man who does what he's told and calls it duty."

He let that sit. The fire cracked. Somewhere below them the river ran on in its silence.

"So when I tell you I know what a secret does to a man, I'm not guessing. A secret is a debt you didn't sign. It compounds. It owns you a little more every year, and it never tells you the interest rate. Merrow—" he turned to the chief— "you've been up here nine weeks. You've kept this camp dark, kept your radio silent, kept eleven men and women in the stone. You think you're protecting the source. But ask yourself what the secret has already made of you. You're standing at the birthplace of the river talking about killing it. That's not what you came here to do. That's what the dark does."

"Careful," Merrow said quietly.

"No," Ivo said. "You asked me to speak, so I'm going to finish. Destroy is fear wearing the mask of principle. It's a man so afraid of losing the river that he'd cut out its heart to keep it safe. And if the collapse goes wrong—and you said it yourself, it might—then you haven't saved the river. You've murdered it and called it mercy, and there won't be a soul alive who can hold you to it, because you'll have made sure no one can ever see the body." He shook his head. "I've buried men in this country. The ones you can't find are the ones that haunt you."

Dorn shifted. "And reveal? You'd hand the coordinates to the world. To Vane. He reads a map, Eastwick. He's already got men on the river."

"Vane has men on the river *because* of the secret," Ivo said. "He's climbing up here in the dark to take something he thinks no one else knows the value of. You want to know what beats a man like that? Light. You drag the thing into the open where every eye in the territory can see it, and suddenly it isn't a prize, it's a *responsibility*. A secret can be stolen. A public fact cannot. Let the towns downstream know where their water comes from. Let them see it. Let them decide. Yes, some of them will be fools. Yes, some of them will be greedy. But they'll be *our* fools, all of us together, arguing in the daylight, instead of eleven exhausted people in a canyon deciding the fate of a river for everyone who ever drinks from it." He spread his hands. "I'm a ranger. I've enforced other people's laws for twenty years, and half of them were written by men who never saw the country they were legislating. I'm done with that. The world should see the source. The world can bear it. The world has borne worse than the truth."

Merrow was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was gentle, and the gentleness was worse than anger.

"You're a romantic, Eastwick. A decent one, which is the dangerous kind." He crouched by the fire, and the light carved deep lines in his face. "I've spent thirty years watching territories argue in daylight. Do you know what they argue about? Not rivers. Leases. Easements. Apportionment schedules. The daylight you're so fond of is where the lawyers live, and the lawyers work for men like Vane. Revelation doesn't end the fight—it just moves it to a field where Vane has a hundred men and we have none. You'd trade a secret we can defend for a publicity we can't. That's not courage. That's naivety with a clean conscience."

Murmurs on the right side of the fire. Dorn nodding. Ivo felt the argument slipping, felt the whole crescent of faces tilting away from him toward the cold certainty of Merrow's arithmetic, and he understood suddenly how a man could stand at the source of a river and still be drowning.

Then Sable stood up.

She didn't raise her voice. She never did. She held the compass out flat on her palm, and the firelight caught the brass, and she waited until the murmuring stopped.

"My father died at the edge of this country," she said. "Twenty years ago. He saw men diverting the outflow—Vane's men, though we didn't know the name then—and he went into the water to stop them, and the water took him. And for twenty years the official record said he was a thief. That he died stealing water. My mother lived under that name until it killed her too, and I grew up carrying it, and I believed it, because a paper said so and a paper is a paper." She closed her hand over the compass. "The paper was a lie. The record was a lie. Every daylight fact I was ever given about my father was purchased by the man who killed him." She looked at Merrow. "You say the daylight belongs to the lawyers. I say the dark belongs to the men who write the lies. My father died in the dark, and the dark kept him a thief for twenty years. I'm not giving this river to the dark. I'm not giving it to a deed or a demolition charge either. I'm giving it to the light, and if the light is imperfect—if the lawyers and the fools get hold of it—then at least the fight happens where my father's name can be cleared in front of witnesses. That's what revelation is. It's not naivety. It's a headstone that tells the truth."

The silence after that was total. Even the river seemed to hold its breath.

Ivo looked at her and felt something in his chest come loose—not the old debt, that was already ash, but something adjacent to it, something he'd carried even longer: the belief that he would always stand alone in these arguments, the lone ranger with his hollow compass, competent and unaccompanied. She stood beside him without standing beside him, and it undid him more than Merrow's contempt ever could.

Merrow looked at Sable for a long time. Then at Ivo. Then at the fire.

"Reveal," he said at last, "requires the world to be able to hear us. Our radio died six weeks ago. The nearest wire is at the Rivergate station, four hundred miles downriver. And Vane's crew is somewhere on that river tonight, climbing toward us, with more guns than we have and a foreman who doesn't argue at fires. So whatever we choose, we choose it knowing we may have to hold this canyon mouth long enough to make it stick." He straightened. "I'll sleep on it. We vote at first light. Until then, Eastwick, your people take the east shelf. We'll post watches."

He walked away into the dark, and the crescent broke apart into murmuring clusters, and the council was over without being decided.

* * *

Later, at the edge of the east shelf, Ivo stood watch while the camp slept. Sable came and stood beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, and for a while neither of them spoke.

"He'll vote destroy," Sable said finally.

"Probably."

"And Dorn's people will vote sell, and it'll come down to Wren and the undecideds."

"Wren will vote with us," Ivo said. "She didn't say anything tonight, but she had her hand on her instruments the whole time Merrow was talking. She's been trying to repair what she erased for twenty years. Revelation is her penance."

"And the others?"

Ivo didn't answer right away. He was watching the dark canyon below, where the river came out of its cathedral and ran silver under the stars. He had learned, in twenty years of ranging, to read country at night—to notice when a stone was wrong, when a shadow had weight, when the silence had a shape in it. And the silence down there had a shape.

"Sable," he said quietly. "Count the tents."

She counted. He watched her do the arithmetic in the dark, watched her shoulders stiffen.

"Eleven surveyors," she said. "We counted eleven at the fire."

"Nine at the fire," Ivo said. "Merrow, Dorn, and seven others. Two of them never sat down. And there's a light in the lower rocks—there was, twenty minutes ago. A shuttered lantern, moving toward the river."

Sable turned to him, and her face in the starlight was very still.

"Somebody's going down to the water," Ivo said. "Somebody's going down to signal."
