## 30. Betrayal

Wren Halloway had never trusted a night she couldn't map.

The council at the canyon mouth had ended near dusk with no decision at all — the survey chief retreating into his tent with his demolition charges and his certainty, Ivo Eastwick standing a long time at the threshold of the final gorge as if he could argue with stone. Now the party was camped in a hollow above the last defensible pass, nine people and one wrecked cartographer, and Wren lay on her bedroll doing what she always did when her body failed her: she drew.

She had no instruments left. They were buried two days back under a cairn in waterless country, wrapped in oilcloth like a coffin, and she had wept over them then and was not ashamed of it now. So she drew in her head instead — the way she'd learned as an apprentice, before anyone handed her a ruling pen. The gorge mouth to the east. The terraces falling away south. The river, somewhere below them all, running thin and cold through its last narrows toward the towns that drank it without knowing where it came from.

Twenty-two days of dry season left, by her arithmetic. Maybe less. The shallows would strand them above the lower rapids soon enough, and after that there would be no going down but by foot, and no going down by foot with Wren Halloway's knee unless someone carried her the whole way, which they had already been doing for six days and which she hated more than she could say.

She heard the camp settle. Sable Corren took first watch — Wren knew her footsteps now, light and quick, a woman who moved like water finding its level. Ivo rolled out his blanket near the fire ring but didn't sleep; she could tell by his breathing, the careful rhythm of a man who had taught himself to rest without ever quite closing his eyes. The surveyors murmured among themselves in the low, clipped cadence they used when they didn't want to be understood. Their chief said nothing at all. He hadn't said much since the confrontation at the cavern mouth, and Wren found his silence worse than his sermons.

She slept eventually, the way the injured do — in shallow pieces, surfacing and sinking.

* * *

She surfaced once, deep in the black hours, because the camp felt wrong.

Not loud. Not alarmed. Just wrong — the way a contour line feels wrong when it doesn't close. She lay still and counted breaths. Ivo's, steady. Sable's, absent — out on watch, fine. The porters', slow. The surveyors'—

Two sets missing.

She told herself it was nothing. Men walked at night; men pissed at night; men who had spent weeks in the high country paced when they couldn't sleep. She told herself this while her hand found the little knife she kept under her bedroll, the one thing she'd refused to bury with her instruments.

Then she heard it, faint and far off, from the direction of the southern rim: a whistle. Two notes, low, then silence. And after a long moment, two notes answered — from below. From far, far below, down where no member of their party had any reason to be.

Wren sat up so fast her knee screamed and she bit the scream back behind her teeth.

The rim. The southern rim, where the terraces dropped away in broken steps toward the river's middle course. Someone was down there. Someone had been climbing the river all along, or waiting for word, or both — and two notes of a whistle meant the word had just been given.

She crawled. That was the indignity of it, the thing she would remember afterward with a hot face: she crawled across the camp on her hands and one good leg, dragging the bad one, past sleeping bodies, to where Ivo lay. She put her hand on his shoulder and he came awake instantly, completely, the way he did — no groggy blur, just open eyes catching the starlight.

"Two of them are gone," she whispered. "The rim. Listen."

They listened together. The wind moved through the gorge mouth with its usual hollow voice. No second whistle came. But the absence had a shape now, and the shape was a door standing open.

Ivo rose without a sound and went to the surveyors' side of the camp. Wren watched him count bedrolls by touch, then stand very still over the two empty ones. When he turned back to her, his face was unreadable in the dark, but his voice, when it came, was flat as a salt pan.

"Dorn. And the young one — Pell." He named them the way you name dead men. "Dorn argued loudest for selling the source. Pell never argued at all."

"That's the kind that walks," Wren said.

Sable materialized beside them out of the dark, silent as her own name. She'd already been to the rim path — Wren saw the dust on her knees, the rock in her hand. "Their tracks go south," Sable said quietly. "Down the terrace stairs. Fresh. An hour old, maybe less." She paused. "There's a signal ledge out there. My father used it. You can see forty miles of river country from it, and be seen from forty miles. If they reached it before we did—"

"They're not reaching anything," Ivo said. "Wake Voss's porters. Wake everyone. We move on the rim in ten minutes."

But even as he said it, Wren saw him do the arithmetic she was doing — the same terrible arithmetic that had governed every day since the dry season began. The rim path was three hours' hard travel for healthy legs. For hers, carried, it was five. The deserters had an hour's start and downhill all the way. There was no catching them. There was only arriving after, and reading the ground, and knowing.

Hope punctured, she thought. That's what this is. She'd read the phrase somewhere years ago, in some explorer's journal, and thought it melodramatic. It wasn't. It was precise. It was exactly the sensation of a needle going through something that had been holding air.

* * *

They didn't catch them. Of course they didn't.

By grey dawn the party stood on the signal ledge — a shelf of pale stone thrust out over four hundred feet of emptiness, marked with old Corren family signs that Sable touched as she passed them like a woman greeting relatives. From the ledge, the whole middle river unrolled below: the silver thread of it, the terraces stepping down in ochre and rust, the dark seam of the gorge they'd climbed. And far to the south, maybe eight miles as the hawk flew, at a bend where the river widened into gravel bars—

Flares.

Three of them, rising in sequence, red and obscene against the pale morning sky. They bloomed, hung, and died, and their smoke drifted up the canyon in thin gray question marks. Then, after a wait that Wren counted second by second, three more answered from farther south. And then — this was the part that stopped everyone's breath — a fourth answer, from due west. From the rim country beyond the river entirely, where nothing should have been.

"They've been climbing the whole time," Ivo said. His voice was quiet, and that made it worse. "Since the ford. Since before the ford. All those days we watched our backs for one rider—" He stopped. Shook his head once, like a man clearing water from his ears. "It was never one rider. It was an army moving slow."

Wren lowered herself carefully onto a boulder and got out the one thing she still owned: her field notebook, half-filled, its cover soft with sweat. Her hands shook — from the knee, from the cold morning, from the thing in her chest she refused to call fear because naming it would make it operational. She began to sketch. It was what she did. It was the only prayer she knew.

"The signal ledge sees the bend," she said, talking to keep her mind working. "Dorn and Pell signaled from here last night — that's why they came, that's why the ledge matters. But the answering flares came from the bend itself, which means the main force is already at the bend, which means—" her pencil stopped. She looked up. "Which means they've been climbing faster than us. On the river. With boats, probably, below the rapids, portaging where they had to. While we buried instruments and hauled me and argued about the soul of the source, they just *moved*."

"How many?" Sable asked. She stood at the lip of the ledge with the wind pulling at her hair, and Wren saw her do something she'd seen her do before: read the land the way other people read letters. "Count the camps," Sable said. "Smoke. See the smudges along the west rim? Three fires, spaced like a picket line. That's a company, not a crew. Fifteen men minimum. Twenty if they're being careful." A pause. "They're being careful. They think we don't know yet."

"We have maybe a day," Ivo said. "A day and a half before they close the pass below us and we're sealed in this country with the chief and his charges and no way down."

"Then we don't go down," Sable said. "We go up. We climb the final gorge today, now, ahead of them, and we reach the springs first, and what happens there happens with us standing at the door instead of them."

Behind them, boots on stone. The survey chief had come up from the camp, moving with that gaunt, unhurried certainty of his, two of his remaining loyalists flanking him. He looked at the dying smoke of the flares for a long moment, and Wren watched his face and could not read it at all — grief, satisfaction, vindication, all wearing the same mask.

"So," the chief said. "My colleagues have chosen their patron. I wondered which way Dorn would break." He turned to Ivo. "You understand what this means. If the financier's force takes the source, it takes everything — the water, the sale, the future of every town downstream. If I take it, at least it dies free." He said it calmly, reasonably, the way men say monstrous things when they've lived with them long enough.

"You're both wrong," Wren said.

Everyone looked at her. She hadn't meant to speak. Her knee throbbed and her notebook was open on her thigh and the words came out of her the way water comes out of a cracked wall — all at once, under pressure.

"You're both wrong, and I can prove it, because I'm the one who drew the lies." Her voice cracked and she let it. "I altered the official maps. Years ago. Under orders from Voss, who took them from the financier's people. Every approach to the source, erased. Every route your father cut, Corren—" she nodded at Sable, "—gone from the record, so that the only people who could ever reach the springs were the ones who paid for the erasing. That was the plan from the beginning. Not damming it. Not destroying it. *Owning* it so completely that no one else could even find it." She closed the notebook. Her hands had gone still now, finally. "And if you blow the entrance, chief, you're finishing their job for them. Sealed is sealed, whether the seal is stone or a deed."

The silence on the ledge went on so long that the wind seemed loud.

It was Ivo who broke it, and Wren heard something in his voice she hadn't heard before — not the tracker's flatness, not the ranger's duty. Something that had been forged slowly, over forty chapters of country, and had come out the other side as conviction.

"Then we go up," he said. "All of us. Today. And when the financier's men arrive, they'll find the source mapped — properly mapped, by her—" he pointed at Wren, and Wren felt her face go hot, "—and witnessed, and *known*. You can't sell a thing the whole world has seen. You can't destroy a thing the whole world is watching. That's the only defense there is. Light."

"The chief calls that naive," one of the loyalists muttered.

"The chief called a lot of things naive," Sable said, "right up until his own men whistled to the enemy from my father's ledge." She stepped away from the lip and faced them all, and Wren saw that her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely steady. "We climb. Now. Before that smoke finishes drifting. Anyone who wants to stay and negotiate with twenty hired men can stay."

* * *

They broke camp in twenty minutes, which Wren noted with grim professional approval was the fastest she'd ever seen a party strike — fear, properly administered, was better than any drill sergeant. The porters took the loads. The chief's loyalists took the charges, because the chief would not surrender them and no one had the strength left to fight that battle too. Ivo carried Wren.

He had carried her for six days and had never once made her feel like cargo, and she had decided, privately, that if she survived this she would draw him — really draw him, with proper instruments, the way he deserved — and give him the drawing and never explain it. For now she rode his shoulders and his grip on her ankle was iron and she held her notebook against the back of his neck and tried to memorize the gorge as they entered it, because that was her work, that was her penance, that was the only repair she had to offer.

The final gorge swallowed them in shadow. The walls leaned together overhead until the sky was a bright ragged ribbon, and the river — the true river, the young river, barely a day old — ran fast and clear over red stone, singing. Somewhere above, Wren knew, it was born. Somewhere above, in a cathedral cavern no honest map had ever recorded, the whole weight of it rose out of living rock.

She was still trying to fix the gorge's bearing in her memory when Ivo stopped.

He stopped so suddenly that she nearly lost her hold, and then she felt him go rigid beneath her, every muscle turning to cable, and she followed his gaze up the ribbon of sky.

On the rim above them — the eastern rim, the high shoulder of the final gorge, silhouetted against the morning — stood a line of men. Small with distance, motionless, looking down into the gorge like spectators at a theater. One of them raised something to his face. Glass, Wren realized. He was studying them through glass, unhurried, cataloguing.

They weren't racing for the springs anymore.

They were already there. Waiting above the only door.

Ivo set her down gently on a stone, and when he spoke, his voice was almost a whisper, and it carried anyway, the way truth does in narrow places.

"They beat us to the top."
