## 31. The Race for the Springs

Wren Halloway woke to the sound of Ivo breaking camp in the dark, and understood before her eyes opened that something had changed.

The flares had come at midnight — three of them, far below on the river terraces, blooming red against black stone like wounds. No radio to answer them with; the radio had died at the ford along with half their supplies and a good measure of Wren's own blood. But nobody needed a radio to read three flares climbing the gorge in sequence. That was a signal. That was *we are coming*.

She sat up too fast and the world tilted, the canyon walls smearing into gray ribbons. Her leg had gone from wound to burden over the past six days, the thigh swollen tight under its wrapping, the skin around it hot and wrong-colored. She'd stopped looking at it. Looking accomplished nothing.

"Easy." Sable Corren crouched beside her, pressing a waterskin into her hands. "Drink. All of it."

"How far?"

"To the springs?" Sable glanced toward the head of the gorge, where the last of the stars still hung between walls so sheer they seemed cut with a blade. "Half a day's climb. Maybe less if the route holds."

"And them?"

Sable didn't answer immediately, and Wren had learned enough about her by now to know what the silence meant. The hired force wasn't half a day behind. It might not even be a quarter. They'd been climbing all night, moving light, and they knew this gorge because Voss's original survey had mapped it — mapped it from below, years ago, before anyone thought to guard the way up.

"Then we don't stop," Wren said.

Ivo Eastwick came out of the dark with his pack already shouldered, his face a pale shape above the coals he was drowning. He looked at her leg the way he always did now — quickly, guiltily, as though the wound were an accusation he hadn't figured out how to answer.

"I can carry you partway," he said.

"You cannot carry me up a gorge."

"Partway."

"No." She said it flatly, and pushed herself upright, and hated the sound her breath made doing it. "Listen to me, both of you. I've spent eleven years drawing maps that lie. I erased the approaches to this place with my own hand — I sat at a drafting table in Meridian House and inked blank space where there was country, because a man paid me to and I told myself it was just lines on paper." Her voice cracked and she let it. "It was never just lines on paper. Everything that's happened — the survey going silent, the men with guns, your father's name dragged through the mud, Ivo's whole life bought and sold on forged paper — every bit of it grew out of those blank spaces. So when we reach the source, someone is going to map it. Properly. Honestly. Every channel, every chamber, every approach. And that someone is me, and I am walking there on my own feet or crawling on my hands, but I am *coming*, and neither of you will waste time carrying me when you could be climbing."

The fire hissed out under Ivo's boot. For a moment nobody spoke, and the gorge held its silence around them like water holds a stone.

"All right," Ivo said finally. "But you climb behind me, and if you fall, I catch you, and we lose whatever time catching costs. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Wren said.

Sable said nothing. She was looking at Wren with an expression Wren couldn't parse in the dark — something old and tired and almost tender — and then she turned and began folding the tarp, and the moment passed.

* * *

They reached the mouth of the final gorge an hour after first light, and Wren forgot her leg for nearly a full minute.

The canyon narrowed to a slot no wider than a wagon road, then began to climb — not gradually, but in great stepped pitches where the river's ancient course had cut down through the rock in stages, leaving staircases of stone hundreds of feet tall. Water threaded down through all of it, silver threads stitching terrace to terrace, and the air itself changed: cooler, wetter, smelling of wet stone and something green. Actual green. On a ledge fifty feet up, a stunted juniper clung sideways out of a crack, the first tree Wren had seen in nine days.

"There." Sable pointed up the slot to where the morning light died against a wall of rock. "My father called it the Ladder. Seven pitches. The third one is the bad one — the holds are far apart and the rock is rotten. After that it eases until the last, and the last is just long."

"The hired men," Ivo said. He'd climbed onto a boulder and stood scanning back down the gorge the way they'd come, one hand shading his eyes. "How long do we have?"

Sable considered. "If they kept moving through the night? We're ahead of them by two hours. Maybe three. But they're fresh and we aren't, and the third pitch will slow us badly."

"Then we don't take breaks we don't need." Ivo dropped off the boulder and came to Wren, and without asking took her pack off her shoulders and added it to his own. She started to protest and he looked at her — that level ranger's look that wasn't unkind but wasn't negotiable either — and she closed her mouth. There were economies worth fighting for and economies that weren't. Her pride, she was learning, fell in the second category more often than she liked.

They climbed.

The first two pitches were brutal but honest — steep scrambles up broken rock where every hold announced itself. Wren went slowly, testing each placement, favoring her good leg, hauling herself up by her arms whenever the thigh refused. Sweat ran into her eyes and she blinked it away and kept counting. She counted everything now: steps, holds, breaths. Counting kept the pain at arm's length. Counting was what cartographers did instead of praying.

Between the second and third pitch they paused at a shelf barely wide enough for the four of them — the fourth being Tam Oduya, the quiet porter who had stayed when the others quit at the Narrows, and who had become the load-bearing wall of the entire expedition without ever seeming to raise his voice. He handed out dried fruit and said nothing, which was its own kind of kindness.

Wren looked down.

The gorge fell away beneath them in a series of sunlit steps, and far below — so far it seemed a trick of scale — she saw movement. Figures. Small as ants, strung out along the lower terraces, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who knew exactly where they were going.

"They're in the gorge already," she said.

Ivo was beside her instantly, following her gaze. A long silence.

"That's not two hours," he said quietly. "That's forty minutes. Less."

"They must have found the survey's old fixed ropes," Sable said. Her face had gone very still. "Voss's crew left lines on the easy pitches when they mapped from below. If the financier's men recovered them—"

"Then the easy pitches cost them nothing." Ivo turned to Wren, and she watched him make the calculation she was already making, the arithmetic of bodies and distance and the angle of the sun. "Can you move faster?"

"No," Wren said. "And I won't pretend otherwise. Go ahead, the three of you. I'll follow as far as I can and—"

"Finish that sentence and I'll leave my boot print somewhere memorable," Sable said, without heat, the way you'd remark on the weather. "Nobody is staying behind. Nobody is being left. My father lost his life in this country because men treated other men as expendable, and I have walked eleven years in the shape of that hole, and I will not become it. So save your breath for the third pitch, cartographer, because that's where you'll need it."

Wren stared at her. Then, absurdly, she felt her eyes sting, and she blamed the wind off the heights, and ate her dried fruit, and got up.

* * *

The third pitch was worse than advertised.

The rock was indeed rotten — a crumbling band of shale interleaved with harder stone, so that holds dissolved under the fingers like stale bread. Sable led it with the sureness of inheritance, calling back the sequence in her father's terms: *the shelf, the chimney, the step-left where the white vein runs.* Ivo followed, then guided Wren, his hands steady under her elbows at every bad move, never hurrying her, never touching more than necessary. Tam brought up the rear with the rope coil and most of the remaining weight.

Two-thirds of the way up, Wren's good foot skated on a slick of wet shale and for one lurching second she hung by her fingertips over a drop that went down and down and down, the gorge spinning, her whole life suddenly very small and very loud in her ears.

Then Ivo's hand closed on her wrist.

"Not today," he said, and hauled her onto the ledge, and held her there while her legs remembered how to be legs.

She wanted to say thank you. What came out instead was: "The white vein. It continues past the chimney — see it? That means the strata continue, which means the pitch above should be limestone again, solid. Your father's route is sound, Sable. Structurally it's *beautiful*."

Above her, Sable laughed — short, startled, real. "You just nearly died and you're reviewing the geology."

"It's how I pray," Wren said, and heard Ivo huff something that was almost a laugh too, and climbed on.

* * *

They topped the seventh pitch in early afternoon, wrung out and staggering, into a country that stopped Wren's breath a second time.

Above the last gorge the land opened into high hanging valleys — grass, actual grass, silver-green and wind-combed, dotted with pools fed by springs that seeped from everywhere and nowhere. Birds moved through air that smelled of water. In the middle distance, the ground rose toward a wall of black rock split by a canyon mouth, and from that mouth came a sound like distant thunder that never ended.

The source. Or the door to it.

"We're close," Sable said softly. "That sound — that's the river being born."

Wren sank down onto the grass, her leg screaming, her hands shaking, and did the only thing she knew how to do with wonder: she took out her notebook. The little waterproof book had survived the ford, the ambush, the fever, everything. She flipped past the altered maps, past the official lies, to clean pages, and began to sketch the valley freehand — the spring lines, the drainage pattern, the bearing of the black wall. Rough. Honest. The first true map she'd drawn in eleven years.

Her hand stopped.

Because there, pressed into the turf ahead of them, was a trail — fresh boot prints, many sets, heading toward the thunder. And among them, unmistakably, the paired parallel scars of a sledge runner. Heavy cargo, hauled up seven pitches of gorge. Which meant the surveyors' chief had gotten his demolition charges this far.

Which meant whatever waited at the canyon mouth, they would not be the first to arrive.

"Company," Tam said quietly, from the rise behind them. "Coming up the gorge. Fast."

Wren looked at Ivo. Ivo looked at Sable. And Sable Corren, starving and hunted and eleven years angry, smiled like a woman reading the last page of her father's letter.

"Then we run," she said.
