## 32. The Source

The last gorge took them a full day and most of another.

It was not climbing in any way Ivo had known before — no route, no switchbacks, nothing a mule or even a sure-footed ranger would call a trail. It was Sable's father's country, and she moved through it like a woman reading a letter written to her alone: a crack here wide enough for fingers, a ledge there that looked like nothing and held everything, a chimney of stone where you pressed your back against one wall and walked your feet up the other until the sky opened above you like a hand letting go.

Wren came last on every pitch, roped between Ivo and the surveyor named Aldous, who had stopped speaking somewhere below the timberline and now communicated only in grunts and outstretched hands. Her fever had broken in the night, but what remained was worse in its way — a clarity that hurt to look at. She climbed with her ruined leg strapped tight and her map case slung across her chest like a child, and when the rock got steep she said, quietly, over and over, *I have to see it. I have to see it with my own eyes,* as if someone had argued.

Behind them, far below, the financier's force was coming up the river. They had seen no flares since the pass, but absence was its own message. The hired men were patient. They were paid to be.

"Rest here," Sable said near midday of the second day, at a shelf where water seeped from the living rock in a thread thin as wire. They drank it one at a time, cupping hands, taking only what the stone offered. Above them the gorge narrowed to a slot no wider than a doorway, and through it came a sound.

Ivo had been hearing it for hours without naming it. A sound beneath the wind, beneath the birdsong that had guided them up from the trees — a deep, continuous note, less heard than felt, the way you feel your own pulse when everything else goes still.

"What is that?" Aldous asked. His first words in a day.

Sable didn't answer. She was looking up the slot with an expression Ivo had never seen on her face — not the hard competence he knew, not the grief, not the fury. Something older. She looked like a girl standing outside a house she'd been told burned down years ago.

"That," she said finally, "is my father's river."

They climbed into the slot single file, shoulders scraping both walls, and the sound grew until it filled the stone around them, until Ivo could feel it in his teeth, in the soles of his boots, in the hollow behind his sternum where the debt papers used to sit. The light changed. It came from ahead and below, green-gold and moving, reflected up through the slot like light through the bottom of a door.

Then the walls fell away, and Ivo Eastwick stood at the source of the Rivergate, and forgot, for a long moment, how to breathe.

* * *

It was a cavern. That word was all his language offered him, and it was a beggar's word for what lay before him.

The chamber was vast beyond measuring — a cathedral of living rock sunk into the heart of the plateau, its ceiling lost in a haze of moisture and dim gold light that seemed to come from nowhere and everywhere, from veins of pale mineral glowing faintly in the stone like embers banked under ash. Pillars rose from floor to roof, some slender as organ pipes, some massive as watchtowers, carved by ten thousand years of mineral-laden water into flutes and folds and faces that might have been deliberate, might have been accident, and did not matter which.

And from the floor of it — from a hundred places in the floor, from fissures and springs and weeping seams in the rock — the river was born.

Not a trickle. Not a stream. Water rose out of the earth itself in broad, silent boils, clear as glass, welling up through vents in the stone floor and spilling over rims of pale travertine the color of bone, joining, gathering, braiding together across the cavern floor into channels, then rivers, then a single broad current that slid away into a dark throat of rock at the chamber's far end — the beginning of the Rivergate, the water that would run four hundred miles to the sea, that fed the station towns and the salt flats and every dry season argument ever held about it, all of it starting here, in silence, in this room under the world.

There was no roar. That was the thing that undid him. A river this size should have thundered. But the springs rose without violence, without hurry, filling the cathedral the way light fills it, and the only sound was that deep sustained note — the voice of the whole plateau breathing water — and the soft endless whisper of overflow finding its way to the dark exit.

Ivo became aware, distantly, that he was crying. He let it happen. There was no one to perform for.

* * *

They came out onto a natural terrace above the springs, and one by one the party stopped arguing with the world.

Aldous sat down where he stood, pack still on, and stared. The two remaining porters — men who had cursed the climb, cursed the river, cursed each other for nineteen days — knelt at the edge of the terrace and dipped their hands in the water without being told, the way you kneel in a church whose customs you don't know but feel anyway. Wren lowered herself against a pillar and opened her map case with shaking fingers, took out paper and pencil, and then did not write anything. She just held the pencil above the page, looking from the springs to the page and back, and Ivo watched her understand, visibly, that there was no scale for this, no contour line, no symbol in any cartographer's alphabet for a place where the world kept its heart.

Sable stood at the terrace edge with the wind of the cavern moving her hair, and said nothing at all. She had her father's compass open in her palm. The needle swung slowly, uselessly, drunk on whatever iron slept in the rock, and she watched it wander like a man watching a dog run free after years on a chain.

And the surveyors — the missing team, found alive two days ago, gaunt and half-feral and divided into camps of destroyers and sellers — went quiet.

That was the strangest part. Ivo had spent three days among them, listening to their schism grind like millstones: Chief Halden's faction speaking of charges and collapse in the flat voices of true believers, the others muttering about contracts and buyers and what the financier's money could build. They had argued in whispers at cold camps, argued with their eyes across fires, argued even while hauling rope. Two factions wearing one set of rags.

Now they stood at the rim of the springs, all of them, destroyers and sellers alike, and the argument simply wasn't there anymore. One of the seller faction — a heavyset man named Brack, who had talked of salvage rights the way other men talked of scripture — took off his hat. Just took it off and held it, standing in the golden half-dark, like a man at a graveside.

Chief Halden stood apart, at the terrace's end, and he was not quiet in the same way. Ivo watched him. The chief was looking at the water the way a man looks at a diagnosis — searching it for something else, for a flaw, for permission. His eyes went along the cavern wall to the left, tracing something, counting something, and his lips moved once, silently, and his hand drifted to the canvas satchel he had carried up the final gorge himself, refusing help, refusing to let it out of arm's reach.

Ivo had seen satchels like that before. Demolition men carried them. He had carried one himself, once, young and stupid, blowing a beaver dam off a rancher's ditch.

He moved to stand beside Sable. Neither of them spoke. There was nothing to say that the cavern wasn't already saying better.

* * *

Time went strange down there. Ivo couldn't have said if they stood on the terrace for an hour or three. The light never changed; the springs never paused; the note held steady as a struck bell that would not fade. Hunger, exhaustion, the ache of Wren's leg, the knowledge of the hired men climbing behind them — all of it receded, the way noise recedes underwater, present but beside the point.

It was Wren who broke the silence, and she broke it laughing — a small, cracked, astonished sound.

"I mapped this river," she said. "Eleven years. Every gauge station, every confluence, every rapid from the Gate to the border. And I never once asked myself where it *started*. Do you understand? I drew the headwaters as a dashed line and a question mark and I *slept fine*." She pressed the pencil to the page at last and wrote, in a hand nothing like her usual drafting script: *It is a room. The river is a room.* Then she put the pencil down and covered her face with both hands.

"They knew," Sable said quietly. "The Survey knew. That's why the maps were erased."

"I erased them," Wren said into her hands. "My own hand. And I told myself it was geology. Unsurveyed terrain. I told myself—" She stopped. Looked up at the springs, at the water rising clean out of the living rock, and whatever she saw there finished the sentence better than she could.

Brack cleared his throat. When he spoke, his voice came out rusted. "My grandfather ran cattle on the lower river. Dry years, we prayed to it. Wet years, we cussed it." He turned his hat slowly in his hands. "Nobody told me it had a *face*."

"It has more than a face," said one of Halden's people, a lean woman called Merrick who had spoken for sealing the cavern since the day they'd found her. She was crying openly and did not seem to know it. "It has a pulse. Can't you feel it? The whole plateau is alive up here. Seal this and you don't kill a spring. You kill—"

"You kill nothing," Halden said.

His voice cut the chamber like a blade through silk, and every head turned. He had come back along the terrace, and the satchel was open at his feet now, and he was kneeling beside it with the calm of a surgeon laying out instruments. Up close Ivo could see what the expedition had cost him: the man was skin over scaffolding, eyes burning in sockets gone hollow, sixty pounds lighter than any photograph of him Wren had shown them. But his hands were steady. That was the terrible thing. Nineteen days of starvation and schism and the death of everything he'd built, and his hands were steadier than Ivo's had ever been.

"Halden," Wren started, "Chief, listen — look at it. Whatever you came here to decide, just *look*—"

"I have looked," Halden said. "For nine days I have looked. I sat where you're sitting, Wren. I wept where Brack is standing. I know exactly what this place does to a person, because it did it to me, and then I read the logs." He lifted one of the demolition charges from the satchel — a waxed cylinder, dull and ordinary, obscene in the golden light — and held it up so they could all see it, the way a preacher holds up a text.

"This is not a decision about beauty," he said. "Beauty is how they'll sell it. This is arithmetic. Corren—" his eyes found Sable, and there was something almost gentle in them, which was worse than hatred, "—your father understood it before any of us. The water can belong to everyone, or it can belong to a ledger. There is no third column. I have read the financier's terms. I have seen what his engineers surveyed downstream. If this source passes into that man's hands, within ten years there will be a gate on that dark throat of rock, and meters, and prices, and towns downriver drinking by subscription. And if it stays wild and *known* — if it goes on the maps, into the record, into the mouths of senators and speculators — then it is already sold. Revelation is just the slowest form of purchase."

"So you'll murder it instead," Sable said. Her voice was low and did not carry far, but in that hush it reached every corner of the cavern. "You'll bring the whole roof down on the only living heart this desert has, because you can't stomach the idea that someone might love it wrongly."

"I'll bury it *unowned*," Halden said. "There's a difference."

"There isn't," Ivo said.

Everyone looked at him. He hadn't planned to speak. The words came up out of him the way the water came up out of the floor — rising through cracks he'd thought were sealed years ago.

"I've spent my whole life carrying a debt to keep a secret about this water," he said. "One secret. One small one. And I know what it did to me — what it cost, what it made me, what it let other men make of me. You want to seal this place, you're not protecting it. You're just digging the biggest hole anyone ever hid a truth in. And truths in holes don't die, Halden. They wait. And the men who dig the holes are always the ones who get buried in them."

Halden regarded him for a long moment. The charge sat in his hands, patient as a sleeping animal.

"You're the boy," he said softly. "From the diversion camp. Twenty-two years ago. I read your name in the old correspondence — Eastwick. The witness they bought." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing Ivo had seen in nineteen days of sad things. "Then you of all people should understand. Secrets are the only currency this water has ever been traded in. I'm simply taking it off the market."

He began, unhurried, to unpack the rest of the satchel: the charges, the wire, the detonator with its worn wooden handle, laying each piece on the travertine in a neat row, a communion set for a church he meant to close forever.

Above them, the cathedral glowed and breathed, and the river went on rising out of the living rock, indifferent and immense, carrying everything it knew toward the sea.
