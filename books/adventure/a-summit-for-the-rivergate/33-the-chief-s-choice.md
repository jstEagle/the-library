## 33. The Chief's Choice

Sable heard the charges before she saw them.

Not the detonation — nothing had blown yet — but the sound of preparation: the flat clink of crates being pried open, the scrape of a pry bar on stone, men's voices pitched low and urgent in the cathedral dark. She stood at the spring's edge with Ivo, lamplight trembling on the black water, and the sound reached her across the vast silence of the cavern the way a stone reaches the bottom of a well.

"They're at the north wall," she said.

Ivo turned his head, listening. The lamplight caught the lines of his face — exhaustion carved deep, dust in the creases of his neck, the look of a man who had climbed a gorge on his hands and knees and arrived to find the war had moved on without him. Behind them, Wren Halloway sat propped against a boulder with her bandaged leg stretched out, her ruined maps spread across her lap like a patient she was trying to talk back to life. The surveyors who had chosen revelation over destruction stood in a loose, uncertain cluster near the entrance passage, their headlamps aimed at the floor.

At the north wall, the chief's faction worked by the light of three storm lanterns.

"Better dead than owned." Sable said it quietly, but the words tasted like iron. "That's what he told them. That's what he's been telling them since the pass."

"And you believe he'll do it." Ivo's voice was flat. Not a question. He'd been a ranger long enough to know the sound of a man who had already decided.

"I believe he'll try." She was already moving. "Come on."

The cathedral swallowed their footsteps. It had swallowed everything else — their argument at the threshold, the chief's contempt, the whole long quarrel that had brought two factions of the same expedition to this chamber at the roof of the river's beginning. The cavern rose above them in ribs of wet stone, higher than any building Sable had ever seen, higher than the canyon walls themselves, and the river came up out of the living rock below in a silence so complete it was less a sound than a presence. The spring did not gush. It *rose* — a slow, muscular upwelling, black water lifting itself out of the earth as if the whole continent had a heart and this was its chamber.

Her father had never seen it. That thought had been sitting in her chest since they'd come down the final gorge, and it sat there now, heavy and patient, waiting for its turn.

The chief heard them coming. He turned from the wall with a detonator case in one hand and the light of absolute conviction in his face, and Sable thought, with a strange clarity: *he looks like a man standing at his own funeral, and pleased with the arrangements.*

"Stop," she said.

Corren. The name was hers, had been her father's, and the chief — Aldous Merrick, gaunt as a winter branch, sixty days in the canyon country and half of them without proper food — looked at her the way a man looks at weather he has already decided to ignore.

"You shouldn't be here," he said. "None of you should. This place was never meant to be found, and now that it has been, it can't be left standing for men like Halloran to buy."

"Then we'll fight him," Sable said. "We'll testify. We'll put it in every paper from Rivergate to the coast—"

"With what?" Merrick's voice cracked across the cavern, echoing off the stone ribs. "Paper? Testimony? Halloran owns the papers. Halloran owns the survey boards. He owns half the men in this party's pensions." He gestured with the detonator case, and behind him his people — four of them, hollow-eyed, faithful — kept working, feeding wire into the drill holes they'd spent the night cutting into the north wall. "You don't stop a man like Halloran by *telling on him*. You stop him by taking away the thing he wants. There is no dam without the spring. There is no sale without the water. I am going to close the account."

Sable's fury came up out of her like the spring came up out of the rock — slow, and then all at once.

"Close the *account*." She stepped into the lantern light. Ivo moved with her, half a pace behind, and she was distantly aware of the surveyors drifting closer, Wren propping herself up to see. "You're going to stand in the house where the river is born and call it an account. My father died at the mouth of this country keeping men from doing exactly what you're about to do, and he didn't do it to *close* anything. He did it so the water would keep going. So the towns downstream would keep drinking. So the terraces would keep their seeps and the desert would keep its one green thread. And you want to kill it because killing it is *simpler*."

"Simpler," Merrick repeated. Something moved behind his eyes — not doubt, not yet, but the place where doubt would enter if it could find a door. "Simpler than watching it sold."

"Nothing is simpler than destruction. That's the whole lie of it. That's what men like Halloran count on." She was close to him now, close enough to see that his hands were shaking, that the shaking wasn't fear but exhaustion and certainty wearing each other down to the bone. "You think you're his opposite. You're his mirror. He wants the water because owning it gives him power over everyone downstream. You want it dead because killing it gives you the same power. Either way, the river stops being the river and becomes a *decision men made about it*."

The drill bit screamed somewhere behind him, and the sound went through the cavern like a needle through silk.

"Show him," Ivo said quietly.

Sable turned. Ivo stood with the expedition logs in his hands — the real ones, the ones Wren had carried up the final gorge against all sense, wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like an infant. Wren had given them up an hour ago with the words *I kept everything. I kept everything because I couldn't fix what I'd erased, so I kept what was true.* Now Ivo held them out to Merrick the way you hold out evidence, or a mirror.

"You should see what your patron intended," Ivo said. "Before you decide what your own hands are for."

Merrick didn't take the logs. For a long moment he simply looked at them, and Sable watched a man weigh his certainty against a book. Then he said, "I've read the logs. I wrote half of them."

"You read the ones you wrote," Sable said. "Wren kept the correspondence. The financial annex. The letters from Halloran's office that came upriver with the supply drops — the ones you filed and never opened because you trusted the man paying for your expedition." She took the logs from Ivo and opened them to the marked pages, and her hands were steady, and she was grateful for that, because inside she was not steady at all. "Page forty. Read it."

"I don't have time—"

"You have time. The charges take hours to set properly and you know it, because you taught half these men their drilling pattern yourself. You have time. Read it."

Merrick took the book.

He read by lantern light, standing, and Sable watched his face the way she had once watched the sky for rain — looking for the first change, the first softening at the horizon. For a long time there was nothing. His jaw was stone. His eyes moved down the page and back up, and down again, as if the words might rearrange themselves if he gave them a second chance.

Then his thumb stopped moving.

Sable knew the passage. She had read it three times by firelight on the climb, and each time it had landed in her chest like a dropped stone. Halloran's man, writing in the smooth confident prose of a man who believes his letters will never be read by the wrong eyes:

*Re: the question of downstream liability following acquisition. Once the source is secured and the diversion works completed, the question of the river's mineral content becomes a matter of engineering rather than nature. The client should understand that the water, as it emerges, carries a dissolved mineral load that renders it unsuitable for the client's intended processing operations downstream. This is not an obstacle. The client's engineers have confirmed that a measured introduction of the neutralizing compound at the headworks — prior to the water's entry into the client's canal system — will render the outflow inert. The downstream towns, in short, need never know what they are drinking, because they will no longer be drinking it. The river below the intake will be, for practical purposes, a dry channel. This should be presented to the client not as a cost but as the principal benefit of the arrangement: a captive water supply, and a captive population, in perpetuity.*

Merrick read it twice. Then he read it a third time, and his lips moved on the third pass, and Sable understood that he was no longer reading — he was reciting it to himself, forcing his mind to hold it, because some part of him was still trying to refuse it entry.

"A dry channel," he said.

"A dead river," Sable said. "Every town from the Narrows to the delta. Every terrace. Every seep. Everything my father—" Her voice caught, and she hated it, and she let it catch anyway, because some things should cost the speaker something. "Everything my father died for. Not sold. *Drained*. And you were going to help him by blowing up the spring, because you'd rather the river die by your hand than his. That's not principle. That's just choosing whose fingerprints you like on the murder."

The drill had stopped. She didn't know when. The whole north wall crew had drifted over, and they stood in the lantern light with their wire spools and their primer caps, listening, and their faces were the faces of men watching the ground open under a church.

Merrick closed the book. Opened it again. Closed it. His hands, Sable saw, were no longer shaking. They were very still, the way water is still just before it goes over a drop.

"I read the supply manifests," he said, and his voice had gone quiet, which was worse than the cracking. "Eleven years of expedition work. I read every manifest, every invoice, every letter of credit. I told myself Halloran was a speculator — a greedy man, but a man who wanted a *product*. Water to sell. I told myself that was a thing I could fight in the open, with surveys and testimony and law." He looked up, and his eyes were wet, and he didn't wipe them. "I never opened the annex. Eleven years, and I never opened the annex, because some part of me knew what a man like that does with a captive supply. You don't sell water to people you can poison. Poisoning is cheaper. Poisoning is *control*."

"Chief," said one of his crew — a young man, Ferro, who had held the lantern — "the charges. Do we—"

"Stop drilling."

"Chief—"

"*Stop.*" It came out of him like a sob dressed as a command. He set the detonator case down on the stone with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down something that has become too heavy to hold and too dangerous to drop. "Pull the wire. Pull all of it. Every hole we cut, we fill. We put this wall back the way we found it if it takes a week."

Ferro looked at the others. The others looked at Merrick. And then, one by one, they began gathering up the wire, and the sound of it — the soft hiss of cordage being drawn back out of the stone, the work of *un*-doing — was the most beautiful sound Sable had heard in two months of canyon country.

She should have felt triumphant. She searched herself for it and found, instead, something else — something that surprised her with its weight. Because Merrick was still standing there with the log book in his hands, and he was not relieved, and he was not saved, and he was looking at the north wall he had meant to bring down with the expression of a man who has just learned that the floor he built his life on was laid by a liar.

"Eleven years," he said again, to no one. "I gave it eleven years."

"I know," Sable said. And because she was her father's daughter, and because fury was a fire that burned down to grief if you let it, she said the harder thing: "He used you the way he used all of us. The rangers. The towns. Me. A man like that doesn't beat you. He *spends* you. And the only thing that beats him is everyone he spent standing up at the same time and saying what he did."

Merrick looked at her. Really looked, for the first time — not at the thief, not at the Corren girl, not at the complication, but at her.

"Your father," he said. "The man at the diversion works. I read the incident file. It was filed as a drowning. An accident." His voice caught on the word. "It wasn't an accident, was it."

"No," Sable said. "It wasn't."

Merrick nodded slowly. He turned to his crew. "Break out the remaining charges," he said, and Sable's whole body went rigid — Ivo's hand came up, ready — but Merrick was already talking over her alarm, his voice steadying into something harder and cleaner than it had been in weeks. "Not for the wall. For the entrance passage below. If Halloran's men are coming up that gorge, they'll come through the narrows at the base, and there's a choke point there where three men with a single charge can hold fifty. We're done destroying this place." He looked around the lantern-lit circle of faces — his people, Wren's people, the ranger, the thief. "Now we defend it. And then we take every page of this book downstream and we nail it to every door in Rivergate."

It was Wren, from her boulder, who said what Sable was already feeling in her spine:

"Then we'd better hope we're faster than he is."

And as if the cavern itself had been waiting for the words, the sound came — faint, from far below the entrance passage, carried up through the stone ribs of the cathedral like a pulse through a wrist.

A flare. Then another.

Orange light, blooming somewhere down in the gorge they had climbed all night to escape.

Halloran's force had reached the foot of the final ascent.

* * *

They had perhaps two hours. Maybe three, if the hired men were cautious, and hired men usually were — they were paid to succeed, not to die, and the gorge below was a killing ground for anyone who climbed it carelessly. But two hours was not a plan. Two hours was a countdown.

Sable stood at the cathedral's entrance mouth with Ivo, looking down the black throat of the final gorge, and the flares had stopped, and the silence they left behind was worse than the light.

"He'll send scouts first," Ivo said. "Eight, maybe ten men in the lead party. The rest will stage at the gorge base until it's cleared."

"You know this how?"

"I was one of those scouts once." He said it without flinching, and she loved him a little for that — for the way he had stopped hiding what he'd been. "Rangers clear ground the same way. You send in the men you can afford to lose and you watch what the ground does to them."

"Then we give the ground something to do." She was already turning the terraces over in her mind — her father's terraces, the hidden routes, the old diversion channels cut into the rock above the gorge mouth by hands two generations dead. "There's a channel. My father showed me. It runs above the narrows — it was cut to bleed floodwater off the gorge in the wet season. It's dry now. But it's wide enough to walk, and it comes out on the rim above the choke point, and from the rim—" She stopped. Looked at him. "From the rim, we're behind them. Whatever they do at the entrance, we're behind them."

Ivo was quiet for a moment. In the lamplight spilling from the cathedral behind them, his face was half gold and half shadow.

"You want to go down into it," he said. "Toward them."

"I want to be where they aren't looking. There's a difference."

"There is." He looked down the gorge, and she watched him do the arithmetic she'd watched him do a hundred times since the salt flats — the ranger's arithmetic, the cost of every route, every risk, every man. "Merrick holds the entrance with the charge. Wren and the surveyors hold the cathedral. And we take the channel and come out on their flank."

"We," she said.

"We." He said it simply, and then, because he was still Ivo Eastwick, who had crossed a desert to arrest her and had instead crossed a country to stand beside her, he added: "I've been paid to bring you in once already. It didn't take. I figure I should stop taking jobs I intend to quit."

Above them, inside the cathedral, the river went on rising out of the living rock in its vast and ancient silence, indifferent to charges and charters and the small furious plans of men — and Sable Corren, thief, daughter, keeper of her father's routes, turned and went to war for it.
