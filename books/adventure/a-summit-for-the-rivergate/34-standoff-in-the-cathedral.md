## 34. Standoff in the Cathedral

The water was talking before the men were.

Ivo heard it first as a change in the sound of the spring — a low, insistent hiss beneath the cathedral's silence, like breath drawn through teeth. The pool at the cavern's heart had climbed three feet since they'd arrived. It lapped now at the shelf of living rock where Wren sat with her ruined leg propped on a survey case, and where the chief's demolition charges lay in their open crates like sleeping animals.

Then he heard boots.

Not from below — from above. From the passage the chief's people had used, the high route through the shattered terraces that Sable's father had pioneered and no map recorded. Which meant the men coming had either found the route themselves or been told of it by someone who knew it.

"Lights out," Sable breathed beside him.

Around the cavern, the surveyors moved without argument. Lamps were shuttered, flames cupped and killed. In ten seconds the great chamber went from lamplit sanctuary to a darkness so complete Ivo could feel it pressing on his eyes. He kept one hand on Sable's shoulder because otherwise he could not have said where she was. The only light left was the faint gray seep of day from the entrance tunnel far above them, and the pale ghost-glow of the water itself, which seemed to hold light the way stone holds cold.

Boots on stone. Echoes multiplying until it sounded like a company rather than a crew. Ivo counted strides, counted pauses, did the arithmetic of approach. Eight men, maybe nine. Moving carefully but not quietly — they didn't expect resistance. They expected a finished job.

A voice rolled down into the dark, amplified by the cavern's throat until it seemed to come from everywhere.

"Meridian Survey! This is Halloran, foreman for Aldous Crane. We know you're down there. We know about the charges." A pause, and when the voice came again it had dropped its parade-ground edge and put on something softer, more reasonable, and infinitely worse. "Nobody has to die in a hole in the ground. Come up, hand over the chief and the documents, and the rest of you walk out free. That's not a threat. That's an offer."

Ivo felt Sable go rigid under his hand.

"That's the man," she whispered. "That's the voice from the letters. He wrote to my mother after my father drowned. *Condolences.*"

The chief stirred somewhere ahead of them. Ivo heard the old man's breathing, quick and shallow, and then the scrape of his boots as he rose.

"Chief—" Wren started.

"He wants me," the chief said, and his voice in the dark was thin and strange, stripped of the certainty that had carried him across a hundred miles of canyon. "He's wanted me since the logs went missing."

"You don't have to give him anything," Ivo said.

"No," the chief agreed. "But he thinks I do. And a man who believes you'll surrender is a man who walks into your hands."

Ivo understood one heartbeat too late what the old man intended.

* * *

The chief had wired the entrance charge himself, days ago, back when his plan had been simple: bring the whole cathedral down and let the river keep its own secret. Most of the charge had been pulled since — Ivo had watched him do it, watched the fury drain out of him crate by crate as the truth of Crane's poison plan came clear. But one line remained. One small package set in the mouth of the upper passage, enough to close the tunnel, nothing more. The chief had kept it, he'd said, *for insurance.*

Now he walked toward it through the dark, and Ivo lunged after him and caught nothing but air.

"CHIEF!" His shout cracked around the vaulted stone. "Don't—"

The detonation was smaller than Ivo expected and larger than anything he had ever felt. It wasn't sound so much as pressure, a fist of compressed air that slammed through the cavern and knocked every lamp flame flat, every man off his feet. Rock fell somewhere far above with a long grinding roar, and dust came down like snow, and then there was silence — a new kind of silence, sealed and total, the silence of a room with no door.

When Ivo got his lamp relit, hands shaking, the upper passage was gone. Where the gray thread of daylight had been there was only rubble, a plug of broken stone packed tight as mortar. No light came through it. Not a thread.

They were inside a bottle.

And around the rim of the great chamber, torches bloomed — eight of them, nine — as Halloran's men revealed themselves from the ledges where they'd taken cover. They hadn't fired. They hadn't needed to. The chief stood at the base of the dead tunnel with the detonator still in his fist, an old man in a sealed room, having delivered every soul in it — allies, enemies, the wounded, the innocent — into the same stone coffin.

"What have you done?" Halloran's voice had lost its reasonableness entirely. "You've sealed us in with you, you mad old—"

"I've sealed us in together," the chief said. He turned, and in the lamplight his face was wet with dust and something else. "All of us. Every man Crane sent, every man Crane bought, every map and every log and every lie. Nobody leaves this room to report anything. Including you."

* * *

They arranged themselves the way enemies do when there is nowhere left to stand apart: on opposite sides of the rising water, lamps turned up, weapons visible but not raised. Ivo counted Halloran's crew again in the light. Nine. Two of them young, barely more than boys, holding their rifles wrong. One — a heavyset woman with a scarred jaw — kept glancing at the waterline on the rock and then away, fast, the way people look at things that frighten them.

Between the two camps, the spring breathed.

It was the first time Ivo had really looked at it since the explosion, and looking at it now, in this enforced stillness, he understood why even the hardened surveyors had gone quiet when they'd first arrived. The entire river rose here. Not from cracks, not from seeps — from the living rock itself, a wall of stone forty feet high and a hundred wide, weeping water along its whole face in thousands of silver threads that gathered and braided and fell into the pool. The sound was not loud. It was vast. It was the sound the desert made when it dreamed.

And the pool was rising.

"The dry season," Wren said from her ledge. Her voice was hoarse but level; she'd done the numbers aloud twice already today and she would do them again, because numbers were how she prayed. "Even in the dry season, the spring delivers better than four hundred second-feet. This chamber holds maybe six acre-feet above our current floor. Without the entrance tunnel to bleed overflow—" she stopped. Started again, flatter. "We have hours. Not days. Hours."

"How many hours?" one of Halloran's boys asked. His voice cracked on *hours.*

"Depends on the geology under us," Wren said. "If the floor drains naturally, longer. If it doesn't—"

"It does," the chief said dully. "There are old diversion channels cut into the east wall. Corren work. Older than Corren, maybe. They carry overflow down to the lower gorge." He looked at his own hands. "That was the original plan, years ago. Before Crane. Cut channels, meter the flow, sell the water downstream. The channels were never finished."

Sable had gone very still. "My father's marks are on those channels."

"Yes," the chief said. "Your father found them. Your father is the reason any of this exists at all."

* * *

Halloran crossed the water.

Not all the way — he stopped on a spine of rock at the pool's center, close enough to talk without shouting, far enough that shooting him would be murder rather than war. He was a big man gone soft in the middle, with the patient face of someone who had spent thirty years making other people's cruelty administratively possible.

"New offer," he said. "Since the old man has made this everyone's problem. We've got rope, pitons, climbing gear. There's a chimney in the south wall — my scout spotted it before you shut us in. Hard climb, but doable. Room for maybe five or six to go out over the top." He spread his hands. "Five or six. So we talk about who."

"And the rest?" Ivo asked.

"The rest take their chances with the water." Halloran said it without heat, without apology, the way a man reads a freight manifest. "Crane wants the chief alive — barely. Wants the logs. Wants the woman Corren, on general principle. The rest of you are weather to him. But I'm offering you a place in the boat, ranger. You strike me as a practical man. You've been chasing that thief across half the territory on somebody's paper. Somebody paid you to do that. You know how this works."

Every eye in the chamber came to Ivo. The surveyors'. Halloran's crew. Sable's — though hers he could feel without turning his head, a heat against his cheek like standing near a forge.

He thought about the debt papers. Twenty-two years of them, folded in oilcloth inside his coat, carried through salt flats and narrows and ambush, the one fixed point in a life he'd built to be unfixed. *Pay to the family of Tomas Corren, in perpetuity, the sum of one-tenth part.* He had never questioned it. Questioning it had never once occurred to him, the way a man doesn't question the walls of the cell he was born in.

He took the papers out. Unfolded them. Held them up to the lamplight so Halloran could see them.

"You know these?" Ivo said.

Something flickered in the foreman's face. "Should I?"

"They're forged. Or so I'm told. Signed by a financier named Crane to buy a boy's silence about what he saw at this river's edge twenty-two years ago." He let that sit. Let it travel around the chamber, into the faces of Halloran's crew, the two boys, the woman with the scarred jaw who had stopped looking at the water and started looking at her employer. "So yes. I know how it works. I've been working for it my whole life without knowing it. And here's what I've learned standing in this room."

He held the papers over the pool.

"Nothing Crane owns is worth the water in this rock. And nobody in this chamber gets out unless everybody in this chamber works."

He dropped the papers in.

They landed on the black water and floated there, white moths on dark glass, ink beginning to bloom and blur as the spring took them. Sable made a sound beside him — not a word, just a sound, low in her chest, twenty-two years of a family's grievance watching itself dissolve. Then her hand found his, hard, and stayed.

Halloran looked at the floating papers for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — quieter, and for the first time honest.

"The channels," he said. "The unfinished ones. How much blasting? How much shoring?"

* * *

They worked by lamplight while the water climbed.

It was the strangest labor Ivo had ever known — surveyors and hired guns hauling the same crates, sharing the same ropes, the chief directing both sides in a voice gone hoarse, Wren calling angles and grades from her ledge with her leg propped up and her eyes fever-bright. The east wall's diversion channels were three feet wide, cut deep into the stone by hands long dead, choked with twenty years of rubble and mineral crust. Clearing them meant blasting — small charges, surgical ones, the last of the chief's explosives spent at last on something that gave water instead of taking it.

The two young gunmen worked beside Sable without a word between them. The scarred woman — Renn, she was called — turned out to know stone, real stone, quarry-work from another life, and she and the chief argued grades and fracture lines like old colleagues who'd met on the wrong side of a border. Ivo hauled rock. His shoulders burned. The water reached his knees, then his thighs, cold enough to make his hands stupid, and still they worked, because the alternative was written on Wren's face every time she checked the waterline against her marks.

Once, passing Sable in the waist-deep channel mouth, Ivo caught her arm.

"If we open those channels," he said, "the water goes down to the lower gorge. Where Halloran's main force is climbing. Where Voss might be."

"I know."

"The flood will scatter them. Maybe kill some."

"I know that too." She pushed wet hair from her face. Her eyes in the lamplight were the color of the water itself. "My father diverted this river once to stop men like them. I'm going to finish the job. If the river chooses to be gentle with them, that's its business. It usually is. It was only ever men who weren't."

She squeezed his arm once and went back to the rock.

Behind them, unnoticed by anyone, the last of the debt papers finally soaked through and sank — or perhaps didn't sink, perhaps slipped down through some hidden fissure in the cathedral floor, out into whatever dark arteries fed the spring, bound at last for the towns and the tributaries and the sea.

The chief set the final charge at midnight. Everyone climbed to the high ledges. The blast was muffled by water and stone, a deep drumbeat felt in the chest more than heard — and then, from the mouth of the lowest channel, came a sound none of them had dared hope for.

A rush. A roar. The river, finding its old road home.

The pool began, slowly, blessedly, to fall.

And in the sudden relative quiet, with water streaming gold in the lamplight down the face of the east wall, Halloran's voice came across the chamber — not to Ivo, not to the chief, but to his own crew, nine tired armed men standing knee-deep in a stranger's cathedral.

"Rope's rigged at the south chimney," he said. "Six can climb. I'm staying." He looked at the falling water, at the surveyors, at Ivo, and something in his heavy face had come loose and rearranged itself. "Somebody from Crane's side ought to be here when the testimony starts. Might as well be the one who's seen it."

The water fell an inch. Then another.

Far overhead, through the plugged throat of the entrance tunnel, came a sound that did not belong to the river at all — faint, rhythmic, unmistakable.

Someone was digging.
