## 35. Water Rising

The sound came again — three strikes, a pause, three more — and Ivo stood in the lamplight with the river climbing his boots and did the only arithmetic that mattered.

Stone above them: the entrance charge had brought down the throat of the tunnel, maybe forty feet of shattered rock and rubble packed tight by its own weight. Stone below their feet: the spring itself, rising now in earnest, fed by whatever pressure the cavern had been holding back for centuries before men came with drills and dynamite. And between those two facts, thirty-one people with perhaps four hours of air and less than that of dry ground.

"Someone's digging," Wren said. She said it the way she read instruments — flat, precise, as if naming it might make it manageable.

"It'll be Crane's men," Sable said. "They sealed us in. They won't leave us to drown — dead men can't testify." Her voice was steady but her hands were not; Ivo saw her fingers working at the strap of her father's compass, over and over, a small unconscious motion.

"Then they'll dig for days," Ivo said. "We don't have days."

He turned to face the chamber, all of it, the way he'd once learned to read a canyon in a single sweep from horseback. The lamplight caught them all: the surveyors huddled against the east wall, gaunt and divided still, half of them watching their chief as if waiting for orders that would never come sane; the hired crew on the south side under their foreman, a broad gray man named Detch who had not spoken since the blast except to count his people aloud, twice; Wren sitting propped against an instrument case she'd refused to abandon even now; Sable beside him, close enough that her shoulder touched his arm.

And everywhere, the water. It had been ankle-deep when the dust settled. It was past their bootlaces now, and cold, and it moved with the patience of something that had never once lost a negotiation.

"All right," Ivo said, and heard his own voice come out rougher than he intended. He cleared his throat. "Listen to me. All of you."

The sound of it — a ranger's voice, a lawman's voice, in this place where law had never reached — turned heads. Even the chief looked up from his charges, his detonator box held against his chest like a child.

"The water's rising about a hand's width every twenty minutes," Ivo said. "I've watched rivers my whole life. This one isn't going to stop, and it isn't going to slow down, because we've plugged the only throat it has. In four hours, maybe five, this floor is gone. The chimney at the south end goes up two hundred feet to a crack that might open onto the rim — or might not. Six can climb it, maybe eight if we're lucky and light." He let that sit. "That leaves better than twenty people with no way out unless we open that tunnel ourselves."

"And how," Detch said. His first words in an hour, gravel-deep. "You got dynamite, ranger? Because what's left of ours is wet."

"We've got channels," Sable said.

She stepped forward, out of the lamplight's edge, and Ivo watched her become what she'd been on the terraces two weeks ago — not a thief, not a fugitive, but a daughter of this country, someone whose blood knew stone the way Detch's hands knew rope.

"My father mapped the seeps and the overflow channels through this whole canyon system," she said. "Before your survey ever put a flag on it. He used to say the source country was built like a house — every room has a door, and most of the doors are water doors. There are diversion channels cut into the rock along the north wall. Old ones. Some are natural, some were widened by hand generations back, when the terraces were farmed. They were cut to carry floodwater *away* from the low chambers." She pointed across the black water toward a shadowed seam in the north wall, forty yards out, already thigh-deep in the flood path. "If we open the ones my father marked, the water splits. Three ways instead of one. That buys time. Maybe hours. Enough to dig."

Nobody moved. The surveyors looked at their chief. The hired men looked at Detch. Two wills, two chains of command, and between them thirty-one people going nowhere.

It was the chief who broke first — not by agreeing, but by standing, slowly, joints cracking, and walking to the water's edge. He was a ruin of a man, Ivo thought. Whatever certainty had carried him up the river had drowned somewhere in this cavern, and what remained looked almost relieved.

"I spent eleven years trying to decide what this place should be," the chief said quietly. "Dead, or owned, or hidden. I never once asked what it wanted." He looked at the rising water with something like respect. "Do it. Open your channels. If we're going to be buried here, let it be while we're working."

* * *

The work began badly, which was to say it began the way all work begins among strangers: with argument.

The channel mouths Sable remembered were behind a shelf of rock reachable only by swimming fifteen feet of current that hadn't existed an hour ago. Ivo went first, because he would not ask anyone to go where he wouldn't, and the cold took his breath like a fist. The water wasn't just rising; it was *moving* now, a slow rotation around the chamber floor as the spring found its new shape. He got a line fixed to the shelf, then a second line, and after that it was ferry work — hauling men across one and two at a time, surveyor and hired gun alike, no order but whoever was closest.

What surprised him was who worked.

Detch's men, to a man, worked. They'd been paid to seal this cavern and they'd done it without asking why, and now that the bill had come due they swung hammers and pried at stone with the dumb loyalty of men who only knew how to finish a job. Half the surveyors worked too, especially the younger ones, the ones who'd signed on for maps and science and gotten a war instead. But three of the senior men sat against the wall and would not move, and the chief did not order them, and Ivo decided there wasn't time to hate anybody.

Sable directed from the shelf, calling measurements from memory. "Two feet left of the seep — there's a chisel mark, look for the chisel mark — my father cut it when I was nine, he let me hold the lamp—" Her voice cracked once, just once, on *nine*, and then went on.

Ivo found the mark. A single clean notch in the stone, deliberate as a signature. Beside it, nearly worn away, a smaller mark: a bird, maybe, or a wave. He put his bar into the channel mouth and felt the old masonry give with a grinding sigh, and the water found the gap and *took* it — poured sideways into the ancient channel with a sound like applause, and the level in the main chamber dropped two inches before anyone dared cheer.

"There," Sable breathed. "There's the second one, sixty feet along—"

They opened the second channel at the hour mark, and the third at the hour and a half, and each one bought them inches. Not enough. Ivo kept checking the high-water stain on the wall, the old dark line where the spring had marked its ceiling in some forgotten century, and the water was climbing toward it faster than the channels could drink. But the panic in the room changed key. Men who had been calculating odds started calculating *work*. Wren, propped on her case with her ruined leg stretched out, took over the tally — water height, air quality, headcounts — calling numbers in her cartographer's voice until the numbers became a rhythm everyone could march to.

It was during the fourth crossing, hauling two of Detch's men on the line, that Ivo felt the chamber shudder.

Not the water. The rock. A deep, subsonic groan rolled through the walls, long and tectonic, and every lamp in the cavern swung toward the entrance tunnel, where the rubble plug sat under forty feet of pressure.

"They're through the first collapse," Detch said. He'd waded out to mid-chamber, and Ivo saw him properly for the first time — a big man gone soft-eyed, breathing hard, his crew's blood on his conscience like everyone else's sins on theirs. "That's my foreman's signal pattern. Three strikes, pause. They're cutting a shaft down through the plug from above. It'll take them a day at least."

"We have four hours," Ivo said.

"Then somebody'd better tell the mountain to wait."

Ivo hauled the last man onto the shelf and stood there a moment, chest heaving, looking at the water. It had dropped six inches total. Against a rise of three feet an hour. The arithmetic didn't work and he was tired of pretending otherwise, and he was about to say so when Detch spoke again, quieter.

"Ranger. Show me the logs."

Ivo blinked. "What?"

"The expedition logs. Your woman there—" he nodded at Sable "—has been waving stolen papers around since the pass. The ones saying Crane meant to poison the water after purchase. I sealed a tunnel today on that man's money. Before I spend another hour bailing his river, I want to see it in ink."

* * *

They read the logs by lamplight on the instrument cases, the pages spread in a fan, Wren holding the lamp because her hands were steadiest. Sable had taken the documents from Voss's strongbox months ago, and they were thorough in the way financial documents are thorough — dates, amounts, codenames, a correspondence between Crane and a chemical broker in the eastern cities, specifying tonnage and delivery windows for compounds that had no honest use near a watershed.

Detch read slowly, his lips moving. Around him his crew gathered, and Ivo watched the reading travel through them like a current — the same men who had fired on them at the ford, who had chased them up the gorge, standing shoulder to shoulder now over paper that undid everything they'd been paid to protect.

"That's Crane's hand," one of them said finally. "I'd know it. I carried his mail for six years."

"He was going to poison it," another said. "The whole river. Every town downstream. For—" he turned a page, and his voice went strange. "For a reservoir bond. He'd have sold the water twice. Once poisoned to force the buyout, once clean after."

Detch closed the log. He sat very still. Then he stood, and the standing took a long time, like a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy.

"I've got a brother in Lowfield," he said, to no one in particular. "Downstream. Farms alfalfa. Got two girls." He looked at Ivo, and his eyes were wet and furious at once. "Crane sent me up here with thirty men and a crate of dynamite and told me it was about claim-jumpers. Claim-jumpers." He spat into the water. "All right, ranger. Your channels. Where do you need bars?"

"You've seen the arithmetic," Ivo said. "Even with the channels, we don't out-climb this water. We need that plug open from our side too — dig from below while they dig from above. Double the speed."

"Then we dig." Detch turned to his crew. "Bars and lamps. You heard the man. Move like it's your brother's town downstream, because it is."

And they moved. That was the thing Ivo would remember afterward, in the years when he tried to explain this night to people who hadn't been there — how fast the line between enemy and crew dissolved once the truth was common property. The hired men dug. The surveyors dug. The three seniors who'd refused to work came off the wall inside the hour, shamed or frightened or both, and dug. Sable called channel locations from her father's memory; Wren called water heights from her tally; the chief, of all people, organized the relay lines with the crisp efficiency of a man finally doing the one uncomplicated right thing available to him.

Ivo dug too, until his palms split, and between shifts he crossed the flooded floor again and again on the ferry line, and somewhere in the fifth hour Sable fell into step beside him on the shelf, and for a moment neither of them said anything at all. The water had climbed past the old stain. They both knew what that meant. Neither said that either.

"My father cut those channels so the water would have somewhere to go," she said finally. "So nobody downstream would ever flood. He spent his whole life giving the river doors." She looked across the chamber at the digging, the lamplight, the enemies swinging bars in rhythm. "Look at them. He'd have liked this. All these fools working together, and none of them know they're doing exactly what he built this place for."

"He knows," Ivo said, nodding at the chisel marks. "Man leaves his signature in the rock, he gets to watch."

She laughed — a short, cracked, real laugh — and pressed her shoulder against his, and they stood there one full minute, which was all the minutes they had.

Then the mountain groaned again, longer this time, and grit sifted down through the lamplight, and from overhead — clear now, closer, echoing down through the rubble plug — came a new sound underneath the digging.

A voice. Thin and distorted by stone, but unmistakably human, shouting down through forty feet of broken rock:

*"Hold your water! We're through the capstone — get clear of the tunnel mouth — we're coming through!"*

And beneath the voice, faint but rising, Ivo heard something else that made the hair stand up on his arms.

Running water. A great deal of it, moving fast — from *above*.
