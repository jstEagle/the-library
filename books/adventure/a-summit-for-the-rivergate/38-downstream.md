## 38. Downstream

Wren Halloway had spent her whole life believing that maps were a form of control — that the hand which drew the line held the country. Now she lay in the bottom of a battered survey skiff, splinted leg propped on a coil of rope, watching the Rivergate carry her home at a speed she could neither direct nor slow, and understood that she had never controlled anything at all. The river drew its own lines. It always had. She had merely been one of the people paid to pretend otherwise.

The early rains had come three days after they breached the wall, arriving not as a storm but as a presence — a gray ceiling that moved up the canyon ahead of them, filling the dry washes, greening the terraces, and lifting the river by inches every morning until the shallows that would have stranded them became instead a broad brown road running downhill. The dry season's arithmetic, the twenty days she had calculated so carefully in her notebook, dissolved like salt in water. They didn't need the days anymore. The river was giving them back all the time it had taken.

"Leg hurts?" Sable asked, crouching beside her with a strip of dried meat and a look Wren was learning to read: concern disguised as logistics.

"The leg is fine," Wren said. "It's everything else that hurts."

Sable almost smiled. She handed over the meat anyway.

They traveled in four boats now, taken from the financier's abandoned supply cache below the ford — good boats, army-green, with the man's initials stenciled on the sterns like a brand on cattle. Ivo had wanted to burn them out of principle. Sable had pointed out, with the flat practicality that seemed to run in her family like grain in wood, that burning boats was how you stayed in the canyon forever, and staying in the canyon forever was what the canyon wanted. So they kept the boats, and Wren privately enjoyed the irony of riding downstream on the enemy's own hulls, his name dissolving under their hands with river mud.

The prisoners rode in the middle boat, guarded by two of the surveyors who had chosen revelation over ruin. The foreman, Dall, sat with his wrists loosely bound and his eyes fixed on the water ahead of him, and he did not speak for the first day and a half. When he finally did, it was to say, without turning around: "There'll be men at the Gate station. His men. Waiting to hear from us."

"We know," Ivo said from the tiller.

"They'll have a story ready. Expedition lost, all hands drowned, tragic." Dall turned then, and his face in the gray light was older than his body, worn smooth like stone in current. "I can unmake that story. But you have to get me to a magistrate before they get to the chief's brother, who runs the telegraph office, and who will unmake me first."

Wren watched Ivo absorb this — the way he went still, the way his eyes moved downriver as if he could see the town waiting there, the wires already humming with someone else's version of events. She had known him for less than a month and she had already learned his silences. This one meant he was counting something. Miles, probably. Or hours. Or the distance between a prisoner's testimony and a bullet.

"How fast can we go?" he asked the river generally, and no one answered, because the river was already answering, faster than any of them liked.

* * *

They passed the first burned camp on the second day.

Wren saw it before anyone spoke of it — the black triangle of scorched earth above the high-water mark, the melted ghost of a tent, the iron legs of a stove standing in the ash like the ribs of something eaten. The lower camp. The place where this whole descent had begun, where she had knelt in cold fire-ash while Ivo read boot prints in the sand and said nothing, and she had thought him cold, and he had been carrying a dead man's debt and a living woman's warrant and had said nothing about either.

"Should we stop?" asked Pell, the youngest surveyor, poling the second boat. "Check for—"

"No," Ivo said.

He said it quietly, but it closed the question the way a door closes. They slid past the burned camp at maybe forty yards, close enough that Wren could see a tin cup lying on its side in the mud, filling slowly with rain, and she found herself cataloguing it automatically — *one cup, enameled, dented, orientation southwest* — and then made herself stop. That was the old habit. The mapmaker's disease: reducing everything to position and quantity so you never had to ask what things meant. She had erased the source approaches from official charts because a man with money told her to, and she had told herself it was just lines on paper, just positions and quantities, and a boy had nearly died in a flooded gorge last week because the honest route existed only in a dead smuggler's memory.

She looked at the cup until the current took it away.

The second camp they passed that evening, and the third the next noon, and each time Ivo gave the same quiet order — *no stops* — and each time she understood it better. It wasn't callousness. It was gravity. Everything behind them pulled: the camps, the ambush site where she'd been shot through the thigh, the pass where Ivo had scattered hired guns in the dark without killing a single one, the cathedral itself, still sealed somewhere above them with its rising water and its terrible silence. If they stopped at every place that had cost them something, they would stop forever. Homeward meant passing your own history at speed and refusing to look back except in glances.

But at the third camp — the middle camp, the one with the message scratched on the canteen — Sable raised a hand, and Ivo, to Wren's surprise, actually brought the boats in.

"Why here?" Wren asked, as they grounded on the shingle.

"Because this one isn't ours to pass," Sable said. She waded ashore and stood a moment looking at the empty fire ring, the flattened grass where tents had been, the ledge where a canteen had once lain with five words scratched into its belly. "My father's routes come through here. He marked the seep behind the camp — there's a stone with our family sign on it, cut when I was nine years old." She walked to the base of the cliff, moved aside a slab of fallen rock, and showed them: a spiral and a downward arrow, cut deep, weathered soft. "He cut it so travelers could find water. Strangers. Anyone." Her voice did something complicated on the word *anyone*. "The survey camped ten feet from it and never knew."

Wren limped over and looked at the mark for a long time. Then she took out her field book — the real one, the one she'd kept hidden inside her coat since the Narrows, the one with the true maps in it, the ones she intended to put in front of a magistrate if it killed her — and she drew the seep, and the spring, and the mark, and labeled it in her own hand: *Corren seep. Public water. By right of cutting.*

"There," she said. "Now it's on a map that doesn't lie."

Sable looked at the page a long moment. Then she touched the edge of the paper, very lightly, the way you touch something you're afraid might be a dream, and said nothing at all.

* * *

On the fourth day the river grew teeth again.

Below the terraces the canyon narrowed into a stretch Wren knew from the false maps — the ones she'd drawn — where the channel was shown as wide and gentle, because Voss had ordered it drawn that way to discourage independent prospectors. In truth it was a mile of standing waves and sucking eddies, and now, swollen with rain, it ran like something hunted.

They portaged what they could and ran what they had to, and Wren, unable to walk, unable even to sit up properly, rode the worst of it lashed into the first boat with Ivo at the tiller and Sable reading the water aloud from the bow — *left channel, right of the gray rock, hard left* — in her father's cadence, using her father's words for currents he had named decades ago. At the worst drop the boat stood on its stern, hung, and Wren felt her whole life hang with it — the forged orders, the altered maps, the fevered confession she'd made on the cold night when she'd finally said aloud what she'd done — and then the bow came down, the river took them, and they shot through into calm water spray-drenched and laughing, all of them, even the prisoners, even Dall, who laughed like a man remembering how.

Afterward, soaked and shaking, Wren caught Ivo looking at her across the boat.

"You ran my false map in reverse," she said. "You knew the channel was wrong and you ran it anyway."

"I had a better navigator," he said, nodding at Sable.

"That's not what I mean." Wren pushed wet hair off her face. "You trusted the truth over the chart. Most people do it the other way around. Most people drown doing it the other way around."

Ivo considered this with the patience she'd come to recognize in him, the desert-ranger patience, the stillness of a man who has learned that most emergencies are solved by not adding to them.

"Charts are just other people's memories," he said finally. "Good ones help. Bad ones kill. Only way to tell the difference is to go see for yourself." He glanced downriver, toward the Gate, toward the testimony and the wires and whatever waited for them in the world of paper and power. "That's all I'm going to do at the station, Wren. Go see for myself. And make them write down what I saw."

She believed him. That was the strange thing — she had spent years distrusting everyone above her on the pay chain, and here was a man with a forged debt in his past and a resignation letter forming in his throat, and she believed every word. Maybe belief was simpler when a person had nothing left to gain by lying to you. Maybe that was why the poor testified more honestly than the rich. They had less practice.

* * *

The fifth night, rain again, and the party camped on a gravel bar under tarps, and Wren lay awake listening to the river rise.

She heard footsteps and then Sable settled nearby, wrapped in a blanket, watching the dark water slide past a few feet away.

"Can't sleep either?" Sable said.

"I keep calculating." Wren shifted her splinted leg. "River speed, distance to the Gate, arrival time. I've done it four times. It comes out the same. We arrive the day after tomorrow, midmorning, and Dall says the financier's men will have had word by then, and I keep calculating because calculating feels like doing something."

"It isn't."

"No," Wren agreed. "It isn't."

They were quiet together. Somewhere upstream an owl called, and the rain made its small sound on the tarp, and the river made its large one underneath everything.

"When we get there," Sable said at last, "they're going to try to make this about my father. A thief's daughter, a smuggler's routes, a criminal's map-marks. They'll dig up everything he ever did to bury what was done to him." Her voice was level, but Wren heard the effort holding it level, the way you hear strain in a rope. "And they're going to try to make it about you too. A cartographer who falsified documents. They'll say your maps prove the expedition was illegitimate from the start."

"My maps *do* prove that," Wren said. "That's rather the point."

"And yours will hold?"

Wren thought about it honestly, because Sable deserved honesty and because lying to yourself was the one forgery no magistrate could void. "Some of it will hurt us. I erased approaches under orders; I'll testify to that, and it will look bad, and it should look bad. But the field books are dated, countersigned, cross-referenced against the survey logs Dall carried out. The forgery has a shape. Forgeries always have a shape — you can see the hand that made them if you know how to look." She paused. "Your father's marks on the stone have a shape too. Old cuts, weathered edges, a child's height above the ground. Truth has a texture. I've spent my whole career learning to feel it, and mostly I used the skill to serve liars. I'd like to use it once for something else."

Sable reached over in the dark and gripped Wren's shoulder, briefly, hard, and let go.

"Midmorning, day after tomorrow," she said. "Then we stop paddling against it."

* * *

They came within sight of the Rivergate station on the seventh afternoon, and Wren, propped upright in the bow for the first time in days, saw the smoke before she saw the buildings.

Not cook-smoke. Too much of it, too black, too steady. Woodsmoke from a large fire fed deliberately, the kind of fire you build to signal or to destroy.

Ivo saw it at the same moment. He brought the boats toward the near bank, and the whole flotilla eddied in behind him, and for a long minute nobody spoke. The station's flagpole rose above the cottonwoods, bare. The radio mast leaned at an angle it hadn't leaned before. And along the bank below the station, moored in a neat row, sat six boats flying no colors at all, which was itself a color — the color of men who preferred not to be identified.

Dall stood up in the middle boat, wrists still bound, and looked at the boats for a long time.

"Those aren't the station's," he said. "Those are Corvane's. His private fleet. He keeps them at the smelter landing." He turned, and there was something in his face Wren hadn't seen before — not fear exactly, but the grim satisfaction of a man whose calculations have come true. "He's here himself. Or his best people are. Which means he thinks the evidence is still in the canyon."

"Or that we are," Ivo said quietly.

"He won't want witnesses," Dall said. "He never has. That's rather the whole architecture of the man."

Wren looked at the smoke, and at the bare flagpole, and at the row of anonymous boats, and felt the homeward gravity reverse itself — the current that had carried them seven days suddenly feeling less like rescue and more like delivery, freight moving toward a buyer. She thought of the field book against her ribs. She thought of the true maps. She thought of the magistrate's office four miles inland at the county seat, and the telegraph office at the station, and which one Dall needed to reach first.

Ivo was already moving, low-voiced, giving orders — Sable to scout the bank, Pell to watch the prisoners, the boats to be cached and camouflaged among the willows. But his eyes came to Wren last, and held.

"How's the leg?" he asked.

"Lying will get us all killed," she said. "I can walk. Slowly. With a stick. For perhaps an hour."

"Then here's the hour," Ivo said. "We go in tonight, in the dark, before they expect us. Not to fight — to testify. You, me, Sable, Dall. Straight to the magistrate's office at the county seat, around the station entirely. Everyone else stays with the boats." He looked up at the leaning radio mast, at the smoke. "Voss may still be alive in there. And the station chief. And I don't leave rangers behind."

"You said no stops," Wren reminded him.

"This isn't a stop," Ivo said, and for the first time since the breach, since the cathedral, since the long brown road home, she saw something hard settle over his face — the old ranger look, but aimed now at the right people. "This is the last mile. And nobody rides it for us."

Above the station, the black smoke bent eastward on the evening wind, pointing away like a finger. And far off, faint under the river-sound, Wren heard — or thought she heard — the thin electronic whine of a radio carrier wave, switched on, searching the empty air for someone to talk to.
