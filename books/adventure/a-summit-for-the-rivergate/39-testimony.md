## 39. Testimony

The Rivergate station smelled of lamp oil and wet wool when they came down out of the canyon country, forty-one days after we'd launched against the current to find a silent survey.

I had imagined this return a hundred times on the long walk downstream — the questions, the forms, the small bureaucratic machinery that would grind us through its gears and spit us out as either heroes or liars. What I hadn't imagined was how ordinary it would feel. The station was the same low stone building I'd left in high summer, its flagpole leaning two degrees east, its rain barrel half full of green water. The world below the rim had not changed. Only we had.

Wren went first, carried in a litter rigged from boat thwarts and pack straps, her leg splinted and swaddled, her face gray with exhaustion but her eyes sharp as ever. She'd kept the field notebook wedged under her arm the whole way down, even sleeping, even feverish. When the station porters tried to take it with the rest of the gear she nearly took a man's fingers off.

"Chain of custody," she said. "Look it up."

Sable walked beside the litter with her hands free and empty, which was itself a kind of testimony. She'd surrendered the stolen survey documents at the ford below the last rapids, wrapped in oilcloth, sealed with her own mark. She could have run then. The river was fast, the banks were broken country, and no one had the strength to chase her. Instead she'd stood on the shingle and handed the packet to me like a woman returning a borrowed tool.

"They'll want my statement too," she'd said. "All of it. Even the parts that hang me."

"Even those," I'd said.

She'd almost smiled. "Good. I'm tired of carrying them."

* * *

Station Chief Aldous Brann met us on the steps. He was a heavy man gone heavier with worry, and when he saw the size of our party — four instead of nine, one on a litter, three prisoners under guard — his face did something complicated that ended in grief.

"The others?" he asked.

"Voss's downriver party, we don't know," I said. "Three went back with him at Split Currents. We found no sign of them above the Narrows." That wasn't entirely true. We'd found a campsite, cold, and a boot we chose not to speak of yet. "The surveyors are alive. Eleven of them. They're coming down behind us with the hired crew under escort — or what's left of both groups after the cavern."

"And the source?"

Brann asked it quietly, the way you ask about a wound.

"Intact," Wren said from the litter. "And mapped. Properly mapped, for the first time in the history of the service." She held up the notebook. "Everything else is in here too. The diversion channels. The purchase agreements. The poisoning plan. Names, dates, amounts."

Brann looked at the notebook the way a man looks at a snake he knows is venomous but also, somehow, on his side.

"You'd better come inside," he said.

* * *

They gave us three days to eat and sleep before the hearings began, which was three days more than any of us expected and exactly as many as we needed. Sable slept like the dead and ate like a living one. Wren dictated notes to a junior clerk until her voice gave out, then wrote with her own hand, propped up in a cot, her splinted leg elevated on a crate of survey stakes. I sat on the station porch in the evenings and watched the river run brown and swollen with the early rains, carrying things downstream I would never see arrive.

On the fourth morning they convened the inquiry in the station's map room, because it was the only room big enough. Brann presided, though he made it clear from the start that he was recording, not judging — everything would go to the territorial commission at Corvane, and the commission would do what commissions do, which is move slowly and decide less. A stenographer sat at a folding table with a machine I'd only seen in courthouses. Along the walls: rangers, porters' families, a handful of townspeople who'd come up from the lower settlements because a story like ours travels faster than a boat.

Sable testified first.

They'd offered her a chair and she'd taken it, but she sat forward the whole time, elbows on knees, like a woman leaning into weather. She told them about stealing the documents from the Meridian Survey's supply cache — how she'd tracked the expedition for weeks after learning what its financier intended, how she'd read enough of the papers to understand that the source her father died near was being priced like timber. She told them about following the survey upstream, about finding their camps abandoned, about the message scratched on the canteen. She did not soften the theft. She did not excuse it.

"I stole because no one would listen," she said. "I'd written letters for two years. To the commission, to the land office, to the newspapers in Corvane. I got one reply, and it was a form." She looked around the room. "So yes. I broke the law. Write that down. And then write down what the law was being used to hide."

A ranger near the door shifted his weight. Someone in the back said something under their breath that might have been approval.

Then they came to me.

I'd prepared a statement. I'd written it out twice, crossed out the second version, and finally decided to say it plain, the way Sable had taught me without meaning to — the way her father's routes were drawn, direct lines through broken ground.

I told them about the drowning. All of it, for the first time in twenty-two years, spoken aloud to strangers.

"I was eleven years old," I said. "My father ran freight through the upper canyons and I went along that summer. We camped at the edge of the source country — the closed country, above the old boundary cairns. In the night I woke and heard tools. I climbed the terrace above camp and I saw men diverting the outflow. Not surveying. Diverting. Building a channel to pull water off before it ever reached the river, feeding some holding basin upstream of the boundary. And there was a man arguing with them. He wasn't armed. He was just standing in their way, and telling them the water belonged to everyone downstream, and they laughed at him, and then there was a scuffle at the lip of the channel cut, and he went into the water."

The room had gone very quiet. The stenographer's keys had stopped.

"He drowned in water that was moving wrong — diverted, channeled, faster than it should have been. I couldn't have saved him. I was a child and I was fifty yards away and I froze. But I ran afterward. I ran all the way down to camp and I never told anyone what I'd seen, because the men who did it found me first. There were three of them. One of them put a paper in front of me and my father beside me, and explained, very reasonably, that a boy who'd seen nothing had nothing to fear, and a boy who'd seen something needed looking after. The paper was a debt. It said my father owed money — freight losses, contract penalties, numbers I couldn't read. It said the debt passed to me. It said the holder of the debt was a man named Meridian Halloway Investments — the same house that financed the survey." I paused. "Not the same family as Wren Halloway. She checked. She spent a night in a slot canyon checking, because she thought I'd suspect her, and I had."

Wren, at the end of the table, didn't look up from her notebook, but her jaw tightened.

"I carried that paper for twenty-two years," I said. "I joined the rangers because rangers are honest, and I wanted to be near something honest. I never questioned the debt because questioning it meant going back to that night, and I would rather have carried anything than go back." I set the original document on the table — Sable had returned it to me at the ford, along with everything else, saying it was mine to burn or surrender. "This is the forgery. My father's signature is copied from a freight manifest of 1871 — the ink is different, the hand is different, and the freight company it names didn't exist until 1874. Wren Halloway verified the dates against shipping registers. The clerk can enter her affidavit."

Brann entered it. His hands were steady; his voice wasn't quite.

"The debt is voided pending commission review," he said. "Mr. Eastwick, why didn't you report this at the time? You were a child, but you've been an adult a long while."

It was the question I'd been asking myself since Cold Camp, and I gave him the true answer, which was also the hard one.

"Because the debt worked," I said. "That's what it was for. Not the money — nobody ever collected a cent. It was a leash. As long as I believed I owed something shameful, I stayed quiet, and stayed useful, and stayed away from the source country. Whoever wrote that paper bought twenty-two years of my silence for the price of ink. I'd like that entered into the record too. Not as an accusation against the service. As an accusation against the men who used it."

The stenographer's keys started again.

* * *

Voss came back on the sixth day.

We heard the boat horn from the porch — two short blasts, the survey's signal — and I went down to the landing expecting ghosts. Anselm Voss stood in the bow of a battered launch, thinner, sunburned to the color of old brick, with two of his three porters alive behind him. The third they'd lost in the Narrows flood, he said, before anyone could ask. Drowned doing the thing Voss himself had ordered, running a line across the current to fix a datum point.

He came up the steps past me without recognition, and then stopped, and turned, and looked at me for a long moment.

"Eastwick." He said it like a word in a language he'd stopped speaking. "You went on. I told you to turn back."

"You did."

"I was wrong." He looked out at the river. "I spent nine days coming downriver thinking about maps. About how I'd have traded every man on that expedition for a complete contour survey of the upper gorge. That's what I was, Eastwick. That's what they hired, when they hired me. A man who valued the drawing over the thing drawn." His voice caught and steadied. "The men at the source — your chief, the surveyors — they sent word ahead by pigeon from the high terraces. I read the logs they forwarded. The poisoning clause." He shook his head slowly. "I signed a contract with that house. I drew maps for it. I'd have drawn the dam site myself if they'd asked cleanly instead of sending killers."

"They asked dirty because clean wouldn't hold," I said.

"No," Voss agreed. "Clean never holds. That's rather the point of dirt." He straightened, and something of the old captain came back into his spine, but humbled, load-bearing now instead of commanding. "I'll testify. Everything. My contracts, my instructions, the payment schedule, the name of the man who gave me my orders. Put it all in."

"You should know it'll cost you your commission."

"It cost three good men their lives," Voss said. "The commission is cheap."

* * *

The hearings ran eight days.

They were imperfect, the way all justice built by tired people is imperfect. The territorial commission's representative arrived on day three, a dry man named Osgood who asked questions in circles and seemed most interested in whether the service's liability insurance covered flash floods. Two of the financier's hired crew refused to talk; the foreman, Halvard, talked for six hours straight and named names up to the top of the house, and his testimony matched Wren's documents line for line. The survey chief — gaunt, certain, half-ruined by what he'd tried to do at the springs — gave evidence in a flat voice that made the stenographer look up twice, and surrendered his authority formally, on the record, to Wren Halloway's maps.

"There will be trials," Brann told us on the last evening. "In Corvane, properly constituted, with lawyers and delays and appeals. Some of these men will buy their way out. Some will die before sentencing. The house itself may dissolve and reconstitute under a new name, because houses do that." He rubbed his eyes. "But the record exists now. The forgery is voided. The poisoning plan is public. Whatever happens next, it happens in daylight. That's more than most inquiries get."

"More than I expected," I said.

"Don't mistake me," Brann said. "The service comes out of this filthy. We took financing from that house for twelve years. We accepted their maps. We posted rangers to keep people away from the source country on their instruction — your postings, Eastwick, half of them. We were the leash." He looked at me steadily. "Which brings us to you."

* * *

He asked me to stay. That was the shape of it, laid out in his office with the lamp turned low: a promotion, a transfer to Corvane, a place on the commission's new water-rights board. My knowledge of the canyons, my testimony, my name — all of it had value now, and the service wanted to spend it.

"You'd be inside the room," Brann said. "That matters. Men like you inside the room matter more than men like you outside it."

I thought about it honestly, which took most of a night. I sat on the porch with Sable until the lamps went out, and then alone after she went in, watching the river carry the dark downstream.

In the morning I wrote the resignation on station letterhead, in ink, and signed it, and brought it to Brann before the boats left.

He read it standing. "You're sure."

"Yes."

"Give me a reason I can write down."

I'd thought about that too. "Because I served twenty-two years under a lie and called it duty," I said. "Every posting, every patrol, every time I turned a traveler back from the boundary cairns — I told myself it was the law. It was the leash. If I stay, everything I do from now on gets weighed against that, and fairly. But the service doesn't need a man weighing himself. It needs men who were never for sale, and I can't be one of them anymore. I can only be a man who knows exactly what he's choosing." I tapped the paper. "This is what I'm choosing."

Brann signed it. His hand hesitated over the signature — not reluctance, I think, but ceremony, the weight of ending something — and then it was done.

"You'll be back," he said. "People always come back to rivers."

"Maybe," I said. "But not on commission."

* * *

They released Sable on the ninth day. The theft charges weren't dropped — Osgood saw to that, muttering about precedent — but they were suspended, commuted to time served in custody of the inquiry, with Wren's sworn statement that the stolen documents had been instrumental evidence, preserved intact, and returned unaltered. The magistrate who signed the order added a handwritten note that would follow Sable for years, quoted in newspapers and argued over in law schools: *Where the law has been purchased, its violation may be the public's only remedy.*

She came out of the station into late-morning light, blinking, and found me waiting by the landing with two packs.

"They gave you back your compass," I said. Her father's — recovered from the evidence lockup, tagged and catalogued and finally, blessedly, hers.

"They gave me back a lot of things." She slung the pack, tested its weight. "Not all of them. Not the years. Not the camps we burned." She looked at me. "You resigned."

"This morning."

"What will you do?"

I looked upriver, where the water ran high and loud with the rains, where the canyon walls went blue into distance, where a cathedral of living rock held the whole river in its dark hands and let it go. Somewhere downriver, in the towns, the truth we'd washed loose was arriving — the logs, the forged debt, the names — doing its slow work in whatever light reached it.

"I don't know yet," I said. "For the first time in my life, I don't know yet. It feels enormous."

Sable smiled — the real one, the rare one, the one I'd crossed a desert and climbed a cliff face to earn. She turned her father's compass over once in her palm, not to read it, just to feel it.

"Well," she said, shouldering past me toward the trail that led south, out of the canyon country, onto the open flats where the horizon ran unbroken in every direction. "Come on, then. Let's go find out."

I followed her. I carried nothing I hadn't chosen.
