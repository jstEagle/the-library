## 40. Open Country

The last morning in the Rivergate came gray and soft, the kind of light that made the canyon walls look like they were still deciding whether to be stone or smoke.

Ivo woke before the others, as he always did, and lay in his bedroll listening to the river. It had changed its voice overnight. All the way down from the source country it had been rising, day by day, the dry season breaking at last in slow gray rains that came without wind, without drama, just the sky finally giving back what it had taken. Now the river spoke with a full throat against the rocks below the station, and the sound of it was so ordinary and so enormous that Ivo lay still for a long time just to hear it.

Forty-one days since he'd walked into the Rivergate with a warrant in his pocket and a debt in his chest. It felt like another man's lifetime.

He rose, folded his bedroll with the crisp economy of twenty years' habit, and then caught himself doing it and stopped. There was no one to inspect the camp. No station chief waiting on a report. No commission to be stripped from him, because he had already laid it on Voss's desk two nights ago, and Voss had taken it with a strange, almost relieved gentleness, the way a man accepts a resignation he'd been too proud to ask for.

The warrant was gone too. He'd watched it burn in a cook fire above the trees, along with the debt papers, and he had not yet decided whether he trusted the feeling of that. Trusting good feelings was a skill he'd never had occasion to learn.

He stepped out of the tent into the wet-smelling morning.

The station yard was mud and trampled grass. The Meridian Survey's people were loading what remained of their gear onto two flatboats, working in the heavy silence of people who had seen too much together to talk about any of it. The chief — gaunt still, his certainty burned out of him and something rawer left in its place — stood at the water's edge directing the loading with a voice that had lost all its command and hadn't yet found a replacement. He'd surrendered his authority to Wren's maps, all of them, the honest ones and the erased ones, and Wren had accepted the burden the way she accepted everything: quietly, and with her jaw set like she was daring the world to make it heavier.

Wren herself sat on a crate by the boathouse, her wounded leg propped on a second crate, a drafting board across her knees. She was drawing. Of course she was drawing. Even now, even with the bandage and the fever finally broken, her pencil moved in small precise strokes, and Ivo knew without looking that she was redrawing the approaches to the source — the routes that had been scrubbed from the official maps on someone's orders, years ago, by her own hand. Putting them back. Every line a small act of repair.

She looked up as he passed.

"You're really not coming down with us?" she said. "The boats go all the way to Meridian Landing. There's a train. There are beds."

"There are also hearings," Ivo said. "Three weeks of them, if the foreman's testimony runs long."

"You're a witness. They can't start without you."

"They can wait for me to walk." He looked downriver, toward the towns, the courts, the whole apparatus of justice that would grind through the financier's empire and the survey's complicity and the forged debt that had bought a boy's silence twenty years ago. It would grind slowly, and imperfectly, and some of the men responsible would walk away with fines and some would walk away with nothing. He'd made his peace with imperfect. "I've spent my whole life going where I was sent. I'd like to try going where I choose, just once, before I get old enough to regret it."

Wren studied him for a moment. Then she tore a corner from her drafting paper, sketched something in three strokes, and held it out.

It was the source. The cathedral cavern, the spring rising from living rock — drawn small and clean and true.

"So there's one honest copy that isn't going into any archive," she said. "In case the world decides to forget again."

Ivo took it. His throat did something he chose not to examine. "Thank you, Wren."

"Don't thank me. Fold it somewhere it won't get wet." She went back to her drawing, and then, as he turned away: "Ivo. The maps I erased — the men who ordered it — they'll try again. Maybe not this year. But water like that, someone will always come for it."

"I know," Ivo said. "That's why the honest copies matter."

* * *

Sable was at the corral, and she was arguing with a mule.

He stopped to watch, because watching Sable Corren argue with a mule was one of the small pleasures the last month had taught him to allow himself. The mule was the station's, gray and opinionated, and it had decided — as mules did — that it was not going. Sable stood in front of it with a lead rope in one hand and her father's compass in her pocket, and she was not pulling, not striking, not bargaining. She was simply standing, patient as stone, letting the mule arrive at the conclusion on its own time.

It took four more minutes. Then the mule sighed the way mules do, all the way down through its whole body, and followed her.

"You could have ridden a boat," Ivo said.

"Boats go where rivers go." She handed him the lead rope. "Rivers go where they're told, eventually. I've had enough of that for one season."

He took the rope. The mule looked at him with deep suspicion, then apparently decided he was the lesser evil, and began eating the corral fence.

They'd talked about this, in the way that the important things between them had stopped needing long conversations. Downriver was testimony and trains and a room with walls. Out there — he nodded at it now, the canyon mouth, the gray light beyond — was the open country, the flats, the salt, the long nothing where he'd once tracked a thief with a compass and a paper of debt and called it purpose. The desert had no jurisdiction. It had no debt either. It only had distance, and weather, and whatever two people decided to make of it.

"You're sure," Sable said. Not a question, really. She asked it anyway, the way she'd learned to ask him things — giving him the door, every time, so he could walk through it on his own legs.

"I've never been sure of anything," Ivo said. "That's the point."

* * *

They left the Rivergate an hour after the boats, walking, the mule carrying what little they had.

What little they had was not much. A bedroll each. A water skin. A small pot and a frying pan that Sable refused to leave behind because, she said, a person who owns a pan is a person with intentions. Dried meat and meal for six days, after which the land itself would have to provide, and Sable knew the land. A tin of tea. The mule.

And the compass — her father's compass, brass-cased, the needle worn loose in its pivot so it swung a half-beat behind true north, always late, always catching up. She'd carried it since the Narrows. She'd carried it, Ivo suspected, since before the Narrows, since before any of this, the way he had once carried his debt papers: not because it weighed anything, but because of what it meant.

Except that hers meant something true.

They walked out through the last of the canyon in the late morning, the walls falling away on either side like hands opening, and then there were no walls at all, and the country opened.

Ivo had crossed these flats a hundred times. He knew them in every light — the white blaze of noon, the violet dusk, the moonlit silver where a track showed like a vein of lead. He knew where the water was, the seeps and the tanks and the one spring that never failed, and he knew the silence of the place the way a man knows his own heartbeat: without thinking about it, and never without it.

But he had never walked out of the canyon like this.

Always before, he had come out of the Rivergate *into* something — a pursuit, a patrol, a delivery, a duty. The flats had been a corridor between obligations. The horizon had been a deadline. Now he stood at the canyon mouth with nothing behind him that anyone could call due, and nothing ahead that anyone could call owed, and the flats ran out ahead of him for forty miles to the broken hills, and the sky went on past that forever, and he found that he did not know what to do with it.

He stood there longer than he meant to.

Sable let him. That was another thing the last month had taught her, or maybe she'd always known it — when to stand beside a man and say nothing while he looked at the country and let it look back at him.

"It's bigger than I remembered," he said finally.

"It's the same size it always was." She shaded her eyes. "You were smaller."

He laughed. It came up out of him rough, from disuse, and the mule started and then settled, and the sound went out over the flats and didn't come back, and that was fine. Some things didn't need to come back.

They walked.

* * *

The first day out was the easiest day Ivo had spent in twenty years, and also the strangest.

He kept catching himself doing it — the old arithmetic. *Water at the seep by the split rock, twelve miles. Shade gone from the west wall by midafternoon. If they made the tank by dark they'd — * and then stopping, because there was no *they* above him, no schedule, no report due at the station by Friday. The numbers were still there, all his competence, all his craft, but they answered to nothing now except the weather and the two of them. It was like setting down a pack he'd worn so long his shoulders had grown around it. He kept reaching for the weight that wasn't there.

Sable navigated. She didn't ask; she simply did it, the way her father must have done it, reading the country the way other people read letters — the lean of a juniper telling her where the wind lived, a line of darker stone telling her where the water ran under the sand. She led them to a seep by midafternoon that Ivo had never found in all his years of ranging, a thread of cold water falling down a shaded face of rock, invisible from fifty feet, marked on no map because no mapmaker had ever been shown it.

"Family mark," she said, and showed him: a small spiral pecked into the stone beside the seep, old enough that lichen had softened its edges. "My grandfather cut it. His father cut one at the next water east. There are eleven of them between here and the terraces. My father said it was a map that couldn't be stolen." She touched the spiral once, lightly, the way you'd touch a shoulder. "The survey's maps got burned and forged and erased. These are still here."

Ivo thought of Wren's torn corner of paper in his breast pocket — the source, drawn honest and small. "Some maps you carry," he said. "Some you become."

Sable looked at him sideways. "That's almost worth writing down."

"Don't tell Wren I said something worth writing down. She'll never let me hear the end of it."

They filled the skins, watered the mule, and walked on into the long gold light of late afternoon, and Ivo found that the arithmetic had gone quiet in him at last. Not gone — he'd need it, tomorrow, and all the tomorrows — but quiet. Off duty. The flats unrolled, and the wind came out of the west smelling of rain that had already fallen somewhere far away, and for the first time since he was a boy he was moving through the desert without being moved by anything but his own will and the company he'd chosen.

It was terrifying. He didn't say so. But Sable glanced at him once, and something in her face said she recognized it — the vertigo of the unowned day — and that she was feeling it too, and that they would be terrified together, which made it not terrifying at all.

* * *

They camped that night in the open, no canyon wall, no rimrock, just the flats and the first stars and the mule dozing on its feet with the lead rope slack between it and a stunted greasebush.

Sable built a small fire — small out of habit, though there was no one to see it now — and made tea, and they sat with their shoulders touching, watching the light go out of the world the way it does on the flats: all at once, and then slowly, and then all at once again.

"Tell me what you're thinking," she said. "The true one. Not the ranger report."

Ivo considered. He had gotten better at this — at the true one — but it still cost him something, every time, like lifting a stone to see what lived under it.

"I'm thinking about the man I was six weeks ago," he said. "Walking across salt with a warrant and a debt, tracking you. I was good at it. I want you to know that. I was *good*. Best tracker the service had, and I knew it, and I was proud of it, and the whole time I was — " He turned the tin cup in his hands. "Hollow. That's the word. I told myself it was purpose. Duty, debt, the work. But it was just weight. Weight isn't purpose. I didn't know the difference until I lost both in the same week."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sitting next to a woman who stole documents and led me up a river to a place no one's permitted to see, and I'm not taking her anywhere, and no one is taking me anywhere, and the fire is small and the tea is bad and I've never been less hollow in my life." He looked at her. "That's the true one."

Sable was quiet for a while. Out on the flats, a nightjar called, once, twice, and the river — far behind them now, but not out of earshot — kept up its low full-throated talk to itself, carrying everything downstream: the logs, the forgeries, the testimony, the truth, all of it moving toward the towns at the speed of water, which is to say at the speed of inevitability.

"My father used to say the desert doesn't forgive," she said. "He was wrong. It doesn't *judge*. There's a difference. It just makes room. All this room." She swept a hand at the dark horizon. "Room enough for a man who spent twenty years carrying a lie, and a woman who spent ten carrying a grudge, and a mule with no opinions worth respecting. It doesn't ask what we were. It just asks where we'll walk tomorrow."

"And what do we tell it?"

She leaned her head against his shoulder. Her hair smelled of woodsmoke and river water and the cold high country they'd come down from, and he felt the question land in his chest and settle there, not as weight — he was done with weight — but as something with a direction, like current.

"Tomorrow," Sable said, "we walk east. There's a place where the terraces come down to the flats, and a spring with my family's mark on it, and grass enough for the mule, and stone enough to build a roof. It'll take years to make it a home. My father always said the best things take years."

"Years," Ivo said. He tried the word out. It fit in his mouth like water fits in a dry streambed — finding every low place, filling them all. "I've got nothing scheduled."

She laughed against his shoulder, and the fire settled, and above them the stars came out in their thousands, in their tens of thousands, all the way down to the rim of the world.

* * *

They walked out at first light, when the flats were pink and cold and their shadows ran ahead of them a hundred feet long, thin as blades.

Ivo carried the bedroll, the pan, the water skins, the torn corner of Wren's map folded in oilcloth over his heart. Everything he carried, he had chosen to carry, and the difference between that and everything he'd carried before was the difference between a burden and a life, and he felt it in every step, and he would never stop noticing it, and he never wanted to stop noticing it.

Sable walked beside him with her father's compass in her hand, not using it, just holding it, the loose needle swinging and settling, swinging and settling, always a half-beat behind true north and always catching up.

"Which way?" he asked, though he knew.

She didn't check the compass. She looked at the horizon — the long unbroken line of it where the hills rose blue out of the morning haze, and beyond the hills the country neither of them had ever had leave to love, and beyond that the edge of seeing, and beyond that whatever they would make.

"That way," Sable said, and pointed, and they went.
