## 1. Weather Holds

The storm came down the valley like something with intent.

Vesper Joubert watched it build from eleven hundred feet, one eye on the gray wall swallowing the northern peaks, the other on the river unspooling below him — a brown ribbon stitched through green, braiding and rebraiding around gravel bars that would not be there next month. The Cessna's windshield wiper ticked against the first fat drops. He didn't like the wipers. They were an admission that the weather had gotten inside your decisions.

"Come on, old girl," he said to the plane, because there was no one else to say it to, and because after nine years she'd earned the courtesy of being spoken to like a crew member rather than a machine.

The manifest in his jacket pocket said: *Two cases insulin, cold chain intact. One crate surgical supplies. Consignee: Kettle River Camp, mile 214.* The manifest did not say what Vesper already knew from ten years of flying this country — that the man waiting at mile 214 was probably dead by now, or close enough, and that the insulin mattered less than someone upriver believing the medicine still came. That was the real cargo, most flights. Not the boxes. The belief.

He keyed the radio out of habit more than hope. "Kettle River Camp, Joubert Air, inbound from the south, twenty minutes out. Anyone reading?"

Static answered him, then a voice frayed almost past recognition. "*...reading you, Joubert. Barely. Wind's coming downriver at your back if you land northbound. You'll want—*" The transmission dissolved into a hiss like water on hot iron.

"I heard you," Vesper told the static. He always talked back. Another habit he'd have denied under oath.

He brought her in the way he'd been taught by a man whose name he no longer used — slow, high, flaring into the wind-shadow of the bluff where the camp's strip hid between two walls of spruce. The gusts hit sideways at fifty feet and he rode them with small corrections, wrist and ankle, patient as a man mending a net. The wheels touched, bounced once, touched again, and rolled out onto grass that whispered against the fuselage.

Rain arrived in earnest before he'd finished chocking the tires.

* * *

The camp at mile 214 was twelve structures arranged along the bank like teeth in a loose jaw: drying racks, stilt houses, a longhouse with a tin roof drumming under the rain, and a dock where two canoes knocked together restlessly, wanting to be somewhere else. A dog announced him before he reached the first house, then decided against commitment and followed at a distance, interested but not invested.

Marta Okonkwo met him under the eave of the dispensary, a broad woman in her sixties with a nurse's hands and a fisherman's squint. She took the insulin case without thanks, cracked it, and pressed a thermometer strip against the second vial with the efficiency of someone who had caught suppliers lying before.

"Cold chain's good," she admitted, as if it cost her something.

"It usually is."

"You were low over the Narrows." She said it the way other people said *you were speeding*. "In this sky."

"The Narrows are fine in this sky. It's the pass behind them that lies."

She snorted. Inside the dispensary, the smell of carbolic and woodsmoke, a kerosene lamp already lit against the early dark. Two children with infected ears. An elder with a cough that Marta clearly didn't like and couldn't name. And in the corner cot, a man Vesper recognized distantly — one of the Kettle brothers, the family that gave the camp its name — laid out under blankets with his leg wrapped to the knee.

"How is he?" Vesper asked, nodding at the cot.

"Gangrenous by Thursday unless I get him downriver to a hospital." Marta repacked the insulin into the camp's icebox with the tenderness she showed no living person. "Which means a boat, which means the river, which means—" she jerked her chin at the window, where the rain had thickened into sheets, "—that."

"I could take him tomorrow, if the weather holds."

"If the weather holds," Marta repeated, and looked at him with an expression he'd learned to recognize across a thousand miles of river country: the look of a woman deciding how much truth a stranger could carry. "You never stay, do you, Joubert. You fly in, you fly out. Ten years, maybe more, and nobody in any camp from here to the delta knows where you sleep twice."

"I sleep in the plane."

"That's not sleeping. That's hiding with extra steps."

Vesper smiled the smile he kept for exactly this conversation — the one that ended it. "I'll be on the strip at first light. If the ceiling's above five hundred feet, I'll take your patient down to Halfway Landing. If it's not, I'll come back the day after, and the day after that, until it is."

"And then you're gone again."

"And then I'm gone again."

She shook her head, but she wrote the flight promise in her ledger, because a promise written down was a promise that could be collected, and everyone on this river knew Vesper Joubert paid his debts even if he wouldn't tell you his father's name.

* * *

Supper was fish and rice in the longhouse, eaten among people who spoke a language Vesper knew only in fragments — enough to trade, enough to laugh at the right moments, not enough to be caught in anything deeper. This was deliberate. He'd learned the fragments the way a man learns the shallow crossings of a river: well enough to get across, never so well that he'd try to swim.

It was during supper that the talk turned to charts.

An older man named Etienne — half Kettle by marriage, half something else by temperament — had come up from Halfway Landing two days before with mail and diesel parts, and he carried news the way other men carried cargo, distributed evenly so nothing shifted. "Buyers in town," he said, tearing bread. "Quiet ones. Paying cash for old survey charts, river maps, anything with soundings on it. Even photographs. My cousin sold a folder of his grandfather's notes for more than the grandfather ever made trapping."

"Salvage men?" someone asked.

"Salvage money," Etienne corrected. "Different animal. Salvage men work wrecks. Salvage *money* buys everything around the wreck first — the charts, the claims, the silence. There's a fleet coming up from the delta. Dredges. Big ones. Word is they've got company title to every wreck on the lower hundred miles already, stamped and filed, and they're working their way up buying paper for the rest."

The rain hammered the tin roof. Someone refilled cups. Vesper ate his fish and let the words wash past him the way he let most things wash past him, which was a skill, and also a wound dressed as a skill.

"Joubert," Etienne said suddenly, and Vesper felt the name land in the room like a dropped tool. "You know anything about salvage? Your people were—"

"My people flew medicine," Vesper said pleasantly, "and before that they were nobody worth charting."

It wasn't quite a lie. It was more like a map with a region left blank — and he'd drawn it himself, carefully, over years, until even he sometimes navigated by it.

But Etienne was watching him with the particular patience of old men who had outlived the need to press. "There was a ship lost up here once," he said. "Before your time, maybe. The Meridian Star. Company vessel, went down with a season's cargo and a dozen men's fortunes aboard. Some say the river took her. Some say otherwise." He shrugged. "If a fleet's buying charts, maybe somebody thinks the river gave her back."

"Rivers don't give things back," Vesper said. "They just move them around and pretend they lost them."

That got a laugh, and the moment passed, and Vesper told himself the small cold feeling under his ribs was just the weather coming in through the door.

* * *

He slept in the plane, as advertised, in the back seat with the seats folded flat, a sleeping bag rated for colder than this, and the rain on the aluminum roof of the world keeping its own rhythm above him. He slept badly, as he always did when a place started to feel familiar. Familiarity was the first symptom. Staying was the disease. He'd watched it kill his father — not quickly, not dramatically, but the way rot kills a hull, quietly, from a compartment nobody inspected. His father had stayed. Had built, had signed, had believed that a name and a fleet and a stretch of river were things a man could hold. And the river had taken all three, and the name had gone into the current after them, and Vesper had been seventeen years old learning that the only thing you can't lose is the thing you refuse to pick up.

So he picked up nothing. He flew medicine and he paid his debts and he kept moving, and if there was a loneliness in it, he had learned to wear it the way bush pilots wore their jackets — layered, broken in, assumed rather than discussed.

At first light the ceiling sat at eight hundred feet and falling, and Marta stood on the strip with the Kettle brother on a stretcher tarp and a face like a closed fist.

"You'll take him?"

"I'll take him."

"He doesn't do well with flying. Give him the bucket before you're wheels-up, not after."

"Noted."

They loaded the man, who groaned once with dignity and then endured the flight in silence, which Vesper respected. The weather held exactly long enough — the way weather does when it has an appointment elsewhere — and he put them down at Halfway Landing's strip with the rain arriving thirty seconds behind him like a creditor who'd missed the payment window by a breath.

Halfway Landing was what it sounded like: the place between places, a river town built on the assumption that everyone there was passing through. A fuel dock. A repair shed with a windsock gone gray as dishwater. A trading post, a church that doubled as a dance hall, and a hotel with rooms rented by the hour to people who mostly weren't sleeping. Vesper had stopped there perhaps forty times in ten years. He could not have told you the color of the hotel door.

He handed the stretcher off to a young orderly, settled accounts with the clinic clerk, and walked through the rain toward the trading post to buy coffee and fuel filters and to leave, ideally, within the hour.

That was when he saw the notices.

They were pasted on the board outside the post, fresh paper under fresh staples, four of them in a neat row, each bearing a crest he hadn't seen in fifteen years and recognized the way you recognize a scar in a mirror. A stylized star over crossed dredge hooks. Beneath it, in type too clean for this country:

*NOTICE OF CLAIM. All submerged vessels, cargoes, and appurtenances within surveyed waters of the Lower Reach are the lawful property of Meridian Recovery Group, Ltd. Salvage without license will be prosecuted.*

Meridian Recovery Group. He read the name twice. Somebody had bought the ghost of his father's company — or worn its skin — and filed papers on the river's drowned dead as though the river were a warehouse and grief a lien.

Behind him, two deckhands waited out the rain under the post's awning, talking low.

"—bought the Henderson charts Tuesday. Cash. Old man Henderson didn't even haggle."

"They're not after charts. They're after *one* chart. That's the talk. Something went down years back that the company never wrote off proper, and now the grandson's come back to finish the arithmetic."

"Whose grandson?"

The deckhand glanced at the notices, then at the river, then lowered his voice further, and the rain kindly covered most of it. Vesper caught only the last of it:

"—Corvin Joubert. And they say he's asking after anyone named Joubert, too. Like he's looking for somebody."

The rain came down. The windsock hung limp and gray. Vesper stood very still in front of the notice board, a man with a full fuel tank and a clear exit, holding a coffee going cold in his hand, and found — to his mild professional irritation — that he wasn't moving.

Somewhere upriver, at a trapping cabin he was in no hurry to reach, a dying man he hadn't met yet was sitting on a secret with Vesper's family name written all over it. But Vesper didn't know that yet. All he knew was that the weather was turning, the notices were fresh, and for the first time in ten years, the rule about never staying anywhere too long had begun, quietly, to argue with itself.

He drained the coffee, folded the collar of his jacket up, and went to find out who in Halfway Landing could patch an engine fast — because whatever was coming up that river, he intended to meet it moving.

The engine, as it happened, had other plans.
