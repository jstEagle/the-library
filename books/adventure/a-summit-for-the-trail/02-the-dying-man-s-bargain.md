## 2. The Dying Man's Bargain

The cabin announced itself a mile before I saw it, the way dying things do. Smoke that was too thin, rising from a stove someone no longer had the strength to feed. A dog — or what had been a dog — lying flat in the yard with its chin on its paws, watching my plane circle twice before I set it down on the frozen slough like it was asking permission.

I hadn't planned to land at all. The medicine run was finished; the insulin was in the hands of the river camp two hundred miles south, and the weather window behind me was closing the way windows do up here in October — not gradually but all at once, like a door slammed by an impatient hand. My plan was to climb over the divide before the front arrived and be drinking bad coffee in Halfway Landing by dark.

But there was smoke from a cabin that shouldn't have any, and a dog too weak to bark, and a woodpile stacked high and untouched since first snow. Ten years of flying the interior teaches you to read a place the way a tracker reads snow. This one read wrong.

I told myself it was the wind. That I'd landed because the crosswind on the divide pass would be ugly and the slough ice was thick enough to hold the wheels. Both true. Neither the reason.

* * *

The man inside was propped against the wall of his bunk like a coat hung on a nail, and he'd been there long enough that the blanket over his legs had worn through at both knees. Sixty, maybe seventy — hard to tell with river men, who age like driftwood, grayed and checked by every season they've stood in. His face was the color of tallow. One leg was wrapped in strips of bedsheet gone brown at the ankle, and the smell coming off the wrapping was the sweet-rotten smell I knew from war surplus field manuals and from one bad winter delivering penicillin to a camp that hadn't gotten it in time.

"Plane," he said, when I ducked through the door. Not a question. Just naming the thing, the way a man names what he sees when he's not sure he'll see it again.

"You're Amos Kettle." I'd heard the name at the river camps for years, always the same way: *old Kettle still traps the upper bends, old Kettle won't sell his charts to nobody.* "Your leg's infected."

"It's been infected since August." He said it without complaint, the way you'd report the weather. "You got medicine in that plane?"

"Some."

"Then you wasted some coming here. Nothing in your kit touches this." He lifted the blanket an inch, looked at his own leg with the mild curiosity of a man checking a trapline, and let it fall. "I know what it is. I've known since the fever started talking to me."

I crouched by the stove anyway and fed it two splits of birch, because the room was cold enough to see my breath and because doing something with my hands bought me time. The cabin was clean in the way of a man who'd kept order until he couldn't: traps hung in rows, mended nets coiled on pegs, a rifle above the door with the bolt working freely when I checked it out of habit. On the table, weighted down with a tin cup, lay a roll of oilcloth tied with waxed string.

"I'll fly you out," I said. "Halfway Landing has a nurse. She's not much, but she's something."

"Took the fever three days to get from my knee to my chest." He shook his head slowly, like the motion cost him. "It'll take another day to reach whatever it reaches next. Your plane does two hundred miles an hour and it wouldn't beat it. I'm not being brave, boy. I'm being arithmetic."

I didn't argue, because he was right, and because I'd done this arithmetic before, once, over a different camp, and lost.

"What about the dog?"

"He'll stay with me. We understand each other." For the first time something moved in his face that wasn't pain. "Sit down, Joubert."

I hadn't given him my name. I hadn't signed the flight plan with anything but initials, and the camps called me nothing at all — just *the pilot*, which suited me fine. I stayed standing.

"Word comes upriver," Amos said, watching me with eyes that were still clear while everything below them failed. "Pilot flies the medicine runs. Won't take payment. Won't stay the night. Won't say his name. But a man who handles a yoke like that — light hands, heavy judgment — well." He tapped his temple. "There was another Joubert flew the river once. Same hands."

"That man's dead."

"No," Amos said. "He's worse than dead. He's remembered."

* * *

I gave him the morphine anyway. Two tablets from a bottle meant for a logging crew's broken backs, washed down with water I heated on the stove because he couldn't lift the kettle himself anymore. It wouldn't touch the infection, as he'd said, but it would touch the pain, and there is a difference between easing a man's body and easing his dignity, and I have never been sure which one matters more.

While the tablets did their slow work he talked. Not about himself — I learned more about Amos Kettle from the mended nets than from his mouth — but about the river. Where the channel had jumped course after the flood year. Which bars were gravel and which were silt wearing a gravel coat. How the dredge companies bought charts from starving trappers and then burned the originals so no one could check their work. He talked like a man putting his affairs in order, except his affairs were the river itself.

Then, near dusk, with the light going copper through the frost-furred window, he said: "Untie the oilcloth on the table."

I untied it. Inside was a sheet of drafting linen, hand-drawn, dense with notation in a small upright hand — soundings, bearings, channel marks, and along one bend of the river, circled twice and annotated in ink gone brown with age, a symbol I recognized from salvage lore the way you recognize a ghost in a photograph: the mark of the *Meridian Star*.

I stood very still. Outside, the wind had begun to arrive ahead of the front, worrying the eaves.

"Every man on this river has heard of her," I said carefully. "Nobody's found her in thirty years."

"Because everybody looks where she's supposed to be." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, fever-bright. "Charts lie, boy. Rivers move. Men move them, sometimes, on purpose." He held out his hand — steady, I noticed, steadier than mine felt. "Bring it here."

I brought it. He laid it across his ruined legs and put his finger on the circled mark, and for a moment the fever receded from his face and left behind something older and harder: a man keeping an appointment.

"Here's my bargain," he said. "I'm dying on terms I chose, and I don't need your pity and I don't need your morphine, though I'll thank you for both. What I need is a promise. You take this map. You find her. And whatever's in her hold — you deliver it. Not to yourself. Not to your family, God help you. To the people she was stolen from. The river people. Their claims, their money, their truth — whatever's in that hold belongs to them, and it's been underwater long enough."

"Why me? You don't know me."

"I know your hands," he said. "And I know your name, and I know what that name did on this river, and I figure a son might want to know what his father drowned." His finger came off the map. "That's the whole price. One promise. Deliver what's hers to the people it was stolen from. Say it plain, or fold the map back up and walk out, and I'll die content anyway, because I asked."

* * *

Here is what I should have done. Folded the oilcloth, set it on the table, told him I'd send the nurse from Halfway Landing with the morning mail plane, and climbed into the sky where I lived. Here is what the smart version of me — the version that had spent ten years perfecting the art of leaving — would have done.

Instead I sat down on the stool across from him, and the stool creaked, and the dog lifted its head, and outside the wind found its full voice in the spruce.

"I can't promise to find her," I said. "Maps rot. Rivers lie even when they don't mean to. I can promise to try."

"Trying is what a man says when he means to quit politely." But there was no anger in it. "All right. Try. But the delivery part — that part you promise whole. Whatever's in that hold goes to the river people. Sworn."

I looked at the map. Hand-drawn soundings in a dead man's handwriting, a shipwreck circled twice, and beneath the circle, in letters so small I had to lean close: *she carries what was taken.*

I thought about my father's fleet going down, the company folding, the name I'd spent a decade sanding off myself. I thought about how a man learns to stop anywhere too long, and how the trick of it is never to accept anything, because everything accepted is an anchor.

"Sworn," I said. "Whatever's in her hold goes to the people it was stolen from. On my word."

Amos Kettle closed his eyes. When he opened them again the fever had come back up behind them like water rising in a lock, but he was smiling.

"There," he said softly. "Now neither of us is alone with it."

* * *

I stayed the night. It was the first night I'd stayed anywhere in four months, and I told myself the front made it necessary, which was true, and that the dog needed feeding, which was also true, and that a dying man shouldn't sit alone with a map and a bad leg, which was truest of all and the one I admitted last.

Through the small hours the fever took him up and set him down, took him up and set him down, and between waves he talked in fragments — deckhand's talk, thirty years old. *She rode low leaving the yard. Manifests sealed before loading, which isn't done. Your father's voice through the speaking tube, calm as Sunday.* I pretended to sleep and listened to all of it, and each fragment fit somewhere against the wreckage of the story I'd grown up on, the way a splinter fits against a nerve.

Toward dawn he surfaced long enough to grip my wrist. His hand was hot as a stove lid.

"The dry season," he said. "Three weeks, maybe less. River drops. She'll be reachable for exactly as long as the water stays low, and no longer. And you won't be the only one looking — the dredges are buying up every chart on the river, and they work for men who'd burn that map rather than read it." His grip tightened. "Deliver it, Joubert. Promise me again."

"You have it already."

"Again," he said. "A dying man gets to hear his promises twice."

So I said it again, plain, word for word, and he let go of my wrist and slept, and near sunrise the sleeping changed quality — went quiet and finished, the way ice goes solid — and I sat with him until the light came gray through the window and the dog put its head on my knee and sighed.

I buried him that afternoon above the high-water line, under a spruce whose roots would hold the ground for a hundred years, and marked the grave with his own adze handle because he had nothing else and would have wanted nothing else. The dog watched from the porch and did not follow, and I understood that too.

Then I folded Amos Kettle's map into my jacket, over my heart where the wind couldn't take it, walked out onto the ice, and turned the plane south toward Halfway Landing — toward parts, and fuel, and a salvage fleet I'd spent ten years pretending I didn't share blood with.

The engine caught on the second try. I remember thinking, as the slough fell away beneath me, that I had just made the longest commitment of my adult life to a dead man, and that the strange part wasn't the promise.

The strange part was that I meant it.
