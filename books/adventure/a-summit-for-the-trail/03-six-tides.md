## 3. Six Tides

The fever came and went like the tide, and Amos Kettle had lived on the river long enough to know how to read it. When it rose, he went somewhere else — back to the Meridian Star's deck, forty years young, rope burns on his palms and a whole life ahead of him. When it fell, he was an old man lying in a trapping cabin that smelled of spruce pitch and mouse droppings, listening to a stranger move around his kitchen because Amos had traded him a map for a promise.

The stranger's name was Joubert. That was the whole joke of it, the whole cruel architecture of the thing, and Amos had laughed until the laughing turned to coughing when he'd first worked it out. Of all the pilots on all the rivers, the one who'd landed on his strip of mud was the son of Elias Joubert and the nephew of Corvin Joubert, the two men who stood on either side of the worst thing Amos had ever seen.

He'd tested the boy first. Asked his name and watched him flinch. Asked what brought him north and watched him lie, badly, about medicine runs and fuel stops. But he'd also watched him splint Amos's leg with a ski pole and a strip of canvas, and heat water without being asked, and sit through the night without once checking his watch. A man could lie about his name and still be honest with his hands. Amos had decided that was enough. It had to be enough. There wasn't time for anything else.

* * *

On the second day, the fever fell and left him clear-headed, the way a storm leaves the air scrubbed and ringing. Vesper Joubert was at the table with the map spread out under the lamp, and Amos watched him study it and knew the boy was seeing only ink — lines and depths and a red X drawn with a steady hand forty years ago and traced over with a shaking one last month.

"You're reading it wrong," Amos said.

Vesper looked up. He had his father's face, God help him. The same long jaw, the same way of holding still when he was thinking, like a heron over shallow water. "Then correct me."

"The river's not where it was." Amos pushed himself up on his elbows, and the cabin tilted, and he waited for it to come back. "You're looking at that channel like it's a road. It's not. It's a scar. The river jumped course in the flood of 'sixty-one — you weren't born, I barely was. It cut a new channel through the willow flats overnight and left the old bend sitting there like a drained bathtub. Everything the current used to carry, it stopped carrying. And everything the current used to hide—" he tapped the map, "—it started burying."

Vesper's eyes went back to the map, and this time Amos could see the change come over him, the ink becoming water. "The oxbow."

"Now you're reading it. Dry season comes, the river drops six, eight feet. It drops every year, but this year the snowpack was thin and the spring was dry, and the old-timers are saying it'll be the lowest in a decade. Low enough that the oxbow goes to mud and silt, low enough that a man with a dredge and a winch and no fear of drowning could dig down to her." Amos lay back, spent by the speech. "Six tides. Maybe seven. That's how long the window stays open before the rains come and close it for another year."

"And after that?"

"After that she's under twelve feet of water and forty of silt, and nobody alive knows where to dig." He looked at the boy. "Except you."

Vesper didn't answer right away. He rolled the map along its old creases, careful, the way you handle a thing that matters. "Why me? You could have taken this to any salvage outfit. You could have sold it."

"To a salvage outfit." Amos laughed, and it hurt. "Boy, the salvage outfits already know something's in that river. That's the problem. That's the whole problem."

He made Vesper help him sit up for this part, propped against the headboard with the quilt bunched behind him, because he wanted to be looking down at the map when he said it, the way you'd look down at a body.

"Three weeks back, before my leg, I was trading at Two Rivers. There was a man there with company paper and a briefcase full of money, buying up old charts. Not surveying. Buying. Paying trappers and guides ten times what a chart's worth, anything with soundings on the middle river, anything older than the flood. And buying silence, too — a bonus if you'd sign a paper saying you hadn't seen anything worth charting." Amos watched the boy's face. "You know whose paper it was?"

Vesper was very still. "No."

"You do. You've just spent ten years pretending you don't. It's Joubert Salvage. It's your uncle's fleet, boy. Corvin's dredges are coming upriver, and they're not coming for the wrecks everybody knows about. Those are picked clean. They're coming because somebody told Corvin that Amos Kettle came out of the bush with a story about the Meridian Star, and Corvin Joubert has spent his whole life making sure that ship stays found by nobody."

* * *

The fever took him again that evening, and in the fever he was young.

He was nineteen and barefoot on the deck plates, and the Meridian Star sat low in the water at her moorings with her cargo hatches battened, and Elias Joubert was walking the deck at midnight with a lantern he kept shaded, and Amos was supposed to be asleep in the fo'c'sle but he'd come up to smoke and instead he'd hidden behind the winch housing and watched.

He watched Elias take the papers out of the oilskin packet one last time. He watched him look upriver, toward the settlements, toward the people whose claims those papers proved — and Amos had never seen a man look at anything the way Elias looked at those papers, like they were a wound he was deciding whether to cauterize. And then he watched him seal them in the drum, and lower the drum into the hold, and give the order in a voice Amos had never heard him use, flat and final as a headstone: *Scuttle her. Now, before the fog lifts.*

He'd told himself for forty years that he'd been a boy, that there'd been nothing he could do, that a deckhand's word against a company owner's was a candle against a flood. He'd told himself that when the river communities started losing their claims, one by one, to paper they'd never signed. He'd told himself that when Corvin Joubert's dredges started working the river like a man searching a house he'd robbed and couldn't remember which room the money was in.

He was done telling himself things. That was what the leg and the fever and the map were for. That was what the boy in his kitchen was for.

* * *

On the fourth day Vesper flew out — said he had a medicine run he couldn't break, and Amos told him to go, because a man who breaks his word to strangers will break it to you too, and he wanted to know which kind this one was. The plane's engine coughed once on takeoff and Amos, watching from the doorway wrapped in two blankets, heard it and winced. That rod was going to let go. He'd heard enough engines die to know the sound of one clearing its throat before the end.

He spent the day alone with the map, and with the drum of kerosene, and with the arithmetic of his own body. The leg was bad. He'd known it was bad since the trap had taken him, and he'd known what bad meant out here, alone, with the nearest doctor four days' floatplane away. He'd made his peace with it on the second night, quietly, the way you make peace with weather. What he hadn't made peace with was the promise. Not because he doubted the boy — he was past doubting, he'd spent his doubt and had none left — but because a promise is a heavy thing to hand a stranger, and Amos Kettle had spent his whole life trying not to be a burden to anybody.

But there was nobody else. That was the truth of it, the plain and ugly truth. The people the papers belonged to were scattered, poor, and forty years tired. The man who could have told them was dying in a cabin with a rotten leg. And the only person who'd landed in the last month was the son of the man who'd scuttled the ship and the nephew of the man who'd stolen everything the ship had carried.

Maybe that was right, though. Maybe that was the river's idea of a joke with a point to it. Let the Joubert name pay the Joubert debt.

* * *

Vesper came back on the fifth day, on foot, which told Amos everything about the engine before the boy opened his mouth. He came up the path carrying his gear and a look on his face that Amos recognized, because he'd worn it himself once: the look of a man whose escape route has just closed behind him.

"Rod's through the case," Vesper said. "I'm grounded in Halfway Landing until I can trade my way into a rebuild."

"Halfway Landing." Amos almost smiled. "The river's way of making a man sit still."

"I don't sit still."

"Then the river's smarter than you." He waved him inside. "Sit. We've got time now, and I haven't got much, so we're going to use it. You're going to listen, and then you're going to repeat it back to me until you can say it in your sleep, because if you get it wrong, everything I am goes in the ground with me."

Vesper sat. And Amos told him.

Not everything — not yet, not the deck and the lantern and the drum, that was for later, that was for if the boy earned it. But the shape of it. The Meridian Star. The papers in her hold. The claims they proved, and the people they belonged to, and the man who'd made sure the ship never delivered them. He watched Vesper's face go through disbelief and then through something worse than disbelief, which was the beginning of understanding, and he watched the boy's hands close slowly on the edge of the table.

"My father," Vesper said, and stopped.

"Your father," Amos said, "sealed the drum. Your uncle's the one who's been hunting it ever since. There's a difference between those two men, boy, though I'll grant you it's a difference measured in inches." He leaned forward. "The papers are in the old oxbow, under the silt, in the hold of a ship that's been dead forty years. The river will give you six tides to get to her, starting when the water drops past the willow stumps on the point. And Corvin's dredges are coming upriver behind you. That's the race. That's the whole of it. You don't have to be fast. You just have to be faster than him."

"Why does he want it found? If the papers ruin him—"

"He doesn't want it found. He wants it *first*." Amos let that sit. "There's a difference too, and it's the whole difference in the world. A man who wants a thing found will dig anywhere. A man who wants it first will dig only where he knows, and burn every map that says otherwise, and buy every mouth that could open. He's been doing it for forty years. You're the last map that says otherwise."

The lamp hissed. Outside, the river ran low and quiet over its bars, already dropping, already counting down.

"Say the promise back to me," Amos said.

Vesper looked at him for a long moment. Amos had watched a lot of men decide things on this river — to run the rapids or portage, to turn back or push on, to tell the truth or the profitable lie — and he knew the look of a man on the edge of a decision he couldn't take back. The boy had it now.

"Deliver what's in her hold," Vesper said, "to the people she was stolen from."

"All of it. The whole truth, not the profitable part. Sworn statements, manifests, the ledger — everything. Even the parts that'll make you want to burn the map and fly south." Amos held his eyes. "Especially those."

"Even those," Vesper said.

Amos nodded, and lay back, and felt the fever start its climb again at the edges of him. "Then we're agreed," he said. "Now get the lamp closer. We're going over the map, and you're going to write down every word I say, because after tomorrow I can't promise my head will hold it."

* * *

He died at dawn on the sixth day, which felt right, because dawn was when the river gave things back.

The fever had burned through the last of him in the night, and near the end he'd been back on the deck of the Meridian Star again, young and barefoot, and Elias Joubert had been standing at the rail looking at him — not at the water, at him — and saying, *You saw nothing, boy. Go back to your bunk.* And Amos, forty years too late and one breath from the end, had finally said what he should have said on that deck: *I saw everything. And I'm going to give it back.*

He felt the boy's hand on his shoulder, big and warm, holding him to the world, or maybe holding the world to him. He heard the boy say, "I've got it. I'll do it. I promise." And the promise went through Amos like warmth through cold hands, and he thought: *that's the sound of a thing being delivered at last.*

The light came up gold on the water. The river ran low and quiet, six tides open and counting. Somewhere downriver, faint under the birdsong, he thought he could almost hear the thump of dredge engines — but that was probably just his heart, finishing.

The last thing Amos Kettle saw was the willow stumps on the point, standing in water that had dropped another inch in the night, and the last thing he thought was that the boy would need a barge, and a crew, and more luck than any honest man deserved —

— and that Corvin Joubert's dredges were already three days upriver, and closing.
