## 4. Grounded

The rod went through the crankcase at four hundred feet, and Vesper Joubert had exactly long enough to be surprised before he was busy.

The sound came first — a knock like a fist on a door, then a fist through the door — and the propeller's smooth pull became a shudder that climbed up through his boots and into his teeth. Oil pressure bled away on the gauge in a slow red surrender. The river was below him, wide and brown and moving fast with the season's melt, and Halfway Landing was behind him, a scatter of tin roofs and pilings already shrinking.

He put her down the way you put down anything you love and have failed: nose high, wings level, into the wind off the water, aiming for the long gravel bar downstream of town because the landing strip itself was a rumor and a mudhole. The Otter's belly kissed the stones with a grinding crunch that ran the length of the airframe, and she slewed left, righted, slewed again, and stopped with her tail still over the water's edge and her nose buried to the cowl in loose shale.

Silence, except for the tick of hot metal and the river going about its business.

Vesper sat with his hands on the yoke and breathed. Then he said one word, quietly, to no one, which was not a word fit for print or for prayer, and climbed out to look at what he'd done to himself.

He knew before he opened the cowling. You don't throw a rod by accident; you throw it by ignoring the small voices an engine gives you — the faint roughness at idle, the oil that darkened a little faster than it should, the way number three cylinder had been running warm since the last cold snap. He'd heard all of it. He'd told himself he'd deal with it after this run, and the run after that, because dealing with it meant stopping, and stopping meant staying, and staying was the thing Vesper Joubert did not do.

The hole in the crankcase was the size of a saucer. Number three rod lay in the gravel fifty feet back along his ground track, bent double like a man shot mid-stride.

"Well," said a voice behind him. "That's a dead one."

* * *

Halfway Landing earned its name honestly: it was halfway between everywhere and nowhere, strung along a bluff above the river like laundry on a line. A fuel dock, a trading post, a church that doubled as a dance hall on Saturdays, a row of stilt houses leaning into each other for company. The airstrip was two hundred yards of corduroy logs and dredged fill that turned to porridge every spring. Vesper had landed there maybe six times in ten years, always for fuel, never for the night. He knew it the way you know a face seen across a crowded room.

Now he stood in the middle of it with a dead airplane and nowhere to be, and the feeling that rose in him wasn't panic. It was worse than panic. It was the particular dread of a man who has built his whole life on motion watching the wheels come off the one machine that kept him moving.

The voice belonged to a woman sitting on an overturned fuel drum outside the hangar at the strip's edge, drinking coffee from a tin cup, watching him with the frank appraisal of someone who assessed wrecks professionally. She was broad-shouldered and gray-templed, with forearms like dock rope and a name stitched on her coveralls that read SEFA T. TUILAGI.

"Otter?" she asked.

"Otter."

"Radial?"

"Nine-cylinder."

She nodded slowly, as if confirming something sad she'd suspected all along. "Threw a rod."

"Threw a rod."

"Everybody does, eventually." She drained her coffee and stood, unfolding to a height that made Vesper feel briefly small. "Question is whether you threw it because of bad luck or bad listening. Come on. Show me."

In the hangar's shade she circled the plane twice without touching it, the way a doctor circles a patient, then hauled herself up onto the wing root and pulled the cowling panels. She looked into the engine for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had gone gentle in a way that frightened him more than anger would have.

"You've been running her lean on the top end. And your oil's older than some of my tools." She wiped her hands on a rag that was cleaner than her hands. "When'd you last do a proper annual? Not a look-over. An annual."

Vesper didn't answer, which was an answer.

Sefa Tuilagi looked at him for a moment, and whatever she saw in his face made her sigh from somewhere deep. "You fly medicine, I'm guessing. Medevac markings, insulin crates in the back." He must have looked startled, because she tapped the side of her nose. "Smell of cold storage. Whole river smells of diesel and fish; you smell of pharmacies." She tossed the rag onto the tire. "I can fix her. Crankcase is scrap, but I've got a case off a wrecked Goose that'll mate up if I machine it, and there's a crank in Fairbanks if somebody flies it out. Parts, machining, labor — call it three weeks and more money than you're hoping I'll say."

"Three weeks." The words tasted like lead.

"Two and a half if the weather holds and you make yourself useful. I don't take charity cases and I don't take liars, and I especially don't take pilots who treat engines like they're optional." She stuck out a hand, huge and scarred. "Sefa Tuilagi. This is my field, such as it is. Welcome to staying put."

He shook it. Her grip felt like a verdict.

* * *

He took a room above the trading post because it was the only room, and spent the first evening doing what he always did when grounded, which was plan his escape. Charts spread across the bed. Alternate fields marked. Routes home calculated around the repair schedule, around the weather, around everything. If he could get the crank flown in faster, if Sefa would work nights, if —

If. The word had a hollow ring to it, like a bucket with a cracked bottom.

On the third morning, Sefa put a wrench in his hand and pointed at the dead engine. "You want to fly again, you learn what keeps you flying. Start with the exhaust stack. Every nut, off and on, until your hands know them blind."

"I pay people to—"

"Not here you don't. Here you trade." She said it without malice, the way you'd tell a child the tide comes in twice a day whether he likes it or not. "Labor for labor. That's the currency of Halfway Landing. Your money's good at the post and nowhere else."

So Vesper Joubert, who had spent ten years keeping the world at arm's length from the cockpit of a borrowed life, took off his jacket and learned the underside of his own airplane. His hands blistered. His back seized. By the fourth day he could pull the exhaust nuts without thinking, and Sefa grunted at him once, which he gathered was praise.

It was on the fifth day, hauling the ruined crankcase out from under the fuselage on a roller dolly, that he met Junie Marsh.

She came around the hangar's open door carrying two mugs of coffee and no introduction, a young woman of perhaps twenty-three with sunburned cheekbones, hair hacked off short for practicality, and the kind of watchful eyes that catalogued everything and surrendered nothing. Deckhand's build. River tan. She set one mug on the dolly beside him without asking if he wanted it.

"Sefa says you're paying in sweat," she said. "Figured you'd need fuel for it."

"Thank you." He took the mug. It was terrible coffee, burnt and bitter, and he drank it anyway.

"Junie Marsh," she said. "I crew deck on the *Maggie B*, freight run upriver. We're laid over till the water drops." She nodded at the opened engine, at the scorched and broken heart of it. "Rod?"

"Rod."

"Bad listening or bad luck?"

The question was so precisely Sefa's that he almost laughed. "Apparently bad listening."

"Huh." She drank her coffee and studied him with those cataloguing eyes, and he had the distinct sensation of being read like a manifest. "You're not from up here. Not originally. You've got that look — like you're counting the days till you can leave. Everybody who's passing through has it. Nobody who lives here does."

"Is that so."

"Mm." She finished her coffee in one swallow and set the mug down. "Fair warning, then. Halfway Landing's got a memory like a dredge bucket. Everything that passes through gets scooped up and kept. You'll be old news by Friday, but you'll never quite leave." She smiled for the first time — quick, crooked, unexpectedly warm — and walked out the way she'd come in.

Vesper stood there holding the empty mug and realized he was smiling too, and stopped immediately, as though caught at something.

* * *

The days found a shape he hadn't asked for and couldn't seem to refuse. Mornings in the hangar under Sefa's blunt instruction, learning the vocabulary of his own survival: wrist pins and bearing clearances, torque sequences chanted like scripture. Afternoons hauling freight at the dock to square accounts with the trading post, because Sefa's parts bill was climbing past what his cash could cover and everyone in town seemed to know it before he did. Evenings on the trading post porch with whoever drifted by, because in Halfway Landing evenings were communal property and privacy was considered either rude or suspicious.

He kept waiting for the itch to leave to become unbearable. It didn't come. That frightened him worse than the rod had.

Junie appeared most evenings, off the *Maggie B* between runs, and she talked the way rivers run — easily, constantly, carrying everything with it. He learned she'd been orphaned at fourteen and raised half by an aunt and half by the river itself. Learned she could read water better than most pilots and had opinions about all of them. Learned she saved money in a coffee tin toward a boat of her own and told no one the amount, though she told him the dream freely enough: her own hull, her own route, nobody's cargo but what she chose to carry.

"What about you?" she asked one evening, feet up on the porch rail, watching the light die gold on the water. "What does Vesper Joubert carry when he's not carrying insulin?"

"Whatever pays."

"That's a freight answer, not a person answer."

He turned his mug in his hands. Down the street, someone was tuning a fiddle badly. "I used to carry a name," he said finally. "Didn't fit. Traded it in."

"For what?"

"Nothing yet. Still shopping."

She looked at him sideways, and he saw her decide — visibly, deliberately — not to push. It was the first kindness anyone had shown him in years that asked nothing back, and it lodged in his chest like a splinter he couldn't work loose.

* * *

On the ninth day, Sefa called him into the hangar and showed him the new crankcase, machined and mated, gleaming on the stand like a resurrection. "Crank's coming on Thursday's mail plane. Two weeks, we bolt her together. Three, she runs." She wiped her hands and gave him a long look. "Which means two more weeks of you, Joubert. Think you can stand it?"

"I've survived worse."

"Sure you have." But she said it oddly, and he understood that she'd been listening — to the trading post, to the dock, to the whole dredge-bucket memory of the town — and had assembled a picture of him that included things he hadn't said. "Word of advice from a woman who fixes what other people break. Engines and men both run fine right up until they don't. The trick is listening early."

That night he dreamed of Amos Kettle's cabin for the first time since he'd left it — the fever-bright eyes, the map pressed into his hands, the promise taken like a debt. *Deliver what's in her hold.* He woke before dawn with his heart hammering and lay staring at the ceiling beams until the light came, telling himself the map was a dying man's fever, the promise a courtesy paid to the dead.

But when he got up, he found himself digging to the bottom of his kit, past the charts and the spare clothes, to where the oilcloth packet lay wrapped and forgotten. He didn't open it. He just held it a while, feeling the stiff paper inside, the weight of a dead man's certainty.

Then he tucked it away again and went to work on his engine, and tried not to notice that for the first time in ten years, being grounded didn't feel like falling.

It might even have felt, in a way he refused to examine, like arriving.

He was torquing the last of the intake bolts when Junie leaned in the hangar door, and the look on her face was not the crooked smile.

"Vesper," she said. "There's a dredge fleet buying up every chart between here and the upper river. Posting notices at the docks. Company title, salvage rights, the whole paper trail." She paused. "The notices are signed Joubert Salvage and Recovery."

The wrench slipped in his oily hand and rang against the cowling like a bell.
