## 5. What the Name Buys

Junie Marsh had grown up on stories the way other kids grew up on milk — fed them every morning whether she asked for them or not.

Her father had been a deckhand for eleven years before the river took his hearing and half his left hand, and in the evenings after that, with his cigarettes and his slow voice, he'd told her everything about the salvage trade: which companies paid honest shares and which ones salted their ledgers, which pilots could read water like scripture and which ones just read depth gauges, and — most of all — the wrecks. The wrecks were the saints and martyrs of his private religion. He knew their tonnages, their last cargoes, the names of men who'd drowned in them. And above all the others, spoken of the way you'd speak of a church fire or a hanging, he knew the Meridian Star.

He also knew the name Joubert.

So when the tall pilot with the careful hands walked into Sefa's shop behind the fuel dock and started asking questions about river levels and old survey charts, Junie kept her mouth busy with a rag and her ears wide open. He gave his name as Vesper. Just Vesper. Nobody on this stretch of the river went by one name unless they were hiding the rest of it, and she'd have bet her father's good wrench it was a surname doing the hiding.

She watched him work, too, because a man's hands told you more than his mouth. He didn't handle tools like a rich man slumming it, but he didn't handle them like labor either. He handled them like a man who'd learned out of necessity and never let the knowledge settle into comfort — competent, economical, and always, always ready to leave. She'd seen that posture before. It was the posture of a man standing in a room with one eye on the door.

Sefa liked him, which was unusual enough to be interesting. Sefa liked almost nobody who hadn't been born within earshot of an ocean, and even then only provisionally. But she'd let Vesper pull his own cylinder heads under her supervision, and she'd laughed at something he said — actually laughed, the low rolling laugh she saved for people who'd earned it — and by the second evening she was letting him touch her torque wrench without reciting the rules of its use first. That was practically a marriage proposal, from Sefa.

Junie wasn't so easily won.

* * *

The third night, she found him in the shop after hours, hunched over the survey charts Sefa had pulled from the drawer nobody opened, a kerosene lamp throwing his shadow long across the concrete. He had Amos Kettle's map beside the charts — she recognized the greasy hand-drawn paper; half the town had heard about the dying trapper's bargain by now, because Halfway Landing digested news the way catfish digested bait, slowly and completely — and he was tracing channels with one finger, lips moving, doing arithmetic against the current of some thought she couldn't see.

"You're up late," she said, leaning in the doorway.

He didn't startle, which she noted. Most people startled. "Charts don't read themselves."

"No," she agreed, coming closer. "And that one's not reading true, is it. That channel." She tapped the map where Amos had inked a bold line through country the survey charts showed as solid timber and high ground. "That's not the river. Not anymore."

His eyes came up, and for a moment there was something raw in them before the shutters came down. "You know these charts?"

"I grew up on them. My father ran deck for eleven years." She dragged a crate over and sat, uninvited, close enough to watch his face in the lamplight. "Kettle & Sons outfit, mostly. Then Meridian Consolidated, toward the end."

Something moved through him at the company name — quick, swallowed, but she saw it. She filed it away.

"Meridian Consolidated," she repeated, watching him. "You know much about them?"

"Old company. Gone now."

"Gone the way things go when they're bought, or gone the way things go when they sink?" She smiled to take the edge off, but only partway. "My father used to tell a story. Big salvage concern, northern rivers, twenty-some years back. Owned half the dredges between here and the divide. Then in one season — one bad season, they said — they lost three vessels and the founder lost everything, and the whole empire got carved up by creditors. My father swore it wasn't the season. He swore somebody sank those boats on purpose." She let that sit. "Joubert Salvage, they were called. Ring any bells?"

The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, a dog was complaining at the moon, and the river made its endless sound against the pilings, the sound Junie had slept inside her whole life.

"Names come up in the business," Vesper said finally. "Joubert's still around, isn't it? I've seen the notices."

"That you have." She nodded toward the door, toward the town beyond it. "Those notices went up yesterday. Every dock, every trading post, every bulletin board from here to the falls. Company title claimed on every wrecked vessel in the watershed. Every one." She leaned forward. "Signed by Corvin Joubert, managing director. You want to tell me what a man named Vesper knows about a family named Joubert?"

He held her gaze, and she had to give him credit — he didn't flinch and he didn't perform confusion. He just went quiet, the way deep water goes quiet, and she understood she was looking at a man deciding how much truth he could afford.

"My father worked for them," he said at last.

It was smooth. It was plausible. It was, she was nearly certain, a lie — or close enough kin to a lie that they shared a roof. His father hadn't worked for Joubert Salvage. His father had *been* Joubert Salvage, or half of it, if the story her father told had any bones in it. Elias Joubert, the founder who drowned with his fortune and his reputation in the same season. And this man — same jaw, same gray eyes, same way of standing like the ground owed him nothing — was either the world's best coincidence or the founder's son, sitting in a lamp-lit shop with a dead trapper's map to the very wreck that had ended the family.

She could have pushed. She was good at pushing; she'd grown up the only girl among deckhands and dredge crews, and she'd learned early that the quiet ones fold fastest when you lean on them right. But she looked at him — at the charts spread out with such care, at the map he was keeping like a promise instead of a payday, at the engine parts he'd cleaned and racked in Sefa's exacting order without being asked twice — and she decided to do something she almost never did.

She decided to wait.

"Your father worked for them," she said, flat, letting him hear exactly how much she believed.

"That's right."

"And now their managing director is papering the whole river with title claims, and you're holding a map to the biggest wreck they ever lost, and you're asking me to believe that's all coincidence."

"I'm not asking you to believe anything." He closed the charts, unhurried. "I'm asking Sefa to sell me a crankshaft. You're the one asking questions."

Fair hit. She stood, brushing off her trousers. "I'll tell you what my father used to say about salvage," she offered at the door. "He said the river gives up nothing honest. Everything it surrenders, somebody drowned for, or lied for, or both. So before you go pulling anything off the bottom, friend, you'd better know which one you are."

She left him there with his lamp and his lies of omission, and she told herself she'd sleep fine.

She didn't, particularly.

* * *

The notices were still going up.

Junie saw the agents at dawn, two of them, crisp-shirted and soft-handed in a way that marked them as office men sent into the field and hating it. They worked the docks methodically, stapling heavy cream-colored paper to every post and piling, and Junie drifted close enough to read over a fisherman's shoulder while pretending to coil a line.

NOTICE OF CLAIM, the heading ran. BE IT KNOWN that all wrecked, stranded, sunken, or abandoned vessels, hulls, cargoes, fittings, and appurtenances whatsoever lying within the navigable waters of the Meridian watershed are the lawful property of JOUBERT SALVAGE & DREDGING COMPANY, by right of prior survey, recorded title, and continuous enterprise. Any party salvaging, disturbing, diving upon, or removing material from any such vessel without written license does so at their own peril and will be prosecuted to the full extent of the law.

"Full extent of the law," the fisherman muttered. "Whose law? River law's older than their paperwork."

"Paperwork wins in courtrooms," his neighbor said gloomily. "River law drowns."

The agents moved on toward the trading post, and Junie watched them go with a cold feeling settling into her stomach. She'd grown up poor on a working river; she knew what company paper looked like when it came ashore. It looked like this — clean, official, printed in the city — and it meant that whatever lay under the water belonged to whoever could afford the lawyers, and everyone else could stand on the bank and watch.

By noon there were notices on the fuel dock, on the chandlery, tacked to the door of Sefa's shop itself. Sefa tore hers down, read it, and pinned it back up upside down, which Junie chose to interpret as a statement of policy.

Vesper read one in silence, standing at the end of the dock with his hands in his pockets. Junie watched him from the deck of the barge where she was splicing rope — watching him was becoming a habit she'd need to manage — and she saw the moment his expression changed. It wasn't anger, exactly. It was recognition, the look of a man seeing a face he knew in a crowd he'd hoped was strangers.

"Corvin Joubert," she said, coming to stand beside him. "That's the uncle, isn't it."

Vesper went very still.

"Not the father who worked for the company," she continued, conversationally, eyes on the river. "The brother. The one who inherited what was left and built it back bigger. The one whose dredges are coming upriver this season buying every chart and hiring every pilot who'll sign." She turned and looked at him straight on. "I did some asking around while you were turning wrenches. Old-timers remember Elias Joubert had a son. Boy would be thirty-odd now. Ran off after the wrecks. Nobody knew where."

The river slapped the pilings. Down the dock, one of the cream-colored notices lifted in the wind and settled again, patient as a spider.

"If you're waiting for me to say it," Vesper said quietly, "you'll wait a long time."

"I'm not waiting for you to say it. I already know it." She crossed her arms. "What I'm waiting to find out is why it matters. Because here's what I can't square, Vesper Joubert — and yes, that's your name, and we both know it now, so let's stop wasting breath. A man running from that family doesn't carry a map to their biggest wreck. A man selling that map would've taken the first offer from Corvin's agents and been gone. But you're still here. Spending money you don't have on a crankshaft. Reading charts at midnight." She tilted her head. "So either you're the best liar on this river, or Amos Kettle made you promise something, and you're the kind of fool who keeps promises to dying men."

For a long moment he said nothing. Then, not quite looking at her: "Does it change anything? What I am?"

"It changes what you're worth," she said. "To Corvin, plenty. To me—" She shrugged. "Depends what you do next."

"And if I never tell you the whole of it?"

"Then I'll spend the whole voyage wondering, and you'll spend the whole voyage knowing I'm wondering, and neither of us will sleep." She stuck out her hand, rough-palmed, rope-hardened. "But I'll sign on anyway. Half share, and honesty whenever it costs you something. Those are my terms. Cheap, considering."

He looked at her hand, then at her face, and something in his expression cracked open — not all the way, just a seam, just enough light to see there was a man inside the locked room after all. He took her hand.

"Honesty when it costs me something," he repeated. "That might bankrupt me."

"Most things worth having do."

* * *

That evening the flagship's advance tender came upriver, black-hulled and too clean, and tied off at the main dock with a swagger the river didn't grant lightly. Men in company colors came ashore. More notices went up. And a clerk with a ledger book began paying cash on the spot for old charts, old logbooks, old survey notes — anything with soundings on it, anything with a channel marked, anything at all that remembered where the water used to run.

Junie stood at the chandlery window and watched the line of sellers form, and felt the race begin in her bones before anyone had said a word aloud.

Behind her, in the lamplight of the shop, Vesper rolled up Amos Kettle's map and slid it inside his jacket, against his chest, where a man keeps the things he means to keep.

"He's buying the river's memory," she said.

"Then we'd better move faster than his money," Vesper answered — and for the first time since she'd met him, he sounded like a man with somewhere to be rather than somewhere to flee.
