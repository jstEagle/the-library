## 7. Crew of Strays

Sefa Tuilagi had a rule about men who came into her shop with money in one pocket and a story in the other: she charged double for the story. Vesper Joubert had been coming around for six days now, trading labor for parts, and he hadn't told her a single story at all. That was the problem.

She watched him from under the Cessna's wing, where she was supposed to be checking his magneto and was actually watching him wrestle the engine hoist across the hangar floor like it owed him something. He was good with his hands — better than most flyboys, who thought a wrench was something you pointed at a mechanic — but he worked like a man trying to outrun the job instead of settle into it. No rhythm. No patience. Six days of labor traded and he still stood like a man with one boot on the dock and the other on a departing boat.

"You're going to shear that bolt," she called out.

He didn't look up. "It'll hold."

"It'll hold until it doesn't, and then you'll be walking home through two hundred miles of muskeg." She set down the magneto and came out into the light. Halfway Landing's afternoon hung thick off the river, the air smelling of diesel and cut grass and the sweet rot of fish waste downstream. "You work like someone left a fire burning behind you."

That got him to stop. He straightened up, wiped his hands on a rag that had given up being clean days ago, and looked at her with those flat gray eyes that gave away nothing except how hard they were trying to give away nothing.

"Engine's almost done," he said.

"The engine's done. It's been done since yesterday. You keep finding reasons to come back." She crossed her arms. "So. Say it."

He held her gaze for a long moment. Sefa had raised two brothers and buried a husband, and she knew every flavor of silence a man could serve. This one wasn't pride. It was calculation — him weighing what she'd do when he finally laid the cards down.

"I need a deckhand," he said. "And a mechanic. And a pilot, if I can find one stupid enough. There's a boat run coming up. Shallow water, night running, salvage patrols that don't ask permission before they board you."

"Salvage whose?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters plenty." She picked up his rag and threw it at his chest. "You want me to leave my shop — my *shop*, the one I spent nine years building after the yards shut down and everyone with sense moved south — to run cargo past Corvin Joubert's dredges, you'd better tell me whose flag we're sailing under. And don't say yours, because I've watched you sign your name as *V. Marsh* on the fuel invoices, and Junie Marsh is standing right there, and last I checked she didn't have a brother."

Junie, who had been coiling line by the door with the studied deafness of someone eavesdropping professionally, went very still.

Vesper exhaled through his nose. "Joubert," he said. "Vesper Joubert."

The name landed in the hangar like a dropped wrench. Everyone on this river knew it. The Joubert Salvage Company had owned half the traffic between here and the delta before it folded — or before it burned, depending on which bar you asked in — and the name still opened doors and closed them, usually both at once.

"Corvin's kin," Sefa said.

"Nephew."

"And you're telling me this now because—"

"Because you asked twice, and because I'm about to ask you to risk your neck, and Amos Kettle taught me—" He stopped. Jaw working. "Because a dying man gave me something to deliver, and I can't deliver it alone, and I'm not going to lie to the people I'm asking."

Sefa studied him. He hadn't said *please*. He hadn't promised her a cut, though Junie would negotiate that part later and well. What he'd offered was thinner than either and worth more: the truth, late but whole, handed over like a man setting down something heavy.

"What did Kettle give you?" she asked.

"A map."

"To what?"

"A wreck that isn't where anyone thinks it is. And proof of something my family did to this river thirty years ago." His voice stayed level, but his hands didn't; they closed around the rag until the knuckles whitened. "I made a promise to deliver it. To the people it was stolen from."

Sefa looked at him for a long time. Then at Junie, who gave a small shrug that meant *I'm in if you are*. Then at the shop — the hydraulic press her husband had welded, the tool wall she'd organized by feel during the winter her eyes went bad, the whole small kingdom she'd built out of the wreckage of an industry that had discarded her along with everything else.

Here was the thing about people like Sefa Tuilagi, and like Junie Marsh, and like every soul Halfway Landing had collected over the years: the industry took your trade, your pension, your good name, and then it had the gall to act surprised when you wanted nothing more than to see it answer for something.

"One condition," she said.

Vesper waited.

"Honesty. Not when it's convenient. Not when the lie's already halfway out of your mouth. If I ask, you answer, even if the answer makes you look like a fool or a thief or a Joubert." She stuck out her hand. "You pay me in honesty, and I'll pay you in work. That's the exchange rate."

He took her hand. His grip was careful, like he wasn't used to being trusted and didn't want to break it.

"That's the worst deal anyone's ever offered me," he said.

"Then you've been dealing with the wrong people." She let go and turned back toward the Cessna. "Now get over here and hold this cowling straight while I time the mags. And send word to Odile Brant. If you want a pilot fool enough to run the upper river at night, there's exactly one, and she drinks at the Anchorline."

* * *

Odile Brant was not drinking at the Anchorline. She was drinking behind it, on the loading dock, where the river breeze could reach her and the regulars couldn't stare. Sefa had warned Vesper about that too — *she'll take the dock over the barstool, always has, since the inquiry* — so when they found her there, boots hanging over the black water, a bottle between her knees and a chart case beside her worn soft as an old Bible, Vesper wasn't surprised.

What surprised him was the chart case. Pilots who'd lost their licenses didn't usually keep their charts.

"Vesper Marsh," she said without turning around. Her voice was low and roughened, a voice that had spent years talking over engines. "Or should I say Joubert? News travels faster than boats on this river."

"Which version do you prefer?"

"Neither. Both are lies of convenience." She tipped the bottle back, then set it down and finally looked at him. She was younger than her voice — mid-thirties maybe — with sun-cracked skin and eyes the pale green of shallow water over sand. "Sefa says you're looking for a pilot. Sefa also says you're honest, which means either she's been fooled or you've changed, and I haven't decided which interests me more."

"I'm looking for someone to run the upper river at night. Low water. Past the company patrols."

"Everyone's looking for that. Corvin's offering triple rates for it." She smiled without any warmth in it. "I hear he pays well. I hear he also forgives. There's a man at the dredge office who told me he could make the Brant inquiry go away — all I'd have to do is keep my ears open for him." She spat neatly over the edge of the dock. "I told him what he could listen to."

Vesper sat down on the dock beside her, leaving a careful arm's length of weathered planking between them. Below, the river slid past, carrying its usual freight of leaves and secrets.

"Tell me about the inquiry," he said.

Her laugh was short and bitter. "Straight to it. All right. Four years ago I was piloting the *Cormorant Queen* for Meridian Line — a freight outfit, before Corvin bought their routes out from under them. We came down through the Narrows at half tide, standard run, channel I'd run forty times. Except somebody had pulled the markers. Not moved them — *pulled* them, dragged the stakes right out of the mud, and re-set them a hundred meters east onto a gravel shelf that wasn't on any chart." Her hand found the bottle again but didn't lift it. "We went aground at speed. Cargo lost. Deckhand broke his back. And the investigation found — imagine this — that the markers had been moved by persons unknown, but that Pilot Brant had failed to exercise due caution, and her license was suspended pending review, and the review never happened because the company folded and the file went into a box somewhere in Corvin's offices."

"Who moved the markers?"

"If I knew that, I wouldn't be sitting on a dock drinking cheap rum." She turned to face him fully. "But here's what I know. Two weeks before the wrecking, the *Cormorant Queen*'s owner refused to sell his route rights to a Joubert holding company. Two weeks after, the route belonged to them anyway, bought for pennies from the insurance settlement. And the only other person who knew our schedule well enough to strip those markers was the company dispatcher — who now manages Corvin's upper-river operations." She shrugged. "Coincidence. The inquiry loved that word."

Vesper was quiet for a moment. Somewhere across the water, one of Corvin's dredges groaned through its night shift, the sound carrying flat and mechanical over the current.

"I can't clear your name," he said at last. "Not yet. But I'm going to open a hold on a wreck called the *Meridian Star*, and what's inside it is going to put Corvin Joubert's whole fleet in front of investigators. When that happens, files come out of boxes. Inquiries get reopened. Names get cleared — or dirtied worse." He met her eyes. "I'm not promising you exoneration. I'm promising you a seat close to the fire."

Odile looked at him for a long time. Behind them, through the tavern's thin walls, somebody's radio played something tinny and old.

"Sefa vouches for you," she said. "Junie vouches for you. That's two people the river hasn't managed to ruin yet, which is more character references than I've had since the inquiry." She picked up the chart case and patted it once, like an old dog. "One condition. When we run, I have the wheel. Nobody touches my wheel, nobody second-guesses my channels, and if I say beach her, we beach her. You can hate me for it tomorrow."

"Done."

"And another thing." She stood, unfolding to her full height, and looked down at him with something that was almost a smile. "Your uncle ruined me with a lie about a wrecking. If this goes wrong — if you're lying, if this map of yours is a ghost, if I lose my license permanently over some dead man's treasure hunt — then I'll have been ruined twice by Jouberts, and I will spend the rest of my life making sure the third time happens to you."

"Fair terms," Vesper said.

She extended her hand, and he took it, and her grip was the grip of a woman who had steered loaded barges through floodwater and trusted no one's hand but her own since.

"Then I'm in," Odile Brant said. "God help us all."

* * *

They gathered that evening in the back of Sefa's shop, around the scarred oak table where she did her books, and Vesper unrolled Amos Kettle's map for the first time in front of witnesses. Sefa had cleared the surface of tools and invoices; Junie had brought coffee strong enough to strip paint and a plate of fried bread that vanished in the first ten minutes; Odile sat with her arms crossed, reading the map upside down out of habit, saying nothing, missing nothing.

"So this is it," Junie said. "The famous map." She leaned close. The paper was soft with age and sweat-stained at the corners, covered in Amos Kettle's cramped, patient hand — soundings, bearings, little annotations in a shorthand that might have been trapping code or might have been madness. "Looks like a treasure map drawn by a schoolteacher."

"It looks like a trap," Odile said. "Half these soundings don't match any channel I know."

"They won't," Vesper said. "The river's moved. I think that's the point."

He told them what he could — the promise to Amos, the shape of the secret, the fact that Corvin's fleet was already buying charts and posting claim notices up and down the watershed. He did not tell them what he suspected about his father; partly because he didn't yet know it himself, and partly because some part of him was already learning what Sefa's condition cost. Honesty paid out in installments. He'd owe them the rest soon enough.

Junie negotiated her cut with the cheerful ruthlessness of a customs officer — ten percent, settled at twelve, plus first pick of anything aboard that wasn't documents — and shook on it before Vesper finished agreeing. Sefa named no figure at all. "I'll take my pay in whatever we learn," she said, which frightened Vesper more than any number would have.

Odile spread her own charts over Amos's map, forty years of river history in faded ink, and began, quietly, to match channels against channels. The four of them bent over the table while the evening darkened outside and the dredge lights pricked on across the water, and for the first time since Amos Kettle had pressed the map into his hands, Vesper felt the weight of the thing change — not lighter, exactly, but shared. Distributed. A load carried on four backs moves differently than one carried alone.

It was Junie who spotted it first, tapping a spot where Amos's inked channel crossed a bend that existed on none of Odile's charts. "Here. He's marked water where there's no water. Unless—"

"Unless there used to be," Odile said slowly. Her finger traced the curve of an oxbow, long abandoned, hidden under decades of silt and willow. "Unless the river jumped course and left her sleeping where nobody dredges because nobody knows there's anything to dredge."

They all looked at Vesper.

He looked at the map, and at the annotation curled beneath the oxbow in Amos's small hand — a single line he'd read a hundred times and never understood:

*Where the river forgot, she remembers.*

"We'll need the water low," he said. "Lower than it's ever been this early in the season."

Outside, far upstream, thunder muttered over the high country — rain falling on ground that needed dryness, filling a river that needed emptiness. Odile heard it too. Her jaw tightened.

"Then we'd better hope the weather holds," she said, "better than it's holding right now."
