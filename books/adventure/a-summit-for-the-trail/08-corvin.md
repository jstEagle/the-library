## 8. Corvin

The dredge came upriver on a Tuesday morning, and Halfway Landing knew it before anyone saw it.

Vesper felt it first as a change in the town's sound — the usual clatter of the fish dock and the argument of gulls thinning out, replaced by something deeper, a bass note rolling up the water like a struck drum. He was under Sefa's plane with a torque wrench in his hand when Junie came off the boardwalk at a run, her boots hammering the planks.

"She's here," she said, breathless. "The *Meridian's Daughter*. Three decks, gold lettering, more lights than the whole town's got bulbs. She's anchoring mid-channel like she owns it."

She did own it, or nearly. Vesper set the wrench down slowly, wiped his hands on a rag that had been clean once, and stood. His heart was doing something he didn't authorize — a quick, childish stutter, the rhythm of a boy hearing his father's truck in the drive.

"Corvin aboard?" he asked.

"Must be. They're lowering a launch."

He had known this day would come. Amos had told him the fleet worked the upper river; Odile had charted their patrol patterns with the grim precision of a woman who'd been burned by them. Knowing a thing was coming and standing in its path were different animals entirely.

"The map," he said quietly. "Where is it?"

Junie's eyes flicked to the plane — to the access panel over the aft baggage bay that Sefa had resealed two days ago, the one Vesper had asked her to make look untouched. She'd never asked why. That was either trust or tact, and he'd stopped trying to decide which.

"Still flying," she said. "Under forty pounds of spare alternator nobody would steal."

"Good." He rolled the rag into his pocket. "If anyone asks, I'm just a pilot waiting on an engine."

"You *are* just a pilot waiting on an engine."

"I know." He looked at her. "That's the point."

* * *

By noon the whole of Halfway Landing had migrated to the waterfront, the way towns do when money arrives wearing a smile. The *Meridian's Daughter* sat at anchor with her dredging arms folded like a sleeping insect's legs, her superstructure white as bone, her funnel banded in the Joubert colors — deep green and tarnished silver. Corvin's flag snapped from her masthead: not the company ensign but a private standard, a river bar crossed by a star. Vesper remembered it hanging in the stairwell of a house he'd been carried out of at nine years old, and he hated how easily his memory served it up, crisp as a photograph.

Corvin came ashore in the early afternoon, and he did it well. No gangplank ceremony, no company men flanking him — just a broad-shouldered man of sixty-odd in a canvas jacket worth more than most boats in the harbor, walking down into the crowd alone, shaking hands, laughing at jokes before they finished. He had his father's build and none of his father's silence. Where Aldous Joubert had moved through rooms like weather, Corvin moved through them like sunlight — warm, everywhere, touching everything, owning nothing you could point to.

Vesper watched from the shadow of the chandlery awning, arms crossed, and told himself the tightness in his chest was disgust.

It took Corvin less than an hour to find him. Vesper had counted on longer.

"There he is," Corvin said, appearing at his shoulder with the ease of a man who had never once been unwelcome anywhere. Up close he smelled of cedar shaving soap and diesel, an odd combination, expensive and industrial at once. "I thought so from the boat. You walk like your father, you know. Same shoulders. Same way of standing like the ground owes you an apology."

"My ground and I have an understanding," Vesper said. "We don't discuss family."

Corvin laughed — a real laugh, full-bodied, and that was the worst part, that it wasn't performance. "God. Aldous all over again. He used to say things like that and then go quiet for three days." He stuck out his hand. "Corvin."

"I know who you are."

"And I know who you are, Vesper." The name landed softly, deliberately, and Vesper felt the crowd around them recede, the way water pulls back from a shore before something big comes through. "You think I flew a flagship four hundred miles up a dying river without knowing whose plane threw a rod at Halfway Landing? I've had men watching this strip since spring. Not for you," he added quickly, and the quickness itself was a kind of confession. "For charts. For rumors. But you were on the list the moment your tail number came through."

Vesper didn't take the hand. Corvin let it fall without offense, turning instead to lean on the rail beside him, facing the water, giving Vesper the courtesy of not being stared at. It was masterful. Everything about the man was masterful.

"Your mother wrote me, after," Corvin said. "Once. Told me where you'd gone. I could have come. I want you to know I considered it every year, and every year I told myself the business couldn't spare me, and every year that was true, and neither fact excuses the other." He said it plainly, without the wet-eyed weight lesser men would have draped over it. "That's the truth. I find the truth buys more than an apology does. Cheaper, too."

"What do you want, Corvin?"

"Same thing you want, I suspect." He nodded at the river, at the brown water sliding seaward with the last of the morning's ebb. "To be there when she's found."

* * *

They walked, because Corvin wanted to walk and because refusing outright would have made a scene, and scenes cost more than walks. He talked the whole way along the boardwalk, past the fish sheds and the fuel dock, and Vesper listened the way Odile had taught him to listen to river reports — taking everything in, believing almost nothing.

The Meridian Star, Corvin said, was a legend the company had buried twice: once when she went down, and again when the insurance questions got uncomfortable. But legends leaked. A dredge hand in the delta camps had talked about a trapper upcountry who'd spent thirty years asking after old survey charts. Charts for a stretch of river no sane operator cared about.

"Amos Kettle," Vesper said.

Something moved behind Corvin's eyes — not surprise exactly. Recalculation. "You've heard the name."

"He sold me some maps once. Junk. Drew them himself, half senile."

"Did he." Corvin smiled, and the smile was warm and bottomless and had a trapdoor in it somewhere. "Well. If a senile trapper's junk ever turns into anything, my door's open. That's actually why I'm here." He stopped at the rail overlooking the fuel dock, and below them Sefa was visible under the Cessna's cowling, sleeves rolled, working with the unhurried violence of a good mechanic. "Not just for the ship. For you."

"Me."

"The company needs pilots who can read water. Real water — the kind above the falls, the kind nobody flies anymore since the runs went corporate. I can offer you a captaincy. Your own aircraft, company maintained, company fuel. A salary with a comma in it." He turned, and now he did look at Vesper directly, and his eyes were his brother's eyes, and that was unbearable. "And a name. You've spent ten years being nobody's son, Vesper. I watched you do it just now — 'Junk maps,' you say, and don't blink. You're good at it. Better than I was at my age. But nobodies don't inherit anything, and you're the last of us who isn't rotten."

The words went in like a splinter driven under a nail. Vesper kept his face still through long practice — ten years of border officials and loan officers and nurses asking *any relation?* on forms.

"My father scuttled that ship," he said. "Or lost her through pride, depending which drunk you ask. Either way, the name bought our family a headstone. Why would I want it back?"

"Because it wasn't what you think." For the first time, Corvin's charm flickered — a hairline crack, gone in a second, but Vesper saw it, and saw what lay behind it: not guilt. Fear. Thin, old, well-managed fear. "Aldous was many things, but careless with other people's money wasn't one of them. When the truth about the Meridian Star comes out — and it will, these things always do — you'll want to be standing inside the family when it does. Not outside it, holding a bag someone else packed."

"That sounds like a warning."

"It sounds like an offer with a deadline." Corvin straightened his cuffs, and the warmth came back up over his face like water filling a footprint. "Think about it. The fleet works downriver for another week — dredging leases, salvage claims, the usual paperwork empire. After that we move upstream. Whatever's up there, we'll find it with you or without you. Family rates either way." He extended his hand again, and this time held it there, patient as a fisherman. "Your father would have taken my hand."

"My father took a lot of your advice," Vesper said. "Look how that ended."

He walked away and left the hand hanging in the river air. He didn't look back, because looking back was a language too, and he refused to speak it.

* * *

That night he couldn't sleep, which was normal, but the shape of the sleeplessness was new. He lay in the bunk room above Sefa's shop listening to the dredge's generators thrumming across the water — a sound like a second heartbeat laid over the town's — and turned Corvin's words over like stones.

*Aldous was many things.*

The man had lied smoothly about everything else. Why stumble there?

Unless he hadn't stumbled. Unless the fear Vesper had glimpsed was real, and real fear meant real stakes, and real stakes meant the Meridian Star held something Corvin genuinely dreaded — not gold, which could be divided, but something that couldn't.

Near midnight he gave up, dressed, and went down to the shop. The lamp over Sefa's bench was already burning; she sat on a crate with tea, going through a parts catalog like scripture, and she looked up without surprise.

"Couldn't sleep either?" she said. "Or the boat's keeping you awake?"

"Both." He crouched at the Cessna's aft bay, checked the seal Sefa had rebuilt, ran his thumb along the seam. Solid. Invisible. Forty pounds of alternator guarding a dead man's promise. "Sefa. If a man offered you everything you'd lost — shop paid off, licenses restored, debts cleared — and all it cost was walking away from something you'd promised a dying stranger. What would you do?"

Sefa considered him over the rim of her cup for a long moment. She had a way of answering questions a beat slower than comfortable, so you knew she'd actually weighed them.

"Depends," she said, "on whether the stranger died knowing I'd keep it." She went back to her catalog. "Go to bed, Vesper. Tide turns at four."

* * *

At dawn he found a note tucked under the Cessna's tie-down ring. Heavy paper, company watermark, handwriting like engraved plate:

*Last chance travels upstream with me. — C.*

Beneath it, in smaller script, as if added as an afterthought and therefore not an afterthought at all:

*Ask yourself what your father was hiding. Then ask who taught him to hide it.*

Vesper read it twice, folded it small, and put it in his breast pocket — not to keep, but because burning evidence in a town full of Corvin's eyes was a luxury he couldn't afford yet.

Across the channel, the *Meridian's Daughter* weighed anchor with a groan of chain, her dredging arms unfolding as she swung her bow upstream. Through the wheelhouse glass, a figure raised one hand — a wave, or a salute, or a countdown, impossible to tell at that distance.

Vesper raised his own hand back, because refusing would have told Corvin everything.

Then he turned to his crew gathering on the dock — Odile with her charts already open against the wind, Junie coiling lines, Sefa wiping her hands — and said the only thing that mattered.

"We run tonight. Before his patrols settle into pattern. Odile — pick our window."

Odile looked up at the sky, reading it the way other people read letters from home, and her face went carefully blank.

"Tonight," she said, "the river's going to be meaner than anything Corvin's got. But it'll be dark, and his lookouts hate the cold." She rolled the chart closed. "Get some sleep, all of you. We leave on the ebb — and we won't see daylight again until we're past the narrows."
