## 9. First Tide Down

The river at night was a different animal, and Odile Brant had learned its habits the hard way.

She stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel's Wake* with the running lights doused and the moon three days from full, enough to silver the water but not enough to show her where the channel lied. And the upper river always lied. It braided and shifted, shed gravel bars in the spring freshet like a dog shedding winter coat, then forgot where it had put them. A chart two years old was a rumor. A chart five years old was a fairy tale. The survey she'd bought in Halfway Landing for the price of a decent bottle was older than Junie Marsh, and Odile trusted it exactly as far as she could throw the barge.

Which was, she reflected, gripping the wheel spokes, about forty tons.

"Talk to me," she said quietly.

Junie crouched at the bow rail with a sounding pole, a lantern shuttered down to a coin of amber light at her feet. She'd been taking soundings every thirty seconds since they'd cleared the last camp's firelight an hour back, and her arm was probably screaming, but she hadn't complained once. That was the thing about Junie that Odile was still learning: the girl worked like someone who'd been waiting her whole life for a job worth doing badly by nobody else.

"Six feet," Junie called back, voice pitched low to carry over the diesel's idle without carrying farther. "Bottom's hard. Gravel."

"Hard bottom's honest, at least." Silt you sank into and kept your prop; gravel took chunks out of it. "Next cast."

Vesper Joubert came forward along the deck the way he did everything — quiet, economical, like a man perpetually trying to take up less space than he did. He'd been aft most of the evening, sitting on the fuel drums with Amos Kettle's map open on his knees under a red-filtered flashlight, and he'd said maybe ten words since dusk. Odile had been counting. Not because she cared what he said, but because she'd spent four years learning what it meant when a man went silent at the wheel of a boat, and silence with a map involved was a particular kind of silence.

"Channel should bend starboard inside the next half mile," Vesper said. "According to this."

"According to a dead trapper's hand-drawn map," Odile said. "Drawn by a man who was dying of fever and wanted to be useful."

"He wasn't wrong yet."

*Yet.* She let that sit. "Sounding, Junie."

"Five feet. Still hard."

The tide was going out of the lower river behind them and would keep going out for another six hours, which meant the upper reaches were dropping too, the whole system exhaling. They'd waited three days at Halfway Landing for exactly this window — the first low-water window of the dry season, when bars that drowned all summer rose close enough to the surface to read by moonlight, and channels narrowed until there was only one way through and you either knew it or you didn't. Corvin Joubert's dredges couldn't follow them up here. Neither could his patrol boats, not at night, not shallow. That was the whole point of running dark.

That was also why one mistake would end them. There was no traffic to hide among, no depth to recover in, no other pilot coming along to throw them a line. Out here the river didn't care whose name was on your papers or whose name was on your blood. It only cared whether you read it right.

"Starboard bend coming up," Vesper said.

"I see the willows." The bank vegetation told its own story if you listened: willow and young alder meant recent silt, soft ground, a bank that moved. Old spruce meant ground that held still. The far bank ahead was spruce-dark and solid; the near bank was a pale tangle of willow catching the moonlight, and Odile did not like what that tangle was telling her.

She eased the throttle back a notch. The barge's wake flattened, the engine note dropped from a growl to a mutter.

"We're losing tide," Vesper said behind her. Not complaining. Just noting.

"We're losing a barge if I push this bend blind." She glanced back at him. In the red glow of his shielded light his face was all planes and shadow, ten years older than his years. "You want to get there fast or get there?"

He didn't answer that, which was itself an answer, and she turned back to the wheel.

* * *

Odile had run rivers since she was fourteen, standing on a crate to reach the wheel of her father's fish tender, and she had run them well enough that by twenty-six she had her own license and a reputation as the best rough-water pilot on the middle reach. Then the *Cormorant* went down in the Narrows with a load of company freight, and the inquiry found pilot error, and the company found it convenient, and the actual cause — a dredge crew working an unmarked cut upstream that dumped a slug of silt into the channel overnight — never made it into the record. The salvage fleet that owned the dredge owned the inquiry too. She'd known that even then. Knowing it and proving it were different countries, and she'd spent four years walking the border between them, taking deckhand work and relief piloting and one long bitter season hauling tourists who asked her how she liked driving boats, as though she were a hobbyist playing at her own ruin.

So when Vesper Joubert had stood in Sefa Tuilagi's shop with grease to his elbows and offered her the wheel of a barge headed upriver against the biggest salvage operation on the coast — offered it in that flat, careful voice, watching her face like a man testing ice — she'd said yes before he finished the sentence. She'd told herself it was about clearing her name. Partly it was. But there was more in it than that, and she'd known it even then: somewhere up that river was whatever the Jouberts had spent thirty years making sure nobody found, and Odile Brant wanted to be holding the wheel when it surfaced.

She just hadn't figured out yet what Vesper Joubert wanted out of it. And the not-knowing was starting to itch.

"Sounding," she called.

There was a pause longer than any pause Junie had allowed all night. Long enough that Odile's stomach dropped before the answer came.

"Three feet," Junie called back. "And I'm seeing boil off the bow. Water's moving funny."

Boil. Water pushing up over something shallow. Odile cut the throttle to idle so fast the barge shuddered, and the current, suddenly unopposed, began walking them sideways toward the willow bank.

"Back her down," Vesper said, already moving. "Easy astern—"

"I know how to back a barge." Her hands were moving anyway, throttle to reverse, wheel hard over to swing the stern away from the bank, and the *Kestrel's Wake* groaned and hesitated and began, grudgingly, to obey. Forty tons doesn't stop on a thought. It stops on persuasion, and persuasion takes distance, and distance was what they didn't have.

"Junie — get off the bow. Now."

The girl scrambled back along the rail without argument, and Odile was glad of that, glad with a fierce suddenness, because the boil ahead was spreading, a long pale seam of broken water opening across the channel like a wound, and beneath it, just breaking the surface now in the dropping water, a ridge of gravel showed itself the color of wet bone.

They'd have hit it at speed. Another hundred yards, another minute of confidence, and the *Kestrel's Wake* would have climbed that bar and stayed there through the whole tide cycle, high and dry and lit up like a carnival when Corvin's morning patrol came nosing upriver.

"Off the bar?" Vesper asked.

"Working on it." She brought the wheel through hand over hand, feeling the current take the bow and swing it, using it instead of fighting it — the river would turn them if she let it, and the trick was letting it turn them toward deep water rather than away from trouble. The stern swung. The bow swung. The gravel ridge slid past their port side close enough that she could hear the water hissing over it, a sound like static, like the river whispering *not this time.*

Then the lead line said six feet, then eight, and the boil fell away astern, and they were through.

Nobody spoke for a full minute. The engine muttered. Somewhere in the willows a bird complained once and went quiet.

"That wasn't on the map," Vesper said finally.

"No," Odile agreed. "It wasn't."

"The old channel ran closer to the south bank. This bar—" He stopped himself. She heard him stop himself, actually heard the click of it, like a door closing.

"This bar is new since your map was drawn," she finished for him. "Fine. Rivers do that. But Vesper." She turned, keeping one hand on the wheel, and looked at him straight across the dim deck. "You've been reading that map all night like it's scripture. You knew the bend was coming before the willows told us. You knew the water was dropping faster than the almanac said. You're navigating off something more than a dead man's drawing, and I'd like to know what it is, because my neck is on this wheel."

The red light caught the edge of his jaw as it tightened. For a moment she thought he'd give her something — anything. He was good at people, she'd noticed that; he gave Sefa honesty in installments and Junie a share and her a chance, each one calibrated, each one real but partial. A man who paid everyone in different currency.

"Amos Kettle ran this river for forty years," he said. "His map has annotations. Riddles, almost. I'm still working them out."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the true part of one."

"And the rest?"

He held her look, and she had to give him this: he didn't flinch, didn't smile it away, didn't do any of the things liars do. He just stood there in the dark holding his silence like a man holding a full cup, careful not to spill.

"When I know the rest," he said, "you'll get it first. Before anyone. That's a promise."

Odile turned back to the wheel. Promises. The river was full of men's promises, sunken ones, rusting ones, promises that snagged your keel in the night. But there was a difference between a promise and a payment plan, and Vesper Joubert seemed to know the difference, and that counted for something.

"It had better be," she said. "Because we just lost our margin. That bar means the channel's migrated north of where any chart says, which means everything past here is guesswork, which means I need whoever's feeding me bearings to be feeding me true ones. Next time you hold something back and it costs us hull, I put this barge aground on purpose and we walk home. Are we clear?"

"Clear," he said. And after a moment, quieter: "Thank you. For the save."

"Don't thank me. Thank the river. I just work here."

* * *

They tied up at dawn in a slough behind a gravel island, willows screening them from the main channel, and Odile sent Junie to sleep and Sefa to check the shaft bearing — the near-miss had rattled something loose below decks, a new vibration she didn't like — and took first watch herself, because she slept worst in strange country and best with an engine cooling nearby.

Vesper didn't sleep either. She watched him from the wheelhouse, sitting on the rail with the map again, turning it toward the rising light like a man interrogating it. His lips moved sometimes. Reading aloud, maybe, or arguing.

At midmorning he came up to the wheelhouse with two tin cups of coffee and handed her one without asking, which she decided to allow.

"Found something," he said.

"You found something, or you're willing to say something?"

A ghost of a smile. "Both, this time." He laid the map on the chart table, weighting the corners with cups and a wrench. "Look at the annotations along this reach. Amos wrote distances in chains, but they don't match the river as it runs now. I measured against our track last night. Every one of them is short by the same proportion — like the river used to be longer here. Slower. Looping."

Odile leaned over the paper. The old ink was brown and sure, a working man's hand, and alongside the channel marks ran little notes in the same hand: *old course*, *cut-off year of the big flood*, and beside one looping curve of dotted line, three words that raised the hair on her arms:

*she lies below.*

"The river jumped course," she said slowly. "Cut off the oxbow. Left the old loop as slackwater, then silted it in."

"If Amos is right, the Meridian Star isn't in the river at all. She's under a field."

Odile straightened. Below a field. Under ground that Corvin Joubert's dredges would never think to dig because no wreck chart in existence marked it, because the river itself had conspired to bury the evidence and move on. Thirty years of salvage fleets dragging every channel on the coast, and the ship they were all looking for was sleeping under a hayfield's worth of silt where the water used to be.

It was elegant. It was the kind of hiding place you chose if you weren't hiding treasure at all — if you were hiding proof, and wanted it forgotten rather than found.

And that was the thing, she realized, looking at Vesper's face in the flat morning light. He wasn't surprised. He'd worked it out hours ago, maybe days ago, and he'd let her nearly put forty tons onto a gravel bar before showing her this. No — that wasn't fair, and she knew it wasn't fair; the bar had nothing to do with the map. But the suspicion was already awake in her now, and suspicions don't go back to sleep just because you tell them they're being unreasonable.

"Why does a medicine pilot know so much about a wreck nobody's seen in thirty years?" she asked. "Why does the name Joubert make Sefa spit? Why does Corvin's fleet buy up charts in every river town between here and the headwaters — and why does that make you flinch every time someone says his name out loud?"

Vesper gathered the map off the table, folded it along its worn creases, and tucked it inside his jacket, against his chest, the way a man carries letters from home.

"First tide's done," he said. "We made the slough. Rest while you can — tomorrow we sound the oxbow."

He went out and down the ladder, and Odile stood alone in the wheelhouse with her coffee going cold, watching him cross the deck toward the bow where Junie was waking, and admitted to herself what she'd been circling all night:

She believed he meant to keep his promises. She just no longer believed she knew what all of them were.

Ahead, up the empty reach, the oxbow country waited under its skin of silt and grass — and somewhere under it, whatever the Jouberts had killed a river to bury.
