## 10. The Hold Beneath the Hold

The river gave them two hours of light worth having, and Odile spent both of them getting the barge exactly where she wanted it.

"Anchor's set," she called from the wheelhouse, one hand on the brass rail, squinting down at the water as if she could read it like a chart. "Thirty-one feet under us. Maybe thirty-three if the current's lying."

Vesper stood at the stern in his dive skin, the tank heavy on his back, and looked at a stretch of brown water that looked like every other stretch of brown water between Halfway Landing and the falls. No marker. No snag breaking the surface. Just the slow green slide of the Meridian around a bend that Amos Kettle had drawn on a piece of butcher paper with a carpenter's pencil.

*Where the heron stands alone,* the map said. *Count nine heartbeats of current past the split stone. She sleeps below the third.*

The split stone was there — two slabs of granite shouldered apart by some ancient flood, standing out of the shallows like a door left ajar. A heron had been standing on it when they came through at dawn, gray and patient, and it was still there now, or its twin was, watching them with one yellow eye.

"You don't have to go first," Sefa said beside him. He'd been coiling rope with those enormous careful hands of his, and he didn't look up when he said it.

"I do, though." Vesper checked the regulator, bit it, tasted rubber and cold. "It's my map."

"It's a dead man's guess is what it is." Sefa finally looked at him, and there was no unkindness in it, just the flat honesty Vesper had already learned was the price of the man's company. "Water like this, visibility's an arm's length if you're lucky. You could put your hand on her hull and not know what you're touching until you feel the rivets."

"That's why I'm going first."

Junie appeared at the rail with the second tank slung over her shoulder like a sack of meal. "And I'm going second, so nobody get sentimental about it." She'd argued her way onto the dive roster the night before with a logic Vesper couldn't beat: *if it's a wreck, there'll be silt, and if there's silt, whoever goes down first stirs it all up blind, so you want someone small and mean behind you who can find their way back by feel.* "Odile, keep the line taut but not tight. If I yank twice, come get us."

"If either of you yanks twice," Odile said, "I'm coming after you myself, and I swim like a stove."

Vesper almost smiled. Almost. Then he swung his legs over the stern, filled his lungs, and let the river take him.

* * *

The cold hit first, then the dark, then the roar — the current overhead like a freight train running on the roof of the world. He equalized, dropped, and the world went from brown to black-brown to a darkness that pressed on his eyes like thumbs.

He found the bottom at thirty feet and it was nothing. That was the first shock of it. Not rock, not gravel bar, but fine gray silt, deep and soft as flour, stretching away into nothing in every direction. His fins stirred it and the cloud swallowed his own hands. He hung on the descent line in a blindness of his own making, heart hammering, feeling like a man buried alive who'd volunteered for it.

Then he remembered Amos's voice, that last morning, papery and certain: *You won't see her, boy. You'll feel her. A ship that big leaves a shadow in the mud long after the wood's gone soft.*

He swam a slow spiral off the line, arms out, fingers trailing.

On the fourth circuit his left hand stopped.

Not mud. Wood. Wide, flat, ridged — planking, and beneath the planking something harder, curved, deliberate. He swept his palm along it. The curve rose and fell like the shoulder of a sleeping animal. He followed it ten feet, twenty, and it did not end, did not narrow, did not become a log or a snag. It became a *side*. Riveted iron plate under rotted sheathing, and his fingertips found the seam where two plates met, and the heads of the rivets, each one smooth as a coin.

His heart wasn't hammering anymore. It had gone very quiet and very large, filling his chest like a second lung.

She was here. Or something was.

He fumbled for the slate on his wrist, scratched in the dark: *HULL. IRON. BIG.* Then he felt along the side until he found what he was looking for — an opening, a hatchway, its cover long since gone to rot, breathing cold water at him like a cellar door.

He went in.

* * *

The interior was worse than the exterior, which was saying something. The silt inside hadn't settled; it hung in the water like weather, and his lamp — he'd brought the little hand lamp against everyone's advice — showed him nothing but a brown fog lit from within, glowing like a jar of river held up to the sun.

But the space was wrong for a ship.

He'd grown up around wrecks the way other kids grew up around churches. He knew what a hold felt like from the inside — the long throat of it, the frames marching past like ribs, the way your lamp found the keel line and ran it home. This was different. The space was shallow. Low-ceilinged. Flat-bottomed. And there were partitions — bulkheads every fifteen feet or so, dividing the length into compartments, which no cargo ship built for open water would bother with on a river run.

He knew what he was in before his mind agreed to say it. He'd seen a dozen of them rusting at the landings of his childhood, riding high and empty, flying the Joubert house flag.

A barge. A river barge, iron-hulled, compartmented for bulk cargo.

The Meridian Star had been a three-masted steamer, ocean-capable, the pride of his father's last season. She had no business being this shape. Whatever lay under his hands, this was not her.

For a long moment he hung there in the brown fog and felt the promise shift under him like the silt itself. Amos had sent him to a place, and a place had answered — but not with the answer he'd been promised.

Then his lamp caught something that made the question change shape entirely.

There were marks on the bulkhead. Chiseled, deliberate, cut into the iron plate itself where no wave or sand could reach without help. He wiped his glove across it and the fog cleared enough to read:

**J.S.C. — MERIDIAN LINE — NO. 7**

Joubert Salvage Company. His father's mark, his family's name, stamped into a barge that had been sunk in a channel that no longer existed, forty miles from any landing anyone used.

And below the number, smaller, deeper, cut by a different hand with a colder patience:

**SEALED BY ORDER — R.J.**

R.J. Rowan Joubert. His father.

Vesper's breath went loud in his own ears. He hung in the drowned dark of a ship his own blood had ordered closed, and understood, with the slow cold certainty of the water pressing on him, that Amos Kettle had not sent him to a treasure.

He'd sent him to a grave. And graves kept things people wanted buried.

He backed out of the compartment, found the line, and pulled twice.

* * *

They gave the site the rest of the day and most of the night, working by lantern on deck while Odile kept the anchor watch and Junie drew everything Vesper described onto a sheet of oilcloth propped against the mast step.

"A barge," she said for the third time, pencil moving. "Number seven. Sealed by order of your father." She didn't look up when she said it, which was how Vesper knew she'd noticed the way he'd gone still at the initials. She'd been noticing things about him since Halfway Landing. She filed them the way she filed everything — quietly, completely, against a day she might need them.

"The Meridian Star was a steamer," Sefa said. He sat with his forearms on his knees, turning a shackle bolt over and over in his hands, thinking with his fingers the way he always did. "Big girl. Ocean lines. I've seen the old photos in the salvage offices — everybody has. She sank in the flood year, that's the story."

"That's the story," Vesper said.

"So what's a company barge doing down there, sealed shut, in a channel that dried up before I was born?" Junie tapped the oilcloth. "Barges sink by accident all the time. Nobody seals a barge. You seal a strongbox. You seal a—"

"—a hold," Vesper finished.

Sefa looked up. "The hold beneath the hold."

That was what Amos had called it, in one of the fever-clear hours near the end, when his eyes had gone sharp as awls: *Everyone looks for the Star's hold, boy. Look under the holds. Under the holds is where my river keeps its receipts.* Vesper had taken it for fever-talk, riddle-talk, the poetry dying men use to make themselves feel less finished. Now it sat differently.

"We go back down tomorrow," Vesper said. "At slack water, with the cutting gear. Those compartments are sealed for a reason, and I want to know what's in them."

"And if it's cargo?" Odile asked from the wheelhouse doorway, arms crossed. "If it's ore, or pelts, or crates of tinned peaches?"

"Then we've found a sunken barge and we've lost a legend, and we go looking for the Star somewhere else." He met her eyes. "And if it isn't cargo—"

"Then your daddy's name is on a locked door at the bottom of the river," Odile said, "and you're the only one of us carrying the key to what it means." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "I'll ask once, Joubert. Is there something you know that we don't?"

The lantern hissed. Downriver, miles off, thunder muttered over the divide like a clearing throat.

"I know what Amos told me," Vesper said. "Which is that the truth is in the water, and I promised to bring it up. That's everything I know. I swear it."

Odile studied him for a long moment, the way she studied water before taking a boat through it — not for what was visible, but for what wasn't. Then she nodded once. "Slack water's at ten. Get some sleep."

* * *

Sleep didn't come. Vesper climbed to the bow instead and sat with his back against the windlass, watching the river run silver-black under the stars, and took out the map.

Amos's pencil lines were faint now, worn soft where Vesper's thumb had worried them across six hundred miles. *Below the third.* They'd found the third thing — the barge — and it wasn't the Star, and yet the map had led true. Which meant either Amos had been wrong about what slept here, or—

Or the map had never pointed at the Star at all. Or it pointed through her. *Look under the holds.* What if the Star herself had been sunk deliberately, not lost — sunk on purpose, over this, over whatever his father had sealed into barge number seven, a great steel lid dropped on a smaller box? Sink a famous steamer in a flood and no one asks questions. Floods are God's work. But a company barge, sealed by order, hidden in a dead channel — that's a man's work. That's a secret that needed hiding twice.

*Deliver what's in her hold to the people she was stolen from.*

Stolen from. Vesper had assumed Amos meant cargo — ore, wages, a community's wealth locked in a rich man's hull. Sitting in the dark with the river talking to itself below him, another reading opened like a crack in ice. What if what had been stolen wasn't goods? What if it was proof?

Footsteps on the deck. Junie, wrapped in a blanket, lowering herself against the windlass beside him without asking. For a while neither of them spoke. That was one of the things he'd begun to love about this crew without meaning to — they knew when silence was the work.

"My father," Vesper said finally, because someone had to break it and it wasn't going to be her. "Rowan Joubert. You've heard the name."

"Everybody in salvage's heard the name." She picked at a thread on the blanket. "Fleet of eleven boats, biggest operation on the river, gone inside two years. My uncle worked one of his dredges. Said Joubert was the only owner who ever paid out a share of a find to the deckhands without being sued into it." She paused. "Also said he went strange the last season. Quiet. Selling boats off one at a time like a man paying debts nobody could see."

Vesper stared at the water. *Paying debts nobody could see.* Or burying them.

"Tomorrow we cut into those compartments," he said. "Whatever's down there, it goes to the light. All of it. That's the promise."

Junie was quiet a moment. "You're scared of what it is."

"Yes."

"But you're going anyway."

"That's the deal I made."

She nodded slowly, and then, unexpectedly, she leaned her shoulder against his, brief and hard, the way you'd bump a horse to tell it you meant no harm. "Well," she said. "Good. Because I signed on for a cut of a legendary wreck, and if it turns out to be a bunch of papers, somebody better be able to tell me why the papers were worth killing a river over."

Vesper looked at her. "Killing a river over?"

She nodded downriver, toward the dark bend where the lanterns of Corvin's survey scow had been visible the night before, patient as ticks. "Your uncle's fleet doesn't buy up every chart from here to the falls because they're sentimental about cartography, Vesper. Somebody wants this stretch of river searched *thoroughly*. And thorough men with dredges don't stop at barges."

Below them, thirty feet down in the cold and the dark, barge number seven waited with its sealed compartments and its chiseled orders, keeping whatever it had kept for thirty years.

Ten more hours to slack water. Vesper found he was counting them like a man counting the seconds before a storm makes landfall — not because he wanted it to arrive, but because he already knew there was no anchoring it out.
