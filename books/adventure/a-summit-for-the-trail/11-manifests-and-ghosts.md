## 11. Manifests and Ghosts

The manifests came out of the water smelling like the bottom of the world.

Junie Marsh had hauled a lot of things off riverboats in her time — drowned furniture, crates of bootleg liquor, once an entire piano that took four men and a prayer to lift — but she had never hauled up paper that still wanted reading. Yet here it was, spread across the galley table of the *Marta Kay* under three lanterns and Odile's oilskin, the pages warped into gentle waves like something breathing. Sefa had dried them over the stove in shifts, turning each sheet with tongs the way you'd handle a bird with a broken wing. The ink had held. Whatever the company had used to print these, it had been meant to last.

*Meridian Star. Manifest of Cargo. Final Sailing.*

The name ran across the top of every page in a typeface Junie knew from a hundred salvaged ledgers, a hundred company notices nailed to river-town walls. Joubert Salvage & Transport. The company that had owned half the upper river before it owned nothing at all. The company whose name she'd grown up hearing the way other children heard ghost stories.

She turned the first page with the tongs, careful as a surgeon.

Grain. Salt pork. Chain and block. Diesel in drums. Medicine — a whole crate of it, marked for the river clinics, the kind of shipment that arrived once a year if the year was kind. And at the bottom, in the column where the clerk recorded *disposition*, a single word repeated down the line like a heartbeat:

*Diverted. Diverted. Diverted.*

Not lost. Not transferred. Diverted — before the ship ever sailed. Every item on the manifest had been struck through and rerouted, and at the foot of each page, where the authorization line waited, the same signature sat in a strong, slanted hand that had clearly been written by a man who never doubted his own pen.

*E. Joubert.*

Edmond Joubert. Vesper's father.

Junie set the tongs down and looked at the signature for a long time.

She wasn't a fool. She'd known from the first week there was something the man wasn't saying — the way he went quiet when the old wrecks came up in river talk, the way he handled Amos Kettle's map like it might bite him, the way he'd flinched — actually flinched — when Odile had said *Joubert* that night at the dock in Halfway Landing, casual as commenting on the weather. She'd filed it away the way you file away a current you can't see but can feel pulling at your keel. You don't fight it yet. You just note it, and you keep your hand near the wheel.

Now the current had a name, and it was written in ink on a dead man's paper, and it was the same name as the man sleeping in the forward bunk not ten feet from where she sat.

* * *

The camp was quiet in the way river camps get quiet after midnight — not silent, never silent, the water talking to itself against the hull, the anchor chain ticking in the swell, Sefa's snore rolling out of the engine compartment where he'd fallen asleep over the fuel lines for the third night running since somebody had cut them. Junie sat wrapped in a blanket with the manifests stacked in front of her, and she had read every page twice, and she had not yet decided what to do with what she knew.

That was the thing nobody told you about found family. It wasn't free. You built it out of strangers the way you built a raft out of driftwood, and it held you up fine on calm water, and then one night the current changed and you found out which pieces were lashed together and which were just leaning.

Vesper came out of the wheelhouse at half past one. She heard the hatch, heard his boots on the deck, heard him stop when he saw the lantern still burning in the galley. He stood there a moment — she could feel him deciding whether to pretend he hadn't noticed — and then he came in, ducking under the low frame, and poured himself coffee from the pot that had been on the stove so long it could have stripped paint.

"You should be sleeping," he said.

"So should you."

"I sleep better on deck." He leaned against the doorframe, cup in both hands, and looked out at the black water. He looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the diving. Three days in cold water in a patched drysuit, hauling silt and chain off a sunken company barge, and he hadn't complained once, and he hadn't slept more than four hours any night, and Junie had watched him do it with something that had started as admiration and was turning, page by page, into something colder.

"Vesper." She said his name flat, the way Odile said *bar* when she meant *you're about to run aground.*

He turned. He saw the manifests. She watched him see them — watched the recognition move through his face like a fish under ice, there and gone, smoothed over so fast a stranger would have missed it. But she wasn't a stranger. Not anymore. That was the whole problem.

"Where'd you find these?" he asked. His voice was level. Too level. She'd heard him talk to Corvin's boarding officer with that same voice, and she'd heard him talk to a frightened kid at the fuel dock with that same voice, and she was starting to hate it.

"Forward hold. Under the false floor with the ledgers." She turned a page with one finger, not the tongs, and let him watch her do it. "You went through this compartment yourself. Day one. You told us it was stripped."

"It was."

"It wasn't."

The river ticked against the hull. Somewhere down the bank an owl asked its question and got no answer.

"Junie." He set the coffee down. "If you've got something to say, say it."

"Fine." She stood up. She wasn't tall — five foot two in boots, and she'd spent her whole life being talked down to by men who thought height was a qualification — so she made up for it the only way she knew, which was to get close and keep her voice low and never blink first. "Edmond Joubert."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not surprise. Confirmation. He'd known this was coming, maybe from the day he'd taken the map off a dying man, maybe from the day he'd learned to walk. He put his hands flat on the table and waited, and that patience of his — that infuriating, bottomless patience — made her want to shake him.

"The manifests say the Meridian Star's cargo was diverted before she sailed," Junie said. "Medicine. Grain. Supplies that half the river was owed. Diverted where, it doesn't say. But it says who signed for it." She turned the last page around and pushed it across the table so the signature faced him. "That's your father's name, Vesper. That's your father's hand. I've seen it on a dozen company papers out of the Halfway records office, and so have you."

Vesper looked at the signature. He looked at it for a long time, and Junie watched his face do the thing she'd seen drowning men's faces do on the river — the moment when the struggle stops being about the water and starts being about whether you want to come up at all.

"Say something," she said.

"What do you want me to say?"

"The truth would be a novelty."

He laughed, but there was no humor in it, just air escaping. He sat down across from her, slowly, like an old man, and put his face in his hands, and for a moment — just a moment — Junie saw past the competence and the calm and the careful walls to the thing underneath, and the thing underneath looked young, and lost, and ten years tired.

"Junie." His voice came out muffled through his fingers. Then he lifted his head, and the walls were back up, and she could have screamed. "I took a promise off a dying man. That promise is the only reason any of us are on this boat. When I can tell you the whole of it, I will. Until then—"

"Until then you want me to haul rope and keep my mouth shut and trust you."

"I want you to trust me, yes."

"On what?" She slapped the table and the lantern flame jumped. "On the name? The name's a lie. On the story? The story's got a hole in it big enough to drive a dredge through. You knew what was in that hold before we ever broke into it. Don't tell me you didn't — you dove straight to the false floor, Vesper. Straight to it, first dive, like you had it charted. Because you did. Because Amos Kettle drew you a map and you've known since the trapping cabin what we'd find down there, and you let us bleed for it — let Odile run the narrows at night, let Sefa get his hands cut to ribbons on that chain, let all of us risk Corvin's guns — without one word of warning about what we were actually digging up."

"I didn't know all of it."

"You knew enough."

He didn't deny it. That was the worst part. He sat there and took it, jaw set, eyes steady on hers, and he didn't deny a word, and Junie realized with a cold drop in her stomach that she'd have preferred a fight. A fight she could work with. Silence she couldn't.

"You know what I did before this?" she said, quieter now, because the anger was burning down to something worse. "I worked the salvage yards. I grew up on stories about the Joubert fleet — how they were honest, how they paid fair, how Edmond Joubert went down with his company because the river turned on him. Half the deckhands on this stretch learned the trade on Joubert boats. My uncle ran deck for your father two seasons. When the company folded, men lost everything. Houses. Boats. Some of them never went back on the water." She pushed the manifest toward him again. "And now you're telling me — or not telling me — that the cargo those river towns were owed never sank at all. That it was diverted. Signed away. By the man whose name half this river still says like a prayer."

"I know what it means," Vesper said.

"Do you? Because from where I sit, it looks like you came up this river to clear your family's name, and now the paper's saying something you didn't bargain for, and you're going to sit on it just like your uncle would sit on it, just a politer version, just with better manners and a medicine plane instead of a dredge fleet—"

"That's enough."

It was the first time she'd heard him raise his voice. It wasn't loud. It was worse than loud — it was the voice of a man who had decided the conversation was over, and Junie had been on enough boats to know that when the man at the wheel decides the conversation is over, you either obey or you jump ship.

She didn't do either. She stood there, and she looked at him, and she said, "Odile's going to figure it out too. She already knows you're a Joubert; she's known since Halfway Landing. And Sefa — Sefa traded you his labor for your honesty, his words, the first night. You shook on it. When he finds out you've been rationing the truth out like it's fuel in a leak tank, he's going to take it worse than I am, because Sefa doesn't get lied to. Not again. Not by anybody. You want this crew, Vesper? Then understand something. We're not your hands. We're not your hired muscle. We came up this river because we thought we were all going after the same thing together."

"And what do you think that thing is?" he asked, and his voice had gone quiet again, and tired, and it was almost worse than the raised one.

"I don't know," Junie admitted. "That's the hell of it. I don't know anymore."

* * *

She left him in the galley with the manifests and went out on deck, because she couldn't stand the smell of the lantern oil and the wet paper and the coffee another minute. The night was cold and enormous. Upstream, the oxbow waited under its blanket of silt and falling water, three tides from now, two, she'd lost count — and somewhere behind them, downstream, the lights of Corvin Joubert's flagship dredge burned like a town that had learned to float.

Odile was at the rail, wrapped in her coat, smoking one of the cigarettes she claimed she'd given up. She didn't look over when Junie came to stand beside her. Odile never looked over. She watched the water the way she did everything — like it might try something.

"Well?" Odile said, after a while.

Junie exhaled. "You heard."

"I hear everything on this boat. Walls are thin and Sefa snores like a landslide." Odile flicked ash into the river. "So. The great Edmond Joubert. Saint of the upper river. Turns out he was signing away medicine while they were naming clinics after him."

"You don't know that's what happened."

"I know what a diverted manifest is, girl. I ran cargo for six years before the *Pelican* went down. Somebody diverted that cargo, and somebody profited, and the signature's the signature." She took a drag. "Question is whether the son knew."

"Did you ask him?"

"Ask him?" Odile's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "He'd have given me that voice. The level one. The one he uses on boarding officers and frightened children." She dropped the cigarette and ground it under her heel. "Here's what I know about men like that, Junie. Men who keep moving. Men who won't stay anywhere, won't owe anybody, won't let a thing be finished. They're not running from a place. They're running from a fact. And sooner or later the fact catches up, and then you find out what they're made of."

"And if he's made of the same thing his uncle's made of?"

Odile was quiet for a long moment. The river talked. The chain ticked. Down the bank, the owl asked again.

"Then we'll know by the oxbow," she said finally. "Three tides. Watch him at the oxbow. That's where the truth comes up or it doesn't." She pushed off the rail and headed for the wheelhouse, then stopped, her hand on the frame. "For what it's worth — I don't think he's Corvin. I think he's something else. I just don't know yet if it's better."

She went inside. Junie stayed at the rail.

* * *

It was nearly three when the galley lantern went out. She heard Vesper's boots cross the deck, heard them stop behind her. He didn't come closer. He stood a body's length back, in the dark, where she could hear him breathing and smell the river on him, and for a moment neither of them said anything, and it was almost — almost — like the old nights, the early nights, when silence between them had been easy.

"I'm not going to tell you what you want to hear," he said at last. "Not tonight. Maybe not before the oxbow. I took a promise, and the promise has an order to it, and if I break the order I break the whole thing, and everything Amos Kettle carried dies with him. That's all I can give you right now. I know it isn't enough."

"It isn't," Junie said, without turning around.

"I know."

"Vesper." She turned then, and the dark made it easier to say the next part, the part she'd been carrying since the galley. "I'm not leaving. I want you to know that, so you don't waste time wondering. Whatever's down in that oxbow, I'm on this boat when it comes up. But I'm watching you now. Odile's watching. Sefa will too, once he knows, and he'll know soon, because I'm not carrying this alone and I won't lie for you. You want this crew? Then you'd better hope that whatever you're hiding turns out to be worth what it's costing. Because we're not your family yet. We're not anything yet. We're just people on the same boat, and the water's getting shallow."

He stood there in the dark, and she heard him take that in, all of it, the way he took everything — completely, silently, without flinching.

"Fair," he said. Just that. And then: "Get some sleep, Junie. Tide turns at six."

His boots went away across the deck. The hatch opened and shut.

Junie stayed at the rail until her hands were numb, watching the black water slide past, thinking about signatures and ghosts and the difference between a family and a crew, and about how the two felt exactly the same right up until the moment they didn't.

Downstream, faint against the stars, the lights of the flagship dredge had moved.

They were closer than they'd been at dusk — and they weren't holding position anymore.
