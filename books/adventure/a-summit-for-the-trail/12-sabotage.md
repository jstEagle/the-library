## 12. Sabotage

Sefa Tuilagi woke to the smell of diesel in the water.

Not the faint sheen that rode the river everywhere, not the ghost of fuel that clung to every barge and dredge from Halfway Landing to the head of navigation — this was wrong. This was close. This was their own bilge breathing it back at them, sweet and sharp as a cut fruit, and it was coming up through the deck boards under her knees before she'd even finished sitting up on her bunk.

She didn't swear. Her mother had taught her that much: when something breaks, swearing is a tax you pay twice. She pulled on her boots, took the flashlight off its nail, and went down into the engine room barefoot-hearted and cold-handed, and there it was.

The fuel line to the port engine had been opened. Not burst — opened. The hose clamp at the filter housing sat loose on the deck plating like a shed skin, and the line itself had been backed off half a turn, just enough to weep. A slow leak, timed to fail in the dark, timed so they'd lose the fuel overnight and find it in the morning with the tide already running out beneath them.

Sefa crouched and put her finger in the wet. Diesel, yes — but around the fitting, on the housing, a smear of grease that wasn't theirs. River grease, black and gritty, the kind the dredge yards used by the drum. She wiped it on her rag without saying anything to anyone yet, because the first thing you do with evidence is keep it, and the second thing you do is think.

The third thing you do is count who was awake.

* * *

By full light the whole crew stood in the engine room, and the whole crew had the same look Sefa had seen once on the faces of men in Apia when a cyclone took the roof off the church — the look of people recalculating what shelter meant.

"Half a turn," Vesper said. He crouched where she'd crouched, turning the fitting gently with two fingers, like it might tell him something if he asked it politely enough. "That's not vandalism. That's arithmetic. Whoever did this wanted us stopped for one tide, not dead."

"One tide," Odile said from the companionway, arms crossed. "We're down to five after today. You do the sums."

"I've been doing nothing else since I woke up." Junie was leaning against the bulkhead with her jaw set hard, and Sefa noticed she hadn't looked at any of them directly since she came below. "Four camps between here and the oxbow. Four places we stopped, bought ice, traded talk. Somebody in one of those places has a radio and an uncle's phone number."

"You don't know that," Vesper said.

"No," Junie said. "I don't. That's the problem."

Sefa let them go around twice more, the way you let a rope twist itself out before you take hold of it. Then she held up the rag with the grease smear, and the room went quiet in a different way.

"This isn't ours," she said. "Dredge-yard grease. Black sand in it, from the lower bars. Whoever opened this line knew engines well enough to leave no tool marks and stupid enough to wipe their hands on the housing." She folded the rag closed. "So. Not a stranger passing through. Someone who worked on our boat or beside it, recently, and got nervous."

"Nervous about what?" Odile said.

"About us getting there first," Junie said.

Vesper stood. In the low light of the engine room his face had the particular stillness Sefa had learned to read over these weeks — the stillness of a man holding a door shut against something on the other side. He'd been carrying that look since the manifests. Since Junie had cornered him in the wheelhouse and asked him whose signature was on the diversion order, and he'd said *I need to be sure* in a voice that meant *I already am.*

"We patch it," Vesper said. "We splice in spare hose, we bleed the lines, and we lose the morning tide doing it. And then we stop being polite about our route."

"And how do we do that?" Odile asked.

Vesper looked at each of them in turn, and Sefa watched him make the decision the way you watch a man step off a dock onto a moving deck — commitment arriving all at once, in the legs.

"We lie," he said. "To everyone. From now on, every camp we touch hears a different story about where we're going and why. We split the truth into pieces and give nobody the whole thing. Including—" he paused, and Sefa saw the cost of the next word land on him before it landed on them, "—including each other, until we're past the last camp. It's the only way to starve whoever's listening."

Junie pushed off the bulkhead. "You want us to lie to each other."

"I want us to survive long enough to never have to again."

Nobody answered that. Sefa rolled up her sleeves instead, because somebody had to bleed the fuel lines, and because the silence in the engine room had gotten heavy enough that work was the only thing that would float it.

* * *

The repair ate the whole falling tide.

It shouldn't have. Splicing a fuel line was an hour's work on a good day, and Sefa was fast — she'd kept the generators running at the cannery through two seasons of salt rot and worse management. But the sabotage had a second layer, and she found it only when she went to draw from the reserve tank and the gauge told her a story that didn't match the sound the tank made when she rapped it.

They'd siphoned her too. Not drained — trimmed. Enough fuel gone that even patched, they couldn't run the upper narrows against the current without stopping to buy more. And the only place to buy more between here and the oxbow was the Kettle Bar camp, the trading post on the left bank where the channel pinched and every boat had to slow to walking speed whether it wanted to or not.

"They want us walking pace at Kettle Bar," Sefa said, wiping her hands, standing in the engine room doorway while the others gathered. "Patched, fueled, slowed down, and visible. We're not being stopped. We're being delivered."

Odile laughed — one short, ugly note with no humor in it. "Whoever's running this knows boats."

"Knows *our* boat," Junie said quietly. "Knew where the reserve gauge was. Knew we'd burn the main tank first."

Sefa watched Vesper take that one. It went into him somewhere deep, the way a fishhook goes in past the barb — no pulling it straight back out. Because there were only so many people who knew this boat's tanks, and most of them were standing in this room, and the ones who weren't had names like Corvin Joubert and *the fleet.*

"It could've been anyone at the last three landings," Vesper said, but his voice didn't believe it, and nobody pretended otherwise.

"So we don't go to Kettle Bar," Odile said.

"There's no other fuel inside forty miles."

"Then we go in dark," Odile said. "No lights, mid-channel, and if they hail us we're a fish buyer running late. And we buy exactly what we say we're buying and nothing else, and we don't tell them our route, and we don't sleep there."

"That's still walking pace past their dock," Junie said.

"Yes," Odile said. "It is. Which means whatever happens at Kettle Bar happens slow enough to see coming. I'd rather see it coming." She looked around the circle of faces. "Anybody got better?"

Nobody did. Sefa went back to bleeding the lines, and the day burned down around them like a wick, and the tide they should have ridden upstream went out without them, carrying four hours of Amos Kettle's borrowed time off toward the sea.

* * *

They ran for Kettle Bar at dusk, and Sefa drove the engines the way she liked to drive them — hands light, ears open, listening to the port engine's new splice the way you listen to a child with a fever. It held. It sang a little high on the load, a thin whistle at the top of its voice, but it held.

The camp came up out of the dark the way river camps do: a scatter of lamplight, the smell of woodsmoke and frying fish, a dock built of salvaged planking that had clearly never met a level in its life. Two boats tied alongside. A dredge tender, company gray, riding high and empty.

Sefa felt the whole crew see it at the same time. Nobody said anything. Odile brought them in slow, and Vesper went ashore alone with the fuel money, which was the plan, and Sefa stayed in the engine room with the hatch cracked open, because she'd decided somewhere in the last hour that she trusted the night air more than she trusted the quiet.

She heard the transaction through the hull. Voices blurred by water and planking, but tones carry — tone is the one thing that doesn't need words. Vesper's voice, flat and bored, playing the fish buyer. The camp man's voice, easy, then easier than easy, the way voices get when they're working at it. And underneath both, a third sound: boots on the dock boards, unhurried, going away from them toward the gray tender.

Then the tender's engine started, and its running lights came up, and it pulled away downstream — downstream, mind, away from the oxbow, away from everything worth knowing — and Sefa stood in her engine room with her hand flat against the hull and understood that she had just watched a message leave.

She climbed out onto the deck. Vesper was coming back up the dock with the fuel chit in his hand and a face like weather.

"Well?" Junie said.

"We're fueled." He handed her the chit without looking at it. "And the man at the desk asked me how the repairs were coming. On the barge." He let that sit. "I told him we lost a day to a bad fuel line. His sympathy sounded very sincere."

"He asked before you said anything?" Sefa said.

"He asked before the gangway touched the dock."

The four of them stood in the lamplight at the rail, and the river moved black and patient beneath them, and somewhere downstream a gray tender was carrying their schedule to someone who paid for schedules.

"Four tides," Odile said finally. "After tonight, four."

"Then we stop sleeping at docks," Vesper said. "Anchor mid-channel. Anchor watches in pairs. Nobody goes ashore alone, nobody tells anybody anything, and from here to the oxbow we are four strangers in a boat who happen to be going the same direction."

"That's a lonely way to run a river," Junie said.

"It's the way I ran rivers for ten years," Vesper said. "It works."

He said it like a boast. It landed like a confession, and Sefa heard it land, and she thought: *this man learned to be alone so well that now, when being alone might save them, it costs him something to teach it.* She didn't say that either. She said, "I'll take first anchor watch," and went to check the splice one more time, because the splice was hers, and whatever else on this boat could be turned against them, the engines would turn only when she said.

* * *

They anchored in the middle of the river where the channel widened below the bar, lights doused except the riding lamp the law required, and the night came down around them thick as wool. Sefa took the bow watch with Junie, the two of them wrapped in blankets against the damp, the current talking to itself around the anchor chain.

For a long time neither spoke. Then Junie said, "You checked my boots."

Sefa didn't pretend otherwise. "I checked everybody's boots. Yours, mine, the skipper's, Odile's. Grease smears, mud, anything from the lower bars."

"And?"

"And yours are clean," Sefa said. "So are mine. So are Odile's." She let the sentence finish itself.

Junie was quiet a moment. "You're telling me it could be him."

"I'm telling you I checked, and I'm telling you what I found, and I'm telling you I hate both halves of that sentence." Sefa pulled the blanket higher. "A man who lies to his own crew about why we're here is a man who can lie about other things. But a man who lies to keep a promise to a dying stranger is a different animal, and I've been watching both animals live in the same body for weeks now, and I still don't know which one feeds at night."

"Do you trust him?"

Sefa considered it honestly, because Junie deserved honesty even if the skipper had ordered them all to ration it. "I trust his hands," she said. "Hands don't learn to lie. When he spliced wire with me on the manifests crate, his hands were shaking. Liars' hands are steady. They've practiced."

Junie huffed something that was almost a laugh. "You judge people by their hands."

"My mother judged people by how they treated dogs and waiters. Same principle. The parts of a person that don't know they're being watched." Sefa nodded forward, into the dark, where the river ran on toward the oxbow and whatever Amos Kettle had died keeping. "His hands shook, Junie. Whatever he's hiding, it's costing him more than it's costing us."

Downstream, far off, a searchlight swept the water once — lazy, proprietary, the gesture of someone checking on property — and was gone. Neither of them said anything about it. There was nothing to say that the light hadn't already said.

Four tides. Sefa counted them the way she counted everything now, in hours of engine time, and the number felt less like distance than like a fuse.
