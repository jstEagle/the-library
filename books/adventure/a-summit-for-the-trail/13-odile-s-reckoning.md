## 13. Odile's Reckoning

The offer came at dusk, the way all the worst things in Odile Brant's life had come — politely, from a man with clean hands.

She was at the wheel when she saw it: a skiff running dark up the slough behind them, no lights, engine muffled to a whisper. Three years since her last command and still her hands knew the feel of a boat being watched. She cut the barge's running lamps one by one, casual as snuffing candles, and watched the skiff in the black glass of the wheelhouse window until it turned away toward the far bank.

They didn't leave. They anchored.

"Company boat," Junie said, appearing at her shoulder with a mug of coffee she hadn't been offered. The girl had a way of arriving exactly when something was about to go wrong, like weather. "Been sitting out there two hours. I counted their mast light blink four times. That's signal work."

"I know what it is." Odile set the wheel against the current and let the barge breathe on its anchor line. "Get Vesper. And Sefa. Quietly."

Junie went. Odile stayed at the wheel and looked out at the dark water sliding past the hull, and felt the old familiar cold settle into her chest, the one that had lived there since the *Marguerite* went down with her name on the pilot's license and someone else's fingerprints on the charts.

She'd known this day would come. From the moment Vesper Joubert had stood on the dock at Halfway Landing with his ruined plane and his careful eyes and asked her if she could run the upper river blind, some part of her had understood that Corvin Joubert's money would eventually find its way to her door. Her name was worth something to him. Not much — but enough. A disgraced river pilot who'd lost her license over a wrecking she didn't cause was exactly the kind of instrument a man like Corvin liked to buy cheap and use once.

Footsteps on the deck plates. Vesper came into the wheelhouse first, then Sefa filling the doorway behind him, big as a door himself, and Junie slipping past both of them to stand at the chart table where she could see everything.

"Skiff off the starboard quarter," Odile said. "Anchored since before sundown. They signaled twice. They want to talk."

"To us?" Sefa asked.

"To me." She said it flat, and watched it land. Watched Vesper's face do the small complicated thing it did when he was deciding how much of a lie to tell. "I ran this river for eleven years, Mr. Joubert. I know what my name fetches on the open market. They're not here for you. Not yet."

Vesper looked at her for a long moment. "You don't have to talk to them."

"No," she agreed. "But they'll talk to me whether I answer or not, and I'd rather know what they're selling." She pulled her jacket off the hook by the door. "Drop the skiff. I'll row over myself. That way nobody else is implicated in whatever gets said."

"You're not going alone," Sefa said.

"She is," Odile said, before Vesper could speak. "Because if I go alone, whatever happens over there stays between me and them. If any of you come, you're witnesses, and witnesses are leverage." She met Vesper's eyes. "You've been keeping your secrets, Captain. Let me keep mine for one hour."

Something moved behind his face — recognition, maybe, of a fellow keeper of locked rooms. He nodded once. "One hour. If you're not back, we come get you."

"If I'm not back," Odile said, shrugging into the jacket, "don't. That's an order I'm giving you, even though it isn't mine to give."

* * *

The water between the barge and the skiff was maybe two hundred yards of black current, and Odile rowed it slowly, letting herself feel every stroke. This was the part she'd rehearsed without meaning to, ever since Halfway Landing — the part where the past caught up and made its pitch. She'd imagined refusing it a hundred ways. She'd imagined accepting it more times than she would ever admit aloud. Full exoneration. Her license restored. Her name cleaned of the *Marguerite*, of the inquest, of the three years of dock work and drinking and small-town pity. There wasn't a price tag she could have named, because she'd never believed anyone would actually pay it.

The skiff was bigger up close than it had looked from the barge. Company-built, company-painted, a cabin cruiser with the working gear stripped out and carpet on the deck. Two men aboard besides the helmsman. One of them she knew.

"Milo Fenn," she said, tying off. "Last time I saw you, you were testifying."

"And lying," Milo said pleasantly. He was a soft-bodied man with a river lawyer's tan and a voice like warm oil, and he'd sat in that hearing room three years ago and sworn the *Marguerite*'s charts were sound while everyone in that room knew whose office had redrawn them. "Sit down, Odile. Have a drink. You look like the river's been hard on you."

"The river's been honest with me. That's more than I can say for you."

Milo smiled like she'd paid him a compliment. The other man — younger, silent, with the wide shoulders and empty eyes of hired muscle — poured whiskey into three glasses anyway, and Odile noted that he didn't drink. She didn't either.

"Here's the shape of it," Milo said, settling back against the rail. "Mr. Joubert has followed your little expedition with great interest. Great interest. He considers you a woman of — " he paused, savoring it — "complicated loyalties. A pilot stripped of her ticket over a wreck she swears she didn't cause. A woman with every reason to hate the industry that discarded her, and every reason to need what only that industry can give back." He produced an envelope from inside his coat and laid it on the deck between them, weighted with a brass fitting so it wouldn't blow away. "Full exoneration. Sworn statements from the survey office admitting the *Marguerite*'s charts were altered. Your license reinstated by week's end. Back pay for three years' grounding. And a bonus figure that would buy you a boat of your own, Odile. A real one. Your name on the stern."

Odile looked at the envelope and did not touch it. "And what does Mr. Joubert want for all that?"

"A map." Milo's voice stayed warm, reasonable, the voice of a man discussing freight rates. "The one Amos Kettle drew. We know the old man passed it along before he died. We know it's aboard your barge. Bring it to us — or bring us to it, we're not particular — and everything I've described is yours by Friday."

"And if I say no?"

Milo spread his hands. "Then nothing. Truly. No threats, no consequences. Mr. Joubert is a generous man, but he doesn't chase. You'll go on running your little errand, and we'll go on following you, and eventually the river will show us what it showed Kettle, and you'll have spent your one chance at getting your life back for the sake of a man who won't even tell his own crew what he's looking for." He leaned forward, and for just a moment the oil in his voice thinned and she could hear the steel underneath. "He hasn't told you, has he? What's in the hold. Why a man who hates his own name is racing his own uncle upriver. You're risking your neck, Odile, for a secret. Think about that. Think about who's kept secrets from you your whole career."

She thought about it. God help her, she thought about it — the hearing room, the altered charts, the years of dock wages and cheap rooms, the way her hands still ached sometimes for a wheel that mattered. Full exoneration. Her name on a stern.

"That's quite an offer," she said.

"It's the only one you'll ever get," Milo said. "We anchor here tonight. Tomorrow we follow. Take the envelope. Sleep on it. Nobody needs to know what you decide except you."

Odile picked up the envelope. Milo's smile widened.

She stood, stepped to the gunwale, and held it over the water.

"Don't," Milo said, and the warmth was gone entirely now. "That's company property."

"So is my name," Odile said. "Somebody stole it three years ago. I'm done letting men like you handle it."

She struck a match against the rail, touched it to the corner of the envelope, and dropped the whole burning thing into the current. It flared once on the black water, guttered, and went out. The ash rode downstream toward the sea, which was where she intended every piece of Corvin Joubert's generosity to end up.

"You stupid woman," Milo said quietly. "Do you know what you just burned?"

"Yeah," Odile said, untying the line. "Paper."

She rowed back through the dark with her heart going like a drum and her hands perfectly steady, the way they always were after the decision was made. That was the thing nobody understood about fear — it lived in the choosing, not the aftermath. Once you'd chosen, the water was just water.

* * *

They were all waiting in the wheelhouse when she came up the ladder — Vesper, Sefa, Junie, and Odile realized with a small jolt that none of them had pretended otherwise, none of them had gone below to give her privacy. They'd stood watch over her absence like a family standing vigil.

"Well?" Vesper said.

"They offered me my license back." She said it plainly, because anything less would be another kind of lie, and she'd had enough lies in her life to last two careers. "Full exoneration for the *Marguerite*. Sworn statements clearing my name. Back pay. A bonus big enough to buy my own boat. All of it, for one thing." She looked around the wheelhouse, at each of them in turn. "Amos Kettle's map."

Silence. Junie's eyes went wide. Sefa's jaw tightened. Vesper didn't move at all, but Odile saw his hand close slowly around the rail.

"What did you tell them?" Junie asked.

"I told them paper burns." She crossed to the chart table, took down the tin box where the map lived wrapped in oilcloth, and set it in the middle of the table where they could all see it. Then she told them the rest — Milo Fenn, the testimony, the promise that no one would chase, the reminder that Vesper had never told them what they were really looking for. She watched him take that last part like a body blow he refused to show.

"He's right about one thing," Odile said. "I signed on to pilot a boat upriver for a man I barely knew, chasing a wreck I knew nothing about, against a fleet that could crush us like eggshell. I did it because you were the first person in three years to ask me to do the thing I'm actually good at." She put her hand flat on the tin box. "But I'm not doing it for a secret anymore. Whatever's in that hold, whatever your father did or didn't do, whatever your uncle is willing to pay pilots and burn barges to bury — I want it said out loud. To all of us. Tonight. Because they'll make this offer again, Captain, and next time it might not be me they make it to. And the only thing that beats an offer like that is a reason to refuse it that everybody in this room owns together."

Vesper looked at the map. At her. At Sefa and Junie, who were both watching him now with the same question in their faces, the same question Odile had been carrying since the manifests — since the signature that belonged to a Joubert.

"You could have taken it," he said quietly. "Nobody would have blamed you. I wouldn't have blamed you."

"I know," Odile said. "That's why I couldn't."

He nodded then, slow, like a man setting down something heavy he'd forgotten he was carrying. "Tomorrow night, when we anchor above the narrows, I'll tell you everything. All of it — my father, the ship, what Amos gave me, what I think is down there. You'll get it straight, no trimming. That's a promise."

"We've heard a lot of promises on this river," Sefa rumbled.

"This one comes with the map," Vesper said, and pushed the tin box across the table to the middle of them — not to Odile, not to himself, to the space between all four of them. "Keep it in the galley safe. Two keys. Sefa holds one."

Sefa blinked. "Me?"

"You're the only one aboard who can't swim," Vesper said, and for the first time in days something almost like a smile crossed his face. "Figure it out."

Junie laughed — a short, startled bark — and then Sefa did, and then, to her own considerable surprise, so did Odile, standing in a wheelhouse on a stolen barge with a burned bridge smoking behind her and a burned envelope drifting somewhere downriver, laughing like a woman who had just gotten her name back after all, though not the way anyone had promised it.

Outside, the company skiff's mast light blinked once, twice, and held steady on the horizon. Following. Always following.

Odile turned back to the wheel and brought the heading around to the narrows channel, and the barge leaned into the current like something alive.

"Two tides left," she said to the dark ahead, to the crew behind her, to whoever was listening. "Let's not waste them."
