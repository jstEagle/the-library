## 16. Two Tides

The dredge started at first light, and the sound carried across the water like a confession.

Vesper stood at the rail of the *Kettle's Promise* — Junie had named her, over his objection, and the name had stuck the way true things do — and watched the mud come up out of the oxbow in long brown ropes. Sefa's rig was a jury-built thing, a crane arm welded to a winch that had spent its life hauling logs, but it worked. It worked slowly, and it worked loudly, and every bucket of silt it lifted off the old channel bed felt like a word spoken in a room where someone might be listening.

Someone was listening.

Corvin Joubert's flagship had come upriver two days ago without hurry, a white-sided monster of a vessel with *Meridian Salvage* painted along her flank in letters you could read from a mile off, and she had anchored four hundred yards downstream of the oxbow mouth. Not closer. Not farther. Four hundred yards was a message written in geometry: *we know where you are, and we are choosing to wait.*

"She hasn't run her pumps once," Odile said beside him, coffee steaming in both hands. She passed him one without looking at him. "A dredge that size burns fuel sitting still. He's paying by the hour just to watch us dig."

"He wants us to do the work."

"He wants to know if we find anything worth taking." Odile sipped. "There's a difference, and I don't like how thin it is."

Below them, Junie ran the winch with Sefa shouting depths, and the bucket came up again, streaming, and dumped its load onto the sorting grate with a wet slap. Nothing yet. Three days of nothing yet. But the water had dropped another foot overnight, the dry season doing exactly what Amos had promised it would do six tides ago, and the shape beneath them was becoming legible. Vesper had seen it at dawn, standing very still on the foredeck while the river exhaled mist around him: a long dark ridge under two feet of water, running parallel to the dead channel, too straight and too regular to be geology.

Hull timbers. Or iron. Something man-made, something big, lying exactly where a hand-drawn map made by a dying man said it would lie.

"Two tides," Odile said. She said it the way other people said *amen.*

Two tides before the dry season bottomed out and the river began its slow climb back. Two working days, maybe three if they pushed into the dark, before the oxbow drowned again and whatever slept down there went back to sleep for another thirty years. And somewhere behind them, Corvin's patrol boats were making their rounds of the camps, buying coffee and selling rumors, and somewhere ahead of them was a flagship full of men who got paid to take things off the bottom of rivers.

Vesper drank his coffee and watched the mud rise, and tried not to think about the fact that he had not slept more than three hours in any night since the manifests.

* * *

It was Junie who spotted the launch.

Midmorning, sun high enough to burn the mist off, and a white boat came around the bend below the flagship at an easy cruising speed — no wake, no urgency, a man out for a morning on the water. Except nobody took a morning on this stretch of river, and everybody knew whose colors flew from the stern antenna.

"They're coming alongside," Sefa called up from the sorting grate, and there was something new in his voice. Not fear. Readiness. He'd spent the night re-hanging the crane arm and checking the fuel lines twice, and Vesper knew what he'd been checking for, and so did everyone else aboard.

"Truce flag," Odile said. She had binoculars up. "He's flying white at the bow. That's either genuine or it's theater, and Corvin Joubert has never once in his life done anything that wasn't theater."

"What does river law say?" Junie asked. She'd climbed up to join them, hands still wet, and there was a wrench tucked into her belt that she hadn't bothered to hide.

"River law says a truce flag is a truce flag," Odile said. "Break one and every pilot on this water will refuse you dockage for the rest of your life." Her jaw tightened. "I would know."

Vesper looked at the launch growing larger, at the figure standing in the bow with one hand raised — palm open, empty, almost friendly — and felt the old cold thing move in his chest, the thing that had lived there since he was nineteen years old and had walked away from a house with his name on the gate.

"Let him board," he said.

* * *

Corvin Joubert came up the ladder like a man boarding his own yacht.

He was sixty-one and moved like fifty, silver at the temples and broad through the shoulders, wearing canvas trousers and a pressed shirt with the sleeves rolled — the uniform of a man who wanted you to know he could work but never had to. His eyes found Vesper immediately and stayed there, and Vesper understood, with a lurch, that everything else about the visit was going to be decoration around those eyes.

"You must be the crew that's been scaring my river camps," Corvin said pleasantly. "Halfway Landing's finest. A Tuilagi with a welder, a Marsh with a grudge, and—" he inclined his head toward Odile, and the pause was surgical— "Odile Brant. Still piloting. Good for you."

"Captain Brant," Odile said flatly.

"Captain Brant." Corvin smiled like the correction cost him nothing, because it did. Then he turned back to Vesper, and the smile changed — not warmer, exactly, but more specific. More interested. "And you. The man with the map."

"I don't know what you've heard."

"I've heard a great deal. I hear everything on this river; it's the only advantage of being old." Corvin walked the deck slowly, hands clasped behind his back, examining the sorting grate, the crane, the buckets of gray-brown silt with the idle curiosity of a man at a museum. "You're digging in the old oxbow. Do you know how many people have dug in the old oxbow? My father did, in '61. Two companies after him. Everyone's certain the Meridian Star went down in the main channel, because everyone's certain the river has always been where it is now." He stopped, and looked at Vesper, and the pleasantness drained out of his face like water out of a cracked hull. "But rivers move. And maps lie. And dying men talk."

The deck was very quiet. Behind Corvin, his launch idled, two crewmen visible aboard, watching. On the sorting grate, Sefa had gone still with a shovel in his hands. Junie had drifted, casually, to stand between Corvin and the cabin door, behind which the documents lay wrapped in oilcloth inside a false-bottomed crate.

"My brother's son," Corvin said.

The words landed on the deck between them and lay there.

Vesper kept his face still. Ten years of practice at keeping his face still. "My father is dead."

"My brother has been dead eleven years, yes. Drowned in debt and disgrace and a plane crash the investigators called weather." Corvin's voice didn't rise. It lowered, if anything, went softer, which was worse. "You have his chin. You have his way of standing when he's cornered — weight forward, like he's about to charge or about to laugh. I watched him do it across a boardroom table for twenty years." He tilted his head. "Elias Joubert's boy, flying medicine into the interior under a name that isn't quite ours, digging up my river with a stolen chart. Did you really think I wouldn't know? Amos Kettle was on the *Star* the night she went down. There were nine men on that ship, and eight of them worked for me afterward, and every one of them told me the same story before they died — except Kettle. Kettle disappeared. And now Kettle's map turns up in the hands of a bush pilot named Joubert who spells it with a *u*."

The river slapped against the hull. Somewhere inland a bird screamed and quit.

"You could have denied it," Corvin said. "You still can. But your mechanic just went pale, and your pilot just moved her hand toward that wrench, and neither of them knew, did they?" He sighed, and for one unguarded moment he looked genuinely tired, an old man standing on a stranger's deck. "You should tell people who you are, boy. Secrets are expensive. I've paid for mine my whole life."

"Why are you here?" Vesper asked. His voice came out level. He was proud of that, later, and ashamed of it too.

"Because you're my brother's son, and I am the last Joubert, and I am offering you a truce." Corvin turned and gestured, and one of his crewmen brought a leather case across the deck and set it on the sorting grate among the mud and the gravel. He opened it. Inside, on green felt, lay papers — neat, typed, stamped. "One: full salvage title to whatever you raise from this oxbow, transferred to you personally, clean and legal, signed today. The *Star*'s cargo was insured through my company; the claim was never fully settled; there are mechanisms. You'd be a wealthy man by Thursday. Two: a seat on the board. Actual equity. Your father's share, held in trust all these years — it's yours, it was always meant to be yours, Elias just never got around to telling anyone he had a son because he was too busy hating me." A muscle moved in his jaw, the first crack in the performance. "Three: the airstrip at Halfway Landing, deeded freehold. I own the paper on it. I'll tear the paper."

Junie made a small sound. Vesper didn't look at her. He was looking at the case, at the clean typed lines, at the signature block already bearing Corvin's name in ink that had dried years ago, waiting.

"And in exchange," Vesper said quietly.

"In exchange you walk away. Today. Take the barge south, take your crew, take the money, build your little airfield, fly your little medicine runs, and let professional salvagers finish a professional job." Corvin closed the case but didn't latch it. "You get everything you ever pretended not to want. Your name restored. Your father's legacy in your hands instead of mine. And I get—" he paused, choosing the word with a jeweler's care— "privacy."

"The hold," Vesper said. "Whatever's in the hold."

"There's nothing in the hold." Corvin said it smoothly, instantly, and that instant was the loudest thing on the deck. "She was a freighter, boy. Freighters carry freight. Freight rots. Thirty years underwater, there'll be nothing down there but sludge and rivets, and you'll have traded two tides of honest labor for a hole full of nothing." He smiled again, and this time it reached all the way to his eyes, and his eyes were cold as channel water in March. "Unless you believe a dying prospector's ghost stories. In which case, I'm sorry for you, and I'm still offering."

Vesper looked at the case. And here was the thing he would carry for the rest of his life, the thing he would examine at three in the morning at the airstrip with a cup of coffee going cold in his hand: for four full seconds, he wanted it.

Not the money, particularly. Not the board seat. He wanted the *name*. He wanted to walk into Halfway Landing and not feel the room recalibrate when someone said *Joubert.* He wanted his father to be the wronged man, the ruined visionary, the hero of a story that had been stolen from him — wanted it so badly his teeth ached. He wanted the version of the past where the Jouberts were robbed instead of robbers, where his exile had been noble instead of necessary, where coming home would mean something besides accounting.

Four seconds. Then he thought of Amos Kettle's hand on his wrist, light as a bird's, saying *promise me,* and of the manifests in the cabin behind Junie, and of a signature in faded ink that belonged to a man he had built a shrine to out of absence — and the wanting curdled, and died, and left something harder underneath.

"No," Vesper said.

Corvin studied him. "You haven't even looked at the numbers."

"I don't need to look at the numbers." Vesper stepped forward and closed the case himself, and latched it, and pushed it gently back across the grate toward the crewman. "You just told me what's in the hold, Uncle. You told me with that half-second. There's something down there you need buried worse than the *Star* needed sinking, and you've offered me a fortune to keep not looking at it." He heard Odile shift behind him; heard Sefa set the shovel down, deliberately, the way a man sets down a tool to pick up a weapon. "So here's my counteroffer. Take your launch, go back to your flagship, and stay four hundred yards away. When we raise what we raise, we'll raise it in daylight, in front of witnesses, and the river communities will see it first. Every camp. Every claim-holder. Before any court, before any company, before you."

Something moved behind Corvin's eyes — not anger. Recalculation. The look of a man revising a ledger.

"Elias was the same," he said softly. "Righteous at the worst possible moments. It's what killed him, you know. Not the weather." He picked up the case. "You think you're honoring him. You have no idea what you're honoring." He turned to go, then stopped at the ladder, one hand on the rail, and looked back, and for a moment the charm fell away entirely and what was underneath was old and hollow and almost sad. "Two tides, nephew. When the water comes back up, whatever you found goes back to being mine, because title follows the tide line on this river, and I wrote the regulation myself in 1988. Whatever you're going to do—" he tapped the rail once, twice, "—do it fast."

Then he was down the ladder, and the launch pulled away, and the white flagship's engines turned over with a sound like a large animal deciding something.

* * *

Nobody spoke until the launch was a speck.

Then Junie said, "Your uncle," in a voice like a door closing, and Sefa said, "Your *uncle*," in a voice like a door opening onto a much larger room, and Odile said nothing at all, just looked at Vesper with the expression of a woman adding up a column she'd been keeping in her head for nine chapters of river.

"Joubert," Sefa said. "As in *the* Joubert. As in the salvage family. As in the company that owns half the docks between here and the coast." He wiped his hands on his rag very slowly. "We've been diving for your family's treasure, and you let us think you were some broke bush pilot with a lucky map."

"It is a lucky map. And I am a broke bush pilot." Vesper leaned on the rail and watched the flagship settle back into its watchful stillness. "Everything else is complicated."

"The manifests," Junie said quietly. She'd known. Vesper saw it in her face — she'd known since the night she confronted him, since the signature, since the fracture she'd chosen not to walk away from. "The signature on the diversion orders. That was your father."

"Yes."

"And Corvin thinks there's something in that hold bad enough to buy you off with a fortune and a deeded airstrip." She crossed her arms. "Which means there's something in that hold bad enough to end him."

"Or end all of us," Odile said. She nodded downstream, at the flagship, at the two patrol boats that had appeared at the far bend, running lights on in full daylight. "He gave us a deadline and a threat in the same sentence. Tide line title. He'll have lawyers filing before sundown, and he'll have divers in this oxbow the minute our backs are turned, truce or no truce. That white flag bought him one look at our faces, and he got it."

Vesper looked at the sorting grate, at the mound of silt, at the dark ridge of hull showing through the shallowing water like a spine through skin. Two tides. Maybe thirty hours of pumping light. And a man anchored four hundred yards away who had just told him, in the language of offers, exactly how afraid he was.

"Sefa," Vesper said. "How fast can you cut through hull plating?"

Sefa grinned, and there was no warmth in it at all. "Depends how much sleep you're planning to give me."

"None."

"Then fast," Sefa said, and spat into the oxbow, and turned to light the torch.
