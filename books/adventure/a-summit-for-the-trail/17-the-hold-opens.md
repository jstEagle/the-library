## 17. The Hold Opens

### VESPER

The river fell all night, and by dawn it had given up its secret.

Vesper stood at the rail of the barge and watched the oxbow drain like a basin with a pulled plug. The water that had hidden the Meridian Star for thirty years slid away south, leaving behind a bowl of gray mud scored with channels, and in the center of it — dark as a wound, ridged along one flank like the spine of a buried animal — the hull.

Nobody spoke for a long time. The morning was cold enough to show breath.

"There she is," Junie said finally, and her voice came out smaller than Vesper had ever heard it.

She was bigger than he'd imagined. He'd spent ten days staring at Amos Kettle's pencil lines, at the riddle of annotations in a hand that shook, and somewhere in that time he'd built a ship out of grief and guesswork. The real thing was longer, blacker, more broken. Her stern had collapsed inward when she settled. Her name was still legible on what remained of the bow house, paint gone to ghost but letters there if you knew to look: MERIDIAN STAR. His father's flagship. The ship that had carried his family's fortune down this river and then, on a night his father never explained, stopped existing.

"Three tides," Odile said from the wheelhouse window. "This one's the last safe one. After tomorrow's drop, the current through the cut gets mean, and after that we're mudbound until spring."

"Then we don't waste it." Vesper turned to the others. Sefa was already in the dive gear, methodical as ever, checking each strap twice. Junie had the winch rigged. Odile stood with her arms crossed, watching the downstream bend where Corvin's dredge *Iron Corvine* sat anchored two hundred meters off, close enough that they could hear her generators, close enough that men on her deck could watch them with binoculars.

"They'll come the moment we find something," Odile said.

"I know."

"You want to say what we do about that?"

Vesper looked at the hull again. "We open the hold before they finish their coffee."

* * *

### SEFA

Sefa went down the anchor line into water the color of steeped tea, cold enough to squeeze his chest, and let himself sink along the hull's flank with one gloved hand trailing the steel.

He'd dived wrecks before — mostly modern ones, barges and tugs lost to honest accidents — and he'd learned that every wreck had a personality. Some were chaotic, torn open, their insides spilled across the bottom like gutted fish. This one was not. The Meridian Star had been put down deliberately and put down well. Her seacocks were open. Her hatches were dogged shut from inside. She had been laid on the bottom by someone who wanted her whole, someone who cared what happened to what she carried.

That thought followed him down through the brown murk, past the collapsed deckhouse, along the main deck where silt lay knee-deep and a drowned tree had rooted itself improbably through a cargo hatch coaming. His light found the forward cargo hold hatch. The dogs were rusted but intact. He worked them loose one at a time, feeling the metal grind, and when the last one gave the hatch cover lifted an inch and exhaled — a slow stream of bubbles rising past his mask, air trapped for three decades escaping into the river.

Inside, his light hit water so still and clear it looked solid. The hold below the waterline had sealed itself, an air pocket at the top now flooded, and everything beneath preserved in cold black silence.

Crates. Rows of them, stacked with care, lashed to ring bolts. Not ore sacks. Not machinery. Crates, stenciled with faded lettering he couldn't read through the silt film, each one small enough for a man to carry.

Sefa hung there in the doorway of the hold and felt the hair rise on his arms under the wetsuit. He'd expected gold. Everyone expected gold. Amos Kettle had talked about treasure the way dying men talk about anything — loosely, reverently, imprecisely. But nobody lashed treasure to ring bolts. Nobody stenciled treasure.

He clipped the lift line to the first crate and signaled the winch.

* * *

### JUNIE

The crate came up dripping and Junie caught it with both hands and nearly dropped it, because it weighed almost nothing.

"That can't be right," she said.

"It's right." Sefa was climbing over the rail, pulling his hood back, steam rising off his shoulders in the cold air. "They're all like that. Paper, I think. Sealed in something — wax, tin, I don't know. There are dozens. Maybe hundreds."

Junie set the crate on the deck planking and knelt over it. The stencil read, in flaking white letters: **RIVER DISTRICT CLAIMS — ORIGINAL INSTRUMENTS — DO NOT OPEN EXCEPT BEFORE WITNESS**.

Her mouth went dry.

She'd grown up on salvage lore the way other kids grew up on Bible stories. Every kid on the river knew the Meridian Star story — the flagship lost with all hands' wages, the Joubert fleet ruined by bad luck, the tragedy that made the family name both famous and cursed. Junie had heard a hundred versions in a hundred river bars, and in every version there was gold, or there was nothing, or there were ghosts. In no version was there paperwork.

"Vesper." She didn't look up. "You need to see this."

He crouched beside her. She watched his face as he read the stencil, watched something move behind his eyes — not surprise. Recognition. He'd suspected. Maybe he'd known since the manifests, since the night Junie confronted him in the wheelhouse and he'd admitted nothing and she'd decided to stay anyway, telling herself she'd signed on for a cut and nothing else. Liar, she'd told herself even then.

"Pry bar," Vesper said quietly.

"No." Odile's voice, sharp, from the wheelhouse steps. "Not yet. Read it again. *Before witness.* We're four people and a dead man's map. If whatever's in those crates is what that stencil says it is, we open it clean or we don't open it at all."

Junie looked up at her. Odile Brant, who'd burned Corvin's offer in front of all of them rather than sell them out, standing there with her jaw set like a woman defending a church.

"She's right," Sefa said. He'd stripped off his gloves and was flexing his bare fingers against the cold. "And we should get them all up first. If we open one and stop, and Corvin's people board us tonight, they take everything and we've told them what matters."

Vesper was quiet a moment. Then he nodded, once, and Junie saw him set aside whatever was burning in him — she recognized the look; she'd seen it on her own father's face over cards he was losing on purpose.

"All of them," Vesper said. "Then we open them together. All four of us. And Amos too." He touched his jacket pocket, where the folded map rode. "He gets to be here for it."

* * *

### ODILE

They worked the tide like a prayer.

Odile held the barge on station over the wreck while the river tried to shove her sideways, and between corrections she watched her strange crew become a machine. Sefa diving, coming up blue-lipped and shaking, refusing to stop until Junie physically took the tank off his back and pointed at the coffee. Junie on the winch, calling depths, keeping rhythm. Vesper everywhere — rigging, hauling, guiding crates onto the tarps they'd spread aft, moving like a man who'd waited his whole life for this work without knowing it.

By early afternoon they had forty-one crates on deck, and the sun broke through the cloud cover for the first time in days, and Odile allowed herself one full breath.

Forty-one crates of paper. She kept turning the arithmetic over in her head and it kept coming out impossible. Paper wasn't worth a salvage fleet. Paper wasn't worth dredges and patrols and sabotage and a fuel line cut in the night. Unless the paper was worth more than gold — unless owning it meant owning the truth, and losing it meant losing everything Corvin Joubert had built.

She'd run boats on this river for nineteen years. She'd watched company men post claim notices on stretches of water her grandfather had fished. She'd assumed, the way everyone assumed, that the companies had bought fair and square from somebody, somewhere, sometime. That was how it worked. That was how it had always worked.

Standing on the deck of a barge above the opened grave of the Meridian Star, holding a crate stenciled with words that said otherwise, Odile felt the assumption crack down its middle.

"We doing this?" Junie asked.

Vesper brought out Amos's map and unfolded it on top of the nearest crate, weighting the corners. The old annotations ran down the margin in that failing hand, and Odile leaned in and read them properly for the first time in full daylight:

*Not for the one who sank her. For the ones she was taken from. Open it where they can see.*

"He knew," Odile said. "Amos knew what was in her. All these years, and he never came back for it himself."

"He was sixteen," Vesper said. "A deckhand. He watched my father order her scuttled, and he carried that for fifty years because he was the only one left who'd seen it." Vesper's voice was steady, but his hands weren't. "He couldn't lift these crates. So he found me."

"Why you?" Junie asked. It wasn't cruel. It was the question they'd all been carrying.

"Because of whose son I am." Vesper straightened. "Because the truth needed a Joubert to deliver it, or no one would believe it. That's what he told me. Deliver what's in her hold to the people she was stolen from." He looked around at them — at the mechanic who'd demanded honesty as wages, the deckhand who'd stayed after catching him in a lie, the pilot whose name the industry had broken. "I made that promise to a dying man I'd known for two days. You four have known me longer and worse. So I'm asking, not telling. Once we open these, there's no cutting a deal, no walking away rich, no version of this where any of us comes out comfortable. Are we agreed?"

Odile answered first. "Agreed."

"Agreed," said Sefa.

Junie hesitated one heartbeat — long enough that Odile saw Vesper brace for it — and then she said, "Agreed. But you're reading them out loud. All of it. No editing."

"All of it," Vesper said.

They pried the first crate open together, four hands on the bar, and the lid came free with a sound like a sigh.

Inside, packed in wax-sealed tins wrapped in oilcloth, lay paper. Deeds — dozens of them, original instruments with surveyor's ribbons still attached, bearing the names of river families Odile had known her whole life: the Okonkwos, the Marshes, the Ferreiras, the Brants. Ledgers bound in oilskin. And sworn statements, witnessed and stamped, dated the week before the Meridian Star sailed — statements describing meetings, payments, signatures forged, claims transferred under duress to a company that had then sold them onward to outside investors, every transfer authorized by one signature that appeared again and again until Odile's eyes blurred.

Not the father's signature.

The brother's.

"Corvin," Junie whispered. "It was Corvin. Your father scuttled her to hide the proof *against his own brother*."

Vesper didn't answer. He was holding a deed with the name Brant on it, and his face had gone the color of the river fog, and Odile understood suddenly that he was looking at his own inheritance — the thing that would have restored his name, bought back the fleet, erased ten years of running — and that it was soaked through with other people's blood.

"The promise," she said quietly. "This is what Amos meant."

"Yes." Vesper set the deed down with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down something explosive. "Every claim Corvin's fleet works. Every dredge. Every notice posted in Halfway Landing. Stolen. Documented. Witnessed." He looked up, and Odile saw the last of the boy leave his face and something harder arrive. "We got them all up?"

"Thirty-eight more crates below," Sefa said. "But the tide's turning. Look at the cut."

Odile turned. Downstream, where the oxbow emptied back into the main channel, the water had begun to move the wrong way — the first push of the falling river funneled through the narrow cut, white-capped already, chewing at the gravel bar. Two hours of safe water left, maybe less. And beyond it, riding at anchor with her lights coming on against the graying afternoon, the *Iron Corvine* had launched a launch. Six men in it, maybe seven, an outboard running flat out toward them across the oxbow.

"Company's coming," Junie said.

Odile put her hand on the wheel. "Then everybody grab a crate. We're going to need ballast."
