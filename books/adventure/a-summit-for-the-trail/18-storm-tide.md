## 18. Storm Tide

I learned to read water before I learned to read letters, and the water was telling me something I did not want to hear.

For three days the river had been dropping, peeling back from the banks in long brown ribbons, exposing gravel bars like the bones of drowned animals. That was the whole arithmetic of the season — the tide falls, the oxbow shallows, the wreck comes within reach of a man on a line. We had spent those three days dredging silt by hand and by winch, hauling bucket after bucket of gray riverbed up into the light, and every hour the water gave us was an hour Corvin Joubert's flagship *Prosperity Grace* spent anchored four hundred yards downstream, her floodlamps burning all night like a promise she had no intention of keeping.

But this morning the water had stopped falling.

I stood at the wheel of the *Kettle's Promise* — Junie had painted the name on the transom without asking, and none of us had the heart to sand it off — and I watched the current do a thing rivers are not supposed to do in a falling season. It had gone slack. Then it had gone still. Then, so gently I might have missed it if I'd been talking instead of watching, it had begun to move the other way. Upstream.

"Odile." Vesper stood at the rail with the wind flattening his shirt against his back, a ledger wrapped in oilcloth tucked under one arm like a man carrying a sleeping child. "How bad?"

"You feel that?" I said. "The river's running backward."

"That's not possible. The tide's been out for days."

"Tides don't read your schedule." I nodded at the southern horizon, where the sky had gone the color of a bruise gone bad. "There's a storm down there pushing the whole estuary ahead of it. All that water's got to go somewhere, and the somewhere is up this river. It's a surge. It'll reverse the current, maybe raise us three, four feet, and it'll do it fast."

Vesper looked at the water the way he looked at engine gauges — like it was lying to him and he needed it to confess. "How long?"

"Hours. Maybe less." I turned the wheel a few degrees out of habit, though we were anchored and the anchor was doing its work. "And when a river runs backward, it runs angry. Everything it's been carrying for a week comes home at once — logs, silt, whole trees with the roots still on. This isn't weather, Vesper. It's a fist."

Behind us, Sefa's voice carried up from the hold, cheerful and muffled, the way it always was. He'd spent the night down there in the flooded dark with a dive lamp strapped to his forehead, cutting the last of the silt away from the hold hatch, and he came up the ladder now with his arms full of rotted canvas and his grin full of triumph, and I thought — I remember thinking it clearly, the way you remember things just before they go wrong — *there is a man who does not know what kind of day this is going to be.*

"We're through," he said, dropping the canvas at Vesper's feet. "Hatch is clear. Another hour to cut the seal and we're into the hold. The documents are down there, Vesper. Amos's promise is down there."

"Then we cut fast," Vesper said, "because the river's about to change its mind about all of us."

Junie came up from below decks with the manifest ledgers stacked in a waterproof satchel — the ones we'd pulled from the sunken company barge, the ones with Vesper's father's signature on every page — and she took one look at the sky and one look at my face and understood both in about two seconds, because Junie Marsh misses nothing except, occasionally, the wisdom of keeping her mouth shut.

"That's a surge sky," she said.

"That's a surge sky," I agreed.

"Then we're getting off this river."

"We're getting off this river *after* the hold opens." Vesper's voice had that flat calm he got when the stakes went up, the calm I'd learned to distrust in pilots and trust in him, and I still hadn't decided which it was. "One hour. Sefa cuts the seal, we bring up what Amos died carrying, and we run for Halfway ahead of the worst of it."

"And if the *Prosperity Grace* has other ideas?" Junie asked.

We all looked downstream. The flagship sat at anchor in the graying light, and I could see movement on her deck — not the idle movement of a crew riding out weather, but the purposeful kind. Cranes swinging. Lines coming off cleats. Boats being swung out on davits.

"Odile," Vesper said quietly, "how long to weigh anchor and run?"

"With that current building against us, and a barge this heavy, and a channel I half know in the dark? Forty minutes if nothing goes wrong."

"And how long for them to reach us?"

I did the arithmetic I didn't want to do. "Less."

* * *

The storm announced itself the way storms do in the interior — not with rain first but with wind, a long low moan coming down the valley like something enormous drawing breath. The trees on the banks began to talk to each other in that rushing language they have. The river, running backward now in earnest, started stacking itself into short steep waves with no rhythm to them, the kind of chop that breaks hulls and hearts with equal indifference.

Corvin's boats came at us through it anyway.

Three of them, aluminum, outboard-driven, riding the reversed current like they'd been born to it. Men in yellow slickers. And in the bow of the lead boat, standing where no sane man would stand in that chop, a figure I recognized even at that distance, because Corvin Joubert had spent forty years making sure everyone on this river knew his silhouette the way they knew the shape of the hills.

"They're not waiting for the tide," Sefa said, coming up the ladder two rungs at a time. "They're not waiting for anything."

"They're waiting for exactly this," I said. "A storm. A surge. If we sink in weather like this, who's going to ask how? If our documents wash downstream into their dredge baskets, who's going to ask why?" I looked at Vesper. "He didn't come out here to talk this time. You had your truce. You had your offer. You turned him down on his own deck, and Corvin Joubert does not get turned down twice."

"Then we hold," Vesper said. "We hold until the hold is open, and then we run."

"Run where? Upstream's a war against the current, downstream is his fleet, and the banks are—" I gestured at the flooded willow scrub sliding past, brown water where dry ground had been a week ago. "There is nowhere to run, Vesper. There's only holding and hoping."

"Then hold and hope," he said, and went down the ladder to help Sefa with the seal, and I put my hands on the wheel and did the only thing I have ever been truly good at, which is to keep a vessel alive when every reasonable voice says let her go.

The first boat came alongside on our starboard quarter with a grinding crunch of aluminum on the old hull plating, and hooks came over the rail — actual grappling hooks, iron, the kind of thing I hadn't seen since the bad years — and men started hauling themselves up the side of my ship.

Junie met them with a boathook.

I will give her this: she did not hesitate, and she did not swing to kill. She hooked the first man's slicker and shoved him back off the rail into the arms of his boatmates, and she stood there in the wind with the rain starting to come in sideways and shouted something I couldn't hear over the storm but could guess the shape of. The second man got a hand on the rail. Sefa appeared out of nowhere and put that big gentle hand of his flat on the man's chest and pushed — just pushed, the way you'd move a drunk off a dock — and the man went over backward into the boat and the boat went sideways and for a moment the whole tangle of hulls and hooks and shouting men seemed to hang there, balanced, deciding what kind of day this was going to be.

Then the *Prosperity Grace* weighed anchor.

I felt it before I understood it — a change in the water's voice, a deepening of the chop — and I looked downstream and saw the flagship's lights swinging toward us through the rain, all of her, four hundred tons of dredge and diesel and Corvin Joubert's whole life, coming up on us with the reversed current doing half her steering for her.

"She's under way," I shouted down the hatch. "Vesper! She's *moving!*"

His voice came back up through the storm, muffled, urgent, and underneath it I heard the ring of the cutting tool against the hold seal, steady as a heartbeat. He wasn't stopping. Of course he wasn't stopping. He'd made a promise to a dying man in a trapping cabin, and I had signed on to that promise, and we were all of us going to keep it or drown keeping it.

* * *

The collision, when it came, was not the cinematic catastrophe you'd imagine. It was worse. It was slow.

The *Prosperity Grace* didn't ram us — Corvin wanted the documents, not the wreckage, and wreckage sinks documents. She came alongside the way a big animal comes alongside a small one in a pen, shouldering, nudging, using her mass as a weapon. Her fenders never went over. Her hull met ours with a groan of oak on steel and held there, grinding, and the grinding was the point — she was going to squeeze us against the current and the chop until something gave, until our rails touched her deck and her men could walk across, until we were not a separate ship anymore but a room in hers.

I threw the wheel hard over and put our engines against hers and for a few minutes we were two animals locked at the shoulder, and the river hammered us both, and the rain came down like it had a grudge.

"Odile!" Junie had the satchel of old manifests strapped across her chest and a knife in her hand, cutting the lines the boarding men kept throwing. "The rail's going to go!"

She was right. I could feel it in the deck under my feet — that particular flex a hull gets when it's being asked to be something it isn't. The *Kettle's Promise* was a river barge with an engine and a history, not a warship, and she was losing the argument.

And then two things happened at once, which is how disasters like to arrange themselves.

The surge peaked.

I felt it as a lifting, a sudden buoyant shove from below, the whole river rising a foot in the space of a minute, and the chop that had been breaking over our rails now broke over the *Prosperity Grace*'s lower deck too, and somewhere on her bridge someone made the wrong call — I'll never know whose — and her engines answered ours with everything she had.

And the rail gave.

Not all of it. A section, ten feet of it, on the starboard quarter where the grinding had been worst, where Sefa had been standing with his hands on the hull keeping their men off. The plating tore with a sound like a giant zipper, the deck canted, and the river — the reversed, enraged, log-strewn river — came through the gap in a brown wall.

I saw him go. I will see it for the rest of my life. Sefa Tuilagi, who had carried a dead man's canvas out of the hold that morning grinning, who had pushed a boarder over the rail with nothing but an open hand, went with the water — not dramatically, not with a cry, just gone, the way the river takes things, one moment part of the ship and the next part of the flood, his dive lamp a fading yellow coin in the brown water, and then not even that.

"Sefa!" Junie's voice went through the storm like a blade through canvas.

I had no hands free. I had the wheel and four hundred tons of hostile steel on my shoulder and a river running backward and I could do nothing, nothing, nothing, and that is a feeling I have drowned in before and sworn I never would again.

Junie went over the side.

She went deliberately — that's the thing people won't believe later, when we tell it. She didn't fall. She clipped the satchel strap to the rail stanchion, leaving the manifests hanging above the flood like a lantern, and she dove into the surge after him, into water full of whole trees moving at the speed of freight trains, and the river took her the way it had taken him.

"Junie!" Vesper was up the ladder now, the cutting tool still in his hand, the hold seal half-open behind him, the promise half-kept, and he stood at the broken rail in the rain and looked at the water where his crew had gone, and I saw something cross his face that I had never seen there in all the weeks since Halfway Landing. Not fear for himself. He'd flown medicine into weather that ate better men whole. Fear for *them*. For the family he'd been assembling without admitting it, plank by plank, out of people the river industry had thrown away.

"Odile," he said, and his voice was terrible and calm, "get us off her."

"I can't get us off her. She's got half our hull in her grip and the current's—"

"Then get us *loose*. I'll get them." And he went over the rail after Junie and Sefa with a line tied around his waist, and the line paid out through the hands of nobody, because there was nobody — and then there was somebody, because the lead boarding boat, Corvin's men, the ones we'd been shoving off our rails all morning, had drifted alongside our broken quarter in the chaos, and one of them — the one Sefa had pushed, I'm nearly certain, the one with the bruise coming up on his chest — caught the line and held it and shouted to his boatmates to hold it too.

I want that written down somewhere. In the ledger, in the record, in whatever account is finally kept of that day: a company man held the line.

* * *

What happened in the water, I only got in pieces — from Vesper, from Junie, from the yellow slicker men who told it in the tavern at Halfway Landing for a season until the story wore smooth. The surge had carried Sefa against a half-sunken log jam at the mouth of the old oxbow and Junie had reached him there, and the two of them had been pinned in the willows with the river trying to strip the bank like bark, and Vesper had come down the line hand over hand through water that wanted him dead, and the three of them had come back aboard the *Kettle's Promise* an hour later — Sefa coughing half the oxbow out of his lungs, Junie bleeding from a cut she didn't remember getting, Vesper gray-faced and shaking and holding the oilcloth bundle above the waterline the whole way, because he'd gone into the flood after them with Amos's documents still under his arm and had not once, not for a second, considered letting go of either.

But I did not know any of that yet. What I knew, at the wheel, in the worst hour of the worst day, was this:

The *Prosperity Grace* was tearing free of us. The surge that had lifted her had also, in its blind enormous way, saved us — she'd been pushed sideways off our hull by the same water that was trying to drown my crew, and her anchor, dragging, caught the old channel edge and swung her stern around, and suddenly there was ten feet of brown water between us instead of none, and I put our engines hard ahead and the *Kettle's Promise* tore loose with a shriek of metal and ran.

I ran her blind. I'll admit it. Up the reversed river, into the teeth of the surge, because downstream was Corvin and the banks were drowned and the only direction left was the one the storm was coming out of, and I have never in my life been so glad of a river's bad temper. The chop tried to break us. A log the size of a church pew came out of the rain and I missed it by an arm's length. And the whole time the satchel of manifests hung off the rail stanchion where Junie had clipped it, swinging over the flood, and I reached out one hand at the top of a wave, at the risk of the wheel and the ship and everything, and grabbed it, and hauled it in, and stuffed it inside my slicker against my heart.

The documents nearly went. I'll say that plainly. They nearly went, and the only reason they didn't is that a deckhand who signs on for a cut of nothing decided they were worth diving into a reversed river for, and a mechanic who pays his crew in honesty went after her, and a pilot who'd been running from his name for ten years went in after both of them.

We found them at the willow jam because Sefa's dive lamp was still burning.

I brought us out of the surge an hour later into a slack eddy behind a drowned island, and I put us aground there on purpose, soft, in the willows, and I stood at the wheel with my hands shaking so hard I had to take them off it, and I watched Vesper carry Sefa's arm across his shoulders up the muddy bank while Junie held the line and the rain came down and down and down.

And downstream, through a tear in the weather, I saw the *Prosperity Grace* at anchor again, her lights burning, her cranes swinging, and I knew — the way you know a river, the way you know a man — that this was not over. That Corvin Joubert had lost the documents' location and gained their certainty, that he knew now beyond all doubt what we'd found in the hold and what it would do to him, and that a cornered man with a fleet is more dangerous in defeat than he ever was in pursuit.

Vesper crouched down beside me in the wheelhouse, soaked through, the oilcloth bundle between us, and for a long moment neither of us said anything. The river ran backward past the hull. Somewhere below, Sefa was breathing, which was the only ledger that mattered just then.

"He'll come for us at dawn," Vesper finally said. "Not the fleet. Him. And he won't offer this time."

"Then what do we do?"

Vesper looked at the documents, and then downriver at the lights, and I watched him arrive at the place I'd known he'd arrive at since the day a dying man put a map in his hands.

"We read it to them," he said. "All of it. Every claim, every name, every signature — before Corvin can buy or bury another soul on this river. Tomorrow. At first light. Every crew within earshot of this water."

"You understand what that costs you," I said. "The inheritance. The name. Everything your father—"

"I understand what it *buys*," he said, and outside, in the rain, Junie laughed — a raw, ragged, alive sound — and Sefa's voice rose up from below decks, weak and grinning, asking if anybody had thought to bring the coffee.
