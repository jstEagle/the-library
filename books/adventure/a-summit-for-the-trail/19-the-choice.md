## 19. The Choice

The river had turned on us and then, as if ashamed, turned back.

By the time the storm spent itself against the bluffs, the surge had drained out of the oxbow the way a fever breaks, leaving the Meridian Star's deck awash in six inches of brown water and the whole world dripping. Mist rose off the current in slow ropes. Somewhere upstream a heron screamed once and went silent, as if it too were waiting to see what we would do with what we'd found.

The documents lay in the wheelhouse of our barge, wrapped in Sefa's spare tarpaulin, stacked in oilcloth bundles we'd carried up from the hold with the tenderness of men handling bones. Deeds. Ledgers. Sworn statements signed by men long dead, their ink brown as old blood. Forty years of theft, pressed flat and sealed under river mud.

And across the channel, listing hard against its own mooring spuds with a gash torn in its flank where our hulls had met, the *Corvin's Pride* sat dark. Her dredge arm hung at an angle no dredge arm was built to hold. Her generators were drowned — we could see the waterline through her open ports, could see men moving lanterns through her corridors like fireflies trapped in a bottle. Disabled wasn't a strong enough word. She was beached in all but name, a whale of iron and ambition stranded by the same river she'd spent a decade gutting.

Corvin's crew had gathered on her foredeck. Thirty men, maybe forty, their rain gear shining in the first gray light, their voices carrying flat across the still water. Behind them, further up the oxbow, the smaller boats had come out of the willows where they'd been hiding since the storm began — river crews, camp families, the dredge hands Corvin had locked out last spring, the people whose claims were sitting in oilcloth three feet from my elbow. They'd heard the collision. They'd come to see who owned the river now.

They were all looking at me.

* * *

Junie found me at the rail. She didn't say anything at first, just stood there with her arms crossed against the cold, watching the water run. Her hands were still shaking — she'd pulled Sefa out of the flood an hour ago, hauled him over the rail by the collar of his coat while the river tried to keep him, and the shaking hadn't caught up with her until now. Sefa was below, wrapped in blankets, coughing river out of his lungs and insisting he was fine in the same breath.

"You could do it," she said finally. "You know that, don't you."

I didn't pretend I didn't. That was the thing about Junie Marsh — she'd stopped letting me pretend anything around week two, and I'd stopped wanting to.

"Walk over there under a truce flag," she went on. "Sit down with him in that fancy cabin of his. He's got no leverage left and he knows it. You've got the documents, the witnesses, the whole river at your back. You could trade. Quiet settlement, sealed papers, your name restored, your cut of the fleet signed over clean. Nobody downriver would ever know what's in these bundles except the people who needed to know." She tapped the tarpaulin with one knuckle. "The Joubert name comes back off the mud. You get to stop being nobody. Isn't that what you flew away from all those years wanting?"

"It's what I flew away from," I said. "The wanting stayed behind."

She looked at me then, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before — not the wariness she'd carried since the manifests, not the anger from the night she'd confronted me in the hold. Something more tired and more generous. "Your father signed those diversion orders," she said quietly. "That's still true. That doesn't stop being true because you read them out loud."

"I know."

"And Corvin will tell everyone it's true. He'll tell it his way. He'll say the Jouberts were thieves together, brothers in it, and that the son burned the father to save himself."

"I know that too."

"Do you?" She turned to face me fully. "Because I've watched you run from that name for ten years, Vesper. Flying medicine into places no one else will go isn't bravery, it's penance at two hundred miles an hour. And now you're standing here with everything you ever lost in your hands, and I need to hear you say what you're going to do, because Odile's already rigged the truce flag and Sefa keeps asking from the bunk if we're going to make the deal, and I can't —" Her voice caught, and she hated it, and pushed through. "I can't watch you decide wrong. Not after all this."

Below us, through the deck plates, Sefa coughed. Odile stood at the wheel, one hand resting on the spokes as if the barge might bolt like a horse. The river ran. The mist burned off the water in long slow sheets, and the sun came up the color of brass, and every eye on both banks and both decks was on the wheelhouse of a mud-scarred salvage barge.

I thought about Amos Kettle. Not the dying man in the cabin — the younger man I'd met only in the papers, the deckhand who had stood on the Meridian Star's deck forty years ago and watched my father give the order, and who had carried that knowledge in his chest like a coal for four decades, waiting for someone he could trust to carry it the rest of the way. He hadn't asked me to restore any name. He hadn't asked me to take anything. He'd made me promise one thing, with a hand like a dried root gripping mine, his fever-bright eyes an inch from my face:

*Deliver what's in her hold to the people she was stolen from. All of it. Not the part that's convenient. All of it.*

I had said yes because he was dying and it cost me nothing to say yes.

It had cost me everything to keep.

* * *

I went below and changed into dry clothes, and I took my time about it, because I wanted the last ten minutes of not knowing to be mine.

That's the honest truth, and I've made my peace with honest truth. For ten minutes more, I could have been the heir. I stood in the cramped bunkroom with the tarpaulin bundles on the table in front of me and I let myself have it — the whole fantasy, laid out like a hand of cards. Corvin across a table, defeated, offering terms. The Joubert Salvage Company restructured, my name on the letterhead, the fleet repainted, the river towns shaking my hand because the name meant something again. My father's grave with something other than shame on it. Me, landing at some company strip with my own hangar, my own crew on salary, belonging to something at last.

It was a good fantasy. I want to be clear about that. It wasn't a cheap temptation; it was the real thing, the kind that costs. Every year of my exile had been spent buying my way back to exactly this moment, and here it was, gift-wrapped by a storm.

And underneath it, like the wreck under the silt, was the other thing. The thing Amos had actually handed me. Not a fortune. A debt.

I picked up the top bundle and carried it up the ladder into the light.

* * *

Odile had rigged the truce flag on the mast, but I told her to take it down.

She looked at me like I'd lost my mind. "You're not going over there?"

"No."

"Then why —"

"Bring the loudhailer," I said. "And tell Junie to get the bundles up on the roof of the wheelhouse. All of them."

The *Corvin's Pride* sat maybe eighty yards off our port beam, close enough that in the post-storm stillness I could see individual faces on her foredeck — the dredge foreman with his gray stubble, the young hands in their too-big rain coats, and at the rail, upright and immaculate even now, a figure in a long dark coat who could only be Corvin himself. He'd come out to watch. He'd want to know which way I'd jump. He'd spent two days believing he knew, the way he'd believed his whole life that blood was the strongest current there was.

I climbed onto the wheelhouse roof with the first bundle under my arm. Junie handed the rest up to me, one by one, and stacked them at my feet, and then she stepped back and stood beside Odile at the rail, and Sefa came up from below wrapped in a blanket and leaned on the doorframe, gray-faced and grinning.

I raised the loudhailer. My voice came out flat and strange over the water, too loud, the way voices do when the world has gone quiet enough to listen.

"My name is Vesper Joubert," I said. "My father was Étienne Joubert, who founded the salvage company that works this river today. My uncle is Corvin Joubert, who runs it now. Some of you have heard the name. Most of you have heard it the way you hear a weather warning."

On the *Pride's* foredeck, Corvin tilted his head. Waiting. I could almost admire it — he was a man who understood theater, and he assumed I'd come to negotiate in public, to leverage the crowd, to make my fortune with an audience. He'd have done it that way himself. He'd have respected it.

"I'm holding the contents of the hold of the *Meridian Star*," I said. "The ship everyone on this river has been hunting for forty years. She was never lost. She was scuttled — deliberately, on my father's order, in the fall of the year the river jumped its course — and the man who gave me the map to her was a deckhand who stood on her deck the night she went down. His name was Amos Kettle. He died three weeks ago in a trapping cabin upriver, and before he died he made me promise one thing."

I untied the oilcloth.

"He made me promise to deliver what was in her hold to the people she was stolen from. All of it. So that's what I'm going to do."

I opened the first ledger.

* * *

I read for two hours.

I read until my voice went raw and Odile brought me up water in a tin cup and I drank it and kept reading. I read the deeds first — forty-one of them, river claims from the bend at Tallow Creek to the delta camps, homesteaders' and fisher-folk's and trap-line runners' titles, each one signed over to Meridian Consolidated for a fraction of its worth or nothing at all, each one witnessed by the same three men, and one of the three names on every page was Corvin Joubert.

Then the ledgers. The diversion records showing that the cargo the *Meridian Star* was supposedly carrying north — the machinery, the bonded goods, the company's whole advertised season — had been sold south before she ever sailed, the profit pocketed, the loss written off against the river communities whose claims the company had already swallowed. And then the sworn statements. Deckhands. Cooks. A purser. Men who had seen the order given, who had helped run her aground in the old oxbow and opened her seams, and who had been paid in silence and threats to keep it. Amos Kettle's own statement was among them, signed in a younger hand, and I read that one slowly, every word, because I owed him that.

The river crews had come closer while I read. The small boats drifted in from the willows until they ringed us, and men stood up in them to see, and on the *Pride's* foredeck the crew had gone very still, and some of the older hands had taken off their hats, which I didn't understand until later — until Junie told me that on this river, you uncover your head for a will being read.

Corvin let me get through the first ledger before he moved. He stepped to the *Pride's* rail and his voice came across the water, still rich, still warm, a voice built for boardrooms and church socials, and even now, even beached and drowned and finished, it had weight.

"Nephew." Just that, at first, letting the word do its work. "You're reading the handwriting of dead men and liars. Men who signed anything for a bottle. You'd hand this river a fortune in claims built on paper that wouldn't survive a clerk's afternoon." A pause, pitched perfectly. "Or — come aboard. We'll go through it together, family, and separate what's true from what's spite. You have my word on the terms. Everything your father built, split clean between us, and this —" his arm swept the water, the boats, the upturned faces, "— handled privately, like civilized people."

There it was. The last door, held open. I stood on the wheelhouse roof with the ledger in my hands and let myself look at him — really look, for the first time since he'd boarded under truce and offered me everything. He wasn't a monster. That was the trap of him. He was a man who loved the same things I loved: the river, the work, the name. He'd just decided, somewhere back down the years, that the name was worth more than the river, and every theft after that was bookkeeping.

"Uncle," I said, and the word came out steady, which surprised me. "Amos Kettle made me promise to deliver all of it, and I'm delivering all of it. There's no version where part of it stays in the family. That was the whole point of the hold. It was built to keep this exactly where no one could bargain it away."

I turned the ledger toward the boats.

"Every claim in this book belongs to the people it was taken from. Not to me. Not to him. The documents go to the river communities at Halfway Landing tomorrow, all of them, with witnesses from every camp on this water. Investigators can have copies. Lawyers can have copies. Anyone who wants to read what my father did and what my uncle did can read it, and I'll stand behind every page."

Corvin's face didn't change. That was the frightening thing. But his hands, resting on the *Pride's* rail, closed slowly, the way a man's hands close on a rope that's already gone out of reach.

"You're your father's son," he said, and this time there was nothing warm in it at all. "He buried the truth to protect the name. You're burying the name to protect the truth. Same river, same family, same instinct to drown what's inconvenient."

"Maybe," I said. "But he did it in the dark. I'm doing it in the daylight, and I'm not keeping anything."

I looked down at Junie. She was watching me with her chin up and her eyes bright and hard, and she gave me a single nod, small as a compass tick. Odile stood at the wheel with her arms folded, and Sefa had come all the way out onto the deck in his blanket, coughing and cheering in the same breath, his voice cracking across the water: "Read the last one! Read Kettle's statement again, the part about the purser!"

So I did. I read until the sun was high and the mist was gone and the ledger was finished, and then I tied the oilcloth back over the bundles with the same knots Amos's generation would have used, and Junie and Odile carried them below and stowed them in the strongest locker on the barge, behind the water tank, where no storm and no uncle was going to reach them.

The boats began to move off, one by one, toward Halfway Landing. Men called things across the water as they went — my name, some of them; Amos's name, more of them; and once, from an old woman standing in the bow of a leaking skiff with her hat off and her fist raised, a word I'll carry as long as the other things: *witnessed.*

On the *Corvin's Pride*, the foredeck emptied slowly, the crew drifting below or to the rails, and Corvin Joubert stood alone at the bow of his drowned flagship in his long dark coat, watching his fleet's inheritance float away in a hundred small boats. He didn't shout. He didn't threaten. He just looked at me across the water for a long moment, and then he touched two fingers to his hat brim — the old river salute, courtesy itself — and turned and went inside.

It should have felt like victory. I stood on the wheelhouse roof in the bright cold morning, empty-handed, voice ruined, name burned down to the waterline in front of the whole river, and I waited for the grief or the triumph or whatever was supposed to come.

What came instead was Sefa's hand on my ankle, and Junie laughing up at me — actually laughing, the first time since the storm — and Odile at the wheel saying, "Well, pilot. We're low on fuel, out of tides, and half the river owes us a drink. Where to?"

I looked south, toward Halfway Landing, toward the derelict airstrip at the edge of town that I'd walked past a dozen times without letting myself see it. Toward a plane with a thrown rod and a field full of weeds and a crew standing around me on a barge that smelled of mud and diesel and river.

"Home," I said, before I could stop myself.

Junie's laugh stopped. Odile's hands went still on the wheel. And far off to the north, past the bluffs, a single engine droned — small, high, coming down the river toward us, a plane I didn't recognize, flying a line no mail run and no patrol ever flew.

Odile saw it first. She always saw the water first.

"Vesper," she said quietly. "Who else knows what's in that hold?"
