## 20. Reckoning at Halfway

Junie Marsh had spent her whole life learning what a river town looked like when it was afraid, so she knew exactly what she was seeing the morning Halfway Landing woke up angry.

It was a different animal entirely. Fear kept people indoors, shutters drawn, voices low. Anger brought them out onto the boardwalk in their Sunday clothes with paper in their hands. By seven o'clock there was a line outside the claim office that ran past the dry goods store and curled toward the dock, and every soul in it carried a document copied from the ledgers they'd pulled out of the Meridian Star — deeds with wax seals gone brittle as autumn leaves, sworn statements signed by men and women whose grandchildren stood in that line now, holding the pages like they were holding the hands of the dead.

Junie stood on the porch of the claim office with a clipboard she'd taken from Sefa's workbench and a pencil behind her ear, doing what she did best: organizing other people's chaos. Names, parcel numbers, which document proved which claim, which family had which grievance going back how many years. She'd been at it since before dawn, and the stack of claim filings beside her was already thick as a Bible.

"Marsh." Old Teodor Kessel pushed to the front of the line, his good hand trembling around a folded paper. His bad hand — the one the river took three fingers from — he kept in his coat pocket, the way he always did. "You tell me straight. This'll hold? They won't find some way to make it nothing?"

Junie looked at the paper, then at the man. Teodor had fished the upper river for forty-one years. His father had held a claim to a slough that Corvin Joubert's dredge had chewed to gravel in '09, and Teodor had spent two decades writing letters to company offices that were never answered, because the company held the title and the company held the courts and the company held everything.

"It'll hold," she said. "There's a sworn statement in there from a surveyor named Aldous Renk, witnessed by two others, saying the transfer papers for your father's claim were forged. There's a ledger entry showing the payment that bought the forgery. And there's a man named Amos Kettle who watched it happen, and a statement in his hand dated four months before he died." She tapped the page. "That's not a grievance, Teodor. That's a case."

The old man's jaw worked. He looked down at the paper like it might change if he blinked. Then he nodded once, hard, and went inside, and Junie heard the clerk's voice behind her say, "Next," and the line shuffled forward, and the morning went on doing what mornings in Halfway Landing had never done before: taking things back.

* * *

The investigators arrived on the afternoon steamer — three of them, gray suits wrong for the climate, carrying document cases and the particular expression of people who had been sent to a backwater expecting nothing and were now confronted with a stack of evidence organized well enough to make their superiors nervous. Junie watched them come down the gangplank and thought, with some satisfaction, that the clipboard had done its work. You could hide a truth. It was much harder to hide a truth that came indexed.

By evening, the notices went up on the company board by the dock — the same board where Corvin's agents had posted their claim-staking announcements a season ago, and there was a poetry in that Junie intended to enjoy. IMPOUNDMENT ORDER, the new notices read. ALL VESSELS OF JOUBERT RIVERWORKS HELD PENDING INQUIRY. The dredges sat at their moorings downriver like a herd of beached animals, their booms locked, their crews paid off and drifting through town with the lost look of men whose whole world had been a company town and whose company had just stopped existing.

One of them found Junie at the dock that evening. A young deckhand, maybe nineteen, cap in his hands.

"You Marsh? Off the barge that found her?"

"I'm Marsh."

He looked at his boots. "I don't suppose you'd know if there's work. Any work. My pa ran a claim up the Sable that the company bought out from under him when I was a kid, and I—" He stopped, swallowed. "I didn't know. What the boats were sitting on. Nobody told us."

Junie softened. She knew what it was to be young and loyal to something that didn't deserve it. "There's a claim office full of families who need deckhands who know the river," she said. "And there's a man named Vesper Joubert who's going to be needing a crew for medicine runs before long, if I know him at all. Ask around. You'll land."

"Joubert." The boy said the name carefully, tasting it. "He's really one of them? The Jouberts?"

"He's really one of them," Junie said. "And that's the whole point, isn't it. What a name's worth isn't what you're born with. It's what you do with it after."

The boy nodded like he'd been given something to carry and went off down the dock, and Junie watched him go and felt the strange, unaccustomed weight of having been right about something that mattered.

* * *

She found Vesper at the airstrip at dusk, because of course she did. The man couldn't sit in a room; when the world got heavy he went to where the wind came off the field and the grass bent in long silver rows, and he stood there looking at his broken plane like it was a puzzle he hadn't solved yet. Sefa had the cowling off and parts laid out on canvas in the order of their reassembly, and the mechanic was talking — Sefa was always talking, a low running commentary of torque values and tolerances — but Vesper wasn't listening. He was looking downriver, toward the moorings, where the Joubert fleet sat dark against the last of the light.

Junie came and stood beside him. Neither of them said anything for a while. That was the thing about the crew they'd become, she thought; silence between them had stopped being empty somewhere back upriver and started being a place you could rest.

"He came by," Vesper said finally.

"Corvin?"

"This afternoon. While you were at the claim office. No lawyers, no leverage. Just him." Vesper's voice was flat, careful, the voice of a man carrying something breakable. "He wanted to see the wreck site before they seal it. I took him out in the skiff. Showed him the oxbow. Showed him where the Star sits under thirty feet of silt with his whole life's work buried on top of her."

"What did he say?"

Vesper was quiet a long moment. "He said my father would have hated what I did today. And then he said—" The flatness cracked, just barely. "He said I'm more my father's son than I know. And I've been standing here trying to decide if that was an insult or an apology."

Junie looked out at the darkening river. She thought about the ledgers, and what they said about Vesper's father — about a man who had seen the betrayal, understood it whole, and instead of exposing it had scuttled a ship and a fleet and a family to bury it, choosing silence over justice and calling it protection. She thought about the boy on the dock, and about Teodor Kessel's trembling hand, and about the difference between a man who hides the truth to protect a name and a man who reads it aloud on a dock with nothing to gain and everything to lose.

"Both," she said. "It's both. And it's neither. He's telling you that you've got his brother's blood, and you do, and there's rot in that blood — Corvin put it there, but your father watered it. But blood's just the map, Vesper. You're the one who picks the channel." She turned to look at him. "Your father kept the truth to protect the family name. You spent it to give the river back its own. Same inheritance. Different man. Corvin can't tell the difference. That's his ruin, not yours."

Vesper looked at her then, really looked, and something in his face came loose — not relief exactly, and not grief, but the place where the two of them meet and can't be told apart. "When did you get so wise?"

"I've been wise the whole time. You people were too busy nearly drowning to notice."

That got a real laugh out of him, short and rough, and Junie counted it a victory. Then his face settled again, quieter. "He offered me everything, you know. Back on the river, and again today. The fleet, the name, the whole rotten estate. Walk away and it's all mine." He looked down at the plane, at the open engine Sefa was rebuilding bolt by bolt. "A month ago I'd have given anything to hear my name said without flinching. And now he's offering me the name on a plate and all I can think is that it isn't mine to take. It never was. It belongs to everyone he stole it from."

"Then you're not his," Junie said. "You're Amos's. And ours. And that's a better family, even if it came with worse cooking."

"Sefa's cooking is a crime against several nations," Vesper agreed, and the heaviness lifted off him by a degree, the way fog lifts off water — not gone, but no longer the whole world.

* * *

The impoundment took three days, and Junie watched all of them.

Investigators in gray suits went aboard the flagship dredge with the recovered ledgers under their arms and came out looking older. Company clerks were subpoenaed. A magistrate arrived from downriver and set up in the claim office — Junie's claim office, she'd begun to think of it, though she'd never say so — and the line of families with their brittle documents stretched out the door for a week straight. Teodor Kessel came every day, not because his claim needed him but because he wanted to watch. "Forty-one years," he told Junie on the third day, sitting on the porch steps in the sun like a man warming himself at a fire he'd been promised all his life. "You get so you don't expect the river to give anything back. And then it does."

It wasn't triumph. That was what struck Junie most, watching it all unfold. There was no cheering in the streets, no burning of the company board. The river communities had been robbed too long and too quietly to celebrate; they just set about the work of taking it back the way they did everything — steadily, in line, with paper in their hands. The dredge crews drifted away to other work. The company signs came down and nobody threw stones at them; they just came down, and the walls behind them looked paler, like a scar showing after a bandage comes off.

Corvin Joubert left on the fourth morning. Junie saw it from the dock: no flagship now, just a passage on the downriver steamer, one gray man with one gray case, standing at the rail. As the steamer backed away from the dock he looked upriver — toward the airstrip, toward the oxbow, toward the wreck that had eaten his empire — and Junie, watching, saw him find Vesper standing at the end of the dock, because Vesper had come down to see him off, because of course he had, because that was the kind of man he was learning to be.

She was too far to hear what passed between them. She saw Corvin lean close and say something — a few words only — and she saw Vesper stand very still, and then nod, once, the same hard single nod old Teodor had given her at the claim office door. Not forgiveness. Not surrender. Acknowledgment. The nod you give a thing you've finally stopped fighting and started carrying.

Then the steamer took him, and the river closed behind him the way rivers do, without opinion, and Vesper stood on the dock a long time after it was gone.

Junie went and stood beside him. She was getting good at that.

"What did he say?" she asked. "At the end."

Vesper watched the water. "He said, 'Your father would have kept it. The documents. He'd have found a use for them.'" A pause. "And I said, 'I know. That's why they're not mine.'"

"And what did he say to that?"

"Nothing." Vesper almost smiled. "Nothing at all. First honest thing he ever gave me."

The light was going gold on the river. Down at the claim office the magistrate's lamp was lit, and Teodor Kessel's silhouette sat on the porch steps like it had grown there. Up at the airstrip, Sefa's voice carried on the evening wind, still talking torque to an engine that was slowly, bolt by bolt, becoming a plane again.

"So," Junie said. "Halfway Landing."

Vesper glanced at her. "What about it?"

"There's a derelict airstrip at the north end of town that Sefa's been eyeing like a starving man eyes a kitchen. And a plane that'll fly in a week. And a river full of camps that need medicine runs, and a claim office that needs a pilot who knows every slough on it, and—" she stopped herself, heard how much she was saying, and finished anyway, softer. "And a crew with nowhere to be. That's all."

Vesper looked upriver for a long time. Junie watched him do it and understood she was watching a man stand at a door he'd spent ten years nailing shut from the outside.

"There's a lease on that strip," he said at last. "The owner wants it gone. Nobody's flown out of it in fifteen years."

"Then I guess," Junie said, "somebody ought to go look at the paperwork."

He laughed — really laughed, the whole man this time — and turned from the river, and they walked up off the dock together toward the lights of the town that had stopped being a stop and started being something else. Behind them the water kept moving, the way it always had, the way it always would, carrying everything toward wherever it was going next.

And Junie, who had spent her life reading rivers and the people on them, read the set of Vesper Joubert's shoulders as he walked, and knew the moving man had finally found his channel.
