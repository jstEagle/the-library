## 21. A Field Worth Keeping

The plane had been sitting on the mudflat for three weeks, and Sefa Tuilagi could not stand it anymore.

Not the plane itself — the *Cormorant* was a good old girl, a de Havilland airframe with more patches than original skin, and she'd earned her rest. What Sefa couldn't stand was the way Vesper Joubert looked at her every morning when he walked out from Junie's boarding house with his coffee, stood at the edge of the grass, and didn't touch her. Like a man visiting a grave. Like a man who hadn't decided yet whether to dig.

"You're going to stare at that engine until it fixes itself out of shame," Sefa said, coming up behind him with two mugs of tea because coffee was a luxury Halfway Landing only pretended to have. "It won't. I've tried shaming engines my whole life. They have no dignity to wound."

Vesper took the mug without looking away from the cowling. "Rod went through the case. She needs a new crankshaft, new cylinders, probably a new oil pump. Parts are three weeks out of the coast even if anyone will sell to us."

"Anyone will sell to us," Sefa said. "That's the part you haven't figured out yet."

He turned then, and there it was — that look Sefa had learned to read over four tides and one storm, the look of a man doing arithmetic on what things cost him. Not money. Vesper Joubert never flinched at money. It was everything else he priced like it might bankrupt him.

"Corvin's people own the parts houses downriver," Vesper said.

"Corvin's people are impounded. Every barge, every dredge, every warehouse with his name on the manifest — chained up under federal seal since the hearing." Sefa blew on her tea. "You did that. You read that ledger out loud in front of forty river crews, and now the man who owns the parts is a man who owes you his freedom, which means the parts are free too. You just haven't gotten used to being owed things instead of owing them."

"I don't like being owed things either."

"I know," Sefa said. "It's very annoying. Drink your tea."

* * *

They started on the engine that afternoon, all of them, because that was how things got done in Halfway Landing now — all hands or not at all.

Junie Marsh arrived first, as she always did, ten minutes early with a canvas tool roll over her shoulder and an opinion already loaded. Odile Brant came down from the barge with grease to her elbows, having spent the morning convincing the *Marta Kaye*'s new owners — a co-op of the same river families whose claims the documents had restored — that their pilot was worth every cent they were paying her. And by mid-afternoon half the town had drifted out to the mudflat, drawn the way river towns drift toward anything that isn't flood or funerals: the ferryman with his sons, the widow Okonkwo who ran the trading post, two boys who should have been minding nets and weren't.

Sefa pulled the broken crankshaft herself, or most of it — it took a chain hoist rigged off the tailwheel strut and Junie swearing at the bolts in three languages until they surrendered. The rod had punched through the crankcase like a fist through wet paper, and when the case came free Sefa held it up to the light and showed them the fracture line running clean through the casting.

"Fatigue," she said. "This didn't happen yesterday. This has been coming for years. She's been telling him she was tired, and he kept asking her for one more river."

"He does that," Junie said.

"I'm right here," Vesper said.

"We know," they said together, and Sefa felt something turn over warm in her chest, some small gear engaging that had been slipping loose ever since her husband died and she'd left Apia with nothing but tools and a grudge against silence. A crew. A strange, patched-together, argumentative crew, standing around a broken airplane in the mud, laughing at the man who owned it.

The new crankshaft came up from the coast in nine days, freighted on the mail boat by a shipping clerk who refused payment and asked instead for Vesper to sign the inside of the crate lid. "For my boy," the clerk said. "He wants to fly someday. He says you're the one who told the truth about the Meridian Star."

Vesper signed it. His hand shook, just slightly, and Sefa saw it and said nothing, because some things you file away and give back to a man later, gently, when he can accept them.

* * *

The airstrip was Odile's idea, though she'd deny it.

She brought it up at Junie's kitchen table, over the stew Widow Okonkwo sent down whenever she wanted all four of them in one room where she could count heads. The derelict strip sat on the bench above town — cut into the treeline during the war and abandoned after, its gravel grown over with alder and fireweed, its windsock rotted to a single gray ribbons of cloth on a leaning pole.

"Medicine runs need a base," Odile said. "You've been flying out of whatever field would have you, sleeping in whatever hangar, moving on before anyone learned your face. That's over. The river families know your name now — the real one, both halves of it. They'll want their insulin and their vaccines and their medevacs, and they'll want them from someone who stays."

"Staying isn't—" Vesper started.

"Isn't what you do. Yes. We've heard the speech." Odile leaned forward, forearms on the table, and Sefa watched her do it — watch the ex-pilot who'd carried a wrecking charge on her name for six years lean toward a man carrying a heavier one, and offer him the only thing that had ever worked on her: blunt truth without mercy. "I flew out of fields like this for eleven years. A field isn't a commitment, Vesper. It's a promise you make to other people that you'll be findable. These people spent a decade being stolen from by men who were never findable. Give them a field."

Silence around the table. Rain starting on the roof, soft as fingers.

Junie reached across and turned Vesper's teacup so the handle faced him, the way you set a place for someone you expect to stay. "The lease is sitting in the trading post," she said. "Widow Okonkwo drew it up. Town council approved it Tuesday. Ninety-nine years, one dollar. They voted on it before we even asked."

Sefa watched Vesper look at the cup, at the handle turned toward him like an open hand, and she saw the exact moment the arithmetic changed — the moment staying stopped being a cost and became a purchase. He wasn't a man who could be pushed anywhere. But he could be shown a door, and he would walk through it himself, always, if you let him believe it was his idea.

"The alder's going to be a fight," he said finally.

"Everything worth keeping is," Sefa said.

* * *

They cut the airstrip clear in eleven days, and Sefa counted it among the best work of her life, though no part of it involved an engine.

The whole town came. That was the thing about Halfway Landing that Sefa was still learning, still being undone by: a place that had been robbed for a generation had not learned helplessness. It had learned each other. The ferryman brought his winch barge and dragged the big stumps. The net-minders' kids cleared brush in relays, racing each other in crews named after boats that had been lost. Widow Okonkwo appeared at dawn each day with bread and disappeared at dusk having somehow settled three disputes and organized tomorrow. Even the two boys who'd skived off nets the first day were there every morning now, and one of them — the quiet one, Tomas, with the burn-scarred forearm — had taken to following Sefa around asking questions about torque until she gave him a wrench and put him to work.

Gravel went down on the runway bed hauled from the bar below the oxbow — Amos Kettle's bar, the old men called it, though the young ones were starting to call it something else, something with Vesper's father's name in it that nobody had quite agreed on yet. The drainage ditches were cut by hand along lines Odile walked out with a surveyor's string and a level borrowed from the impoundment auction. The windsock pole was straightened, re-set in concrete mixed in a wheelbarrow, and Junie sewed a new sock from orange signal panel cloth she'd been saving for reasons she wouldn't explain, and it caught the first morning breeze like a struck match.

And through all of it, the *Cormorant* came back together on the flat below, piece by piece, under Sefa's hands and everyone else's learning ones.

New crankshaft seated and torqued. New cylinders honed and ringed. The oil pump rebuilt twice because Sefa didn't trust the first rebuild, and the second time she made Vesper do it while she watched, because a man should know his own engine the way he knows his own hands. Junie ran the wiring. Odile, who knew airframes from a decade of flying anything with wings, inspected the fabric and found two soft patches near the tail that nobody else had caught, and they recovered them with the last of the grade-A cloth, stitched and doped and painted a slightly wrong shade of silver that Vesper declared characterful.

On the seventeenth day, Sefa closed the cowling, wiped her hands on a rag that had been clean that morning, and stepped back.

"Well?" Vesper said.

"She's ready."

"Are you sure?"

"No," Sefa said. "I'm never sure. Sure is for priests and liars. I'm *confident*, which is what you get instead when you've done the work right." She handed him the rag. "Go start her."

He climbed in. The whole town had drifted down again — word traveled through Halfway Landing faster than weather — and they stood back along the tree line the way you stand back from a launch. Vesper ran the checks slow, deliberate, every item touched and called, and Sefa realized she'd taught him that, over the years of patching the *Cormorant* in a dozen river towns, without ever meaning to: the discipline of not rushing a machine that carries you.

Mags on. Prime. Contact.

The propeller spun twice, caught, and the old radial lit with a cough and a roar and then settled into that deep, uneven, magnificent idle that only old radials have, like a big animal clearing its throat. The crowd cheered. Junie whooped. Odile just nodded once, the way pilots nod, which contains more approval than most people's applause.

Vesper ran her up, checked the mags, and shut her down, and when he climbed out he was grinning — actually grinning, open-faced, a look Sefa suspected had been rare currency in his life for a long time.

"Not bad," he said.

"Not bad," Sefa agreed. "Now go sign the paper. Before you remember you're scared."

* * *

The lease signing happened at the trading post on a bright cold morning, with frost still on the boards and the new windsock standing straight out in the east wind.

Widow Okonkwo had laid it all out on the counter with terrible formality: the lease in careful ink, ninety-nine years, one dollar; a pen; and a plate of sweet biscuits that nobody touched because touching them first would have been bad luck, or good manners, or both. The town council stood along the wall. The two boys knelt on a crate by the window to see over the counter. Junie, Odile, and Sefa stood where family stands — close, arms crossed, saying nothing, because this part belonged to him.

Vesper read the whole document. Of course he did; Sefa would have been worried if he hadn't. He read every clause, and at the end there was a line the widow had added herself, in her own hand, and Sefa watched him stop on it.

*The lessee shall keep the field open to any river community in need, at fair cost or none.*

"That's not standard," he said.

"No," the widow said. "It's better than standard."

He looked at it a long moment. Then he picked up the pen, and Sefa saw him take one breath — the breath of a man setting down something heavy he'd been carrying so long he'd forgotten it was there — and sign.

Vesper Joubert. His full name, both halves of it, written steady on the line.

"One dollar," the widow said, holding out her palm.

He paid it. She bit the coin, which was theatrical and unnecessary and exactly right, and the room broke into noise — handshakes, back-slaps, the boys cheering from their crate — and Sefa stood back and let it happen, watching Vesper be congratulated the way you watch a wild thing decide, cautiously, that the campfire might be safe.

Later, walking back up to the field together, he fell into step beside her and was quiet for a long stretch of the trail.

"My father never signed anything longer than a season," he said finally. "Contracts, leases, marriages. Everything was temporary so nothing could be lost." He looked up at the strip, the fresh gravel pale in the sun, the windsock burning orange against the trees. "I used to think that was freedom."

"And now?"

"Now I think it was just fear with good posture." He stopped at the edge of the gravel and put his hands in his pockets, and Sefa waited, because she'd learned that with Vesper the waiting was the work. "Amos traded me a map for a promise. You all traded me a field for a dollar. I keep wondering when someone's going to tell me the real price."

Sefa shrugged. "The real price is you show up. Tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that. That's the whole bill. Most people find it ruinous."

"And if I can pay it?"

"Then you'll be the richest bankrupt man on this river," she said, "which is the only kind of rich worth being."

He laughed — short, surprised, real — and turned up the slope toward the hangar they were going to build next week, and the windsock, and the field with its hundred years of lease, and Sefa followed him, and for the first time since she'd come down off the mountain of her own grief, following didn't feel like losing ground.

Behind them, far downriver, the mail boat sounded its horn — once, twice, a third time, the signal they'd all learned meant *come to the dock, there's news.*
