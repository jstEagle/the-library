## 1. The Town That Forgot His Name

The trouble with Puerto Ceniza was that it remembered everything except what you wanted it to.

Ines Halloran had lived there fourteen months, and in that time the town had learned his coffee order, his limp on cold mornings, and the exact pitch of his whistle that made Doña Ferreira's dog stop barking — but it had never once learned his name. He was *el gringo de la madera*, the wood gringo, because he split firewood behind the ferretería three mornings a week. He was *el que arregla techos* to the widow Ocampo, who paid him in soup and unsolicited opinions about his posture. To the boys who fished off the pier he was simply *Señor Cuerdas*, Mr. Ropes, because of the coils of climbing line he kept coiled like sleeping animals under his bed, though he'd told them a dozen times they were for tying down tarps, which was even true, some of the time.

He preferred it that way. A man without a name could not be searched for. A man called Señor Cuerdas could patch a roof in peace until he died of old age or bad soup, whichever came first, and both options suited him fine.

On the morning Mara Voss arrived, he was up to his elbows in the innards of the town's only outboard motor, which had chosen that particular Tuesday to die in a puddle of its own ambitions outside Bautista's boat shed.

"You're flooding it," Ines said, without looking up.

"I am *encouraging* it," said Bautista, working the starter rope with the optimism of a man who had been encouraging the same motor for six years. "There is a difference. Encouragement is an art."

"Encouragement is how you get a motor that expects encouragement." Ines wiped his hands on a rag that had once, possibly, been white. "Give it here."

Bautista surrendered the rope with the dignity of a general ceding territory. Ines knelt, did three things to the carburetor that would have taken anyone else twenty minutes and took him forty seconds, and pulled. The motor coughed, considered its options, and caught with a sound like a contented pig.

"*Hijo de—*" Bautista crossed himself and laughed at the same time, which took practice. "You know what you should do? You should work engines full time. Boats. You have the hands for it."

"I have the hands for whatever pays," Ines said, which was true and also a wall, and Bautista, being a Puerto Ceniza native, respected walls the way other men respected fences — he didn't climb them, but he knew exactly where they stood.

It was while they were arguing about whether the fix constituted one beer or two that Ines saw her.

She came down the hill from the bus depot the way nobody came down the hill from the bus depot: fast, looking at nothing but the ground, wearing city boots that had already lost their argument with the mud. She carried a canvas duffel over one shoulder and a leather folder clamped under her arm like a shield, and she walked past the boat shed, stopped, walked back, and looked at the sign — BAUTISTA Y HIJOS, BARCOS Y REPARACIONES, which was generous, since Bautista's sons had emigrated years ago and sent postcards instead of labor — and then she looked at Ines.

Not at him the way people look at strangers. At him the way people look at a photograph they've studied.

Ines felt it happen in his chest first, that cold drop, the way a guide feels the mountain shift before anyone hears anything. Fourteen months. Fourteen months of splitting wood and fixing roofs and being Señor Cuerdas, and here was a woman with mud on her boots reading his face like a route description.

"Buenos días," she said. Her Spanish was good but secondhand, learned late, the consonants too careful. "Do you know where I might find a man named Halloran?"

The world went very quiet, the way it does at altitude, and Ines heard himself say, in the flattest voice he owned:

"Never heard of him."

* * *

He spent the rest of the morning being furious at himself, which was inefficient, because he was also furious at her, and at Bautista, whose beer he now owed and drank with poor grace.

"She asked for Halloran," Bautista said, wiping down the bar of the Cantina del Faro with a cloth that predated the town's electricity. "I said, Halloran? There is no Halloran. There is Señor Cuerdas, who fixes roofs and insults motors. And she said—" he paused for effect, because Bautista believed all stories deserved staging, "—she said, 'The man who fixed the Ocampo roof last month. Tall. Gray coming in early. Scar through the left eyebrow.' Señor, I have known you two years and I did not know you had a scar through your eyebrow."

"It's very subtle."

"It is *dramatic*. Like something from a film. And this woman, she has money — not rich money, worried money, which is worse — and she is asking everyone. The depot. The ferretería. She asked Doña Ferreira, and Doña Ferreira told her—" Bautista winced theatrically, "—everything."

"There is no everything," Ines said. "There's a dog, a whistle, and a roof."

"There is a dog, a whistle, a roof, a scar, gray hair, strong hands, a limp when it rains, and the fact that you once climbed the church tower in a storm to free a trapped gull, which Doña Ferreira describes as 'the act of a man running from something.' She was very impressed. She has decided you are either an ex-priest or a criminal."

"And which did she tell the woman?"

"Both. With equal confidence." Bautista set down the cloth. His face did the thing it occasionally did, dropping the performance and showing the steady man underneath, the friend underneath. "Ines. Listen to me. I don't ask questions. This is my gift; ask my wife. But a woman comes to a town of four hundred people asking for a name that does not exist, describing a man who clearly does — that is not a question. That is weather coming. You want to tell me to mind my own business, I will mind it beautifully. But you should know the sky."

Ines turned his glass on the bar, watching the ring it left. The truth was he'd known the sky for fourteen months. Every stranger off the bus, every new face at the market, he'd catalogued them automatically, the old habit, the guide's habit: *threat, threat, tourist, threat, family visiting, threat.* Fourteen months of clear skies. And now this.

"What does she look like?" he asked, hating himself for asking.

"Young. Thirty, maybe. Dark hair cut short like she did it herself with kitchen scissors and got mostly away with it. Eyes like—" Bautista waved a hand, searching, "—like someone who hasn't slept in nine days and doesn't intend to start until something is finished."

Nine days. The number snagged in Ines's chest like a fishhook, and he didn't know why, and not knowing why frightened him more than the woman did.

* * *

That evening he climbed to the roof of the church — legally, more or less; Father Anselmo had given him a standing invitation after the gull incident, on the theory that a man comfortable on slate at height was a gift from God and should be used as such — and sat where he always sat, on the lee side of the bell tower, looking down at the town going gold and then blue in the dusk.

Puerto Ceniza from above was a handful of lights around a dark harbor, backed by hills that folded up into country nobody mapped anymore. Somewhere beyond those folds was the border, and beyond the border were the mountains where he'd made his living and then lost it, and between here and there lay two blacklists, one dead mentor, and a lie so large it had its own weather system.

Aldous Rennick. Even thinking the name still hurt, fourteen months later, like pressing a bruise to check if it had healed. It hadn't. Twenty-two years Ines had followed that man up routes that didn't have names yet, had trusted him the way you trust gravity — completely, without examination, because examination seemed like an insult. And then the San Cristóbal inquiry, and the testimony, and the maps, and the word *negligent* spoken by men in suits who had never been above the treeline, and Rennick dead before he could explain, dead in a way that felt less like an ending than an exit.

They'd taken his certification in one country and his reputation in the other, and Ines had done the arithmetic and discovered that a man with neither could still carry a pack, so he'd carried one south until the roads ran out, and here he was.

Below him, a light moved along the harbor road. Small, purposeful, wrong. Everyone in Puerto Ceniza strolled; strolling was practically municipal law. Nobody walked the harbor road at night like they were following coordinates.

He watched the light stop below the cantina. Watched it wait. Watched it, after a long moment, turn and look directly up at the church tower, as if a woman thirty meters below in the dark could see a man sitting sixty meters up against a black sky.

His skin prickled. Not fear exactly. Something older. The feeling of a snow slope under his boots going from solid to suspicious.

*Running from something,* Doña Ferreira had said. She wasn't wrong. He'd just always told himself it was slower than running. More dignified. *Relocating.*

But the past had just gotten off a bus in muddy boots, and it was asking for him by name.

* * *

She found him the next morning at the ferretería, where he was loading split wood into the bed of Sra. Ocampo's ancient pickup, because the widow Ocampo had decided that since he wouldn't accept money, he would accept the use of her truck, which she could not drive anyway due to her eyesight and her opinions about modern vehicles.

"Ines Halloran."

Not a question this time. She stood at the tailgate with the leather folder under her arm and the sleepless eyes Bautista had promised, and up close she looked worse and better than expected — worse because there were shadows under her eyes deep enough to store things in, better because her chin was set like someone who had decided that exhaustion was a scheduling problem, not a condition.

"That depends," Ines said, setting down the last log, "on who's asking, and how they got the name."

"Mara Voss." She didn't offer a hand; she offered the folder instead, holding it out flat between them like a document at a checkpoint. "And I bought it. The name. From a man in Valparaíso who sells things he shouldn't have. Your name, your last port, the boat you left on. Two hundred dollars. I don't know if that means you were cheap or he was, and honestly I don't care."

Behind him, Ines heard the ferretería door chime and knew, with total certainty, that half the town was now listening from inside with their backs pressed to shelves.

"Ms. Voss—"

"Halloran." Her voice cracked, just once, and she hated it, he could see her hate it, and she pushed through anyway. "My father is missing. Nine days. Nine days of radio silence, up a river that doesn't officially exist, in a valley that two governments signed papers saying no one should enter, and the consulates keep telling me to be patient, and the patience is going to kill him faster than the river will. They told me in Valparaíso you were the best guide on that coast once. They also told me you'd deny it." Her jaw tightened. "So go ahead. Deny it. Tell me I've wasted a bus ticket. But do it looking at me, and do it knowing that every day you spend being nobody is a day my father spends being dead."

The wood dust hung in the light between them. Somewhere inside the ferretería, someone dropped something small and did not pick it up.

Ines looked at her — really looked, the way he'd look at a route before committing to it, reading the rock for what it actually was rather than what he hoped it was. He saw grief held together with wire. He saw money that had run further than sense. And he saw, underneath all of it, the thing that had put her on a bus to a town that forgot names: a certainty that he was the last person who could help, which meant somewhere in her story was a piece of his.

"Voss," he said slowly. The name tickled something at the back of his memory, some ledger entry from another life. "Your father. What's his first name?"

Something flickered across her face — hope, terrible and sudden, like sun through breaking cloud.

"Emil," she said. "Emil Voss. He was mapping the headwaters of the Rio Sombra."

The Rio Sombra. The Shadow River. Closed valley. '61. The mining disaster the old men still wouldn't discuss above a whisper, the one they changed hands and lowered voices for, the one Aldous Rennick had—

The cold dropped through Ines's chest again, all the way through this time, down to the bedrock.

Because he remembered now where he'd seen the name Voss. Not in a ledger. On a map. On *Rennick's* map, in the margin of the old route sheets he kept in the locked box under his bed, next to the ropes — a survey party, decades ago, a name inked and crossed out and inked again, like a man couldn't decide whether to remember it or erase it.

Mara Voss was watching his face, and whatever she saw there made her grip the folder tighter.

"You know the river," she said. It wasn't a question either.

Ines looked past her, down the harbor road, at the hills folding up toward the border, at the closed country beyond them where a missing man was nine days silent, and felt the trap close around him with a click almost too soft to hear.

"No," he said. "But I think it knows me."
