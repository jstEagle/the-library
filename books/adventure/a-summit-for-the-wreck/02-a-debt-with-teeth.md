## 2. A Debt With Teeth

The money Mara Voss left on my table was still sitting there at dawn, and I had not touched it.

I want to say I hadn't touched it out of principle. The truth is I'd spent half the night staring at it the way a man stares at a snake coiled on his doorstep — with respect, and from a distance, and with a growing certainty that sooner or later it was going to be a problem. It was a thick envelope, bank-banded, and the top bill was a five-hundred note, which in Puerto Ceniza is not money so much as a rumor. I have hauled tourists up glaciers for less than what that envelope represented. I have hauled tourists up glaciers for considerably less than what the *bottom* bill represented.

I made coffee instead. There is a discipline to it — the little aluminum pot, the pinch of salt, the patience — and discipline was what I needed, because the envelope had a second shape underneath the money-shape, and that shape was a hook.

Her name had been Mara Voss. She had said it like she expected it to mean something, and when my face did the thing my face does — nothing, mostly, a door closing politely — she had said, "You know it. Don't insult both of us."

She was right, of course. Voss. Emil Voss. The name had come up over the years the way certain names do in my trade, always at the edges of conversations, always in the tone people use for weather that's coming. Voss Expeditions. Voss the elder, who mapped rivers the way other men collected stamps — compulsively, beautifully, and with a total disregard for the fact that some rivers do not wish to be known. I had never met him. I had, however, met his maps, and I had trusted them, which in my experience is a more intimate thing than meeting a man.

And now his daughter had found me in a border town where I had spent three years being no one, and she had put money on my table, and she had known to do it.

That was the part I kept circling back to while the coffee went bitter in the pot. Not the money. The *knowing*. Puerto Ceniza is a town that forgot my name on purpose — I paid the harbormaster to forget it, paid the pension owner to forget it, and paid a little extra to a man named Cuellar whose entire profession is forgetting things on behalf of other people. It is, I will admit, a strange economy. But it worked. For three years it worked.

Until somebody sold it.

* * *

I found Cuellar where I always found him, at the back table of the Cantina del Faro, doing a crossword in a language neither of us spoke, on the theory that it kept the mind honest.

"Cuellar," I said. "Who's been asking about me?"

He didn't look up from his puzzle. Cuellar is a small, dry man, the color and approximate texture of an old boot, and he has the gift of making silence feel like a fair price for whatever he's about to say. Around us the cantina did its morning business: two fishermen arguing about an engine, the owner's daughter stacking glasses with the aggression of someone who had wanted to be anywhere else since birth.

"Nobody," Cuellar said finally.

"Cuellar."

"Nobody asked *me*." He set down his pencil with great ceremony. "But there's a man at the Hotel Miramar. Been there nine days. Pays cash. Doesn't drink, doesn't fish, doesn't gamble. Sits in the lobby in the evening with a newspaper he reads the same page of for an hour." He finally looked up. His eyes were the pale, washed-out blue of glacier melt, and they were not unkind. "You want to know what page?"

"No."

"Obituaries," said Cuellar. "He's reading the obituaries and checking the shipping notices. I asked the desk girl. She thought he was waiting for news of a death." He picked his pencil back up. "Maybe he is. Maybe he's waiting to make sure nobody's died. Hard to say with those men."

"Those men," I said. "You know who he is."

"I forget things for a living, Ines. I don't *collect* them." But he'd said it with a small, dry twist of the mouth, and I understood. There are men whose business is debt, and there are men whose business is the *fear* of debt, and the second kind travel better. They show up in towns like mine the way weather shows up — you don't get a say in it, you just get an umbrella or you get wet.

"Is he alone?"

"He eats alone. He walks alone. But he makes phone calls from the telegraph office, and the telegraph office charges him for calls to three different cities." Cuellar went back to his crossword. "You should pay me for this conversation."

"I paid you to forget me."

"I forgot you beautifully. Three years. Ask anyone." He filled in a letter. "But forgetting has a maintenance cost, and you, my friend, have been behind on your payments since spring."

There it was. I stood there in the cantina with the smell of fish and diesel coming through the door, and I felt the shape of it settle over me — not a trap exactly, because traps at least have the decency to be built by someone with a plan. This was more like a tide. It had been going out for months, my little economy of anonymity, my carefully purchased nothing, and I had noticed the water dropping and told myself it was seasonal.

"Cuellar. The woman who came to my room. How did she find me?"

"Ah." He actually smiled, which on Cuellar is roughly as reassuring as a shark smiling. "That's the interesting part. She didn't find you. She found the man at the Miramar, and the man at the Miramar — whether he meant to or not — led her to you. She's been following *him* for two days, and he's been following you for nine." He tapped his temple. "You see? You were never hiding from her. You were hiding from him. She just used his map."

I want to record, for the sake of accuracy, that I did not immediately understand the implications of this. I stood there for a good ten seconds doing the arithmetic, and when it finished, the sum was this: a debt collector had spent nine days in Puerto Ceniza establishing where I was, what I did, and how to reach me — and a woman with money had spent two days following *him* — and both of them had converged on my door within a week, and I had done nothing to cause any of it.

I had simply been the destination. Two different roads, one wreck of a guide.

* * *

Mara Voss found me again that afternoon at the boatyard, which surprised me less than it should have. I'd gone there to check on the *Perla*, Tomás's old launch, mostly because the boatyard was the one place in Puerto Ceniza where my hands had something honest to do, and partly because I wanted to see the river.

The Rio Sombra comes down out of the mountains through the town in a brown, muscular hurry, carrying snowmelt and silt and, if you believe the old men at the cantina, the occasional memory. You can stand on the quay and look upstream into the gorge, where the walls go up and up until the river is just a bright thread between them, and past that — though you can't see it, nobody can, there's a bend and then a fog bank that never lifts — past that is the valley. The closed valley. The one with the treaty line drawn on maps in red ink and the words *ZONA VEDADA — ACCESO PROHIBIDO* in three languages, and beneath that, in smaller print, a reference number, and beneath *that*, in no print at all but in the voice every local uses when they mention it: *and good riddance*.

I was tightening a cleat that didn't need tightening when her shadow fell across the planks.

"You didn't take the money," she said.

"Good morning to you too."

"It's two o'clock." Mara Voss was not a woman who wasted motion, I was learning. She had the same quality I'd noticed the night before — a stillness that wasn't calm, that was more like a river held behind a dam. She wore a traveling coat too good for Puerto Ceniza and boots that had actually been walked in, and she stood on the quay looking at the *Perla* with an expression I recognized, because it was the expression of someone who had already decided to get on a boat and was simply waiting for the boat to agree.

"Mr. Halloran," she said. "I'd like to buy you lunch."

"I know this town's lunch options intimately. There's one."

"Then I'd like to buy you the one."

* * *

The one turned out to be the cantina, because of course it did, and the owner gave us the table by the window with the wobbly leg, which is the table you give people when you want to hear what they say without appearing to want to hear what they say. Mara ordered for both of us without consulting me, which I confess I admired. She ordered the fish, and then she waited until the owner had retreated to a distance of exactly not-quite-eavesdropping, and she opened a leather folder on the table between us.

Inside was a photograph.

A man in his sixties, gray-bearded, squinting into a sun that was clearly too bright for him, standing on a riverbank with a theodolite on a tripod beside him and his hand resting on it the way you'd rest a hand on a horse's neck. He looked like a man who had spent his whole life outdoors and enjoyed precisely none of the parts of it that involved standing still.

"My father," she said. "Emil Voss. He went up the Sombra eleven days ago with a survey team. Four men. They were mapping the headwaters." She turned the photograph toward me. "The radio schedule was every evening at eight. He never missed one in thirty years of fieldwork. Not once. Not in storms, not when he had a fever, not when his second wife left him — he called the radio schedule *from the hotel*." Her voice stayed level. Her hands, I noticed, did not entirely. "Nine days ago, the eight o'clock call didn't come. Or it came, and then it stopped. The last transmission was eleven seconds of static, and then — my brother was listening on the base set — he says there was a voice. Just a word, maybe. He can't swear to what it was."

"What did the authorities say?"

"The authorities," she said, "said that the valley is closed by treaty, that the expedition entered it against advisories, and that a search operation would constitute a violation of international agreement requiring ministerial approval in two countries, and that ministerial approval is, at present, unavailable." She said it in the flat reciting voice of someone who had heard it many times and had stopped hearing it as words. "They said this to me in an office with air conditioning. They offered me tea."

She closed the folder. Underneath it, her hands were folded now, still again, and I understood that the folder had been doing the shaking so she didn't have to.

"I know who you are, Mr. Halloran. I know you guided the Cordillera routes for nine years. I know you were Rennick's man. And I know what happened to your name." She said the last part carefully, the way you'd walk on ice you didn't trust. "I'm not here to talk about that. I'm here because my father talked about you. In his letters. He said — " she paused, and I watched her decide whether to give me the exact words or a softened version, and I watched her decide on exact, because that was the kind of woman she was — "he said, *if I'm ever lost up a river and need someone to find me, find Halloran. He's the only one who reads water like Rennick taught him, and he's the only one who'll be angry enough to do it right.*"

The fish arrived. Neither of us touched it.

"And the money," I said.

"Is a fee. A legitimate fee, for a legitimate service, which is locating and recovering a missing expedition, and I have the paperwork to make it legitimate if anyone asks." She slid the envelope across the table. It was the same envelope from my table, returned to me like a boomerang. "There's a second payment on recovery. And Mr. Halloran — I did my research. I know what you charge. This is triple."

"Money's not the problem."

"No," she agreed. "The problem is the valley. The problem is the treaty. The problem is that everyone who's ever gone up that river past the gorge has either come back changed or not come back, and the mining town at the top of it drowned in 1961 and the government sealed the whole watershed like a wound." She leaned forward. "I've read everything. I know the stories. I'm not asking you because it's safe. I'm asking you because it's *you*."

And there it was — the hook, set clean. Not the money. *It's you.* Because for three years I had been nobody, and nobody was a role I had gotten quite good at, and the terrible seductive thing about being nobody is that nobody is never asked to go anywhere or do anything or be responsible for the space between a river and the truth.

I looked at the envelope. I looked at the window, where the gorge stood open like a doorway at the end of the street, fog bank and all.

And that's when I saw him.

Through the glass, across the quay, a man in a gray jacket stood by the fish stalls, examining a newspaper he held upside down. Not entirely upside down — he'd corrected it, mostly, a moment too late — but enough. He was maybe forty, solidly built, with the patient stillness of a man who is paid to wait. As I watched, he turned a page without looking at it and let his gaze drift, casually, terribly, to the cantina window. To our table. To Mara.

He didn't look at me at all. That was the professional part. He looked at *her* — at the folder, at the envelope, at the set of her shoulders — and he made a small note in a small book, and he turned, and he walked away unhurried, the way a man walks when he wants you to know he has all the time in the world and you don't.

"The man in the gray jacket," I said quietly. "Do you know him?"

Mara didn't turn to look. That told me something too. "His name is Ferro," she said. "He works for the holding company that holds my family's debts. He's been assigned to me for four months." She finally looked at me, and her eyes were dry and furious and very tired. "I'm not going to insult you, Mr. Halloran. I'm not going to pretend I came here only for my father. I came here for my father, and I also came here because I am out of options, and because the men who hold my debts have made it very clear what happens to my family's assets — to *me* — if my father's estate can't be settled, and an estate with a missing owner doesn't get settled, it gets *liquidated*." She took a breath. "You recognized him. I saw you recognize him. So you understand: I can't go to the police, I can't go to the authorities, I can't wait for ministerial approval. The only thing I can do is find my father before Ferro's employers find a reason to stop waiting."

The fish sat between us, cooling, judging.

"Nine days," I said.

"Nine days."

"Up the Sombra. Past the gorge. Into a valley that two governments sealed for a reason nobody will say out loud."

"Yes."

"And you want me to take you."

"I want you to take me," she said, "or I want you to tell me to my face that you're the man the newspapers say you are, and then I'll find someone else and you can go back to tightening cleats."

The wobbly table leg rocked. Somewhere behind me, the owner's daughter dropped a glass, and the fishermen outside had started up again about the engine, and the river went on hurrying past the quay toward the sea, brown and busy and completely uninterested in any of us.

I thought about my name, which I had buried in this town at considerable expense. I thought about Rennick, who taught me to read water, and about what it means that a man I never met had told his daughter to find me *angry*. I thought about the obituaries, and the man reading them, and the small book with the small notes, and I understood with a clarity that felt almost like relief that the choice had never really been on the table. Ferro had seen me. Ferro's employers now had a name, a face, and a location, and men like that do not file information; they *use* it. Refuse the money, and in a month I'd be back to hauling crates — except worse, because now I'd be a known quantity, a loose thread, and loose threads get pulled.

There is a particular kind of cornered feeling where the fear burns off and what's left underneath is almost like appetite. I hadn't felt it in three years. I felt it now, sitting across from a woman who had ordered my lunch without asking, and it tasted, God help me, like the old days.

"Triple," I said, "plus expenses. And I choose the team. And you do what I say on the water, the first time, every time, no inheritance and no grief and no Ferro overrides a guide's word on a river. Ever."

"Agreed."

"And we leave before he finishes his newspaper."

Something moved through her face then — not a smile, exactly. The place where a smile would live, if it had somewhere to stand. "How fast can you be ready?"

I looked out the window. The fog bank at the end of the gorge had not moved in three years, and it did not move now, and I felt it looking back at me the way it looks at everyone in this town — patiently, the way a debt waits.

"Two days," I said. "I need to talk to a man who owes me his life."

* * *

Tomás, as it happened, was not hard to find. He was hard to *approach*, because he was at that moment standing in the shallows of the harbor in his undershirt, wrestling the *Perla*'s spare propeller out of the mud, and swearing at it in two languages and a third that I believe he invented.

"Tomás," I called down from the quay.

He surfaced, dripping, gray-stubbled, forty-nine going on seventy, with the propeller held aloft like an offering. "Ines! You're up early."

"It's three in the afternoon."

"That's early for you." He squinted at me, and then at the cantina window behind me, where Mara Voss stood with her folder, and something moved behind his eyes — the quick animal arithmetic of a man who had run rivers with me for nine years and knew exactly what it meant when I came to him with a particular set to my jaw.

"No," he said.

"You don't know what I'm going to ask."

"I know that face. That's the Rennick face. That's the *we're going somewhere the maps don't want us to go* face." He climbed up onto the quay, wrung out his undershirt with tremendous dignity, and looked at me for a long moment. "How far up?"

"Past the gorge."

He closed his eyes. The harbor slapped against the pilings. Somewhere a gull made its opinion known, as gulls do, for free.

"Nine years I've owed you," Tomás said at last, opening them. "Nine years, and I keep hoping you'll forget, and you never do, because you're the kind of man who never forgets a debt." He picked up the propeller and slung it over his shoulder. "All right. But Ines — I want it said plainly, once, between us. Whatever's up that river drowned a town. Whatever's up that river made two governments sign a piece of paper just to keep people away from it." He started toward the boatyard, then stopped, and looked back at me, and his voice dropped into something quieter and older.

"What could a man like Emil Voss possibly want up there badly enough to die for it?"

I didn't have an answer. I had an envelope full of money, a woman with a folder full of grief, a debt collector reading obituaries, and two days.

"Let's find out," I said.
