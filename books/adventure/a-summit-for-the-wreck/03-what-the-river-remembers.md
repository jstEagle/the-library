## 3. What the River Remembers

The morning after Mara Voss said his name out loud in the doorway of the Casa Ferro, Ines Halloran did what he always did when the past came knocking: he made coffee and pretended the world was a smaller place than it was.

He drank it standing at the window of his room above the boatyard, watching the Rio Sombra's lower reaches slide brown and patient through Puerto Ceniza on their way to the sea. The river didn't know about treaties. It didn't know about blacklists or debt collectors or dead mentors. It just went where gravity told it, which was more than could be said for most men.

Nine days of silence from an expedition upriver. A woman with her father's handwriting shaking in her pocket. And somewhere behind her, a man in a gray suit who smelled like interest accruing.

Ines rinsed the cup and went to work.

* * *

Gathering gear in Puerto Ceniza was an exercise in being remembered by people you'd hoped had forgotten you.

He started at Ferreira's, the chandlery that smelled of rope tar and diesel, because Ferreira didn't ask questions — mostly because Ferreira couldn't hear them anymore and refused to admit it, so conversations with him were conducted by shouting and pointing, which suited Ines fine.

"Rope," Ines shouted, holding up four fingers.

Ferreira squinted at him. "Four? You hanging somebody?"

"Anchoring something."

"Same thing, some marriages." Ferreira hauled a coil off the shelf and slapped it on the counter, then leaned in close, which for Ferreira meant shouting directly into Ines's ear. "You going upriver."

It wasn't a question. News traveled faster than boats in Puerto Ceniza, and it traveled uglier.

"Maybe," Ines said.

"The Sombra's high this year. Melt came early off the cordillera." Ferreira rang up the rope without looking at it, an act of trust he extended to no one else alive. "And you know what they say about the upper valley."

"I know what everybody says about the upper valley. That's the problem. Everybody says it, nobody's seen it."

"Nobody's seen it *because* of what happened," Ferreira said, and then, because even deaf men know when they've made their point, he added a spool of repair tape to the pile and waved off payment for it. "For your mother's sake," he said, though everyone in town knew Ines's mother had died eleven years ago in a town two hundred kilometers south. It was just how Ferreira gave gifts. You wrapped them in a lie so nobody had to say thank you.

From there it was fuel canisters from the depot — where the clerk, a young man named Beto with a mustache he was clearly still negotiating with, looked at Ines's list and then at Ines and said, "Upstream? Like, *up* upstream?" with the particular reverence Puerto Ceniza reserved for the closed valley, part dread, part pride, the way coastal towns talk about a shipwreck that put them on the map.

"It's a big river," Ines said. "I'm only going partway up it."

"That's what the '61 men said." Beto filled the canisters slowly, enjoying himself. "Partway. My grandmother used to say the river took the town of Vado Seco whole. Houses, church, the lot. One night in June. She said the water came down warm."

"Warm?"

"Flooded through the mine first, she said. Came up out of the ground already hot, like the mountain had been holding it in its mouth." Beto shivered theatrically, pleased with his grandmother's story, having told it so many times it no longer frightened him. "That's why the treaty. My grandfather helped build the fence posts on the north ridge. He said the government men wouldn't go inside themselves. Paid locals to do it and stood well back, smoking."

Ines paid him and carried the canisters out into the heat, and found that his hands were steadier than his stomach.

* * *

Tomás Iriarte lived at the far end of the fisherman's beach in a house built mostly of salvaged boat parts, which meant it leaked in the shape of whatever vessel had donated its hull. Ines found him on the porch mending nets, a cigarette parked behind one ear for later, as if the future were something Tomás planned for in small installments.

"Ines Halloran," Tomás said without looking up. "Either you need money or you need forgiveness. Those are the only two reasons anyone visits me."

"I need a third thing."

"Those are the only two things in the world, my friend. Everything else is packaging." But he set down the netting needle and looked up, and his face did the thing it always did when he saw Ines — a quick flicker of something old and heavy, there and gone, like a fish turning under the surface. "Sit. You're making the porch nervous."

Ines sat on a crate labeled FLOUR in letters faded to rumor. Below them the tide worked at the beach, unhurried, faithful.

"Mara Voss," Ines said. "Her father took an expedition up the Sombra nine days ago. Radio's gone quiet. She wants me to go find them."

Tomás lit the cigarette from behind his ear, which was somehow both now and later at once. "The Voss expedition. The surveyors. Half the dock's been talking about them for a month — rich foreigners mapping the headwaters, asking questions about the old mine." He exhaled. "They hired local porters at the mouth of the gorge. Two of those boys are cousins of my cousin. Nobody's heard from them either."

"You've heard something."

"I hear everything. It's my tragedy." Tomás drew on the cigarette. "What I haven't heard is why you're telling me. You don't need me to find a missing boat. You've done harder things alone, God knows, and look how that went for you."

"I need someone on the lines. Someone I trust."

"Ah." Tomás nodded slowly, smoke curling. "There it is. The word."

"Tomás—"

"No, no, let me enjoy it. Trust. From Ines Halloran. Do you know what they call that in certain circles?" He held up a hand before Ines could answer. "They call it a liability."

Ines let the silence sit. This was the arrangement between them, unspoken since the day six years ago on the Quebrada del Halcón when Ines had gone back down into a rockfall field that every protocol said was suicide, and dug Tomás out from under a slab with a broken femur and a broken sense of humor, and carried him — actually carried him, eleven hours, down scree that moved like bad conscience — to the road. Tomás had never thanked him properly. Thanking would have made it a transaction. Instead he'd simply never said no to him again, and both of them understood this was worth more than thanks.

"The upper valley's closed," Tomás said at last. "Treaty line. If we cross it, we're not guides anymore, we're trespassers with equipment."

"We're already trespassers with equipment. We're just currently doing it in a legal location."

"And the drowned town. Vado Seco."

"What do you remember about it? Really. Not the dock version."

Tomás was quiet for a while. He was one of the few people Ines knew who could be quiet on purpose, as a form of respect rather than avoidance.

"My father ran supply barges before the closure," he said finally. "He went up once, after. Official recovery run, very hush-hush. He never talked about it except once, drunk, and what he said was—" Tomás turned the cigarette over, studying it. "What he said was that the river hadn't destroyed the town. It had *filed* it. Everything in its place. A chair against a wall. A door leaning where a door should lean. He said it was like walking through a house where the family had just stepped out, except the family was forty years gone and the house was underwater until the drought pulled the water back last summer." He looked up. "He said the quiet up there wasn't empty. It was *kept*. Like something was maintaining it."

"Your father drink much?"

"On that occasion, extensively. But Ines — he wasn't a fanciful man. He thought weather forecasts were witchcraft." Tomás stubbed out the cigarette and immediately lit another, transferring the future from one ear to the other. "So. When do we leave?"

"Day after tomorrow. Tide and loadout."

"Of course day after tomorrow. Never today. Today is for packing badly." Tomás was already standing, brushing net fibers off his knees, and Ines felt the strange double weight of it — relief and guilt in equal measure, because he'd known Tomás would say yes, and knowing it, he'd asked anyway.

"One condition," Tomás said at the door of his salvage-palace. "If we find them dead, we bring them down whole. All of them. No leaving anyone for the mountain to keep. I've seen what it does to a man, leaving pieces behind." His eyes held Ines's, and neither of them mentioned the Quebrada del Halcón, or the fact that the piece Tomás had nearly left behind was himself.

"Whole," Ines agreed. "Everyone comes down whole."

* * *

That night, in his room above the boatyard, Ines opened the tin box.

It was an ammunition tin originally, olive drab, older than he was, and inside it lived the only inheritance Aldous Rennick had ever given him besides habits and damage: the route maps. Not the published ones — the working drafts, the ones Rennick had drawn by hand over thirty years of guiding, annotated in that small severe architect's script, corrections layered on corrections like sediment. Ines had taken them from Rennick's effects after the funeral that almost nobody attended, and he had not opened the tin in three years, because opening it meant smelling the pipe tobacco that had soaked into the paper, and smelling the tobacco meant hearing the voice, and hearing the voice meant—

Well. He opened it.

The smell rose up gentle and terrible, cherry-sweet and stale, and for a moment Ines was twenty-six years old again in a hut below the northern col, watching Rennick bend over a drafting table with a ruler held like a scalpel, saying: *A map is a promise, boy. Every line you draw, someone will bet their life that you meant it.*

He spread the maps across the bed. He was looking for the Sombra surveys — Rennick had guided up the lower gorge twice in the fifties, before the disaster, before the treaty, and Ines wanted the old water levels, the camp ledges, the portage lines around the falls. Competence was a comfort. He intended to wrap himself in it all the way upriver.

He found the 1958 sheet first. Then the 1960 revision. He laid them side by side under the lamp, and it was the 1960 revision that stopped him.

At first he couldn't name what was wrong. The draftsmanship was flawless, unmistakably Rennick's hand — the same confident contours, the same tiny annotations in the margins (*scour zone after rains*, *ledge unreliable in thaw*). But something about the middle section nagged at him, the stretch above the falls where the gorge narrowed toward the headwaters, and he sat with it for a long time, the way you sit with a face you almost recognize, before he got up, found his straightedge, and laid it along the contour lines.

The interval was wrong.

Not drawn wrong — *measured* wrong. The vertical intervals between contours through the narrows were consistent, beautiful, evenly spaced. Too evenly spaced. Real terrain doesn't space itself like sheet music. Somewhere in that section, real elevation change had been smoothed away, ironed flat, as if the mountain had been politely asked to be less dramatic there. And at the point where the smoothing began, almost invisible unless you were hunting it, the ink changed. Same pen, same hand, but a slightly different pressure — the tell of a line redrawn, traced over an earlier one.

Ines sat back on the bed with the lamp buzzing and his heart doing something slow and cold.

Because he knew this trick. He had spent five years in hearings and depositions learning every variety of it, because it was the trick they said *he* had used — the altered route map, the falsified line, the guide who drew the safe path where the safe path wasn't. They had stripped his certification for it in one country and quietly declined to renew it in another, and the whole machinery of his disgrace had turned on the premise that such a falsification was possible, that a man might redraw a mountain.

Rennick had testified against him. Rennick, his mentor, his north star, had sat in that hearing room with his hands folded and said, in that calm gravel voice, *I reviewed Mr. Halloran's route plan and I cannot account for the discrepancies.* Not an accusation. Worse than an accusation — a shrug. A door closing with the courtesy of a hotel porter.

And here, twelve years earlier, in Rennick's own hand, on a river Ines was now contracted to ascend: the same trick. Practiced. Neat. A lie drawn beautifully enough to pass, hidden in the one place no one would ever check — the work of the most trusted mapmaker on the coast.

Ines thought of Emil Voss's expedition, asking questions about the old mine. Of a journal page he hadn't found yet, waiting somewhere upstream. Of the word that would be circled twice.

He thought, unwillingly, of the hearing room, and of the moment he'd looked at Rennick across the tables and searched the old man's face for one flicker of doubt, one signal, and found nothing — and how he had carried that nothing for years like a stone in his boot, proof of his own betrayal by the only man whose faith he'd ever wanted.

But what if the nothing hadn't been nothing? What if Rennick had kept his face shut the way you keep a door shut when there's a flood behind it?

"Don't," Ines said aloud to the empty room. "Don't start writing his defense for him."

The maps lay on the blanket, patient as evidence. Outside, the Sombra went on sliding past the town in the dark, carrying meltwater and silt and whatever else down from the closed valley, keeping its own records, filing everything in its place.

He gathered the sheets together with hands that were not quite steady, and as he squared the edges, a corner of the 1960 revision caught the lamplight — and there, in the lower margin, so faint he'd missed it entirely, was a pencil notation he had never seen before. Not Rennick's script. Smaller, hurried, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper:

*Ask E.V. about the shaft. He knows what his father sealed.*

E.V.

Emil Voss.

Ines sat in the lamplight for a long time, listening to the river, feeling the last comfortable floor of the world tilt gently beneath him — because the letter was dated, in the corner, in Rennick's hand:

Three weeks before the disaster. Three weeks before Vado Seco drowned.

Three weeks before Rennick burned the rest of his papers, and let a twenty-six-year-old guide carry the blame downstream.

Ines folded the maps along their old creases and put them in the tin, and the tin in his pack, and lay down without undressing, and did not sleep so much as wait for morning, when the tide would turn and the boat would be loaded and there would be, at last, something to do with his hands besides hold them still.
