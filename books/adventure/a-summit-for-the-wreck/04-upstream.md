## 4. Upstream

The boat was called the *Perdida*, which Tomás insisted was a joke and Ines suspected was a confession. She was a flat-bottomed river skiff with a two-stroke engine that had opinions about cold mornings, and she smelled of diesel, fish, and the particular despair of a man who had bought her at auction from a hotel that had gone bankrupt.

"She floats," Tomás said, patting her gunwale the way you'd pat a nervous horse. "Everything else is negotiable."

"She leaks."

"She *weeps*. There's a difference. A boat that leaks is dying. A boat that weeps has feelings, and you respect her, and she repays you." He tossed Ines the last of the fuel drums to stow. "Also I patched the weeping with chewing gum and a tin sheet, so maybe keep your boots dry."

Ines had spent two days assembling the expedition out of Puerto Ceniza's thin inventory: rope of uncertain vintage, a medical kit whose morphine had been replaced at some point by aspirin and a saint's card, dried meat, rice, the fuel, and a second-hand radio that the hardware store owner swore worked "when it wants to." Mara Voss had paid for all of it in cash, counted out in a back room, and had not once asked whether the price was fair, which in Ines's experience meant she was paying someone else's price too, somewhere, and knew it.

She arrived at the dock at dawn with her father's expedition manifest folded into a plastic bag she kept touching like a rosary.

"Three of us," she said, looking at the *Perdida* with the expression of a woman recalculating her odds. "This is the boat?"

"This is the boat."

"It's named *Lost*."

"In Spanish," Tomás said cheerfully, "which makes it fine."

Mara climbed aboard without another word, which Ines was beginning to understand was her way of consenting to things she found ridiculous. She sat amidships with the manifest on her knees and did not look back at the town as it slid away. Ines found himself looking back for her. Puerto Ceniza shrank to a strip of rust roofs and drying nets, and the feeling that came off it was not sadness exactly. It was the feeling of a door closing behind him that he'd been leaning against for three years.

The debt collector — a wide, patient man called Ferro who smiled like a man waiting for a bus — had watched them load from the end of the pier. He had not spoken. He had simply raised two fingers from his coffee cup, a small salute, and Ines had understood it perfectly: *I know where you're going. I know when you're due back.*

"Nice fellow," Tomás said, following his gaze.

"He's the reason we're going."

"He's the reason you're going. I'm going because you pulled me out of the Quebrada Honda with a broken ankle and a rope you tied one-handed in the dark, and a man doesn't forget a thing like that, even if he'd like to." Tomás started the engine. It caught on the fourth pull, sneezed, and settled into a ragged heartbeat. "Also the money. Mostly the money. The rope thing is the *reason*, the money is the *motive*. There's a legal distinction."

The Rio Sombra began, as rivers do, by pretending to be harmless.

For the first hours it was wide and brown-green and lazy, sliding between banks of cane and second-growth forest, and the three of them almost relaxed. Mara read the manifest aloud at one point — forty kilos of survey equipment, a satellite phone her father had never activated, "one (1) camp guitar, Emil's, do not let Brandt near it" — and for a moment she laughed, a small startled sound like she'd surprised herself, and Ines felt the boat get lighter by several kilos.

Then the walls came.

The gorges of the upper Sombra did not arrive so much as close around you, the way a hand closes. The banks steepened, then became cliffs, then became the river's whole world — walls of grey and rust-striped stone a hundred meters high, so close in the narrows that the *Perdida*'s hull brushed the rock on both sides and the sky shrank to a bright, indifferent ribbon overhead. The water quickened. The temperature dropped. And the sound changed: the gorge took the engine's noise and the river's noise and folded them together into something with a pulse.

That was when the radio started.

Tomás had it wedged in the stern, tuned to the emergency band out of habit, and it had been hissing static since the town. But in the gorge the hiss changed character. It came in swells, in phrases almost, and every few minutes something in it arranged itself — for half a second, no more — into a shape that the human ear refused to file under *weather*.

"There." Mara sat up. "There, did you — that was a voice."

"That was the gorge," Tomás said. "Sound does funny things in stone. Echoes off the water, off the walls, comes back scrambled. You get the same thing in the Quebrada Honda. Sounds like someone calling your name. First time it happened to me I answered."

"What did you say?" Mara asked.

"I said *what*. Very brave. *What.* To a canyon." He adjusted the radio's dial without looking at it, which Ines noticed, and noted, and did not comment on. The static swelled again, and this time it carried something with a rhythm to it, four beats, a pause, four beats, like a man walking.

Ines took the radio and turned it off. The silence in the gorge was worse.

"It's the rock," he said. "Water over stone, thirty different surfaces, the echo comes back in pieces. Your ears try to build a voice out of it because ears are stupid. They'd rather hear a ghost than nothing."

"And you?" Mara asked. "What do your ears hear?"

Ines looked at the walls sliding past, at the water-stains on the rock that ran down in long dark fingers, like the gorge had been trying to climb out for a very long time.

"My ears hear a river that's had sixty years of practice sounding like people," he said. "I'll tell you if that stops being comforting."

* * *

They camped that first night on a gravel bar shaped like a sleeping animal, and Ines dreamed of a classroom in a mountain school twenty years gone, and Aldous Rennick at the blackboard drawing a contour line that curved the wrong way, and young Ines raising his hand to say *teacher, that's wrong*, and Rennick smiling at him with real warmth and saying, *good eye, Halloran. Now — is the map wrong, or is the mountain?* And the whole class laughing, because it was an old joke, the oldest one in guiding, and Ines had laughed too, and then the blackboard had filled with water.

He woke with the dawn and the cold and Mara already up, standing at the water's edge with her boots on and her arms wrapped around herself, watching the fog come off the river in ropes.

"You sleep badly," she said, when he joined her. It wasn't a question.

"I sleep like a guide."

"Which is how?"

"Half asleep. The other half is listening." He handed her coffee from the battered pot. It was terrible. She drank it anyway, all of it, which he was beginning to suspect was also her way of doing things. "Your father. Was he a good traveller?"

"He was a good *leaver*," Mara said. "He was wonderful at the beginning of things. Expeditions, businesses, marriages — he'd light them like matches. My mother used to say the trick was never to be the thing he started." She turned the manifest bag over in her hands. "He got careful in the last few years. Slower. He'd check a route three times. Brandt told me once it was because of a mistake he made early on, before I was born. He never said what."

The fog was lifting off the water in long white banners, and somewhere upstream the radio, off, sat in its box like a thing pretending to sleep.

"Everyone's got one mistake," Ines said. "The good ones just get one."

"And you?"

"I got mine. That's why I'm here." He stood, and the gravel crunched, and the day began whether they wanted it or not. "Eat. Today the river gets honest, and I want us fed before it does."

* * *

The river got honest around midday, in installments.

First the rapids: short, steep, mean, the kind that look passable until you're in them and discover the rock in the middle has an opinion. Ines ran the boat; Tomás read the water from the bow and called the lines in the shorthand of men who'd done this together before, in other years, on other rivers, before everything: "*Right of the tongue — left — hard left, hole, punch it —*" and the *Perdida* slammed and slewed and shipped half a ton of freezing water that Mara bailed with a cooking pot, swearing with a fluency that made Tomás grin.

Then the falls, where they portaged, hauling the boat and the drums up a goat track that wasn't a track so much as a suggestion the goats had declined. It was there, in the scree above the falls, that Tomás stopped and crouched and said, without any of his usual music in it:

"Ines."

Ines came up beside him. On the flat stone at his feet, half-buried in gravel, was a strip of orange flagging tape. Expedition tape — he knew it at a glance, the same roll he'd seen in the photograph of Emil Voss's team at the trailhead, the same orange, printed with the survey firm's mark. It was tied off neatly around a stone. A marker. Someone had wanted this place found.

Or wanted whoever followed to know they'd passed.

"Fresh," Tomás said. "Faded none. Weeks, maybe less."

Mara crouched and touched the tape and said nothing at all, and her saying nothing was louder than the falls.

They found the cache an hour later, in the mouth of a side ravine where the walls opened just enough to make a room. Ines saw it before he saw it: a wrongness in the talus, a stack of stones too regular. Under a lean-to of flat slabs, waterproofed in tarps tied with proper knots, sat the expedition's reserve — rice, fuel, a rope coil, a first-aid tin, spare boots. Everything a team would cache against the return journey. Everything a team would come back for.

Everything still here.

"They didn't come back," Mara said. Her voice was very level, the levelness of a woman standing on ice and listening for the crack.

Ines knelt and looked, and what he looked at made the back of his neck prickle. The tarps had been untied — not cut, not torn, untied, the knots eased open the way you'd open your own gear. The rice sacks were restacked. The fuel drums were upright, capped. The rope was recoiled in a proper mountaineer's coil, not a hurried one.

Nobody had looted this cache. Somebody had *visited* it. Somebody careful. Somebody with time and hands that knew knots, who had opened everything, looked at everything, and closed it again better than they'd found it.

"Brandt," Ines said quietly. "The chief surveyor. If he's alive, he came through here. He took what he needed and left the rest for—" He stopped.

"For whoever followed," Mara finished.

"For us," Tomás said. "He left it for *us*. Which means he knew someone would come. Which means—" He looked at Ines.

"Which means he wanted us to keep coming," Ines said.

Under the cache's floor slab — the last thing you'd check, the first thing a careful man would leave something — they found the journal page.

It was torn from a surveyor's field book, the paper soft with damp at the edges, the writing cramped and slanting, a surveyor's hand that wrote fast and pressed hard. Coordinates, a full set, latitude and longitude to the second. And beneath them, in the same hand, pressed so deep into the paper it had nearly torn through:

WRONG

Circled. Circled again. The second circle drawn so hard and so many times the pen had gone through the page, and on the reverse, faintly, the same word backwards, like a bruise.

No name. No date. No explanation. Just the coordinates, and the word, and the two circles, and the silence of a gorge that had been practicing sounding like voices for sixty years.

Mara held the page in both hands. Ines watched her read it three times, watched her lips move on the second pass.

"That's my father's hand," she said. "The coordinates. That's Emil's writing." Her thumb moved to the word. "This isn't."

"You're sure?"

"I've read his field books my whole life. I know his hand like I know my own." She looked up at him, and her face in the grey light of the ravine was the face of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't want finished. "Someone else wrote this. Someone with him. Someone who read his coordinates and—" she stopped, and the word came out of her small and flat, "—and disagreed."

Ines took the page. He held it up to the thin light, and the pressed-through word showed on the back, WRONG reversed, and he thought of a blackboard, and a contour line curving the wrong way, and a warm voice saying *is the map wrong, or is the mountain?*

The coordinates were for a point high in the valley. He knew the ground from Rennick's old maps — he'd memorized them the way you memorize a scar — and the point sat above the drowned town, above the mine, in country the 1961 maps had left blank. Country the treaty had closed.

"Someone found what Emil was looking for," he said. "And it wasn't what he thought it was. And instead of leaving — they stayed. They cached the gear so the followers could come up. They marked the route." He folded the page along its old creases and handed it back to her. "They're not running from something up there, Mara. They're waiting."

Tomás had drifted to the mouth of the ravine while they talked, and now he came back fast, and there was no grin anywhere on him.

"Fuel drum," he said. "The spare. It's gone."

Ines turned. The cache was exactly as they'd found it — tarps, knots, restacked rice — except where five drums had sat that morning there were four, and the space where the fifth had stood was swept clean of dust.

They had not heard a thing. Not a footstep, not a stone shift, not a bird. The gorge had swallowed the sound the way it swallowed everything, and given back static shaped almost like voices.

Mara looked at the empty space, then at the journal page in her hands, then at Ines.

"Are we being helped," she asked, "or herded?"

Ines looked up the ravine, where the walls narrowed to a slot of grey light and the river came down cold and quick from the closed valley, from the drowned town, from whatever was waiting there with a fifth of their fuel and a word written twice through the paper.

"Yes," he said, and went to check the knots on the *Perdida*.
