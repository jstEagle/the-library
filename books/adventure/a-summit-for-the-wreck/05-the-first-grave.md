## 5. The First Grave

The river told them where to camp, the way rivers always do. It flattened out below a bend where a gravel bar had built itself up over centuries, patient as an accountant, and the gravel was dry and high enough above the waterline that even Tomás, who had spent the morning complaining about everything from the mosquitoes to the quality of the instant coffee to the general injustice of being alive at his age, pronounced it acceptable.

"Acceptable," Ines said. "High praise."

"Don't get used to it." Tomás dropped his pack with the groan of a man unloading not gear but decades. He was fifty-three, built like a door that had been left out in the weather, and he moved through the jungle with a surprising delicacy for such a large object. He owed Ines his life from a crevasse fall on the Serranía nine years back, a debt he had never once mentioned and never once let Ines forget by mentioning it himself. "In my day we camped on granite. Granite doesn't shift in the night. Granite doesn't have ambitions."

"This gravel has never done anything wrong in its life."

"That's what they said about the last gravel bar, and now it's a lake somewhere."

They made camp the way old partners do, without discussion—Ines on the stove, Tomás on the tarp, Mara on firewood, because she had asked for a job and Ines had learned on the boat that the fastest way to keep a grieving woman steady was to give her hands something to do that wasn't wringing. She came back with an armload of driftwood and a small, fierce satisfaction, and Ines thought: *her father taught her that. Somewhere there's a man who taught his daughter that deadwood snaps clean when it's ready, and that man is nine days silent up this river.*

They ate. The radio hissed. Tomás fiddled with the dial out of habit, caught a half-second of something that might have been a voice or might have been a bird with opinions, and shut it off with the finality of a man closing a casket.

It was Mara who found the grave.

She had gone downstream to wash the pots—insisted on it, though Ines would have done it—and she came back up the gravel bar at a walk that was too fast and too controlled at the same time, the way people move when they are trying not to run because running would make it real.

"Ines," she said. "You should come see this."

*You should come see this* is never a sentence that ends well on a river. He set down the pot he was scrubbing and followed her along the bank, past the last of their firelight, to where the gravel gave way to a shelf of packed earth above the high-water mark.

Someone had cleared the ground. That was the first thing he noticed—not the marker, not the stones, but the clearing. A rectangle maybe two meters by one, swept clean of leaf litter and sticks with the kind of care that takes time, and time was the thing that made his chest tighten before his brain caught up. This wasn't done in an hour. This was done by someone who had hours to spend, which meant someone who wasn't running, which meant someone who had decided to stay.

The stones were river cobbles, laid in courses, not just heaped but *laid*, fitted, the way a mason would do it if a mason had been in a hurry and grieving. And at the head of it, driven into the earth at a slight and deliberate angle, stood an ice axe.

Ines knew it. He had never seen it before in his life and he knew it the way you know a face from a photograph. Blue shaft, worn tape grip, a hairline crack in the adze that someone had wrapped with wire instead of replacing—because the owner was the kind of man who wrapped things with wire instead of replacing them, who believed tools had tenures. Emil Voss had described the axe in his last radio call, the one Mara had played for Ines twice in her rented room in Puerto Ceniza, her finger on the pause button like it was a wound: *—and if anything happens, look for the axe, I'll mark our trail with the axe—*

Except he hadn't marked a trail. He had marked a grave.

Mara didn't touch it. She stood with her arms wrapped around herself, and her voice came out flat and careful, a voice walking on ice. "That's his."

"I know."

"That's his axe, Ines."

"I know." He crouched by the cairn, not touching it either, reading it the way he'd read snow slopes and river eddies, the way he'd once read a whole mountain for a client and gotten it wrong, or been made to get it wrong, depending on which version of history you bought. The stones were dry. The earth beneath them was dry. No rain had touched this since it was built, and it hadn't rained in four days, which meant it was built—what, five days ago? Six? Fresh. Fresh as a wound.

"Is he—" Mara started, and stopped, and started again. "Is he *in there*?"

And here was the thing, the thing Ines would turn over in his mind for years afterward: he didn't know. A grave this size, this well-built, should hold a body. That was what graves were *for*. But the proportions were wrong, somehow, or maybe not wrong, maybe exactly right in a way he couldn't articulate, and the honest answer was that a cairn like this could hold a man or could hold a man's boots or could hold nothing at all but the intention of a grave, a promise made to someone who might not need it.

"I don't know," he said, because he had decided somewhere around the age of thirty that he would never again lie to a client, even kindly, even to protect them. It had cost him work. It might have cost him everything. He was keeping the policy.

Tomás came up the bank behind them, saw the cairn, and went very still in the way of large animals. Then he took off his hat, which was the most Tomás thing imaginable—grief, fear, and etiquette all arriving at the same gesture.

"Madre mía," he said quietly. "Emil Voss."

"You knew him?" Mara asked.

"I drank with him in Ceniza, years ago. He beat me at cards and then bought the next round, which is the mark of either a saint or a con man." Tomás put his hat back on. He looked at Ines, and his face had already changed, had already arrived at the conclusion Ines could see forming in him like weather. "Ines. We should go back."

"Tomás—"

"No. Listen to me. Listen." He kept his voice low, the way you do near graves and near animals you don't understand. "Nine days of silence. A fresh grave. An ice axe in the ground. We are three people with a rubber boat and a radio that speaks in bird. Whatever is up this river killed a full expedition with radios and satellite phones and—what did Mara say, seven people? Seven. And they buried one of them and the river swallowed the rest. That's arithmetic, Ines. I can do arithmetic even at my age."

Ines didn't answer him right away. He was looking at the ground.

The grave sat on a shelf above the bank. Below it, the gravel ran down to the water. And the water was the thing—because if Emil Voss had died here, if his people had buried him here, then the natural thing, the almost compulsory thing, would be for the story to end in the river. The Sombra takes its dead downstream. Every flood, every mining town, every drowned expedition—the river is the direction of endings. Downstream is where things go when they are finished.

But the drag marks went up.

They were faint—heel scuffs in the packed earth at the edge of the clearing, a disturbed patch of moss, a snapped fern with the break still green—and they led uphill, away from the water, into the treeline, in a line that was straighter than a panicked line. Straight like a chosen line. Straight like a line drawn by someone carrying a heavy load who knew exactly where he was taking it.

"Tomás," Ines said. "Come look at this."

"I've seen drag marks before, Ines. I've *made* drag marks before. That's rather my point—"

"Uphill."

Tomás came and looked. The three of them stood over the scuffed earth like detectives in a novel, and the jungle breathed around them, and somewhere upstream the river made its long indifferent argument.

"Uphill," Tomás repeated, as if the word had personally offended him. "Why would anyone—"

"Carry a body uphill?" Ines stood. "You don't. Not unless the body's going somewhere. Not unless—" He stopped himself, because the next part was the part he didn't want to say in front of Mara, and then he said it anyway, because of the policy. "Not unless the body isn't a body yet. Or not unless the grave is for something else."

"Like what?"

"Like a message."

Mara had gone very white. "A message to who?"

"To whoever came looking." Ines looked at the ice axe again, at its deliberate angle, its wire-wrapped adze catching the last light. *Look for the axe, I'll mark our trail with the axe.* And here was Emil Voss, nine days silent, and here was his axe, planted like a flag on a grave that might be empty, on a route that went up instead of down. Either Emil was dead and his people had done something strange and deliberate with his body, or Emil was alive and had done something strange and deliberate with his own grave, and Ines found, to his own surprise, that the second possibility didn't comfort him as much as it should have. A dead man was a tragedy. A living man faking his own grave was a *plan*, and plans up this river had a way of including other people without asking them first.

They walked back to camp in the blue dark. Nobody cooked. Tomás checked the perimeter twice, which he never did, and then a third time, which he really never did, and Ines let him, because checking a perimeter is mostly a way of checking yourself, and Tomás needed checking.

"We should go back," Tomás said again, by the fire, in the tone of a man laying down his last card. "Ines. Old friend. Brother of mine. We should go back *tonight*."

"We can't run the gorge in the dark and you know it."

"Then at first light."

"Maybe."

"*Maybe*—" Tomás threw up his hands. "He says *maybe*. Nine days of silence, a fresh grave, and the man says *maybe*, like we're deciding where to have lunch." He pointed at Mara, gently, the way you point at something fragile. "She hired you to find her father. I understand that. I do. But if her father is behind that cairn, then the finding is done, and if her father is *not* behind that cairn, then whoever built it wants us to think he is, and either way, Ines, either way, we are being *told a story* by someone upriver, and I have never once in my life enjoyed the ending of a story told to me by a stranger in the jungle."

It was a good argument. Ines gave it the respect of silence. Then he looked at Mara, who was sitting with Emil's last letter in her lap—not reading it, just holding it, the way you hold a hand—and he thought about what he would want, if it were his father under that cairn or not under it, and the answer was so obvious it was almost embarrassing.

"We go up at first light," he said. "Slow. Wide spacing. Nobody touches anything we didn't bring."

Tomás muttered something in the old river dialect that translated roughly to *of course we do* and *I hate this* and *pass the coffee*, and Ines passed the coffee, and that was the argument, because that was how it went with them: Tomás fought, Ines decided, Tomás grumbled and obeyed, and both of them understood that the grumbling was half the engine.

* * *

He took first watch, because he wouldn't have slept anyway.

The fire had burned to a red heart. Mara was asleep sitting up, which she'd been doing for days, her chin dropping and jerking up and dropping again, fighting sleep like it owed her money. Tomás was a shape under the tarp, breathing in the deep tidal rhythm of a man who had lost the argument and won the sleep.

The river ran. The jungle made its night noises—the frogs, the thing that might have been a monkey or might have been a branch, the long insect drone like the jungle humming to itself while it thought. Ines sat with the rifle across his knees that he'd sworn he wouldn't bring and had brought anyway, and he thought about Aldous Rennick, and the altered maps, and the word *WRONG* circled twice on the journal page they'd found pinned under a stone two days downstream, and he thought: *everybody up this river is writing something down. The question is who's still alive to read it.*

He didn't know what woke him to it. That was the thing he'd replay later—the not-knowing. There was no snap of twig, no splash, none of the dramatic cues. It was more that the jungle's hum changed key, a half-step, the way a room changes when a person enters it, and Ines was on his feet with the rifle before his conscious mind had filed the paperwork.

Nothing. The fire's red heart. The black wall of trees. Tomás breathing. The river.

He stood there for a full ten minutes, feeling foolish, feeling *right*. Then he checked the gear, because that was the drill, because paranoia without procedure is just fear with better posture.

The fuel was gone.

Both cans. The white gas, the reserve, the whole liquid heart of their expedition, lifted out of the gear pile between one breath of the jungle's hum and the next, from a camp with three sleepers in it, one of them allegedly on watch. The tarp hadn't been disturbed. The ground showed nothing—no prints he could read in the dark, and he could read prints, that was one of the few things left on his résumé that nobody had managed to take away.

And in the place where the fuel cans had stood, weighted down with a pebble so he'd be sure to find it, was a strip of flagging tape.

He knew the tape. He'd been seeing it for two days, tied to branches along the bank—the expedition's route markers, orange, sun-faded, Emil Voss's survey crew had flagged their way up the Sombra like breadcrumbs. This strip was fresh-cut, the edge still bright, and it was torn to about the length of a man's forearm, and it meant, in the plain language of people who work in the bush, the same thing it has always meant:

*I was here. I know you're here. I could have done worse.*

Ines crouched over the pebble and the tape for a long time. Behind him, Tomás's breathing had stopped being tidal. He was awake, and he had been for some part of it, and he said, very quietly, from under the tarp, in a voice with no grumbling left in it at all:

"Ines. Tell me you're seeing this."

"I'm seeing it."

"The fuel."

"The fuel."

"Then we're walking out. Both cans. That's not a warning, that's a sentence. Without fuel we can't run the stove, we can't—"

"We can eat cold and boil water on a wood fire. We've done worse." Ines turned the tape over in his fingers. Fresh-cut. Deliberate. Left as an exchange, a trade, a *transaction*—and that was the word his mind kept circling back to, the word that made the cold walk up his arms. Thieves don't leave receipts. Predators don't leave messages. This was neither. This was a *negotiation*, conducted by someone who had walked into their camp, taken what he wanted, and paid for it, because he wanted them to understand something.

They're not attacking us, Ines thought. They're *managing* us.

Somebody up this river was herding them. Taking the fuel to slow them down or stop them—and then leaving the tape to say *I'm not your enemy*, to say *turn around*, or maybe—maybe—to say *come on, then, but come the way I want you to come.* The grave with the axe, uphill instead of down. The journal page with WRONG circled twice. The cache opened carefully, not looted. It was all one sentence, and Ines was only now learning to read it, and the sentence was: *I know exactly who you are and I have known since the day you launched, and everything you have found so far, you found because I let you find it.*

"Ines," Tomás said. "Say something. You're doing the thing where you go quiet and the rest of us age."

"We're being managed," Ines said. "Not attacked. Managed. Somebody wants us to go a certain way, or not go, and they're spending a lot of effort on the difference." He held up the tape. "You don't leave change for a man you're going to kill."

Tomás was quiet for a moment. "Or," he said, "you leave it so the body reads better."

And Ines, who had been thinking the same thing and hating it, said: "Go back to sleep, Tomás."

"You first."

"Nobody's sleeping," said Mara, from the dark, in the flat careful voice she'd used at the grave, and none of them slept, and the river ran, and upstream, in the black gorge where the temperature dropped without reason and the fog pooled like something poured, somebody who knew they were coming sat with a radio and a knife and a strip of orange tape, and waited to see which way the herding would take them.

At first light, Ines stood at the head of the gravel bar and looked upriver, where the Sombra narrowed into the gorge and the gorge narrowed into the fog, and he made the decision the way you make all the decisions that matter: already made, just now admitted.

"Pack up," he said. "We're going up."
