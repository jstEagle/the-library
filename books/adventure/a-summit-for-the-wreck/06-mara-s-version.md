## 6. Mara's Version

Three days before she ever spoke to Ines Halloran, Mara Voss sat in the back of a church in Puerto Ceniza that had been deconsecrated and turned into a hardware store, holding her father's last letter and trying not to cry in front of a man selling pipe fittings.

The letter had arrived eleven days after the radio went silent, which was impossible, which meant Emil Voss had written it before he left and paid someone to hold it and mail it late — a dead man's trick, or a careful man's trick, and Mara could no longer tell the difference between those two things when it came to her father.

*If I don't come back,* the letter began, in handwriting that slanted downhill like everything else he'd done in his last decade, *don't come looking. That's an order.*

She had read that line forty times. She had underlined it once, in pencil, and then erased the underline because underlining felt like arguing with a dead man and she wasn't ready to win.

*But if you're going to ignore me, and you will, you always were your mother's daughter — go to Puerto Ceniza. Find Ines Halloran. He works odd jobs and pretends he's nobody. He is not nobody. And there is something you need to understand about Rennick's lie before anyone tells you what your grandfather did. Ask Halloran about the '61 route map. He'll say he doesn't know what you mean. Make him say it twice.*

Mara looked up from the letter. The man at the counter was wrapping a hammer in newspaper with the tenderness of someone who loved his inventory more than most people loved their children.

"Excuse me," she said. "Do you know where I can find Ines Halloran?"

The man stopped wrapping. The two other customers stopped pretending to browse. Somewhere in the back, a radio played a song about heartbreak with the enthusiasm of a man who had never experienced any.

"Halloran," the man repeated, the way you'd repeat the name of a disease.

"He used to guide," Mara said. "Upstream work."

"Nobody guides anymore." He said it flatly, not unkindly, like reporting weather. "River took that away from us."

"I've heard he fixes roofs."

The man's eyes did something complicated. "Roofs," he agreed. "Boats. Fences. Whatever needs doing." He leaned across the counter, lowering his voice as though Halloran might be hiding among the pipe fittings. "You want the blue house past the cannery, the one with the crooked chimney. But listen to me, señorita." He held up one finger. "Whatever you want from that man, ask yourself first whether it's worth what it costs him."

"It's my father," Mara said.

Something in the shopkeeper's face softened and closed at the same time, like a door with a broken latch. "Then God help you both," he said, and gave her directions, and would not take her money for the hammer she hadn't asked for and didn't buy anyway.

* * *

The blue house had a roof that was, in fairness, mostly attached. Ines Halloran was on top of it when she arrived, prying up shingles with the grim focus of a man punishing the roof for something. He was younger than she expected — mid-thirties, maybe, with sun damage that made him look older and hands that made him look exactly his age: rope-scarred, nicked, sure of themselves.

"Ines Halloran?" she called up.

He didn't stop working. "Depends who's asking."

"Mara Voss."

That got him. The pry bar stopped moving. For three full seconds he stood very still on the roofline, a man doing rapid invisible arithmetic, and then he said, without turning around:

"No."

"You haven't heard—"

"There's nothing to hear. Whatever it is, whoever sent you, whatever they're paying — no." Now he did turn, and she saw what the shopkeeper had meant by *what it costs him*. His face had the particular hollowness of a man who had already survived the loss of the thing he cared about most and knew better than to reach for anything again. "I'm sorry about your father. I mean that. But the answer is no, and it was no before you finished climbing my stairs."

"My father is Emil Voss," she said. "His expedition went up the Rio Sombra nine days ago and stopped answering its radio."

The pry bar slipped. He caught himself on the ridge beam, swore softly, and looked down at her with an expression she would spend weeks learning to read: not surprise. Recognition. Like a man hearing a sound he'd been dreading for years and finally, almost gratefully, identifying it.

"The Sombra's closed," he said. "Treaty line. Nobody goes up there."

"Somebody did. Four somebodies. My father, two surveyors, and a camp cook named Pilar Ochoa, who has a daughter in Valdivia who writes me every week asking if I've heard anything." Mara pulled the letter from her jacket. It had gone soft at the folds from handling. "And my father wrote this. Before he left. He wrote it so it would arrive after he disappeared, which means he knew he might disappear, which means—" Her voice did something she hated, wobbled, and she steadied it the way her mother had taught her: chin down, breath through the nose, finish the sentence. "Which means he went up that river knowing what he was looking for might kill him, and he went anyway, and he sent me to you."

Halloran came down the ladder slowly, taking each rung like a man descending into testimony. Up close he smelled of tar and woodsmoke. There was sawdust in his hair. She had imagined, in her long nights planning this, that she would find a ruined man, a drunk, someone she could pity into compliance. Instead she found someone tired and sharp and watching her the way her father watched river water — for what moved underneath.

"Let me see the letter," he said.

She handed it over. She watched him read it, and she watched the exact moment he reached the part about Rennick, because his jaw tightened and his thumb pressed white against the paper and he stopped breathing for the length of a sentence.

"'Rennick's lie,'" he read aloud, quietly. Then, folding the letter and handing it back with exaggerated care, as though returning something explosive: "I don't know what that means."

"Make him say it twice," Mara quoted. "My father wrote that too. So — that's once. Say it again."

For a moment she thought he would laugh. What actually happened was worse: he looked at her with something close to respect, and respect, she could see, was the thing he least wanted to feel.

"You've done this before," he said. "Negotiating."

"I collect debts for a living. Well — I *did*, before my own debts started collecting me." She said it lightly. She had learned that lightness was armor, and also that armor showed everyone exactly where you expected to be hit. "Mr. Halloran. I'm not here to drag you anywhere. I'm here because my father believed you were the only person alive who could read a certain map correctly, and because I have money — not much, but real — and because I am going up that river with or without you, and my father would rise from his grave specifically to haunt me if I did it alone."

"Your father might not be dead."

"Then he'd haunt me anyway. He's thorough."

There it was — the almost-laugh again, quickly suppressed. He sat down on the bottom rung of the ladder, forearms on knees, and studied the ground between his boots as if the answer were written there in a language he'd been avoiding.

"Ask me tomorrow," he said finally.

"Why tomorrow?"

"Because tonight I'm going to look at an old map, and drink half a bottle, and decide whether I'm the kind of man who says yes to you or the kind who lets you die politely somewhere out of his sight." He glanced up. "Either way, come back at eight. Bring the letter. Don't bring anyone else."

* * *

What Mara did not tell him, that first day, was about the man in the grey jacket.

She had noticed him in Valdivia first — a broad, patient man who appeared at the edge of her vision the way bad news appears at the edge of a good week. He was outside the bank when she withdrew the money. He was across the street from the pension where she slept. On the bus to Puerto Ceniza he had sat three rows back reading a newspaper he never turned pages of, and when she'd gotten off, he'd gotten off.

His name, she learned later, was Ferrer, and he worked for a man named Cortázar, who held paper against the Voss family estate — her grandfather's estate, the mine shares, the land holdings, the whole soft rotten inheritance she had spent her twenties pretending wasn't built on anything. Her father had borrowed against it to fund the expedition. If Emil died, the debt came due immediately, and Cortázar's men would pick the estate clean, and Mara would be left with photographs and furniture and a name worth less than the paper it embarrassed itself on.

Unless, of course, the expedition's findings could be *managed*. Ferrer had explained this to her on her second night in Puerto Ceniza, standing too close in the doorway of the cantina while she waited for a taxi that was not coming.

"Señor Cortázar is not a monster," Ferrer had said, in the tone of a man who had been assured of this by monsters. "He simply wants certainty. If Señor Voss found documents upriver — old papers, mining surveys, anything of that nature — those documents affect the value of certain assets. If they come down the mountain, they come down through proper channels. Through us. You understand? Everyone is protected. Even you."

"And if I refuse?"

Ferrer had smiled. It was a good smile, well-maintained, like the rest of him. "Then we help you anyway. We're helpful people."

So she understood, standing in Halloran's yard with the letter going soft in her pocket, that she was not a rescuer. She was bait being walked upstream. Every step she took toward the valley, she led Cortázar's men a little closer to whatever her father had found, and every coin she paid Ines Halloran was a coin Cortázar would eventually invoice her for, with interest, in ways she couldn't yet imagine.

She hired him anyway.

* * *

On the third evening, over coffee in the blue house kitchen while Tomás Reyes — a big cheerful man who owed Halloran his life and mentioned it approximately every four minutes — inventoried rope in the corner, Halloran spread Rennick's old route maps across the table and went quiet in a way that frightened her more than shouting would have.

"Aldous Rennick taught me everything," he said, not looking up. "How to read water. How to read weather. How to be the last one to eat and the first one awake. When I was blacklisted, he—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "He vouched for me. Publicly. It cost him."

"And now?"

"Now I'm looking at his map of the upper Sombra, and the contour lines around the gorge don't match the drainage pattern, and I can't make myself believe he made a mistake, because Aldous Rennick did not make mistakes." Halloran tapped one finger on a bend in the river where the ink seemed slightly heavier, retouched, deliberate. "Someone changed this. Or he did. And either way, your father circled these coordinates and wrote *wrong* twice, which means Emil Voss figured out the same thing I'm looking at right now."

"Which is?"

"That the reason the valley is closed might not be the reason we were told." He finally looked at her, and whatever he saw in her face made him soften by a degree. "You knew. Didn't you. Some of it, anyway. That's why you really came."

Mara wrapped her hands around her cooling coffee and told him the true shape of it — not all of it, not Ferrer, not yet, not the debt — but the letter, and the name Rennick, and the way her father had said, in the last phone call before he left: *Mara, whatever you think our family is, hold it loosely.* She had thought he was being sentimental. Her father was not a sentimental man. Sentimental men did not mail letters timed to arrive after their own silence.

"I may have sent him to his death," she said. It came out smaller than she intended. "I helped fund the expedition. I found him the surveyor. I told him the family name was worth recovering. I said — God, I actually said — *we're Vosses, we don't leave things buried.*" She laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "I thought I was giving him permission. I think I gave him a reason."

Halloran was quiet for a long moment. In the corner, Tomás had stopped counting rope and was studying the ceiling with the elaborate innocence of a man eavesdropping professionally.

"Here's what I know about reasons," Halloran said at last. "They don't kill people. Rivers kill people. Bad maps kill people. Men with secrets kill people. Reasons just get blamed afterward." He began rolling the maps back up, carefully, along their original creases. "We leave Thursday. You'll stay in town."

"Like hell I will."

"Ms. Voss—"

"Mara. And no. Wherever this ends, I'm there. My father ordered me not to come looking, and I intend to disobey him personally, at the source, so he can watch me do it."

Halloran looked at her for a long time — assessing, she realized later, not her stubbornness but her ballast, whether she'd capsize the boat or steady it. Apparently he decided on steady, because he sighed the sigh of a man adding weight to a crossing he already doubted.

"Thursday," he said. "Six a.m. Wear things you can lose."

* * *

She watched them load the boat at dawn — Halloran checking knots with a ferocity that looked like prayer, Tomás singing something obscene and cheerful about a widow — and she stood on the dock with her father's letter in her pocket and Ferrer's grey silhouette, she was nearly certain, reflected in the warehouse glass behind her.

*Everyone is protected,* Ferrer had said. *Even you.*

The river took them north in a haze of spray and engine smoke, and Mara Voss stood on the dock of Puerto Ceniza, holding the rope she'd been given to fend off at the lock, and understood with perfect clarity that she had just hired an honest man to walk into a valley full of liars, carrying the only truth anyone had — and that she had not yet told him who was following.

By then, the current had already taken the question out of her hands.
