## 7. Rapids and Reckoning

The falls announced themselves a full hour before they arrived — first as a change in the river's voice, from conversation to argument, then as a fine cold mist that beaded on the outboard housing and made the aluminum gunwales sweat. Ines cut the engine and let the current carry them the last few hundred meters, listening.

"Portage on the left," he said. "There's a trail. Old logging track, probably."

"You can hear a trail through fog?" Mara asked. She was learning to read the river's moods faster than he'd expected — three days in and she no longer flinched when the hull kissed rock.

"I can hear where the water isn't arguing with itself." He pointed his chin at a seam of unnaturally smooth water sliding off to the left, behind a curtain of hanging moss. "That's an eddy. Eddies mean slack water, and slack water means somebody at some point dragged a boat there and was grateful for it."

Tomás said nothing. He'd been saying very little since dawn, which was unlike him. Tomás's silences usually came in flavors — sulking, scheming, hungover — but this one had a new taste, and Ines had been chewing on it all morning without wanting to admit what it was.

They nosed into the eddy and tied off. The portage trail was real, overgrown but passable, and for two hours the work was simple and honest: packs, fuel drums, the boat itself on rollers improvised from cut saplings, all of it hauled up and over a shoulder of granite while the falls thundered somewhere below like a giant clearing his throat before a speech. Mara carried more than her share and pretended not to notice Tomás noticing. The forest here was old and wet and indifferent, ferns growing straight out of cracks in the cliff, and Ines felt something in his chest unclench slightly — the particular peace of a man doing physical labor with a clear purpose, which was the only kind of peace he'd ever trusted.

Then the trail dropped toward the lower pool, the ground went slick with spray, and everything happened at once.

The rope Tomás held — the bow line, the one keeping the boat from slewing sideways down the chute — came free of its anchor. Not slipped. Came *free*, and later Ines would turn the memory over a hundred times and never decide whether the knot had failed or been failed. The boat swung. Tomás, instinctively, heroically, stupidly, grabbed for it.

The river took him the way the river takes everything: without malice, without effort, without a second thought.

Ines heard Mara scream his name and was already moving, which surprised him, because some part of his brain had apparently been waiting three days for exactly this. He ran the slick ledge downstream, past the chute, to where the pool below the falls swirled in slow exhausted circles, and dove.

Cold is a word for weather. This was something else — a fist of meltwater closing around his chest, squeezing the air out of him in one involuntary grunt. He surfaced, spat, got his bearings by sound: the roar behind, the eddy-line ahead, and a splashing, coughing struggle off to his right where Tomás's head appeared and vanished between the white folds of the boil.

"Don't grab me!" Ines roared, swimming hard. "Grab the rope! Grab the *rope*!"

He'd coiled the throw rope across his shoulder before the portage, out of habit, out of thirty years of habit, and now the habit saved them both. He got a loop around Tomás's chest on the third try, felt the man's weight go taut and terrible against his arms, and kicked for the eddy line while Tomás thrashed and the river tried its arithmetic on them both. Mara was on the rocks hauling when they reached shallowness, hauling with a fury that would have impressed a dock crane, and then they were all three of them down on the gravel, gasping, alive, absurd.

Tomás vomited half the Rio Sombra onto the stones and lay there shaking.

Ines sat back on his heels and looked at him. Really looked. At the man's hands, which had gone to the boat instead of the bank. At the bow line, lying twenty feet upstream, neatly coiled around a stump — *neatly*, in a way that knots do not coil themselves after being torn loose. At Tomás's pack, sitting apart from the others, dry, positioned where a falling man would not land on it.

"You knew," Ines said quietly. "You knew the line was going to part."

Tomás didn't answer. Water ran out of his hair and pooled under his cheek. Above them the falls went on thundering their speech to nobody.

"*Tomás.*"

"I didn't know you'd dive in," Tomás said at last, hoarse. His voice sounded scraped raw, and not entirely by the river. "I want that understood. I did not think you were stupid enough to jump into a falls pool after a grown man who can swim."

"You can't swim."

"I grew up on this river, Ines. Of course I can swim. I just prefer not to make a habit of it." He pushed himself up on his elbows, and his eyes, when they finally met Ines's, were the worst thing about the whole morning — because they weren't guilty. They were *tired*. "Ferrer's people found me in Ceniza. Before we even launched. They paid me to send word of your position. Where you stopped, where you camped, how fast you were making. A radio schedule. Every evening, one burst transmission, coded coordinates. That's all. They said nobody gets hurt. They said—" He laughed, a horrible wet sound. "They always say that, don't they. Nobody gets hurt."

Mara had gone very still. "How much?" she asked, and her voice was flat as slate.

"Mara—"

"How much did my father's life cost?"

"They didn't pay me for your father." Tomás sat up fully now, dripping, wretched. "They paid me for *him*." He nodded at Ines. "Your father was incidental. That's the part you should be angry about. I sold a friend for less than a season's wages, and the men buying didn't even consider your family worth mentioning."

The silence stretched. Somewhere a bird made a sound like a rusty hinge opening and thought better of it.

Ines found, to his own surprise, that he wasn't furious. He'd expected fury — had perhaps been saving some up, the way a miser saves coins — but what rose in him instead was colder and older and much more familiar. It was the feeling of the hearing room in Quito, seven years ago, when the man whose maps he'd trusted read his statement aloud and Ines understood that the floor was not going to open up and swallow him, that the world simply intended to go on being wrong.

"Every evening," Ines said. "One burst transmission."

"Yes."

"Tonight's hasn't gone out yet."

"No."

Ines looked at the radio in its waterproof bag, at the falls, at the gray sky pressing down through the canopy. Then he stood, picked up the throw rope, and began coiling it with the mechanical calm of a man who has decided that the alternative to calm is drowning.

"Here's what happens now," he said. "You're going to eat, you're going to help carry the boat around the next bend, and you're going to keep sending your reports — exactly as scheduled, exactly as agreed."

"Ines—" Mara started.

"Because if the reports stop," Ines continued, over her, "they stop believing everything is fine, and they come looking, and then we have helicopters over this valley instead of one man with a shortwave and a guilty conscience. You are going to be our lie, Tomás. You spent three days selling them the truth; you can spend the rest of the trip selling them fiction. It pays better anyway. Everything about this arrangement pays better than the truth ever has."

Tomás stared at him. "You're not going to leave me here."

"No. Because leaving you here is what you'd deserve, and I've had enough of people getting what they deserve. It doesn't fix anything. Believe me. I'm the leading expert in this hemisphere." He tossed the man a towel from the dry bag — a small, stupid kindness, and he saw it land harder than any blow could have. "But understand this clearly, old friend. I will paddle with you, eat with you, and if you fall in again I will probably pull you out again, because that's reflex, not choice. But the trust is gone. What's left is arithmetic. Don't confuse the two."

Tomás took the towel. His hands shook around it. "I owed you a life," he said. "From the Condor traverse. I told you that when you asked me to come."

"You did."

"I thought maybe this square us. Now I owe you two."

"Now you owe me the truth," Ines said. "Everything else is decoration."

* * *

They ate cold rice and beans among the boulders above the lower pool, and the meal had the quality of a funeral where nobody liked the deceased enough to say so. Mara sat apart, arms around her knees, watching the water with an expression Ines couldn't file. Anger, grief, calculation — possibly all three running concurrently, the way rivers run under ice.

"He's right, though," she said at last, not looking at either of them. "About my father being incidental. Ferrer's people didn't hire guides to find him. They hired guides to find *you*. My father has been missing nine days and the men who claim to represent my family's creditors sent one boat and a bribe." She turned her head. "Why does everyone want to know where you are, Ines Halloran? You're supposed to be nobody. You moved to Puerto Ceniza specifically to be nobody."

"Apparently nobody has a market rate now."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny." He fed another stick to the spirit stove, mostly for something to do with his hands. "Rennick used to say that about me. That I was funnier than my résumé suggested."

She watched him. "You loved him. Your mentor. Even now."

It wasn't a question, and he didn't answer it, and she was gracious enough not to press. But the words sat in his chest like swallowed stones, and he thought: *that's the trap, isn't it. That's how they get you. Not with threats — with love. You'll forgive almost anything of someone who taught you to read a river.*

Above the pool, Tomás had begun, silently, to repack the boat. Working. Doing the next thing. It was, Ines reflected, the oldest apology there was, and the only kind he'd ever accepted.

* * *

They launched below the falls in late afternoon light, the river here broader and slower, gathering itself for the long climb into the mountains. The gorge walls rose on either side, green-black and dripping, and the air cooled degree by degree as though the valley ahead were exhaling winter at them.

Ines navigated by Rennick's map, spread on his knees in the stern. And it was on this stretch — flat water, easy miles, nothing to do but steer — that he finally allowed himself to see what had been nagging him since Puerto Ceniza.

The map showed the falls they'd just portaged. Showed them accurately: height, pool, portage line. Rennick had drawn this reach himself, decades ago, in the confident blue-black ink Ines would have known anywhere. But the portage trail on the map ran along the *right* bank. They had taken the left, because the left was the only side with an eddy, a trail, a landing — the only side the river permitted.

A small thing. Cartographers erred constantly; banks changed; trails moved. Except Rennick hadn't been a cartographer who erred. Rennick had been the man who taught Ines that a map was a moral document, that every line on it was a promise made to whoever came after, and that the difference between a guide and a murderer was often a single unverified contour.

And there was a second thing, smaller still, visible only if you tilted the sheet to the fading light: along the left-bank route, faintly, almost erased, a pencil ghost beneath the ink. As if Rennick had drawn the truth once, correctly, and then inked over it.

Ines lowered the map and looked north, upriver, to where the gorge narrowed into shadow and the last of the daylight gave out.

"Tomás," he called forward. "Tonight's report. Send it exactly as usual."

"It's sent. Did it at the lunch stop." A pause. "Told them you're making slow time. Arguing with the woman."

"Were you?"

"A little. On your behalf, mostly." Tomás turned around in the bow, and for a moment his face was the old face, the one from the Condor traverse, young and scared and loyal. "Ines. The coordinates they gave me for the check-ins. They cover the whole upper valley. All of it. Do you understand? Whoever wrote those numbers down expects you to go all the way to the source. They're not tracking you. They're *herding* you."

The river slid on beneath them, dark now, patient, carrying them exactly where it had always intended.

Ahead, invisible in the dusk, lay a line no map admitted to — a treaty border drawn in 1962 by men in a distant capital, sealing the upper Sombra behind law and rumor and the bones of a drowned town. There were no markers. No fence. No sign. Just a bend in the gorge that Ines would recognize when he reached it, because Rennick's ghosted pencil line stopped precisely there, and beyond it the old man's ink began to lie.

Ines steered into the dark and did not slow down.

Behind him, Mara watched the gorge walls close like a book, and Tomás keyed his radio one last time to report that the guide was exactly where everyone wanted him — and somewhere far upstream, in a valley that had been closed on purpose, something that had been waiting twenty years for company heard an engine note echo off the stone, and went quiet to listen.
