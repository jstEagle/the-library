## 8. The Valley That Was Closed on Purpose

The treaty line was invisible, which Ines found to be the treaty's most honest feature.

"There," he said, and stopped where the trail bent around a leaning alder. "That's it. We're inside."

Mara looked around at the ordinary trees, the ordinary light slanting through them, and frowned. "It looks the same as outside."

"That's what makes it strange." He crouched and touched the ground, more for ceremony than information. "Every border I've ever crossed announced itself. Fences, signs, a man with a rifle and a bad attitude. This one just... stops being talked about. The maps go blank. The radios go polite. You're supposed to feel it in your teeth."

"Do you?"

He considered. His teeth felt fine. What he felt was something older and less convenient — the particular hush of a room when someone has been speaking about you and then sees you standing in the doorway.

"Ask me again tonight," he said.

Tomás said nothing at all. Since the falls, since his confession, he had become a man made mostly of shoulders and apology, carrying his pack like penance, volunteering for every miserable chore — hauling the heaviest load, taking the wettest tent site, wading first into every crossing. It was not forgiveness, but it was useful, and Ines had decided to let usefulness stand in for trust until trust could be rebuilt or until they were all safely out of this valley, whichever came first. Probably the latter. Trust was a bridge that took years to build and one confession to burn, and Tomás had confessed with gasoline in hand.

They walked upstream into the quiet.

Ines noticed the silence before he could name it. A healthy forest is never silent; it mutters constantly — birds arguing over territory, insects conducting their small industries, wind negotiating with leaves. This forest had been muzzled. They heard their own boots, their own breathing, the creak of Mara's pack frame, and nothing else. Once, distantly, a single bird called three notes and then seemed embarrassed, and did not call again.

"It's wrong," Mara whispered, and then, catching herself: "Sorry. That's not scientific."

"No, it's wrong," Ines agreed. "Scientifically wrong. There should be birds. There should be *something*." He looked up into the canopy, tall cedars and coigüe, old growth by any measure, and yet the understory was thin, sparse, almost tidy, like a house kept by someone who no longer lived there. "Forests don't do this on their own. Something taught them to be quiet."

"Or killed everything loud," Tomás offered from behind, helpfully.

"Thank you, Tomás."

"Anytime."

* * *

They came upon the terraces in the late afternoon, and Ines stopped so abruptly that Mara walked into his pack.

At first he thought they were natural — those broad steps in the hillside above the river, green-slicked and regular. But regular was exactly the problem. Nature abhors a straight line; it had never met a mining company. As they climbed the first terrace, the truth assembled itself out of the undergrowth: foundation stones laid in courses, a row of fence posts gone soft as cheese, the stub of an orchard — apple trees, impossibly, still alive, still bearing small hard fruit nobody had picked in sixty years.

"The town," Mara breathed.

"What's left of it," Ines said. "What the drought left of it."

Everyone knew the story, or a version of it. San Esteban del Río, a mining town of some four hundred souls, swallowed in the flood of '61 when the upper workings let go and the river took the valley floor. The treaty closing the valley had been signed within the year — officially to honor the dead, unofficially because the dead included several men whose families owned things, and grief is easier to legislate than liability. For sixty years the valley had been a blank space with a ribbon around it.

But rivers are patient thieves, and lately the river had been falling. Drought had pulled the water back year by year, and the town was surfacing like a body rising slowly in a lake — first the church bell tower, then the rooftops, then the streets themselves, emerging green and dripping into daylight after six decades underwater.

They walked down what had been the main street.

It was the strangest walk of Ines's life, and he had once guided a honeymoon couple across a glacier during a whiteout while the husband recited poetry. The street ran straight and true between foundations on either side, each lot identifiable — here a house with its stone hearth still standing, chimney open to the sky like a raised hand; there a workshop, its iron tools fused into a single orange sculpture of rust. Silt lay over everything in soft gray drifts, and out of the silt grew grass, moss, young trees — the forest moving back in, tenantless, reclaiming rooms.

A church stood at the end of the street, roofless, walls sound. Its bell tower leaned at an angle that suggested it had opinions about the flood and wished to share them.

Mara drifted toward a foundation and knelt. Half-buried in the silt, a child's tin cup, green with age. She didn't touch it. She looked at it for a long moment, then rose and brushed off her knees and said nothing, and Ines understood that she was doing arithmetic — her family's name, her family's money, and this cup, all on the same ledger somewhere, though she couldn't yet read the column they shared.

"We shouldn't take anything," she said quietly.

"We won't," Ines said. "We're guests. Bad ones, technically, but guests."

* * *

They made camp in the shell of the schoolhouse, because it had walls on three sides and a view of the river, and because Ines told himself it was practical and refused to examine why the other buildings had felt worse to sleep in. Supper was rice and sausage, and Tomás cooked it without being asked, and it was good, and everyone pretended not to notice that this was also an apology.

Night came down like a lid. No owls. No frogs from the flooded margins. Just the river, talking to itself in the dark, and the three of them listening harder than they meant to.

It was near midnight when Ines gave up on sleep and took the flashlight out along the terraces, telling himself he was checking the perimeter, which was true in the way that most comforting lies are true.

The moon was up, half-full, and the ruined town lay silvered and still. And that was when he saw the boot prints.

Not theirs. Theirs were confined to the street and the schoolhouse, and he knew their patterns the way he knew his own handwriting — Tomás's big square heels, Mara's narrow tread, his own worn left sole. These prints were different: heavy lug-soled boots, size large, pressed deep. Dozens of them. They crossed the street, climbed the terraces, wove among the foundations. Some were days old, edges crumbled. Some were fresh — hours old, maybe less, the silt still sharp-edged inside the tread.

He followed them.

They went everywhere and they went nowhere. Up to the church. Along the riverbank. To the orchard, where someone had picked apples — a pile of cores, browned but recent. Back and forth, a network of paths worn into the silt like animal trails. A person — persons? — had been living here, walking these streets daily, tending this ghost town like a garden.

And not one set of prints led out.

He checked twice, three times, working the logic like a knot. The valley had two mouths: the gorge downstream, which they'd come up, and the high country upstream, where the headwaters gathered. The prints covered the town thoroughly. At the town's upstream edge they thinned, funneled, and converged — all of them — onto a single track climbing toward the mountainside north of the valley. None came back down toward the gorge. None crossed the river south. Whoever walked these streets arrived from the mountain and returned to the mountain, always, every time, like water running uphill out of spite.

Nobody leaves downriver. Nobody leaves at all, except upward, into the rock.

Ines stood in the moonlit street of a drowned town, sixty years from the nearest legal human being, and felt the hair rise on his arms — not fear exactly, or not only fear. It was the feeling of opening a door you believed abandoned and finding the kettle still warm.

"You're awake too."

Mara stood at the schoolhouse door, wrapped in her sleeping bag like a reluctant caterpillar. She saw his face and came down the steps without another word.

He showed her the prints. She crouched over the freshest one, touched the crisp edge of it, and looked up at him with an expression he was beginning to know well — the look of a woman recalculating everything.

"How many?"

"At least two. Maybe more, over weeks. Months." He swept the flashlight across the converging tracks. "They live up there." He pointed at the dark mass of the mountain, where the old mine workings had been driven into the slope above the flood line. "And they come down here every day, and they walk around, and they go back up."

"Why would anyone—"

"I don't know. Warden. Hermit. Ghost with excellent boots." He rubbed his face. "There's an adit up there. The main entrance to the San Esteban workings. If I remember Rennick's old maps right — the real ones, the ones he drew before—" He stopped. He always stopped there. "Before. It should be maybe an hour's climb."

"Then we should see it."

"In the morning."

"In the morning," she agreed, but neither of them moved, both looking up at the mountain where the dark was deepest, as if it might blink first.

* * *

Morning came gray and cold, and the climb took longer than an hour because the mountain did not believe in switchbacks and Ines refused to argue with scree. They gained the bench of solid rock below the old workings by mid-morning, breath smoking, and there it was.

The adit.

Even ruined, even sealed, it had presence — a black rectangle cut into the living rock, framed in rotting timber, big enough to drive a cart through, because men had once driven carts through it, loaded with ore and ambition. Sixty years of weather had silvered the timbers and draped the portal in hanging moss, so that it looked less like a mine entrance than like the mouth of something that had learned patience.

Except.

"Mara," Ines said. "Look at the door."

Someone had sealed the adit. Not with rockfall, not with the casual masonry of decades. With steel plate — a sheet of it fitted across the entire portal, bolted into the rock through drilled anchors. And the bolts were new. Ines put his hand on one and the galvanizing was bright, unweathered, the nut seated tight, threads greased. Modern. Recent. Someone had measured this opening, fabricated plate to fit it, hauled the steel up a thousand meters of broken mountain, and bolted the past shut with hardware-store precision.

Six bolts across, four high. Twenty-four reasons to believe somebody cared very much about what stayed inside.

"They sealed it from the outside," Mara said slowly. "So nothing gets out."

"Or so nothing gets in." Ines stepped back and studied the whole face of the cliff, and then he saw the rest of it, and his stomach performed a slow, cold roll. "Mara. Look at the ground."

The bench before the adit was packed earth and rock, and it was printed with boot tracks the way a kitchen floor is printed after a long day. Heavy lug soles, dozens of overlapping passages, some fresh enough that the edges hadn't begun to crumble. Paths led away from the adit — down toward the town, along the bench, to a flat spot where a tent had clearly stood, its footprint ringed with stones, a fire scar at its center still holding ash that looked no more than a week old.

And every path ended at the steel door.

None went in — of course not; the door was bolted. But none went down the mountain either. None continued along the bench toward the high country. The tracks simply accumulated, a census taken in mud, of a life lived entirely within fifty meters of a sealed hole in the rock.

"He comes down to the town," Ines said, hearing his own voice come out low, "and he walks the streets, and he climbs back up, and he sits in front of this door. Every day. For God knows how long." He turned in a slow circle. "There's a camp. There's a fire. There's food waste somewhere, there's—"

He stopped.

Because he'd seen it now, tucked against the cliff wall twenty meters east of the adit: a structure. Not a tent — a hut, low and long, built of salvaged timber and sheet metal and what looked suspiciously like church roofing, camouflaged with cut brush, so well matched to the cliff that he'd walked past it twice without seeing it. Smoke-stained vent pipe. A door with a latch.

And from inside it, faint but unmistakable, carried on the still morning air:

A voice. Muffled. Repeating.

Not words he could make out — a rhythm of speech, rising and falling, pausing, beginning again. The same cadence, over and over, like a recording. Like a message. Like a man reading coordinates aloud to a radio that might, someday, answer.

Mara had gone rigid beside him. "Ines."

"I hear it."

"That's—" Her hand found his sleeve. "That's my father's cadence. When he reads numbers. He reads them twice, always twice, he says numbers want to be heard twice—"

The voice paused. Began again. Same rhythm. Twice.

Behind them, down in the green silence of the drowned town, a single bird called three notes — and this time, far up the slope, something answered: the soft, deliberate click of a rifle bolt, drawn back and let forward, once, the way a man clears his throat before saying something he has rehearsed for twenty years.
