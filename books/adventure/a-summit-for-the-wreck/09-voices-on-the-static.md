## 9. Voices on the Static

The radio had been sitting in Tomás's pack for three days, and for three days it had done nothing but sulk. That was how Ines thought of it—the old Motorola had a personality like a mule with opinions, all crackle and grievance, and he'd stopped expecting anything from it somewhere around the second gorge. So when it woke him at two in the morning, he didn't understand at first what he was hearing.

It wasn't static. It was static *shaped*—rhythmic, patient, a hiss that rose and fell like breathing. Then, beneath it, a voice.

Ines sat up in the dark. The camp was a shallow overhang above the river, the fire down to a red memory of coals. Mara was asleep on her side with her back to the cold, one hand curled near her chin like a child's. Tomás had first watch, which meant Tomás was forty meters away pretending to watch while he smoked the last of a cigarette he'd been rationing since Tuesday.

"Tomás," Ines whispered.

"I hear it." The cigarette ember moved in the dark. "Been going ten minutes."

The voice came through again, thin as paper, warped by distance and interference, but unmistakable. A man's voice. Measured. Tired in the way of someone who has been tired for a very long time and has made peace with it.

*"...bearing zero-four-seven from the split boulder... follow the water to where it goes quiet..."*

Ines knew that voice. He'd heard it once, nine days ago, through this same radio, when Emil Voss's last transmission had come through in fragments before dissolving into nothing. He'd told Mara afterward that he recognized it, and she had looked at him with an expression he hadn't been able to read then and could read too well now: hope, held together with wire.

He woke her gently. She came up out of sleep the way she did everything—completely, instantly, no transition—and was crouched by the radio before he finished saying her name.

"That's him," she said.

"It might be a recording."

"It's *him*." She said it the way you say a thing you've decided not to be argued out of. Her hand hovered over the radio without touching it, the way you hover over something hot or holy. "Listen. He repeats the coordinates every ninety seconds. Exactly ninety seconds. Papa never did anything exactly. He was a man who estimated."

She was right about that. Ines had met Emil Voss exactly once, years ago, across a table in Puerto Ceniza, and the man had ordered coffee by saying "something dark, whatever's closest." A man who estimated. This voice on the loop was precise as a metronome.

Which was, Ines thought, either very good news or very bad news, and he genuinely could not tell which.

* * *

They played it eleven more times before dawn. Mara transcribed the coordinates onto the back of a supply list, her handwriting getting steadier with each repetition, as if the act of writing her father's voice down were itself a kind of rescue. Tomás listened with his arms crossed and his face doing something complicated.

"Bait," Tomás said finally.

"You don't know that," Mara said.

"No. I don't know anything. That's my whole point." He gestured at the radio with the dead cigarette. "Somebody stole our fuel four nights ago and left us flagging tape. Polite thieves. Now a dead man's voice is giving us directions up a closed valley, on a frequency nobody uses, repeating like a lighthouse. I'm just saying, in my experience, when the wilderness starts being *helpful*, somebody's helping it."

Ines turned the radio over in his hands. It was a good argument. It was, in fact, almost exactly the argument he would have made himself, and the fact that it came from Tomás—a man who until recently had been reporting their position to debt collectors for money—made it no less true and somehow much harder to hold.

"There are three possibilities," Ines said. He'd found, over the years, that numbering things calmed him, even when the numbers were lies. "One: Emil Voss is alive, injured, and broadcasting on a loop because he can't stay awake to key the mic. Two: someone else has the radio and is using his voice to draw us somewhere. Three: it's atmospheric bounce off some other transmission entirely, and we're chasing a ghost made of weather."

"And which do you believe?" Mara asked.

Ines looked at the fire. "I believe the voice is real. Whether the man is—that's the part I can't verify from here."

"But you'll go," she said. Not a question. She'd already seen it in him, and he hated how legible he'd become on this river, how every doubt he had showed on his face like weather coming over a ridge.

"I'll go," he said. "Because if there's even a chance it's possibility one, and we sit here debating, then we're the ones who killed him. And if it's bait—" he shrugged "—then whoever set it knows we're coming anyway. We haven't exactly been subtle. Tomás, you portage like a wounded ox."

"Thank you," Tomás said.

"Don't mention it."

Mara folded the coordinates into her jacket, over her heart, which Ines thought was sentimental and also exactly what he would have done.

* * *

The gorge began an hour upstream of camp, where the river narrowed between walls of gray stone and the forest leaned in overhead until the sky was a rumor. Fog lived there. Not the ordinary fog of mornings that burns off by nine, but a standing fog, settled into the gorge like water into a bowl, and the temperature dropped as they entered it the way temperature drops when a door opens onto winter.

Not gradually. All at once. Ten degrees, maybe twelve, between one step and the next, so that Ines's breath went from invisible to streaming and the sweat on his back turned to a cold sheet. Tomás swore softly behind him. Mara pulled her collar up and said nothing, but Ines heard her breathing change—the short, controlled rhythm of someone refusing to be afraid.

"Geography," Tomás offered, because Tomás always needed a reason for things. "Cold air drains downhill at night. Pools in low ground. It's a thing."

"It's noon," Ines said.

"So it's enthusiastic geography."

The fog thickened until sound went strange—close sounds too close, distant sounds impossibly far. Their own footsteps arrived late, as if the gorge were considering them before permitting them forward. The river ran beneath them somewhere, audible but unseen, and its voice had changed too: slower, deeper, the water moving with the unhurried confidence of something that had been going where it was going for a very long time.

Ines navigated by bearing and by feel, counting paces, keeping the split boulder on their left shoulder when they reached it—an enormous glacial erratic split clean down the middle, furred with moss, looking like something that had been arguing with itself for ten thousand years. The coordinates checked. Whatever else was happening, the numbers were real numbers, tied to real stone.

That was the thing that kept the dread sharpening instead of dulling. If it were nonsense, he could dismiss it. But the loop pointed at actual places, actual features he could put his hands on, which meant someone who knew this gorge intimately had built it—which meant intent, which meant a mind, and a mind was always worse than a ghost.

"Papa came through here," Mara said quietly. They were walking single file, so she spoke toward Ines's back, and the fog carried her words straight into his ear as if she'd leaned against his shoulder. "His letter mentioned a place where the water goes quiet. I thought it was poetry. Papa wasn't poetic."

Ahead, the river's voice faded. Faded further. And then, between one step and the next, stopped altogether—not because the river ended, but because it slid beneath the stone, plunging underground through a slot no wider than a coffin, leaving a dry channel of smooth rock running on ahead into the white.

Where the water goes quiet.

They stood at the lip of it, three people in a fog that shouldn't exist at noon, listening to silence so complete it had texture. Ines felt the compulsion settle over him fully then—not hope, not quite, and not fear either, but the third thing underneath both: the certainty that the answer was uphill, and that he had been walking toward it since Puerto Ceniza, since the blacklisting, maybe since Rennick first put a compass in his hand and taught him that a map was a promise.

"Coordinates end here," Mara said, checking her transcription. "But the bearing continues. Zero-four-seven. Uphill."

Tomás looked at the dry channel, then at the fog behind them, then at Ines. "For the record," he said, "I think this is a terrible idea."

"Noted for the record," Ines said, and started uphill.

Behind him, faint through the fog, so faint he could almost believe he'd imagined it—the radio, still in Tomás's pack, crackled once. And in the crackle, for half a second, there was a second voice under Emil Voss's loop. Older. Rougher. Saying something too broken to catch.

A voice Ines had known his whole life.

Aldous Rennick's voice, saying what sounded terribly, impossibly, like his name.
