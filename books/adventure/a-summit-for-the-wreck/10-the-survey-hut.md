## 10. The Survey Hut

The voice in the static did not come again.

Ines stood in the fog with his head tilted like a dog listening to a whistle only it could hear, and after a full minute Tomás said, gently, the way you talk to a man standing on a ledge, "Ines. It's a radio. Radios do that."

"Radios don't say my name."

"Radios say everything," Tomás said. "That's the whole problem with radios. My mother bought one to hear the news and by Christmas she was convinced the government was speaking to her personally through the weather report." He adjusted his pack and started up the dry channel. "Come on. If a dead man wants you, he can wait until we're somewhere with walls."

It was the most sensible thing anyone had said in three days, so Ines followed.

The channel climbed between walls of gray stone furred with moss, and the fog came down with them, thickening until Ines could see perhaps ten meters in any direction and Mara, walking behind him, had become mostly a sound of boots and breathing. The cold arrived without announcement. One moment the air was merely damp; the next it had teeth. Ines checked his thermometer out of habit and watched the needle sit at four degrees and then, over the course of an hour, slide to one, though nothing about the morning explained it. No wind. No altitude they hadn't already earned. Just a valley exhaling winter from somewhere it had no business keeping it.

"The mine," Mara said quietly, when he mentioned it. "My father's letter said the deep workings never warmed. Even in summer. The men who worked them wore coats in July."

"You've read the letter a lot," Ines said.

"I've read it until the words stopped meaning anything," she said. "Then I read it some more."

He understood that better than he wanted to. He'd done the same thing with Rennick's route maps for eleven years—spread them on whatever table he had, in whatever town would forget him fast enough, and traced the lines until the lines were more familiar than his own signature. Looking for the error. There had to be an error; there always was, in every disaster, some small human mistake a man could point to and say *there, that's where I went wrong*, because the alternative—that the map had been wrong on purpose, that the world contained people who would falsify a route knowing what a falsified route cost—was not an alternative a guide could live inside and keep working.

Except he hadn't kept working. That was the joke of it.

The channel opened without warning into a shelf of flat ground, and Ines nearly walked into the hut.

* * *

It shouldn't have been there. That was his first thought, and his second, and honestly his third: *this should not be here.* A low structure of stone and salvaged timber, roofed with corrugated iron gone the brown of old blood, set back against the cliff wall as if the cliff had grown it. No trail led to it. No smoke rose from it. But the door was new—newer than everything else about the building—and it hung on hinges that had been oiled within living memory, and there was a hasp, and the hasp was locked.

Ines put his hand flat against the door and felt, faintly, warmth.

"Someone's inside," Mara whispered.

"Someone was," Ines said. "Recently." He crouched. The threshold was swept. Actually swept—a clean arc in the grit, a broom's width wide. Beside the door, two stones held down a stack of split firewood covered by a sheet of plastic, tucked with the neatness of a housewife and the paranoia of a soldier. He looked at Tomás. Tomás looked at the lock, then at the treeline, then at Ines, and said nothing, which from Tomás was a paragraph.

"We could go around," Tomás offered. "There's a lot of valley."

"There's exactly one valley," Ines said, "and everything in it points here." He stood. "Mara. Stay behind me. Tomás—" he hesitated, and hated that he hesitated, and said it anyway, "watch our backs."

Tomás's face did something complicated. Since the falls, since the confession Ines still carried around like a stone in his boot, *watch our backs* meant something different than it used to. But Tomás unslung his pack, took out the machete instead of anything louder, and moved to the edge of the clearing without being asked twice. Whatever else he was now, he was still the man who had once pulled Ines out of a crevasse by his collar with one arm and cursed at him the entire time.

The lock was a padlock, brass, cheap, the kind sold in every border-town market from here to the coast. Ines broke it with a rock in three strikes, wincing at each one as if the sound might summon whoever deserved it, and pushed the door open onto darkness and the smell of woodsmoke, paper, and paraffin.

Mara found the lamp first. A hurricane lamp sat on a shelf just inside, and beneath it, arranged with terrible courtesy, a box of matches and a note weighted under a carabiner: *Light it. Mind the drafts.* The handwriting was cramped, precise, an engineer's hand.

"That's my father's carabiner," Mara said. Her voice had gone very small. "That's—he marked all his gear with red tape. Look at the gate."

It was. Red electrical tape, half peeled, the way Emil Voss apparently marked everything he owned. Which meant either Emil had been here, alive, recently enough to leave a lamp lit for visitors—or someone who had Emil's gear had left it here the way you leave bread crumbs, or bait, or an apology.

Ines struck the match.

* * *

The light filled the hut and Ines forgot, for a moment, how to breathe.

One room. A bunk against the far wall, blankets folded with military corners. A stove, blackened, its pipe running up through the roof with a patch of solder around the joint—someone had repaired it, carefully, more than once. A table under the lamp's center of gravity, and covering the table, pinned at the corners with flat stones, a survey.

Not a copy. Not a reprint. Ines knew paper the way other men knew horses, and this paper was fifty years old at least, yellowed to the color of weak tea, brittle at the folds, stamped along the margin with the faded blue seal of the Compañía Minera del Alto Sombra. The 1961 original survey. The one that officially burned in the company offices the winter after the flood. The one whose destruction was the reason every map since—the maps Rennick drew, the maps Ines had learned on, taught from, trusted with clients' lives—was built on nothing but memory and intention.

And across it, in pencil, in a hand Ines would know blindfolded, drunk, dead—the narrow, impatient, beautiful hand that had written every birthday card Ines had received between the ages of nine and thirty-one—were corrections.

Aldous Rennick had annotated the original survey the way a surgeon annotates an X-ray. Marginal notes in that slanted script. Arrows. Small, furious underlinings. And at the center of the main sheet, circled twice and boxed, a shaft marked *No. 4 — AUXILIARY*, with Rennick's pencil driving through the word *auxiliary* and writing beside it:

**NOT auxiliary. Primary haulage. Floor of adit sits on fractured serpentine. Water table above. This will fail. Told them. Told them in writing. They moved my note to the appendix and renumbered the sections. — A.R., March 1961**

Below that, smaller, added later—the graphite darker, the pressure harder, as if the pencil had been gripped like a knife:

**They surveyed the access road anyway. Through the fault zone. Because it was shorter and shorter was cheaper. When it goes, it will take the town. I am drawing the official route map myself. I will draw it wrong. Let them hang the disaster on a bad map rather than a good mine.**

Ines read it three times. His eyes kept sliding off the words the way water slides off waxed canvas. Around him the hut went on existing—Mara's breath, quick and shallow; the stove ticking as it cooled; rain starting somewhere on the iron roof, a soft drumming—but he was elsewhere, he was twenty-six years old and standing in a hearing room in Puerto Ceniza while men in good jackets explained to him that the route he had guided, the route drawn by the celebrated Aldous Rennick and signed off by his protégé, had been fatally wrong, that the flood on the upper Sombra tributary that took three climbers had followed a canyon that no competent guide would have entered, and that Ines Halloran, protégé, had either not read his mentor's map or had not understood it, and either way—

Either way.

"He wrote it down," Ines heard himself say. His voice sounded borrowed. "He wrote it down before it happened. He knew the shaft would fail, he knew the road was surveyed through the fault zone, and he—" Ines touched the page, very lightly, the way you touch a sleeping animal. "He drew the wrong map on purpose. To protect the company. And then when it failed anyway, when the flood came, the wrong map was the perfect thing to blame. A bad map. A careless young guide. A tragedy, an accident, everyone shrugging, the mine closed by treaty, the records conveniently ash."

"Ines." Mara's hand was on his arm. He hadn't seen her move. "Read the bottom corner."

There was more. Of course there was more; Rennick never wrote one sentence when a paragraph would wound deeper. Pinned to the lower right of the main sheet, a separate leaf, older even than the rest, water-stained at one edge:

**To whoever finds this. If it is Halloran: I am sorry. I chose you to carry the blame because you were the only one good enough that they would believe you capable of the route, and the only one loyal enough not to fight it. Both things were true and both things were unforgivable. The map I gave you was false. The map I kept was true. I could not give you the true one without giving you everything, and I was a coward about everything. Burn this or publish it. I do not deserve to choose for you. — A.R.**

The hut was very quiet. Rain on the roof. Stove ticking. Somewhere outside, in the fog, a bird made one sound and thought better of it.

"I trained on his maps," Ines said. It came out level, almost conversational, which frightened him more than shouting would have. "Eleven years. I taught other people on his maps. I put clients on routes he drew wrong on purpose, and I got lucky, that's all, that's the whole career—luck, stacked on top of a lie, and when the luck ran out they fed me to the inquiry and he stood up—" Ines stopped. Swallowed. "He stood up at the hearing and said, *the boy read the map correctly.* Present tense. Like it was still my map to read. Like he was defending me. And everyone in that room heard a mentor protecting his student, and what he was actually doing was nailing the lid down."

Mara said nothing. What was there to say. She was looking at the survey with an expression Ines couldn't read yet—grief, yes, but underneath it something working, turning over, a woman doing arithmetic with her family's name.

Tomás appeared in the doorway, dripping fog, machete still in hand. He looked at the table, at their faces, and asked the only useful question left.

"Who sweeps the step?"

* * *

Because that was the thing. Ines made himself look away from the survey—physically turn his body, an act of will comparable to walking upstream—and look at the room again, properly, with a guide's eye instead of a mourner's.

The bunk was slept in. Not long ago. The blanket bore the long crease of a body. On the crate beside it: a tin cup, a spoon worn thin, a paperback novel swollen with damp and read so many times its spine had gone to white powder. On the shelf above the stove, rows of preserved food in glass jars—berries, some kind of root, fish—labeled in that same cramped engineer's hand with dates. The oldest date was fourteen months ago. The newest was nineteen days.

Nineteen days. Nine days after the expedition's radio went silent.

On a nail by the door hung a coat, oiled canvas, patched at both elbows. Too big for Emil Voss. Boots under the bunk, resoled twice with tire rubber. And on the table, beside the survey, a second object Ines had taken at first for a paperweight: a brass compass, old pattern, its case dented, its needle housing clouded with age.

Rennick's compass. The twin of the one sitting in Ines's own pocket at that moment, the one Rennick had pressed into his hands at seventeen and said, *a guide is only lost if he agrees to be.*

"He's been living here," Mara said slowly. "Whoever he is. For over a year. Before my father ever came." She turned a slow circle, taking in the jars, the swept step, the firewood stacked against weather that hadn't come yet. "He knew Emil was coming. Or he knew someone was coming. The lamp, the matches, the note—*mind the drafts*—that's not a trap. Traps don't tell you to mind the drafts."

"It's an invitation," Ines said. "From someone who doesn't expect guests to be friendly." He looked at the note again. *Mind the drafts.* You wrote that to someone you expected to handle themselves. Someone you'd assessed. Someone you'd decided could be trusted with fire.

Or someone you'd been watching for days and had already judged.

Tomás had drifted to the window, such as it was—a shuttered slit—and stood with his head cocked. "Ines," he said. "The fog's lifting off the shelf below us. And there's a path. Switchbacking up from the gorge. Cut steps, actually cut, with a hand line." He paused. "Fresh prints going up. And fresh prints coming down. Same boots."

"Coming down from where?"

"Higher," Tomás said. "Toward the glacier. Toward wherever your radio loop is broadcasting from." He turned around, and in the lamplight his face was doing something Ines had rarely seen it do, which was hoping. "Nine days silent. A loop of coordinates. A grave with an ice axe and no body. Drag marks uphill. Fuel stolen and flagging tape left in trade—polite, Ines, all of it polite, everything in this valley robs you and says thank you." He spread his hands. "What if nobody's dead? What if the answer to every horrible thing we've found is just—men, up there, too hurt or too stubborn to come down, managing us the only way they could manage us?"

Ines wanted to believe it. He examined the wanting the way he'd examine a rope before trusting it—frayed in places, but load-bearing. Emil Voss alive was possible. The evidence bent that way if you leaned on it. But the second voice in the static, the rough old voice that had said his name—

That voice belonged to a man who had died two winters ago in a coastal town, alone, of a heart that had finally presented its resignation letter. Ines had paid for the funeral. He had been one of six people at it.

"Aldous," he said aloud, without deciding to, and Mara looked at him sharply.

"What about Aldous?"

"Nothing." He folded Rennick's confession leaf along its old crease and slid it inside his jacket, against his chest, where it lay like a coal. The rest of the survey he rolled with the care owed to scripture. "We go up. Tonight if the weather holds, dawn if it doesn't. Somebody cut those steps, somebody stocked this hut, somebody left a lamp burning for us, and I'm done being managed by ghosts." He looked at the door, at the fog beyond it, at the invisible heights where a looped voice was saying coordinates into the dark.

Outside, as if in reply, the radio in Tomás's pack crackled to life on its own—not the loop this time, not Emil's flat recitation, but the other voice, the rough one, closer now or stronger, threading through the static with three words before the band swallowed it whole.

Three words Ines had heard a thousand times across a thousand camps, in a voice he had buried with his own hands.

*Check your bearing.*

Tomás stared at the pack. Mara's hand found Ines's sleeve.

"That's not a recording," Tomás whispered. "Recordings don't know when you're about to leave."

Ines picked up the lamp, blew it out, and stepped into the fog.
