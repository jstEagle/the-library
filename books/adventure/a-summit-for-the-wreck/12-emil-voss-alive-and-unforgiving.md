## 12. Emil Voss, Alive and Unforgiving

Mara had rehearsed this moment for nine days.

She had rehearsed it in the boat, shouting over the static; she had rehearsed it on the portage trail, breath ragged under the weight of a pack that was mostly rope and regret; she had rehearsed it lying awake in the survey hut while Brandt's kettle ticked itself cold on the stove. In every version, her father looked up when she came through the door and said something dry and impossible—*You're late, mija, the tea's gone bitter*—and she would say something equally dry back, and then they would embrace, and everything after would be simple.

None of the versions had prepared her for the smell of the high camp: woodsmoke and wet wool and something medicinal, camphor or pine tar, sharp enough to sting the eyes. None of them had prepared her for how small he would look.

Emil Voss lay propped against a wall of stone and canvas in a shelter built half from a collapsed prospecting cabin and half from expedition tarping lashed with survey cord. His right leg was splinted from ankle to hip with aluminum poles and strips of flagging tape—the same orange tape she'd found traded for their fuel, and understanding arrived sideways, unwelcome: *he* had been managing them up the valley. He'd been rationing strangers like sugar. Her father, who could not walk, had been running an expedition of one.

He was thinner than she remembered. That was the thing that broke her first—not the leg, not the gray in his beard, but the wrists. Her father had always had wrists like dock rope. Now they looked like something a strong wind could argue with.

"Mara." He said it the way he said coordinates. Precise. As if confirming her position before allowing her closer.

"Papá."

"You're standing crooked," he said. "Left shoulder down. You've been carrying too much on one side. Give the pack to Halloran."

And there it was—the voice out of every kitchen of her childhood, out of every argument about her career, out of every letter that had ever ended *be sensible*. Nine days of imagining him dead, and the first thing he did was critique her posture.

She dropped the pack where she stood. It hit the stone floor with a sound like a verdict. Then she crossed the shelter in four steps and knelt and put her arms around him as carefully as if he were made of the same ice the river came from, and he held her back with those ruined wrists, and for a long time neither of them said anything at all, because some reunions are conducted entirely in grip strength.

Behind her, she heard Ines set his own pack down quietly. Heard Brandt say, low, "I'll see to the stove." Heard Tomás—Tomás, who had come back up through the fog with his confession still hanging off him like wet clothes—mutter something about checking the perimeter, which was Tomás for *this is family business and I have been in enough families to know when to vanish.*

When she finally pulled back, Emil was studying her face with the expression he used on suspect data.

"You've been crying," he said.

"No."

"Liar. You cry exactly the way your mother cried, which is to say you deny it first." He touched her cheek with two fingers, and his hand was steady, and that steadiness frightened her more than the leg had. "You shouldn't have come. I told Brandt to send you home."

"He didn't send me anywhere. He sent a radio loop of your voice into a haunted gorge."

"That was my idea too," Emil admitted. "The radio part. The gorge was geography's fault." A pause. "Did it work?"

"It nearly killed us."

"Nearly," he repeated, and something in his face eased by a single degree, which from Emil Voss was a standing ovation. "Then it worked."

* * *

They made him tell it properly, because Ines insisted—Ines, who had barely spoken since the hut, who kept looking at Emil the way a man looks at a locked door he suspects holds his own name behind it. So Emil told it, propped up on his bad leg with a tin cup of Brandt's terrible tea balanced on his chest, in the flat competent voice he used for field reports.

The fall, first. A cornice above the moraine, three weeks ago now, giving way the way cornices do—without negotiation. He'd gone forty meters down a rock chute. Brandt had splinted the leg with survey poles and flagging tape and had wanted to carry him down; Emil had refused, because a man being carried down the Sombra gorge was a man being carried to his funeral, and Emil Voss had never once in his life retreated at less than full speed.

"So we stayed," Emil said. "And since I could not walk, I worked. There was nothing else to do. There is never anything else to do."

"The survey," Ines said. Not a question. Mara watched him say the word the way other men said a woman's name they weren't supposed to love anymore.

"The survey." Emil reached beside him and lifted a oilcloth-wrapped bundle onto his lap, and even wrapped, even in this firelight, it seemed to change the temperature of the room. "Three weeks, Halloran. Every page. Every line. I checked Rennick's figures against my grandfather's field books—I'll come to my grandfather—and against the mine's own shaft logs, which Brandt pulled from the adit before they bolted it. Do you know what I found?"

"I can guess," Ines said quietly.

"Guessing is for journalists." Emil unwrapped one corner of the bundle, just one, like a man showing a card. "I found that Aldous Rennick drew the official route map himself. Not copied—drew. And I found that the version submitted to the company in March of 1961 differs from his own original field survey by exactly one feature." He let the corner fall closed. "The east shaft. The unstable one. On Rennick's field copy it is marked *condemned, do not work.* On the company map, that annotation is gone, and the shaft appears sound, and the haulage road runs directly past it. Three months later the east shaft let go, the tailings dam went with it, and the town drowned in eleven minutes."

Mara heard the words. She understood each of them individually. What she could not do, sitting there with her father's hand still resting on the oilcloth, was make them assemble into anything that included her.

"Why?" she asked. It came out smaller than she intended. "Why would he falsify it? He was your teacher. He was—" She stopped, because the sentence she was about to finish was *he was practically family*, and she suddenly, horribly, saw where the sentence led.

"Because the company told him to," Emil said. "Or paid him to. Or both. And because—" and here, for the first time, her father's voice lost its surveyor's flatness, and she heard underneath it something old and tired and furious, "—because the man who owned the mine asked him to. Personally. Over dinner. And the man who owned the mine was my father's father. Was *your* great-grandfather, Mara. Augustin Voss."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the glacier groaned—that long submarine complaint of ice under strain that she had been hearing for days without hearing it.

"No," Mara said.

"His name is in the margin of the company map. Initials, approving the revision. A.V. I have spent three weeks hoping I was wrong. I am not wrong. I have never once in my life been wrong about a number, mija, you may ask anyone, it is my single most annoying quality." He tried for humor and missed by a meter. "Augustin signed off on the falsified route. When the dam failed, he helped bury the cause—bought the inquiry, bought the witnesses, bought the silence. And when the questions got close, he bought a scapegoat too. A young guide who had carried the original field notes down the mountain and noticed they didn't match."

Mara turned her head slowly and looked at Ines.

Ines was looking at the stove. He had the particular stillness of a man who has known a thing for twenty years and has just heard it confirmed by a stranger in a freezing hut, and discovered that confirmation does not feel like victory. It feels like weather.

"They blamed you," she said. "For the disaster. For the deaths. They took your certification and they—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "You were *twenty-four*."

"Twenty-three," Ines said. "But who's counting."

It was meant as a joke. It landed like a wound dressed in a joke's clothing, and Mara felt it go in, because she understood suddenly the arithmetic of her own life: the Voss money that had paid for her schooling, her apartment in the capital, her mother's clinic bills, all of it flowing down from a grandfather who had drowned a town and framed a boy to keep the river quiet. She had grown up inside the lie the way fish grow up inside water. She had never once thought to call it water.

"And the inheritance," she said. Her mouth was dry. "The estate. The trust. All of it—"

"Is built on the lie," Emil said. "Yes. Every peso of it. I'm sorry, Mara. I didn't know either, until I started checking. I came up this river thinking I was recovering our family's honor." He laughed, short and awful. "Instead I found its invoice."

* * *

Later—much later, when Brandt had added wood to the stove and Tomás had returned smelling of snow and had silently handed everyone strips of dried meat like a man paying rent—Mara sat beside her father's splinted leg and asked the question she had carried up the whole river in her chest like a stone.

"The letter. Your last letter. You wrote that you'd found 'Rennick's lie.' Why didn't you tell me what it was?"

Emil was quiet for a while. Outside, the light was failing into that blue hour when the glacier stopped pretending to be scenery and became what it actually was: a reservoir of patient violence.

"Because I was afraid," he said finally. "Of what you'd do with it."

"What I'd do?"

"Your mother's people were careful people, Mara. Mine were careful too—we were careful the way owners are careful, which is a different animal entirely. I thought: if I tell her the truth and I don't come back, she will spend her life fighting lawyers, and the lawyers are paid out of the trust, and the trust is paid out of the lie, so she will be fighting the lie with the lie's own money, and the lie always wins that fight. I thought I'd come back myself, with proof, and we'd decide together." He looked at his hands. "Then the cornice decided for me."

"So you sent a stranger up the river instead."

"I sent the only guide alive who had walked Rennick's routes and survived the aftermath," Emil corrected. "Halloran knew the maps were wrong before anyone else did. That's why they destroyed him—he wasn't a scapegoat because he was guilty. He was a scapegoat because he was *right*, and right men ask questions." Emil's eyes went across the fire to Ines, and something passed between the two old men that Mara could not read and did not try to. "Though I notice he brought you anyway. Against my explicit instruction. Which suggests he's either disobedient or he understands something about daughters that I taught him myself."

"He's disobedient," Mara said.

"Good," said Emil. "We'll need it."

She should have asked then what he meant. She was tired, and her father was alive, and grief and relief had left no room in her for anything else. Instead she leaned her head against the stone wall beside him and let herself, for ten minutes, simply be a daughter whose father was breathing.

It was Ines who broke the stillness. He had been turning one of the survey pages in the firelight—Brandt had grudgingly allowed a single sheet loose from the oilcloth—and now he held it up, frowning.

"Voss. This verification note of yours. Dated three days ago." He tapped the margin. "You dated it with the mine's old coordinate system. Pre-1961 datum."

"Of course. The originals are in the old datum. Any cross-check has to—"

"No, I mean—" Ines was on his feet now, and Mara saw the change come over him, the shift from tired man to guide, that total alertness she had watched grow on him mile by mile up the river. "Where did you get pre-1961 datum tables? Those were purged from every registry after the closure. They exist in exactly two places. One is the sealed archive in the capital."

Emil smiled. It was not a comforting smile.

"And the other," he said, "is the mine office. Which Brandt opened for me in the second week, when I needed the shaft logs. The bolts on that adit are modern, Halloran, but the lock was older than I am, and the man who installed it assumed no one would ever come who knew the old system." He settled back against the wall, insufferably pleased with himself. "There is a complete duplicate of the 1961 survey inside that mine. Signed. Witnessed. With Augustin's initials on the falsification and Rennick's original annotations beneath. Physical paper. Fireproof cabinet. I verified its existence personally, on one leg, in the dark, which I mention purely so that someone eventually appreciates it."

Silence. Mara watched Ines absorb it, watched Brandt go very still by the stove, and understood a half-second before anyone said it aloud why the air in the shelter had changed.

"There's a second copy," Brandt said flatly. "Inside the mine. Inside the valley. Where anyone who wants this story dead doesn't have to chase us down the river—they only have to burn one building."

"They'd need to reopen the adit," Ines said.

"The bolts are modern," Brandt said. "Modern bolts come out."

Outside, far down the valley toward the gorge, the sound came again—the one that was not quite thunder, the one Brandt had flinched at last night. Closer now. Rhythmic. Patient.

Mara knew engines. She had grown up around her father's expeditions, around helicopters lifting loads onto ridgelines, and there was no mistaking that beat for weather, not twice.

Her father heard it too. He did not flinch. He reached over and gripped her hand with those ruined wrists, with surprising strength, and said, in the tone he used for ordering porters through a storm:

"Well. It seems the family business has come to collect."
