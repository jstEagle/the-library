## 13. Three Versions of One Night

The storm came down off the ridge an hour after dark and pinned them all inside the survey hut like specimens in a case.

It was Brandt's idea, or his insistence, that they spend the night there rather than push for the high camp in the whiteout. "Nobody climbs in this," he said, feeding the little iron stove with split planks he'd clearly been hoarding for years. "Not even your father, Mara. Especially not your father."

So they waited out the wind together — Ines, Mara, Tomás, Brandt — in a room no bigger than a chapel, its walls papered with the 1961 survey, and the storm did what storms do in closed valleys: it made confession feel inevitable.

It was Tomás who started it, because Tomás could not bear silence longer than a pot takes to boil.

"So," he said, hunched over his mug of melted snow with the wariness of a man approaching a dog of unknown temperament, "the night Rennick burned everything. I've heard Ines's version maybe forty times. Usually at two in the morning, usually after bad rum." He glanced at Ines, then at Brandt, with the tactlessness of long friendship. "I'd like to hear it from someone who wasn't drunk."

"I wasn't drunk," Ines said.

"You were weeping into a river."

"That's different."

* * *

**INES**

Here is what Ines Halloran remembers, and he has had twenty years to polish every edge of it, which means he trusts it less now than he did at twenty-four:

The night Rennick burned the evidence was the third of November, and it rained in Puerto Ceniza the way it only rains in river towns — sideways, personal, as though the sky held a grudge against roofs specifically.

Aldous Rennick came to Ines's room above the chandlery at eleven o'clock, soaked through, carrying a canvas map case under one arm and a bottle of aguardiente in the other hand, and he said, "Boy, I need you to hold something for me."

Ines was twenty-four. He worshipped the man. Rennick had taught him to read water, to read weather, to read the particular silence a mountain makes before it decides to kill you. When Rennick said *hold something*, Ines took it the way you take a christening.

"It's the '61 survey," Rennick said. "Originals. Don't open it."

"Why me?"

"Because everyone watches me and nobody watches you." He smiled when he said it, and the smile was the thing Ines would turn over for two decades afterward, examining it from every angle like a stone with a fossil in it. "That's a compliment."

Rennick stayed for one drink. He talked about the old days, about the mine, about a man named Ossler who'd run the hoist and sang off-key. He laughed twice. Then he left, and Ines put the map case under his floorboards and went to sleep like an idiot, like a loyal dog, like a son.

At four in the morning the knocking started. Two men from the mining board, one policeman, and behind them, in the rain, Aldous Rennick, dry under an umbrella, not meeting his eyes.

They found the survey under Ines's floorboards in nine minutes. They found, folded inside it, a page in Ines's own handwriting — a route note he'd written weeks earlier, innocently, for a training exercise — altered just enough. Just enough to suggest he'd been planning to sell the valley's access routes. Just enough to make him the man who'd stolen the records before they could be audited.

"Tell them," Ines said to Rennick, in the doorway, in the rain. "Tell them I was holding it for you."

And Rennick — this is the part Ines has polished smoothest, the part he can recite like scripture and hate like scripture — Rennick looked at him with something that was almost tenderness and said, "Son, I don't know what you're talking about."

Then he walked home under his umbrella, and the next spring he died in his sleep, and Ines spent twenty years being the guide who falsified maps, the guide whose error killed eleven miners when the flood came, the guide no expedition would touch in two countries.

"Every word true?" Brandt asked, when Ines finished. The stove ticked. Wind leaned on the hut like a drunk on a door.

"Every word," Ines said. "Which is the problem. It's all true and none of it explains anything. Why me? Why the page in my handwriting? Why did he give it to me at all if he meant to burn it?"

"He didn't mean to burn it," Brandt said quietly. "I know because I watched him try."

* * *

**BRANDT**

Silas Brandt told his version sitting with his back to the wall, facing the door, the way he evidently did everything, including sleep, including grieve.

"In '61 I was nineteen," he began. "I worked the assay office. My father ran the lower adit. My wife—" He stopped. Recalibrated, the way a man recalibrates an instrument he suspects is broken. "Elena. She was nineteen too. We'd been married four months."

Mara made a small sound. Brandt didn't look at her. Looking at people, Ines was learning, cost Brandt something, and he paid it only when he had to.

"The night of the flood, the upper shaft had been seeping for three days. Everyone knew. The company knew. Your grandfather knew, Miss Voss — he owned a third of it. The survey crew went up to document the seepage because the engineers wanted it on record before they sealed the shaft, and the man leading that crew was Aldous Rennick, thirty-one years old, the best route-mapper on this side of the cordillera."

Brandt fed the fire. His hands were steady; his voice wasn't, quite.

"What nobody outside the survey team knew was that Rennick found more than seepage. He found that the official route map — the one showing the safe haulage tunnel, the one every man in that mine had memorized — was wrong. Drawn wrong. Deliberately. The stable tunnel was marked where the unstable shaft actually was, and the shaft was marked where the tunnel ran. Somebody had swapped them years earlier to hide how far they'd cut into the aquifer. Every escape route on that map led into the flood path."

"And Rennick drew the corrected version," Ines said. "The one in my floorboards."

"He drew it that night. By lamplight. I watched him do it — I carried his lamp. He said, *When this is on record, they'll have to close her down, Silas. Eleven hundred men.* He believed paper could stop a mine." Brandt almost smiled. "He was young."

"But the flood came first."

"The flood came first. The aquifer let go at 2:14 in the morning. The corrected map was still wet with ink. Rennick ran it down the mountain through the flood itself — I went with him until the water took the road away. We lost the case in the torrent. Or he told me we did." Brandt's jaw worked. "Forty years later I find out he didn't lose it. He kept it. And then he sat on it for eighteen years while the company rebuilt, while the town rebuilt, while men went back underground on the same lying map — until 1979, when the board finally ordered an audit of the old surveys, and Rennick panicked, and decided the evidence had to disappear."

"And chose me to disappear it with him," Ines said.

"He chose you because you were the only person alive who'd never seen the original survey. You couldn't describe it. You couldn't testify to what was in it. You were the perfect blind courier." Brandt finally turned and looked at him, and his eyes were red-rimmed and terrible and honest. "And then the audit leaked early, and he needed a thief faster than he needed a courier, and you were already holding the case. I'm sorry. I've been sorry since the night I read the tribunal transcript and recognized his handwriting in the margins of your ruin."

Tomás set his mug down very carefully, as though the room might otherwise tip.

"So the man who taught you everything," Tomás said slowly, "used you as a firebreak."

"Yes," said Brandt.

"No," said Ines, at the same moment, and both of them looked at him.

* * *

**EMIL**

Emil Voss's version arrived the next afternoon, when the storm broke and they climbed through knee-deep snow to the high camp, and found him propped against a rock wall with his shattered leg splinted in a lattice of ski poles and survey stakes, three weeks of beard, eyes like a man who has been arguing with God and winning on points.

He heard their news. He heard about the hut, the survey, Brandt's surrender of it. He heard Ines's account and Brandt's account, delivered side by side like two witnesses who'd rehearsed without meaning to.

Then Emil laughed. It was a shocking sound, up there at the top of the world — rusty, wheezing, genuine.

"You're both right," he said. "That's the hell of it. I've spent six weeks in this hole verifying every line of that survey, cross-checking it against the company ledger my father left me, and I can tell you exactly what happened on both those nights, and I can tell you that neither of you knows the half, because neither of you knew my father."

Mara knelt in the snow beside him. "Papa—"

"Hush. Let an old man perform." He shifted against the rock, wincing, settling in like a storyteller by a fire, which, Ines thought, was precisely what he was. "My father, Konrad Voss, owned a third of the Sombra mine. He also, in the winter of 1959, personally approved the swap of the tunnel markings on the official route map. Not because he was evil. Because the engineers told him the aquifer risk was *probably* nothing, and closing the mine for re-survey would bankrupt him before spring. He signed the falsification order. He told himself it was temporary."

Emil's breath smoked. Somewhere below, the river ground its teeth under the ice.

"Now. Here is what your two versions miss. Rennick didn't discover the falsification by accident in '61. My father confessed it to him. Confessed — got drunk, wept, dragged the boy up the mountain, showed him the original drawings, and begged him to fix it quietly. Thought Rennick owed him loyalty. Thought a corrected map, filed properly, would absolve them both." Emil shook his head. "My father understood money and understood fear and understood nothing else on this earth. Rennick drew the correction intending to publish it. My father intended to bury it. And between those two intentions, the aquifer moved on its own schedule."

"And the night of the burning," Mara said. Her voice was very small. "1979."

"Ah." Emil looked at her, and something in his face gentled and hardened at once. "The night of the burning, my father sent for Rennick. I know because I drove him to the meeting — I was thirty, I was already doing his dirty driving. He offered Rennick a pension, a house, silence. Rennick refused. So my father gave him a choice: burn the survey and blame someone expendable, or watch the Voss family lawyers destroy the audit, destroy the surviving flood families' claims, and destroy Rennick's name along with them. My father had already chosen the scapegoat. He'd chosen the young apprentice Rennick doted on. The one whose handwriting he had samples of. The one who'd trust him with anything."

The wind moved through the rocks above the camp with a sound like a long exhalation.

"He told me afterward," Emil said. "Years later, dying, full of morphine, proud of himself in some rotten corner of his soul. He said, *The boy took the fall so cleanly. Rennick wept, but the boy — the boy never even fought.*" Emil's hands, scarred and frost-nipped, lay open on the blanket. "I have spent my life trying to outrun that sentence. This expedition was me trying to buy back our name with the truth. Instead I found out the truth costs more than the lie ever did."

Mara was crying without noise, the way she seemed to do everything important.

"Papa. The inheritance. The company. All of it—"

"Is built on eleven graves and one ruined guide," Emil said. He reached out and took Ines's wrist — a strong grip, for a man with a shattered leg, a grip like a man taking responsibility by the bone. "Halloran. Rennick wept when he did it. That doesn't forgive him. But understand what you're carrying: he didn't sacrifice you because you meant nothing to him. He sacrificed you because you were the only thing he loved enough for my father to use as a lever. That's not innocence. But it isn't the story you've been telling yourself in the dark for twenty years, either."

Ines found he couldn't speak. Twenty years of a story with a clean villain in it, and here at the top of the world the villain dissolved into three frightened men and a flood that didn't care about any of them.

"Three versions," Tomás said into the silence, counting on his fingers with the gravity of a scholar. "All true. None complete." He snorted. "Like my marriages."

Despite everything — despite the leg and the storm and the graves — Mara laughed, wet and startled, and Emil grinned, and even Brandt's mouth twitched at one corner, and for a moment the high camp felt almost warm.

* * *

It was Brandt, later, standing the night watch while the others slept, who ended the day the way he seemed to end everything: with a fact nobody wanted.

Ines crawled out of the tent at midnight to relieve him and found him rigid, binoculars up, staring south down the valley toward the drowned town.

"Storm cleared the air," Brandt said, without turning. "You can see a long way tonight."

"See what?"

"Headlights." Brandt passed him the glasses. Far below, tiny and patient as insects, a column of lights was winding up the old haulage road into the closed valley — vehicles where no vehicle had any legal business being, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who owned the road.

Ines counted them twice. Nine lights. Maybe ten.

"They didn't follow us up," Brandt said. "They were always coming. Someone paid for this valley to stay shut for forty years, Halloran. Tonight somebody's paying for it to be opened."

Behind them, inside the tent, the stolen radio clicked on by itself, its little power light glowing green in the dark, and began, softly, to transmit.
