## 14. The Debt Collectors Arrive

The sound came first as a wrongness in the birds.

Ines had been mending a bootlace by the fire when the valley went quiet—not gradually, the way evening quiets things, but all at once, like a door closing. He'd spent enough years in high country to know that silence was never empty. It was always listening for something.

Then the thump of rotors rolled down the gorge, bouncing off the walls, arriving from everywhere at once.

"Emil," he said quietly. "Company."

The old man looked up from the survey pages he'd been verifying for the hundredth time, his shattered leg propped on a pack, his face gray with pain and stubbornness in equal measure. "They said nine days without radio would bring someone."

"They said it would bring rescuers." Ines stood, shading his eyes toward the eastern ridge where the sound was loudest. "This isn't rescue. Rescue doesn't make the birds leave."

Mara was already moving, gathering the survey into its oilcloth wrap with the quick, careful hands of someone who had grown up around valuable paper. "How many men do they send for a thing like this?"

"As many as they can afford to lose track of." Ines took the wrap from her and stowed it inside his jacket, against his back, where a knife wouldn't reach it easily. "Which is why it stays on my body until we're out of this valley. No offense meant."

"Some taken," Mara said. But she didn't argue.

* * *

The helicopter didn't land. It couldn't—there was no shelf flat enough above the high camp—but it circled twice, low enough that the rotor wash flattened the scrub and sent Emil's tarp snapping like a whip, and then it set down three men on the saddle below the camp and lifted away again, heading north. Coming back later, Ines understood. Coming back for whatever was left.

He watched the three figures climb toward them through his binoculars and felt something old and cold settle in his chest. He knew the walk. That unhurried, deliberate stride of men who were paid by the day and had nowhere else to be. The debt collectors in Puerto Ceniza had walked exactly like that, the one who shadowed Mara through the market, the one who'd sat outside Ines's rented room smoking cheap cigarettes and pretending not to be there.

"Three," he told Tomás's empty spot by the fire, out of habit, before remembering Tomás wasn't there.

That still hurt in a way he hadn't sorted out yet. Two nights ago, after the confession at the falls—*they paid me, Ines, they paid me to report where you slept*—Tomás had stripped off his pack, laid his knife on a stone, and walked back downstream into the fog without a word. Ines had let him go. Stripped him of trust, the way you'd strip a rope of its sheath to see the broken strands. And now here came the consequence of that broken trust walking up the mountain wearing boots.

"Friends of yours?" Brandt asked. He'd appeared at the edge of camp with his rifle held loosely, the way a man holds a tool he's used too long to fear. Half-starved, twenty years alone in a closed valley, and still the calmest person present.

"No. Friends of money." Ines checked his own rifle—the old bolt-action he'd carried since before the blacklisting, oiled and honest—and found his hands steady, which surprised him. "They want the survey destroyed or seized. Probably destroyed. Dead men can't testify about what they found."

"And we're the dead men," Mara said.

"Not yet." Ines looked at Emil, who was trying to stand and failing with tremendous dignity. "Can you walk?"

"I can be *carried*," Emil said, "if the carrier keeps his opinions to himself."

"That's the spirit of this whole expedition, honestly."

* * *

They chose the ground rather than letting it choose them. There was a bench of rock above the camp, maybe forty meters up, with two approaches: a scree gully on the left and a ramp of solid stone on the right. Ines put Brandt on the gully because Brandt shot like a man who'd been shooting at hunger for two decades, and put himself on the ramp with Mara below him holding the spare rifle she'd turned out to know how to use, which was one more thing about Mara Voss that kept rearranging themselves in his understanding of her.

Emil they moved behind a fin of rock with the medical kit and instructions to yell if he was dying and to keep quiet if he was merely furious.

The three men came up the ramp first, which Ines had expected, because ramps are what you take when you've been paid to come back alive. The lead man was broad, sunburned, wearing a jacket too clean for the mountains. The other two hung back with the loose readiness of hired muscle.

"That's far enough," Ines called down.

The broad man stopped and smiled up at him, and it was a good smile, professional, the kind of smile that had probably closed a lot of deals in a lot of dim rooms. "Halloran. Ines Halloran. You know, I feel like I know you already. Your friend Tomás talks about you a lot."

The words landed exactly where they were aimed. Ines let the silence sit.

"He also sold your position twice," the man continued pleasantly. "Once in Puerto Ceniza, once at the falls. We pay well for reliable informants. Unreliable ones—" he spread his hands, an entire philosophy of employment in a single gesture, "—well. Here's the offer, guide. The paper. All of it. And the old man's notes. In exchange, you four walk south, free and clear, debts dissolved, names cleaned. Ms. Voss's obligations vanish overnight. Poof. Everybody upstream of this valley gets to keep their pensions."

"You have the authority to dissolve her debt?" Mara called down.

"I have the authority to *lose* it," the man said. "There's a difference, but you wouldn't feel the difference."

Ines found, to his own mild surprise, that he almost laughed. It was such an honest pitch. Such a reasonable-sounding lie. He'd spent six years being managed by men who talked exactly like this—men who explained that the mountain route was closed, that the permit was denied, that the inquiry had found what it found, that his name would follow him to every trailhead in two countries, and wouldn't it be easier for everyone if he simply stopped guiding?

"The answer's no," he said.

The smile flickered—just a hairline crack, but Ines saw it, the way you see a hairline crack in rock that means the whole face is about to go. "You should think about the people around you, Halloran. Old man's got one good leg between him and the grave. The girl's soft. And Brandt—" the man's voice dropped into something almost gentle, "—Brandt's been dead up here for twenty years anyway. What's he protecting? A town that's underwater?"

"You know his name," Ines said slowly.

"We know everything the file says. The file says Silas Brandt, sole survivor, 1961, refused relocation assistance, entered the closed zone repeatedly, presumed—" the man shrugged, "—dead, mostly. Files are convenient that way."

And that was when Ines understood the shape of the thing fully. They hadn't come for the survey by accident. They'd come because someone, somewhere, had known for twenty years that a witness was living in this valley, and had finally decided the cost of leaving him alone exceeded the cost of collecting him. The disaster hadn't ended in 1961. It had just gone quiet, the way the birds had gone quiet.

"Last chance," the broad man said. "Paper or blood. I genuinely don't care which, but my employer prefers paper, because blood brings helicopters with red crosses, and red crosses bring questions."

"Then you'll be pleased to know," said a voice from the scree gully, "that nobody's bleeding yet."

* * *

Tomás stepped out of the rocks like a man stepping out of a bad dream he'd finally decided to wake up from.

He looked terrible. He'd lost weight in two days, which shouldn't have been possible, and his eyes had the raw, scraped look of someone who hadn't slept so much as run out of the energy to keep them closed. He still had his rifle, slung across his back, and his hands were raised, empty, palms out.

"Tomás," Ines breathed.

"Don't," Tomás said, without looking at him. His voice cracked on it. "Don't say anything. Not yet. Let me get through this part."

The broad man's smile returned, warmer now, genuine relief in it. "Tomás! Good man. Come down here. These people have been filling your head with—"

"I reported his position," Tomás said loudly, over him, to the mountains as much as to anyone. "Twice. For money. Because I owed money, and because it was easy, and because I told myself he'd survived worse than a visit from you people." His jaw worked. "But I never agreed to this. Never agreed to guns in a closed valley. Never agreed to old men and girls. So here's what happens now. You turn around and go down, or I start telling everyone—judges, newspapers, whoever will sit still for it—exactly who paid me, and how much, and where the offices are."

The silence that followed had a texture to it. Ines had heard that silence before, on ridges, in couloirs, in the half-second before weather turns. It was the silence of a decision being made somewhere below the level of words.

"Tomás," the broad man said, and the warmth was gone now, replaced by something patient and tired, "you understand your situation."

"I understand it fine."

"You're standing between us and a very expensive piece of paper."

"I'm standing between you and *them*," Tomás said. "The paper's incidental. That's the whole point. That's what I finally—" He stopped. Looked up at Ines for the first time, and what was in his face was worse than apology; it was the look of a man who has done his arithmetic and found the sum unbearable. "I'm sorry I'm late."

Everything happened fast after that, the way bad things do—no windup, no dramatic pause, just the world lurching from one arrangement of itself into a worse one.

The man on the right moved first, swinging his rifle up, not at Tomás but past him, up the ramp, at Ines—at the jacket, at the oilcloth-wrapped weight against Ines's spine—and Ines was dropping behind the stone when Tomás did the arithmetic one final time and stepped into the line.

The shot went off twice, or sounded like twice, echo folding echo in the gorge.

Ines came up shooting at the muzzle flash and hit nothing vital, or nothing at all; the two men were already breaking downhill, dragging their leader, whose shoulder was streaming red where Ines's second round had clipped him. Then the rocks were empty and the birds were screaming again and the only sound that mattered was coming from the scree gully, a wet, effortful sound, breathing with a hole in it.

"No," Ines said, sliding down into the gully, stones avalanching around him. "No no no, you idiot, you beautiful idiot—"

Tomás was on his back with his hands pressed under his collarbone, and blood was coming up between his fingers in pulses, and his face was already the color of the fog that lived in the lower gorge. Mara reached them first—she must have run the whole way—and tore open the medical kit with hands that shook and then, remarkably, stopped shaking.

"Through and through?" she asked, in the clipped tone of someone who had bandaged things before, real things.

"Front," Tomás managed. His teeth were chattering. "Didn't wait for the back. Always impatient. Since school."

"Shut up," Ines said. "Save your breath. Save it all."

"For what?" Tomás smiled, and it was the old smile, the one from the river towns, from the years before any of it. "You going to carry me out, Ines? Through the gorge? Down Rennick's pretty little lie of a route?"

Ines's hands were pressed over Mara's, over the wound, feeling the pulse fight under his palms, and the question went through him like a probe finding the exact depth of a crevasse. The gorge. The unclimbed gorge. The corrected lines from the survey, memorized years ago in a mentor's kitchen, every bearing and break in slope, the route that had killed eleven miners because a great man had redrawn it over breakfast to protect a shaft he knew was rotten.

"Yes," Ines said.

"It's suicide," Brandt said quietly, from above. "Nobody's run that gorge. Nobody's ever run it."

"Rennick drew it safe," Ines said. "It wasn't safe. But I know where every lie sits. Every false bearing, every shaft he buried on paper. I can walk us down the truth of it." He looked at his hands, red to the wrist, then at Tomás's graying face. "I have to. I dragged him up here. I dragged all of you up here. This is the price of that, and I'm done paying it in pieces."

Above them, faint but unmistakable, the helicopter was coming back.

Mara tied off the last knot and sat back on her heels, bloodied to the elbows, and looked at Ines with an expression he'd seen once before on a woman deciding to renounce an inheritance: terror and certainty occupying the same face, neither winning.

"He can't wait for daylight," she said. "And we can't stay here. So when do we leave?"

Ines looked up at the gorge wall, black against the last light, at the invisible line where his mentor's lie ran down through the rock like a fault through stone.

"Now," he said. "We leave now."
