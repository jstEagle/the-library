## 15. Descent Is Not Retreat

The helicopter passed over the high camp twice, low enough that its rotor wash flattened the snow against the tent fabric and sent Brandt's cook pot skittering across the rocks. It didn't land. It didn't need to. It was counting them.

Ines stood in the open with his hands on his hips and let it look at him. There was a certain arithmetic to being watched from above: four alive, one dying, one survey, no way out but down. He could do that math himself. The men in the helicopter could too. The only question left was who got to finish the equation first.

When the sound faded southward, he turned back to the camp. Tomás lay inside the tent where they'd dragged him, gray as old candle wax, a field dressing wrapped around his shoulder and another around his ribs, both already blooming through. Mara knelt beside him with her hand flat on his forehead, doing the small useless work of keeping him cool. Emil propped himself against a boulder with his shattered leg splinted straight out before him like a fallen spar, and Brandt stood apart from all of them, rifle slung, watching the gorge wall as if it might apologize.

"He needs a hospital," Mara said without looking up. "Not tomorrow. Not dawn."

"I know," Ines said.

"Then say what you're thinking, because you've been standing there doing your statue impression for five minutes and I'm losing patience with statues."

What he was thinking had been assembling itself since the bullet went in—piece by piece, the way he'd once assembled routes, testing each hold before trusting his weight to it. He crouched by the boulder so he could see all of them at once, which was a habit from guiding: never deliver bad news to people you can't see.

"The east trail is closed," he said. "Weather's coming down the col—you can smell it—and even if it weren't, that's the way the helicopter came. They'll have eyes on it. Maybe men on it." He let that sit. "There's one other way down."

Brandt's head came around slowly. "No."

"You haven't heard it yet."

"I don't need to hear it. I know which way you mean." Brandt came closer, boots crunching, and pointed at the gorge wall with a finger that did not quite tremble, though it wanted to. "That route doesn't exist. That's the point of it. Rennick scrubbed it off every map because it killed two of his own men in '58, and then he falsified the survey lines to make sure nobody ever tried it again. You want to walk us into the lie itself."

"I want to walk us down the truth," Ines said. "The lie is the map everyone else is carrying. I memorized the corrected lines. All of them. Every station, every bearing, every place where Rennick's ink says *impassable* and the actual rock says otherwise." He looked at his hands for a moment. "I spent three years after the blacklisting going over those maps the way a man goes over a letter from someone who wronged him. Looking for the flaw. Finding instead that the flaw was put there on purpose, and that underneath it was the most honest route I've ever read. Rennick couldn't destroy the real survey. So he buried it inside the false one, where only someone looking for his mistake would ever find it."

"That's a romantic story," Brandt said.

"It's the only story we have. And it's the only exit they won't be watching, because officially it isn't there."

Silence, except the wind beginning to arrive in long gusts off the col, carrying the smell of snow that hadn't fallen yet but intended to.

Emil Voss spoke from his boulder, his voice dry as gravel. "How long?"

"Two days if nothing goes wrong. Three if something does."

"Something will go wrong," Brandt said.

"Almost certainly," Ines agreed. "But the east trail guarantees it. This only offers it."

Emil looked at his leg, then at Tomás through the tent flap, then at the sky, reading it the way an old expedition man reads it—not the clouds themselves but the pressure behind them. "My daughter asked me once why I kept coming back to mountains that had already hurt me." He said it to no one in particular. "I told her it's because the mountain doesn't change its mind. Only people do." He looked up at Ines. "I'm sixty-one years old, Mr. Halloran, and I cannot walk, and I am asking you—in the formal sense—to guide me. Do you accept the commission?"

Ines felt something turn over in his chest, some engine that had been cold for six years catching on the second try. "I accept," he said. "Terms are: everyone does exactly what I say, when I say it, including you, Mr. Voss. Especially you."

"Especially me," Emil agreed gravely. "God help us all."

"No," Brandt said again, but quieter now, and Ines recognized the tone. It wasn't refusal anymore. It was grief negotiating with necessity.

"Silas." Ines stood and faced him fully. "You've guarded this valley for twenty years. I'm not asking you to stop guarding it. I'm asking you to guard the people in it for two more days. After that—" he touched the oilcloth-wrapped survey in Brandt's pack, "—that goes where it goes, and you get to decide how. But Tomás doesn't get to decide anything, and he's bleeding out while we debate."

Brandt stared at him for a long moment. Then he unslung the rifle, checked it with the automatic tenderness of a man checking something he loved, and said, "I'll carry the rear. If anyone falls, I catch them. If anyone chases us—" he patted the stock, "—I handle that part."

"That's the job description exactly," Ines said.

* * *

They broke camp in ninety minutes, which Ines considered a small miracle given that half his party was held together by splints and stubbornness. He'd built a litter from the tent poles and rope, padded with everything soft they owned, and rigged it to be carried by two with a third relieving. Mara took the front handles first and refused to discuss it. Brandt carried Tomás's pack along with his own, plus the survey, plus the rifle, moving under the weight like a man who had decided long ago that suffering was just weather you chose yourself.

Tomás surfaced from unconsciousness once, as they were lashing the last knots, and found Ines's sleeve with fingers that had gone papery.

"Ines." His voice was a ruin. "The gorge. You're not—tell me you're not taking us down the gorge."

"Would it change your vote?"

"My vote fell off a cliff somewhere above the falls." A ghost of the old grin moved through his face and died of exhaustion. "You always did like the routes nobody else would touch."

"Somebody has to," Ines said. "Rest. Save the complaining for when you can do it properly."

Tomás laughed, which cost him visibly, and went under again.

* * *

The entrance to the gorge announced itself the way Rennick must have hated it announcing itself: honestly. Where the official map showed a solid wall of unbroken cliff, the rock split open in a slot barely wider than a cart track, hidden behind a screen of fallen slabs that nature had arranged with the casual cunning of a liar covering his tracks. Ines stopped the party in the shadow of the screen and made them wait while he went ahead alone, the way he'd been taught—the right way, the way he'd taught a hundred clients before the teaching had been taken from him.

Inside, the air changed. Colder, wetter, tasting of stone and old water. The walls rose sheer on either side, close enough in places that a man with a pack brushed both at once, and forty meters overhead they leaned toward each other until they shut out most of the sky, leaving a ribbon of darkening blue like a river running the wrong direction.

He found the first survey station within ten minutes—a drilled bolt, green with age, set into the wall at chest height with a stamped number still legible beneath the corrosion. Station 14. Rennick's false map placed Station 14 three hundred meters further north, in the middle of a cliff face, where any surveyor checking it would find nothing and conclude the whole line was fantasy. But here it was. Here it had always been.

Ines put his palm flat against the bolt, cold as a coin from a dead man's pocket.

"All right, old man," he murmured. "Show me the rest of it."

* * *

They descended through the afternoon and into the blue hour, and the gorge did everything the corrected lines promised and several things they hadn't. There was a traverse across a slab polished by centuries of meltwater where Ines fixed a rope and ferried Emil's litter across hand over hand, Mara holding her father's head clear of the stone, both of them silent in the way of people performing trust as a physical act. There was a chokestone the size of a chapel that the map's lie rendered as open passage, and the truth required a squeeze behind it that Brandt had to widen with the rifle butt and considerable blasphemy. There was a moment—Ines would remember it later in the specific, merciless detail that memory reserved for its favorite cruelties—when the litter slipped on a wet ledge and slid a meter before he caught the frame with his shoulder and held it, alone, boots skating, until Brandt arrived and took the other end without a word.

Afterward Mara looked at him and said only, "Station 19?"

"Station 19," he confirmed, and she nodded, and that was the whole conversation, and somehow it was enough.

The snow began an hour after full dark. Fine at first, almost polite, then thickening with the wind funneled down the gorge into a white roar that erased the ribbon of sky entirely. Ines called a halt at a hollow beneath an overhang—Station 23, if the bolt he found by touch was honest—and they crowded into it, backs to the rock, litter in the middle, and ate cold food by feel.

"We're making good time," he told them, and heard how it sounded, and amended: "We're making time."

"Guide's honesty," Emil said from the litter. "Rennick never had it, you know. Not really. He could tell the truth about rock better than any man I ever met. It was only people he lied to."

"Mr. Voss," Brandt said quietly, from the edge of the shelter, facing out into the storm, "you should sleep."

"I should. I'm not going to. I'm going to listen to this young man navigate in the dark, because it's the finest thing I've seen since '59." A pause, and then, softer: "Aldous would have been proud of him. That's the cruelest part."

Nobody answered that. The wind filled the silence for them.

* * *

They moved again at first light, or what passed for it—a gray suggestion filtering down through the snow. Ines led on memory and the numbered bolts, touching each one like a rosary, and somewhere in the third hour the gorge began to widen and drop, and the sound reached them.

Water.

Not meltwater trickle. Volume. A deep, muscular, rising sound, coming up the gorge from below like something inhaling.

Ines stopped the party and went forward alone around a bend, and stopped again.

Below, where the corrected survey showed a series of stepped cascades descending toward the drowned town, the river had come up to meet them. Meltwater from the storm, backing up the gorge throat, turning the lower cascades into a single brown torrent. The route continued—but along ledges that the water was already licking, ledges that would be underwater within hours. Perhaps less.

Behind him he heard the litter set down, heard Mara's boots on stone, and then her voice, very close:

"How bad?"

He didn't answer immediately. He was looking at the water, and the water was looking back, and between them ran a ledge perhaps forty centimeters wide, slick as glass, running two hundred meters along the gorge wall to where the route climbed free of the flood line.

"It's passable," he said finally. "For about six hours. Then it isn't."

"And Tomás?"

Ines looked back down the gorge. Tomás had woken again—had insisted on walking, actually, one arm over Brandt's shoulders, gray-faced and swaying, refusing the litter with the last of his pride. He was watching Ines with eyes that had gone clear and calm in the way that badly hurt men's eyes sometimes did, as if pain had burned away everything except the seeing.

"Don't carry me fast," Tomás said. "Carry me right. I'd rather arrive late than arrive dead, and I've been both before."

Ines laughed despite everything—a short, cracked sound the wind took away. Then he turned back to the ledge, and the rising water, and the route his mentor had lied about, and felt the strange settling in his chest that he supposed was what leadership felt like when it stopped being a title and became simply the next correct thing to do.

"Rope up," he said. "Single file. Nobody moves until I call the stations. We go down together or not at all—and Silas, if that water rises past my knees, you tell me, because I'll be too busy lying to myself to notice."

Brandt almost smiled. "First honest thing you've said all trip."

"Liar's privilege," Ines said, and stepped onto the ledge, and the river rose to meet him.
